TOPIC: Writing A Christmas Carol
===
IMG: dickens-writing-late-night-candle-pages | a young Victorian writer with long dark hair writing quickly with a quill by candlelight late at night at a desk covered with handwritten pages full of crossings-out, a small fire glowing, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm candlelight
HEAD: Dickens wrote A Christmas Carol in only six weeks, in October and November 1843.
BODY: Charles Dickens began writing A Christmas Carol in October 1843, soon after his visit to Manchester. Historians note that he finished it by the end of November, in only about six weeks. He wrote it while also working on the monthly instalments of his novel Martin Chuzzlewit. Even so, he wrote with great excitement and emotion. He later said that he wept and laughed while writing it, and that he walked the streets of London at night thinking about it. The original manuscript survives and is kept in the Morgan Library in New York. It shows many corrections and changes, crossed-out words and additions written in the margins. Historians note that it reveals how carefully Dickens worked on every sentence. He chose words for their sound as well as their meaning, because he knew the story would be read aloud. The book was short, only about thirty thousand words, so that it could be read in a single evening.
===
IMG: publisher-office-printing-proofs-1843 | a Victorian publisher in a dark frock coat and a young author examining printed proofs of a small book at a table in a publishing office in London in 1843, stacks of books and papers around, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft window light
HEAD: Dickens paid for the book's printing himself and insisted that it should be beautifully made.
BODY: A Christmas Carol was published by Chapman and Hall, who had published many of Dickens's earlier books. Historians note that Dickens had quarrelled with them over money, so he made an unusual arrangement. He paid for the production of the book himself and received the profits, while the publishers received a commission. He insisted that the book should be beautiful, with a red cloth binding, gold lettering, gilt edges and coloured illustrations. Even so, this made it expensive to produce. He set the price at five shillings, which was affordable for middle-class readers but too much for the poor. The book sold very well, but because of the high production costs, Dickens's profits were disappointing. He had hoped to earn about one thousand pounds, but he received only about two hundred and thirty. Historians note that he was bitterly disappointed. Yet the book's success with readers was enormous, and it made his name even more famous.

IMG: john-leech-artist-etching-plate | a Victorian artist in a waistcoat carefully drawing on a small copper etching plate at a table by a window, small paintbrushes and colour pots beside him, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft daylight
HEAD: The artist John Leech made four coloured illustrations and four black and white drawings for the book.
BODY: The illustrations for A Christmas Carol were made by John Leech, a popular artist who worked for the comic magazine Punch. Historians note that he made four full-page coloured etchings and four black and white wood engravings. The coloured pictures had to be painted by hand, copy by copy, which was slow and expensive. One of the most famous shows Mr Fezziwig's Christmas ball, with dancing couples and a fiddler. Another shows the Ghost of Christmas Present, a jolly giant in a green robe trimmed with fur, surrounded by food. Even so, Dickens was very particular about the illustrations. He checked each one carefully and asked for changes when he was not satisfied. Historians note that Leech's pictures helped shape how people imagined the characters, especially the Ghost of Christmas Present. His image of a large, bearded figure in a fur-trimmed robe later influenced the popular image of Father Christmas.
===
IMG: bookshop-window-red-books-december | a Victorian bookshop window in London in December 1843 displaying small red books with gold edges, customers in top hats and bonnets looking in, gaslight glowing, light snow, cool blue evening
HEAD: The first six thousand copies of A Christmas Carol sold out by Christmas Eve.
BODY: A Christmas Carol went on sale on the nineteenth of December 1843. Historians note that it was an immediate success. The first printing of six thousand copies was sold out by Christmas Eve. More printings followed quickly, and by the end of 1844, it had been reprinted many times. Reviewers praised it warmly. The writer William Makepeace Thackeray called it a national benefit. Even so, the book's success also caused problems. Within weeks, a publisher named Parley's Illuminated Library produced a cheap, pirated version, changing some words to avoid copyright. Dickens sued and won, but the pirate publishers declared bankruptcy, and Dickens had to pay large legal costs. Historians note that he was angry and frustrated. Yet the book continued to sell. Stage versions also appeared quickly. By February 1844, at least eight different plays based on the story were being performed in London theatres. The story had captured the public's imagination.

IMG: scrooge-character-old-miser-counting-house | an old thin Victorian businessman with a sharp face in a dark coat sitting alone at a desk counting coins by a single candle in a gloomy counting house, a tiny fire in the grate, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, dim light
HEAD: The story of Ebenezer Scrooge showed readers that it was never too late to change.
BODY: The hero of A Christmas Carol, Ebenezer Scrooge, is a cold, mean and lonely old businessman. Historians note that he hates Christmas and refuses to give money to the poor. He pays his clerk, Bob Cratchit, very little and keeps his office cold to save coal. On Christmas Eve, he is visited by the ghost of his dead business partner, Jacob Marley, who warns him to change his ways. Then three more spirits visit him: the Ghosts of Christmas Past, Christmas Present and Christmas Yet to Come. Even so, the story is not only a ghost story. It is a story about memory, regret and hope. The spirits show Scrooge his lonely childhood, the joy of others at Christmas and the sad future that awaits him if he does not change. Historians note that by the end, Scrooge has become generous and kind. The name Scrooge has since entered the English language, meaning a mean and miserly person.

IMG: fezziwig-ball-dancing-warehouse-fiddler | a joyful Victorian Christmas dance in a cleared warehouse lit by lamps, couples dancing in a line to a fiddler on a box, a stout cheerful employer and his wife leading, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm golden light
HEAD: One of the happiest scenes in the book is a Christmas dance held by a kind employer named Fezziwig.
BODY: In A Christmas Carol, the Ghost of Christmas Past takes Scrooge back to his youth, when he was an apprentice to a kind old merchant named Mr Fezziwig. Historians note that on Christmas Eve, Fezziwig cleared his warehouse and held a party for his workers, family and neighbours. There was a fiddler, dancing, cake, roast meat and mince pies. Everyone was happy, and the party cost Fezziwig only a little money. Even so, Scrooge realises that his old master's kindness meant a great deal to his workers. The scene shows that a good employer could make his workers happy with simple kindness. Historians note that Dickens may have based Fezziwig's party on memories of happy celebrations from his own life. The scene was illustrated by John Leech, and it became one of the most popular parts of the book. It showed the joy that Christmas could bring when people shared their good fortune with others.

TOPIC: Christmas customs, old and new
===
IMG: yule-log-fireplace-old-manor-hall | a large log burning in a great stone fireplace in an old English manor hall decorated with holly and ivy at Christmas, a family gathered around it, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a timber ceiling, warm firelight
HEAD: Many Victorian Christmas customs were very old, going back to medieval and even ancient times.
BODY: Christmas had been celebrated in England for more than a thousand years by 1843. Historians note that many customs were very old. In medieval times, Christmas was a twelve-day festival, from Christmas Day to Twelfth Night on the fifth of January. There were feasts, games, plays and music. Great houses burned a Yule log, a large log that was supposed to burn throughout the festival. Even so, Christmas celebrations had declined in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. In the 1640s and 1650s, the Puritans, who thought the festival was too wild and too Catholic, banned Christmas celebrations. After the restoration of the monarchy in 1660, Christmas returned, but in a quieter form. By the early nineteenth century, many old customs had faded. Historians note that the Victorians revived and reshaped Christmas. Writers such as Washington Irving, Walter Scott and Dickens helped create a new interest in old traditions, mixing them with new customs.
===
IMG: mistletoe-hanging-doorway-couple-parlour | a sprig of mistletoe with white berries hanging from a ribbon in a doorway of a Victorian parlour decorated with holly, a smiling young couple beneath it, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm candlelight
HEAD: Kissing under the mistletoe was a popular Christmas custom, especially among young people.
BODY: Mistletoe is a plant that grows on trees, with small white berries. Historians note that it was considered special by ancient peoples, including the Celtic druids, who believed it had magical powers. In England, it became connected with Christmas. A sprig of mistletoe, sometimes made into a kissing bough with greenery, ribbons and apples, was hung from the ceiling or in a doorway. Couples who met beneath it were expected to kiss. Even so, there were rules. In some households, a berry was picked from the mistletoe after each kiss, and when the berries were gone, the kissing had to stop. Historians note that the custom was popular among servants and young people, though some disapproved. In The Pickwick Papers, Dickens described a lively Christmas party at a country house, with kissing under the mistletoe. The custom remains popular today, and mistletoe is still sold at Christmas in markets across Britain.

IMG: christmas-pudding-boiling-copper-steam | a Victorian cook in an apron lifting a round Christmas pudding wrapped in a cloth out of a steaming copper pot in a kitchen in 1843, steam rising, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm firelight
HEAD: The Christmas pudding was boiled in a cloth for hours and served flaming with brandy.
BODY: The Christmas pudding was the highlight of the Victorian Christmas dinner. Historians note that it was made with suet, flour, breadcrumbs, sugar, raisins, currants, candied peel, eggs and spices. The mixture was tied up in a cloth and boiled for many hours in a large pot or copper, a big metal container used for washing clothes. When it was ready, the pudding was turned out onto a plate, decorated with a sprig of holly, doused with brandy and set alight. Even so, puddings were often made weeks before Christmas. Families gathered on Stir-up Sunday, the last Sunday before Advent, to mix the pudding. Each member of the family stirred it and made a wish. Coins or small charms were sometimes hidden inside. Historians note that in A Christmas Carol, Mrs Cratchit's pudding is described as being like a speckled cannonball, hard and firm, blazing with brandy and with holly stuck into the top.

IMG: german-christmas-tree-candles-1840s | a small fir tree decorated with lit candles, sweets, paper ornaments and small gifts standing on a table in a Victorian drawing room in the 1840s, children gazing up at it in wonder, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm candlelight
HEAD: The Christmas tree was a German custom that was only beginning to appear in England in the 1840s.
BODY: The custom of decorating a tree at Christmas came from Germany. Historians note that it had been popular there since at least the sixteenth century. In England, Queen Charlotte, the German wife of King George the Third, had a decorated tree at court in 1800. Prince Albert, Queen Victoria's husband, also brought the custom with him from Germany. Even so, in 1843, Christmas trees were still rare in English homes. Most families decorated with holly, ivy and mistletoe instead. The trees became popular after 1848, when the Illustrated London News published a picture of the royal family around their tree. Families across the country began to copy them. Victorian trees were usually small, placed on a table and decorated with candles, sweets, fruit, paper ornaments and small presents. Historians note that the lit candles were beautiful but dangerous. Fires caused by Christmas trees were common. A bucket of water was often kept nearby.

IMG: twelfth-night-cake-party-crown | a large decorated Twelfth Night cake with sugar figures on a table at a Victorian family party, a child wearing a paper crown, adults laughing, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm candlelight
HEAD: In 1843, Twelfth Night, on the fifth of January, was still an important part of the Christmas season.
BODY: In 1843, the Christmas season did not end on Christmas Day. Historians note that it continued until Twelfth Night, the evening of the fifth of January, before the feast of the Epiphany. Twelfth Night was a time of parties, games and fun. The centre of the celebration was the Twelfth Night cake, a large, rich fruit cake decorated with sugar figures and icing. A bean and a pea were hidden inside. Whoever found the bean became the king of the party, and whoever found the pea became the queen. Even so, Twelfth Night celebrations declined during the Victorian period. Queen Victoria is said to have disapproved of the rowdy parties. Many Twelfth Night customs, including the cake, were moved to Christmas. Historians note that the Twelfth Night cake became the Christmas cake, decorated with white icing and festive figures. Dickens himself loved Twelfth Night and held a party each year, also celebrating his son Charley's birthday.

IMG: parlour-games-blind-mans-buff-family | a Victorian family and guests playing blind man's buff in a parlour decorated with holly, a laughing young man with a scarf over his eyes reaching out, children dodging around the furniture, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm candlelight
HEAD: Victorian families played games at Christmas, such as blind man's buff and snapdragon.
BODY: Games were an important part of Victorian Christmas celebrations. Historians note that families and guests played games in the parlour after dinner. One of the most popular was blind man's buff, in which one player was blindfolded and tried to catch the others. Another was snapdragon, in which raisins were placed in a dish of brandy and set alight, and players tried to snatch them from the flames. Even so, some games were quieter. Word games, guessing games and charades, in which players acted out words or phrases, were popular. Historians note that in A Christmas Carol, Scrooge's nephew Fred holds a Christmas party where the guests play blind man's buff and a guessing game called Yes and No. Scrooge, invisible beside the Ghost of Christmas Present, becomes so excited that he joins in, though no one can hear him. Dickens himself loved games and played them enthusiastically with his family and friends.

IMG: carol-book-hymns-church-choir-1843 | a Victorian church choir in white surplices singing from hymn books by candlelight in a decorated parish church on Christmas Eve, holly on the pews, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a timber ceiling, warm candlelight
HEAD: Many of the Christmas carols sung today were written or became popular in the Victorian period.
BODY: The Victorian period was a great age of Christmas carols. Historians note that many carols that are still sung today were written, translated or made popular during Victoria's reign. Some were old carols revived by collectors. Others were new. Hark! The Herald Angels Sing had been written by Charles Wesley in the eighteenth century and was set to music by Felix Mendelssohn in the 1850s. Good King Wenceslas was written by John Mason Neale in 1853. Silent Night, written in Austria in 1818, was translated into English in the 1850s and 1860s. Even so, in 1843, carol singing was still mainly a popular custom, sung in the streets and in homes, rather than in churches. Many churches preferred hymns and psalms. Historians note that by the end of the century, carols had become an important part of church services at Christmas. Collections of carols were published, and choirs sang them in churches across the country.

TOPIC: Christmas Eve, 1843
===
IMG: christmas-eve-shops-closing-snow-street | a London street on Christmas Eve 1843 with shopkeepers closing their shutters, a few late shoppers hurrying home with parcels, gas lamps glowing and light snow falling, cool blue evening light
HEAD: On Christmas Eve, the shops closed late, and people hurried home through the cold streets.
BODY: Christmas Eve in Victorian London was a busy and exciting day. Historians note that shops stayed open late, as people bought the last things they needed for Christmas. Markets were crowded, and street sellers did a brisk trade. Workers were paid their wages, and some received extra money as a Christmas bonus. Even so, not everyone had a day off. Many people worked until late on Christmas Eve, including shop assistants, clerks and servants. In A Christmas Carol, Scrooge keeps Bob Cratchit working until the office closes on Christmas Eve, and grudgingly gives him Christmas Day off. Bob then runs home, sliding on the ice in the street with a group of boys, in honour of its being Christmas Eve. Historians note that as evening fell, the shops closed their shutters, the streets grew quieter, and people hurried home to their families. The bells of the churches began to ring for evening services.
===
IMG: family-decorating-parlour-holly-ivy | a Victorian family decorating their parlour on Christmas Eve 1843, the mother hanging holly over a picture frame, children twisting ivy around the mantelpiece, a fire burning, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm candlelight
HEAD: On Christmas Eve, families decorated their homes with holly, ivy and other evergreens.
BODY: In Victorian homes, decorations were usually put up on Christmas Eve. Historians note that it was considered unlucky to bring evergreens into the house before then. Families decorated their rooms with holly, ivy, laurel, rosemary and mistletoe. They placed greenery over pictures, mirrors and doorways, and around the mantelpiece. Some made garlands and wreaths. Even so, decorations were simple compared with today. There were no electric lights or shop-bought ornaments for most families. Paper chains, ribbons and candles added colour and light. In wealthier homes, the decorations were more elaborate. Historians note that the decorations were usually kept up until Twelfth Night, when they were taken down and burned. It was considered unlucky to leave them up after that. The scent of evergreens, the glow of candles and the warmth of the fire created a special atmosphere. For children, the decorating was one of the most exciting moments of the season.

IMG: christmas-eve-ghost-story-fireside | a Victorian family gathered close around a fireplace on Christmas Eve with only firelight and a single candle, a grandfather telling a story while the children listen wide-eyed, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm flickering light
HEAD: Telling ghost stories around the fire was an old Christmas Eve tradition.
BODY: In Victorian England, Christmas Eve was a time for telling ghost stories. Historians note that this was an old tradition, going back centuries. In the long, dark nights of winter, families gathered around the fire and told tales of ghosts, spirits and strange happenings. The darkness outside and the warmth inside made the stories both frightening and comforting. Even so, ghost stories at Christmas were not meant to be truly terrifying. They were a kind of shared entertainment, enjoyed by young and old. Dickens loved the tradition. He described A Christmas Carol as a ghost story of Christmas, and he wrote it to be read aloud by the fire. Historians note that he wrote several other Christmas books and stories in the following years, many of them with ghosts. Other Victorian writers, such as Elizabeth Gaskell and later M. R. James, also wrote ghost stories for Christmas. The tradition continued throughout the century.

HEAD: Some people attended church services late on Christmas Eve, while bells rang across the city.
BODY: On Christmas Eve, some churches in London held services in the evening. Historians note that the bells of many churches rang to mark the beginning of Christmas. In some places, bell ringers rang the bells at midnight to welcome Christmas Day. Even so, midnight services were not as common in 1843 as they are today. They were held mainly in Catholic churches and in a few Anglican churches. Most people went to church on Christmas morning instead. Historians note that the sound of bells ringing across the city on Christmas Eve was a special part of the festive season. People walking home through the dark, cold streets heard the bells from near and far, echoing over the rooftops. In A Christmas Carol, the bells play an important part. Scrooge hears a bell ring before Marley's ghost appears, and each spirit arrives as a church bell strikes the hour. The bells mark the passing of his long night.

IMG: children-hanging-stockings-fireplace | Victorian children in nightgowns hanging long woollen stockings from the mantelpiece of a fireplace on Christmas Eve, a mother holding a candle smiling behind them, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm candlelight
HEAD: In 1843, Father Christmas was a jolly figure of feasting, not yet the bringer of presents to children.
BODY: In 1843, the figure of Father Christmas was well known in England, but he was different from the Father Christmas of today. Historians note that he was a jolly old man, dressed in a long green or brown robe trimmed with fur, who represented feasting, drinking and merriment. He did not bring presents to children. Even so, things were beginning to change. In America, the poem A Visit from Saint Nicholas, published in 1823, described Saint Nicholas arriving on Christmas Eve in a sleigh pulled by reindeer, bringing toys and filling stockings. This idea slowly spread to Britain. By the end of the nineteenth century, Father Christmas had become a bringer of presents, and children hung up stockings for him on Christmas Eve. Historians note that John Leech's picture of the Ghost of Christmas Present, in a green robe trimmed with fur, helped shape the image of Father Christmas in Britain.

IMG: quiet-snowy-street-night-christmas-eve | a quiet London street of Victorian houses late on Christmas Eve 1843, snow covering the roofs and cobbles, gas lamps glowing softly, a single window lit with candlelight, a deep blue starry sky
HEAD: By late on Christmas Eve, the streets were quiet, and the city waited for Christmas morning.
BODY: Late on Christmas Eve, London grew quiet. Historians imagine the scene: the shops closed, the markets empty, the streets silent except for the footsteps of a few late walkers and the occasional cab. Snow might cover the roofs and pavements, though white Christmases were less common than people imagined. Gas lamps glowed softly along the streets. Even so, in many homes, people were still awake. Mothers finished preparing the Christmas pudding and stuffing the goose. Fathers wrapped small presents. Children lay in bed, too excited to sleep. Historians note that the image of a snowy Victorian Christmas owed much to Dickens. When he was a child, in the 1810s, England had several very cold and snowy winters. He remembered them all his life and filled his Christmas stories with snow and frost. In A Christmas Carol, Scrooge wakes on Christmas morning to find a clear, bright, cold day, with frost in the air and bells ringing.

TOPIC: Christmas Day

IMG: christmas-morning-church-families-walking | Victorian families in their best clothes walking to church on a bright frosty Christmas morning in London in 1843, a stone church with a spire ahead, bells ringing, bare trees white with frost, clear winter light
HEAD: On Christmas morning, families dressed in their best clothes and went to church.
BODY: On Christmas morning in 1843, many families in London went to church. Historians note that people dressed in their best clothes, and children were washed, combed and dressed carefully. The churches were decorated with holly and evergreens, and the services included special readings, prayers and hymns about the birth of Christ. Even so, not everyone went to church. Many working people were too tired or too poor, and some had lost touch with religion. Historians note that a religious census held in 1851 showed that less than half of the people in London attended church on a typical Sunday. Yet Christmas was a special day, and attendance was usually higher. After the service, people greeted their neighbours and friends, wishing each other a merry Christmas. In A Christmas Carol, the transformed Scrooge goes to church on Christmas morning and then walks through the streets, smiling at everyone and patting children on the head.

IMG: boy-carrying-goose-to-bakers-oven | a Victorian boy carefully carrying a goose in a dish covered with a cloth through a London street on Christmas morning 1843, other families carrying dishes to a baker's shop with a glowing oven, frosty light
HEAD: Families without ovens carried their geese and joints to the baker's shop to be roasted.
BODY: On Christmas morning, the streets of poorer neighbourhoods in London were filled with people carrying dishes to the bakers' shops. Historians note that many families did not have ovens large enough to roast a goose or joint of meat. Instead, they took their dinner to the local baker, who cooked it in his large bread oven for a small fee. The dishes were usually placed on a bed of potatoes, which roasted in the fat. Even so, this custom required careful timing. The baker cooked many dinners at once and had to make sure that each family got the right dish. Families collected their dinners at about one o'clock and carried them home, steaming and smelling delicious. Historians note that Dickens described this custom in A Christmas Carol. The Ghost of Christmas Present takes Scrooge through the streets, where people carry their dinners to the bakers' shops. The ghost sprinkles water from his torch, and quarrels turn into laughter.

IMG: christmas-dinner-table-wealthy-family | a wealthy Victorian family seated around a long dining table laden with a roast turkey, a joint of beef, vegetables and silver dishes on Christmas Day 1843, servants serving, candles and holly decorations, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm light
HEAD: Wealthy families sat down to grand Christmas dinners with turkey, beef, pies and many other dishes.
BODY: For wealthy families in Victorian London, Christmas dinner was a grand affair. Historians note that the table might include roast turkey or goose, roast beef, boiled ham, game pies, soups, vegetables, sauces, jellies, mince pies and the Christmas pudding. Wine, port and punch were served. Servants cooked and served the meal, which could last for hours. Even so, the servants themselves often had to work hard on Christmas Day. They prepared and served the family's dinner and cleaned up afterwards. In many households, they were given their own Christmas dinner, sometimes with gifts from their employers. Historians note that Christmas dinner became more elaborate during the Victorian period. Cookery books, such as Eliza Acton's Modern Cookery for Private Families, published in 1845, gave recipes for Christmas dishes. Later, Mrs Beeton's Book of Household Management, published in 1861, became the most famous Victorian cookery book, with many Christmas recipes.

IMG: servants-own-christmas-dinner-kitchen | Victorian household servants in aprons and caps sharing their own Christmas dinner around the kitchen table in the basement of a large house, a small roast and a pudding, smiling and toasting, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm firelight
HEAD: Servants worked hard on Christmas Day, but many were given their own dinner and small gifts.
BODY: In 1843, a great many young women in London worked as domestic servants. Historians note that large households employed many servants, including cooks, maids, footmen, nursemaids and coachmen. Even modest middle-class households often had one or two servants. At Christmas, servants had extra work, preparing food, decorating the house and serving guests. Even so, many employers treated their servants kindly at Christmas. Servants were often given a special dinner, sometimes with a goose or a joint of meat, a pudding and beer or wine. They might also receive small presents, such as cloth for a new dress, gloves or money. Historians note that some households held a servants' ball or party during the Christmas season. Yet the servants' Christmas depended greatly on the kindness of their employers. Some were treated generously, while others were given little or nothing. Their day off usually came later, after the family's celebrations were over.

IMG: queen-victoria-windsor-christmas-1843 | a Victorian royal family celebrating Christmas in a richly decorated room at Windsor Castle in 1843, a young queen and her husband with small children around a table with presents, candles glowing, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm light
HEAD: Queen Victoria and Prince Albert spent Christmas 1843 at Windsor Castle with their young children.
BODY: Queen Victoria and Prince Albert usually spent Christmas at Windsor Castle, west of London. Historians note that they celebrated in a mixture of English and German styles. In 1843, they had three young children: Victoria, Albert Edward and Alice. The royal family exchanged presents on Christmas Eve, following the German custom, with decorated trees on tables in the royal apartments. Even so, their Christmas also included English traditions, such as roast beef, plum pudding and a boar's head. Victoria wrote in her journal about the joy of seeing the children's delight. Historians note that the royal family's Christmas became a model for families across Britain. Newspapers and magazines described their celebrations, and people copied their customs. Victoria and Albert's example helped make Christmas a family festival, centred on children, home and gifts. Their love of the Christmas tree, in particular, made it popular throughout the country.

IMG: afternoon-family-games-fireside-christmas | a cheerful Victorian family in a modest parlour on Christmas afternoon 1843, the father playing a fiddle, children dancing, the grandmother in a chair by the fire, nuts and oranges on a table, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm firelight
HEAD: After dinner, families spent the afternoon and evening playing games, singing and telling stories.
BODY: After Christmas dinner, Victorian families relaxed together. Historians note that they played games, sang songs, danced, told stories and ate nuts, oranges and sweets. In homes with a piano, someone might play while others sang. In poorer homes, a fiddle or a voice might be the only music. Even so, the afternoon was also a time for rest. After a large meal, some people dozed by the fire. Others took a walk in the fresh air. Visitors came to wish the family a merry Christmas. Historians note that in A Christmas Carol, the Cratchits spend the evening around the fire, roasting chestnuts and drinking a hot punch made from gin and lemons. Tiny Tim sings a song about a lost child travelling in the snow. The family is poor, but happy and grateful. Dickens wanted to show that the true spirit of Christmas lay in love, family and kindness, not in wealth.

HEAD: In the story, Scrooge wakes on Christmas morning a changed man, and sends the Cratchits the prize turkey.
BODY: At the end of A Christmas Carol, Scrooge wakes up on Christmas morning and discovers that the spirits have done their work in a single night. Historians note that he is overjoyed. He throws open his window and asks a boy in the street what day it is. When the boy says it is Christmas Day, Scrooge sends him to buy the prize turkey from the poulterer's shop and has it delivered to the Cratchits. Even so, this is only the beginning of his change. He gives a large gift of money to the gentlemen collecting for the poor. He goes to his nephew Fred's house for Christmas dinner and is welcomed warmly. The next day, he raises Bob Cratchit's salary and promises to help his family. Historians note that the ending made readers cry and laugh. It showed that anyone could change, and that kindness could make a real difference. Dickens wrote that Scrooge became as good a friend as the city knew.

TOPIC: Boxing Day and after
===
IMG: boxing-day-tradesmen-christmas-box-door | a Victorian maid at the door of a London house handing small boxes and coins to a postman, a baker's boy and a milkman on a frosty morning, the day after Christmas 1843, clear winter light
HEAD: On the day after Christmas, called Boxing Day, tradesmen and servants received Christmas boxes.
BODY: The twenty-sixth of December was known in Britain as Boxing Day. Historians note that the name came from the custom of giving Christmas boxes, small gifts of money or goods, to tradesmen, servants and others who had served people during the year. Postmen, milkmen, bakers' boys, lamplighters and other workers went from house to house collecting their Christmas boxes. Even so, the origin of the name is debated. Some historians believe it came from the boxes kept in churches to collect money for the poor, which were opened on the day after Christmas, Saint Stephen's Day. Others believe it came from the boxes given to servants. In many households, servants were given the day off on Boxing Day to visit their families, after working hard on Christmas Day. Historians note that Boxing Day became an official public holiday in 1871. It is still a holiday in Britain and many other countries today.

HEAD: On Boxing Day, the theatres opened their Christmas pantomimes, which were loved by families.
BODY: Pantomime was one of the great entertainments of the Victorian Christmas season. Historians note that new pantomimes opened in London theatres on Boxing Day, and families flocked to see them. Victorian pantomimes were based on fairy tales and nursery rhymes, such as Cinderella, Jack and the Beanstalk and Puss in Boots. They included music, dancing, jokes, spectacular scenery and magical transformations. Even so, the most famous part of the early Victorian pantomime was the harlequinade, a comic chase involving the characters Harlequin, Columbine, Pantaloon and the Clown. The great clown Joseph Grimaldi had made the role famous in the early nineteenth century. Historians note that Dickens loved pantomime and edited Grimaldi's memoirs in 1838. Over time, the harlequinade became shorter, and the fairy tale became the main part of the show. Pantomimes remain a popular Christmas tradition in Britain today, with audiences joining in with songs and shouts.

HEAD: Within weeks of its publication, A Christmas Carol was being performed on the stages of London.
BODY: A Christmas Carol was so popular that theatres quickly turned it into plays. Historians note that by February 1844, at least eight different stage versions were being performed in London. In those days, there were few laws to protect authors, so theatres could adapt books without asking permission or paying the author. One of the most successful versions was produced at the Adelphi Theatre, with the approval of Dickens. Even so, Dickens was not always pleased with the adaptations. Some changed the story or added scenes. Yet he attended at least one and was moved by it. Historians note that the plays helped spread the story to people who could not afford to buy the book or could not read. Since then, A Christmas Carol has been adapted for the stage and the screen countless times. It is one of the most frequently adapted stories in the English language, performed every Christmas around the world.

HEAD: In later years, Dickens performed public readings of A Christmas Carol to huge audiences.
BODY: In December 1853, Charles Dickens gave his first public reading of A Christmas Carol at the Town Hall in Birmingham, to raise money for a new institute for working people. Historians note that it was a great success. About two thousand people came to listen. Dickens read for about three hours, using different voices for the characters and acting out the scenes. Even so, this was only the beginning. Over the next seventeen years, Dickens gave hundreds of public readings across Britain and America. A Christmas Carol was always one of his most popular pieces. He shortened it for performance and read it in a dramatic and moving style. Historians note that the readings were exhausting and may have damaged his health. He gave his last reading in March 1870, finishing with A Christmas Carol. He died on the ninth of June 1870, at the age of fifty-eight, and was buried in Westminster Abbey.

HEAD: In the years after 1843, the Victorian Christmas grew into the festival that is known today.
BODY: In the decades after 1843, Christmas in Britain changed greatly. Historians note that the Christmas tree became popular after 1848. Christmas cards became common in the 1870s and 1880s, as printing became cheaper and the postal service grew. Christmas crackers, invented by the London sweet maker Tom Smith around 1847, became a popular part of Christmas dinner. Turkey gradually replaced goose as the favourite Christmas meal. Even so, the heart of the Victorian Christmas remained the same: family, food, generosity and kindness. Historians note that Dickens's A Christmas Carol played an important part in shaping these values. It encouraged people to celebrate Christmas joyfully and to remember the poor. After reading it, some employers gave their workers extra money or a turkey at Christmas. Charities received more donations. The book helped make Christmas a time not only of celebration, but also of giving, which it remains for many people today.

HEAD: Today, the house where Dickens once lived in Doughty Street is a museum about his life and work.
BODY: Charles Dickens lived in many houses in London during his life. Historians note that the only one that survives is at 48 Doughty Street, in Bloomsbury, where he lived from 1837 to 1839. There he wrote Oliver Twist and Nicholas Nickleby. Today, the house is the Charles Dickens Museum. It is furnished as it might have been in his time, with his desk, chairs, books and personal objects. Even so, the house he lived in when he wrote A Christmas Carol, at Devonshire Terrace, no longer exists. Each December, the museum decorates its rooms for a Victorian Christmas and holds readings of A Christmas Carol. Historians note that Dickens's legacy is celebrated around the world. His books are read in many languages, and his characters are known to millions. His Christmas story, in particular, has never lost its popularity. Every December, families and theatres around the world tell it again.

HEAD: On Christmas night in 1843, the city slept, and the little red book lay waiting on many tables.
BODY: On Christmas night in 1843, London was quiet at last. Historians imagine the scene: families gone to bed after a long and happy day, fires burning low in the grates, the streets empty and silent. In some homes, a small red book with gold edges lay on the table, its pages read aloud by the fire that evening. Even so, not every home was warm and full. In the slums and the workhouses, Christmas had brought little comfort. Dickens knew this, and he hoped that his book would help change it. Historians note that A Christmas Carol did not end poverty in London, but it touched the hearts of many readers. It reminded them that Christmas was a time to care for others, and that it was never too late to change. More than one hundred and eighty years later, its message is still heard every December, as the lights glow and the bells ring once again.
