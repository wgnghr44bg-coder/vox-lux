TOPIC: Charles Dickens in 1843
===
IMG: charles-dickens-portrait-1843-study | a young Victorian writer of about thirty with long dark wavy hair and a colourful waistcoat sitting at a writing desk with a quill and papers in a comfortable study in 1843, books on shelves, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm lamplight
HEAD: In 1843, Charles Dickens was thirty-one years old and already the most famous writer in Britain.
BODY: Charles Dickens was born in 1812 in Portsmouth, on the south coast of England. Historians note that by 1843, he was already famous. His first novel, The Pickwick Papers, published in monthly parts in 1836 and 1837, had made him a household name. It was followed by Oliver Twist, Nicholas Nickleby, The Old Curiosity Shop and Barnaby Rudge. Readers across Britain and America eagerly waited for each new instalment. Even so, 1843 was a difficult year for him. His latest novel, Martin Chuzzlewit, was not selling as well as his earlier books. He had a growing family and large expenses, and he was worried about money. He had also been disappointed by a visit to America in 1842. Historians note that Dickens was energetic, ambitious and sensitive. He loved theatre, long walks and parties. He was also deeply concerned about poverty and injustice, which he had experienced himself as a child.
===
IMG: devonshire-terrace-house-regents-park | a large elegant Georgian house with a garden and iron railings near Regent's Park in London in 1843, a horse-drawn carriage waiting outside, bare winter trees, soft afternoon light
HEAD: Dickens lived with his family in a large house near Regent's Park.
BODY: In 1843, Charles Dickens lived at 1 Devonshire Terrace, a large house near Regent's Park, in northwest London. Historians note that he had moved there in 1839 with his wife, Catherine, and their growing family. By the end of 1843, they had four children, and a fifth was born in January 1844. The house had a large garden and was comfortably furnished. Dickens wrote in a study on the ground floor, overlooking the garden. Even so, running such a household was expensive. There were servants to pay, children to feed and clothe, and many guests to entertain. Dickens loved to give dinners and parties, especially at Christmas. His family later remembered the joyful Christmas celebrations at Devonshire Terrace, with games, music, conjuring tricks and dancing. Dickens himself often led the fun. Historians note that the house was demolished in the twentieth century. A stone carving on the building that replaced it shows Dickens and some of his characters.
===
IMG: boy-working-blacking-factory-1824 | a thin boy of about twelve in worn clothes sticking paper labels onto small pots on a wooden table in a dim, damp warehouse beside the Thames in 1824, other workers nearby, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, dim grey light
HEAD: As a boy of twelve, Dickens had been sent to work in a factory while his father was in a debtors' prison.
BODY: When Charles Dickens was twelve years old, his family fell into debt. Historians note that in 1824, his father, John Dickens, was sent to the Marshalsea, a prison for people who could not pay their debts. His mother and younger siblings joined his father in the prison, as was common at the time. Charles was sent to work at Warren's Blacking Factory, a warehouse near the Thames that made shoe polish. He worked long hours, sticking labels on pots, for six shillings a week. Even so, the experience lasted only a few months. When his father inherited some money, the family left the prison, and Charles went back to school. Yet he never forgot the shame and loneliness of that time. He told almost no one about it. Historians note that his childhood suffering gave him deep sympathy for poor children, and it influenced many of his books, including A Christmas Carol.
===
IMG: dickens-walking-london-streets-night | a young Victorian man in a long coat and top hat walking alone through dark empty London streets at night in 1843, gas lamps glowing, his breath steaming in the cold air
HEAD: Dickens loved to walk through the streets of London at night, sometimes for fifteen or twenty miles.
BODY: Charles Dickens was a tireless walker. Historians note that he often walked through the streets of London at night, covering fifteen or twenty miles. He walked when he was thinking about his stories, when he could not sleep and when he needed to calm his mind. He explored every part of the city, from the grand streets of the West End to the poorest slums of the East End. Even so, his walks were not only exercise. They were also research. He observed the people, the buildings, the shops and the street life of London. He noticed details that other people missed, such as the faces of the poor, the sounds of the streets and the smells of the markets. Historians note that he later said that while writing A Christmas Carol, he walked about the black streets of London many a night, after all sober people had gone to bed. The city itself was one of his greatest inspirations.
===
IMG: field-lane-ragged-school-children-1843 | poor Victorian children in ragged clothes sitting on wooden benches in a bare, crowded schoolroom lit by gas lamps, a teacher in a dark coat holding a book, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, dim warm light
HEAD: In September 1843, Dickens visited a school for the poorest children of London, and he was deeply shocked.
BODY: In September 1843, Charles Dickens visited the Field Lane Ragged School, near Saffron Hill, in one of the poorest parts of London. Historians note that ragged schools were free schools for very poor children, run by volunteers. They taught reading, writing and religion to children who could not afford ordinary schools. Many of the children were dirty, hungry and dressed in rags, which gave the schools their name. Even so, the schools did important work. They gave children some education and hope. Dickens was deeply moved and shocked by what he saw. He wrote to a friend that he had seldom seen such misery. He wanted to do something to help. Historians note that this visit was one of the inspirations for A Christmas Carol. In the book, the Ghost of Christmas Present shows Scrooge two ragged children, a boy and a girl, named Ignorance and Want. They represent the poor children Dickens had seen.
===
IMG: government-report-children-mines-1843 | a thick Victorian government report with a plain cover lying open on a desk, showing printed text and an engraving of a child pulling a coal cart in a mine tunnel, beside an oil lamp, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm lamplight
HEAD: A government report about child labour in 1843 made Dickens angry and determined to act.
BODY: In 1842 and 1843, a government commission published reports on the employment of children in mines and factories. Historians note that the reports described terrible conditions. Children as young as five or six worked in coal mines, pulling carts through narrow tunnels for twelve hours a day. Others worked in factories and workshops, in dangerous and unhealthy conditions. The reports were illustrated with engravings that shocked the public. Even so, many employers defended child labour, arguing that poor families needed the children's wages. Dickens read the second report in early 1843 and was horrified. At first, he planned to write a pamphlet called An Appeal to the People of England on behalf of the Poor Man's Child. Historians note that he later changed his mind. He decided that a story would reach more people than a pamphlet. He said that his book would come down with twenty thousand times the force of a pamphlet.
===
IMG: manchester-athenaeum-speech-hall-1843 | a Victorian gentleman giving a speech from a platform in a large hall in Manchester in 1843, an audience of men and women in their best clothes listening, gas lamps on the walls, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm light
HEAD: In October 1843, Dickens gave a speech in Manchester about the importance of education for the poor.
BODY: In October 1843, Charles Dickens travelled to Manchester to speak at a fundraising event for the Manchester Athenaeum, an institution that offered education to working people. Historians note that Manchester was one of the great industrial cities of Britain, full of cotton mills and factories. It was also a place of great poverty. In his speech, Dickens spoke about the importance of education in fighting poverty and crime. He argued that ignorance was the most dangerous enemy of society. Even so, the visit gave him more than a chance to speak. During his stay, he walked through the poor districts of the city and saw the conditions in which factory workers lived. Historians believe that it was during this visit that he first had the idea for A Christmas Carol. Soon after returning to London, he began to write. In just six weeks, he finished the book that would make him famous all over again.

TOPIC: The poor of London
===
IMG: slum-court-washing-lines-children-1843 | a crowded slum courtyard in London in 1843 with tall dilapidated brick houses, washing lines between windows, barefoot children playing in the mud and women talking in doorways, grey winter light
HEAD: Hundreds of thousands of Londoners lived in crowded and unhealthy slums.
BODY: In 1843, many of London's poor lived in slums, areas of crowded, old and decaying housing. Historians note that some of the worst were in Saint Giles, near Covent Garden, in Saffron Hill and in the East End. Families often lived in a single room, sometimes sharing it with other families. There was little light, no running water and no proper drainage. Toilets were shared by many people, and waste often overflowed into the streets. Even so, people in the slums tried to make the best of their lives. They worked hard, cared for their children and helped their neighbours. Many worked as labourers, street sellers, washerwomen and servants. Historians note that disease spread quickly in the slums. Cholera, typhus and tuberculosis killed thousands of people each year. Children were especially at risk. In some areas, almost half of all children died before the age of five. Reformers began to campaign for better housing and sanitation.
===
IMG: workhouse-gates-grey-building-1843 | a large grim grey brick workhouse building with high walls and iron gates in London in 1843, a poor family in ragged clothes standing at the gate, bare trees, cold grey light
HEAD: For the very poorest, the only help was the workhouse, a place many feared more than hunger.
BODY: In 1834, the British Parliament passed the New Poor Law. Historians note that it changed the way the poor were helped. Instead of giving money or food to the poor in their own homes, the law said that people in need should go into a workhouse. Workhouses were large institutions where the poor lived and worked in return for food and shelter. Even so, the workhouses were deliberately harsh. The idea was that only the truly desperate would choose to enter them. Families were separated, with men, women and children living in different parts of the building. The food was plain, the work was hard and the rules were strict. Many poor people feared the workhouse and would do almost anything to avoid it. Historians note that Dickens strongly criticised the workhouses. In Oliver Twist, he described a workhouse where the boys were starved. In A Christmas Carol, Scrooge asks whether there are no prisons and no workhouses for the poor.
===
IMG: mother-sewing-candle-poor-room-night | a tired young Victorian mother sewing shirts by the light of a single candle in a bare, cold attic room in London in 1843, a baby sleeping in a basket beside her, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a sloping ceiling, dim candlelight
HEAD: Many poor women earned a living by sewing, working late into the night for very low pay.
BODY: In Victorian London, thousands of poor women earned their living by sewing. Historians note that they made shirts, dresses, trousers and other clothes for shops and merchants, working at home or in small workshops. They were paid by the piece, which meant they earned money for each item they finished, rather than for the hours they worked. The pay was very low. Even so, sewing was one of the few kinds of work that women could do at home while caring for their children. Many seamstresses worked twelve to sixteen hours a day, often late into the night, by candlelight. Their eyes and health suffered. In December 1843, the magazine Punch published a poem by Thomas Hood called The Song of the Shirt. It described the suffering of a poor seamstress and became very popular. Historians note that it helped raise awareness of the conditions of working women, in the same month that A Christmas Carol was published.
===
IMG: soup-kitchen-queue-snow-volunteers | poor Victorian men, women and children in ragged coats queuing in the snow outside a soup kitchen in London in December 1843, volunteers ladling hot soup into bowls from large pots, steam rising, cold grey light
HEAD: In winter, charities opened soup kitchens to feed the hungry poor.
BODY: Winter was the hardest time of year for the poor of London. Historians note that cold weather meant higher costs for coal and warm clothing, while many types of work, such as building and dock labour, became scarce. Families struggled to buy food and fuel. Even so, many people tried to help. Churches, charities and wealthy individuals opened soup kitchens, where the hungry could receive free bowls of soup and bread. Some gave out coal, blankets and clothing. At Christmas, many charities made special efforts to provide dinners for the poor. Historians note that Victorian charity was often well intentioned, but it could not solve the deep problems of poverty. There were too many people in need, and charity was uneven. Dickens believed that kindness and generosity were important, but he also wanted society to change. In A Christmas Carol, two gentlemen visit Scrooge to ask for money for the poor at Christmas.
===
IMG: debtors-prison-courtyard-families | the high-walled courtyard of a Victorian debtors' prison in London with families in worn clothes sitting on benches, children playing, a gatekeeper at the door, grey daylight
HEAD: People who could not pay their debts could be sent to prison, sometimes with their whole families.
BODY: In Victorian England, people who owed money and could not pay it back could be sent to a debtors' prison. Historians note that London had several, including the Marshalsea, the Fleet and the King's Bench. Prisoners stayed there until they could pay their debts, which could take years. Some never left. Their families often lived with them in the prison, because they had nowhere else to go. Even so, life in a debtors' prison was not like life in an ordinary prison. Prisoners could have visitors, buy food and sometimes work. Those with money could rent better rooms. The poor lived in crowded, dirty conditions. Historians note that Dickens's own father had been imprisoned in the Marshalsea. Dickens later wrote about debtors' prisons in several novels, including Little Dorrit, in which the heroine is born and grows up inside the Marshalsea. Imprisonment for debt was finally abolished in England in 1869.
===
IMG: costermonger-family-small-room-supper | a poor Victorian family of street sellers sharing a simple supper of bread and potatoes around a small table in a cramped room with a tiny fire, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a low ceiling, warm dim light
HEAD: Even in the poorest homes, families tried to celebrate Christmas in some small way.
BODY: For poor families in London, Christmas could be a difficult time. Historians note that many could not afford a special dinner, presents or decorations. Some worked through Christmas Day because they could not afford to lose a day's wages. Even so, most families tried to mark the day in some small way. They might buy a little meat, a few oranges or some sweets for the children. They might decorate their room with a sprig of holly or a bit of greenery. Neighbours sometimes shared what they had. Historians note that some poor families saved for months to have a proper Christmas dinner. Others received help from charities or employers. Dickens understood the importance of these small celebrations. In A Christmas Carol, the Cratchit family is poor, but they make their Christmas dinner a joyful feast. Their goose is small, and their pudding is modest, but they are happy, because they are together.
===
IMG: ragged-boy-girl-shadows-street-corner | two thin ragged children, a boy and a girl, huddled together on a cold street corner in London at dusk in 1843, a gas lamp above them, people in coats passing by, cool blue light
HEAD: In 1843, many thousands of children in London lived on the streets, with no home at all.
BODY: In 1843, thousands of children in London had no proper home. Historians note that some were orphans, while others had run away from cruel or poor families. They slept in doorways, under bridges, in markets and in cheap lodging houses. They survived by begging, running errands, selling small goods or stealing. Even so, some of these children found help. Ragged schools, missions and charities offered them food, shelter and education. Later in the century, the doctor Thomas Barnardo founded homes that cared for thousands of homeless children. Historians note that the plight of street children shocked many Victorians and inspired reforms. Dickens wrote about such children in Oliver Twist, describing a gang of young pickpockets. In A Christmas Carol, the children named Ignorance and Want are a warning. The ghost tells Scrooge to beware them both, but most of all the boy, Ignorance. Dickens believed that education was the key to a better future.

TOPIC: Street sellers in winter
===
IMG: chestnut-seller-brazier-street-snow | a Victorian street seller roasting chestnuts over a glowing charcoal brazier on a snowy London street corner in December 1843, customers in coats and bonnets buying paper cones of nuts, warm firelight against blue evening
HEAD: In winter, street sellers roasted chestnuts over glowing braziers on the street corners.
BODY: The streets of Victorian London were full of people selling food, drink and goods. Historians note that these street sellers were a vital part of the city's life, especially for the poor, who bought most of their food from them. In winter, chestnut sellers set up small braziers, metal containers filled with burning charcoal, on street corners. They roasted chestnuts over the coals and sold them hot, in paper cones. Even so, it was a hard way to earn a living. The sellers stood outside for hours in the cold, wind and rain. They earned only a few shillings a week. Yet their braziers gave out a welcome warmth, and people gathered around them to warm their hands. The smell of roasting chestnuts filled the streets. Historians note that hot food was especially popular in winter. Sellers also offered baked potatoes, hot pies, pea soup, coffee and other warming foods.
===
IMG: baked-potato-man-can-oven-street | a Victorian baked potato seller with a shiny metal can oven on legs, glowing coals underneath, handing a hot potato to a working man on a London street corner in winter 1843, steam rising, warm light
HEAD: Hot baked potatoes, sold from shiny metal ovens, warmed the hands and stomachs of working people.
BODY: One of the most popular street foods in Victorian London was the baked potato. Historians note that potato sellers carried large metal cans, heated by a charcoal fire underneath, which kept the potatoes hot. The cans were often polished and decorated with brass, so that they shone in the gaslight. The sellers cried out to passers-by, offering potatoes with butter and salt for a halfpenny each. Even so, the potato sellers did more than sell food. On cold days, working people bought hot potatoes to warm their hands, holding them in their pockets before eating them. The journalist Henry Mayhew later estimated that there were about two hundred potato sellers in London in the 1850s. They sold their potatoes mainly from October to April. Historians note that potatoes were a cheap and filling food, important in the diet of the poor. A hot potato on a winter's evening was a small comfort in a hard life.
===
IMG: muffin-man-bell-tray-on-head-fog | a Victorian muffin man carrying a wooden tray of muffins covered with a cloth on his head and ringing a hand bell in a foggy London street at dusk in 1843, a woman calling from a doorway
HEAD: The muffin man walked the streets in the late afternoon, ringing his bell to announce fresh muffins for tea.
BODY: In Victorian London, the muffin man was a familiar figure. Historians note that he walked the streets in the late afternoon, carrying a wooden tray of muffins on his head, covered with a green baize cloth to keep them warm. He rang a hand bell to announce his arrival, and people came out of their houses to buy muffins and crumpets for tea. Even so, the muffin man's trade was not always welcome. In 1839, Parliament passed a law banning the ringing of bells in the streets, because of the noise. But the muffin men continued, and the law was rarely enforced. Victorian muffins were soft, flat, round breads, toasted by the fire and eaten with butter. Historians note that they were a popular treat at teatime, especially in winter. The muffin man and his bell became a symbol of cosy Victorian afternoons, as families gathered around the fire, toasting muffins and drinking tea.
===
IMG: holly-ivy-seller-basket-market-stall | a Victorian woman in a shawl selling bunches of holly, ivy and mistletoe from a large basket on a busy London street in December 1843, customers choosing greenery, gas lamps, cool evening light
HEAD: Before Christmas, sellers brought holly, ivy and mistletoe from the countryside to sell in the streets.
BODY: In the weeks before Christmas, the streets of London were filled with sellers of greenery. Historians note that they brought holly, ivy, mistletoe and laurel from the countryside around London and sold them from baskets, barrows and stalls. People bought them to decorate their homes, shops and churches. Holly, with its bright red berries and shiny green leaves, was especially popular. Even so, the custom of decorating with greenery was very old, older than Christianity. Ancient peoples had brought evergreen plants into their homes in winter as a sign of life and hope during the dark season. Christians later gave these plants new meanings. Holly came to represent the crown of thorns and the blood of Christ. Historians note that mistletoe had its own special custom. Couples who met beneath it were expected to kiss. In A Christmas Carol, the Ghost of Christmas Present sits on a throne made of holly, mistletoe and ivy.
===
IMG: carol-singers-waits-lantern-door-snow | a small group of Victorian carol singers in coats, scarves and bonnets holding a lantern and singing outside the door of a house on a snowy London street at night in 1843, warm light from the windows
HEAD: Groups of singers, called waits, went from house to house singing carols at Christmas.
BODY: Singing carols at Christmas was an old tradition in England. Historians note that in the Middle Ages, carols were songs sung and danced in a circle, not only at Christmas but at other festivals too. Over time, they became connected with Christmas. Groups of singers, called waits, went from house to house in the weeks before Christmas, singing carols and playing instruments. In return, they hoped to receive money, food or drink. Even so, carol singing had declined in the early nineteenth century. Many old carols had been forgotten. In the 1820s and 1830s, collectors such as Davies Gilbert and William Sandys began to gather and publish old carols. Historians note that this helped revive the tradition. In A Christmas Carol, a boy stops at Scrooge's keyhole to sing a carol, but Scrooge seizes a ruler and frightens him away. Later in the century, many of the carols still sung today became popular.
===
IMG: ballad-seller-song-sheets-crowd | a Victorian ballad seller holding up long printed song sheets and singing to a small crowd on a busy London street in 1843, people in coats and bonnets listening and buying, soft afternoon light
HEAD: Ballad sellers sang the latest songs in the street and sold printed copies for a penny.
BODY: Among the street sellers of Victorian London were the ballad sellers. Historians note that they sang songs in the streets and sold printed copies, called broadsides, for a penny or less. The songs told stories of love, crime, disasters, battles and daily life. Some were old folk songs, while others were new, written about recent events. At Christmas, ballad sellers sold carols and songs about the festive season. Even so, their trade was changing. Cheap newspapers and magazines were becoming more common, offering news and entertainment to working people. Yet the ballad sellers remained popular, especially among the poor. Historians note that many people who could not read learned songs by listening to the ballad sellers. Some broadsides were decorated with simple woodcut pictures. Collections of these printed songs survive in libraries today, giving historians a glimpse of the songs and stories that ordinary Victorians enjoyed in the streets of London.

TOPIC: A clerk's family in Camden Town
===
IMG: camden-town-terrace-houses-1843-winter | a row of small brick terraced houses in Camden Town in north London in 1843 with smoking chimneys, a muddy street, a few people in shawls and coats walking, bare trees, grey winter light
HEAD: Dickens imagined the poor clerk Bob Cratchit and his family living in Camden Town, where Dickens himself had lived as a boy.
BODY: In A Christmas Carol, Bob Cratchit, Scrooge's poorly paid clerk, lives with his family in Camden Town, in north London. Historians note that Dickens knew Camden Town well. He had lived there as a boy, in 1822 and 1823, when his family first came to London. They lived in a small house in Bayham Street, a poor and shabby area at the time. Even so, Camden Town was not a slum. It was a district of small, cheap houses, home to clerks, craftsmen and labourers. In 1843, it was changing quickly. The railway from London to Birmingham had been built through it, and new houses were going up. Historians note that the Cratchits' home was probably based on Dickens's memories of Bayham Street. The family lives in a small house with four rooms. They are poor, but clean, loving and cheerful. Dickens wanted his readers to see the dignity of such families.
===
IMG: clerk-high-stool-ledger-cold-office | a thin Victorian clerk in a worn coat and long white scarf sitting on a high stool writing in a large ledger by the light of a small candle in a cold, dim office in 1843, a tiny fire in the grate, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling
HEAD: Clerks worked long hours in cold offices, copying letters and keeping accounts by hand.
BODY: In 1843, there were no typewriters or computers. Historians note that every letter, account and document had to be written by hand. This work was done by clerks, who sat at high desks in offices, writing with pens and ink. Clerks copied letters, kept accounts, recorded business transactions and filed papers. They worked long hours, often from early morning until evening, six days a week. Even so, the pay was often low, especially for junior clerks. In A Christmas Carol, Bob Cratchit earns fifteen shillings a week, which was not enough to support a large family comfortably. His office is cold, because Scrooge will not let him have more than a tiny fire. Historians note that many clerks in London lived on the edge of poverty. They had to dress respectably for work, which was expensive, yet their wages were barely enough. Dickens had worked as a clerk himself as a young man, in a lawyer's office.
===
IMG: mother-daughters-cooking-small-kitchen | a Victorian mother and two daughters in simple dresses and aprons preparing vegetables and stirring a pot in a small, cosy kitchen with a coal range in 1843, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a low ceiling, warm firelight
HEAD: In a working family, the mother managed the home, the money and the meals with great skill.
BODY: In a Victorian working family, the mother was usually responsible for running the home. Historians note that she managed the household money, shopped for food, cooked, cleaned, washed clothes and cared for the children. In poor families, this required great skill. She had to make a small income stretch to feed and clothe everyone. Older daughters helped with the cooking, cleaning and care of younger children. Even so, many mothers also earned money, by sewing, washing or taking in lodgers. Their work was hard and never ending. In A Christmas Carol, Mrs Cratchit is described as brave in a twice-turned gown, which means a dress that had been taken apart and resewn inside out to make it last longer. She prepares the Christmas dinner with care and pride. Historians note that Dickens showed great respect for the working mothers of London, who held their families together in difficult circumstances.
===
IMG: small-boy-crutch-family-hearth | a small pale Victorian boy with a little wooden crutch sitting on a stool beside his father by the fire in a modest room in 1843, the father with his arm around him, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a low ceiling, warm firelight
HEAD: In Victorian London, many children suffered from illness and disability, and few could afford a doctor.
BODY: In A Christmas Carol, the youngest Cratchit son, Tiny Tim, is small, weak and walks with a crutch. Historians note that his illness is not named in the book. Some doctors have suggested that he may have suffered from rickets, a disease caused by a lack of vitamin D, or from tuberculosis. Both were common among poor children in Victorian London. Rickets was caused partly by poor diet and lack of sunlight, in a city darkened by smoke and fog. Even so, many poor families could not afford a doctor or proper medicine. Children with illnesses or disabilities often suffered without help. Many died young. Historians note that Dickens wanted his readers to feel for children like Tiny Tim. In the book, the Ghost of Christmas Present warns Scrooge that Tim will die if nothing changes. At the end, Scrooge helps the family, and Tim lives. His words, God bless us, every one, became famous.
===
IMG: children-playing-street-hoops-camden | Victorian children in caps, shawls and boots playing with hoops and a skipping rope on a muddy street of small brick houses in Camden Town in 1843, a mother watching from a doorway, soft winter light
HEAD: Children in Camden Town played in the streets, but many also helped their families by working.
BODY: Children in working families in Victorian London often had short childhoods. Historians note that many began working at an early age, running errands, selling goods in the streets, helping in shops or working in factories. Some went to school, but education was not yet compulsory. It would not become so until the 1870s and 1880s. Even so, children also found time to play. They played games in the streets, such as hopscotch, marbles, skipping and chasing. They rolled hoops with sticks and spun tops. Poorer children made their own toys from whatever they could find. Historians note that in A Christmas Carol, the Cratchit children are excited about Christmas. Martha, the eldest daughter, works as an apprentice to a milliner, a maker of hats. Peter, the eldest son, is about to start his first job. The younger children help their mother and dream of the Christmas goose. Their joy is simple and real.
===
IMG: cratchit-style-family-goose-table | a poor but happy Victorian family gathered around a small table with a modest roast goose, potatoes and a small round pudding, the father raising a glass, children smiling, a small fire in the grate, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a low ceiling, warm candlelight
HEAD: For a family like the Cratchits, a roast goose and a pudding were a great Christmas treat.
BODY: In A Christmas Carol, the Cratchit family's Christmas dinner is one of the most famous scenes. Historians note that it shows what Christmas meant to a poor family in 1843. The family has a small goose, with apple sauce and mashed potatoes, followed by a Christmas pudding. The goose is not large, but the family praises it as if it were a feast. Everyone helps, and everyone is happy. Even so, the scene also shows their poverty. The goose has been cooked at the baker's shop, because the family has no oven big enough. The pudding is small for so large a family. Yet no one says so. Historians note that this was a realistic picture. Many poor families in London took their Christmas dinner to the local baker to be cooked. The bakers charged a small fee. At the end of the meal, Bob Cratchit raises a toast to Mr Scrooge, the founder of the feast, despite his meanness.

TOPIC: Markets before Christmas
===
IMG: leadenhall-market-geese-turkeys-hanging | a crowded Victorian covered market in London in December 1843 with rows of geese and turkeys hanging from hooks above poulterers' stalls, shoppers in top hats and bonnets, gaslight glowing, warm light
HEAD: Before Christmas, the poulterers' shops and markets were filled with geese and turkeys.
BODY: In the days before Christmas, London's markets were busy with shoppers buying food for the holiday. Historians note that poulterers, sellers of poultry, hung geese, turkeys, chickens and ducks in rows outside their shops. Leadenhall Market, in the City of London, was especially famous for its poultry and game. Thousands of birds were brought to London from the countryside for Christmas. Even so, in 1843, the goose was more common than the turkey for Christmas dinner, especially among ordinary families. Turkeys were larger and more expensive. Historians note that geese and turkeys were often walked to London from farms in Norfolk and Suffolk, sometimes wearing little boots to protect their feet. The walk could take weeks. By the end of the nineteenth century, the turkey had become more popular. Historians note that Covent Garden remained a market until 1974, when it moved to a new site. Today, the old market building is a popular place for shopping and entertainment, still full of life.

BODY: Smithfield, just outside the old City walls, was London's main market for live animals for centuries. Historians note that every week, thousands of cattle, sheep and pigs were driven through the streets of London to Smithfield, where they were sold to butchers. The market was crowded, noisy and dirty. The animals were kept in pens, and the ground was covered with mud and waste. Even so, Smithfield was essential to feeding the city. In the days before Christmas, it was especially busy, as butchers bought beef, mutton and pork for Christmas dinners. Historians note that many people complained about the market. The animals caused chaos in the streets, and the conditions were cruel and unhealthy. Dickens described the market in Oliver Twist. In 1855, the live animal market was moved to a new site in Islington. A covered meat market was later built at Smithfield, and it still operates today.
