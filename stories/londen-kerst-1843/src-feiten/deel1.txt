TOPIC: Christmas in Victorian London
===
IMG: victorian-london-street-snow-gaslamps-1843 | a snowy Victorian street in London in December 1843 with glowing gas lamps, brightly lit shop windows full of geese and holly, people in top hats, bonnets and shawls hurrying past, cool blue evening light
HEAD: In December 1843, London was preparing for Christmas, and the shop windows glowed with gaslight.
BODY: In December 1843, the streets of London were busy with people preparing for Christmas. Shop windows were filled with geese, turkeys, puddings, oranges, sweets and toys, lit by the warm glow of gas lamps. Historians note that the city was the largest in the world, with about two million inhabitants. It was the capital of a growing empire and the centre of trade, industry and finance. Even so, Christmas in 1843 was not quite the same as Christmas today. Some customs, such as Christmas trees and Christmas cards, were only just appearing. Others, such as Christmas dinner, carol singing and decorating homes with holly, were old traditions. For the rich, Christmas was a time of feasts and parties. For the poor, it could be a time of hunger and cold. In that same month, a new book appeared in the shops, which would change the way the world celebrated Christmas. It was called A Christmas Carol.
===
IMG: family-carrying-goose-parcels-street | a Victorian family in worn coats and bonnets carrying a goose wrapped in paper and small parcels along a busy London street in December 1843, gas lamps and shop windows glowing, light snow, cool evening light
HEAD: For ordinary families, Christmas was a day of rest, a special dinner and time together.
BODY: For most working families in London in 1843, Christmas Day was one of the few holidays of the year. Historians note that many people worked six days a week, often for long hours, and had little free time. On Christmas Day, most shops, offices and workshops closed. Families went to church, shared a special dinner and spent time together. Even so, not everyone had the day off. Servants, cooks, cab drivers and some shop workers still had to work. For many families, the Christmas dinner was the best meal of the year. They saved money for weeks to buy a goose, a joint of beef or a pudding. Some joined goose clubs, paying a small amount each week to receive a goose at Christmas. Historians note that Christmas was becoming more important in Victorian times. It was increasingly seen as a family celebration, centred on the home, children, food and kindness to others.
===
IMG: book-a-christmas-carol-red-cloth-gilt | a small Victorian book bound in red cloth with gilt edges and decorations lying on a wooden table beside a candle and a sprig of holly, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm candlelight
HEAD: On the nineteenth of December 1843, Charles Dickens published A Christmas Carol.
BODY: On the nineteenth of December 1843, a small book with a red cloth cover and gold edges appeared in the bookshops of London. It was called A Christmas Carol, and it was written by Charles Dickens, the most popular author in Britain. Historians note that the book told the story of Ebenezer Scrooge, a mean old businessman who is visited by ghosts on Christmas Eve and learns to be kind and generous. It cost five shillings, which was quite expensive at the time. Even so, it sold very quickly. The first printing of six thousand copies was sold out by Christmas Eve. Readers loved its warmth, humour and message of kindness. The book was illustrated with four coloured pictures by the artist John Leech. Historians note that A Christmas Carol has never been out of print since. It helped to shape the idea of Christmas as a time of family, generosity and goodwill, which is still important today.
===
IMG: victorian-london-skyline-st-pauls-winter | the skyline of London in winter 1843 with the dome of Saint Paul's Cathedral, church spires and smoking chimneys rising above rooftops, the river Thames with sailing barges in front, hazy grey light
HEAD: London in 1843 was a city of great wealth and great poverty, side by side.
BODY: In 1843, London was a city of contrasts. Historians note that it was home to some of the richest people in the world, who lived in grand houses in the West End, with servants, carriages and fine furniture. It was also home to hundreds of thousands of poor people, who lived in crowded, dirty and unhealthy conditions. Often, the rich and poor lived only a few streets apart. Even so, the city was full of energy and change. New railways, bridges, docks and buildings were being built. Factories and workshops produced goods of every kind. Ships from around the world brought tea, sugar, cotton, spices and other goods to the London docks. Historians note that this mix of wealth and poverty, progress and suffering, deeply affected Charles Dickens. In A Christmas Carol, he showed both sides of London. He wanted his readers to remember the poor at Christmas, and to share their good fortune with those in need.
===
IMG: queen-victoria-albert-1843-palace | a young Queen Victoria in a silk gown with a lace collar and a young Prince Albert in a dark coat standing together in a richly furnished palace drawing room in 1843, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm light
HEAD: In 1843, Britain was ruled by the young Queen Victoria, who had married Prince Albert three years before.
BODY: In 1843, Queen Victoria was twenty-four years old. She had become queen in 1837, at the age of eighteen, and had married her cousin, Prince Albert of Saxe-Coburg and Gotha, in 1840. Historians note that the royal couple were very happy together, and they had three young children by the end of 1843. Prince Albert brought some German Christmas customs to the British court, including the Christmas tree. Even so, these customs were not yet widely known in Britain in 1843. They became popular a few years later. In 1848, a newspaper called the Illustrated London News printed a picture of the royal family gathered around a decorated Christmas tree at Windsor Castle. Families across Britain began to copy them. Historians note that the royal family's example, together with Dickens's book, helped create the image of the Victorian family Christmas. The queen and her husband spent Christmas 1843 at Windsor Castle with their children.
===
IMG: fog-street-lamplighter-ladder-dusk | a Victorian lamplighter on a ladder lighting a gas street lamp in a foggy London street at dusk in 1843, a few figures in top hats and shawls passing in the mist, soft blue and gold light
HEAD: As darkness fell each December afternoon, lamplighters walked the streets lighting the gas lamps.
BODY: In December, darkness fell early in London, at about four o'clock in the afternoon. Historians note that the streets were lit by gas lamps, which had been introduced in the early nineteenth century. Each evening, lamplighters walked through the streets with long poles or ladders, turning on the gas and lighting each lamp with a small flame. In the morning, they returned to put them out. Even so, gas lighting was not as bright as electric light today. The lamps gave a soft yellow glow, which created pools of light along the streets, with darkness in between. On foggy nights, the light was even dimmer. London's fogs were famous. They were caused by smoke from millions of coal fires mixing with damp air from the river. The fog could be thick, yellow and choking. In A Christmas Carol, Dickens described the fog creeping into Scrooge's office through every crack and keyhole.
===
IMG: victorian-family-fireside-christmas-evening | a Victorian family gathered around a cosy fireplace in a modest parlour in December 1843, a mother sewing, a father reading aloud, children on the rug, holly on the mantelpiece, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm firelight
HEAD: For many families, the best part of Christmas was gathering around the fire together.
BODY: In the cold, dark days of December, the fireplace was the heart of every home in Victorian London. Historians note that most houses were heated by coal fires, which burned in grates in each room. In poorer homes, there might be only one fire, in the main room, where the family cooked, ate, worked and gathered in the evenings. Even so, families made the most of their time together. They told stories, sang songs, played games and read aloud. Reading aloud was a popular pastime, especially in families where not everyone could read. Many families gathered around the fire to listen to the latest novel by Charles Dickens, read by a parent or an older child. Historians note that Dickens wrote A Christmas Carol to be read aloud in this way. He called it a ghost story of Christmas, and he hoped that families would enjoy it together by the fireside, as the winter nights drew in.

TOPIC: A city of two million
===
IMG: thames-docks-sailing-ships-warehouses-1843 | the busy London docks in 1843 with tall sailing ships, steamships with paddle wheels, brick warehouses and dockworkers unloading barrels and crates, the river Thames grey and crowded, soft overcast light
HEAD: In 1843, London was the largest city in the world and the busiest port on Earth.
BODY: By 1843, London had grown into the largest city in the world, with about two million inhabitants. Historians note that it had doubled in size in the previous forty years, as people moved from the countryside and from other countries to find work. It was the centre of the British Empire, which stretched across the globe. The river Thames was crowded with ships, and the London docks were the busiest in the world. Even so, the city's rapid growth brought problems. Housing could not keep up with the number of people. Streets were overcrowded, and many people lived in poor conditions. The river was polluted with sewage and waste. Diseases such as cholera, typhus and tuberculosis spread easily. Historians note that London was both a place of opportunity and a place of danger. People came hoping for a better life, but many found only hard work, low wages and crowded rooms.
===
IMG: railway-station-steam-train-1843-crowd | an early Victorian railway station in London in 1843 with a small green steam locomotive and wooden carriages under an iron and glass roof, passengers in top hats and bonnets with luggage, steam and soft light
HEAD: New railways connected London with other cities, and stations were full of travellers going home for Christmas.
BODY: In the 1830s and 1840s, Britain was gripped by railway fever. Historians note that new railway lines were being built across the country, connecting London with Birmingham, Manchester, Bristol, Brighton and other towns. The first railway station in London, London Bridge, opened in 1836, followed by Euston in 1837 and Paddington in 1838. Even so, rail travel was still new and exciting in 1843. Trains were pulled by small steam locomotives. Passengers travelled in first, second or third class carriages. Third class carriages were often open to the weather, with wooden benches and no roof. Yet trains were much faster than stagecoaches. A journey that had taken days could now be done in hours. At Christmas, railways made it easier for people to travel home to their families. Historians note that this helped make Christmas a family celebration, bringing relatives together from distant parts of the country.
===
IMG: omnibus-horses-street-passengers-roof | a horse-drawn omnibus with passengers seated inside and on the roof travelling along a busy London street in 1843, hansom cabs and carts around it, shop fronts and gas lamps, soft daylight
HEAD: Horse-drawn omnibuses and cabs carried passengers through the crowded streets.
BODY: Getting around London in 1843 was not easy. Historians note that most people walked, sometimes for miles each day. Those who could afford it travelled by horse-drawn omnibus, a large carriage that carried passengers along fixed routes. The first London omnibus had started in 1829. Passengers sat inside or on the roof, which was cheaper but cold and wet in winter. Hansom cabs, small two-wheeled carriages pulled by a single horse, carried one or two passengers. Even so, the streets were often blocked by traffic. Omnibuses, cabs, carts, wagons, carriages and horses competed for space. The noise of wheels and hooves on the cobblestones was constant. The streets were also dirty, covered with mud and horse manure. Crossing sweepers, often poor children, cleared paths across the streets for pedestrians, in return for a few coins. Historians note that the traffic of London was one of the wonders, and one of the trials, of the Victorian city.
===
IMG: thames-tunnel-opening-1843-crowds-arches | the brick-lined arched passages of a tunnel under a river in London in 1843 lit by gas lamps, crowds of Victorian visitors in top hats and bonnets walking through it, small stalls along the walls, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, an arched ceiling, warm gaslight
HEAD: In March 1843, the Thames Tunnel opened, the first tunnel ever built under a river in a city.
BODY: On the twenty-fifth of March 1843, the Thames Tunnel opened between Rotherhithe and Wapping, in east London. Historians note that it was the first tunnel ever built beneath a navigable river. It was designed by the engineer Marc Brunel and his son Isambard Kingdom Brunel, and it took eighteen years to build. The work was dangerous, and the tunnel flooded several times. Even so, it was finally completed. It had been intended for horse-drawn carriages, but the money ran out before ramps could be built. Instead, it was opened to people on foot, who paid a penny to walk through. In its first fifteen weeks, about a million people visited it. Stalls sold souvenirs inside, and fairs were held there. Historians note that it was one of the great sights of London in 1843. In the 1860s, it was sold to a railway company, and trains still run through it today.

IMG: nelsons-column-trafalgar-square-1843 | the tall stone column in Trafalgar Square in London in 1843 with a statue of Admiral Nelson on top, workers and scaffolding at the base, Victorian people and carriages in the square, grey winter light
HEAD: In November 1843, the statue of Admiral Nelson was raised to the top of his column in Trafalgar Square.
BODY: Trafalgar Square was laid out in the 1830s and 1840s, in memory of Admiral Horatio Nelson's great naval victory at the Battle of Trafalgar in 1805. Historians note that a tall column was built in the middle of the square, about fifty-two metres high, with a statue of Nelson on top. In November 1843, the statue was raised to the top of the column. Before it was lifted, fourteen workmen climbed up and had a dinner on top of the column, high above the square. Even so, the monument was not yet finished. The famous bronze lions at the base were added later, in 1867. The National Gallery, at the north side of the square, had opened in 1838. Historians note that Trafalgar Square was becoming a symbol of London and of British national pride. In December 1843, Londoners walking through the square could admire the newly raised statue, looking out over the city towards the sea.
===
IMG: penny-post-letters-post-office-counter | a busy Victorian post office counter in London in 1843 with clerks in dark coats sorting letters and selling stamps, customers in top hats and bonnets posting letters, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft light
HEAD: Since 1840, a letter could be sent anywhere in Britain for a single penny, using a stamp.
BODY: In 1840, Britain introduced a new postal system called the Penny Post. Historians note that it was invented by Rowland Hill, who argued that letters should be paid for by the sender, using a stamp, and should cost the same to send anywhere in the country. The first stamp, the Penny Black, showed the head of Queen Victoria. Before 1840, sending letters was expensive, and the receiver usually paid. Even so, the Penny Post was a great success. The number of letters sent in Britain doubled in the first year. People could now write to family and friends cheaply and easily. At Christmas, this made it possible to send greetings to distant relatives. Historians note that in 1843, a civil servant named Henry Cole, who had helped Rowland Hill, commissioned the first commercial Christmas card. It was designed by the artist John Callcott Horsley and showed a family raising their glasses in a toast.
===
IMG: first-christmas-card-family-toast-1843 | a Victorian Christmas card from 1843 lying on a wooden table, hand-coloured, showing a family raising glasses around a table, framed by trellis and small scenes of giving food and clothing to the poor, beside an envelope and a candle
HEAD: The first Christmas card, made in 1843, showed a family raising glasses in a Christmas toast.
BODY: In 1843, Henry Cole was a busy man with many friends. Historians note that he did not have time to write personal Christmas letters to all of them. So he asked his friend, the artist John Callcott Horsley, to design a card with a Christmas message. The card showed a family gathered around a table, raising glasses of wine in a toast. On either side were scenes of people giving food and clothing to the poor. The message read: A Merry Christmas and a Happy New Year to you. Even so, the card was not universally admired. Some people disapproved of it, because it showed children drinking wine. About one thousand copies were printed, and they were sold for a shilling each, which was expensive. Historians note that Christmas cards did not become popular until later, when printing became cheaper. Today, only a few of the original 1843 cards survive. They are valuable collectors' items.

TOPIC: Streets of fog and gaslight
===
IMG: thick-yellow-fog-london-street-figures | a London street in 1843 filled with thick yellow fog, the dim shapes of people in top hats and shawls, a horse-drawn cab and glowing gas lamps barely visible through the mist, soft eerie light
HEAD: London's famous fogs were caused by the smoke of millions of coal fires.
BODY: In the winter, London was often covered by thick fog. Historians note that it was caused by the smoke from coal fires in homes, factories and workshops, mixing with damp air from the river. The fog could be yellow, brown or even green, and it had a sharp, sooty smell. It could be so thick that people could not see across the street. Even so, Londoners carried on with their daily lives. They lit lamps during the day and walked carefully through the gloom. Link boys, boys carrying torches, guided people through the streets for a few coins. Historians note that the fogs were also harmful to health. They caused coughing, breathing problems and illness, especially among children and the elderly. In later decades, they became even worse. Dickens described the London fog in many of his books. In A Christmas Carol, the fog comes pouring in at every chink and keyhole of Scrooge's office.
===
IMG: gas-lamps-glowing-narrow-court-evening | a narrow courtyard between tall old brick houses in London in 1843 lit by a single gas lamp, wet cobblestones reflecting the light, a cat on a doorstep, quiet evening
HEAD: Gas lamps had transformed London at night, making the streets brighter and safer.
BODY: Before the nineteenth century, London streets were lit by oil lamps and candles, which gave only a dim light. Historians note that in 1807, the first gas street lamps were installed on Pall Mall, in the West End. Within a few decades, gas lighting had spread across most of the city. Gas was produced at gasworks by heating coal and was carried through pipes beneath the streets. Even so, gas lighting was not available everywhere. Poorer areas often had fewer lamps, and some narrow courts and alleys remained dark. Many homes still used candles and oil lamps. Historians note that gas lighting changed London life. It made the streets safer at night and allowed shops to stay open later. Shop windows glowed with light, attracting customers in the evening. At Christmas, the brightly lit shops were a magical sight, especially for children. Dickens described them in A Christmas Carol, glowing with warmth and colour.
===
IMG: police-constable-top-hat-street-1843 | a Victorian police constable in a tall top hat and long dark blue tailcoat walking his beat along a foggy London street in 1843, a lantern on his belt, gas lamps and shop fronts behind
HEAD: The Metropolitan Police, founded in 1829, patrolled the streets in tall hats and dark blue coats.
BODY: In 1829, the Home Secretary, Sir Robert Peel, founded the Metropolitan Police, the first modern police force in London. Historians note that before that, the city had been protected by watchmen and parish constables, who were often old, poorly paid and ineffective. The new police officers wore dark blue tailcoats and tall top hats, to look different from soldiers. They were nicknamed Bobbies or Peelers, after Robert Peel. Even so, the police were not popular with everyone at first. Some people feared that they would be used to spy on citizens or to suppress protests. Over time, they won more public trust. By 1843, police constables walked their beats day and night, watching for crime and helping people in need. Historians note that in 1842, a detective department had been created. Dickens was fascinated by the new detectives. He later wrote articles about them and created one of the first detectives in English fiction, Inspector Bucket.
===
IMG: crossing-sweeper-boy-broom-muddy-street | a ragged boy with a broom sweeping a path across a muddy London street in 1843 for a gentleman in a top hat and a lady in a long dress, carts and horses passing, grey winter light
HEAD: Poor children earned a few coins by sweeping paths across the muddy streets.
BODY: The streets of Victorian London were muddy, dirty and covered with horse manure. Historians note that crossing sweepers, often poor children, swept paths across the streets so that people could cross without dirtying their shoes and clothes. In return, they hoped to receive a small coin. Some crossing sweepers worked the same corner for years and became familiar figures. Even so, it was a hard and uncertain way to earn a living. The sweepers worked outside in all weathers, often barefoot or in worn shoes. They depended on the kindness of passers-by. Many earned only a few pence a day. Historians note that the journalist Henry Mayhew later interviewed many crossing sweepers for his famous study of the London poor. He found boys and girls, old men and women, some disabled, all struggling to survive. Dickens wrote about a crossing sweeper named Jo in his novel Bleak House, a boy who had nothing in the world.
===
IMG: warm-shop-window-toys-children-looking | poor Victorian children with their noses pressed against a brightly lit shop window full of toys, dolls, wooden horses and sweets in London in December 1843, gaslight glowing, cold blue evening
HEAD: At Christmas, children pressed their noses against shop windows full of toys and sweets.
BODY: In the weeks before Christmas, the shops of London were filled with goods for the holiday. Historians note that toy shops displayed dolls, wooden animals, toy soldiers, spinning tops, rocking horses and picture books. Sweet shops sold sugar mice, gingerbread, toffee and candied fruit. Grocers' shops were filled with raisins, currants, oranges, lemons, nuts and spices for Christmas puddings and mince pies. Even so, many children could only look. Poor children gathered outside the brightly lit windows, admiring the treasures they could never afford. For them, the shop windows were a free show, a glimpse of a world of plenty. Historians note that many Victorian writers described these scenes. Dickens remembered his own childhood, when his family was poor, and he felt deeply for children who went without. In A Christmas Carol, he described the poulterers' and grocers' shops in loving detail, as a feast for the eyes.
===
IMG: church-bells-snowy-steeple-night | a Victorian church with a tall steeple in London on a snowy night in December 1843, warm light glowing in its windows, people in coats and bonnets walking up the steps, gas lamps along the street
HEAD: Across London, church bells rang to mark the hours and call people to worship.
BODY: In 1843, London had hundreds of churches, each with its own bells. Historians note that their sound filled the city, marking the hours of the day and calling people to services. On Christmas Eve and Christmas morning, the bells rang out joyfully. Many people attended church on Christmas Day, before going home for their Christmas dinner. Even so, not everyone went to church. In the poorer parts of the city, many people had no time or money for church, or did not feel welcome there. Some churches had pews that had to be rented by families, leaving little space for the poor. Historians note that this worried many Victorian reformers. New churches were built in poor areas, and missions and ragged schools were set up to help the poor. In A Christmas Carol, the bells play a special role. They ring out as the ghosts come and go, and on Christmas morning, Scrooge hears them pealing across the city.
