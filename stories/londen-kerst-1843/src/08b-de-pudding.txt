In many English homes, the preparations for Christmas begin on a Sunday at the end of November, five weeks before Christmas.

It is called Stir-up Sunday, because the prayer that is read in church on that day begins with the words: Stir up, we beseech thee, O Lord, the wills of thy faithful people. And the families take it as a reminder that it is time to stir up the Christmas pudding.

--

The Christmas pudding takes a long time to make, and must be made well in advance, so that it can mature.

The ingredients are laid out on the kitchen table: suet, which is the hard fat from around the kidneys of a cow, chopped very fine; breadcrumbs and flour; dark sugar and treacle; eggs; raisins and currants and sultanas; candied peel, of orange and lemon; chopped almonds; grated nutmeg and cinnamon and cloves and allspice; a little brandy, or beer, or both.

Everything is mixed together in a great earthenware bowl, into a thick, dark, sticky mixture, which smells wonderful.

--

Then, every member of the family takes a turn to stir it.

Each person must stir it three times, from east to west, in honour of the three wise men, who came from the East, and while they stir, they close their eyes, and make a wish. The father stirs, and the mother, and the grandmother, and all the children, even the baby, with someone holding its little hand around the wooden spoon.

{img:small-child-stirring-pudding-mother-helping-wish}
In some families, little charms are stirred into the mixture: a silver threepenny bit, for wealth; a ring, for a wedding; a thimble, for a happy single life; a button, for a bachelor. Whoever finds them in their slice of pudding on Christmas Day will have that fortune in the coming year.

--

Then the mixture is wrapped in a floured pudding cloth, tied tightly at the top with string, so that it makes a round ball, and boiled in a great copper pot of water, for many hours, six or eight or ten.

The whole house fills with the warm, rich, spicy smell of the pudding. Then it is taken out, and hung up in the cool larder, still in its cloth, until Christmas Day, when it will be boiled again, for a few more hours, and turned out onto a plate, dark and shining and round, and carried to the table, with brandy poured over it, and set alight, so that it blazes with a blue flame.

Mrs. Cratchit, in A Christmas Carol, boils her pudding in the copper, the great boiler that is used for washing the clothes. That is why, Dickens says, there is a smell like a washing day, on Christmas Day at the Cratchits'.

--

There are mince pies to be made, too.

The mincemeat is made weeks in advance, from chopped apples, and raisins and currants, and suet, and candied peel, and spices, and sugar, and brandy, and in some families, a little minced meat as well, as in the old days. It is packed into jars, and left to mature.

{img:mince-pies-baking-tin-tray-oven-warm-kitchen}
Then, in the days before Christmas, the pastry is made, and rolled out, and cut into little rounds, and the mince pies are filled and baked, by the dozen, and stored in tins, to be eaten all through the Christmas season. There is an old saying that you should eat a mince pie on each of the twelve days of Christmas, in a different house each time, for twelve happy months in the coming year.

--

And there is the Christmas cake, rich and dark and full of fruit, and the gingerbread, and the sweets, and the punch, a great bowl of hot wine or rum, with sugar and lemons and spices, which is served to visitors.

In the poorer homes, the Christmas food is much simpler. But even there, the mothers try to make a little pudding, however small, for the children, and a few mince pies, and to have an orange for each child on Christmas morning.

--

By Christmas Eve, the larder is full.

The pudding hangs in its cloth from a hook in the ceiling. The mince pies are in their tins. The goose or the turkey is waiting in the coldest corner, or at the poulterer's, to be collected. The vegetables and the fruit are in their baskets. The holly is over the pictures, and the mistletoe is hanging in the hall.

{img:larder-pudding-hanging-cloth-goose-holly-evening}
And the children lie in their beds, on Christmas Eve, smelling the warm, spicy smell of the kitchen drifting up the stairs, and listening to the bells ringing across the city, and trying very hard to go to sleep.
