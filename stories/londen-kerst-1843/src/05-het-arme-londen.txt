Not far from the grand streets of London, there are the poor districts.

They are called rookeries, because the people are crowded together in them like rooks in a tree. There is one at Saint Giles, near Covent Garden. There are others in Southwark, across the river, and in the East End, near the docks, and at Saffron Hill and Field Lane, where the ragged school is.

--

In the rookeries, the old houses are packed together along narrow, winding alleys and courts, so close that the sunlight hardly reaches the ground.

Many of the houses are very old, and falling down. Whole families live in a single room, sometimes two or three families together, sharing one fireplace, and one bed, or a pile of straw on the floor. Water comes from a single pump in the court, shared by dozens of families. In winter, the rooms are cold and damp, and the wind blows through the broken windows, which are stuffed with rags and paper.

It is a hard life. But even here, people help each other, and share what little they have, and laugh, and sing, and look after their children as best they can.

--

The poor of London work at whatever they can find.

The men are labourers and porters, dockworkers and carters, bricklayers and street sweepers. The women take in washing, or sew shirts for a few pennies each, working late into the night by the light of a single candle. Many people sell things in the streets: fruit and vegetables, flowers and matches, shoelaces and ribbons, hot food and drink.

{img:flower-seller-girl-covent-garden-basket-winter}
Children work too, from a very young age. They sweep crossings and run errands, hold horses for gentlemen, sell matches and newspapers, and help their parents. Some of them have no parents at all, and live in the streets, and sleep under the arches of the bridges, or in doorways.

--

For those who cannot support themselves at all, there is the workhouse.

The workhouse is a large, plain, forbidding building, where the poorest people can go to get food and shelter, in return for hard work, like breaking stones or picking apart old ropes. But the rules are strict, and families are separated: husbands from wives, parents from children. People are very afraid of the workhouse, and will do almost anything to avoid going there.

Dickens hates the workhouses. He wrote about one in Oliver Twist, where the hungry boy Oliver dares to ask for more food: Please, sir, I want some more.

--

In the winter, life in the poor districts is hardest of all.

Coal is expensive, and many families cannot afford enough to keep their rooms warm. Food is expensive too. There is often not enough to eat. Many people fall ill, in the cold and the damp, with coughs and fevers.

{img:charity-soup-kitchen-queue-winter-1843-snow}
But there is help, too. There are churches and charities that give out soup and bread and blankets and coal to the poor in winter. There are kind people, rich and poor, who give what they can. And at Christmas, especially, there are many acts of kindness.

--

At Christmas, even the poorest families try to have something special.

Many of them belong to goose clubs. They pay a few pennies every week into a club at a local pub or shop, all through the year, and at Christmas, they get a goose, or a joint of meat, and a bottle of gin or a few oranges.

{img:family-carrying-goose-home-christmas-eve-street}
Most poor families do not have an oven at home. So, on Christmas morning, they take their goose, or their joint of meat, and their potatoes, in a dish, to the local baker's shop, where the baker cooks them in his big oven for a penny or two, while the families go to church. Then, after church, they collect their dinners, steaming hot, and carry them home through the streets.

--

Dickens knows all of this, from his own childhood.

He remembers the years in Camden Town, when his family was poor, and the months in the blacking factory, and the visits to his father in the Marshalsea prison. He remembers walking through the streets of London as a hungry boy, looking in at the windows of the cookshops, at the puddings and the pies, with no money to buy them.

And in A Christmas Carol, he will write about a poor family, the family of a clerk named Bob Cratchit, who lives in a small house in Camden Town, just like the house that Dickens lived in as a boy, and about their Christmas dinner, and about their little son, Tiny Tim.

--

On winter evenings, in the poor districts, the windows glow with the light of little fires and candles.

{img:poor-street-windows-glowing-candles-fog-evening}
Inside, families are sitting together, around the fire, as close as they can get, telling stories, singing songs, mending clothes, and talking about Christmas: about the goose, if they have one, and the pudding, and the oranges, and the little presents for the children. And for a while, in the warmth of the fire, they forget the cold, and the fog, and the hard times, and are happy.
