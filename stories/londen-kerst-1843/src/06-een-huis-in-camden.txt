Let us visit a family that we might call the Browns, who live in a small house in Camden Town, in the north of London.

Mr. Brown is a clerk. He works in an office in the City, for a merchant, copying letters and keeping accounts, at a tall desk, with a quill pen, from nine in the morning until seven at night, six days a week. He earns about fifteen shillings a week, which is not very much, but it is a steady wage, and he is grateful for it.

--

The Browns live in a narrow, two-storey brick house, in a long row of houses that all look exactly the same.

On the ground floor, there is a front parlour, which is kept for best, and only used on Sundays and special occasions, and a back room, which is the kitchen, where the family spends most of its time. Upstairs, there are two small bedrooms. In the yard at the back, there is a privy, and a coal shed, and a little patch of garden, where Mrs. Brown grows a few herbs and some flowers.

The kitchen is the warmest room in the house, because of the fire. There is an iron range, set into the fireplace, with a coal fire burning in the middle, a little oven on one side, and a boiler for hot water on the other. A kettle sings on the hob. A clock ticks on the mantelpiece.

--

Mrs. Brown runs the household.

She gets up before dawn, to clean out the ashes and light the fire, and boil the water, and make the breakfast: bread and butter, and tea, and perhaps a little porridge for the children. Then she sends Mr. Brown off to work, with a bundle of bread and cheese for his dinner, and sends the older children off to school.

{img:mother-blacking-range-kitchen-morning-candle}
Then she begins her day's work: cleaning the house, scrubbing the floors, blacking the range, washing the clothes, mending, sewing, shopping, cooking. It never stops.

--

They have five children.

The eldest, a girl of fourteen, has just started work as an apprentice to a dressmaker, and comes home at night tired, with sore fingers. The eldest boy, who is twelve, is a messenger boy in an office in the City, and runs through the streets all day with letters and parcels. The three youngest, who are eight and six and three, are at home with their mother, or at the little school at the end of the street, run by an old lady, where they learn their letters and their sums.

{img:children-slates-dame-school-small-room}
The Browns are not poor, like the families in the rookeries. But they must be careful with every penny. They eat bread and potatoes and porridge, with a little meat on Sundays. The children wear clothes that have been handed down, and mended many times. In the winter, they can only afford to keep one fire going, in the kitchen, and the bedrooms are very cold.

--

In the evenings, the family gathers in the kitchen, around the fire.

Mr. Brown comes home in the dark, cold and tired, and takes off his wet boots, and sits by the fire in his chair, and the little ones climb onto his lap. They have supper together: bread and dripping, perhaps a herring, a cup of tea. Then the mother sews, and the father reads aloud from the newspaper, or from a book borrowed from a friend, and the children play on the rag rug in front of the fire.

{img:father-reading-aloud-kitchen-fire-children-rug}
Sometimes, they sing together, the old songs and hymns. Sometimes, the father tells them stories about when he was a boy. And then the children are sent up to bed, with a candle, and a hot brick wrapped in flannel to warm their beds.

--

In December, the children are excited about Christmas.

They have been saving their pennies for weeks, to buy little presents for each other and for their parents: a ribbon, a handkerchief, a twist of sweets, a little tin toy. Mrs. Brown has been putting a few pennies a week into the goose club at the shop on the corner. And this year, Mr. Brown has been promised a little extra money at Christmas, from his employer, who is a kind man.

In the evenings, the children sit by the fire, and talk about what they will have for Christmas dinner, and whether it will snow.

--

On Saturday evenings, the whole family goes out together, to do the shopping for the week.

The shops stay open late on Saturdays, until ten or eleven at night, and the streets are crowded with families, doing their shopping, by the light of the gas lamps and the flaring naphtha lamps of the street stalls. There is music, and laughter, and the cries of the sellers. It is a treat for the children, to be out so late, in the bright, busy streets.

{img:saturday-night-market-naphtha-flares-families-shopping}
And in December, the shop windows are full of Christmas things, and the children press their noses against the glass, and look at the toys and the sweets and the puddings and the geese, and dream.
