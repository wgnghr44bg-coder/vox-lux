In the weeks before Christmas, the great markets of London are busier than at any other time of the year.

The most famous of them all is Covent Garden, in the middle of the city, where the fruit and the vegetables and the flowers are sold. Long before dawn, the carts and wagons come rumbling in from the market gardens around London, from Chelsea and Fulham and Battersea, and from the countryside beyond, piled high with cabbages and carrots, turnips and potatoes, onions and celery, apples and pears.

--

The market is in a great open square, with a handsome new market building in the middle, with colonnades and glass roofs, built about fifteen years ago.

By five o'clock in the morning, the square is crowded and noisy and full of life. Porters carry great baskets on their heads, piled six or seven high, balancing them perfectly as they walk through the crowds. Costermongers, the street sellers of London, come with their donkey carts and their barrows to buy fruit and vegetables, which they will sell later in the day, in the streets, all over the city.

In December, there are piles of holly and ivy and mistletoe, brought in from the woods and the hedges of the countryside, in great green bundles, with bright red berries. There are oranges and lemons, from Spain and Portugal, in wooden crates, and figs and dates and raisins, from the Mediterranean, and nuts of every kind: chestnuts and walnuts and hazelnuts and almonds.

--

The flower sellers sit along the edges of the market, mostly women and girls, with baskets of flowers, even in winter: violets and snowdrops and hothouse roses, and little bunches of holly and mistletoe, which they will sell in the streets, for a penny a bunch.

{img:flower-girls-violets-holly-bunches-market-edge}
There are coffee stalls, too, where the porters and the market people can buy a mug of hot coffee and a slice of bread and butter for a penny, in the cold early morning, and stand around the stall, warming their hands, and talking.

--

In the east of the City, there is Leadenhall Market, where the poultry and the game are sold.

In December, Leadenhall is a wonderful sight. The shops and stalls are hung with hundreds and hundreds of birds: geese and turkeys, ducks and chickens, pheasants and partridges, hares and rabbits, hanging in long rows from hooks, from the ceiling to the floor. Some of the geese and turkeys have been walked all the way to London from Norfolk and Suffolk, in great flocks, over many weeks, with their feet dipped in tar to protect them on the roads.

The poulterers stand in front of their shops, in their white aprons and straw hats, calling out to the passers-by, and weighing the birds, and wrapping them up.

--

Not far away, there is Smithfield, the great meat market, where cattle and sheep and pigs are brought in from all over the country, and sold.

In the week before Christmas, there is a great Christmas show at Smithfield, where the farmers bring their finest, fattest animals, and the butchers buy them, and decorate them with ribbons and rosettes, and hang the meat in their shop windows for everyone to admire.

{img:butcher-shop-window-ribbons-rosettes-christmas-meat}
On the last Saturday before Christmas, the butchers' shops all over London are decorated with holly and evergreens and paper flowers, and lit with gas, and the butchers stand at the doors, calling out: Buy, buy, buy!

--

Then there is Billingsgate, by the river, where the fish is sold.

Billingsgate is famous for the rough language of its fish sellers, which is so colourful that people say a person who swears a lot talks Billingsgate. In December, there are oysters, in great heaps, which are cheap and popular, and eaten by everyone, rich and poor, and salmon and cod and herring, and eels, which are stewed or jellied or baked in pies.

{img:billingsgate-fish-market-oysters-river-morning}
In the streets near the market, there are oyster stalls, where people stand and eat a dozen oysters with bread and butter and a glass of porter, for a few pennies.

--

In the poorer parts of London, there are street markets, which are busiest on Saturday nights.

The stalls stretch along the pavements, lit by flaring naphtha lamps, which burn with a bright, smoky, flickering flame. They sell everything: meat and fish, vegetables and fruit, bread and cheese, crockery and saucepans, old clothes and boots, toys and sweets.

The crowds push and jostle along the pavements, bargaining and laughing. Children run between the stalls. Organ grinders play. Sellers shout: Here's your fine Christmas oranges, a penny apiece! Here's your holly, a penny a bunch!

--

All this food, flowing into London from all over the country and all over the world, is one of the wonders of the city.

Every day, the markets feed nearly two million people. And at Christmas, they feed them better than at any other time of the year.

{img:carts-leaving-market-dawn-fog-loaded-holly}
When Dickens describes Christmas in A Christmas Carol, he describes the shops and the markets of London, piled high with good things to eat: the great round, pot-bellied baskets of chestnuts, the ruddy, brown-faced, broad-girthed Spanish onions, the pears and apples clustered high in blooming pyramids, the bunches of grapes, the piles of filberts, the Norfolk biffins, squat and swarthy, the gold and silver fish, the raisins and almonds and cinnamon, and the candied fruits, so caked and spotted with molten sugar as to make the coldest lookers-on feel faint.
