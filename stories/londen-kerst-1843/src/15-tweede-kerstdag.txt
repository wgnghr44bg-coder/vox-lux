The day after Christmas is Boxing Day.

In the morning, the tradesmen and the delivery boys and the postmen and the lamplighters and the crossing sweepers come to the doors of the houses where they have worked all year, and knock, and wish the family a merry Christmas, and hope for a Christmas box: a little gift of money, a shilling or a half crown, to thank them for their service. The servants of the house are given their Christmas boxes too, and often a day off, to visit their own families.

--

For many Londoners, Boxing Day is the day for the theatre.

On Boxing Day night, all the great theatres of London open their Christmas pantomimes. The pantomime is a great, noisy, colourful entertainment, for children and grownups alike, with music and dancing, and jokes, and magic, and spectacular scenery, which changes in front of the audience's eyes.

It begins with a fairy tale, Cinderella, or Aladdin, or Jack and the Beanstalk, or Puss in Boots. And then, at the end, there is a magical transformation scene, where the good fairy waves her wand, and the scenery changes into an enchanted palace, glittering with lights, and the characters of the story are transformed into the characters of the harlequinade: Harlequin, the magical hero, in his diamond-patterned costume; Columbine, his sweetheart; Pantaloon, the foolish old man; and the Clown, with his white face and his red cheeks, who plays tricks on everyone, and steals sausages, and falls over, and makes the children scream with laughter.

--

Dickens loves the pantomime.

As a boy, he loved the famous clown Joseph Grimaldi, the greatest clown of them all, and later, as a young writer, he edited a book of Grimaldi's memoirs. Every Christmas, he takes his children to the pantomime, at Drury Lane, or Covent Garden, and laughs as loudly as they do.

{img:family-theatre-box-children-laughing-pantomime}
The theatres are huge, and lit by gas, with tier upon tier of boxes and galleries, rising up to the ceiling, all full of families. The poorer people sit in the gallery, high up near the roof, where the seats are cheap, and shout and cheer and throw orange peel. The richer families sit in the boxes. Everyone has a wonderful time.

--

In the days between Christmas and New Year, London is quieter than usual.

Many of the offices are closed, or half empty. The shops are open, but not so busy. The families visit each other, and go for walks in the parks, and skate on the frozen ponds, if it is cold enough.

In Regent's Park, and in Hyde Park and Saint James's Park, when the lakes freeze over, thousands of people come to skate, and slide, and watch, and the ice is crowded with skaters, gliding and wobbling and falling over, and laughing, while the sellers of hot chestnuts and gingerbread set up their stalls along the banks.

--

On New Year's Eve, there are parties again, and at midnight, the church bells ring out the old year and ring in the new.

People open their front doors, to let the old year out and the new year in. In some houses, there is an old custom called first footing: the first person to cross the threshold after midnight should be a tall, dark man, carrying a piece of coal, a piece of bread, and a little salt, for good luck in the coming year.

{img:new-years-eve-bells-church-tower-crowd-lanterns}
In London, crowds gather outside Saint Paul's Cathedral to hear the great bells ring in the new year, and cheer, and shake hands, and wish each other a happy new year.

--

And then there is Twelfth Night, on the fifth of January, the end of the Christmas season.

In the days before Twelfth Night, the windows of the confectioners' shops are filled with great Twelfth cakes, iced in white sugar, and decorated with sugar figures of kings and queens, castles and knights, and stars and crowns, and crowds of people stand in front of the windows to admire them.

{img:crowd-admiring-twelfth-cakes-shop-window-gaslight}
In the houses, there are Twelfth Night parties. A great cake is cut, with a bean and a pea hidden inside it, and whoever finds the bean is the king for the night, and whoever finds the pea is the queen. There are games and dancing and characters, drawn from a hat, so that everyone pretends to be someone else for the evening.

--

At Devonshire Terrace, Twelfth Night is a very special day.

It is the birthday of Dickens's eldest son, Charley. So every year, Dickens gives a great Twelfth Night party for Charley and his friends, with a huge Twelfth cake, and games, and dancing, and a magic show, with Dickens himself as the conjuror, in a long robe and a tall hat, calling himself The Unparalleled Necromancer, Rhia Rhama Rhoos.

The children's parties at Devonshire Terrace are famous among the families of Dickens's friends. Everyone wants to be invited.

--

And then, after Twelfth Night, the holly and the ivy are taken down, and burned, and the Christmas season is over.

The children go back to school. The fathers go back to their offices and their workshops. The shops take down their Christmas decorations. The pantomimes go on for a few more weeks, and then close. And London settles down into the long, grey, foggy months of January and February, waiting for the spring.

{img:january-london-street-fog-ordinary-day-clerks}
But that winter, many people kept a little red book by their fireside, and read it again, now and then, in the long evenings, and remembered.
