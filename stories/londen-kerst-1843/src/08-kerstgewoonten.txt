In December 1843, in London, something new appeared in the shops: the first Christmas card.

It was the idea of a busy, energetic civil servant named Henry Cole, who worked hard to improve all kinds of things, from the post office to museums and schools. Every Christmas, he had a great many friends and acquaintances to write to, and not enough time to write to them all. So he had an idea.

--

He asked an artist friend, John Callcott Horsley, to design a card, which could be printed, and sent to everyone, with a simple message.

The card that Horsley designed showed a happy family, around a table, raising their glasses of wine in a toast, with the words A Merry Christmas and A Happy New Year to You. On either side, there were little pictures of people doing good deeds: giving food and clothes to the poor.

About a thousand copies were printed, and coloured by hand, and sold for a shilling each, which was rather expensive. Some people disapproved of the picture, because even the little children in the family were drinking wine. But the idea was a good one. Over the next few years, more and more people began to send Christmas cards, and soon, it became one of the great customs of Christmas.

--

Sending cards had become easier, because of another new idea.

Just three years earlier, in 1840, the Penny Post had been introduced. Before that, letters were expensive to send, and the person who received the letter had to pay. Now, anyone could send a letter anywhere in Britain for just one penny, paid in advance, with a little sticky stamp, with the head of young Queen Victoria on it, called the Penny Black.

{img:postman-red-coat-delivering-letters-snowy-door}
The postmen, in their red coats, now delivered letters all over London several times a day. And at Christmas, they were busier than ever.

--

In 1843, Christmas in England was beginning to change.

For a long time, it had been a rather quiet feast, in many places. The old Christmas customs of the countryside, the twelve days of feasting, the Yule log, the mummers and the wassailers, had faded away in many towns, as people moved into the cities to work in the factories, and had only one day's holiday, or none at all.

But now, Christmas was coming back. And one of the people who helped to bring it back was the young Queen Victoria, and her German husband, Prince Albert.

--

In Germany, families had decorated Christmas trees for many years: little fir trees, brought into the house, and decorated with candles and sweets and little gifts.

Prince Albert had grown up with Christmas trees, in his childhood home in Coburg, and he loved them. So, every Christmas, at Windsor Castle, he set up Christmas trees for the royal family, decorated with candles and sweets and gingerbread and little presents, for the Queen and their young children.

{img:royal-children-candlelit-tree-windsor-drawing-room}
In a few years, in 1848, a picture of the royal family gathered around their Christmas tree at Windsor would be printed in a newspaper, and seen by people all over the country. And soon, families everywhere would want a Christmas tree of their own.

--

In 1843, the Christmas tree was still quite rare in English homes.

Instead, people decorated their houses with greenery: holly and ivy and evergreen branches, along the mantelpieces and over the pictures and the doors. And everyone hung up a bunch of mistletoe, or a kissing bough, a round ball of evergreens and ribbons and apples and candles, hanging from the ceiling, under which people were allowed to kiss.

{img:kissing-bough-mistletoe-hanging-parlour-candles}
There were Christmas carols, too. In the weeks before Christmas, groups of singers, called waits, went from house to house in the evenings, carrying lanterns, singing the old carols at the doors, and were given money, or a glass of something warm, or a mince pie.

--

There were Christmas games, played in the parlour, by the fire, after dinner.

There was blind man's buff, where one person was blindfolded and tried to catch the others. There was hunt the slipper, and forfeits, and charades, and a game called snapdragon, where raisins were put in a bowl of brandy, and the brandy was set alight, and everyone tried to snatch the raisins out of the blue flames, and pop them in their mouths, without burning their fingers.

And there were stories, especially ghost stories, told by the fire on Christmas Eve, in the darkness, with only the light of the flames. It was an old custom, to tell ghost stories at Christmas. And that is why Dickens called his little book a Ghost Story of Christmas.

--

After Christmas, there was Boxing Day, on the twenty-sixth of December, when people gave Christmas boxes, little gifts of money, to the tradesmen and the servants and the postmen who had served them during the year.

And on Twelfth Night, on the fifth of January, there was a Twelfth Night party, with a great Twelfth cake, iced and decorated with sugar figures, and games, and dancing, and characters drawn from a hat, so that everyone pretended to be someone else for the evening.

{img:twelfth-cake-sugar-figures-confectioner-window}
In the windows of the confectioners' shops, in the first days of January, there were great Twelfth cakes on display, decorated with sugar castles and crowns and figures, and the children of London pressed their noses against the glass to look at them.
