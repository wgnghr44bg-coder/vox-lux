In September 1843, Charles Dickens visited a school in one of the poorest parts of London.

It was called the Field Lane Ragged School, near Saffron Hill, not far from Holborn. It was a school for the poorest children in the city, children who were too poor, and too ragged, to go to any other school: children who lived in the streets, and slept in doorways and under arches, and earned a few pennies by sweeping crossings or selling matches, or did not earn anything at all.

--

The school was held in the evenings, in a couple of dark, crowded rooms, lit by candles.

A few kind, patient volunteers, mostly young men and women from churches, came to teach the children to read and write, and to tell them about the Bible, and to give them a little warmth and kindness. The children came in from the streets, cold and hungry, in rags, many of them without shoes.

Dickens was deeply moved by what he saw there. He wrote to a friend that he had seldom seen, in all the strange and dreadful things he had seen in London, anything so sad as the faces of those children. And he gave money to help the school.

--

At about the same time, he read a report by a government commission on the employment of children in mines and factories.

It described children of five and six years old working in coal mines, deep underground, in the dark, for twelve hours a day, and children working in factories and workshops for long hours, for very little pay. Dickens was angry. He decided that he would write something to make people care about these children.

{img:writer-reading-government-report-study-lamp}
At first, he planned to write a pamphlet, a little political essay, called An Appeal to the People of England on behalf of the Poor Man's Child. But then he had a better idea.

--

At the beginning of October, he travelled to Manchester, in the north of England, to make a speech.

Manchester was the great factory city of England, the city of cotton mills, with tall chimneys pouring black smoke into the sky, and thousands of people, men and women and children, working long hours at the looms and the spinning machines. Dickens spoke at a great hall, called the Athenaeum, which had been built to give working people a place to read and learn in the evenings.

He spoke about the importance of education for the poor, and about the children he had seen in London, and about how ignorance and want were the enemies of everyone, rich and poor alike.

--

And it seems that it was there, in Manchester, perhaps walking through the dark streets after the speech, that the idea came to him.

He would not write a pamphlet. He would write a story. A Christmas story. A story about a hard, cold, mean old man, who cared only about money, and who learned, through one long Christmas night, to care about other people. A story that would make people laugh and cry, and that would remind them, at Christmas, of the poor and the lonely, and of the joy of kindness.

{img:walking-alone-dark-manchester-street-thinking}
It would be short, so that it could be finished in time for Christmas, and cheap enough for people to buy as a gift. He would call it A Christmas Carol, in Prose, Being a Ghost Story of Christmas.

--

He came home to London, and began to write.

He wrote very fast, in about six weeks, from the middle of October to the end of November, while he was also writing the monthly parts of Martin Chuzzlewit. He wrote in his study at Devonshire Terrace, at a desk by the window, with a quill pen, in his small, quick, slanting handwriting, crossing out and rewriting as he went.

{img:study-desk-manuscript-crossings-out-window-autumn}
He was completely caught up in the story. He wrote later that he wept over it, and laughed, and wept again, and that he walked about the black streets of London, fifteen and twenty miles, many a night, when all sober people had gone to bed, thinking about it.

--

On those night walks, he passed through every part of the city.

He walked through the quiet squares of the rich, where the gas lamps glowed on the white houses. He walked through the City, where the counting houses and the banks stood dark and empty, and the old churches loomed over the narrow lanes. He walked along the river, past the dark wharves, and over the bridges. He walked through the poor districts, where the lights burned late in the little rooms, and people huddled around tiny fires.

He looked in at the windows, and saw families eating their suppers, and children playing, and old people sitting alone. He saw clerks working late in cold offices, by a single candle. He saw it all, and it all went into the story.

--

At the beginning of December, the story was finished.

Dickens wrote to a friend that he had never been so moved by anything he had written. And he wanted the book to be beautiful: bound in red cloth, with gold lettering on the cover, gold edges to the pages, and coloured pictures, drawn by his friend John Leech, a young artist who drew for the comic magazine Punch.

{img:red-cloth-book-gold-lettering-illustrations-table}
It would be expensive to make. But Dickens did not care. He wanted it to be a Christmas present that people would treasure.
