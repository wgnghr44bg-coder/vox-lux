Every morning, as the church clocks strike eight, an enormous crowd of men walks into the City of London.

They come across London Bridge from the south, in a great black stream of coats and hats, thousands and thousands of them. They come down from Islington and Camden Town in the north, and along the Strand from the west. They walk, mostly, because the omnibus costs too much. They are the clerks.

--

The City of London is the old heart of London, the square mile around Saint Paul's and the Bank of England, where the merchants and the bankers and the insurance companies and the shipping companies have their offices.

In 1843, almost all the work of the City is done by hand, with a quill pen and ink, by clerks. They copy letters, and keep accounts, and write out bills and receipts and contracts, all day long, in neat, careful handwriting, in great leather-bound books.

There are tens of thousands of clerks in the City. Some of them are young men, starting out, hoping one day to become merchants themselves. Some of them are old men, who have worked at the same desk for forty years. Most of them earn very little.

--

A clerk's office, or counting house, is usually a dim, narrow room, on the ground floor or the first floor of an old building, in a narrow lane.

The clerks sit on high stools, at tall, sloping desks, along the walls, near the windows, to catch the light. There is a small fire in the grate, and the master of the office sits in a separate room, with a bigger fire. In winter, the offices are cold and dark, and the clerks work by the light of candles, from early afternoon, wearing their coats and their scarves, and blowing on their fingers to keep them warm.

{img:clerk-copying-letter-quill-candle-cold-office}
The work is quiet and repetitive and dull. The only sounds are the scratching of the pens, and the ticking of the clock, and the shuffling of papers, and now and then, a cough.

--

At midday, the clerks go out for their dinner.

The richer ones go to the chop houses, where they can sit at a wooden table in a cosy, wood-panelled room, and eat a mutton chop or a beefsteak, cooked over the fire, with bread and potatoes and a pint of porter. The poorer ones buy a pie or a baked potato from a street seller, or bring bread and cheese from home, wrapped in a handkerchief, and eat it at their desks, or standing in a doorway.

{img:city-chop-house-wood-panelled-booths-clerks-lunch}
In the old taverns and coffee houses of the City, the merchants and the brokers meet, to buy and sell and make deals, and read the newspapers, and talk about the price of tea and cotton and the arrival of the ships.

--

In the afternoon, the work goes on, until six or seven o'clock in the evening, or even later, if there is a lot to do.

Then the clerks put on their hats and their coats, and go out into the dark, foggy streets, and walk home, in another great stream, back across London Bridge, and up toward Islington and Camden Town, through the gaslit streets, to their families and their suppers.

Bob Cratchit, in A Christmas Carol, is one of these clerks. He works for Scrooge in a cold office, for fifteen shillings a week, and walks home every evening to Camden Town, through the streets, to his wife and his children. Dickens knew many clerks like him. He had been one himself, as a young man, in a lawyer's office.

--

At Christmas, a kind employer would give his clerks a little present: a goose, or a turkey, or a little extra money, and the day off on Christmas Day.

But not every employer was kind. In 1843, Christmas Day was a holiday for most people, but Boxing Day was not yet a holiday by law, and many clerks had to go back to work on the twenty-sixth of December.

{img:kind-merchant-giving-goose-to-clerk-office-door}
In some offices, on Christmas Eve, the master would bring in a bowl of punch, and some mince pies, and the clerks would drink a glass together, and wish each other a merry Christmas, before going home, early, for once, through the foggy streets, to their families, and their Christmas.
