On the nineteenth of December, 1843, A Christmas Carol was published, by the firm of Chapman and Hall, in the Strand.

It was a small book, of about a hundred and sixty pages, bound in red cloth, with gold lettering on the cover and the spine, and gold edges to the pages. Inside, there were eight pictures by John Leech: four of them small black and white engravings, and four of them full-page etchings, coloured by hand, in bright, cheerful colours.

--

It cost five shillings.

That was quite a lot of money for a little book, about as much as a labourer earned in two days. Dickens had wanted it to be cheaper, so that more people could buy it. But the red cloth and the gold edges and the coloured pictures were expensive, and he would not do without them.

And so the book went on sale, in the bookshops of London, a week before Christmas, and Dickens waited anxiously, to see what would happen.

--

What happened was wonderful.

By Christmas Eve, five days later, the first printing of six thousand copies had been sold out. People bought it as a Christmas present, for their families and their friends. People read it aloud, by the fire, on Christmas Eve, to their families, and laughed and cried. The bookshops ordered more, and more, and the printers worked day and night to print them.

{img:family-reading-red-book-aloud-fireside-christmas-eve}
Within a few months, it had been reprinted many times, and was being read all over Britain, and soon, all over America too.

--

The critics loved it.

The writer William Makepeace Thackeray, who was not always kind about Dickens, wrote that it seemed to him a national benefit, and to every man or woman who reads it, a personal kindness. A Scottish writer wrote that it had made people kinder, and that people who read it went out and gave money to the poor, and were good to their servants and their workers.

There was even a story, told many years later, of an American factory owner who read A Christmas Carol on Christmas Eve, and was so moved by it that he closed his factory on Christmas Day, and gave every worker a turkey.

--

Dickens himself was delighted.

He wrote to a friend that the book was a most prodigious success, the greatest, he thought, that he had ever achieved. He received letters from strangers, all over the country, telling him how much the book had meant to them, and how it had made them think about the poor, and about kindness, and about Christmas.

{img:writer-reading-letters-study-delighted-fireside}
But there was a disappointment, too. Because the book had been so expensive to make, with its red cloth and gold edges and coloured pictures, Dickens made very little money from it, at first. He had hoped for a thousand pounds. He got about two hundred and thirty. He was furious with his publishers, and soon left them.

--

And there was another trouble. A few weeks after the book came out, a cheap magazine published a pirated copy of the story, without permission, rewritten a little, and sold it for twopence.

Dickens went to court to stop them. He won the case. But the pirates went bankrupt, and he never got the money that they owed him, and had to pay the costs of the court case himself. It cost him more than he had earned from the book.

Even so, A Christmas Carol was never out of print again. It has been printed in hundreds of editions, in dozens of languages, and read by hundreds of millions of people, all over the world. It has been turned into plays, and films, and radio programmes, and musicals, and cartoons, again and again.

--

So what is the story that Charles Dickens told, in the winter of 1843?

{img:open-old-book-candlelight-armchair-fireside-night}
Let us settle down by the fire, as the families of London did on that Christmas Eve, and hear it once again, gently, in a few short chapters. Dickens called his chapters staves, like the verses of a song, because the book was a carol.
