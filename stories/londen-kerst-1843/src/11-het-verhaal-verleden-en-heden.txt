When Scrooge wakes, it is dark, and the church bells are chiming the hour. One o'clock.

The curtains of his bed are drawn aside, and there stands the first of the three spirits: the Ghost of Christmas Past. It is a strange figure, like a child, and also like an old man, with long white hair, and a bright, clear jet of light shining from the top of its head, and a sprig of fresh green holly in its hand.

--

The spirit takes Scrooge by the hand, and they pass through the wall, and suddenly, they are standing on an open country road, on a cold, clear winter's day, with fields on either side, and snow on the ground.

Scrooge knows this place. It is the place where he grew up, as a boy. He sees the little market town, with its bridge and its church and its winding river, and boys on ponies, riding home for the Christmas holidays, shouting merry Christmas to each other. And Scrooge, the hard old man, finds that he has tears in his eyes.

They come to an old school, and there, in a bare, dreary classroom, a lonely boy is sitting alone, reading, by a feeble fire, left behind at school by his family over Christmas. It is Scrooge himself, as a child. And the old man sits down beside the boy he used to be, and weeps.

--

Then the spirit shows him another Christmas, when he was a young man, an apprentice, in the warehouse of a kind, jolly old merchant named Mr. Fezziwig.

On Christmas Eve, when the work is done, old Fezziwig claps his hands, and calls out: No more work tonight! Christmas Eve! Clear away! And the apprentices clear away the desks and the stools, and sweep the floor, and light the lamps, and heap coal on the fire, until the warehouse is as snug and warm and dry and bright a ballroom as you would wish to see upon a winter's night.

{img:fezziwig-warehouse-ball-fiddler-dancing-lamps}
In comes a fiddler, and tunes up his fiddle. In comes Mrs. Fezziwig, one vast substantial smile. In come the three Miss Fezziwigs, beaming and lovable, and all the young men and women who work in the business, and the housemaid, and the baker, and the cook, and the milkman. And they dance, and dance, and eat cake and roast and mince pies, and drink negus, until eleven o'clock, when the ball breaks up, and old Fezziwig and his wife stand at the door, shaking hands with everyone, and wishing them a merry Christmas.

--

Scrooge watches it all, and remembers how happy he was that night, and how much a little kindness from his old master meant to him and the others.

And then the spirit shows him a sadder scene: a young woman, whom he loved, and was going to marry, telling him, gently, that she must let him go, because he has come to love money more than he loves her.

Scrooge cannot bear any more. And suddenly, he is back in his own bed, in the dark, and falls into a deep sleep.

--

When he wakes again, the bell is striking one once more. And there is a strange, bright light coming from the next room.

He goes in, and finds that his dark, gloomy sitting room has been transformed. The walls and the ceiling are hung with living green, holly and mistletoe and ivy, glistening with berries. A mighty blaze is roaring up the chimney. And heaped upon the floor, to form a kind of throne, are turkeys, geese, game, poultry, great joints of meat, sucking pigs, long wreaths of sausages, mince pies, plum puddings, barrels of oysters, red-hot chestnuts, cherry-cheeked apples, juicy oranges, luscious pears, immense Twelfth cakes, and seething bowls of punch.

{img:jolly-giant-spirit-green-robe-feast-room-holly}
And on the throne sits a jolly giant, in a long green robe trimmed with white fur, with a holly wreath on his head, and a glowing torch in his hand. It is the Ghost of Christmas Present.

--

The spirit takes Scrooge out into the streets of London, on Christmas morning.

It is snowing, and the people are shovelling the snow from the pavements and the roofs, and throwing snowballs at each other, and laughing. The shops are open, and full of good things. The people are hurrying to church, in their best clothes, and carrying their dinners to the bakers' shops, to be cooked. And wherever the spirit goes, he sprinkles a little incense from his torch on the people's dinners, and on any quarrels, and makes them kind and happy again.

{img:snowy-london-street-christmas-morning-people-snowballs}
Then the spirit takes him to a small house in Camden Town. It is the home of Bob Cratchit, his clerk.

--

The Cratchits are poor. Bob earns fifteen shillings a week from Scrooge. Mrs. Cratchit is dressed out but poorly, in a twice-turned gown, but brave in ribbons, which are cheap. The children are busy getting the dinner ready, and Bob comes home from church, carrying his youngest son, Tiny Tim, on his shoulder.

Tiny Tim is small and thin and lame, and walks with a little crutch, and has his legs supported by an iron frame. But he is the gentlest, most cheerful little boy in the world, and everyone loves him.

--

Then comes the goose, which Mrs. Cratchit has roasted, and the apple sauce, and the mashed potatoes, and the whole family gathers round the little table, and Bob says grace.

The goose is not very big, but there is enough for everyone, with the apple sauce and the potatoes, and everyone says that there never was such a goose. And then Mrs. Cratchit goes out to fetch the pudding, nervously, in case it has broken in the copper. But it has not. In she comes, smiling proudly, with the pudding, like a speckled cannonball, so hard and firm, blazing in half of half a quartern of ignited brandy, and bedight with Christmas holly stuck into the top.

{img:mother-carrying-flaming-christmas-pudding-holly}
Oh, a wonderful pudding! Everybody says so. And afterwards, the family gathers round the fire, with apples and oranges and roasted chestnuts, and Bob raises his glass, and says: A merry Christmas to us all, my dears. God bless us!

And Tiny Tim, the last of all, says: God bless us every one!
