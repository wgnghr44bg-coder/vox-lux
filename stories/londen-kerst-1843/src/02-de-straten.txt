The streets of London in 1843 are full of life from before dawn until long after dark.

In the early morning, while it is still dark, the carts begin to rumble in from the countryside, loaded with vegetables and milk and hay and meat, toward the great markets. The milkmaids walk through the streets with their yokes and their pails, calling out at the doors. The chimney sweeps, little boys with brushes over their shoulders, go from house to house, to clean the chimneys before the fires are lit.

--

As the light grows, the streets fill up.

There are carts and wagons, carriages and cabs, horses and donkeys, and crowds of people on foot, all pushing and jostling along the narrow pavements. Clerks in black coats and tall hats hurry to their offices in the City. Shop boys take down the shutters from the windows. Maids in white caps scrub the front steps of the houses. Street sellers set up their stalls on the corners.

There are omnibuses, too: big, boxy carriages pulled by two or three horses, with passengers crammed inside and perched on the roof, and a conductor standing on a little step at the back, shouting out the stops. A ride across London costs sixpence. And there are the cabs, light two-wheeled carriages, with the driver sitting high up behind, which can be hailed in the street.

--

The noise is tremendous.

Iron-shod wheels and horses' hooves clatter on the cobblestones. Drivers shout and crack their whips. Street sellers cry their wares: hot chestnuts, baked potatoes, oranges, muffins, watercress, matches, old clothes. Organ grinders play on the corners. Church bells ring the hours, one after another, from a hundred towers.

{img:street-sellers-hot-chestnuts-baked-potatoes-cart}
Visitors from the country are amazed and bewildered by it all. Londoners hardly notice.

--

At every crossing, there is a crossing sweeper.

They are poor boys and girls, and sometimes old men and women, with a broom, who sweep a clean path across the muddy street, so that ladies and gentlemen can cross without getting mud on their shoes and their long skirts. In return, they hope for a penny, or a halfpenny.

The streets are very muddy, especially in winter. They are covered with a thick layer of black mud, made of earth and soot and horse manure and rain, which splashes up onto everyone who passes.

--

{img:gas-lamplighter-ladder-foggy-street-dusk}
In the late afternoon, in December, as the light fades early, the lamplighters come out.

They walk through the streets with their long poles and their little ladders, from lamp post to lamp post, turning on the gas and lighting the flames, one after another. Gas lighting is still quite new and wonderful. London has had gas lamps in its streets for about thirty years, and they are much brighter than the old oil lamps. In the main streets, the shops are lit by gas too, and their windows glow with warm, golden light.

--

On a winter evening, when the fog comes down, the streets of London become strange and mysterious.

The fog is thick and yellow-brown, made of the smoke of a million coal fires, mixed with the damp air from the river. It smells of soot and sulphur. It is so thick, sometimes, that people cannot see the other side of the street, or even their own hands in front of their faces. The gas lamps glow faintly through it, like little smudges of light. Carriages creep along at a walking pace, with boys walking in front of them carrying torches. People lose their way, a few streets from home.

{img:link-boy-torch-guiding-carriage-thick-fog}
Charles Dickens loves the London fog. He will describe it, many times, in his books.

--

The great streets of London are lined with fine shops.

Regent Street, near Dickens's house, is the most elegant street in London, with a long, curving row of white stucco buildings, and shops selling silks and lace, hats and gloves, jewellery and perfume, to the ladies of fashion, who arrive in their carriages. The Strand and Oxford Street and Cheapside are crowded with shops of every kind.

But just behind the grand streets, there are narrow alleys and courts, where the poor live, crowded together in old, damp, falling-down houses. London is a city of the very rich and the very poor, living side by side, a few streets apart.

--

At night, the streets grow quieter, but they never become completely silent.

There are always cabs rattling home, and late travellers, and policemen walking their beats, in their blue coats and tall hats, with their lanterns. The policemen are still quite new, too. They were introduced only fourteen years ago, by Sir Robert Peel, and people call them bobbies, or peelers, after him.

{img:victorian-policeman-lantern-quiet-street-night}
And there is a young man, walking through the streets, mile after mile, late into the night, thinking: Charles Dickens, who often cannot sleep, and who walks the streets of London in the dark, ten or fifteen or twenty miles, to think about his stories.
