On every street in London, there are people selling things.

They are called costermongers, and street sellers, and hawkers, and there are tens of thousands of them. A few years later, a journalist named Henry Mayhew walked all over London, talking to them, and writing down their stories, in a great book called London Labour and the London Poor. And because of him, we know a great deal about their lives.

--

The costermongers sell fruit and vegetables and fish from barrows and donkey carts.

They get up at four in the morning, and go to Covent Garden or Billingsgate to buy their goods, and then push their barrows through the streets all day, calling out their wares, in a special sing-song cry that everyone in the neighbourhood knows. They are proud and independent people, who look after each other, and have their own customs, and their own slang, and love their donkeys, and dress up in bright neckerchiefs and velvet waistcoats on Sundays.

In winter, there are the sellers of hot food, which is very welcome in the cold. There is the hot chestnut man, with his little iron brazier full of glowing coals, and the chestnuts roasting on top, which he sells in twists of paper for a penny. There is the baked potato man, with his shiny tin oven on a barrow, with a little chimney, and steam coming out, who sells hot potatoes with butter and salt. There are sellers of hot pies, and muffins and crumpets, and hot pea soup, and hot eels.

--

There are girls who sell watercress, getting up before dawn to buy it at the market, and then walking the streets with their baskets, crying: Fresh watercress! Four bunches a penny!

Mayhew talked to one of them, a little girl of eight years old, who had been selling watercress since she was a very small child. She told him, quite seriously, about her work, and the prices, and the cold in the winter mornings, which hurt her fingers. She had never played in a park, or had a doll. But she said she liked going to the market in the morning, and she was proud of being able to help her mother.

{img:little-watercress-girl-basket-dawn-cold-street}
There are sellers of matches and bootlaces and needles and pins, and old women selling oranges and apples from little trays, and men selling newspapers and song sheets, ballads printed on long strips of paper, for a halfpenny, which they sing in the street to attract customers.

--

There are street performers, too.

There are organ grinders, with little barrel organs on wheels, and sometimes a monkey in a red coat, collecting pennies in a little cap. There are German bands, small groups of musicians with brass instruments, who play on the street corners. There are Punch and Judy shows, in little striped booths, where the puppets squeak and fight and make the children shriek with laughter.

{img:punch-and-judy-booth-children-crowd-street}
There are acrobats and jugglers, and men with dancing dogs, and fire eaters, and stilt walkers. There are singers and fiddlers, who play outside the pubs in the evening. There are people who draw pictures on the pavement in coloured chalk, and wait for passers-by to throw them a coin.

--

At Christmas, the street sellers do their best business of the year.

They sell holly and mistletoe, and little Christmas trees, and oranges and nuts and chestnuts, and toys: little tin toys, and wooden toys, and paper toys, cheap and colourful, which they make themselves, or buy from the toy makers in the East End. There are sellers of Christmas cards, and almanacs for the new year, and song sheets with Christmas carols.

The streets ring with their cries, in the cold, dark December evenings, under the gas lamps: Holly, holly, a penny a bunch! Mistletoe, fine mistletoe! Christmas oranges, sweet and juicy! Toys for the little ones, toys for Christmas!

--

And at night, when the shops have closed, the street sellers push their barrows home, through the dark streets, to their homes in the poor districts, tired and cold, with a few shillings in their pockets.

{img:costermonger-pushing-barrow-home-night-gaslight}
They stop at the pub on the corner for a pint of beer and a warm by the fire, and talk with their friends about the day's business. And then they go home to their families, and their little rooms, and their beds, to sleep a few hours, before getting up again in the dark, to go back to the market, and begin again.
