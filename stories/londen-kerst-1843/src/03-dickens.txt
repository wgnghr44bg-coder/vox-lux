Charles Dickens lives in a large, handsome house at Number One, Devonshire Terrace, near Regent's Park, in the north of London.

It is a big, comfortable family house, with a garden behind it, and a high wall, and tall windows looking out onto the street. Dickens has filled it with good furniture, and books, and pictures, and mirrors, which he loves, and he keeps it bright and clean and tidy, because he hates untidiness.

--

He is thirty-one years old in 1843.

He is slim and lively and handsome, with long, wavy brown hair, bright, quick, sparkling eyes, and a restless energy that never seems to stop. He dresses rather flamboyantly, in bright waistcoats and colourful neckties, which some people think a little vulgar. He talks fast, and laughs a great deal, and he is wonderful company.

He loves parties and games and theatre and conjuring tricks. He loves long walks, very fast, for many miles. He loves his friends, and his children, and his work.

--

He is married to Catherine, a gentle, plump, kindly woman, the daughter of a Scottish journalist. They have been married for seven years, and they have four children: Charley, who is six; Mamie, who is five; Katey, who is four; and Walter, who is two. And Catherine is expecting another baby, in the new year.

{img:victorian-family-parlour-four-children-fire}
The house is full of children's voices, and toys, and nursemaids, and pets: a talking raven, like the raven called Grip that he once had and put into one of his novels, and a dog.

--

Dickens has worked very hard to get where he is.

He was born in 1812, in Portsmouth, the son of a clerk in the navy pay office, a cheerful, generous, hopeless man named John Dickens, who was always in debt. When Charles was ten, the family moved to London, to a small, poor house in Camden Town.

When he was twelve, his father was arrested for debt, and sent to the Marshalsea prison, in Southwark, and his mother and the younger children went to live there with him, as families sometimes did in those days. And young Charles was sent out to work, in a factory beside the river, pasting labels onto pots of boot blacking, for six shillings a week, ten hours a day.

--

He never forgot those months.

He felt that his childhood had been taken from him, and that he had been abandoned, and he was deeply ashamed, all his life, of what had happened. He told almost nobody about it, not even his wife. But it made him understand, as few writers of his time did, what it was like to be a poor child in London: hungry, lonely, frightened, working long hours in a dark, dirty place, with no one to care for you.

{img:boy-working-blacking-factory-window-thames-light}
After a few months, his father inherited a little money, and was released from prison, and Charles was sent back to school. Then, at fifteen, he went to work as a clerk in a lawyer's office, and taught himself shorthand, and became a reporter, writing down the speeches in Parliament, and then a journalist, and then, at twenty-four, a writer.

--

His first great success was The Pickwick Papers, published in monthly parts in 1836 and 1837.

It was a funny, warm-hearted book about a kindly old gentleman, Mr. Pickwick, and his friends, and his servant, Sam Weller, travelling around England, having adventures. At first, it did not sell very well. But then, when Sam Weller appeared, it became a sensation. Everyone in Britain was reading it, from the Queen to the butcher's boy.

{img:people-reading-monthly-parts-bookshop-crowd-1830s}
Then came Oliver Twist, and Nicholas Nickleby, and The Old Curiosity Shop, and Barnaby Rudge. Each one was more popular than the last. When the little girl in The Old Curiosity Shop, Little Nell, died, people all over Britain and America wept.

--

But in 1843, things are not going so well.

His newest novel, Martin Chuzzlewit, which is coming out in monthly parts, is not selling as well as his earlier books. His publishers are worried, and have told him that they might have to reduce his payments. He has a big house, and a large family, and many expenses, and his father and his brothers are always asking him for money.

Dickens is worried too. He needs money. And he wants to write something that will touch people's hearts again.

--

In the evenings, after dinner, the family gathers in the drawing room.

Dickens plays with the children, and tells them stories, and makes them laugh, with funny voices and faces. He loves to do magic tricks for them. He reads aloud to Catherine and her sister, Georgina, who lives with them. Friends come to dinner, and stay late, talking and laughing.

{img:victorian-drawing-room-father-magic-trick-children}
And then, when the house is quiet, and everyone has gone to bed, he often goes out alone, into the dark streets, and walks, and thinks.
