Tonight we are going nearly two hundred years back, to the great grey city of London, in the weeks before Christmas, in the year 1843.

--

It is a cold December afternoon, and the light is already fading.

A thick, yellowish fog is rolling up from the river Thames, and creeping through the streets, so that the gas lamps, just being lit, glow like little blurred moons in the gloom. Above the rooftops, the great dome of Saint Paul's Cathedral looms out of the mist, grey and enormous, and then disappears again.

If you enjoy quiet journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel. Now, let us begin.

--

London, in 1843, is the largest city in the world.

Nearly two million people live here, more than in any city that has ever existed. Every year, more of them arrive: from the countryside of England, where there is not enough work on the farms; from Ireland, where the people are poor and hungry; from Scotland and Wales, and from every country in Europe.

The city stretches for miles along both banks of the Thames, an endless maze of streets and squares, alleys and courtyards, churches and chapels, markets and warehouses, factories and docks, grand mansions and crowded little houses, all built of brick and stone, and all blackened by the smoke of a million coal fires.

--

The river is the heart of the city.

It is crowded with ships, so many that, in the Pool of London, below London Bridge, their masts stand together like a leafless forest. There are tall sailing ships from India and China and America, with cargoes of tea and silk and cotton and spices. There are little steamboats, puffing up and down the river, carrying passengers from one landing stage to another for a penny or two. There are barges, with rust-red sails, loaded with coal and bricks and hay.

{img:pool-of-london-ships-masts-steamboats-winter}
On the banks, there are wharves and warehouses, cranes and docks, where thousands of men work, loading and unloading the ships, all day long, in all weathers.

--

This year, there are two new wonders in London, which everyone has been to see.

In March, the Thames Tunnel opened: the first tunnel ever built under a river, a long, gaslit brick passage beneath the water, from Wapping to Rotherhithe, designed by the great engineer Marc Brunel, and his son, Isambard. On the first day, fifty thousand people walked through it, marvelling at the thought of the ships sailing over their heads.

{img:thames-tunnel-gaslit-brick-arches-visitors}
And in Trafalgar Square, just a few weeks ago, in November, the workmen finished putting up a tall column, with a statue of Admiral Nelson on the top, high above the square, looking out over the city toward the sea.

--

But in December, Londoners are thinking about other things.

They are thinking about Christmas.

In the shops along the great streets, the windows are being filled with good things: geese and turkeys, puddings and pies, oranges and figs, toys and books and gifts. In the markets, the stalls are piled high with holly and mistletoe. In the kitchens of the houses, the women are stirring the Christmas puddings, and the children are being sent out to fetch the ingredients.

And in a large, comfortable house near Regent's Park, a young writer of thirty-one is working, late at night, by the light of a candle, on a little book.

--

His name is Charles Dickens.

He is already famous. His stories, The Pickwick Papers, Oliver Twist, The Old Curiosity Shop, have been read and loved by millions of people, all over Britain and America. But this little book will become, in the end, the most famous of all his works, and one of the most famous stories ever written.

{img:young-writer-desk-candle-quill-night-1843}
He calls it A Christmas Carol.

In the next few hours, we are going to spend the weeks before Christmas, in the year 1843, in the London that he knew and loved, and walked through, night after night, and wrote about. We will meet the people of the city, rich and poor, and see how they lived, and how they celebrated Christmas. And we will hear, once more, the story that he told.
