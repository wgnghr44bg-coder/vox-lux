The Ghost of Christmas Present takes Scrooge on a long journey that Christmas Day, far beyond London.

They visit a lonely moor, where a family of miners is singing a Christmas song together in a little hut. They visit a lighthouse, far out on a rock in the stormy sea, where two lighthouse keepers are wishing each other a merry Christmas, with their hands joined across a rough table. They visit a ship, far out on the dark ocean, where every sailor, on watch or below deck, is humming a Christmas tune, or thinking of home.

--

Then they visit the house of Scrooge's nephew, Fred, where there is a merry Christmas party going on.

There is music, and laughter, and games. They play blind man's buff, and forfeits, and a game called Yes and No, where Fred thinks of something, and everyone else must guess what it is, by asking questions, to which he can only answer yes or no. The answer turns out to be: Uncle Scrooge.

Scrooge, invisible, enjoys the games so much that he joins in with the guessing, and begs the spirit to let him stay a little longer. And Fred raises a glass to his uncle, and wishes him a merry Christmas and a happy new year, wherever he is.

--

As the day goes on, the spirit grows older. His hair turns grey.

And then, from under his green robe, he shows Scrooge two thin, ragged children, a boy and a girl, clinging to him. Their names, he says, are Ignorance and Want. They are the children of all humankind, and Scrooge must beware of them both, and especially of the boy.

{img:old-spirit-green-robe-two-ragged-children-gentle}
It is the message that Dickens wanted to give, from the beginning: that a society that leaves its children poor, and hungry, and uneducated, will suffer for it. And Scrooge, remembering his own hard words to the kind gentlemen the day before, is ashamed.

--

Then the clock strikes twelve, and the second spirit is gone, and in its place, there is a third.

It is the Ghost of Christmas Yet to Come, a tall, silent figure, wrapped in a long dark robe, which never speaks, but only points, with one hand.

It shows Scrooge some scenes from the future, in the cold grey light of a winter's morning. He sees a few businessmen in the street, talking carelessly about a man who has died, a man whom nobody seemed to care about. He sees the Cratchits' little house, very quiet and sad, with the little crutch standing in the corner, by the fire, carefully kept, and no Tiny Tim. And at last, he sees a neglected grave, in a lonely churchyard, and on the stone, his own name.

--

Scrooge falls to his knees, and clutches at the spirit's robe, and begs.

Spirit, he says, hear me. I am not the man I was. I will honour Christmas in my heart, and try to keep it all the year. I will live in the Past, the Present, and the Future. The spirits of all three shall strive within me. Tell me that I may yet change these shadows that you have shown me.

{img:old-man-kneeling-praying-bedpost-dawn-light}
And as he holds up his hands in prayer, the spirit's robe shrinks, and collapses, and dwindles down into a bedpost. His own bedpost.

--

He is in his own bed, in his own room, and it is morning, and he is alive.

And he is so happy, that he laughs, and cries, and dances around the room, in his nightcap, and does not know what to do with himself. I don't know what day of the month it is, he says. I don't know how long I've been among the spirits. I don't know anything. I'm quite a baby. Never mind. I don't care. I'd rather be a baby.

He runs to the window, and opens it, and puts out his head. No fog, no mist. Clear, bright, jovial, stirring, cold. Golden sunlight. Heavenly sky. Sweet fresh air. Merry bells.

--

Down in the street below, there is a boy, in his Sunday clothes.

What's today, my fine fellow? Scrooge calls down. Today? says the boy, amazed. Why, Christmas Day. It's Christmas Day! says Scrooge to himself. I haven't missed it. The spirits have done it all in one night.

And he asks the boy whether he knows the poulterer's shop on the next street, and whether they have sold the prize turkey that was hanging up there, the big one. And he sends the boy to buy it, and has it sent, secretly, to Bob Cratchit's house in Camden Town. It is twice the size of Tiny Tim.

{img:boy-carrying-giant-turkey-cab-snowy-street-laughing}
Then Scrooge dresses himself in his best, and goes out into the streets, smiling at everyone, and wishing everyone a merry Christmas. He meets one of the kind gentlemen who came to ask for money for the poor, and whispers in his ear a sum so large that the gentleman's breath is taken away. And he goes to church, and walks about the streets, and pats children on the head, and finds that everything gives him pleasure.

--

In the afternoon, he goes to his nephew Fred's house, and knocks on the door, and asks, nervously: Will you let me in, Fred?

Let him in! Dickens says. It's a mercy he didn't shake his arm off. And Scrooge spends the afternoon at the party, playing games, and is happier than he has been in all his life.

The next morning, he is at the office early, waiting for Bob Cratchit to arrive late. And when Bob creeps in, eighteen minutes late, Scrooge pretends to be angry, and then tells him that he is going to raise his salary, and help his struggling family. Make up the fires, Bob, he says, and buy another coal scuttle before you dot another i.

--

And Scrooge was better than his word, Dickens tells us.

He did it all, and infinitely more. And to Tiny Tim, who did not die, he was a second father. He became as good a friend, as good a master, and as good a man, as the good old city knew. And it was always said of him, that he knew how to keep Christmas well, if any man alive possessed the knowledge.

{img:old-man-young-boy-crutch-walking-snowy-park-smiling}
May that be truly said of us, and all of us. And so, as Tiny Tim observed, God bless us, every one.
