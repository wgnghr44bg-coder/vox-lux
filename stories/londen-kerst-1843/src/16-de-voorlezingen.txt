Charles Dickens wrote a Christmas book every year for the next few years, after A Christmas Carol.

There was The Chimes, in 1844, about the bells of an old London church, and The Cricket on the Hearth, in 1845, about a happy family and a cricket chirping by the fire, and three more after that. They were all popular. But none of them was ever loved as much as the first.

--

He went on to write many more novels: Dombey and Son, David Copperfield, which was his favourite, and partly the story of his own childhood, Bleak House, Little Dorrit, A Tale of Two Cities, Great Expectations, Our Mutual Friend.

He became the most famous writer in the world. He travelled to America, and to Italy, and to Switzerland. He started his own magazines, and wrote articles about the poor, and the schools, and the prisons, and the hospitals, and helped to make many of them better.

He worked very hard, all his life, and helped many people: poor families, struggling writers, homeless women, sick children. He helped to found a hospital for children, in Great Ormond Street, in London, which is still there today, one of the most famous children's hospitals in the world.

--

In 1853, ten years after he wrote it, he read A Christmas Carol aloud, in public, for the first time.

It was in Birmingham, in a great hall, just after Christmas, to raise money for a new institute for working people. Two thousand people came to hear him, mostly working men and women, who had paid very little for their tickets, because Dickens had asked that the tickets should be cheap, so that working people could come.

{img:great-hall-birmingham-working-people-audience-1853}
He read for three hours, standing at a little desk, on the stage, in the gaslight. He did all the voices: Scrooge's cold, cracked voice, and Bob Cratchit's timid one, and Fezziwig's jolly one, and Tiny Tim's little piping voice. The audience laughed and cried and cheered. Dickens said afterwards that it was one of the happiest evenings of his life.

--

After that, he gave many more readings, all over Britain and Ireland, and in America.

He had a special reading desk made, of red wood, with a little shelf for his book, and a rail for his gloves, and a little block of wood to lean his elbow on. He stood behind it, on a stage, with a dark red screen behind him, and gas lights shining on him, and read from his books, acting out all the parts.

A Christmas Carol was always the favourite. He read it more than a hundred and twenty times, in great halls, to audiences of thousands. People queued for hours to get in. When he read it in America, in 1867 and 1868, people stood in line all night in the snow to buy tickets.

--

The readings made Dickens very rich. But they also wore him out.

He was getting older, and his health was not good. The travelling and the excitement and the long readings exhausted him. His doctors told him to stop. But he loved the audiences, and the applause, and he would not stop.

{img:tired-writer-train-carriage-window-countryside-evening}
In 1857, he bought a house in Kent, called Gad's Hill Place, a red-brick house on a hill, near the old road from London to Canterbury, which he had admired as a little boy, when he walked past it with his father, who told him that if he worked very hard, one day he might live in a house like that. And he did.

--

In March 1870, he gave his farewell reading in London. He read A Christmas Carol, for the last time.

At the end, he stood in the gaslight, before the great audience, with tears in his eyes, and said: From these garish lights I vanish now for evermore, with a heartfelt, grateful, respectful, affectionate farewell.

{img:gads-hill-place-red-brick-house-kent-summer-garden}
Three months later, in June 1870, at Gad's Hill, he died suddenly, at the age of fifty-eight.

--

He had asked to be buried quietly, in a small country churchyard. But the nation would not allow it. He was buried in Westminster Abbey, in Poets' Corner, among the greatest writers of England.

For days afterwards, thousands of people came to the grave, and left flowers, until it was covered with them: rich and poor, old and young, from every part of the country. A little girl, it is said, when she heard that he had died, asked: Mr. Dickens dead? Then will Father Christmas die too?

He did not. Father Christmas, and the Christmas that Dickens loved, and wrote about, and helped to bring back, lived on.

--

Many people say that Charles Dickens invented the modern Christmas.

That is not quite true. Christmas was celebrated long before him, and many of the customs that he wrote about were already old. But he helped, more than almost anyone, to make Christmas what it is today: a feast of family and home, of good food and warm fires, of games and stories and laughter, of generosity and kindness, especially to the poor and the lonely.

{img:victorian-family-christmas-later-years-tree-candles}
And above all, he gave the world a story, which has been told again and again, every Christmas, for nearly two hundred years: the story of an old man who learned, in one long night, to open his heart.
