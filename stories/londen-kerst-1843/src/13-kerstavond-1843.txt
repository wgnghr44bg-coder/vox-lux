On Christmas Eve, 1843, the streets of London are crowded until late at night.

The shops stay open, lit by gas, with their windows full of good things, decorated with holly and evergreens. The poulterers and the grocers and the butchers are busy until midnight, selling the last geese and turkeys and puddings to the people who have just been paid their wages, and have hurried to the shops to buy their Christmas dinners.

--

The people of London are out in their thousands, carrying parcels and baskets, and geese under their arms, and bunches of holly, and Christmas trees, a few of them, for those who have heard about the new German custom.

Children run between the legs of the crowds, shouting with excitement. Street sellers cry their wares: hot chestnuts, oranges, holly, mistletoe, a penny a bunch. The waits are singing carols on the corners, with their lanterns. A brass band is playing in a square.

The cold is sharp, and the breath of the people rises in clouds in the gaslight. But nobody seems to mind.

--

At Number One, Devonshire Terrace, the Dickens family is preparing for Christmas.

The house is decorated with holly and ivy and evergreens. There is a great fire burning in the drawing room. The children are wild with excitement. Their father has promised them a wonderful Christmas, and their father always keeps his promises about Christmas.

{img:victorian-drawing-room-holly-fire-children-excited}
Dickens loves Christmas more than almost anyone. He loves the games and the food and the decorations, the parties and the dancing, and above all, the children's excitement. He is like a big child himself, at Christmas, his friends say.

--

On Christmas Eve, he plays with the children in the nursery, and tells them stories, and does his magic tricks.

He is a very good conjuror. He has bought a set of conjuring apparatus, and practised for hours, and he can make coins appear and disappear, and turn a handkerchief into a bunch of flowers, and cook a plum pudding in a gentleman's hat, and produce it, hot and steaming, to the astonishment of everyone.

The children scream with delight. Their mother laughs. And then they are sent up to bed, with a candle, and hang up their stockings at the end of their beds, and try very hard to go to sleep.

--

Later, Dickens sits by the fire, with Catherine and Georgina, and a few friends, and talks about the book, which has been in the shops for five days, and has already sold out.

He is very happy. All over London, that very night, he knows, families are sitting by their fires, reading his little red book aloud: the story of Scrooge, and the ghosts, and Fezziwig's ball, and the Cratchits' Christmas dinner, and Tiny Tim.

And all over London, people are laughing, and crying, and thinking about the poor, and about kindness, and about Christmas.

--

Late in the evening, the church bells begin to ring, all over the city, for the midnight services.

People walk through the frosty streets to church, wrapped in their coats and shawls and mufflers, carrying lanterns. In the great churches, Saint Paul's Cathedral and Westminster Abbey and the old churches of the City, the candles are lit, and the choirs sing the old carols, and the organs play.

{img:old-city-church-candles-choir-midnight-christmas}
In the small churches and chapels, too, all over London, the people gather, in the candlelight, and sing, and pray, and wish each other a happy Christmas.

--

In the poor districts, too, there is Christmas.

In the little rooms, by the light of a single candle, families are getting ready for the next day. The goose from the goose club is hanging in the corner, or has been taken to the baker's, ready for the morning. There are a few oranges, and some nuts, and a little bunch of holly over the fireplace. The children's stockings are hanging by the little fire, with perhaps an orange and a penny and a sugar mouse in each.

At the ragged school in Field Lane, the volunteers are giving the children a Christmas supper, of bread and meat and pudding, and singing carols with them.

--

And at last, late in the night, the streets grow quiet.

The shops close their shutters. The last carriages rattle home. The waits put out their lanterns. The bells stop ringing. The great city settles down to sleep, under a sky that, for once, is clear of fog, and full of cold, bright stars.

{img:sleeping-london-rooftops-chimneys-stars-christmas-night}
In a million houses, rich and poor, families are asleep, waiting for Christmas morning. And in a house near Regent's Park, a young writer lies awake for a while, listening to the silence, and smiling.
