Today, London is one of the greatest cities in the world, with almost nine million people. Most of the damage of the Blitz has long been repaired. New buildings stand where old ones were lost. But if you know where to look, you can still find traces of 1940, all over the city.

--

Under the streets of Westminster, near the Houses of Parliament, there are the Churchill War Rooms. A secret underground headquarters, where Churchill and his government worked during the war. Today, it is a museum. You can walk through the narrow corridors, and see the map room, with its maps still pinned to the walls, and the coloured pins showing where the ships and armies were. The telephones. The desks. Churchill's bedroom. Everything as it was left, on the day the war ended.

St Paul's Cathedral still stands on its hill, with its great dome rising above the City. Inside, there is a memorial to the people of London who lost their lives in the Blitz. And there is the American Memorial Chapel, built after the war, to remember the Americans who died helping Britain. Visitors can climb up to the Golden Gallery, at the top of the dome, as Bert did, and look out over the whole of London.

{img:st-pauls-golden-gallery-view-today-london-skyline-river-modern-towers}
The Underground still runs, every day, carrying millions of people. Some of the stations where people sheltered during the Blitz are still in use. And if you look at the platforms, and the curved tiled walls, you can imagine the rows of people, lying on their blankets, all night, during the raids. In some stations, there are little plaques, remembering them.

In the Imperial War Museum, in south London, there are many things from the Blitz. An Anderson shelter, which you can go inside. Gas masks, ration books, wardens' helmets and armbands. The drawings of Henry Moore, of the people sleeping in the Underground. Photographs, letters and diaries. And recordings of people who lived through the Blitz, telling their stories, in their own voices.

--

{img:imperial-war-museum-anderson-shelter-display-visitors-today-gas-masks}
In the East End, a few of the old streets of terraced houses still stand, like Lily Road. Some of them have been lovingly restored. In Columbia Road, on Sunday mornings, there is a famous flower market, where the street fills with flowers and plants, and the stall holders call out their prices, just as they did a hundred years ago. And in the little pubs on the corners, people still sing around the piano, sometimes, on a Saturday night.

The National Gallery is still on Trafalgar Square, full of paintings again. There is a little plaque, remembering Myra Hess and her lunchtime concerts. And there are still free lunchtime concerts in London, in churches and halls, all over the city, carrying on the tradition. People still come in their lunch breaks, with their sandwiches, to listen to Bach and Mozart, in the middle of the busy city.

{img:lunchtime-concert-london-church-today-office-workers-listening-pianist}
And every year, on the fifteenth of September, Battle of Britain Day is remembered. In the sky over London, sometimes, there is a flypast. Old Spitfires and Hurricanes, beautifully restored, flying low over the city, with the sound of their engines, deep and powerful, rolling over the rooftops. People stop in the streets, and look up, and some of them have tears in their eyes.

--

And in many families in London, the stories are still told. By grandparents and great-grandparents, who remember. About the shelters, and the Underground, and the cups of tea, and the neighbours who helped each other. About the milkman, and the queen, and the concerts. About the long, dark autumn of 1940, when the city did not give up. The people who lived through it are very old now. But their stories live on.
