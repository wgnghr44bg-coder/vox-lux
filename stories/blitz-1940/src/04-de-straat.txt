The East End of London is a world of its own. It is the part of the city closest to the docks, where the great ships come up the Thames from all over the world. Hundreds of thousands of people live here, crowded together in narrow streets of small houses. Most of them work in the docks, the factories, the markets and the workshops. They are not rich. But they are proud, tough, funny and loyal to each other.

--

The streets are full of life. There are markets, with stalls selling fruit, vegetables, fish, clothes and everything else. The stall holders, called costermongers, shout their wares, in loud, cheerful voices. There are little shops on every corner. Bakers, butchers, grocers, tailors and pawnbrokers. There are pubs, where people meet in the evenings, to drink, talk, sing around the piano, and play darts. And there are cafés, where you can get a cup of tea and a bacon sandwich, for a few pennies.

The people of the East End come from everywhere. Many families have lived here for generations, like the Collins family. But there are also many newcomers. Irish families, who came in the last century. Jewish families, from Russia and Poland, who came about forty years before, and who run many of the shops and workshops. Sailors from India, China and Africa, who came on the ships, and stayed. All of them living side by side, in the same crowded streets.

{img:children-playing-hopscotch-street-1940-chalk-marks-mothers-watching-doorsteps}
The children of the East End play in the streets, because there is nowhere else to play. They play hopscotch, with chalk marks on the pavement. Skipping, with a long rope, and songs. Football, with a ball made of rags, between lamp posts. Marbles, in the gutter. Hide and seek, in the alleys. Cricket, with a dustbin for a wicket. And on hot summer days, they swim in the canal, or play in the water from a fire hydrant.

The women of the street meet on their doorsteps, in the evenings, and talk. About their husbands and their children, about the prices in the shops, about the war, and about each other. They help each other with everything. If someone is ill, the neighbours bring soup. If someone has a baby, the neighbours bring clothes. If someone is short of money, the neighbours lend a little. Nobody has much. But they share what they have.

--

{img:pub-interior-1940-piano-singing-crowd-pints-darts-warm-light-evening}
On Saturday nights, the pubs are full. People sing around the piano. The old songs, from the music halls, which everyone knows. Songs about London, about love, about beer, about mothers and the old country. Nan knows all of them. Sometimes she goes to the pub on the corner with Peg, and plays the piano, and everyone sings. She has a strong voice, for an old woman. And she knows more songs than anyone in the street.

Peg works at the bakery on the corner, in the afternoons. It belongs to an old couple, Mr and Mrs Baker, which everyone thinks is very funny. They bake bread, rolls, buns and cakes, in a big oven at the back of the shop. The smell of fresh bread fills the whole street, in the mornings. June helps in the shop after school, serving customers, and wrapping loaves in paper. And she gets to take home the broken biscuits, at the end of the day.

{img:bakery-shop-1940-fresh-loaves-window-girl-serving-customers-counter}
In September, many families from the East End go hop-picking, in Kent, in the countryside to the south-east of London. For a few weeks, they live in huts on the hop farms, and pick the hops, which are used to make beer. It is hard work, but it is also like a holiday. The children run in the fields, and the families sit around campfires in the evenings, singing. For many East End children, it is the only time they see the countryside.

--

This year, some families have gone hop-picking as usual, in spite of the war. The Murphys have gone, with all five children. The street is a little quieter without them. Peg would have liked to go, but she has her work at the bakery, and Bert has his work at the docks. Maybe next year, she says. June thinks of the fields of Kent, green and quiet, and wishes she could see them too. But she is happy at home, in the busy, noisy, friendly street, where she has always lived.
