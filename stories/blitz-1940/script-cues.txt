Tonight we travel back more than eighty years, to the year 1940. We go to London, the capital of Britain, at the end of a long, warm summer. Britain is at war. So let us begin, on a sunny Sunday morning, in a little street in the East End of London, in September 1940. The children are playing. The radio is playing. The kettle is on. And for one more day, at least, everything is calm.

The war began a year before, in September 1939. Germany, ruled by Adolf Hitler, had invaded Poland. And Britain and France, who had promised to help Poland, declared war on Germany. On a Sunday morning, the prime minister spoke on the radio, and told the British people that the country was at war. Families all over Britain sat around their radios, and listened in silence.

[long-pause]

For the first months, not much happened in Britain. People called it the phoney war. They waited for bombs that did not come. Children were sent to the countryside, and many came back home again, because nothing seemed to be happening. Everyone carried gas masks, in little cardboard boxes. And windows were covered at night, so that no light could be seen from the sky. But life went on, mostly as normal.

[pause]

Then, in the spring of 1940, everything changed. Germany invaded Denmark, Norway, the Netherlands, Belgium and France, one after another, very quickly. The British army in France was pushed back to the sea, at a place called Dunkirk. And in the summer, hundreds of little boats sailed across the Channel, fishing boats, pleasure boats and ferries, as well as navy ships, and brought most of the soldiers home. People called it the miracle of Dunkirk.

[pause]

{img:little-ships-dunkirk-1940-fishing-boats-crossing-channel-calm-sea-distance}
By the summer of 1940, Britain was almost alone. France had surrendered. Most of Europe was under German control. A new prime minister, Winston Churchill, spoke on the radio. He told the people that they would never surrender. And they believed him. All over Britain, people prepared to defend their country. Old men joined the Home Guard. Women joined the services. Signposts were taken down, so that invaders would not know where they were.

[pause]

In the summer and early autumn of 1940, the war came to the skies above Britain. German planes came across the Channel, to attack the airfields of the Royal Air Force, in the south of England. And the young pilots of the RAF flew up to meet them, in their Spitfires and Hurricanes. Day after day, in the bright summer skies, there were battles high above the fields of Kent and Sussex. People called it the Battle of Britain.

[long-pause]

{img:spitfire-planes-white-trails-blue-summer-sky-kent-fields-below-people-watching}
People on the ground watched the battles in the sky. Little planes, far above, like silver insects, with long white trails behind them, twisting and turning, in the blue sky. Farmers stopped working in their fields to look up. Children counted the planes. It was frightening, but also strangely beautiful. And everyone hoped for the young pilots, many of them only nineteen or twenty years old.

[pause]

In London, people read about the battles in the newspapers, and heard about them on the radio. They knew that the German planes might come to London too, one day. Everyone had been told what to do. Where the shelters were. How to put out a small fire. How to put on a gas mask. But it still seemed far away, in the summer. The sky over London was quiet and blue. The parks were full of people. And the children played in the streets.

[pause]

{img:london-park-summer-1940-deckchairs-people-relaxing-barrage-balloons-sky}
There was one strange new thing in the sky over London. Barrage balloons. Huge silver balloons, like fat fish, floating high above the city, tied to the ground with long steel cables. They were meant to stop enemy planes from flying low over the city. There were hundreds of them, all over London. Children gave them names. People got used to them. They looked almost friendly, floating in the summer sky.

[long-pause]

And then, on the seventh of September, 1940, a sunny Saturday afternoon, the planes came to London. That day was the beginning of the Blitz. The word comes from the German word blitzkrieg, which means lightning war. For the next eight months, London would be visited by the bombers almost every night. But the people of London would not give up. And this is their story.

[long-pause]

{img:east-end-terraced-house-1940-front-step-scrubbed-lace-curtains-door}
The family lives in a small terraced house, in a street called Lily Road, in Poplar, in the East End of London, near the docks. The house is one of a long row of houses, all exactly the same, built about sixty years before, for the workers of the docks and the factories. It has two rooms downstairs and two upstairs, a tiny kitchen at the back, and a little yard, with an outside toilet and a washing line.

[long-pause]

The father is called Bert Collins. He is about forty-five years old. He works at the docks, as a crane driver, unloading the ships that come up the Thames from all over the world. He fought in the First World War, as a young man, in France. He was too old to be called up this time. So he has joined the Air Raid Precautions, the ARP, as a volunteer warden. At night, after his work, he puts on his tin helmet and his armband, and patrols the streets.

[pause]

The mother is called Peg. She is a cheerful, practical woman, with curly brown hair, a loud laugh, and an apron that she never seems to take off. She works part of the day in a bakery, on the corner. And she runs the house, cooks, cleans, washes, mends and queues for the rations. She knows everybody in the street, and everybody knows her. When anybody has a problem, they come to Peg.

[pause]

{img:mother-1940-apron-curly-hair-laughing-kitchen-doorway-cup-of-tea}
The grandmother, Bert's mother, lives with them. Everyone calls her Nan. She is very old, about seventy-five. She was born in the same street, in the same house, and she has lived there all her life. She remembers the old Queen Victoria, and the horse buses, and the first motor cars. She is tough, sharp-tongued and funny. She sits by the window all day, knitting socks for the soldiers, and watching the street. Nothing happens that Nan does not see.

[pause]

The daughter is called June. She is thirteen. She is tall and thin, with her mother's curly hair and her father's serious grey eyes. At the beginning of the war, she was evacuated to the countryside, with her school. But she was so homesick that her mother brought her home again, after six months. Now she goes to a school that has been reopened nearby, in the mornings, and helps her mother in the bakery in the afternoons.

[long-pause]

{img:girl-thirteen-1940-reading-letter-window-seat-terraced-house-light}
And there is a little brother, Billy, who is seven. But Billy is not at home. When the war began, he was evacuated too, to a farm in Devon, in the west of England, far from London. He lives there with a farmer and his wife, who have no children of their own. He is happy there. He has a dog, and a pony, and fields to run in, and fresh eggs every morning. He writes letters home, every week, in his big, wobbly handwriting.

[pause]

The family is not rich. But they are not poor either, as long as Bert has his work at the docks. They have enough to eat, a warm house, and each other. The house is small, but cosy. In the front room, there is a fireplace, a sofa, two armchairs, a piano that Nan plays on Sundays, and the radio, in a big wooden case, which is the most important thing in the house. On the mantelpiece, there are photographs. Billy, in his school cap. Bert, as a young soldier, in 1916.

[pause]

{img:front-room-1940-radio-wooden-case-piano-photographs-mantelpiece-armchairs}
The street is like a big family. Everyone knows everyone. Their neighbours on one side are the Cohens, an old Jewish couple, who have a tailor's shop. On the other side, there are the Murphys, an Irish family with five children, who are always noisy. Across the street, there is Mr Ali, a sailor from India, who married a local girl, and now runs a little café. The children of the street play together, in and out of each other's houses, all day long.

[long-pause]

On this Sunday evening, at the beginning of September, the family sits together in the front room, after supper. The radio is playing music. Nan is knitting. Peg is darning socks. Bert is reading the paper. And June is writing a letter to Billy, telling him about the street, and the neighbours, and the cat, who has had kittens under the stairs. Outside, the sun is setting, red and gold, over the rooftops and the cranes of the docks. It is a peaceful evening. The last one, for a while.

[long-pause]

{img:east-end-street-life-1940-market-stalls-costermongers-barrows-crowds-busy}
The East End of London is a world of its own. It is the part of the city closest to the docks, where the great ships come up the Thames from all over the world. Hundreds of thousands of people live here, crowded together in narrow streets of small houses. Most of them work in the docks, the factories, the markets and the workshops. They are not rich. But they are proud, tough, funny and loyal to each other.

[long-pause]

The streets are full of life. There are markets, with stalls selling fruit, vegetables, fish, clothes and everything else. The stall holders, called costermongers, shout their wares, in loud, cheerful voices. There are little shops on every corner. Bakers, butchers, grocers, tailors and pawnbrokers. There are pubs, where people meet in the evenings, to drink, talk, sing around the piano, and play darts. And there are cafés, where you can get a cup of tea and a bacon sandwich, for a few pennies.

[pause]

The people of the East End come from everywhere. Many families have lived here for generations, like the Collins family. But there are also many newcomers. Irish families, who came in the last century. Jewish families, from Russia and Poland, who came about forty years before, and who run many of the shops and workshops. Sailors from India, China and Africa, who came on the ships, and stayed. All of them living side by side, in the same crowded streets.

[pause]

{img:children-playing-hopscotch-street-1940-chalk-marks-mothers-watching-doorsteps}
The children of the East End play in the streets, because there is nowhere else to play. They play hopscotch, with chalk marks on the pavement. Skipping, with a long rope, and songs. Football, with a ball made of rags, between lamp posts. Marbles, in the gutter. Hide and seek, in the alleys. Cricket, with a dustbin for a wicket. And on hot summer days, they swim in the canal, or play in the water from a fire hydrant.

[pause]

The women of the street meet on their doorsteps, in the evenings, and talk. About their husbands and their children, about the prices in the shops, about the war, and about each other. They help each other with everything. If someone is ill, the neighbours bring soup. If someone has a baby, the neighbours bring clothes. If someone is short of money, the neighbours lend a little. Nobody has much. But they share what they have.

[long-pause]

{img:pub-interior-1940-piano-singing-crowd-pints-darts-warm-light-evening}
On Saturday nights, the pubs are full. People sing around the piano. The old songs, from the music halls, which everyone knows. Songs about London, about love, about beer, about mothers and the old country. Nan knows all of them. Sometimes she goes to the pub on the corner with Peg, and plays the piano, and everyone sings. She has a strong voice, for an old woman. And she knows more songs than anyone in the street.

[pause]

Peg works at the bakery on the corner, in the afternoons. It belongs to an old couple, Mr and Mrs Baker, which everyone thinks is very funny. They bake bread, rolls, buns and cakes, in a big oven at the back of the shop. The smell of fresh bread fills the whole street, in the mornings. June helps in the shop after school, serving customers, and wrapping loaves in paper. And she gets to take home the broken biscuits, at the end of the day.

[pause]

{img:bakery-shop-1940-fresh-loaves-window-girl-serving-customers-counter}
In September, many families from the East End go hop-picking, in Kent, in the countryside to the south-east of London. For a few weeks, they live in huts on the hop farms, and pick the hops, which are used to make beer. It is hard work, but it is also like a holiday. The children run in the fields, and the families sit around campfires in the evenings, singing. For many East End children, it is the only time they see the countryside.

[long-pause]

This year, some families have gone hop-picking as usual, in spite of the war. The Murphys have gone, with all five children. The street is a little quieter without them. Peg would have liked to go, but she has her work at the bakery, and Bert has his work at the docks. Maybe next year, she says. June thinks of the fields of Kent, green and quiet, and wishes she could see them too. But she is happy at home, in the busy, noisy, friendly street, where she has always lived.

[long-pause]

{img:evacuee-children-1939-railway-station-labels-gas-mask-boxes-suitcases-teachers}
At the very beginning of the war, in September 1939, the government had a great plan. To send the children of the big cities away, to the countryside, where they would be safe from the bombs. It was called the evacuation. In just a few days, more than a million and a half people, most of them children, were taken by train from the cities to the countryside. It was the biggest movement of people in British history.

[long-pause]

Billy was six, then. He remembers it well. The morning when his mother woke him early, and dressed him in his best clothes. The little cardboard box with his gas mask in it, hanging on a string around his neck. The brown label, tied to his coat button, with his name and his school written on it, like a parcel. And the small suitcase, with his pyjamas, his toothbrush, a spare shirt, and his teddy bear.

[pause]

He went with his school, walking in a long line, to the railway station, with all the other children, and the teachers. The mothers walked beside them, to the station, but were not allowed onto the platforms. Peg kissed him at the gate, and told him to be a good boy, and to write. Then she watched him go, waving, until he disappeared into the crowd. She cried all the way home. Many mothers did.

[pause]

{img:steam-train-countryside-children-at-windows-waving-green-fields-1939}
The train took the children west, out of London, through the countryside. Most of them had never seen it before. Fields, cows, sheep, woods and little villages. They pressed their faces to the windows, and stared. Some of them had never seen a cow. The train went on for hours, all day. And in the evening, it arrived at a little station in Devon, in the south-west of England, near the sea.

[pause]

In the village hall, the children were lined up, and the people of the village came to choose which children they would take into their homes. It was a strange and frightening moment, for the children. Billy stood at the end of the line, holding his teddy bear, feeling very small. And a farmer and his wife came up to him, and the wife bent down, and asked him if he liked dogs. Billy said yes. And she smiled, and took his hand.

[long-pause]

{img:devon-farmhouse-thatched-roof-cob-walls-farmer-wife-little-boy-dog}
The farmer and his wife are called Mr and Mrs Penhale. They are kind, quiet people, about fifty years old, with no children of their own. Their farm is a thatched farmhouse, with thick white walls, in a green valley, with a stream, and hills all around. They have cows, sheep, pigs, chickens, two big horses for the plough, a pony, and a black and white sheepdog called Fly.

[pause]

At first, Billy was very homesick. He cried every night, in the big strange bed, in the quiet house, with no traffic and no voices outside, only owls and the wind. But Mrs Penhale sat with him, and told him stories, and let Fly sleep at the foot of his bed. And slowly, he got used to it. And then, he began to love it. The fields. The animals. The fresh milk and eggs. The freedom.

[pause]

{img:little-boy-riding-pony-devon-field-sheepdog-running-beside-summer}
Now, a year later, Billy is a country boy. He helps Mr Penhale on the farm. He feeds the chickens, collects the eggs, and helps to bring the cows in for milking. He goes to the village school, with the village children, and the other evacuees. He has learned to ride the pony. He knows the names of all the cows. His cheeks are rosy, and he has grown taller. And he has started to speak with a little Devon accent, which makes his mother laugh, when he writes.

[long-pause]

Many evacuated children had good experiences, like Billy. Others were less lucky, and were unhappy, and went home. In the quiet months of the phoney war, many children came back to the cities. But when the Blitz began, many were sent away again. Billy stays in Devon, all through the war. His mother misses him every day. But she knows he is safe, and happy. And every week, his letter arrives, with its big wobbly handwriting, and its drawings of the pony, and the dog, and the cows.

[long-pause]

{img:blackout-london-street-night-1940-dark-houses-no-lights-moonlight-only}
Since the beginning of the war, there has been the blackout. Every evening, at sunset, every window in London must be covered, so that no light can be seen from outside. Not a crack. Not a gleam. The streetlamps are switched off. The headlights of cars and buses are covered, except for a narrow slit. And the whole great city becomes completely dark. So that enemy planes, flying overhead at night, cannot see where London is.

[long-pause]

Every family has blackout curtains. Thick, dark curtains, of black cloth, which are drawn across every window, every evening. Peg made theirs herself, on her sewing machine, from cloth she bought at the market. Some people use cardboard, or black paint, or blankets. And every evening, at dusk, Peg goes around the house, drawing the curtains, and checking that there are no gaps. Because if a light shows, the warden will knock on the door.

[pause]

And in this street, the warden is Bert. Every evening, he walks around the streets, in his helmet, looking at every window. If he sees a light, he knocks on the door, and calls out, put that light out! It is the most famous phrase of the war. Most people are careful. But sometimes, someone forgets, and there is a little argument. Bert is polite but firm. Even with his own mother, when Nan once forgot to close the curtain in the bedroom.

[pause]

{img:air-raid-warden-tin-helmet-armband-knocking-door-torch-dark-street}
The blackout makes London a strange place, at night. It is so dark that you cannot see your own hand in front of your face, on a cloudy night. People bump into lamp posts, trip over steps, and fall off kerbs. Cars and buses crawl along, slowly, with their dim slits of light. To help people, the kerbs and the lamp posts are painted with white stripes. And people carry little torches, with the light covered with tissue paper, and pointed at the ground.

[pause]

But on clear nights, there is something wonderful. The stars. For the first time in a hundred years, the people of London can see the stars, from the middle of the city. The Milky Way, stretching across the sky above the rooftops. The moon, bright and clear. Many Londoners have never seen so many stars before. June stands in the yard, at night, and looks up at them, and thinks of Billy, in Devon, looking at the same stars.

[long-pause]

{img:family-gas-masks-cardboard-boxes-hall-coat-hooks-1940-everyday}
And there are the gas masks. Everyone in Britain has been given a gas mask. Even babies, who have special ones, like little bags. Everyone must carry their gas mask with them, everywhere, in its cardboard box, hanging on a string. To work. To school. To the shops. To church. People were afraid that the enemy might drop gas bombs on the cities. In fact, this never happened. But nobody knew that, in 1940. So everyone carries their gas mask, just in case.

[pause]

The gas masks are made of rubber, with a little window of plastic to see through, and a round filter at the front. They smell of rubber and disinfectant. When you breathe out, the rubber makes a rude noise, against your cheeks. The children think this is very funny. At school, they have gas mask practice, every week, and sit in their classroom, wearing their masks, making rude noises, until the teacher tells them to stop.

[pause]

{img:schoolchildren-practising-gas-masks-classroom-1940-teacher-laughing-desks}
In the evenings, behind the blackout curtains, the houses are cosy. The fire is lit. The lamp is on. The radio is playing. Nobody can see in, and nobody can see out. It is like being in a little warm cave, in the middle of the dark city. Families sit together, and talk, and read, and listen to the radio, and play cards. And outside, in the darkness, the wardens walk the empty streets, checking the windows, and looking up at the sky.

[long-pause]

Bert says that the blackout is the strangest thing about the war. Stranger than the rationing, or the sirens. He has lived in London all his life. He has never seen it dark before. He says that sometimes, walking the streets at night, under the stars, with no lights anywhere, it feels like the London his grandmother told him about. Before the gaslights came. Quiet, dark and ancient. And he finds that he likes it, a little, in spite of everything.

[long-pause]

{img:ration-book-1940-buff-cover-coupons-grocer-counter-stamping-queue}
In wartime Britain, food is rationed. Much of Britain's food comes from abroad, on ships. And the enemy's submarines are attacking those ships, in the Atlantic. So there is not enough food for everyone to have as much as they want. To make sure that everyone gets a fair share, the government has introduced rationing. Every person has a ration book, with little coupons inside, for each week.

[long-pause]

With the ration book, each person can buy a fixed amount of certain foods, every week. In September 1940, an adult can buy, each week, about a hundred grams of bacon or ham, about a hundred grams of butter and margarine together, two hundred and twenty grams of sugar, about fifty grams of tea, and meat worth about one shilling and tenpence. That is not very much. Bread, potatoes, vegetables, cheese and fish are not rationed yet, but they are sometimes hard to find.

[pause]

Every family must register with a particular shop, for each kind of rationed food. A grocer, a butcher. When Peg goes shopping, she takes the ration books of the whole family. The shopkeeper cuts out the coupons, or stamps the book, and gives her the right amount. She also pays, of course. Rationing does not make food free. It just makes sure that everyone gets the same, rich or poor.

[pause]

{img:women-queueing-outside-butcher-shop-1940-baskets-chatting-patient}
And there are queues. Long queues, outside every shop, especially when something special arrives. If people see a queue, they join it, even if they do not know what it is for. Because it must be something good. Peg spends hours every week, standing in queues. But she does not mind too much. The queues are full of neighbours, and gossip, and jokes. You learn all the news of the street in a queue.

[pause]

Peg is very good at making the rations stretch. She uses every scrap of food. Nothing is wasted. She makes stews with lots of vegetables and a little meat. Pies with potato crusts. Bread pudding, from stale bread. Soups from bones and peelings. She follows the advice of the Ministry of Food, which publishes recipes, on the radio and in the newspapers. Some of them are good. Some of them are very strange. Like the famous carrot fudge.

[long-pause]

{img:dig-for-victory-allotment-vegetables-family-digging-park-1940}
Everyone is encouraged to grow their own vegetables. The government has a slogan for it. Dig for Victory. Parks, gardens, playing fields and even the moats of castles are dug up and turned into vegetable gardens. People grow potatoes, carrots, cabbages, onions, beans and lettuces, wherever there is a patch of earth. Some people keep chickens, for eggs, and rabbits, and even pigs, in their back yards.

[pause]

The Collins family has a tiny back yard, mostly paved. But Bert has dug up a small patch, by the wall, and grows potatoes, runner beans and a few tomatoes. Nan grows herbs in pots on the windowsill. And in the little park at the end of the street, which has been turned into allotments, Bert has a small plot, where he grows cabbages and onions, on Sunday mornings, and talks with the other gardeners about the war.

[pause]

{img:back-yard-terraced-house-small-vegetable-patch-runner-beans-tomatoes-washing}
In the streets, there are pig bins. Big metal bins, where people put their kitchen scraps, potato peelings and leftover food. The scraps are collected, and fed to pigs, in farms and in city pig clubs, so that nothing is wasted. Even the children help. They collect old paper, metal and rubber, for recycling, to help the war. They take pots and pans to the collection points, to be made into aeroplanes.

[long-pause]

The rations are small. But in a strange way, many people in the East End eat better than before the war. Before, many poor families did not have enough to eat, or ate only bread and tea. Now, everyone gets a fair share of meat, butter, sugar and tea. And the government gives extra milk and orange juice to children and mothers. Peg says that the children of the street have never looked so healthy. Even if they do complain about the carrot fudge.

[long-pause]

{img:sunny-saturday-afternoon-1940-east-end-street-people-looking-up-at-sky}
On Saturday, the seventh of September, 1940, it is a beautiful afternoon. The sky is clear and blue. The sun is warm. In the East End, people are enjoying their weekend. Some are at the market. Some are in the park. Children are playing in the streets. Bert has finished his half-day at the docks, and is sitting in the yard, in his shirtsleeves, reading the newspaper. Peg and June are at the bakery. Nan is at her window.

[long-pause]

At about five o'clock, the sirens begin. All over London, the air raid sirens wail, rising and falling, a long, sad, howling sound. People have heard them before, many times, in practice. But this time, it is different. Because this time, high up in the blue sky, coming from the east, there are planes. Hundreds of them. A great swarm, flying in formation, glinting in the afternoon sun.

[pause]

We will not follow what happened next too closely. It is enough to say that the planes came to the docks, and the East End, that afternoon, and that evening, and that night. Many people sheltered as best they could. The docks were hit, and great fires burned, in the warehouses full of timber, sugar, rum and spices, along the river. The smoke rose up into the sky, and could be seen for many kilometres. It was the beginning of the Blitz.

[pause]

{img:family-hurrying-to-shelter-garden-gate-sirens-grandmother-helped-evening}
When the sirens sound, Bert puts on his helmet and goes out to his post, as a warden. Peg and June run home from the bakery. They help Nan down the garden, to the shelter in the yard. The Cohens and the children from the house next door come too. They squeeze into the little shelter together, and close the door. And they sit there, in the dark, holding each other's hands, listening to the sounds outside, and waiting.

[pause]

It is a long night. The sounds come and go. Sometimes far away. Sometimes closer. The little shelter shakes. Dust falls from the roof. A baby next door cries. Mrs Cohen prays quietly, in Hebrew. Nan, of all people, starts to sing. An old music hall song, about a girl called Daisy, and a bicycle made for two. And one by one, the others join in. Softly at first, then louder. And somehow, it helps.

[long-pause]

{img:dawn-over-east-end-smoke-rising-docks-people-emerging-shelters-quiet}
In the morning, the all-clear sounds. A long, steady note, without rising and falling. It means that the danger has passed. The people come out of the shelters, blinking in the daylight, stiff and tired. The air smells of smoke. Over the docks, great clouds of smoke are still rising. Some streets nearby have been badly damaged. But Lily Road is still standing. All the houses. All the people.

[pause]

Bert comes home, at seven o'clock, black with smoke, and exhausted. He has been out all night, helping people, and guiding them to the shelters, and helping the firemen. He sits down at the kitchen table, and Peg puts a cup of tea in front of him, and he holds it in both hands, and does not say anything for a long time. Then he says that he is proud of the people of the East End. That they were wonderful. All of them.

[pause]

{img:neighbours-sharing-tea-on-doorsteps-morning-after-tired-smiling-1940}
That Sunday morning, the people of Lily Road come out of their houses, and stand in the street, and talk. They share tea, and bread, and news. They help those who have lost things. They laugh at little things, and they comfort each other. And then, slowly, they begin to clean up, and to get ready for the week ahead. Because on Monday morning, there will be work, and school, and shopping, and life must go on.

[long-pause]

Nobody knew, on that first morning, that this was only the beginning. That the planes would come back that night, and the next night, and the next, for fifty-seven nights in a row. And then, often, for eight more months. But the people of London would get used to it, in a way, as much as anyone can. And they would find ways to sleep, and to work, and to laugh, and to keep going. That is what the rest of this story is about.

[long-pause]

{img:anderson-shelter-back-garden-curved-metal-earth-covered-flowers-on-top}
In the back yard of the Collins family's house, there is a little shelter. It is called an Anderson shelter, after the government minister who was in charge of it. Millions of them were given to families all over Britain, before the war began. It is made of curved sheets of corrugated steel, bolted together into a little arched hut, about two metres long and one and a half metres wide. It is half buried in the ground, and covered with a thick layer of earth.

[long-pause]

Bert put it up himself, the year before, with the help of Mr Murphy from next door. They dug a big hole in the yard, about a metre deep, and set the steel sheets into it, and bolted them together, and piled the earth from the hole on top. Then Nan planted flowers in the earth on the top. Marigolds, nasturtiums and some runner beans. Now, in September, the shelter is covered with orange and yellow flowers. It looks almost pretty. Like a little hill in the yard.

[pause]

Inside, the shelter is small, dark and damp. Bert has put a wooden floor in it, and built two narrow bunks, one above the other, on each side. There is room for about six people, if they squeeze together. There are blankets, pillows, a little oil lamp, a flask of tea, some biscuits, a first aid box, a bucket, and a few books and toys. And a little curtain over the door, made from an old blanket, to keep out the draughts.

[pause]

{img:inside-anderson-shelter-bunks-blankets-oil-lamp-family-cosy-night}
After the first night, the family sleeps in the shelter every night. When the sirens sound, usually in the evening, soon after dark, they gather their things, and go down the garden, into the shelter. Nan, with her knitting. Peg, with the flask of tea. June, with her book and her pillow. The cat follows them, and curls up on the bottom bunk. And Bert, if he is not on duty, comes too, last, after checking that the house is dark and the fire is out.

[pause]

It is not comfortable. The bunks are narrow and hard. It is cold, and damp, and smells of earth. When it rains, water sometimes comes in, and they must bail it out with a bucket. It is crowded, with everyone squashed together. But it is safe. Much safer than the house. And in a strange way, it becomes cosy. The little lamp glows. The tea is hot. The blankets are warm. And the family is together.

[long-pause]

{img:grandmother-telling-stories-in-shelter-lamplight-girl-listening-blankets}
To pass the long nights, they talk, and tell stories. Nan tells stories about the old days in the East End. About her own childhood, in the 1870s, when the streets were lit by gas, and the milk came from cows kept in the back yards, and the children worked in the match factories. About her husband, Bert's father, who was a docker, and sang in the pub every Saturday night. June listens, fascinated. She has never heard these stories before.

[pause]

They also play games. I spy, which is difficult in the dark. Twenty questions. Word games. June reads aloud from her book, by the light of the lamp, until her voice is tired. Peg knits. Nan sings. And sometimes, if they are very tired, they actually sleep, in spite of the noise outside, and the cold, and the narrow bunks. Peg says that you can get used to anything. Even sleeping in a tin hut at the bottom of the garden.

[pause]

{img:dawn-light-shelter-door-family-emerging-garden-tired-cat-stretching}
In the morning, when the all-clear sounds, they come out of the shelter, stiff and tired, and blink in the daylight. They stretch, and yawn, and look around, to see if everything is all right. The house. The street. The neighbours. They call over the walls, to the Cohens and the Murphys. Are you all right? Yes, we're all right. Are you? And then Peg goes into the kitchen, and puts the kettle on, and starts the day, as if it were an ordinary morning.

[long-pause]

Not everyone has an Anderson shelter. Many houses in the East End have no garden, only a tiny yard, or nothing at all. So other people go to public shelters. Big brick shelters in the streets. The basements of churches, schools and factories. Railway arches. And many go to a place that the government did not plan for, at first. But which became the most famous shelter of the Blitz. The stations of the London Underground.

[long-pause]

{img:london-underground-platform-1940-families-sleeping-blankets-rows-tube-station}
Deep under the streets of London, there is the Underground. A great network of railway tunnels, with stations, deep below the ground. Some of the stations are thirty metres deep or more, reached by long escalators or lifts. And when the Blitz began, thousands of Londoners went down into the Underground, at night, to shelter from the bombs.

[long-pause]

At first, the government did not want people to use the Underground as a shelter. They were worried that people would never come out, and that the trains would not be able to run. But the people of London simply bought a platform ticket, for a penny and a half, and went down, and stayed. And there were so many of them that the government had to give in. Soon, about a hundred and seventy thousand people were sleeping in the Underground, every night.

[pause]

In the afternoon, people start to queue outside the stations, with their blankets, pillows, flasks and bags of food. When they are let in, they go down the escalators, and find a place on the platform, and spread out their blankets, to claim their space for the night. Some families have the same spot every night. Soon, the platforms are covered with people, lying side by side, all along the walls, from one end to the other.

[pause]

{img:shelterers-tube-platform-knitting-reading-children-sleeping-train-passing}
The trains still run, until late in the evening. The people on the platforms must leave a narrow space along the edge, about a metre wide, for the passengers getting on and off the trains. The trains roar in, and stop, and the doors open, and passengers step carefully over the sleeping people, and the trains roar away again. After the last train, at about half past ten, the electricity in the rails is switched off. And people sleep on the tracks too.

[pause]

Sometimes, June goes with her friends to sleep in the Underground, at the big station nearby, instead of the shelter in the garden. It is an adventure. The platform is crowded, noisy and warm. There are families with babies, old people, young couples, children running around. People play cards, knit, read, talk and sing. Someone has a mouth organ, and plays old songs. And everybody joins in.

[long-pause]

{img:volunteers-serving-tea-buns-from-trolley-tube-platform-night-shelterers}
Slowly, the Underground shelters become better organised. Wardens are appointed, to keep order. Bunk beds are put up on the platforms, in three layers, for thousands of people. Toilets are installed. First aid posts are set up, with nurses. And refreshment trains run, at night, along the tracks, stopping at each station, with volunteers handing out cups of tea, buns and cocoa, to the people sleeping there.

[pause]

Some stations even have little libraries, and entertainments. Concerts, by musicians and singers. Film shows. Classes, for children and adults. Some stations publish their own little newspapers, with news, jokes, and messages for the people who sleep there. People become friends with their neighbours on the platform, night after night. It becomes a little community, under the ground.

[pause]

{img:child-sleeping-on-platform-bunk-teddy-bear-tiled-wall-station-sign-dim}
The famous artist Henry Moore went down into the Underground, during the Blitz, and drew the people sleeping there. Rows of figures, wrapped in blankets, lying along the curved tunnels. Mothers holding their children. Old people sitting up, asleep. His drawings are some of the most famous pictures of the war. They show the stillness, the patience and the strange beauty of the sleeping crowds, deep under the city.

[long-pause]

June likes the Underground at night, when most people are asleep. The lights are dimmed. The platform is quiet, except for the sound of breathing, and an occasional snore, and a baby murmuring. The tunnel stretches away into the darkness. And far above, she knows, the city is waiting for the morning. She lies on her blanket, with her head on her pillow, looking at the curved tiled ceiling, and listening to the quiet. And she falls asleep, deep under London, among hundreds of strangers, who have become friends.

[long-pause]

{img:arp-warden-post-sandbags-wardens-helmets-tea-maps-telephone-night}
During the Blitz, there are many people who do not go to the shelters at night. Instead, they go out into the streets, to help. The wardens, the firemen, the ambulance drivers, the rescue teams, the nurses and the volunteers. Most of them are ordinary people, who do their normal jobs during the day, and volunteer at night. They are the quiet heroes of the Blitz.

[long-pause]

Bert is an ARP warden. ARP means Air Raid Precautions. There are hundreds of thousands of wardens, all over Britain. Most of them are volunteers, men and women, young and old. In London, there is a warden's post every few streets. It is a little room, protected by sandbags, with a telephone, a map of the area, a first aid box, and a kettle. Bert's post is in the basement of the old church, at the end of the street.

[pause]

The warden's job is to know his streets, and the people who live in them. Who lives in each house. Who is old, or ill, or alone, and might need help. Who sleeps in which shelter. During a raid, the wardens patrol their streets, in their tin helmets, with a whistle and a torch. They guide people to the shelters. They report any fires or damage by telephone. They help the injured. And they help the rescue teams to find anyone who needs help.

[pause]

{img:auxiliary-fire-service-firemen-hoses-water-glow-night-london-distance}
The firemen of London work all night, every night, during the Blitz. There are the regular firemen, and thousands of volunteers, in the Auxiliary Fire Service. Many of them are taxi drivers, shopkeepers, clerks and artists, who have joined for the war. They drive old taxis, painted grey, pulling fire pumps behind them. They are often wet, cold, exhausted and black with smoke. But they keep going, night after night. People call them heroes with grimy faces.

[pause]

There are also many women volunteers. In the Women's Voluntary Service, the WVS, hundreds of thousands of women help in every way they can. They run canteens, serving tea and food to the firemen and the wardens. They look after people who have lost their homes, finding them clothes, food and somewhere to stay. They help with the evacuation of children. They knit, sew, cook and organise. Peg joins the WVS, and helps in a canteen, two evenings a week.

[long-pause]

{img:wvs-women-volunteers-mobile-canteen-van-serving-tea-firemen-dawn}
The WVS canteens are often mobile. Little vans, which drive to wherever they are needed, during or after a raid. The ladies of the WVS, in their green uniforms and hats, hand out cups of strong, sweet tea and sandwiches to the firemen, the wardens, the rescue workers, and anyone else who needs them. A cup of tea, at three in the morning, after hours of work, is the best thing in the world, the firemen say.

[pause]

Peg works in a canteen in the church hall, near the warden's post. She makes tea, in a huge urn, and spreads margarine on hundreds of slices of bread, and serves the tired men and women who come in from the streets. She chats with them, and makes them laugh, and gives them extra sugar, when nobody is looking. The firemen call her Mum, although she is younger than some of them. She does not mind.

[pause]

{img:rescue-workers-resting-church-hall-tea-cups-blankets-tired-smiles}
There are also the ambulance drivers, many of them women, who drive through the dark streets, without lights, to take injured people to the hospitals. There are the nurses and doctors, who work all night in the hospitals. There are the rescue squads, strong men, often builders, who know how houses are made. And there are the messengers, boys on bicycles, some only fifteen or sixteen, who carry messages between the posts when the telephones do not work.

[long-pause]

Bert comes home every morning, after his night on duty, tired and dirty. He never talks much about what he has seen. But he talks about the people. The old lady who made tea for the firemen, in her nightdress. The boy messenger who rode his bicycle through the streets, all night. The dog who stayed beside its owner, until help came. He says that he has never been so proud of his neighbours. And then he washes, and eats his breakfast, and goes to sleep for a few hours, before his shift at the docks.

[long-pause]

{img:milkman-1940-delivering-bottles-crate-street-morning-calm-rubble-edges}
Every morning, after a night of raids, London wakes up. And the most remarkable thing about the Blitz is what happens next. People simply carry on. They come out of the shelters, brush the dust off their clothes, have a cup of tea, and go to work. As if nothing had happened. They do not give up. They do not panic. They just keep going.

[long-pause]

The milkman comes, every morning, as always, with his horse and cart, or his little electric float, delivering bottles of milk to every door. Even when the streets are full of broken glass, he picks his way through, carefully, and leaves the milk on the doorstep. There is a famous photograph, from October 1940, of a milkman, in his white coat, walking calmly through a street full of rubble, carrying a crate of milk bottles. It became one of the symbols of the Blitz.

[pause]

The postman comes too, with the letters. The newspaper boy, with the papers. The baker, with the bread. The buses run, as well as they can, on different routes, around the damaged streets. People walk to work, if the trains are not running, sometimes for hours. And they arrive at their offices, factories and shops, a little late, a little tired, but ready to work.

[pause]

{img:shop-window-boarded-up-sign-more-open-than-usual-shopkeeper-smiling-1940}
Shopkeepers whose windows have been blown out put up signs, with jokes. More open than usual, says one. Blasted but not beaten, says another. Business as usual, say hundreds of them. A barber's shop with no front wall puts up a sign saying, we have had a close shave, come and have one too. People laugh at the signs, and take photographs of them. Humour is one of the ways Londoners cope.

[pause]

Some days, there is damage in the streets. Broken glass, fallen bricks, water from broken pipes. The council workers come quickly, and clear the roads. The wardens put up ropes, around anything that is not safe. And the people step carefully around it, and carry on. Children collect pieces of shrapnel, little twisted bits of metal, and swap them at school, like marbles. The best collection in Lily Road belongs to the youngest Murphy boy.

[long-pause]

{img:office-workers-walking-to-work-london-street-1940-hats-umbrellas-determined}
June goes to school every morning, even after a night in the shelter. The school has a shelter too, in the basement. If the sirens sound during the day, the children go down, with their teachers, and carry on with their lessons by the light of lamps. They sing times tables, and spelling, and songs. The teachers are calm and cheerful. June says that she has learned more geography in the shelter than in the classroom, because the teacher brought a big map.

[pause]

Peg goes to the bakery in the afternoon. The bread must be baked, every day, raids or no raids. Mr Baker gets up at four in the morning, after a night in the shelter, and lights the oven, as he has done for forty years. And by seven o'clock, the smell of fresh bread is drifting along the street, as always. People say that it is the most comforting smell in the world, after a long night.

[pause]

{img:baker-pulling-fresh-loaves-from-oven-dawn-bakery-warm-light-1940}
Many people are very tired. They have not slept properly for weeks. They fall asleep on the buses, at their desks, in the queues. But they keep going. They go to the cinema in the afternoon, and laugh at the films. They go to the pub in the early evening, and sing around the piano. They play football in the parks, on Saturdays. They go to church on Sundays. They try to live as normally as possible. It is their way of saying to the enemy, you cannot beat us.

[long-pause]

Nan says that the people of the East End have been through hard times before. Poverty, and hunger, and disease, and the old war, in 1914. And they always came through. She says that they will come through this too. Because they have each other. And because they have tea. She says this every morning, with a cup of tea in her hand, sitting by the window, watching the street wake up. And every morning, June believes her.

[long-pause]

{img:king-george-vi-queen-elizabeth-visiting-east-end-street-1940-crowd-greeting}
During the Blitz, the king and queen of Britain do something that makes them very much loved. They stay in London. Many people expected them to go to the countryside, or even to Canada, where they would be safe. The government suggested that at least the two young princesses should go. But the queen refused. She said that the children would not go without her, she would not go without the king, and the king would never leave.

[long-pause]

The king is George the Sixth. He is forty-four years old, a shy, quiet, serious man, who never expected to be king. His older brother was king before him, but gave up the throne in 1936, to marry the woman he loved. So George became king, unexpectedly. He has a stammer, which makes it very hard for him to speak in public. But he works hard to overcome it, with the help of a speech teacher, and gives speeches on the radio, slowly and carefully.

[pause]

The queen is Elizabeth. She is forty, warm, friendly and cheerful, with a lovely smile. She always wears her best clothes when she visits bombed areas, in soft pastel colours, with a hat and pearls. Some people asked her why she did not wear something simpler. She said that the people would want to see her looking her best. Just as they would put on their best clothes, if they came to visit her.

[pause]

{img:queen-elizabeth-1940-pastel-coat-hat-pearls-talking-to-east-end-women-smiling}
The king and queen visit the East End many times, during the Blitz. They walk through the damaged streets, talking to the people, shaking hands, listening to their stories. They visit the shelters, the hospitals and the canteens. They talk to the wardens and the firemen. People crowd around them, and cheer, and call out to them. The queen talks to everyone, especially the women and children, and remembers their names.

[pause]

One day, in September, the king and queen come to Poplar, to visit the streets near the docks. The people of Lily Road hear that they are coming, and go to the end of the street to see them. Nan, who is seventy-five, insists on coming too, in her best coat and her hat with the feathers, leaning on June's arm. They stand in the crowd, waiting. And then, the king and queen walk past, slowly, talking to the people.

[long-pause]

{img:old-woman-shaking-hands-with-queen-street-crowd-1940-girl-beside-her}
The queen stops in front of Nan. She asks her how long she has lived in the East End. Nan says, all my life, Your Majesty, seventy-five years, in the same house. The queen smiles, and says that she must have seen many changes. Nan says yes, and that she has seen the old Queen Victoria too, once, when she was a little girl, in a carriage. The queen laughs, and shakes Nan's hand, and moves on. Nan does not stop talking about it for weeks.

[pause]

On the thirteenth of September, Buckingham Palace itself is damaged, in a raid. The king and queen are inside. They are not hurt. But part of the palace is damaged, including the chapel. The next day, the queen says something that becomes famous. She says that she is glad that the palace has been hit. Because now, she feels that she can look the East End in the face. The people of the East End hear this, and they love her for it.

[pause]

{img:buckingham-palace-1940-guard-calm-morning-flag-flying-gates-people}
The king also creates a new medal, during the Blitz. It is called the George Cross. It is given to ordinary people, not soldiers, for great bravery. Wardens, firemen, nurses, rescue workers and ordinary men and women, who risked their lives to save others. It is the highest award for bravery that a civilian can receive. Many of the first George Crosses are given to the heroes of the Blitz.

[long-pause]

The two princesses, Elizabeth and Margaret, stay at Windsor Castle, near London, for most of the war. In October 1940, the young Princess Elizabeth, who is fourteen, speaks on the radio for the first time. She speaks to the children of Britain, especially those who have been evacuated, far from their homes. She tells them to be brave, and that everything will be all right in the end. June listens to the broadcast, on the radio in the front room. And she thinks of Billy, in Devon, listening too.

[long-pause]

{img:rooftops-london-people-watching-sky-planes-contrails-september-blue-sky-1940}
While the Blitz is happening at night, the battle in the sky goes on during the day. The Battle of Britain. Every day, in the bright September sky, the young pilots of the Royal Air Force fly up to meet the German planes, over the south of England and over London itself. And from the streets and the rooftops, the people of London watch.

[long-pause]

The pilots of the RAF are very young. Many of them are only nineteen or twenty years old. They come from all over Britain, and from many other countries too. From Poland, Czechoslovakia, Canada, New Zealand, Australia, South Africa, France, Belgium, Ireland and even America. The Polish pilots are especially famous, for their bravery and their skill. They had already lost their own country, and were fighting to win it back.

[pause]

The pilots fly two kinds of planes. Hurricanes, which are strong and reliable. And Spitfires, which are fast, graceful and beautiful, with elliptical wings, like a bird. The people of Britain love the Spitfire. It becomes the symbol of the battle. Towns and villages all over Britain collect money to buy Spitfires, and some of them are named after the towns that paid for them.

[pause]

{img:rafpilots-relaxing-airfield-deckchairs-waiting-scramble-planes-behind}
Between flights, the pilots wait at their airfields, in the countryside, sitting in deckchairs on the grass, in the sun, in their flying jackets. They read, play cards, sleep, and talk. They try to relax. And then the telephone rings, and someone shouts, scramble! And they run to their planes, and climb in, and take off, and fly up into the sky, to meet the enemy. Sometimes several times a day.

[pause]

On Sunday, the fifteenth of September, 1940, there is the biggest battle of all. The Germans send hundreds of planes, in two great waves, to attack London. And the RAF sends up every plane it has. The battle is fought high over London and the south of England, all day long. From the streets, people watch the white trails in the sky, twisting and turning, and cheer when a plane falls, hoping that it is not one of theirs.

[long-pause]

{img:control-room-wooden-markers-map-table-women-plotters-1940-calm-focus}
Behind the pilots, there is a great organisation. Radar stations along the coast, which can see the enemy planes coming, from far away, using a new invention. Observers on hilltops and rooftops, watching the sky with binoculars. And control rooms, where young women of the Women's Auxiliary Air Force move little wooden markers around a great map table, with long sticks, showing where all the planes are. The officers above watch the map, and decide which squadrons to send up.

[pause]

On that day, the fifteenth of September, the prime minister, Winston Churchill, is in one of these control rooms, watching the battle on the map table. At one moment, he asks the commander how many planes he has in reserve. The commander says, none. Every single plane is in the air. Churchill is silent. But by the evening, the battle is won. The German planes have been driven back. And soon afterwards, the plans to invade Britain are put away.

[pause]

{img:sunset-over-kent-fields-quiet-airfield-planes-returning-golden-light}
Today, the fifteenth of September is remembered in Britain as Battle of Britain Day. And Churchill's words about the pilots, spoken a few weeks earlier, are still remembered. He said that never, in the field of human conflict, was so much owed by so many to so few. The few. That is what the pilots of the Battle of Britain are still called today. About three thousand young men, who defended their country in the sky.

[long-pause]

June and her friends sometimes stand on the roof of the bakery, in the afternoons, watching the sky. They cannot see much. Just little silver specks, very high up, and white trails, curving across the blue. But they know that up there, young men are fighting, to protect them. June thinks about them, at night, in the shelter. She says a little prayer for them, every night, before she goes to sleep. For the pilots. For her father, out in the streets. And for Billy, safe in Devon.

[long-pause]

{img:family-gathered-around-wooden-radio-set-evening-1940-listening-intently-lamp}
In 1940, the radio is the heart of every home. Most families in Britain have one. A big wooden box, with a round speaker covered in cloth, and two knobs, standing on a table or a shelf in the front room. It is called the wireless. And in wartime, it is more important than ever. It brings the news, the music, the comedy, and the voice of the prime minister, into every living room in the country.

[long-pause]

The radio in Britain is run by the BBC, the British Broadcasting Corporation. Its studios are in a big building in the middle of London, called Broadcasting House. All through the Blitz, the BBC keeps broadcasting, every day and every night. Even when Broadcasting House itself is damaged, in October 1940, in the middle of the nine o'clock news, the newsreader pauses for just a moment, and then carries on reading, calmly, as if nothing had happened.

[pause]

The most important programme is the news. At nine o'clock every evening, millions of families gather around their radios, to listen. The newsreaders tell them about the war. About the battles in the air, about the raids, about the fighting in Africa, about the ships at sea. The newsreaders always say their names, at the beginning of the news, so that people know it is really the BBC, and not an enemy pretending to be the BBC.

[pause]

{img:winston-churchill-speaking-microphone-1940-cigar-desk-determined-calm}
And sometimes, there is the prime minister, Winston Churchill. When he speaks on the radio, the whole country stops to listen. He has a deep, slow, growling voice, and he uses wonderful words. He tells the people the truth about how hard things are. But he also gives them courage and hope. He tells them that they will never give up. That they will fight on, whatever happens. And that one day, they will win.

[pause]

The Collins family listens to every one of Churchill's speeches. They sit in the front room, very still, with their tea going cold. Nan nods along, and says, that's right, that's right. Bert sits with his arms folded, looking at the floor. Peg wipes her eyes. And June listens, and feels the words go into her heart, like warm tea on a cold day. Afterwards, nobody says anything for a while. Then Peg says, well, and puts the kettle on.

[long-pause]

{img:dance-band-radio-studio-1940-musicians-singer-microphone-big-band}
But the radio is not only about the war. It is also about fun. There is dance music, played by big bands, with saxophones and trumpets, which people dance to in their front rooms, or in the shelters. There are popular singers, like Vera Lynn, a young woman from the East End, whose sweet, clear voice makes everyone think of their loved ones far away. They call her the forces' sweetheart. Her songs are about love, and hope, and seeing each other again.

[pause]

And there is comedy. The most popular comedy programme is called It's That Man Again, or ITMA. It is fast, silly and full of catchphrases, which everyone repeats. Children shout the catchphrases in the playground. Workers shout them in the factories. Even soldiers shout them in the trenches. It makes everyone laugh, even in the darkest days. And laughter is very important in wartime.

[pause]

{img:factory-workers-listening-radio-music-while-working-1940-women-machines-smiling}
There is a programme called Music While You Work, played in the factories twice a day, to help the workers keep going. Cheerful music, with a strong rhythm, so that the workers can work in time with it. There are programmes for children, with stories and songs. There are talks, about gardening, cooking, health and how to make the rations stretch. And on Sundays, there are church services, and hymns.

[long-pause]

The radio also brings people together. All over Britain, millions of people are listening to the same programmes, at the same moment. Laughing at the same jokes. Singing the same songs. Hearing the same news. It makes people feel that they are all in it together. In the shelter, at night, the Collins family takes a little battery radio with them, and listens to music, very softly, until they fall asleep. And the music floats out of the shelter, into the dark, quiet yard, under the stars.

[long-pause]

{img:st-pauls-cathedral-dome-london-1940-rising-above-smoke-skyline-symbol}
In the middle of the City of London, on top of a hill, stands St Paul's Cathedral. It is one of the most famous buildings in Britain. A great church of white stone, with a huge dome, built by the architect Christopher Wren, after the Great Fire of London, in 1666. For almost three hundred years, its dome has stood above the rooftops of London, like a great crown. And during the Blitz, it becomes a symbol of the city's courage.

[long-pause]

During the Blitz, St Paul's is protected by a group of volunteers, called the St Paul's Watch. They are architects, artists, clergymen, and members of the cathedral staff. Every night, they climb up onto the roof and the dome of the cathedral, and keep watch, in the dark. If a fire bomb lands on the roof, they put it out, quickly, with buckets of sand, stirrup pumps and water. They know every corner of the building, every gutter, every hidden space under the roof.

[pause]

It is dangerous and exhausting work. The roof is high, and steep, and dark. The wind is cold. The watchers must climb ladders and walk along narrow ledges, high above the ground. But they love the cathedral, and they are determined to save it. Night after night, through the whole of the Blitz, they keep watch. And thanks to them, the cathedral survives.

[pause]

{img:st-pauls-watch-volunteers-on-roof-night-helmets-buckets-sand-dome-behind}
On one night, at the end of December 1940, there is a great raid on the City of London. Thousands of fire bombs fall. Fires break out everywhere, all around the cathedral. The whole City seems to be burning. But the cathedral stands, in the middle of the flames and the smoke. The watchers work all night, putting out the fire bombs on the roof. And Churchill sends a message, saying that St Paul's must be saved, whatever happens.

[pause]

That night, a photographer climbs onto a roof nearby, and takes a famous photograph. It shows the great dome of St Paul's, rising out of the smoke, untouched, with the fires all around it. The photograph is printed in the newspapers the next day. And people all over Britain see it, and feel that if St Paul's can survive, then so can London. And so can they. It becomes one of the most famous photographs of the war.

[long-pause]

{img:dome-of-st-pauls-rising-through-smoke-night-silhouette-iconic-calm-glow}
Bert has a friend, an old architect, who is one of the St Paul's Watch. One night, when he is off duty, Bert goes with him to the cathedral, and climbs up to the Golden Gallery, at the very top of the dome, to see the view. It is a clear, quiet night. No raid. From up there, the whole of London is spread out below, dark and silent, under the stars. The river shines in the moonlight. The barrage balloons float in the sky, like silver fish.

[pause]

Bert stands at the rail, for a long time, looking at the city. His city. Where he was born, and his mother, and her mother. The docks, where he works. The East End, where he lives. The churches, the bridges, the markets, the streets. All dark, all quiet, all waiting. And he thinks that he has never loved it so much as now. He tells Peg about it, the next morning. She says that he must have been mad, to climb up there. But she smiles.

[pause]

{img:inside-st-pauls-nave-quiet-candles-people-praying-wartime-dim-light}
Inside the cathedral, during the Blitz, the services go on, every day, as they have for centuries. People come in from the streets, to pray, or to sit quietly for a while, in the great peaceful space, under the dome. The candles are lit. The choir sings. The organ plays. And for a little while, in the middle of the war, there is calm, and beauty, and peace.

[long-pause]

St Paul's was damaged twice during the war, by bombs. But it was never destroyed. When the war ended, it was still standing, with its great dome rising above the city, as it had for three hundred years. Today, it still stands. And in the cathedral, there is a memorial to the St Paul's Watch, and to the people of London who lost their lives in the war. People still come to see it, and to remember.

[long-pause]

{img:national-gallery-concert-1940-myra-hess-piano-audience-empty-frames-walls}
In the middle of London, on Trafalgar Square, there is the National Gallery, one of the greatest art museums in the world. At the beginning of the war, all its paintings were taken away, for safety. They were hidden in a slate mine, deep in the mountains of Wales. The great halls of the gallery were left empty. Bare walls, with nails where the pictures had hung. And then a woman had a wonderful idea.

[long-pause]

Her name was Myra Hess. She was a famous concert pianist, born in London, in a Jewish family. She thought that in wartime, with the theatres and concert halls closed, people needed music more than ever. Music to comfort them, to give them hope, and to remind them of beauty, in the middle of all the ugliness of war. So she asked if she could give concerts in the empty National Gallery. And the director agreed.

[pause]

The first concert was in October 1939. Myra Hess expected a few dozen people to come. Instead, about a thousand people came, and queued around Trafalgar Square, to get in. After that, there were concerts every weekday, at lunchtime, for the whole of the war. More than seventeen hundred concerts, in six and a half years. They never stopped. Not even during the worst days of the Blitz.

[pause]

{img:queue-of-londoners-outside-national-gallery-trafalgar-square-lunchtime-1940}
The concerts were short, about an hour, at lunchtime, so that office workers, shop girls, soldiers and anyone else could come in their lunch break. The tickets were cheap, only a shilling, so that everyone could afford them. People sat on chairs, or on the floor, or stood at the back. They brought their sandwiches. And in the café of the gallery, volunteers served sandwiches and cups of tea.

[pause]

And the music was the greatest music in the world. Bach, Mozart, Beethoven, Schubert, Brahms. Played by the best musicians in Britain, who all wanted to take part, for very little money. During the Blitz, if the sirens sounded during a concert, the musicians moved to a smaller room, in the basement, and carried on playing. And the audience followed them. Nobody wanted to miss the music.

[long-pause]

{img:office-workers-and-soldiers-listening-piano-concert-gallery-sandwiches-rapt}
One day in October, Peg takes June to a concert at the National Gallery. It is June's birthday present. They take the bus to Trafalgar Square, and queue outside, in the autumn sunshine. Inside, the great halls are bare. The walls are empty. The windows are boarded up. But in the middle of one hall, there is a grand piano. And at the piano sits Myra Hess herself, a woman of fifty, with dark hair, in a simple dress.

[pause]

She plays a piece by Bach. It is slow, gentle and calm, like water flowing, or like someone praying. June has never heard music like it. She sits very still, on her hard chair, among hundreds of people. Office workers in their suits. Soldiers in uniform. Old ladies with shopping bags. Firemen, who have come straight from their night's work. Nobody moves. Nobody coughs. Everyone is listening. And some of them are crying.

[pause]

{img:girl-and-mother-listening-concert-eyes-closed-sunlight-through-window}
After the concert, Peg and June walk out into Trafalgar Square. The sun is shining. The pigeons are flying around Nelson's Column. The buses are passing. The city looks almost normal. June does not say anything for a long time. Then she says that she did not know that music could make you feel like that. Peg puts her arm around her shoulders, and says that she did not know either, until today.

[long-pause]

In 1941, Myra Hess was made a Dame, one of the highest honours in Britain, for her concerts. People said that she had done as much for the spirit of London as anyone. The National Gallery concerts showed that, even in the darkest times, people need beauty. And that a piece of music, played on a piano, in an empty room, can give hope to a thousand people. June never forgot that afternoon. She went to concerts all her life. And whenever she heard Bach, she thought of the bare walls, and the boarded windows, and the quiet faces, listening.

[long-pause]

{img:boy-writing-letter-farmhouse-kitchen-table-devon-pencil-dog-at-feet}
Every week, a letter comes from Devon. From Billy. It arrives in a small brown envelope, addressed in his big, wobbly handwriting, to Mr and Mrs Collins, Lily Road, Poplar, London. And when it comes, the whole family stops what they are doing, and gathers around the kitchen table, and June reads it aloud.

[long-pause]

Billy writes about the farm. About the cows, and their names. Daisy, Buttercup, Primrose and Old Bess, who kicks. About Fly, the sheepdog, who has had puppies. About the pony, which he is learning to ride, and which he has fallen off three times. About Mr Penhale, who lets him drive the tractor, sitting on his knee. About Mrs Penhale's apple pies, which are the best in the world, he says. Peg pretends to be offended.

[pause]

He writes about the harvest. In September, everyone in the village helps with the harvest, even the evacuees. Billy helps to bring in the corn, and to stack the sheaves. He rides on top of the haycart, back to the farm, in the evening, singing. And there is a harvest festival, in the village church, with sheaves of corn, baskets of apples, marrows, potatoes and loaves of bread, decorated with flowers. And afterwards, a supper, with games and dancing, in the village hall.

[pause]

{img:devon-harvest-festival-village-church-sheaves-apples-marrows-children-singing}
He writes about the school. The village school is small, with just two classrooms, and two teachers. The village children and the evacuees sit together. At first, the village children made fun of the London accents. And the London children made fun of the country accents. But now they are friends. Billy's best friend is a farmer's son called Jack, who knows everything about birds, and can whistle like a blackbird.

[pause]

And he writes about the countryside. The hills, the woods, the streams. The blackberries in the hedges, which he and Jack pick, until their fingers are purple. The apples in the orchard, which they climb the trees to pick. The rabbits, the foxes, the badgers, the owls. The stars at night, which he says are much brighter than in London. He has never been so happy, he writes. But he misses them all, very much.

[long-pause]

{img:little-boy-picking-blackberries-hedgerow-devon-autumn-purple-fingers-friend}
He also asks questions. Are you all right? Is the house all right? Is Nan all right? Has the cat had her kittens yet? Have you been in the shelter? Is it scary? He has heard about the Blitz, on the wireless, and in the newspapers that Mr Penhale reads. He is worried about them. At the end of every letter, he writes, please write back soon, and stay safe, and give the cat a stroke from me.

[pause]

The family writes back, every week. June writes the letters, because her handwriting is the best. She tells Billy about the street, and the neighbours, and the cat's kittens, five of them, all different colours. About Nan meeting the queen. About the concert. About the bakery. She does not tell him about the bad nights. She tells him that they are all fine, and that the shelter is quite cosy, and that the tea is still good. Peg adds a few lines at the end. And Bert draws a little picture, of a horse, for Billy.

[pause]

{img:girl-writing-letter-kitchen-table-lamp-family-adding-notes-drawings}
Sometimes, Mrs Penhale writes too. A short letter, in neat, careful handwriting. She tells Peg that Billy is well, and eating well, and growing. That he is a good boy, helpful and cheerful. That he is no trouble at all. And that she and her husband have come to love him very much. Peg reads these letters many times, and keeps them in a tin box, with Billy's letters, on the mantelpiece.

[long-pause]

Peg misses Billy every day. Sometimes, at night, in the shelter, she lies awake, thinking of him, in his warm bed in the farmhouse, in the quiet Devon valley, with the dog at his feet, and the owls outside, and the stars over the hills. And she is glad that he is there, and not here. It is hard, to be apart. But he is safe. And one day, when all this is over, he will come home. And she will hold him, and never let him go again.

[long-pause]

{img:neighbours-helping-carry-furniture-street-1940-community-together-smiling}
During the Blitz, the people of the East End help each other, more than ever. In Lily Road, the neighbours were always close. But now, they become like one big family. They share everything. Their shelters, their food, their tea, their homes, and their courage. This spirit, of helping each other, of not giving up, of laughing together in hard times, became famous. People called it the Blitz spirit.

[long-pause]

When a family loses their home, the neighbours take them in. When the Murphys come back from hop-picking, in late September, they find that a house at the end of their street has been damaged, and that the family who lived there, an old couple called the Greens, have nowhere to go. So the Murphys take them in, even though they already have seven people in a small house. The Greens sleep in the front room, on the sofa and the floor. Nobody complains.

[pause]

The Cohens, the old Jewish couple next door, have a tailor's shop. When people lose their clothes, Mr Cohen mends and alters old clothes for them, for nothing. He works late into the night, by the light of a little lamp, with his needle and thread. Mrs Cohen makes chicken soup, which she says is the best medicine in the world, and takes it round to anyone who is ill, or sad, or tired.

[pause]

{img:old-tailor-mending-clothes-shop-lamp-night-needle-thread-kindly}
Mr Ali, the sailor from India who runs the café across the street, keeps his café open all night, during the raids. He makes tea and sandwiches for the wardens and the firemen, and for anyone who needs them. He gives them away for free, to anyone who cannot pay. His café becomes a meeting place for the whole street. People come in, at all hours, for a cup of tea and a chat, and to forget the war for a few minutes.

[pause]

And the children help too. They collect wood, for the fires. They carry messages. They help old people to the shelters. They run errands for the wardens. They look after the younger children, when the mothers are busy. June helps old Mrs Green, who is almost blind, to find her way to the shelter every night. She holds her arm, and guides her down the dark street, and finds her a comfortable seat. Mrs Green calls her an angel.

[long-pause]

{img:girl-guiding-elderly-blind-woman-arm-dark-street-shelter-torch-gentle}
People share whatever they have. If one family has some extra sugar, they share it. If another has some eggs from their chickens, they share those. If someone gets a parcel of food from relatives in the country, they share it with the whole street. When Billy sends a parcel from Devon, with apples, a pot of honey and a pound of butter from the farm, Peg divides it among all the neighbours. Everyone gets an apple and a spoonful of honey.

[pause]

There are also street parties, sometimes, on Sundays, when there has been a quiet night. Tables are put out in the street, with whatever food people can find. Someone brings a piano out of the house, onto the pavement. And everyone sings, and dances, and laughs, for an afternoon. The children run around, and the old people sit on chairs in the sun. It is a way of saying, we are still here. We are still together. And we are still smiling.

[pause]

{img:street-party-1940-tables-in-road-piano-pavement-singing-flags-sunday}
Of course, not everyone was always kind. There were some people who stole, or cheated, or behaved badly. There were quarrels and arguments, as there always are. Some people were very frightened, and some were very angry. And later, historians said that the Blitz spirit was sometimes exaggerated, in the newspapers and the films. But most people, most of the time, did their best. And many people were kinder and braver than they had ever been before.

[long-pause]

Nan says that the war has shown people what is really important. Not money, or things, or houses. But each other. She has lived in Lily Road for seventy-five years, and she has never seen the neighbours so close. She says that when the war is over, she hopes they will remember it. That they will go on helping each other, and sharing, and laughing together. June hopes so too. She thinks that, in spite of everything, she will remember these months, in Lily Road, for the rest of her life.

[long-pause]

{img:london-autumn-1940-park-golden-leaves-barrage-balloon-children-kicking-leaves}
September turns into October. The leaves on the trees in the parks turn yellow and gold, and begin to fall. The evenings get darker earlier. The air gets colder. The mornings are misty. And the raids go on, night after night. But the people of London have learned to live with them, in a way. They have their routines. Their shelters. Their tea. Their jokes. And they carry on.

[long-pause]

In the autumn, there are small pleasures. Conkers, the shiny brown seeds of the horse chestnut trees, which the children collect in the parks, and thread on strings, and play with, trying to break each other's conkers. Bonfires of leaves, in the allotments. Roasted chestnuts, sold from little stoves on the street corners, hot and sweet, in paper bags. The smell of woodsmoke. And the first frost, on the roofs of the Anderson shelters.

[pause]

Bert's potatoes are ready to dig up, in the back yard. A good crop, considering the small patch. He and June dig them up together, on a Sunday morning, and put them in a sack in the coal shed. Nan says that they are the best potatoes she has ever tasted. Peg makes a big pot of potato and onion soup, and invites the neighbours. Everyone squeezes into the little kitchen, and eats soup, and talks, and laughs.

[pause]

{img:father-and-daughter-digging-potatoes-back-yard-autumn-sunday-sack}
In October, Bert gets a few days' leave from the docks. And he and Peg do something they have been wanting to do for months. They go to Devon, to visit Billy. They take the train from Paddington station, early in the morning, and travel all day, through the countryside, which is golden and red with autumn. June stays at home, with Nan, to help in the bakery. But she sends Billy a long letter, and a drawing of the cat's kittens.

[pause]

When they arrive at the farm, in the evening, Billy is waiting at the gate, with Fly, the sheepdog. He has grown so much that Peg hardly recognises him. He is brown from the sun, and rosy, and strong. He runs to his mother, and she catches him, and holds him, and cries, and laughs, all at once. And Bert stands beside them, with his hand on his son's head, and does not say anything at all.

[long-pause]

{img:parents-visiting-son-devon-farm-gate-reunion-sheepdog-autumn-evening}
They stay at the farm for three days. Billy shows them everything. The cows, the pony, the puppies, the orchard, the stream, the woods, the hill behind the farm, from which you can see the sea. Mr and Mrs Penhale are kind and welcoming. They feed them enormous meals, of roast chicken, vegetables from the garden, and apple pie with cream. Bert and Mr Penhale talk about the war, by the fire, in the evenings, and find that they agree about almost everything.

[pause]

It is very quiet in Devon. No sirens. No planes. No shelters. Peg and Bert sleep in a soft bed, under a thick quilt, in a room with a window looking out over the hills. On the first night, they wake up, out of habit, at the time when the sirens usually sound. And they lie there, in the silence, listening to an owl, and the wind, and the stream. And they cannot believe how quiet it is.

[pause]

{img:quiet-devon-valley-night-farmhouse-window-light-owl-moon-peaceful}
On the last day, they ask Billy if he wants to come home with them. Billy thinks about it, very seriously. Then he says that he misses them very much, and Nan, and June, and the cat. But that he would like to stay at the farm, until the war is over. Because Mr Penhale needs him, for the harvest next year. And because the puppies need him. Peg looks at Mrs Penhale. And Mrs Penhale nods, and says that he is welcome to stay as long as he likes.

[long-pause]

On the train back to London, Peg looks out of the window, at the fields and the woods, golden in the evening light, and thinks about her son. She is sad, to leave him. But she is glad that he is happy, and safe, and loved. She holds Bert's hand. And as the train comes into London, in the dark, and they hear the sirens sounding, far away, she thinks of Billy, asleep in the farmhouse in Devon, with the dog at his feet. And she smiles.

[long-pause]

{img:london-may-1941-last-raid-dawn-quiet-city-st-pauls-dome-later}
The Blitz went on, all through the autumn and winter of 1940, and into the spring of 1941. London was raided on fifty-seven nights in a row, in September and October. And then, often, for months after that. Other cities were raided too. Coventry, Birmingham, Liverpool, Manchester, Bristol, Glasgow, Belfast, Plymouth and many more. The people of all these cities showed the same courage, and the same spirit.

[long-pause]

The last great raid on London came in May 1941. After that, the German planes turned east, toward Russia, and the Blitz came to an end. It had lasted for eight months. In that time, many thousands of people in London had lost their lives, and more than a million homes had been damaged. We remember them, gently. But London had not given up. And neither had Britain.

[pause]

The war went on for four more years. There were more raids, later in the war, and new and strange weapons. But there was never anything quite like the Blitz again. And in May 1945, at last, the war in Europe ended. People danced in the streets of London, all night long. The church bells rang. The lights came on again, after almost six years of blackout. And the children came home.

[pause]

{img:children-returning-railway-station-1945-parents-embracing-joy-crowd}
Billy came home in the summer of 1945. He was twelve years old, tall and brown, with a Devon accent. He stepped off the train at Paddington station, with a suitcase, a fishing rod and a puppy, a gift from the Penhales. Peg was waiting on the platform. She held him for so long that the puppy got impatient, and barked. Billy stayed friends with the Penhales all his life. He visited them every summer. And when he grew up, he became a farmer himself.

[pause]

After the war, the East End was rebuilt. Many of the old streets were gone. New houses and blocks of flats were built in their place. Many families moved out of London, to new towns in the countryside. The old community of the East End changed. Some people missed the old days, the old streets and the old neighbours. Others were glad of the new houses, with bathrooms and gardens and hot water.

[long-pause]

{img:london-rebuilding-1950s-new-flats-cranes-workers-hopeful-sunny-later}
The docks of the East End went on working for many years after the war. Bert worked as a crane driver until he retired. But slowly, the ships became too big for the old docks, and moved to new ports, down the river. By the 1980s, the docks were empty. And then they were rebuilt, as offices, flats, shops and a new business district, with tall glass towers, called Canary Wharf. Today, it is one of the busiest places in London.

[pause]

Nan lived to be ninety. She saw the end of the war, and the coronation of the new queen, the young Princess Elizabeth, who had spoken on the radio to the children in 1940. She watched it on a little black and white television, with all the neighbours crowded into her front room. She said it was the second time she had seen a queen. And this one, she said, was just as nice as the last one.

[pause]

{img:old-woman-watching-coronation-tv-1953-neighbours-crowded-front-room-later}
June grew up to be a teacher. She taught in a school in the East End for forty years. She told her pupils about the Blitz, every year. About the shelter, the Underground, the radio, the concerts, the queen, and the neighbours. Not about the danger. About the people. About how they helped each other, and laughed, and kept going. She said that it was the most important lesson she ever taught.

[long-pause]

The Blitz spirit became one of the great stories of British history. Of ordinary people, who faced terrible danger with courage, humour and kindness. Of keeping calm, and carrying on. Historians say that the story was sometimes made to sound simpler and braver than it really was. That people were also frightened, and tired, and angry. But it was also true. And it still inspires people today, whenever they face hard times together.

[long-pause]

{img:churchill-war-rooms-today-underground-map-room-desks-telephones-museum}
Today, London is one of the greatest cities in the world, with almost nine million people. Most of the damage of the Blitz has long been repaired. New buildings stand where old ones were lost. But if you know where to look, you can still find traces of 1940, all over the city.

[long-pause]

Under the streets of Westminster, near the Houses of Parliament, there are the Churchill War Rooms. A secret underground headquarters, where Churchill and his government worked during the war. Today, it is a museum. You can walk through the narrow corridors, and see the map room, with its maps still pinned to the walls, and the coloured pins showing where the ships and armies were. The telephones. The desks. Churchill's bedroom. Everything as it was left, on the day the war ended.

[pause]

St Paul's Cathedral still stands on its hill, with its great dome rising above the City. Inside, there is a memorial to the people of London who lost their lives in the Blitz. And there is the American Memorial Chapel, built after the war, to remember the Americans who died helping Britain. Visitors can climb up to the Golden Gallery, at the top of the dome, as Bert did, and look out over the whole of London.

[pause]

{img:st-pauls-golden-gallery-view-today-london-skyline-river-modern-towers}
The Underground still runs, every day, carrying millions of people. Some of the stations where people sheltered during the Blitz are still in use. And if you look at the platforms, and the curved tiled walls, you can imagine the rows of people, lying on their blankets, all night, during the raids. In some stations, there are little plaques, remembering them.

[pause]

In the Imperial War Museum, in south London, there are many things from the Blitz. An Anderson shelter, which you can go inside. Gas masks, ration books, wardens' helmets and armbands. The drawings of Henry Moore, of the people sleeping in the Underground. Photographs, letters and diaries. And recordings of people who lived through the Blitz, telling their stories, in their own voices.

[long-pause]

{img:imperial-war-museum-anderson-shelter-display-visitors-today-gas-masks}
In the East End, a few of the old streets of terraced houses still stand, like Lily Road. Some of them have been lovingly restored. In Columbia Road, on Sunday mornings, there is a famous flower market, where the street fills with flowers and plants, and the stall holders call out their prices, just as they did a hundred years ago. And in the little pubs on the corners, people still sing around the piano, sometimes, on a Saturday night.

[pause]

The National Gallery is still on Trafalgar Square, full of paintings again. There is a little plaque, remembering Myra Hess and her lunchtime concerts. And there are still free lunchtime concerts in London, in churches and halls, all over the city, carrying on the tradition. People still come in their lunch breaks, with their sandwiches, to listen to Bach and Mozart, in the middle of the busy city.

[pause]

{img:lunchtime-concert-london-church-today-office-workers-listening-pianist}
And every year, on the fifteenth of September, Battle of Britain Day is remembered. In the sky over London, sometimes, there is a flypast. Old Spitfires and Hurricanes, beautifully restored, flying low over the city, with the sound of their engines, deep and powerful, rolling over the rooftops. People stop in the streets, and look up, and some of them have tears in their eyes.

[long-pause]

And in many families in London, the stories are still told. By grandparents and great-grandparents, who remember. About the shelters, and the Underground, and the cups of tea, and the neighbours who helped each other. About the milkman, and the queen, and the concerts. About the long, dark autumn of 1940, when the city did not give up. The people who lived through it are very old now. But their stories live on.

[long-pause]

{img:east-end-street-dusk-1940-blackout-curtains-being-drawn-last-light-calm}
So we leave London tonight, in the autumn of 1940, as the evening falls. The sun is setting behind the rooftops and the cranes of the docks. The sky is turning pink and grey. The barrage balloons are floating high above the city, silver in the last light. And in Lily Road, the blackout curtains are being drawn, one by one, across every window.

[long-pause]

In the house of the Collins family, the kettle is on. Peg is making cocoa, in a big jug, for the shelter. Nan is putting her knitting into her bag. June is finding her book and her pillow. The cat is waiting by the back door, ready to go. And Bert is putting on his helmet and his armband, and kissing his wife and his daughter and his mother, before he goes out to his post, for the night.

They walk down the little garden, to the shelter. The flowers on its roof are still blooming, a few late marigolds, orange in the dusk. They climb inside, and light the little lamp, and settle down on the bunks, with their blankets. Peg pours the cocoa. Nan starts to knit. June opens her book. The cat curls up on the bottom bunk. And the little shelter is warm and cosy, in the gathering dark.

Next door, the Cohens are settling down in their shelter too. And across the street, the light of Mr Ali's café is glowing, very faintly, behind its blackout curtain, where the wardens will come for tea, later in the night. In the church hall, the volunteers are filling the tea urn. And deep under the city, in the stations of the Underground, thousands of people are spreading out their blankets, on the platforms, and saying goodnight to their neighbours.

Far away, in Devon, in a farmhouse in a quiet valley, a little boy is asleep, in a warm bed, with a sheepdog at his feet. The owls are calling in the woods. The stars are shining over the hills. In the shelter, June closes her book, and lies down, and pulls the blanket up to her chin. She listens to the sounds of the night. Nan's knitting needles, clicking softly. Her mother's quiet breathing. The cat's purring. And she thinks of her father, out in the dark streets, and her brother, safe in Devon, and the music she heard at the gallery. And she says a little prayer, for all of them. And then, slowly, in the warm, small, lamp-lit shelter, at the bottom of the garden, she falls asleep.
