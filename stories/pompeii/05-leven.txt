{img:stabian-baths}
Towards the end of the morning, many people made their way to the baths.

[pause]

For the Romans, going to the baths was not just about getting clean.

[pause]

It was a daily ritual, a chance to relax, and to meet friends, and to hear the latest gossip.

[long-pause]

Pompeii had several public bath houses.

[pause]

The oldest and largest were the Stabian Baths, near the centre of town.

[long-pause]

Visitors entered through a courtyard, surrounded by columns, where men exercised in the open air.

[pause]

Some lifted weights.

[pause]

Some wrestled.

[pause]

Some played ball games, or simply walked up and down, talking.

[pause]

There was even a swimming pool.

[long-pause]

Then came the bath rooms themselves, one after another.

[pause]

First, the changing room, with little niches in the walls where you could leave your clothes.

[pause]

Then the warm room, gently heated, where you could sit and let your body relax.

[pause]

And then the hot room, full of steam, where the air was thick and heavy and the walls dripped with moisture.

[long-pause]

The heat came from furnaces beneath the floor.

[pause]

Hot air flowed through spaces under the floor and inside the walls, warming the rooms from all sides.

[pause]

The floors could get so hot, that bathers wore wooden sandals to protect their feet.

[long-pause]

After the hot room, you might plunge into a pool of cold water, and gasp, and laugh, and feel wide awake again.

[pause]

Then an attendant would rub your skin with oil, and scrape it clean with a curved metal tool.

[pause]

There was no soap, as we know it.

[pause]

Just oil, and warm water, and a good scrape.

[long-pause]

The ceilings of the bath rooms were beautifully decorated, with delicate plaster patterns and painted stars.

[pause]

Women had their own separate rooms, with their own entrance, where they could bathe in peace.

[pause]

And all around, the sound of water, and voices echoing softly off the curved ceilings.

[long-pause]

{img:fullery-workers}
Not everyone had time for the baths, of course.

[pause]

For many people in Pompeii, the day was full of work.

[long-pause]

In the fulleries, workers cleaned and finished woollen cloth.

[pause]

They stood in large tubs, treading the cloth with their feet, in water mixed with special clays.

[pause]

Then the cloth was rinsed, and brushed, and stretched out to dry.

[pause]

Pompeii's wool trade was important, and one of the grandest buildings in the forum was paid for by a wealthy woman named Eumachia, who was the patron of the wool workers.

[long-pause]

There were potters, shaping clay jars on their wheels.

[pause]

There were metal workers, and stone cutters, and carpenters.

[pause]

There were dyers, whose hands were stained with bright colours.

[pause]

And there were painters, climbing up ladders in half finished rooms, carefully adding the last touches to a wall.

[long-pause]

In one house, archaeologists found a room where painters had been working that very day.

[pause]

Their pots of paint were still there, with the colours ready to use.

[pause]

Part of the wall was finished.

[pause]

Part was only sketched in.

[pause]

It looks as if the painters simply put down their brushes, and stepped out for a moment.

[long-pause]

{img:banker-wax-tablets}
Near the forum lived a banker named Lucius Caecilius Iucundus.

[pause]

His house was comfortable, with a pretty garden and fine paintings.

[pause]

In the rooms upstairs, he kept his business records.

[long-pause]

They were written on wax tablets, small wooden boards coated with a thin layer of wax, which you could write on with a pointed stick.

[pause]

Hundreds of them have survived.

[pause]

They record loans, and payments, and sales at auction.

[pause]

The sale of a mule.

[pause]

The rent of a piece of land.

[pause]

A payment to the town council.

[long-pause]

They are, in a way, rather ordinary documents.

[pause]

Receipts, and contracts, and signatures.

[pause]

But they tell us how the people of Pompeii bought and sold, borrowed and repaid, trusted each other, and sometimes didn't.

[long-pause]

{img:school-children}
Somewhere in town, a group of children was having a lesson.

[pause]

There were no school buildings as such.

[pause]

Often a teacher simply gathered his pupils under a covered walkway, or in a corner of the forum.

[long-pause]

The children sat on benches, holding their wax tablets on their knees.

[pause]

They practised their letters, and learned to count, and recited lines of poetry.

[pause]

Especially the great poem of Virgil, about the wanderings of the hero Aeneas.

[long-pause]

We know they learned it, because lines from Virgil are scratched all over the walls of Pompeii.

[pause]

Often only the very first line, as if a child had started to write it, and then got bored, and ran off to play.

[long-pause]

Children in Pompeii played many of the same games children play today.

[pause]

They had dolls, and marbles made of glass, and little wooden toys.

[pause]

They played with hoops, and balls, and knucklebones.

[pause]

And they drew pictures on the walls, little stick figures, and animals, and gladiators, low down, at exactly the height a child could reach.

[long-pause]

{img:amphitheatre}
At the eastern edge of town stood the amphitheatre.

[pause]

It was the oldest stone amphitheatre in the Roman world that still survives today, built more than a century before our story.

[pause]

Its oval arena was surrounded by rows of stone seats, rising steeply on every side.

[pause]

It could hold perhaps twenty thousand people, more than the entire population of the town.

[long-pause]

On the days of the games, people came from all the towns around.

[pause]

Awnings were stretched over the seats, to shade the crowd from the sun.

[pause]

Sellers walked up and down, offering drinks and snacks.

[pause]

And the gladiators became famous, their names painted on the walls, their fans cheering them on.

[long-pause]

Twenty years before our story, there had been a famous riot in this amphitheatre.

[pause]

During the games, a quarrel broke out between the people of Pompeii and visitors from the neighbouring town of Nuceria.

[pause]

Insults turned into stones, and stones turned into fighting.

[pause]

The news reached Rome, and the emperor's government banned games in Pompeii for ten years.

[pause]

A painting of the riot was found in a house nearby, showing the crowd spilling out of the amphitheatre.

[long-pause]

But that was long ago.

[pause]

By the time of our story, the games had returned, and the amphitheatre was quiet between shows.

[pause]

In the grassy space beside it, there was a large exercise ground, with a swimming pool, surrounded by plane trees.

[pause]

Young men trained there, and ran, and swam, and rested in the shade.

[long-pause]

{img:theatre-stage}
Not far away were the theatres.

[pause]

A large open air theatre, carved into the slope of the hill, where comedies and tragedies were performed.

[pause]

And a smaller covered theatre, for music and poetry.

[long-pause]

Actors wore masks, with exaggerated faces, happy or sad, young or old, so that everyone in the audience could see who they were supposed to be.

[pause]

Some performers were so popular, that their fans wrote their names on the walls, along with little messages of praise.

[long-pause]

On this ordinary day, there may not have been any show at all.

[pause]

The stage stood empty in the sunshine, and the stone seats were warm to the touch.

[pause]

A few birds hopped along the rows, looking for crumbs.

[long-pause]

Midday was approaching.

[pause]

In the streets, people began to think about lunch.

[pause]

At the food counters, the jars were filled with fresh stew.

[pause]

In the houses, tables were laid with bread and cheese, and olives, and fruit.

[long-pause]

It was, by every sign, a perfectly ordinary day.
