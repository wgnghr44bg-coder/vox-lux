The first discovery came by accident.

[pause]

In the early seventeen hundreds, a farmer near the town of Resina was digging a well.

[pause]

Deep down, he struck something hard.

[pause]

Not rock, but marble.

[pause]

Finely cut, polished marble.

[long-pause]

Word spread, and before long, treasure hunters arrived.

[pause]

They dug tunnels down from the well, and pulled out statues, and columns, and pieces of coloured stone.

[pause]

They didn't know it yet, but they had found the theatre of Herculaneum.

[long-pause]

A few decades later, the King of Naples ordered proper excavations.

[pause]

Workers dug long, narrow tunnels deep underground, working by candlelight in the damp and the dust.

[pause]

It was slow, difficult, dangerous work.

[pause]

And in those dark tunnels, they found rooms, and paintings, and statues, and more.

[long-pause]

{img:first-excavations-pompeii}
Then, in the year seventeen forty eight, the diggers turned their attention to the fields of La Civita.

[pause]

Here, the ground was much easier to dig.

[pause]

Not hard volcanic rock, as at Herculaneum, but soft ash and pumice.

[pause]

And almost immediately, they began to find things.

[long-pause]

At first, they thought they had found an ancient town called Stabiae.

[pause]

It was only after many years of digging, in seventeen sixty three, that they found an inscription carved in stone, which mentioned the town council of the Pompeians.

[pause]

And at last, the buried city had its name again.

[pause]

Pompeii.

[long-pause]

{img:villa-papyri-scrolls}
Around the same time, digging in the tunnels at Herculaneum, workers broke into the great villa outside the town.

[pause]

The one with the long gardens, and the bronze statues, and the library.

[long-pause]

In one of its rooms, they found lumps of something black, rather like pieces of burnt wood, or coal.

[pause]

Some workers threw them away.

[pause]

Others used them as torches.

[pause]

Until someone looked more closely, and noticed faint traces of writing.

[long-pause]

They were scrolls.

[pause]

Hundreds of them.

[pause]

Carbonised by the heat, rolled up tight, fragile as ash.

[pause]

The only library to survive intact from the ancient world.

[long-pause]

For a long time, nobody knew how to open them without destroying them.

[pause]

A patient priest named Antonio Piaggio invented a machine, with silk threads and tiny weights, that slowly unrolled a scroll, a fraction at a time.

[pause]

It could take years to open just one.

[pause]

And many were too damaged to read at all.

[pause]

But we will come back to those scrolls, one more time, before the end.

[long-pause]

{img:grand-tour-visitors}
News of the discoveries spread across Europe.

[pause]

And soon, visitors began to arrive.

[long-pause]

In those days, young men and women from wealthy families often made a long journey through Europe, to complete their education.

[pause]

They called it the Grand Tour.

[pause]

And Pompeii quickly became one of its most important stops.

[long-pause]

Artists came, to sketch the ruins and copy the paintings.

[pause]

Writers came, to walk the empty streets and imagine the lives of the people who had lived there.

[pause]

A young composer named Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart visited as a boy, with his father.

[pause]

Some say that the Temple of Isis, which he saw there, inspired a scene in one of his operas many years later.

[long-pause]

The German poet Goethe visited too, in the year seventeen eighty seven.

[pause]

He wrote that there had been many disasters in the world, but few that had given so much pleasure to those who came afterwards.

[pause]

It sounds a little strange.

[pause]

But he meant that, through its loss, Pompeii had given the world a precious gift.

[pause]

A window into the past.

[long-pause]

{img:fiorelli-excavation}
For a long time, the excavations were rather disorganised.

[pause]

Diggers went looking for treasures, statues, and paintings, and often didn't record where they found them.

[pause]

Beautiful paintings were cut out of the walls and carried away to museums.

[pause]

Much was lost.

[long-pause]

That began to change in the eighteen sixties, when a young archaeologist named Giuseppe Fiorelli took charge.

[pause]

Fiorelli was careful, and patient, and methodical.

[long-pause]

He divided the town into regions, and blocks, and houses, and gave each one a number.

[pause]

So that everything found could be recorded precisely, in its place.

[pause]

He had the ash removed layer by layer, from the top down, instead of tunnelling in from the side.

[pause]

And he wanted to understand, not only the treasures, but everything.

[pause]

How people cooked.

[pause]

How they worked.

[pause]

How they lived.

[long-pause]

Fiorelli also noticed that, in the hardened ash, there were sometimes hollow spaces.

[pause]

Empty shapes, where something had once been, and had slowly disappeared over the centuries.

[pause]

A wooden door.

[pause]

A piece of furniture.

[pause]

The roots of a tree in a garden.

[long-pause]

He had the idea of carefully pouring liquid plaster into these hollows, and letting it set.

[pause]

When the ash was cleared away, the plaster showed the exact shape of what had once been there.

[pause]

A wooden cupboard, with its doors and shelves.

[pause]

A garden bed, with the roots of its plants.

[pause]

A shutter, from a shop along the street.

[long-pause]

In some of those hollows, he found the shapes of people, too.

[pause]

Those who had stayed behind.

[pause]

Quiet and still, just as the ash had held them.

[pause]

Today, these casts are treated with great care and respect, as the last traces of real people, who once walked these streets, and laughed, and loved, and hoped for a long life.

[pause]

In recent years, scientists have been studying them gently, to learn who these people were, and where their families had come from.

[pause]

Not to disturb their rest, but to remember them.

[long-pause]

{img:excavated-street-sunlight}
Slowly, over the years, the streets of Pompeii came back into the light.

[pause]

The forum, with its temples.

[pause]

The theatres, and the amphitheatre.

[pause]

The bakeries, with their ovens and mills.

[pause]

The houses, one after another, with their painted walls and their mosaics and their gardens.

[long-pause]

It was like opening a book that had been closed for eighteen hundred years.

[pause]

And finding every page still there.
