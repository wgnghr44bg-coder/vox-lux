Beside the Thames, in the middle of London, there is a long, wide, new road, along the river: the Victoria Embankment.

It runs from Westminster, to Blackfriars, with a wide pavement, and plane trees, and a granite wall, along the river, decorated with lamps, on iron posts, with dolphins twisting around them. And beside it, there are gardens, with flowers, and lawns, and statues.

--

It looks simple. But under it, there is one of the great wonders of Victorian London.

Thirty years before, in the hot summer of 1858, there was the Great Stink. All the sewage of London, from millions of people, flowed straight into the Thames, and the river was so dirty, and smelled so terrible, in the heat, that the Members of Parliament, in the Houses of Parliament, beside the river, could hardly bear it. They hung curtains, soaked in lime, at the windows. And at last, they decided that something must be done.

{img:bazalgette-engineer-inspecting-brick-sewer-tunnel-workers-lamps}
The man they chose to do it was an engineer, called Joseph Bazalgette. He was a small, quiet man, with a big moustache, and he planned a great system of sewers, under the whole of London: more than a hundred and thirty kilometres of great brick tunnels, and more than eighteen hundred kilometres of smaller street sewers, carrying the sewage away, from the city, to the east, far down the river, beyond the houses.

--

It took nearly twenty years to build, and used more than three hundred million bricks. Part of it was built under the new Embankment, along the river, with the underground railway, and pipes for gas, and water, all hidden beneath the road, and the gardens.

There are great pumping stations, to lift the sewage, along the way. And they are beautiful, like palaces, or churches. At Crossness, beside the river, the pumping station is decorated, inside, with wonderful ironwork, painted in red, and green, and gold, like a cathedral. Visitors call it the cathedral on the marsh.

{img:crossness-pumping-station-ornate-painted-ironwork-beam-engines}
Bazalgette's sewers changed London. The terrible outbreaks of cholera, which had killed thousands of people, again and again, stopped. The river became cleaner. And the city became healthier, and could grow even more.

--

Bazalgette made his sewers bigger than they needed to be, at the time, because, he said, we will only do this once, and we must think of the future. Because of this, his sewers are still used, today, more than a hundred and fifty years later, by a city of nearly nine million people.

There is a monument to him, on the Embankment, with a Latin inscription, which means, he put the river in chains. And the Embankment, with its plane trees, and its dolphin lamps, is still one of the loveliest places in London, to walk, in the evening, beside the river.
