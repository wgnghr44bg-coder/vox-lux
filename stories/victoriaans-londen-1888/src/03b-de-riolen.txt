Beside the Thames, in the middle of London, there is a long wide new road along the river. It is called the Embankment.

It has a wide pavement and plane trees. A granite wall runs along the river. On the wall stand lamps on iron posts, with dolphins twisting around them. Beside the road there are gardens with flowers and lawns and statues.

--

It looks simple. But underneath it is one of the great wonders of Victorian London.

Thirty years ago, there was a terrible summer. People called it the Great Stink. In those days, all the dirty water of London flowed straight into the Thames. Millions of people's waste. In the summer heat, the river smelled so bad that people could hardly breathe. The members of Parliament sit right next to the river. They hung curtains soaked in chemicals at their windows. But it did not help. At last they decided something had to be done.

{img:bazalgette-engineer-inspecting-brick-sewer-tunnel-workers-lamps}
They chose an engineer called Joseph Bazalgette. He was a small quiet man with a big moustache. He planned a huge system of sewers under the whole of London. A sewer is a tunnel for dirty water. He planned more than a hundred and thirty kilometres of big brick tunnels. And many hundreds of kilometres of smaller ones under the streets. They would carry the dirty water far down the river, away from the houses.

--

It took nearly twenty years to build. It used more than three hundred million bricks. Part of it was built under the new Embankment. Under the same road run the underground railway and pipes for gas and water. All hidden under the road and the gardens.

There are big pumping stations along the way. They lift the water so it keeps flowing. And they are beautiful, like palaces or churches. One of them, by the river, is painted inside in red and green and gold. Its ironwork is so fine that visitors call it the cathedral on the marsh.

{img:crossness-pumping-station-ornate-painted-ironwork-beam-engines}
Bazalgette's sewers changed London. Before, a sickness called cholera came back again and again. It spread through dirty water, and thousands of people died. Now it stopped. The river became cleaner. And the city became healthier.

--

Bazalgette built his tunnels bigger than they needed to be. We will only do this once, he said. We must think of the future. Because of that, his sewers are still used today, more than a hundred and fifty years later. They serve a city of nearly nine million people.

There is a monument to him on the Embankment. And the Embankment, with its plane trees and its dolphin lamps, is still one of the loveliest places in London to walk in the evening, beside the river.
