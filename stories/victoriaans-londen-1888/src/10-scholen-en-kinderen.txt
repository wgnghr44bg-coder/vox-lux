In 1888, every child in London between five and ten must go to school.

This is quite new. Not long ago, there were no schools for everyone. There were only church schools and charity schools. Many poor children never went to school at all. Then a new law said there must be a school near every child. And a few years later, another law said every child must go.

--

So London has built hundreds of new schools all over the city. They are big tall buildings of yellow and red brick, three floors high. They have tall windows and a little bell tower. They stand out above the rows of small houses around them. Many of them are still there today. And many are still schools.

They have separate doors for boys and for girls and for the little ones. The words are carved in stone above each door. Some even have playgrounds on the roof.

In the classrooms there are rows of wooden desks. There is a blackboard and a big map of the world. The British Empire is coloured pink on it. There is a stove for winter. And the teacher's desk stands on a little platform at the front.

--

The children learn reading and writing and sums. They learn some history and geography and singing. They do exercises in the playground. The girls learn sewing and cooking. They write on small slates with slate pencils that squeak. They rub out their work with a cloth, or with their sleeves. The older children write in copybooks with ink and steel pens.

The classes are big. Sixty or seventy children, sometimes more. The teachers are strict. But many of them are kind and work hard. For many poor children, school is the warmest and brightest and safest place they know.

{img:victorian-street-children-playing-marbles-hopscotch-pavement}
After school and on Saturdays, the children of London play in the streets.

--

They play marbles and hopscotch, chalked on the pavement. They skip with a long rope and sing. They roll hoops along with a stick. They play leapfrog and tag and football with a ball of rags. When the organ grinder comes down the street with his monkey, they dance to the music. And they watch the Punch and Judy puppet show on the corner.

Many poor children also work. They sell newspapers and matches and flowers. They run errands and mind babies. They help their parents in their workshops at home.

--

For rich children it is different. They have teachers at home. Then the boys go away to boarding schools. More and more girls go to the new girls' schools. A very few even go to the new colleges for women at the universities.

{img:victorian-toy-shop-window-dolls-tin-soldiers-children-looking}
And on special days, the children of London gaze into the toy shop windows. They see dolls with china faces and tin soldiers. They see rocking horses and toy theatres and wonderful wind-up toys. And they dream.
