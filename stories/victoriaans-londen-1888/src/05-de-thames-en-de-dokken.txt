The heart of London's wealth is the river Thames, and the great docks.

All through the century, the merchants of London have built great docks, along the river, to the east of the Tower of London: the West India Docks, and the East India Docks, and the London Docks, and Saint Katharine's Docks, and the Surrey Docks, and the great Victoria, and Royal Albert Docks, further down the river.

--

They are huge, square lakes of water, dug out of the marshes, and surrounded by high walls, and great warehouses, of brick, five and six storeys high, where the goods of the whole world are stored.

The ships come up the Thames, from the sea, and are let into the docks through locks, and tied up, alongside the quays. There are tall sailing ships, with their forests of masts, and rigging, and their sails furled, and steamships, with their funnels, painted in the colours of their companies, and the little sailing barges, of the Thames, with their red-brown sails, and the tugs, puffing, and whistling.

{img:dockers-unloading-tea-chests-ship-hold-ropes-cranes-warehouse}
And everywhere, there are dockers: thousands of men, unloading, and loading, the ships, carrying sacks, and bales, and barrels, and chests, on their backs, or with ropes, and hand cranes, and carts.

--

The warehouses are full of wonderful things, and wonderful smells: tea, from India, and China, in wooden chests, and coffee, and cocoa, and sugar, from the West Indies, and spices, pepper, and cinnamon, and cloves, and nutmeg, and wine, and brandy, in great casks, in the cellars, and tobacco, and rum, and ivory, and wool, from Australia, and timber, from Canada, and Scandinavia, and grain, and meat, frozen, in the holds of the new refrigerated ships.

Visitors are sometimes taken down, into the great wine vaults, under the London Docks, which stretch for kilometres, lit by lamps, where thousands of casks lie, in long rows, in the cool darkness, with a smell of wine, and fungus, and old wood.

--

But the life of the dockers is hard. Most of them have no regular work. Every morning, they gather at the dock gates, hundreds of them, hoping to be chosen, for a day's work, or half a day, by the foremen. Those who are chosen work hard, all day, for a few pennies an hour. Those who are not go home, with nothing.

{img:thames-river-traffic-barges-red-sails-steamboat-tower-of-london}
Next year, in 1889, they will go on strike, peacefully, for a fair wage, of sixpence an hour, the docker's tanner, and, with the help of many people, and the support of the public, they will win.

--

On the river itself, there is constant traffic: barges, and lighters, carrying goods, from the ships to the wharves, and the little passenger steamboats, which carry people up and down the river, from pier to pier, from Chelsea, to Westminster, to London Bridge, to Greenwich, and watermen, rowing their little boats, across the river, and the police boats, of the Thames police, keeping watch.

And at night, the lights of the ships, and the wharves, are reflected in the dark water, and the river flows on, quietly, toward the sea.
