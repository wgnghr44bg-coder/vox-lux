The heart of London's wealth is the river Thames and its great docks.

All through the century, London's traders have built huge docks along the river. They lie to the east of the Tower of London. Each has its own name, after the places its ships trade with. There are the West India Docks and the East India Docks. And there are many more, further down the river.

--

The docks are huge square lakes, dug out of the marshes. High walls surround them. Big brick warehouses stand around them, five or six floors high. The goods of the whole world are stored inside.

Ships come up the Thames from the sea. They are let into the docks through gates and tied up along the quays. There are tall sailing ships with forests of masts. There are steamships with painted chimneys. There are the little Thames barges with their red-brown sails. And there are tugboats, puffing and whistling.

{img:dockers-unloading-tea-chests-ship-hold-ropes-cranes-warehouse}
And everywhere there are dockers. Thousands of men unload and load the ships. They carry sacks and bales and barrels and chests on their backs. Or they use ropes and hand cranes and carts.

--

The warehouses are full of wonderful things and wonderful smells. There is tea from India and China in wooden chests. There is coffee and cocoa and sugar. There are spices, like pepper and cinnamon and cloves. There is wine in great barrels in the cellars. There is wool from Australia and wood from Canada. And there is meat, frozen in the new cold ships.

Visitors are sometimes taken down into the great wine cellars under the docks. They stretch for kilometres, lit by lamps. Thousands of barrels lie in long rows in the cool dark. It smells of wine and old wood.

--

But the dockers have a hard life. Most of them have no steady work. Every morning hundreds of them gather at the dock gates. They hope to be picked for a day's work, or half a day. The foremen walk along and point. You, and you, and you. Those who are picked work hard all day for a few pennies an hour. Those who are not picked go home with nothing. And tomorrow they will stand at the gate again, and hope.

{img:thames-river-traffic-barges-red-sails-steamboat-tower-of-london}
Next year the dockers will stop work together, peacefully. They will ask for a fair wage of sixpence an hour. Many people will support them. And they will win.

--

On the river itself there is always traffic. Barges carry goods from the ships to the quays. Little passenger steamboats carry people up and down the river, from pier to pier. Boatmen row their small boats across. And the river police keep watch.

At night, the lights of the ships and the quays shine on the dark water. And the river flows on quietly toward the sea.
