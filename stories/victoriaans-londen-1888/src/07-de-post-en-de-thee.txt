In 1888, the post is wonderful.

In the centre of London, postmen bring letters up to twelve times a day. A letter posted in the morning can arrive across town before lunch. And the answer can come back the same afternoon. People write to each other all day long, as we send messages today.

--

It costs only a penny to send a letter anywhere in the country. You stick on a little stamp with the Queen's head on it. The first stamp in the world was made in England, about fifty years ago. And the red post boxes on the street corners have been there for more than thirty years.

The postmen wear dark blue uniforms and caps. They carry big bags of letters. At each door they knock twice, sharp and quick. That way everyone knows it is the post.

{img:telegraph-boy-uniform-bicycle-telegram-message-door}
For urgent news there is the telegraph. A message can be sent along wires in a few minutes, anywhere in the country. It can even go under the sea to America or India. A boy in uniform brings it to your door. When a telegraph boy knocks, people often feel a little nervous. A telegram usually means big news. Good or bad.

--

And there is something new. The telephone. It was invented only twelve years ago. There are already a few thousand telephones in London, in offices and in rich homes. But most people have never used one.

Every afternoon at about four, in the houses of London, there is tea.

{img:victorian-afternoon-tea-drawing-room-ladies-china-cups-cakes}
The story goes that a duchess started it, about fifty years ago. She felt hungry in the long afternoons between lunch and dinner. So she asked for tea and a little bread and butter and cake. Soon she was inviting her friends to join her. And the custom spread.

--

Now everyone has tea. In grand houses it is served in the drawing room. There is a silver teapot and fine china cups. There are thin slices of bread and butter, little sandwiches, scones with jam, and cake. The lady of the house welcomes her visitors at this hour. They leave their name cards in a little silver dish in the hall.

In poorer homes, tea is a real meal. When the workers come home in the evening, there is bread and butter and jam. Perhaps a smoked fish or an egg. And lots of strong sweet tea with milk.

{img:abc-tea-shop-ladies-waitresses-marble-tables-1880s}
And there are tea shops. About twenty-five years ago, a company opened a tea room at a railway station. Anyone could sit down there and have a cup of tea and a bun, cheaply.

--

This was very important for the women of London. Before, there were very few places a woman could go alone to sit and rest and eat in the city. Only the homes of friends. Now there are tea shops all over London. They are full of shop girls and typists and ladies out shopping. They chat over their tea and buns.

And so London drinks tea all day long, from morning till night. Tea from India and China, unloaded at the docks. Brewed in brown teapots and silver ones, in every house in London.
