Let us spend a day with the housemaid of the house in Kensington.

Her name is Annie. She is sixteen. She comes from a village in the country north of London. Her father works on a farm. Two years ago she came to London to work as a servant. Most village girls do. There is little other work for them.

--

Her day begins at six or earlier, in the dark. She gets up in her little attic room at the top of the house. She shares it with the nursemaid. She washes in cold water from a jug. She puts on her work dress, her apron and her cap. And she creeps down all the stairs to the kitchen with a candle.

First she lights the big iron kitchen stove, so the cook can make breakfast and heat water. Then she goes up to the other rooms. She cleans out every fireplace. She sweeps out the ashes. She polishes the grates with black polish until they shine. And she lays new fires and lights them. She must be quick. The family must not come down to a cold house.

{img:housemaid-scrubbing-front-steps-whitening-morning-street}
Then she sweeps and dusts the downstairs rooms. She opens the curtains. She scrubs the front steps and whitens them with a special stone. Soon they gleam white in the morning sun, for everyone on the square to see.

--

At half past seven she carries cans of hot water up the stairs to the bedrooms, so the family can wash. Then she helps the cook carry up breakfast. Porridge and eggs and bacon and toast and tea.

When the family is at breakfast, she goes up to make the beds and clean the bedrooms. Many times a day she carries heavy buckets of coal from the cellar up to all the fires. She answers the front door. And she answers the bells. In the kitchen there is a row of bells on springs. Each one has the name of a room. When a bell rings, she must hurry to that room.

{img:servants-hall-basement-tea-break-cook-housemaid-bells-wall}
In the afternoon she changes into her black dress and a white apron with frills. Now she is ready to open the door to visitors. She brings in the tea for the mother and her guests.

--

In the evening she helps to serve dinner. She clears the table and washes up with the cook. She turns down the beds and fills the hot water bottles. She closes the shutters and puts out the lights.

It is a long day. She works about fifteen hours, with only short rests. She eats her meals downstairs with the cook and the nursemaid. She has one afternoon off a week. Every other Sunday she has the afternoon free. And in summer she has one week's holiday. Then she goes home to her family in the village. She earns about fourteen pounds a year, plus her food and her bed.

--

{img:housemaids-afternoon-off-kensington-gardens-best-hats-walking}
On her afternoon off, she walks in the park with the nursemaid, in her best hat. She looks in the shops. She goes to church. She writes letters home to her mother. And she sends home a little of her money.

At night, at last, she climbs the stairs to her little room under the roof. She takes off her cap and her apron. She lies down in her narrow bed. She listens to the rain on the roof and the church bell striking eleven. And she falls asleep at once.
