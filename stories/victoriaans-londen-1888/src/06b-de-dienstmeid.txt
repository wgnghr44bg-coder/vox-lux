Let us spend a day with the housemaid of the house in Kensington.

Her name is Annie, and she is sixteen years old. She comes from a village in Hertfordshire, in the country, north of London, where her father is a farm labourer. She came to London two years ago, to go into service, as most girls from the villages do, because there is little other work for them.

--

Her day begins at six o'clock, or earlier, in the dark, in her little attic room, at the top of the house, which she shares with the nursemaid. She gets up, and washes, in cold water, from a jug, and puts on her print dress, for the morning work, and her apron, and her cap, and creeps down, all the stairs, to the basement, with a candle.

First, she opens the shutters, and lights the great iron kitchen range, with paper, and sticks, and coal, so that the cook can make breakfast, and heat the water. Then she goes up to the dining room, and the drawing room, and the study, and cleans out the fireplaces, sweeping out the ashes, and blackening the grates, with black lead, and polishing them, until they shine, and lays new fires, and lights them.

{img:housemaid-scrubbing-front-steps-whitening-morning-street}
Then she sweeps, and dusts, the downstairs rooms, and opens the curtains, and scrubs the front steps, and whitens them, with a special stone, so that they gleam, white, in the morning sun, for everyone in the square to see.

--

At half past seven, she carries cans of hot water up the stairs, to the bedrooms, so that the family can wash. Then she helps the cook to carry up the breakfast: porridge, and eggs, and bacon, and kidneys, and toast, and tea, and coffee.

When the family have gone down to breakfast, she goes up to the bedrooms, and makes the beds, and empties the washbasins, and cleans the rooms. She carries coal up, in heavy scuttles, from the cellar, to all the fires, in the house, many times a day. She answers the door, to visitors, and the bell, when the family ring for her, from their rooms. There is a row of bells, in the kitchen, on springs, each one marked with the name of a room, so that the servants know where they are wanted.

{img:servants-hall-basement-tea-break-cook-housemaid-bells-wall}
In the afternoon, she changes into her black dress, and her white frilled apron, and her cap, with streamers, ready to answer the door, and bring in the tea, for the mother, and her visitors, in the drawing room.

--

In the evening, she helps to serve the dinner, and clears away, and washes up, with the cook, and turns down the beds, and fills the hot water bottles, and closes the shutters, and puts out the lights.

It is a long day. She works about fifteen hours, with little rests, between the tasks, and her meals, in the servants' hall, with the cook, and the nursemaid. She has one afternoon off a week, and one Sunday afternoon, every two weeks, and a week's holiday, in the summer, when she goes home, to her family, in the village. She earns about fourteen pounds a year, with her food, and her room.

--

{img:housemaids-afternoon-off-kensington-gardens-best-hats-walking}
On her afternoon off, she walks, with the nursemaid, in Kensington Gardens, in her best hat, and goes to the shops, and to church, and writes letters home, to her mother, and sends a little of her money.

And at night, at last, she climbs the stairs, to her little attic room, under the roof, and takes off her cap, and her apron, and lies down, in her narrow bed, and listens to the rain on the slates, and the bells of the church, striking eleven, and falls asleep, at once.
