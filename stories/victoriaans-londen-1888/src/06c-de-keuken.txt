Let us go down the stairs, to the kitchen, in the basement of the house in Kensington.

It is a big room, with a stone floor, and whitewashed walls, and a high window, near the ceiling, looking out, at the level of the pavement, so that, from the kitchen, you can see the feet of the people walking past, outside, in the street, and the area railings.

--

In the middle, there is a long, scrubbed wooden table, white from years of scrubbing, where the cook prepares the food. Along one wall, there is a great iron range, black, and shining, with a fire in the middle, and ovens on each side, and a boiler, for hot water, and hotplates on the top, where the pans bubble, and simmer, all day long. It must be blackened, with black lead, every morning, and it burns coal, all day, and keeps the kitchen warm, and in summer, very hot.

On the walls, there are rows of copper pans, and moulds, for jellies, and puddings, gleaming, polished, and shelves of plates, and dishes, and a great dresser, and the row of bells, for the servants.

{img:victorian-cook-making-pastry-flour-rolling-pin-kitchen-table}
The cook is a big, kind woman, of about fifty, from Devon, who has worked in kitchens since she was a girl. She is the most important of the servants, and she rules the kitchen, and she knows how to make everything.

--

Her day begins early, and ends late. In the morning, she makes the breakfast, and bakes the bread, and makes the soup, for lunch. The tradesmen come to the area door, below the street, with their deliveries: the milkman, with his cans, and the baker, with the bread, and the butcher's boy, with the meat, and the fishmonger, and the greengrocer, with their baskets. The cook chooses, and checks, and orders, and argues about the prices.

In the afternoon, she makes the cakes, and the scones, and the sandwiches, for tea, and then, the dinner, for the evening: soup, and fish, and a roast, and vegetables, and a pudding, steamed, in a basin, wrapped in a cloth, or a jelly, made in a copper mould, which wobbles, and shines, on its plate.

{img:victorian-larder-shelves-jars-preserves-cheese-hams-cool-room}
Next to the kitchen, there is the scullery, with a big stone sink, and a pump, or a tap, where the dishes, and the pans, are washed, and the vegetables are prepared. And there is the larder, a cool room, with stone shelves, and a window covered with fine mesh, against the flies, where the meat, and the milk, and the butter, and the cheese, are kept cool, because there are no refrigerators, only, in some houses, an ice box, filled with blocks of ice, delivered by the iceman.

--

The cook has a book, a very famous one, Mrs Beeton's Book of Household Management, first published in 1861, with more than a thousand pages, and recipes, and advice, on everything, from how to make a soup, to how to manage the servants, and what to do if the children are ill.

And in the evening, when the dinner is served, and cleared away, and the dishes are washed, the cook sits down, at last, by the kitchen fire, in her armchair, with a cup of tea, and puts her feet up, and reads the newspaper, while the kettle sings, and the kitchen cat sleeps, on the warm rag rug, in front of the range.
