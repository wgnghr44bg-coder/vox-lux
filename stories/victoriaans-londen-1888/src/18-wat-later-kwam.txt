In the years after 1888, London changes fast.

Next year, a new council is created to run the whole great city for the first time. It will build new houses and schools and parks and roads. And later it will clear away the worst of the poor streets.

--

In 1890, the first electric tube railway opens. Others follow, deep under the city. Tower Bridge is finished and opened. The first motor cars appear on the streets. They are noisy and smelly and slow, and they frighten the horses. Then come the first motor buses. Slowly, over the next thirty years, the horses disappear from the streets.

Electric light spreads through the streets and houses. One by one, the gas lamps go out. But even today, about fifteen hundred gas lamps still burn in the streets of London every evening.

{img:queen-victoria-diamond-jubilee-1897-procession-st-pauls-crowds}
Nine years later, the Queen celebrates sixty years on the throne, with an even bigger procession. And in January 1901 she passes away, peacefully, with her family around her. She is eighty-one. Her son becomes King Edward.

--

The Victorian age ends. But the London of 1888 lives on. It lives on in its buildings and streets and parks. In its museums and railways and bridges and sewers. In its schools and markets. Many of them are still there today.

And it lives on in the books of the time. In the stories of Sherlock Holmes. In the novels of Charles Dickens. In The Strange Case of Doctor Jekyll and Mister Hyde, a story about a man with two sides. In Oscar Wilde's fairy tale The Happy Prince, published this very year. In The Time Machine, about a Victorian gentleman who travels far into the future. And in Peter Pan. A few years from now, its writer will walk his big dog in Kensington Gardens. There he will meet the little boys who give him the idea.

{img:victorian-london-book-illustration-style-library-reading-lamp-fire}
And it lives on in the memories of the people who lived then.

--

The children of 1888 played hopscotch on the pavements. They sat in rows in the new schools. They rode on top of the horse buses. They will grow up and live through huge changes. Two world wars, cars and aeroplanes, radio and television. Some of them will even live to see men walk on the moon. And they will tell their grandchildren what London was like when they were young. About the gas lamps and the horses and the fog. And about the muffin man, ringing his bell in the street on a winter afternoon.
