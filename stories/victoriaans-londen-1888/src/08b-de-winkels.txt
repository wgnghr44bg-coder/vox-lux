In 1888, shopping in London is turning into something new.

For hundreds of years, people bought things in small shops. Each shop sold just one kind of thing. Cloth here, buttons there, hats somewhere else. But now there are big new shops where you can buy almost everything under one roof. They are called department stores.

--

One of them calls itself the Universal Provider. It says it can supply anything, from a pin to an elephant. In Knightsbridge there is Harrods. It started as a small grocer's shop. Now it grows bigger every year. In Oxford Street and Regent Street there are big shops with tall glass windows. Inside are rooms full of silk and lace and gloves and hats and coats.

And in Regent Street there is Liberty's. It is full of beautiful things from the East. Silks from Japan and India and China. Fans and fine china and soft flowing cloth in lovely colours. Artists and fashionable ladies love it.

{img:liberty-shop-silks-japanese-fans-oriental-goods-1880s-display}
The department stores are a new world for the ladies of London. They can come and walk around and look without having to buy. That was not the custom before. There are tea rooms inside, and rooms to rest and read. Ladies can sit down and meet their friends.

--

The shop assistants are young men and young women in neat black clothes. They stand behind long counters. They measure out cloth and ribbons and show gloves and hats. Many of them live in rooms above the shop. They work from eight in the morning until nine or ten at night.

When you pay, the money goes to the cashier in a little wooden ball or cup. It whizzes along a wire above the customers' heads. And a moment later it comes whizzing back with your change.

{img:victorian-shop-window-display-hats-gloves-ribbons-gaslit-evening}
In the evening, the shop windows are lit by gas. They are full of wonderful displays. People walk along the streets just to look at them.

--

And there are still thousands of small shops everywhere. The corner shops and grocers smell of tea and coffee and spices and cheese. There are bakers and butchers. There are chemists with big glass bottles of coloured water in their windows, glowing red and green and blue. And there are sweet shops with jars of toffee and peppermints. The children press their noses against the glass.

And the shopkeepers stand in their doorways in their aprons. They greet their customers by name.
