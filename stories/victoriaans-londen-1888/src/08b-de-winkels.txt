In 1888, shopping in London is becoming something new.

For centuries, people bought things in little shops, each one selling only one kind of thing: a draper, for cloth, a haberdasher, for buttons, and ribbons, a grocer, a bootmaker, a hatter, an ironmonger. But now, there are new, great shops, where you can buy almost everything, under one roof: the department stores.

--

In Westbourne Grove, in Bayswater, there is Whiteleys, which calls itself the Universal Provider, and says it can supply anything, from a pin, to an elephant. In Knightsbridge, there is Harrods, which began as a little grocer's shop, and is growing, year by year. In Oxford Street, and Regent Street, there are great drapers' shops, with tall plate-glass windows, and gas lights, and rooms full of silks, and laces, and gloves, and hats, and coats.

And in Regent Street, there is Liberty's, opened in 1875, by Arthur Lasenby Liberty, full of beautiful things from the East: silks, from Japan, and India, and China, and fans, and porcelain, and lacquer, and carpets, and soft, flowing fabrics, in lovely colours, which the artists, and the fashionable ladies, love.

{img:liberty-shop-silks-japanese-fans-oriental-goods-1880s-display}
The department stores are a new world for the ladies of London. They can come, and walk around, and look, without having to buy, which was not the custom before. There are tea rooms, and restrooms, and reading rooms, inside the shops, where they can sit down, and rest, and meet their friends.

--

The shop assistants, young men, and young women, stand behind the long counters, in their neat black clothes, and serve the customers, measuring out cloth, and ribbons, and showing gloves, and hats. Many of them live in rooms above the shop, and work long hours, from eight in the morning, until nine, or ten, at night.

The money is sent from the counter to the cashier, in little wooden balls, or cups, which whizz along wires, above the heads of the customers, and come back, with the change.

{img:victorian-shop-window-display-hats-gloves-ribbons-gaslit-evening}
In the evenings, the shop windows are lit by gas, and later by electricity, and are full of wonderful displays, and people walk along the streets, just to look at them.

--

And there are still thousands of little shops, everywhere: the corner shops, and the grocers, smelling of tea, and coffee, and spices, and cheese, and the bakers, and the butchers, and the chemists, with their great glass bottles, of coloured water, in the windows, red, and green, and blue, glowing, and the tobacconists, and the sweet shops, with their jars of humbugs, and toffee, and peppermints, and barley sugar, where the children press their noses against the window.

And the shopkeepers stand in their doorways, in their aprons, and greet their customers, by name.
