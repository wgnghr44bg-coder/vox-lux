Let us go east, to the East End of London.

Here, east of the City and north of the docks, live hundreds of thousands of people. They are the working people of London. Many of them are very poor.

--

The streets are narrow and crowded. There are old houses and new blocks of flats. There are small workshops and factories, pubs and chapels and shops. Often a whole family lives in one room. They share one tap and one toilet in the yard with all the other families in the house.

The people work at a thousand trades. They are dock workers and porters and carters. They are seamstresses and tailors and shoemakers. They make furniture and matches and brushes. They work long hours, in small workshops or at home, for very little money.

{img:jewish-immigrant-family-arriving-london-docks-bundles-1880s}
The East End has always been a place where newcomers arrive. Two hundred years ago, people came from France and became silk weavers here. Then came people from Ireland. And now, in the 1880s, thousands of Jewish families are arriving from Russia and Poland. They are escaping danger and poverty. They come with their bundles and their children. They settle here and open shops and bakeries and workshops and synagogues. And in the streets you hear their language, Yiddish.

--

Life in the East End is hard. But it is also full of life. There are markets and music and street games. There are families and neighbours who help each other.

And in 1888, people elsewhere are starting to notice. Some are starting to help.

{img:matchgirls-strike-1888-young-women-shawls-street-gathering-peaceful}
In the summer of 1888, the young women and girls at a match factory in the East End stop work.

--

They work long hours for very low pay. They are fined for small things, like talking or dropping matches. And some get ill from the chemicals used to make the matches. A writer called Annie Besant wrote about this in her newspaper. The factory asked the girls to sign a paper saying it was not true. They would not sign. They knew they might lose their jobs. But they stood together. In July, fourteen hundred of them walked out at once.

Within two weeks they won. The newspapers and the public were on their side. The fines stopped. Things got better. And they started a union, a group that would stand up for them. It was one of the first great wins for ordinary working women. And it gave courage to others, like the dock workers the next year.

{img:charles-booth-researcher-notebook-walking-london-street-policeman}
That same year, a rich businessman called Charles Booth is walking through London's streets. His helpers walk with him. Street by street and house by house, they write down how people live and what they earn. He wants to know, for certain, how many Londoners are poor.

--

He will make a great map of London. Every street gets a colour, depending on how rich or poor its people are. Yellow for the rich. Red and pink for those doing well. Blue for the poor. And black for the very poorest. He will find that about one in three Londoners lives in poverty. It shocks the country. And it helps to change how people think about the poor.

In the East End there are places that try to help. In one big house, young men from the universities come to live among the poor. They teach classes and help where they can. There are missions and churches. And there is the Salvation Army, started right here in the East End. It gives soup and shelter and help.

--

In the autumn of 1888, the East End is also in the newspapers every day. There is a dark mystery in the streets of Whitechapel that frightens the whole city. It will never be solved. We will leave it there, far away in the shadows of the past. And we will remember instead the ordinary people of the East End. Every day they went on working and raising their children and helping each other.
