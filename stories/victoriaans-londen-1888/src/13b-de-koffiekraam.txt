Late at night, when the theatres have closed and most of London is in bed, some people are still working in the streets.

The cab drivers wait with their cabs in the cold for the last passengers. Policemen walk slowly through the dark streets with their lanterns. They try the shop doors to check they are locked. Night watchmen sit in little huts beside holes in the road, warming their hands by small fires.

--

For the cab drivers there are little green wooden huts. They stand in the middle of the street, at the places where cabs wait. A charity built them. Inside, the drivers can get out of the cold and the rain. They can have a hot meal and a cup of tea. And they do not have to leave their horses. The huts may not be bigger than a horse and cart, because they stand in the road.

Inside there is a tiny kitchen and a bench and a table for about ten men. A cook makes them bacon and eggs and tea. Some of these little green huts are still in London's streets today. They still serve tea and bacon sandwiches to the taxi drivers.

{img:coffee-stall-street-corner-night-lamp-workers-steaming-mugs}
On the street corners there are coffee stalls. They are little carts with a lamp and a big pot of hot coffee and tea. They sell bread and butter and cake and boiled eggs. They stay open all night. Cab drivers come, and policemen, and market porters on their way to work before dawn. And people who have nowhere to sleep come too, for a little warmth.

--

And there are the pubs.

There are thousands of pubs in London, on almost every corner. Their names are painted in gold letters over the doors. Their windows are of coloured and patterned glass. Gas lamps hang outside.

{img:victorian-pub-interior-mahogany-bar-etched-glass-mirrors-gaslight}
Inside they are warm and bright. There are polished wooden bars and brass rails and big mirrors. Wooden screens divide the room into little separate bars. One bar is for workers. One is a bit more comfortable and a bit dearer. And one is a tiny room for a few quiet people.

--

For many working people, the pub is like their living room. It is warm and bright and friendly. They meet their friends there. They talk and sing and play darts or dominoes. They read the newspaper and get warm after work.

At the end of the night, the landlord calls time. People go home through the quiet streets. The pub lamps are turned down and the doors are closed. And in their little green hut, the cab drivers drink one last cup of tea. They wait for one more passenger before they too go home.
