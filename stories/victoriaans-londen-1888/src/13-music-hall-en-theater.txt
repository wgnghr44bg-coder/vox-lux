In the evenings, Londoners go out to the music hall.

The music hall is the great fun of Victorian London. There are hundreds of them all over the city. Some are grand palaces in the West End. Some are small halls behind a pub in the East End.

--

Inside there is a stage with lights along the front and painted scenery. There are red velvet seats and golden balconies. The ceiling is painted with clouds. A great sparkling gas chandelier hangs in the middle. In the older halls, people sit at tables and eat and drink while they watch. A chairman sits at a table beside the stage with a little hammer. He announces each act with long grand words. And he keeps order.

The show goes on all evening, one act after another. Singers and comedians and dancers. Jugglers and acrobats and magicians. Dogs that do tricks and strong men. Each one does a few minutes, and then the next one comes on.

{img:music-hall-singer-on-stage-audience-singing-along-chorus}
The great stars are the singers and comedians. They sing funny songs and sad songs about everyday life. Songs about landladies and lodgers. About trips to the seaside and falling in love. About the lives of ordinary Londoners.

--

And the audience sing along with the chorus, hundreds of voices together. Everyone knows the songs. They sing them in the streets and at home around the piano. And the next day the barrel organs play them in the streets.

One of the brightest young stars this year is Marie Lloyd. She is a girl from the East End, only eighteen. She will become the queen of the music hall, loved by everyone in London.

{img:savoy-theatre-electric-light-elegant-audience-gilbert-sullivan-stage}
And in the West End there are the theatres.

--

On the Strand stands the Savoy Theatre. When it opened seven years ago, it was the first public building in the world lit only by electric light. Many people were afraid. Would the new lights set the theatre on fire? So on the first night, the owner walked onto the stage holding a glowing light bulb. In front of everyone, he broke it. Nothing caught fire. The audience cheered.

The most popular writers in London are Gilbert and Sullivan. Gilbert writes the words, clever and funny. Sullivan writes the music, beautiful and catchy. Their funny operas make everybody laugh. People sing and whistle their songs everywhere.

--

And in October 1888, their new opera opens at the Savoy. It is set in the Tower of London, long ago. There are guards and a jester. And there is a young man in prison who must escape in disguise before it is too late. It is a little more serious than their other operas. And it has some of Sullivan's most beautiful music. The audience on the first night love it.

{img:west-end-theatre-street-night-carriages-crowds-lights-strand}
There are many other theatres too. At one of them, the great actor Henry Irving plays Shakespeare, with the actress Ellen Terry. The audience hardly dare to breathe. The theatre's manager is a quiet Irishman called Bram Stoker. Some years later he will write a famous spooky book called Dracula.

--

And at Christmas there are the pantomimes. They have wonderful scenery and fairies and giants. Every child in London wants to go.

When the shows are over, late in the evening, the streets of the West End fill with people and carriages and cabs. The lights of the theatres shine on the wet streets. And the crowds go home through the gaslight, humming the songs.
