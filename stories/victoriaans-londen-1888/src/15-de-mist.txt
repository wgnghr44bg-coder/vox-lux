In 1888, London is famous for its fog.

In autumn and winter, on still cold days, the fog comes. It rises from the river and the marshes. It mixes with the smoke of millions of coal fires and factories and trains. It turns thick and yellow and brown. And it settles over the city, sometimes for days.

--

Londoners call it a pea souper, because it is as thick and yellow as pea soup. Sometimes you cannot see across the street. Sometimes you cannot even see your own hand in front of you.

In the fog, the whole city changes. The gas lamps become small blurry yellow glows that light nothing. The buildings disappear. People appear out of the fog and vanish again, like ghosts. Sounds change too. Footsteps and hooves and voices sound soft and strange. They seem to come from everywhere and nowhere.

In the thickest fog, the traffic stops. Cabs and buses crawl along. The drivers climb down and lead their horses by hand. Boys carry flaming torches. For a penny, they will guide you home through the fog. Trains go slowly. The railway men put little bangers on the tracks. When a train rolls over them they go bang. That warns the driver to be careful.

--

On the river the boats stop. Their foghorns sound low and sad through the mist. And the church bells ring, muffled, through the fog.

The fog is bad for people's health. It makes them cough. But it is also strangely beautiful. Writers and painters love it. The writer Charles Dickens began one of his books with fog. Fog everywhere, he wrote. Fog up the river, fog down the river. And a few years later, a French painter called Monet will come to London. He will paint the Houses of Parliament again and again, glowing in the fog in purple and gold and blue.

{img:painterly-houses-of-parliament-fog-sunset-thames-purple-gold}
An American painter who lives by the river paints the Thames at night, in the mist. He paints in soft blues and greys and golds. The lights of the bridges and boats glow through the dark. He calls his paintings nocturnes, like pieces of music for the night.

--

Inside the houses, on a foggy day, the gas lamps burn all day long. The fires are piled high with coal. People stay home if they can. They look out of the window at the blank yellow nothing outside. Then they draw the curtains and sit by the fire and read.

{img:cosy-victorian-room-fire-window-fog-outside-cat-armchair-book}
After a day, or two, or three, a wind comes. The fog lifts and drifts away. The city appears again, wet and shining and grey, under a pale sky. And people come back out into the streets.

--

Much later, after one terrible fog that lasted five days, new laws will stop people burning coal in the city. And slowly the great London fogs will disappear. But in 1888 they are still part of London. As much as the river and the Tower and the sound of Big Ben.
