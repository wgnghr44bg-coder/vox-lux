It is an autumn evening, and the light is fading over the great city. [pause]

[pause]

[long-pause]

London, in 1888, is the greatest city in the world.

[pause]

{img:victorian-london-rooftops-chimneys-smoke-st-pauls-dome-autumn}

[long-pause]

[pause]

The old Queen, Victoria, has been on the throne for more than fifty years. Last year, in 1887, she celebrated her Golden Jubilee, and the whole city was decorated with flags, and flowers, and lit with lights, and fireworks, and crowds filled the streets to see her drive past, in her open carriage, to Westminster Abbey.

{img:victorian-london-busy-street-crowds-omnibuses-shop-signs-afternoon}
London, in 1888, is enormous.

[pause]

It has grown, and grown, all through the century. In 1800, about a million people lived here. Now, there are more than five and a half million, and every year, tens of thousands more arrive, from the countryside, and from Ireland, and Scotland, and Wales, and from all over Europe, and the world, looking for work, and a new life.

[long-pause]

The city spreads out, in every direction, for miles: street after street, of houses, and shops, and churches, and factories, and warehouses, and railway lines, and chimneys, swallowing up the old villages, and the fields, and the market gardens, around it. Places that were country villages, when the Queen came to the throne, Hampstead, and Islington, and Kensington, and Clapham, and Hackney, are now part of the great city, joined to it, by long, straight streets, of new brick houses.

[pause]

Every morning, hundreds of thousands of people pour into the centre, to work, by train, and omnibus, and on foot, across the bridges, from the suburbs. And every evening, they pour out again.

[pause]

The heart of London is the City, the old square mile, inside the ancient walls, where the Romans built their town, nearly two thousand years before. Here are the Bank of England, and the Royal Exchange, and the great banks, and insurance companies, and shipping offices, which run the trade of half the world. In the daytime, its streets are packed with clerks, in their black coats, and top hats, hurrying from office to office. At night, and on Sundays, it is almost empty, and silent.

[long-pause]

To the west is the West End, the London of the rich, and the fashionable: of Mayfair, and Belgravia, and Kensington, with their tall white houses, and their quiet squares, and the great shops of Regent Street, and Oxford Street, and Bond Street, and the theatres, and the clubs, and the parks.

[pause]

To the east is the East End, the London of the docks, and the workshops, and the poor, crowded, and busy, and noisy, where the sailors, and the dockers, and the immigrants, and the workers of a thousand trades live.

[pause]

{img:victorian-terraced-houses-new-suburb-street-brick-lamp-posts}
And all around, in every direction, are the suburbs, the new London of the middle classes: of clerks, and shopkeepers, and teachers, and engineers, living in long rows of neat brick houses, each with its little front garden, and its bay window, and its iron railings, and its back yard.

[long-pause]

London is noisy. The streets are full of the clatter of iron wheels, and horses' hooves, on the stones, and the cries of the street sellers, and the rumble of the carts, and the whistles of the trains, and the bells of the churches.

[pause]

And London is dirty. Its air is full of the smoke of a million coal fires, and its streets are full of mud, and horse manure, swept up by the crossing sweepers, little boys, with brooms, who sweep a path across the road, for the ladies, and gentlemen, in return for a penny.

[long-pause]

But London is also magnificent, and exciting, and full of life, and every visitor who comes here, from anywhere in the world, is amazed by it.

[long-pause]

{img:st-pancras-station-train-shed-iron-glass-roof-steam-trains-1888}
Around the centre of London, there is a ring of great railway stations.

[pause]

Each one was built by a different railway company, in the great railway age, forty, or fifty years before, at the end of its line, into London: Euston, and King's Cross, and Saint Pancras, in the north, and Paddington, in the west, and Waterloo, and Victoria, and London Bridge, in the south, and Liverpool Street, in the east, and others.

[long-pause]

They are like cathedrals, of the new age of steam. The greatest of them all is Saint Pancras: in front, a great hotel, the Midland Grand, built of red brick, and stone, like a Gothic palace, with towers, and spires, and pointed windows, and a great clock tower. And behind it, the train shed, a single, enormous arch, of iron, and glass, more than seventy metres wide, the widest roof of its kind in the world, when it was built, covering the platforms, and the trains, and filled with light, and steam, and smoke.

[pause]

Next door, at King's Cross, there are two great plain arches, of yellow brick, side by side, with a clock tower, between them, where the trains leave, for York, and Edinburgh, and the north.

[pause]

{img:paddington-station-platform-crowds-porters-luggage-steam-locomotive}
Inside the stations, there is always noise, and bustle, and excitement.

[long-pause]

The great steam locomotives stand at the platforms, hissing, and puffing, painted in the bright colours of their companies, green, and red, and blue, and brown, with gleaming brass, and polished copper, with their drivers, and firemen, in the cabs, leaning out, and waiting for the signal.

[pause]

Porters, in their uniforms, wheel the luggage, on barrows, piled with trunks, and hatboxes, and bags. Families hurry along the platforms, looking for their carriages. Newspaper boys, and bookstalls, sell newspapers, and magazines, and cheap novels, for the journey. Railway clocks tick, above the platforms, all set to exactly the same time.

[long-pause]

Because of the railways, all of Britain now keeps the same time, London time, set by the Royal Observatory, at Greenwich. Before the railways, every town kept its own time, by the sun, so that, in Bristol, the clocks were ten minutes behind London. But the railways needed timetables, and so, the whole country agreed on one time. And in 1884, at a great conference, in Washington, the whole world agreed that the line through Greenwich would be the starting line for the world's time zones, and its maps. It is still called Greenwich Mean Time.

[pause]

{img:victorian-railway-carriage-compartment-passengers-window-countryside}
From the stations, the trains go out, to every part of the country: to the seaside, to Brighton, and Margate, and Southend, where Londoners go, for a day out, on bank holidays, by the thousand, and to the great cities of the north, and to Scotland, and to the ports, for ships, to France, and Ireland, and America.

[long-pause]

The station hotels are grand, and comfortable, and the station restaurants serve tea, and sandwiches, and soup, and the bookstalls, of W. H. Smith, sell books for the journey.

[pause]

And at night, the great stations are lit by gas, and later by electricity, and the last trains come in, from the country, with their passengers, sleepy, and tired, and the porters, and the cabmen, wait, by the platforms, to take them home, through the dark, quiet streets.

Let us ride through London on the top of an omnibus.

[pause]

The omnibus is a large wagon, pulled by two horses, painted in bright colours, red, or green, or yellow, or blue, according to its route, with the names of the places it goes to painted on its sides, in big letters, and advertisements, for soap, and tea, and cocoa, and medicines.

[long-pause]

Inside, there are two long benches, facing each other, where passengers sit, in the warm, a little squashed together, with straw on the floor, to keep their feet warm, in winter. But on top, on the roof, there is a long bench, back to back, called the knifeboard, where passengers can sit in the open air, and see the whole city, passing by below. It is cheaper, and much more fun.

[pause]

Ladies did not sit on top, in earlier years, because they had to climb a little iron ladder, in long skirts. But in the 1880s, the new omnibuses have proper stairs, at the back, and more and more ladies climb up.

{img:busy-victorian-street-carriages-carts-hansom-cabs-crossing-sweeper}
From the top of the omnibus, we see the whole traffic of London, below us.

[long-pause]

There are other omnibuses, by the hundred, and hansom cabs: little two-wheeled carriages, with a single horse, and the driver sitting high up, behind, holding the reins over the roof, fast, and light, the taxis of Victorian London. There are four-wheeled cabs, called growlers, slower, and heavier, for families with luggage. There are private carriages, gleaming, with coats of arms on the doors, and coachmen in livery. There are carts, and wagons, and drays, loaded with barrels of beer, and coal, and vegetables, and furniture, and parcels, pulled by great, heavy horses.

[pause]

And there are horse trams, running on rails, in the streets of the suburbs, and costermongers' barrows, pushed by hand, and delivery boys, on foot, and, now and then, a new bicycle, weaving through the traffic.

[long-pause]

Altogether, there are probably about three hundred thousand horses in London. They need stables, and hay, and oats, and water, and blacksmiths, and they fill the streets with the smell, and the sound, of horses.

[pause]

The traffic is often terrible. At the busiest corners, near the Bank, and at the end of London Bridge, the carts, and cabs, and omnibuses, are packed so tightly together, that nothing can move, for minutes at a time, and the drivers shout, and argue, and the policemen, in their tall helmets, try to sort it out.

The streets are lined with shops, with big glass windows, lit by gas, and with signs, and advertisements, painted on the walls, and on boards, carried through the streets, by sandwich men: men walking slowly along the gutter, with a board hanging in front, and another behind.

[long-pause]

On the pavements, there are crowds of people: clerks, and shop girls, and gentlemen in top hats, and ladies in long dresses, with bustles, and little hats, and parasols, and workmen in caps, and errand boys, and nursemaids, pushing prams, and flower girls, selling violets, and newspaper boys, shouting the headlines, and the muffin man, ringing his bell, with a tray of muffins on his head.

[pause]

And from the top of the omnibus, swaying gently, through the noise, and the bustle, we watch it all go by.

{img:victoria-embankment-thames-gas-lamps-trees-granite-wall-river-1888}
Beside the Thames, in the middle of London, there is a long, wide, new road, along the river: the Victoria Embankment.

[pause]

It runs from Westminster, to Blackfriars, with a wide pavement, and plane trees, and a granite wall, along the river, decorated with lamps, on iron posts, with dolphins twisting around them. And beside it, there are gardens, with flowers, and lawns, and statues.

[long-pause]

It looks simple. But under it, there is one of the great wonders of Victorian London.

[pause]

Thirty years before, in the hot summer of 1858, there was the Great Stink. All the sewage of London, from millions of people, flowed straight into the Thames, and the river was so dirty, and smelled so terrible, in the heat, that the Members of Parliament, in the Houses of Parliament, beside the river, could hardly bear it. They hung curtains, soaked in lime, at the windows. And at last, they decided that something must be done.

[pause]

{img:bazalgette-engineer-inspecting-brick-sewer-tunnel-workers-lamps}
The man they chose to do it was an engineer, called Joseph Bazalgette. He was a small, quiet man, with a big moustache, and he planned a great system of sewers, under the whole of London: more than a hundred and thirty kilometres of great brick tunnels, and more than eighteen hundred kilometres of smaller street sewers, carrying the sewage away, from the city, to the east, far down the river, beyond the houses.

[long-pause]

It took nearly twenty years to build, and used more than three hundred million bricks. Part of it was built under the new Embankment, along the river, with the underground railway, and pipes for gas, and water, all hidden beneath the road, and the gardens.

[pause]

There are great pumping stations, to lift the sewage, along the way. And they are beautiful, like palaces, or churches. At Crossness, beside the river, the pumping station is decorated, inside, with wonderful ironwork, painted in red, and green, and gold, like a cathedral. Visitors call it the cathedral on the marsh.

[pause]

{img:crossness-pumping-station-ornate-painted-ironwork-beam-engines}
Bazalgette's sewers changed London. The terrible outbreaks of cholera, which had killed thousands of people, again and again, stopped. The river became cleaner. And the city became healthier, and could grow even more.

[long-pause]

Bazalgette made his sewers bigger than they needed to be, at the time, because, he said, we will only do this once, and we must think of the future. Because of this, his sewers are still used, today, more than a hundred and fifty years later, by a city of nearly nine million people.

[pause]

There is a monument to him, on the Embankment, with a Latin inscription, which means, he put the river in chains. And the Embankment, with its plane trees, and its dolphin lamps, is still one of the loveliest places in London, to walk, in the evening, beside the river.

[long-pause]

{img:victorian-underground-station-steam-locomotive-gas-lamps-platform-smoke}
Under the streets of London, there is something that no other city in the world has: an underground railway.

[pause]

The first underground railway in the world, the Metropolitan Railway, opened in London, in January 1863, twenty-five years before, running from Paddington, to Farringdon, under the New Road, about six kilometres long.

[long-pause]

It was built in a very simple way. The builders dug a great trench, along the middle of the street, built the tunnel, of brick, inside it, and then covered it over again, and put the street back on top. This is called cut and cover. It caused enormous disruption, for years, but when it was finished, it was a great success. On the first day, nearly forty thousand people rode on it.

[pause]

By 1888, the Metropolitan, and the District Railway, have grown, and joined together, into a great loop, the Inner Circle, around the centre of London, with branches running out, into the suburbs.

[pause]

{img:victorian-underground-train-carriages-first-class-gaslit-passengers}
The trains are pulled by steam locomotives, which run through the tunnels, puffing out smoke, and steam. The engines have been specially built, to condense some of their steam, and to burn coke, to make less smoke. But still, the tunnels, and the stations, are often full of smoke, and the smell of sulphur, and the passengers arrive, sometimes, with smuts on their faces.

[long-pause]

There are open sections, between the tunnels, where the smoke can escape, and the stations have glass roofs, to let it out, and to let in the daylight. Some people say that the air in the tunnels is good for asthma. Most people do not agree.

[pause]

The carriages have first, second, and third classes, and are lit by gas lamps, fed from bags of gas on the roof. Every morning, and evening, they are full of clerks, and workers, travelling to, and from, the City, many of them on special cheap workmen's tickets, on the early trains.

[pause]

{img:tunnel-digging-workers-shield-clay-candles-1880s-deep-tube}
And now, in 1888, something new is being built, deep underground.

[long-pause]

Under the river Thames, and under the streets of the City, and south London, engineers are digging a new kind of railway: not just under the surface of the street, but deep below, in round tunnels, of iron, dug with a great iron shield, pushed forward, through the London clay, by the workers, inch by inch.

[pause]

It will be called the City and South London Railway. And its trains will not be pulled by steam, but by electricity. It will open in 1890, the first deep, electric, underground railway in the world. And because of its round tunnels, Londoners will soon call it the tube.

[long-pause]

From these beginnings will grow the London Underground, the great network of lines, under the whole city, that millions of people use every day, today. The original stations of the Metropolitan, at Baker Street, with their brick walls, and their arches, can still be seen, and used, today.

[long-pause]

{img:victorian-london-docks-tall-ships-masts-warehouses-cranes-busy}
The heart of London's wealth is the river Thames, and the great docks.

[pause]

All through the century, the merchants of London have built great docks, along the river, to the east of the Tower of London: the West India Docks, and the East India Docks, and the London Docks, and Saint Katharine's Docks, and the Surrey Docks, and the great Victoria, and Royal Albert Docks, further down the river.

[long-pause]

They are huge, square lakes of water, dug out of the marshes, and surrounded by high walls, and great warehouses, of brick, five and six storeys high, where the goods of the whole world are stored.

[pause]

The ships come up the Thames, from the sea, and are let into the docks through locks, and tied up, alongside the quays. There are tall sailing ships, with their forests of masts, and rigging, and their sails furled, and steamships, with their funnels, painted in the colours of their companies, and the little sailing barges, of the Thames, with their red-brown sails, and the tugs, puffing, and whistling.

[pause]

{img:dockers-unloading-tea-chests-ship-hold-ropes-cranes-warehouse}
And everywhere, there are dockers: thousands of men, unloading, and loading, the ships, carrying sacks, and bales, and barrels, and chests, on their backs, or with ropes, and hand cranes, and carts.

[long-pause]

The warehouses are full of wonderful things, and wonderful smells: tea, from India, and China, in wooden chests, and coffee, and cocoa, and sugar, from the West Indies, and spices, pepper, and cinnamon, and cloves, and nutmeg, and wine, and brandy, in great casks, in the cellars, and tobacco, and rum, and ivory, and wool, from Australia, and timber, from Canada, and Scandinavia, and grain, and meat, frozen, in the holds of the new refrigerated ships.

[pause]

Visitors are sometimes taken down, into the great wine vaults, under the London Docks, which stretch for kilometres, lit by lamps, where thousands of casks lie, in long rows, in the cool darkness, with a smell of wine, and fungus, and old wood.

[long-pause]

But the life of the dockers is hard. Most of them have no regular work. Every morning, they gather at the dock gates, hundreds of them, hoping to be chosen, for a day's work, or half a day, by the foremen. Those who are chosen work hard, all day, for a few pennies an hour. Those who are not go home, with nothing.

[pause]

{img:thames-river-traffic-barges-red-sails-steamboat-tower-of-london}
Next year, in 1889, they will go on strike, peacefully, for a fair wage, of sixpence an hour, the docker's tanner, and, with the help of many people, and the support of the public, they will win.

[long-pause]

On the river itself, there is constant traffic: barges, and lighters, carrying goods, from the ships to the wharves, and the little passenger steamboats, which carry people up and down the river, from pier to pier, from Chelsea, to Westminster, to London Bridge, to Greenwich, and watermen, rowing their little boats, across the river, and the police boats, of the Thames police, keeping watch.

[pause]

And at night, the lights of the ships, and the wharves, are reflected in the dark water, and the river flows on, quietly, toward the sea.

[long-pause]

{img:tower-bridge-under-construction-1888-steel-frames-towers-scaffolding}
Beside the Tower of London, in 1888, something new is rising from the river.

[pause]

Two great piers, of granite, and concrete, have been sunk, into the bed of the Thames, and on top of them, workers are building two tall towers, of steel. It is a new bridge: Tower Bridge.

[long-pause]

For many years, the people of the East End have needed a new bridge, across the river, below London Bridge. But the bridge must not stop the tall ships, from sailing up the river, to the wharves, and the Pool of London, beside the Tower.

[pause]

So the engineer, Sir John Wolfe Barry, and the architect, Sir Horace Jones, designed a very special bridge: a bascule bridge, with two great leaves, or arms, in the middle, which can be lifted up, like a drawbridge, to let the tall ships pass through, and then lowered again, for the traffic.

[pause]

{img:workers-riveting-steel-high-tower-bridge-river-below-boats}
The building began in 1886, and it will take eight years. Hundreds of workers are building it, riveting the steel together, high above the river, without safety nets.

[long-pause]

The towers are being built of steel, for strength, but they will be covered with stone, granite, and Portland stone, so that they look like old Gothic towers, to match the Tower of London, beside them. High up, between the towers, there will be two walkways, so that people can cross, even when the bridge is raised.

[pause]

And the great arms of the bridge will be lifted, by steam engines, hidden in the piers, powering hydraulic machinery, so that they rise, smoothly, and quickly, in about a minute.

[long-pause]

When it is finished, in 1894, it will be opened by the Prince of Wales, and it will become the most famous bridge in the world, the symbol of London.

[pause]

{img:tower-of-london-white-tower-walls-river-thames-autumn-afternoon}
Beside it, the Tower of London, which is already eight hundred years old, looks on, as it has looked on at everything, since William the Conqueror built it. The ravens hop about on the grass. The Yeoman Warders, the Beefeaters, in their dark blue uniforms, show the visitors around, and tell them the old stories. And the Crown Jewels glitter, behind their railings.

[long-pause]

In 1888, many Londoners come to watch the new bridge being built. They stand on the riverbank, and on London Bridge, and look at the great steel towers, rising up, higher every month, and wonder what it will look like, when it is finished.

[pause]

Today, more than a hundred and thirty years later, Tower Bridge still opens, several hundred times a year, for tall ships, and sailing boats, and cruise ships, and the traffic stops, and people stop, and watch, as the great arms rise up, slowly, into the sky.

[long-pause]

{img:victorian-kensington-tall-white-stucco-houses-square-garden-railings}
Let us visit a family home, in Kensington, in the west of London.

[pause]

The house stands in a quiet square, with a private garden in the middle, with tall plane trees, and lawns, and flower beds, surrounded by iron railings, where only the families who live in the square have a key. The houses around the square are tall, and narrow, and white, covered with smooth plaster, called stucco, with pillars, by the front doors, and black iron railings, and steps going down, to the basement.

[long-pause]

The family who live here are comfortable, and respectable, but not very rich: a father, who is a lawyer, in the City, and a mother, and four children, and a grandmother. And, like most middle-class families in London, they have servants: a cook, a housemaid, a nursemaid, for the little children, and a boy, who comes in, every day, to clean the boots, and the knives, and carry the coal.

[pause]

The house has five floors. Down in the basement, there are the kitchen, and the scullery, and the larder, and the coal cellar, under the pavement, and the servants' hall, where the servants eat.

[pause]

{img:victorian-parlour-drawing-room-fireplace-piano-ferns-gaslight}
On the ground floor, there are the dining room, and the father's study. On the first floor, the drawing room, the best room in the house, where the mother receives her visitors, in the afternoons, and the family gathers, in the evenings.

[long-pause]

The drawing room is full of things. Victorians love things. There are heavy curtains, of velvet, at the windows, with lace curtains behind them, and patterned wallpaper, and patterned carpets, and a big marble fireplace, with a mirror above it, and on the mantelpiece, a clock, under a glass dome, and china figures, and vases, and photographs, in silver frames. There are armchairs, and a sofa, and little tables, covered with cloths, and ornaments, and books, and albums. There is a piano, and a stand, of ferns, and palms, in pots, and a bird, in a cage, a canary, singing.

[pause]

In the evening, the room is lit by gas, from lamps on the walls, with glass shades, giving a soft, warm, hissing, golden light. And there is a fire, of coal, burning in the grate.

[pause]

{img:victorian-nursery-children-rocking-horse-toys-nanny-fireside}
On the floors above, there are the bedrooms, of the parents, and the grandmother. And at the top of the house, the nursery, where the children live, and play, and have their lessons, and their meals, with the nursemaid, and, at the very top, under the roof, the little attic rooms, where the servants sleep, cold in winter, and hot in summer.

[long-pause]

The children do not see much of their parents, during the day. They come down to the drawing room, washed, and brushed, in their best clothes, for an hour after tea, to see their mother, and play quietly, and talk, and perhaps be read to, or play the piano, and then they go back up, to the nursery, for supper, and bed.

[pause]

In the nursery, there is a rocking horse, and a dolls' house, and a toy theatre, and tin soldiers, and a Noah's ark, with pairs of wooden animals, and picture books, and a big fireguard, in front of the fire, where the children's clothes are hung, to warm, before bed.

[pause]

{img:victorian-family-dinner-table-silver-candles-father-carving}
In the evening, the family has dinner, in the dining room, at seven, or eight: soup, and fish, and a joint of meat, roast beef, or mutton, carved by the father, at the table, with vegetables, and potatoes, and then a pudding, and fruit, and cheese, served by the housemaid, in her black dress, and white apron, and cap.

[long-pause]

And after dinner, they go up to the drawing room, and the mother plays the piano, and the eldest daughter sings, and the father reads the newspaper, and the grandmother does her needlework, by the fire, and the gas lamps hiss softly, and the clock ticks, under its glass dome, and outside, in the square, the lamplighter has lit the lamps, and the rain is falling, gently, on the trees.

[long-pause]

{img:young-housemaid-lighting-kitchen-range-dawn-basement-candle}
Let us spend a day with the housemaid of the house in Kensington.

[pause]

Her name is Annie, and she is sixteen years old. She comes from a village in Hertfordshire, in the country, north of London, where her father is a farm labourer. She came to London two years ago, to go into service, as most girls from the villages do, because there is little other work for them.

[long-pause]

Her day begins at six o'clock, or earlier, in the dark, in her little attic room, at the top of the house, which she shares with the nursemaid. She gets up, and washes, in cold water, from a jug, and puts on her print dress, for the morning work, and her apron, and her cap, and creeps down, all the stairs, to the basement, with a candle.

[pause]

First, she opens the shutters, and lights the great iron kitchen range, with paper, and sticks, and coal, so that the cook can make breakfast, and heat the water. Then she goes up to the dining room, and the drawing room, and the study, and cleans out the fireplaces, sweeping out the ashes, and blackening the grates, with black lead, and polishing them, until they shine, and lays new fires, and lights them.

[pause]

{img:housemaid-scrubbing-front-steps-whitening-morning-street}
Then she sweeps, and dusts, the downstairs rooms, and opens the curtains, and scrubs the front steps, and whitens them, with a special stone, so that they gleam, white, in the morning sun, for everyone in the square to see.

[long-pause]

At half past seven, she carries cans of hot water up the stairs, to the bedrooms, so that the family can wash. Then she helps the cook to carry up the breakfast: porridge, and eggs, and bacon, and kidneys, and toast, and tea, and coffee.

[pause]

When the family have gone down to breakfast, she goes up to the bedrooms, and makes the beds, and empties the washbasins, and cleans the rooms. She carries coal up, in heavy scuttles, from the cellar, to all the fires, in the house, many times a day. She answers the door, to visitors, and the bell, when the family ring for her, from their rooms. There is a row of bells, in the kitchen, on springs, each one marked with the name of a room, so that the servants know where they are wanted.

[pause]

{img:servants-hall-basement-tea-break-cook-housemaid-bells-wall}
In the afternoon, she changes into her black dress, and her white frilled apron, and her cap, with streamers, ready to answer the door, and bring in the tea, for the mother, and her visitors, in the drawing room.

[long-pause]

In the evening, she helps to serve the dinner, and clears away, and washes up, with the cook, and turns down the beds, and fills the hot water bottles, and closes the shutters, and puts out the lights.

[pause]

It is a long day. She works about fifteen hours, with little rests, between the tasks, and her meals, in the servants' hall, with the cook, and the nursemaid. She has one afternoon off a week, and one Sunday afternoon, every two weeks, and a week's holiday, in the summer, when she goes home, to her family, in the village. She earns about fourteen pounds a year, with her food, and her room.

[long-pause]

{img:housemaids-afternoon-off-kensington-gardens-best-hats-walking}
On her afternoon off, she walks, with the nursemaid, in Kensington Gardens, in her best hat, and goes to the shops, and to church, and writes letters home, to her mother, and sends a little of her money.

[pause]

And at night, at last, she climbs the stairs, to her little attic room, under the roof, and takes off her cap, and her apron, and lies down, in her narrow bed, and listens to the rain on the slates, and the bells of the church, striking eleven, and falls asleep, at once.

[long-pause]

{img:victorian-basement-kitchen-range-copper-pans-cook-table-window}
Let us go down the stairs, to the kitchen, in the basement of the house in Kensington.

[pause]

It is a big room, with a stone floor, and whitewashed walls, and a high window, near the ceiling, looking out, at the level of the pavement, so that, from the kitchen, you can see the feet of the people walking past, outside, in the street, and the area railings.

[long-pause]

In the middle, there is a long, scrubbed wooden table, white from years of scrubbing, where the cook prepares the food. Along one wall, there is a great iron range, black, and shining, with a fire in the middle, and ovens on each side, and a boiler, for hot water, and hotplates on the top, where the pans bubble, and simmer, all day long. It must be blackened, with black lead, every morning, and it burns coal, all day, and keeps the kitchen warm, and in summer, very hot.

[pause]

On the walls, there are rows of copper pans, and moulds, for jellies, and puddings, gleaming, polished, and shelves of plates, and dishes, and a great dresser, and the row of bells, for the servants.

[pause]

{img:victorian-cook-making-pastry-flour-rolling-pin-kitchen-table}
The cook is a big, kind woman, of about fifty, from Devon, who has worked in kitchens since she was a girl. She is the most important of the servants, and she rules the kitchen, and she knows how to make everything.

[long-pause]

Her day begins early, and ends late. In the morning, she makes the breakfast, and bakes the bread, and makes the soup, for lunch. The tradesmen come to the area door, below the street, with their deliveries: the milkman, with his cans, and the baker, with the bread, and the butcher's boy, with the meat, and the fishmonger, and the greengrocer, with their baskets. The cook chooses, and checks, and orders, and argues about the prices.

[pause]

In the afternoon, she makes the cakes, and the scones, and the sandwiches, for tea, and then, the dinner, for the evening: soup, and fish, and a roast, and vegetables, and a pudding, steamed, in a basin, wrapped in a cloth, or a jelly, made in a copper mould, which wobbles, and shines, on its plate.

[pause]

{img:victorian-larder-shelves-jars-preserves-cheese-hams-cool-room}
Next to the kitchen, there is the scullery, with a big stone sink, and a pump, or a tap, where the dishes, and the pans, are washed, and the vegetables are prepared. And there is the larder, a cool room, with stone shelves, and a window covered with fine mesh, against the flies, where the meat, and the milk, and the butter, and the cheese, are kept cool, because there are no refrigerators, only, in some houses, an ice box, filled with blocks of ice, delivered by the iceman.

[long-pause]

The cook has a book, a very famous one, Mrs Beeton's Book of Household Management, first published in 1861, with more than a thousand pages, and recipes, and advice, on everything, from how to make a soup, to how to manage the servants, and what to do if the children are ill.

[pause]

And in the evening, when the dinner is served, and cleared away, and the dishes are washed, the cook sits down, at last, by the kitchen fire, in her armchair, with a cup of tea, and puts her feet up, and reads the newspaper, while the kettle sings, and the kitchen cat sleeps, on the warm rag rug, in front of the range.

[long-pause]

{img:victorian-postman-red-pillar-box-uniform-delivering-letters-street}
In 1888, the post is wonderful.

[pause]

In the centre of London, the postmen deliver letters up to twelve times a day. A letter, posted in the morning, in one part of London, can arrive in another part of London, before lunch. And the answer can come back, the same afternoon. People write to each other, all day long, as we send messages today.

[long-pause]

It costs only a penny to send a letter, anywhere in the country, with a little stamp, with the Queen's head on it. The Penny Post began in 1840, with the Penny Black, the first postage stamp in the world. And the red pillar boxes, which stand on the street corners, have been there since the 1850s.

[pause]

The postmen wear dark blue uniforms, with red piping, and caps, and carry great bags of letters, and walk their rounds, knocking twice, on each door, with a sharp double knock, so that everyone knows that it is the post.

[pause]

{img:telegraph-boy-uniform-bicycle-telegram-message-door}
For urgent messages, there is the telegraph. A message can be sent along the wires, in a few minutes, to anywhere in the country, or even under the sea, to America, or India, and is delivered to the door, by a telegraph boy, in his uniform, on foot, or on a bicycle.

[long-pause]

And there is something new: the telephone. It was invented only twelve years ago, in 1876, and there are already a few thousand telephones in London, in offices, and in the homes of the rich. But most people have never used one.

[pause]

Every afternoon, at about four o'clock, in the houses of London, there is tea.

[pause]

{img:victorian-afternoon-tea-drawing-room-ladies-china-cups-cakes}
Afternoon tea became fashionable about fifty years before, they say, because a duchess, the Duchess of Bedford, felt hungry, in the long afternoons, between lunch and dinner, and asked for tea, and a little bread and butter, and cake, to be brought to her room. Soon, she was inviting her friends to join her, and the custom spread.

[long-pause]

Now, in 1888, everyone has tea. In the grand houses, it is served in the drawing room, on a tray, with a silver teapot, and fine china cups, and thin slices of bread and butter, and little sandwiches, and scones, and jam, and cake. The ladies of the house receive their visitors, at this time, who come to call, leaving their cards, in a little silver dish, in the hall.

[pause]

In the poorer houses, tea is a meal, with bread, and butter, and jam, or dripping, and perhaps a kipper, or an egg, and lots of strong, sweet tea, with milk, when the workers come home, in the evening.

[pause]

{img:abc-tea-shop-ladies-waitresses-marble-tables-1880s}
And there are tea shops. In 1864, a company, the Aerated Bread Company, opened a tea room, at London Bridge station, where anyone could sit down, and have a cup of tea, and a bun, cheaply, and respectably.

[long-pause]

This is very important for the women of London, because, before this, there were very few places, apart from the homes of their friends, where a respectable woman could go, alone, or with a friend, to sit down, and rest, and eat, and talk, in the middle of the day, in the city. By 1888, there are tea shops all over London, full of shop girls, and typists, and ladies out shopping, chatting over their tea, and buns.

[pause]

And so, in 1888, London drinks tea, all day long, from morning till night: tea from India, and Ceylon, and China, unloaded at the docks, and sold in the shops, and brewed in brown teapots, and silver ones, in every house in London.

[long-pause]

{img:covent-garden-market-dawn-carts-vegetables-flowers-porters-baskets}
Before dawn, while most of London is still asleep, the markets are already awake.

[pause]

At Covent Garden, in the middle of the West End, the great market hall, with its columns, and its glass roofs, is full of light, and noise, and colour. All night long, the carts, and wagons, have been coming in, from the market gardens, and the farms, around London, piled high with vegetables, and fruit, and flowers.

[long-pause]

There are cabbages, and carrots, and onions, and potatoes, and turnips, and celery, and lettuces, and great heaps of apples, and pears, and plums, and in the season, strawberries, and cherries, and baskets of watercress. And from further away, by train, and ship, oranges, and lemons, and bananas, and pineapples, from warm countries, far away.

[pause]

The porters carry the baskets on their heads, piled up, ten, or twelve, one on top of another, balanced, and walk, quickly, through the crowds, without dropping them.

[pause]

And there are flowers: roses, and violets, and lilies, and carnations, and daffodils, in spring, and chrysanthemums, in autumn, and ferns, and palms, in pots, filling the flower market with their scent, and colour.

[long-pause]

The flower girls, and women, in their shawls, and their old hats, come early, to buy their flowers, and then go out, into the streets, with their baskets, to sell little bunches of violets, and buttonholes, to the gentlemen, on their way to work, for a penny.

[pause]

At Billingsgate, beside the river, near London Bridge, there is the fish market. Here, from five o'clock in the morning, the fish comes in, from the boats, and the trains: cod, and haddock, and herrings, and plaice, and sole, and salmon, and eels, and oysters, and shrimps, and crabs, and lobsters, in great piles, on the stone floor, in baskets, and boxes, glittering, and wet, and silver.

[pause]

{img:billingsgate-fish-market-porters-leather-hats-fish-baskets-river}
The fish porters wear special hats, of leather, flat on the top, with a broad brim, on which they carry the heavy boxes of fish, on their heads, so that the water, and the fish scales, run off, and do not go down their necks.

[long-pause]

Billingsgate is famous for its noise, and for the language of its porters, which is said to be the roughest in London.

[pause]

At Smithfield, in the north of the City, there is the great meat market, in a new hall, of iron, and glass. And at Leadenhall, in the heart of the City, there is a beautiful new market, built only a few years ago, in 1881, with a glass roof, and iron arches, painted in red, and cream, and green, and gold, selling poultry, and game, and meat, and cheese.

[pause]

{img:costermonger-barrow-fruit-street-market-gaslight-saturday-night}
And all over London, in the streets of the poorer districts, there are street markets, where the costermongers sell fruit, and vegetables, and fish, from their barrows, and stalls, by the light of flaring naphtha lamps, on Saturday nights, crying their wares, in a hundred different cries.

[long-pause]

There are street sellers of everything: of hot potatoes, and roasted chestnuts, and pies, and sheep's trotters, and pea soup, and whelks, and ginger beer, and coffee, from stalls, on the street corners, for the workers, early in the morning. And of matches, and bootlaces, and newspapers, and songs, printed on sheets of paper, and toys, and birds, in cages.

[pause]

And so, every morning, the markets of London feed five and a half million people, from all over the country, and all over the world.

[long-pause]

{img:victorian-department-store-interior-counters-ladies-shopping-gaslight}
In 1888, shopping in London is becoming something new.

[pause]

For centuries, people bought things in little shops, each one selling only one kind of thing: a draper, for cloth, a haberdasher, for buttons, and ribbons, a grocer, a bootmaker, a hatter, an ironmonger. But now, there are new, great shops, where you can buy almost everything, under one roof: the department stores.

[long-pause]

In Westbourne Grove, in Bayswater, there is Whiteleys, which calls itself the Universal Provider, and says it can supply anything, from a pin, to an elephant. In Knightsbridge, there is Harrods, which began as a little grocer's shop, and is growing, year by year. In Oxford Street, and Regent Street, there are great drapers' shops, with tall plate-glass windows, and gas lights, and rooms full of silks, and laces, and gloves, and hats, and coats.

[pause]

And in Regent Street, there is Liberty's, opened in 1875, by Arthur Lasenby Liberty, full of beautiful things from the East: silks, from Japan, and India, and China, and fans, and porcelain, and lacquer, and carpets, and soft, flowing fabrics, in lovely colours, which the artists, and the fashionable ladies, love.

[pause]

{img:liberty-shop-silks-japanese-fans-oriental-goods-1880s-display}
The department stores are a new world for the ladies of London. They can come, and walk around, and look, without having to buy, which was not the custom before. There are tea rooms, and restrooms, and reading rooms, inside the shops, where they can sit down, and rest, and meet their friends.

[long-pause]

The shop assistants, young men, and young women, stand behind the long counters, in their neat black clothes, and serve the customers, measuring out cloth, and ribbons, and showing gloves, and hats. Many of them live in rooms above the shop, and work long hours, from eight in the morning, until nine, or ten, at night.

[pause]

The money is sent from the counter to the cashier, in little wooden balls, or cups, which whizz along wires, above the heads of the customers, and come back, with the change.

[pause]

{img:victorian-shop-window-display-hats-gloves-ribbons-gaslit-evening}
In the evenings, the shop windows are lit by gas, and later by electricity, and are full of wonderful displays, and people walk along the streets, just to look at them.

[long-pause]

And there are still thousands of little shops, everywhere: the corner shops, and the grocers, smelling of tea, and coffee, and spices, and cheese, and the bakers, and the butchers, and the chemists, with their great glass bottles, of coloured water, in the windows, red, and green, and blue, glowing, and the tobacconists, and the sweet shops, with their jars of humbugs, and toffee, and peppermints, and barley sugar, where the children press their noses against the window.

[pause]

And the shopkeepers stand in their doorways, in their aprons, and greet their customers, by name.

[long-pause]

{img:east-end-street-1888-crowded-houses-washing-lines-children-playing}
Let us go east, to the East End of London.

[pause]

Here, east of the City, and north of the docks, in Whitechapel, and Spitalfields, and Stepney, and Bethnal Green, and Poplar, live hundreds of thousands of people, the working people of London, many of them very poor.

[long-pause]

The streets are narrow, and crowded, with old houses, and new tenement buildings, and workshops, and little factories, and pubs, and chapels, and shops, and markets. Whole families live in a single room, and share a tap, and a toilet, in the yard, with all the other families in the house.

[pause]

The people work at a thousand trades. They are dockers, and porters, and carters, and labourers, and seamstresses, and tailors, and shoemakers, and furniture makers, and match makers, and sack makers, and brush makers, working long hours, in little workshops, or at home, for very little money.

[pause]

{img:jewish-immigrant-family-arriving-london-docks-bundles-1880s}
The East End has always been a place where newcomers arrive, from other countries. Two hundred years ago, the Huguenots came, from France, and became silk weavers, in Spitalfields. Then came the Irish. And now, in the 1880s, thousands of Jewish families are arriving, from Russia, and Poland, escaping persecution, and poverty, with their bundles, and their children, and settling in Whitechapel, and Spitalfields, where they open shops, and synagogues, and workshops, and bakeries, and markets, and speak Yiddish, in the streets.

[long-pause]

Life in the East End is hard. But it is also full of life: of markets, and music, and pubs, and chapels, and street games, and families, and neighbours, who help each other.

[pause]

And in 1888, people are beginning to notice it, and to try to help.

[pause]

{img:matchgirls-strike-1888-young-women-shawls-street-gathering-peaceful}
In the summer of 1888, the young women, and girls, who work at the match factory of Bryant and May, in Bow, in the East End, go on strike.

[long-pause]

They work long hours, for very low wages, and are fined for little things, like talking, or dropping matches, and some of them become ill, from the phosphorus, used to make the matches. A writer, Annie Besant, wrote about their conditions, in her newspaper, and the factory tried to make the girls sign a paper, saying it was not true. They refused. And in July 1888, fourteen hundred of them walked out, together.

[pause]

Within two weeks, with the support of the public, and the newspapers, and members of Parliament, they won: the fines were stopped, and their conditions improved, and they started a union. The matchgirls' strike was one of the first great victories of ordinary working women, and it inspired many other workers, like the dockers, the next year.

[pause]

{img:charles-booth-researcher-notebook-walking-london-street-policeman}
And in 1888, a rich businessman, Charles Booth, is walking through the streets of London, with his helpers, street by street, house by house, writing down how the people live, and how much they earn, to find out, scientifically, how many people in London are poor.

[long-pause]

He will make a great map of London, with every street coloured, according to how rich, or poor, its people are: yellow, for the wealthy, and red, for the comfortable, and pink, for the fairly comfortable, and blue, for the poor, and black, for the poorest. And he will find that about a third of the people of London live in poverty. It shocks the country, and helps to change the way people think about the poor.

[pause]

In Whitechapel, there is Toynbee Hall, opened in 1884, where young men, from the universities of Oxford, and Cambridge, come to live, among the poor, and teach classes, and give lectures, and help. And there are the missions, and the churches, and the Salvation Army, founded in the East End, by William and Catherine Booth, giving soup, and shelter, and help.

[long-pause]

In the autumn of 1888, the East End is also in the newspapers, every day, because of a series of terrible crimes, in the streets of Whitechapel, which were never solved, and which frightened the whole city. We will leave them there, far away, in the shadows of the past, and remember instead the ordinary people of the East End, who went on, every day, working, and raising their children, and helping each other, through all of it.

[long-pause]

{img:victorian-board-school-classroom-rows-desks-slates-teacher-blackboard}
In 1888, every child in London, between the ages of five and ten, must go to school.

[pause]

This is quite new. Until 1870, there were no schools for everyone, only church schools, and charity schools, and little private schools, and many poor children never went to school at all. Then, in 1870, a new law said that there must be a school, within reach, for every child, and in 1880, another law said that every child must go.

[long-pause]

So, in London, the School Board has built hundreds of new schools, all over the city: big, tall buildings, of yellow and red brick, three storeys high, with tall windows, and high gables, and a bell tower, standing out above the rows of little houses, around them, in every poor district. Many of them are still there, today, and are still used as schools.

[pause]

They have separate entrances, for boys, and girls, and infants, with the words carved in stone, above the doors, and playgrounds, with high walls, and some of them have playgrounds on the roof.

[pause]

In the classrooms, there are rows of wooden desks, for two, or more children, and a blackboard, and a big map of the world, with the British Empire coloured pink, and a stove, for the winter, and the teacher's desk, on a raised platform, at the front.

[long-pause]

The children learn reading, and writing, and arithmetic, the three Rs, and some history, and geography, and singing, and drill, exercises, in the playground, and the girls learn needlework, and cooking. They write on slates, with slate pencils, which squeak, and rub out their work, with a cloth, or their sleeves. The older children write in copybooks, with steel pen nibs, and ink, from inkwells, in their desks.

[pause]

The classes are big, sixty, or seventy children, sometimes more, and the teachers are strict. But many of the teachers are kind, and work hard, and for many poor children, the school is the warmest, and brightest, and safest place they know.

[pause]

{img:victorian-street-children-playing-marbles-hopscotch-pavement}
After school, and on Saturdays, the children of London play in the streets.

[long-pause]

They play marbles, and hopscotch, chalked on the pavement, and skipping, with a long rope, and songs, and hoops, bowled along with a stick, and spinning tops, and leapfrog, and tag, and football, with a ball of rags. They dance to the music of the barrel organ, when the organ grinder comes down the street, with his monkey. They watch the Punch and Judy show, on the street corner.

[pause]

Many poor children also work: selling newspapers, and matches, and flowers, running errands, minding babies, carrying parcels, helping their parents, in their workshops, at home.

[long-pause]

For the children of the rich, it is different. They have governesses, and tutors, at home, and then, the boys go away, to boarding schools, and the girls, more and more, to the new girls' schools, and even, a very few, to the new women's colleges, at Oxford, and Cambridge, and London.

[pause]

{img:victorian-toy-shop-window-dolls-tin-soldiers-children-looking}
And on special days, the children of London gaze into the windows of the toy shops, at the dolls, with their china faces, and the tin soldiers, and the Noah's arks, and the rocking horses, and the toy theatres, and the wonderful mechanical toys, and dream.

[long-pause]

{img:hyde-park-rotten-row-riders-carriages-sunday-afternoon-trees}
London is a city of parks.

[pause]

In the middle of the city, there are the great royal parks, which once belonged to the kings, and queens, for hunting, and riding, and are now open to everyone: Hyde Park, and Kensington Gardens, and Green Park, and Saint James's Park, and Regent's Park, wide, and green, and peaceful, with great trees, and lawns, and lakes, and flowers.

[long-pause]

On a fine Sunday afternoon, in 1888, all of London comes to the parks.

[pause]

In Hyde Park, along Rotten Row, a wide sandy track, the fashionable ladies, and gentlemen, ride their horses, slowly, up and down, to see, and be seen. Along the carriage drive, beside it, the carriages of the rich drive slowly past, with ladies, in their finest dresses, and hats, and parasols, nodding to their friends. And on the grass, and the paths, the crowds walk, and watch.

[pause]

On the Serpentine, the long lake in the middle of the park, people row in little boats, and children sail their toy boats, and feed the ducks, and the swans. In winter, when it freezes, thousands of people skate on the ice.

[long-pause]

In Kensington Gardens, beside it, the nursemaids push the prams, along the long paths, under the trees, and the children play, by the Round Pond, sailing their boats, and flying their kites. And there is the Albert Memorial, a great Gothic monument, gleaming with gold, and coloured stone, and marble, with the statue of Prince Albert, the Queen's husband, who died more than twenty-five years ago, sitting under a great spire, looking out, toward the Royal Albert Hall.

[pause]

In Regent's Park, there is the Zoo, the Zoological Gardens, where Londoners come to see the lions, and the tigers, and the bears, and the monkeys, and the elephants, and the giraffes, and the hippopotamus, and the children can ride on the elephant.

[pause]

{img:london-zoo-1888-children-elephant-ride-howdah-crowd}
And in Saint James's Park, there is the lake, with its pelicans, which have lived there since the time of King Charles the Second, two hundred years ago.

[long-pause]

There are other parks, too, all over London, new ones, for the people of the crowded districts: Victoria Park, in the East End, and Battersea Park, south of the river, and many others, with bandstands, where military bands play, on summer afternoons, and the people sit in deckchairs, and listen.

[pause]

On Sundays, most of London goes to church, in the morning, or chapel, and the shops are closed, and it is quiet. And in the afternoon, people walk, in the parks, or along the river, in their Sunday best, or go out, by train, to the country, or to the seaside, for the day.

[pause]

{img:hampstead-heath-view-over-london-families-walking-kites-autumn}
On Hampstead Heath, on the hills to the north of London, there are wide, wild open spaces, of grass, and woods, and ponds, where the people of London come, on bank holidays, by the thousand, to walk, and play games, and fly kites, and ride the donkeys, and look out, over the whole city, spread out below them, under its haze of smoke, with the dome of Saint Paul's, rising in the middle.

[long-pause]

And in the evenings, in summer, as the sun sets, the parks grow quiet. The park keepers ring their bells, and close the gates. The swans settle on the lakes. And the lamps come on, along the paths, under the trees.

[long-pause]

{img:thames-passenger-steamboat-pier-westminster-families-summer-day}
On a summer day, in 1888, many Londoners go up the river.

[pause]

From the piers, at Westminster, and Charing Cross, the little passenger steamboats leave, every few minutes, crowded with families, and couples, and children, in their summer clothes, and straw hats, going up the river, to Chelsea, and Putney, and Kew, and Richmond, and Hampton Court.

[long-pause]

As the boat goes upriver, the city slowly falls behind. The great buildings, and the warehouses, and the factories, give way to houses, with gardens, running down to the water, and boathouses, and willow trees, and meadows, and the river becomes narrower, and quieter, and greener.

[pause]

At Kew, there are the Royal Botanic Gardens: wide lawns, and great trees, from all over the world, and flower beds, and lakes, and the great glasshouses, of iron, and glass, the Palm House, warm, and steamy, inside, full of tropical palms, and plants, and the Temperate House, and the Chinese Pagoda, rising above the trees.

[pause]

{img:kew-gardens-palm-house-glasshouse-victorian-visitors-lawn-summer}
At Richmond, there is the hill, with its famous view, over the bend of the river, and the meadows, and the woods, which painters have painted for a hundred years. And there are boats to hire, and people row on the river, the young men in their striped blazers, and boaters, and the young ladies in white, with parasols, trailing their fingers, in the cool water.

[long-pause]

This is the great age of boating on the Thames. Every summer weekend, the river, above London, is crowded with rowing boats, and punts, and skiffs, and canoes, and little steam launches, and houseboats, moored along the banks, with flowers in boxes, on their roofs.

[pause]

In 1889, the year after our story, a writer, Jerome K. Jerome, will publish a funny book, about three young men, and a dog, who take a boat trip up the Thames, called Three Men in a Boat. It will become one of the best-loved comic books in the English language, and it is still read, and loved, today.

[pause]

{img:rowing-boats-punts-river-thames-willows-summer-afternoon-picnic}
Further up, at Hampton Court, there is the great Tudor palace of King Henry the Eighth, with its red brick, and its chimneys, and its gardens, and its famous maze, where the visitors get lost, and laugh, and try to find the way out.

[long-pause]

And in the evening, as the sun sets, the steamboats carry the tired, happy families, back down the river, toward the city, through the golden light, past the willows, and the meadows, and the boathouses, and the children fall asleep, on their mothers' laps, and the lights of London begin to shine, ahead of them, in the dusk.

[long-pause]

{img:natural-history-museum-1888-terracotta-facade-towers-visitors}
In South Kensington, in the west of London, there is a whole district of great museums, built with the money from the Great Exhibition, of 1851.

[pause]

There is the South Kensington Museum, full of art, and decorative things, furniture, and silver, and glass, and pottery, and tapestries, and sculptures, from all over the world, which will later be called the Victoria and Albert Museum.

[long-pause]

And there is the new Natural History Museum, which opened only seven years ago, in 1881: a wonderful building, like a great cathedral, of nature, covered with terracotta tiles, of pale blue, and honey colour, and decorated, all over, with carvings of animals, and plants, climbing up the pillars, and the arches, and peering down, from the walls. On one side, living animals. On the other, animals that have died out, long ago.

[pause]

Inside, there is a great hall, with a high roof, like a church, and the cases are full of stuffed animals, and birds, and fish, and fossils, and skeletons, and minerals, and gems, and dinosaurs.

[pause]

The idea of dinosaurs is still quite new. The word dinosaur was invented only about forty-five years before, in 1842, by a scientist, Richard Owen, who was the man behind this museum, and its first director, until he retired, a few years ago. In the park at the Crystal Palace, in south London, there are great models of dinosaurs, made in the 1850s, beside a lake, which children love to visit. They are not quite right, as we know now, but they are still there, today.

[long-pause]

In Bloomsbury, in the middle of London, there is the British Museum, with its great columns, and its treasures from the ancient world: the Rosetta Stone, and the Egyptian mummies, and the sculptures of the Parthenon, in Athens, and the great winged bulls, from Assyria, and the treasures of Greece, and Rome.

[pause]

And in the middle of the British Museum, there is the Reading Room: a great round room, under a huge dome, painted blue, and gold, with long desks, arranged like the spokes of a wheel, and shelves of books, all around the walls, where scholars, and writers, from all over the world, come to read, and study, and write. Karl Marx wrote his books here. Charles Dickens had a reader's ticket. Many of the great books of the Victorian age were written under this dome.

[pause]

{img:british-museum-reading-room-dome-readers-desks-lamps-quiet}
It is very quiet, in the Reading Room, with only the sound of pages turning, and pens scratching, and the occasional cough.

[long-pause]

The museums are free, and open to everyone, and on Sundays, and holidays, crowds of working families come, to look at the treasures, and the animals, and the machines, and learn about the world. The Victorians believe that museums, and libraries, and parks, will make people's lives better, and happier, and wiser.

[pause]

There are also free public libraries, opening in every district of London, where anyone can borrow books, and read the newspapers, in warm, well-lit reading rooms.

[pause]

{img:science-museum-steam-engines-machines-victorian-families-looking}
In the South Kensington Museum, there are also wonderful machines: steam engines, and locomotives, and models of ships, and looms, and clocks, and telescopes, which will later become the Science Museum. Among them is the Rocket, the famous early locomotive of George Stephenson, from 1829.

[long-pause]

And so, on a rainy afternoon, in 1888, Londoners can spend hours, in the warm, wandering through the great halls, among the dinosaurs, and the mummies, and the steam engines, and the paintings, for nothing, and come out, into the gaslight, and the rain, with their heads full of wonders.

[long-pause]

{img:victorian-music-hall-interior-stage-gaslight-audience-tables-chairman}
In the evenings, Londoners go out, to the music hall.

[pause]

The music hall is the great entertainment of Victorian London. There are hundreds of them, large, and small, all over the city, from the grand palaces of the West End, to little halls, behind the pubs, in the East End.

[long-pause]

Inside, there is a stage, with footlights, and painted scenery, and a great auditorium, with red plush seats, and gilded balconies, and a ceiling painted with clouds, and gods, and a great glittering chandelier, of gas, hanging in the middle. In the older halls, the audience sit at tables, and eat, and drink, while they watch the show. And there is a chairman, sitting at a table, beside the stage, with a hammer, who announces each act, in grand, long words, and keeps order.

[pause]

The show goes on all evening: one act after another, singers, and comedians, and dancers, and jugglers, and acrobats, and magicians, and ventriloquists, and performing dogs, and strongmen, each doing their turn, for a few minutes, and then the next.

[pause]

{img:music-hall-singer-on-stage-audience-singing-along-chorus}
The great stars of the music hall are the singers, and comedians, men and women, who sing funny, and sad, and sentimental songs, about everyday life: about lodgers, and landladies, and mothers-in-law, and holidays at the seaside, and falling in love, and bank holidays, and the life of the ordinary people of London.

[long-pause]

And the audience sing along, with the choruses, all together, hundreds of voices, filling the hall. Everyone knows the songs, and sings them, in the streets, and the pubs, and at home, around the piano. And the barrel organs play them, in the streets, the next day.

[pause]

One of the brightest young stars, in 1888, is Marie Lloyd, a girl from Hoxton, in the East End, who is only eighteen, and will become the queen of the music hall, loved by everyone in London.

[pause]

{img:savoy-theatre-electric-light-elegant-audience-gilbert-sullivan-stage}
And in the West End, there are the theatres.

[long-pause]

In the Strand, there is the Savoy Theatre, built by Richard D'Oyly Carte, for the comic operas of Gilbert and Sullivan. It was the first public building in the world to be lit entirely by electric light, when it opened, in 1881. On the first night, Mr D'Oyly Carte came onto the stage, holding a glowing electric light bulb, and broke it, in front of the audience, to show that it was safe, and would not set the theatre on fire.

[pause]

Gilbert and Sullivan are the most popular writers in London. W. S. Gilbert writes the words, clever, and funny, and Arthur Sullivan writes the music, beautiful, and catchy. Their comic operas, H.M.S. Pinafore, and The Pirates of Penzance, and The Mikado, have made everybody laugh, and are sung, and whistled, everywhere.

[long-pause]

And in October 1888, their new opera opens at the Savoy: The Yeomen of the Guard, set in the Tower of London, in Tudor times, with the Beefeaters, and a jester, and a young man, in prison, about to be executed, who escapes, in disguise. It is a little more serious than their others, and it has some of Sullivan's most beautiful music. The audience on the first night love it.

[pause]

{img:west-end-theatre-street-night-carriages-crowds-lights-strand}
There are many other theatres, too, along the Strand, and Drury Lane, and Shaftesbury Avenue, a new street, opened only recently, in 1886. At the Lyceum, the great actor Henry Irving plays Shakespeare, with the actress Ellen Terry, and the audience are spellbound. And the business manager of the Lyceum, Irving's right-hand man, is a quiet Irishman, named Bram Stoker, who, some years later, will write a famous book, called Dracula.

[long-pause]

And at Christmas, there are the pantomimes, at Drury Lane, with their wonderful scenery, and transformation scenes, and fairies, and giants, and the dame, and the principal boy, and the clown, where every child in London wants to go.

[pause]

When the shows are over, late in the evening, the streets of the West End fill with people, and carriages, and cabs, and the lights of the theatres shine on the wet streets, and the crowds go home, through the gaslight, humming the songs.

[long-pause]

{img:cabmens-shelter-green-wooden-hut-cabmen-eating-hansom-cabs-night}
Late at night, when the theatres have closed, and most of London has gone to bed, there are still people working, in the streets.

[pause]

The cabmen wait, with their hansom cabs, at the cab ranks, in the cold, for the last passengers. The policemen walk their beats, slowly, through the dark streets, with their lanterns, trying the door handles, to check that the shops are locked. The night watchmen sit in their little huts, beside the holes in the road, with their braziers, glowing.

[long-pause]

For the cabmen, there are the cabmen's shelters: little green wooden huts, standing in the middle of the street, at the cab ranks, built by a charity, from 1875, so that the cab drivers can come in, out of the cold, and the rain, and have a hot meal, and a cup of tea, or coffee, without having to go to the pub, and leave their horses. They are not allowed to be bigger than a horse and cart, because they stand in the road.

[pause]

Inside, there is a little kitchen, and a bench, and a table, for about ten men, and a cook, who makes them bacon, and eggs, and chops, and tea. Some of these little green huts are still there, in the streets of London, today, still serving tea, and bacon sandwiches, to the taxi drivers.

[pause]

{img:coffee-stall-street-corner-night-lamp-workers-steaming-mugs}
And on the street corners, there are the coffee stalls: little carts, with a lamp, and a great urn, of hot coffee, and tea, and plates of bread and butter, and cake, and hard-boiled eggs, which stay open all night, for the cabmen, and the policemen, and the market porters, going to work, before dawn, and the people who have nowhere to sleep.

[long-pause]

And there are the pubs.

[pause]

In 1888, there are thousands of pubs, in London, on almost every street corner, with their names painted in gold letters, over the doors, and their windows of engraved, and coloured glass, shining, and their gas lamps, hanging outside.

[pause]

{img:victorian-pub-interior-mahogany-bar-etched-glass-mirrors-gaslight}
Inside, they are warm, and bright, with polished mahogany bars, and brass rails, and great mirrors, and beer pumps, with china handles, and the room divided, by wooden screens, and partitions, into little separate bars: the public bar, for the workers, and the saloon bar, more comfortable, and a little more expensive, and the snug, a tiny room, for a few quiet people.

[long-pause]

For many working people, the pub is their sitting room: warm, and bright, and friendly, where they can meet their friends, and talk, and sing, and play darts, or dominoes, and read the newspaper, and get warm, after work.

[pause]

And at the end of the night, the landlord calls time, and the people go home, through the quiet streets, and the gas lamps of the pub are turned down, and the doors are closed, and the cabmen, in their little green shelter, drink one last cup of tea, and wait, for one more fare, before they, too, go home.

[long-pause]

{img:baker-street-victorian-fog-gaslight-hansom-cab-door-evening}
At the end of 1887, a few months before our story, a story appeared in a Christmas magazine, Beeton's Christmas Annual, called A Study in Scarlet.

[pause]

It was written by a young doctor, from Edinburgh, called Arthur Conan Doyle, who had a small practice, in Southsea, near Portsmouth, and wrote stories in the evenings, when there were no patients. And it introduced a new character: a detective, called Sherlock Holmes.

[long-pause]

In the story, a young army doctor, Doctor John Watson, comes back to London, from the war in Afghanistan, wounded, and tired, and with little money. He needs somewhere to live, and a friend introduces him to a strange young man, who is looking for someone to share his rooms, at 221B Baker Street.

[pause]

The young man is Sherlock Holmes. He is tall, and thin, with sharp eyes, and a hawk-like nose, and he is a consulting detective: when the police are puzzled, they come to him. He can tell, from a glance, where a person has been, and what they do, from the mud on their boots, and the marks on their hands, and the way they walk. When he first meets Watson, he says, you have been in Afghanistan, I perceive. And Watson is astonished.

[pause]

{img:victorian-sitting-room-221b-fireplace-armchairs-violin-chemistry-table}
Holmes's rooms, at 221B Baker Street, are comfortable, and untidy: a sitting room, with two armchairs, on each side of the fire, and a table, covered with chemistry equipment, and bottles, and test tubes, and papers, and books, everywhere, and his violin, which he plays, late at night, when he is thinking, and his pipe, and his slippers, full of tobacco.

[long-pause]

Their landlady, Mrs Hudson, brings them breakfast, and tea, and visitors come, at all hours of the day, and night, to ask for Holmes's help: worried ladies, and puzzled policemen, from Scotland Yard, and noblemen, and kings, in disguise.

[pause]

In 1888, Sherlock Holmes is not yet famous. A Study in Scarlet has been noticed, by a few readers, and in 1888, it is published as a book. The next story, The Sign of Four, will come in 1890. And then, in 1891, the stories will begin to appear, every month, in a new magazine, The Strand, with pictures, and Sherlock Holmes will become the most famous detective in the world.

[pause]

{img:holmes-watson-walking-foggy-london-street-gas-lamps-silhouettes}
The stories of Sherlock Holmes are full of the London of 1888: of hansom cabs, rattling through the fog, and gaslit streets, and railway stations, and telegrams, and the river, and the docks, and the great houses, and the poor streets, and the strange characters of the great city.

[long-pause]

Holmes knows London perfectly, every street, and alley, and he can find his way through it, even in the thickest fog. He has a gang of street boys, the Baker Street Irregulars, who run errands for him, and find things out, and are paid a shilling a day.

[pause]

And at the end of every story, after all the adventures, and the mysteries, and the explanations, Holmes, and Watson, sit by the fire, at 221B Baker Street, in their armchairs, with the fog pressing against the windows, and the cabs going by, in the street below, and the fire glowing, and Holmes takes up his violin, and plays, softly, into the night.

[pause]

{img:sherlock-holmes-museum-baker-street-today-blue-plaque-visitors}
Today, in Baker Street, there is a Sherlock Holmes Museum, at a house numbered 221B, with the sitting room, and the armchairs, and the violin, as they are described in the stories, and visitors come from all over the world, to see it. And letters still arrive, addressed to Mr Sherlock Holmes, asking for his help.

[long-pause]

{img:london-pea-souper-fog-street-gas-lamps-blurred-figures-yellow}
London, in 1888, is famous for its fog.

[pause]

In the autumn, and the winter, on still, cold days, the fog comes. It rises from the river, and the marshes, and mixes with the smoke, of millions of coal fires, and the factories, and the trains, and becomes thick, and yellow, and brown, and green, and settles over the city, sometimes for days.

[long-pause]

Londoners call it a pea-souper, because it is as thick, and yellow, as pea soup. And it is so thick, that you cannot see across the street, or sometimes even your own hand, held out in front of you.

[pause]

In the fog, the whole city changes. The gas lamps become little blurred yellow glows, which do not light anything. The buildings disappear. People loom up, out of the fog, and disappear again, like ghosts. The sounds change too: the cries of the street sellers, and the footsteps, and the horses' hooves, and the voices, are muffled, and strange, and seem to come from everywhere, and nowhere.

[pause]

In the thickest fog, the traffic stops. The cabs, and omnibuses, crawl along, slowly, and the drivers get down, and lead their horses, by the bridle. Boys, called link boys, carry flaming torches, and offer, for a penny, to guide people home, through the fog. The trains run slowly, and the signalmen put little explosive caps, called fog signals, on the rails, which go off with a bang, when the train passes over them, to warn the drivers.

[long-pause]

On the river, the boats stop, and their foghorns sound, low, and sad, through the mist. And the bells of the churches ring, muffled, through the fog.

[pause]

The fog is bad for people's health, and makes them cough. But it is also strangely beautiful. Writers, and painters, love it. Charles Dickens began his novel Bleak House with a famous description of fog: fog everywhere, fog up the river, fog down the river. And a French painter, Claude Monet, will come to London, a few years later, and paint the Houses of Parliament, and the bridges over the Thames, again and again, glowing, in the fog, in purple, and gold, and orange, and blue.

[pause]

{img:painterly-houses-of-parliament-fog-sunset-thames-purple-gold}
The American painter, James McNeill Whistler, who lives in Chelsea, beside the river, paints the Thames at night, in the mist, in soft blues, and greys, and golds, with the lights of the bridges, and the boats, glowing, through the darkness. He calls his paintings nocturnes, like pieces of music for the night.

[long-pause]

Inside the houses, on a day of fog, the gas lamps are lit, all day long, and the fires are piled high with coal, and people stay at home, if they can, and look out of the windows, at the blank, yellow nothing, outside, and draw the curtains, and sit by the fire, and read.

[pause]

{img:cosy-victorian-room-fire-window-fog-outside-cat-armchair-book}
And after a day, or two, or three, a wind comes, and the fog lifts, and drifts away, and the city appears again, wet, and shining, and grey, under a pale sky, and the people come out, into the streets, again.

[long-pause]

Much later, in 1952, after one terrible fog, which lasted for five days, the government will pass laws, to stop people burning coal, in the city, and slowly, the great London fogs will disappear. But in 1888, they are still part of London, as much as the river, and the Tower, and the sound of Big Ben.

[long-pause]

{img:lamplighter-long-pole-lighting-gas-lamp-dusk-london-street}
Every evening, at dusk, in the streets of London, the lamplighters come.

[pause]

There are about five hundred of them, and each one has his own round, of streets, and lamps, perhaps a hundred, or more. They carry a long pole, with a little flame at the top, and a hook, and they walk quickly, from lamp to lamp, and reach up, and open the little glass door, at the top of the lamp, and turn on the gas, and light it, and move on, to the next one, without stopping.

[long-pause]

Behind them, one by one, the lamps come on, all along the street, small, soft, yellow lights, in the blue dusk, like a string of beads, stretching away, into the distance. Children run along behind the lamplighters, to watch, and the lamplighters wave to them.

[pause]

And in the morning, at dawn, they come again, and put the lamps out, one by one, and clean the glass, and trim the burners, and go home, to sleep.

[pause]

{img:gaslit-london-square-evening-lamps-glowing-rain-wet-pavement}
The gas comes from the gasworks, great factories, beside the river, and the canals, where coal is heated, in great ovens, to make the gas, which is stored in great round gasholders, of iron, which rise up, and sink down, as they fill, and empty, and is sent, through pipes, under the streets, to every lamp, and every house, in London.

[long-pause]

Gas light is soft, and warm, and golden, and flickering. It does not light up the whole street, like the electric lights of today, but makes little pools of light, under each lamp, with shadows, in between. On a misty evening, each lamp has a soft halo, around it, and on a rainy evening, the light shines, and shimmers, on the wet pavements, and the cobblestones.

[pause]

Inside the houses, too, there is gas light, from lamps on the walls, and hanging from the ceiling, which hiss gently, and must be lit with a match, and turned up, and down, with a little tap. In the poorer homes, there are oil lamps, and candles.

[pause]

{img:victorian-bedroom-candle-washstand-window-gaslamp-street-below}
And when people go up to bed, they carry a candle, in a candlestick, up the dark stairs, and the little flame makes shadows dance, on the walls, and the ceiling. And from the bedroom window, they can look down, into the street, and see the gas lamp, below, glowing softly, all through the night, and the policeman, walking slowly past, on his beat, and hear the distant clatter of a cab, going home, and the church clock, striking the hours.

[long-pause]

Electric light is coming. Already, there are electric lights, in a few streets, and theatres, and grand houses, bright, and white, and steady. But for many years yet, the soft, golden gaslight will light the streets of London, and the lamplighters will walk their rounds, at dusk, and dawn.

[pause]

And even today, in some streets of London, in Covent Garden, and Westminster, and around the royal parks, the old gas lamps are still burning, every evening, as they did in 1888.

[long-pause]

{img:queen-victoria-elderly-black-dress-white-cap-windsor-castle-window}
Over all of this, there is the old Queen.

[pause]

Queen Victoria came to the throne in 1837, when she was only eighteen, a small, young girl, with blue eyes, and fair hair, woken early one morning, at Kensington Palace, to be told that her uncle, the King, had died, and that she was now the Queen.

[long-pause]

Now, in 1888, she is sixty-nine years old, and has reigned for more than fifty years, longer than almost any monarch before her. She is small, and stout, and dressed always in black, with a little white lace cap, or a small crown, and she looks stern, in her photographs, though those who know her say that she laughs, and has a lovely smile.

[pause]

She has dressed in black since 1861, when her beloved husband, Prince Albert, died, of typhoid fever, at Windsor Castle, at the age of only forty-two. She was heartbroken, and for many years, she hid away from the world, at Windsor, and at her houses at Osborne, on the Isle of Wight, and at Balmoral, in the Highlands of Scotland, and hardly ever came to London. People called her the Widow of Windsor.

[pause]

{img:golden-jubilee-1887-procession-open-carriage-crowds-flags-london}
But in June 1887, last year, she came out, to celebrate her Golden Jubilee, fifty years on the throne.

[long-pause]

There was a great procession, through the streets of London, from Buckingham Palace, to Westminster Abbey, for a service of thanksgiving. The Queen rode in an open carriage, drawn by cream-coloured horses, surrounded by princes, from all over Europe, many of them her own sons, and grandsons, and sons-in-law, on horseback, and soldiers, from all over the Empire, from India, and Canada, and Australia. She refused to wear a crown, and wore a bonnet, instead, decorated with diamonds, and lace.

[pause]

The streets were lined with cheering crowds, hundreds of thousands of people, and decorated with flags, and banners, and flowers. And in the evening, all over the country, bonfires were lit, on the hills, one after another, and there were fireworks, and parties, and dinners, for the poor, and the children were given jubilee mugs, and medals.

[pause]

{img:jubilee-street-party-children-tables-bunting-mugs-1887}
The Queen wrote in her diary that she would never forget that day, and that the crowds were so kind, and enthusiastic.

[long-pause]

The Queen has nine children, and many grandchildren, who have married into the royal families of Europe, so that she is called the grandmother of Europe. Her eldest daughter, Victoria, is the Empress of Germany. In 1888, there are three emperors of Germany, one after another: her daughter's father-in-law dies, in March, and then her daughter's husband, Frederick, who was very ill, reigns for only ninety-nine days, and dies, in June, and her grandson, William, becomes Kaiser, at the age of twenty-nine.

[pause]

She works hard, every day, reading her government papers, in their red boxes, and writing letters, and her diary, and seeing her ministers. In 1888, her Prime Minister is Lord Salisbury.

[pause]

{img:queen-victoria-indian-secretary-garden-tent-writing-letters}
And she is learning a new language. In 1887, two young Indian servants came to work for her, and one of them, Abdul Karim, became her teacher, her Munshi, and teaches her to speak, and write, Hindustani, a little every day, and tells her about India, which she rules, as Empress, but has never seen.

[long-pause]

To the people of London, the Queen is far away, and a little mysterious, rarely seen, but always there, like the river, and the Tower. Her face is on every coin, and every stamp, and in every schoolroom, and her name is everywhere: on the stations, and the streets, and the parks, and the docks, and the pubs, and, soon, a whole age will be named after her. When she dies, in 1901, after nearly sixty-four years on the throne, it will seem to many people as if the world itself has ended.

[long-pause]

{img:victorian-cricket-match-oval-gentlemen-whites-crowd-gasholder}
The Victorians love sport.

[pause]

Many of the great sports of the world were given their rules, in Victorian England, in the years before 1888: football, and rugby, and lawn tennis, and boxing, and many others.

[long-pause]

Cricket is the great summer game. In 1888, the Australian team is touring England, and in the summer, they play England, in three test matches, at Lord's Cricket Ground, in north London, and at the Oval, in Kennington, south of the river, and at Old Trafford, in Manchester. Australia win the first, at Lord's, but England win the other two, and keep the Ashes.

[pause]

The crowds come in their thousands, and sit in the sunshine, in their straw hats, and watch the slow, peaceful game, all day long, with the sound of the bat on the ball, and the polite applause, drifting across the green grass.

[pause]

And football is becoming the great game of the working people.

[long-pause]

The rules of football were written in London, in 1863, by the Football Association, at a meeting in a pub, the Freemasons' Tavern. And in 1888, the first Football League is started, with twelve clubs, from the north, and the midlands, of England, where football is most popular. The first league season begins in September 1888. The FA Cup final, in March 1888, is played at the Oval, in London, before nearly twenty thousand people.

[pause]

On Saturday afternoons, now that many workers have the afternoon off, the crowds go to watch, in their thousands, in their cloth caps, and scarves, and cheer, and shout, and go home, happy, or disappointed.

[pause]

{img:lawn-tennis-victorian-ladies-long-dresses-garden-net-summer}
Lawn tennis is very new, and very fashionable. It was invented in the 1870s, and the first championships, at Wimbledon, were held in 1877. Ladies, and gentlemen, play it, in their gardens, and at clubs, all over London, the ladies in long dresses, and hats.

[long-pause]

And there is the bicycle.

[pause]

For years, there have been bicycles in London, but they were difficult, and dangerous: the penny-farthing, with a huge front wheel, and a tiny back wheel, and the rider sitting, high up, on top, so that, if he hit a stone, he would fly over the handlebars. Only brave young men rode them.

[pause]

{img:safety-bicycle-1888-young-woman-riding-park-road-hat}
But in 1885, a new bicycle was invented, the Rover safety bicycle, with two wheels of the same size, and a chain, driving the back wheel, like the bicycles of today. And in 1888, a Scottish vet, John Boyd Dunlop, invents the pneumatic tyre, a tyre filled with air, to make his little son's tricycle more comfortable, on the bumpy roads.

[long-pause]

Together, these two inventions will cause a great bicycle craze, in the 1890s. Everyone, men, and women, young, and old, will ride bicycles. And for women, especially, the bicycle will bring a new freedom, to go where they like, alone, or with friends, and to wear lighter, simpler clothes.

[pause]

On a sunny Saturday afternoon, in 1888, in Battersea Park, the first young ladies, and gentlemen, on their safety bicycles, wobble along the paths, under the trees, ringing their bells, and laughing, while the older people watch, and shake their heads.

[long-pause]

{img:london-1890s-electric-tram-motor-car-horse-cab-street-change}
In the years after 1888, London changes, quickly.

[pause]

In 1889, a new council, the London County Council, is created, to govern the whole great city, for the first time, and to build new houses, and schools, and parks, and roads, and fire stations, and, later, to clear away the worst slums.

[long-pause]

In 1890, the first electric tube railway opens, the City and South London. Others follow, deep under the city, the Central London Railway, in 1900, nicknamed the twopenny tube, and many more. In 1894, Tower Bridge is finished, and opened. In the 1890s, the first motor cars appear, on the streets of London, noisy, and smelly, and slow, frightening the horses. And in 1904, the first motor buses. Slowly, over the next thirty years, the horses disappear, from the streets.

[pause]

Electric light spreads, through the streets, and the houses, of London, and the gas lamps, one by one, go out. Though, even today, there are still about fifteen hundred gas lamps, in the streets of London, lit every evening, by the gas company.

[pause]

{img:queen-victoria-diamond-jubilee-1897-procession-st-pauls-crowds}
In 1897, the Queen celebrates her Diamond Jubilee, sixty years on the throne, with an even greater procession, to Saint Paul's Cathedral. And in January 1901, she dies, at Osborne House, on the Isle of Wight, with her family around her, at the age of eighty-one, after reigning for nearly sixty-four years. Her son becomes King Edward the Seventh.

[long-pause]

The Victorian age ends. But the London of 1888 lives on: in its buildings, and its streets, and its parks, and its museums, and its railways, and its bridges, and its sewers, and its schools, and its markets, many of which are still there, today.

[pause]

And it lives on in the books of the time: in the stories of Sherlock Holmes, and in the novels of Charles Dickens, and in Robert Louis Stevenson's Strange Case of Doctor Jekyll and Mister Hyde, published in 1886, and Oscar Wilde's The Picture of Dorian Gray, and his fairy tales, The Happy Prince, published in 1888, and H. G. Wells's The Time Machine, in which a Victorian gentleman travels into the far future. And in Peter Pan, by J. M. Barrie, who, a few years later, will walk his big dog in Kensington Gardens, and meet the little boys who will inspire him.

[pause]

{img:victorian-london-book-illustration-style-library-reading-lamp-fire}
And it lives on in the memories of the people who lived then.

[long-pause]

The children of 1888, who played hopscotch on the pavements, and sat in the rows of desks, at the new Board schools, and rode on the tops of the omnibuses, will grow up, and live through the great changes of the twentieth century: two world wars, and motor cars, and aeroplanes, and radio, and television. Some of them will live to see men walk on the moon, in 1969. And they will tell their grandchildren what London was like, when they were young: about the gas lamps, and the horses, and the fog, and the muffin man, ringing his bell, in the street, on a winter afternoon.

[long-pause]

{img:tower-bridge-today-raised-tall-ship-passing-sunset-london}
Today, London is still one of the great cities of the world, with nearly nine million people, from every country on earth.

[pause]

Much of the London of 1888 is still there, if you know where to look.

[long-pause]

Tower Bridge still stands, beside the Tower of London, and still opens, for the tall ships, and visitors can walk across the high walkways, between the towers, which now have glass floors, and look down, at the river, and the traffic, far below.

[pause]

The Houses of Parliament, and Big Ben, still stand beside the river. The great museums of South Kensington are still there, free, and open to everyone, and the children still come, to see the dinosaurs, in the great hall of the Natural History Museum, under the terracotta arches, with the carved animals, peering down.

[pause]

{img:leadenhall-market-today-victorian-glass-roof-painted-iron-lunch-crowd}
Leadenhall Market, in the City, with its glass roof, and its painted iron arches, in red, and cream, and gold, still stands, and is full of people, at lunchtime, eating, and drinking, as they did in 1888. Covent Garden market has moved away, but the old market building is still there, full of shops, and cafés, and street performers. And Billingsgate's fish market has moved, too, but its old building still stands, beside the river.

[long-pause]

The Underground still runs, under the streets, and the old stations, of the Metropolitan, at Baker Street, with their brick arches, are still used, every day, by thousands of people. And in Baker Street, there is the little Sherlock Holmes Museum, and a statue of the great detective, outside the station, in his deerstalker hat.

[pause]

The old Board schools, built of yellow and red brick, with their tall windows, and their bell towers, still stand, all over London, many of them still schools, full of children, and some of them turned into flats.

[pause]

{img:regents-park-london-today-autumn-trees-families-walking-path}
The parks are still there, green, and peaceful, in the middle of the city, with the lakes, and the swans, and the pelicans of Saint James's Park, and the boats on the Serpentine, and the children sailing their toy boats on the Round Pond, in Kensington Gardens, and the Zoo, in Regent's Park.

[long-pause]

And the river still flows, through the middle of the city, past the Houses of Parliament, and Saint Paul's, and the Tower, and under Tower Bridge, toward the sea, as it has done for thousands of years, though the great docks are quiet now, turned into homes, and offices, and marinas, and the great towers of Canary Wharf, where the West India Docks once were.

[pause]

So we leave London tonight, in the year 1888, as the evening falls.

[pause]

{img:victorian-london-night-thames-embankment-gas-lamps-big-ben-quiet}
The lamplighters are going along the streets, with their long poles, lighting the gas lamps, one by one, and the soft yellow lights come on, all along the Embankment, and reflect in the dark, quiet river. The last omnibuses are going home, with their horses, tired, and slow. In the houses of Kensington, the families are sitting by the fire, and the housemaids are climbing the stairs, to their little attic rooms. And in Baker Street, a light is still burning, in an upstairs window. And over the rooftops, and the chimneys, and the river, Big Ben strikes the hour, deep, and slow, and soft, through the evening mist.
