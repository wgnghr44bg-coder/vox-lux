Tonight we go nearly a hundred and forty years back. We go to London, in the year 1888.

[long-pause]

It is an autumn evening. The light is fading over the great city. Along the river Thames, the gas lamps are being lit, one by one. Their soft yellow light shines on the wet stones by the river. It shines on the dark water, where barges and little steamboats are heading home.

[pause]

Across the river stand the Houses of Parliament, golden and grey. The great clock tower rises above them. Its bell, Big Ben, strikes the hour, deep and slow, over the rooftops. And out on the water, a thin mist is starting to rise.

[pause]

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

In 1888, London is the greatest city in the world.

[pause]

{img:victorian-london-rooftops-chimneys-smoke-st-pauls-dome-autumn}
It is the capital of the British Empire. That empire is the biggest the world has ever seen. It stretches across a quarter of the earth, from Canada to India to Australia. People say the sun never sets on it. And London is the biggest city on earth. More than five and a half million people live here. No city has ever had so many before.

[pause]

Ships come up the Thames from every corner of the world. They bring tea and sugar, cotton and wool, spices and wood. Money flows through the banks and offices. Railways run out of London in every direction. And people come from everywhere to live and work here.

[long-pause]

It is a city of great wealth and of great poverty. There are grand houses on quiet squares, with gardens and servants. And there are crowded narrow streets where a whole family lives in one room. It is a city of new inventions, like the telephone and electric light and trains under the ground. And it is a city of old ways, too. Horses and gas lamps, coal fires and fog.

[pause]

Queen Victoria has been on the throne for more than fifty years. Last year she celebrated it. The whole city was covered in flags and flowers. There were lights and fireworks. Crowds filled the streets to see her ride by in her open carriage.

[pause]

{img:hansom-cab-horse-wet-cobbled-street-gaslight-evening-london}
Tonight we are going to walk through the streets of Victorian London.

[long-pause]

We will ride on top of a bus pulled by horses. We will travel under the ground on steam trains. We will visit a family home and spend a day with a young maid. We will walk through the markets and down to the great docks on the river. We will see a new bridge rising beside the Tower of London. We will spend an evening at the theatre. And we will walk through the fog with the most famous detective in the world. He has only just been born, in the pages of a magazine.

[pause]

So let us step out into the evening and the gaslight. Listen to the horses' hooves on the wet stones. And let us begin.

[long-pause]

{img:victorian-london-busy-street-crowds-omnibuses-shop-signs-afternoon}
In 1888, London is enormous.

[pause]

It has grown and grown all through the century. In 1800, about a million people lived here. Now there are more than five and a half million. And every year tens of thousands more arrive. They come from the countryside and from Ireland and Scotland. They come from all over Europe and the world. They are looking for work and a new life.

[long-pause]

The city spreads out for miles in every direction. Street after street of houses and shops. Churches and factories and warehouses. Railway lines and chimneys. The city swallows up the old villages and fields around it. Places that were quiet villages fifty years ago are now part of London. Long straight streets of new brick houses join them to the centre.

[pause]

Every morning, hundreds of thousands of people pour into the centre to work. They come by train and by bus and on foot across the bridges. And every evening they pour out again.

[pause]

{img:london-bridge-crowds-workers-walking-morning-fog-carts}
The heart of London is the City. It is the old square mile inside the ancient walls. The Romans built a town here nearly two thousand years ago. Now it is full of banks and offices. From here, people run the trade of half the world. In the daytime its streets are packed with clerks in black coats and top hats. They hurry from office to office. At night and on Sundays it is almost empty and silent.

[long-pause]

To the west is the West End. This is the London of the rich and the fashionable. There are tall white houses and quiet squares. There are the great shops and the theatres and the parks.

[pause]

To the east is the East End. This is the London of the docks and the workshops and the poor. It is crowded and busy and noisy. Sailors and dock workers live here. So do newcomers from far away, and workers of a thousand trades.

[pause]

{img:victorian-terraced-houses-new-suburb-street-brick-lamp-posts}
And all around are the suburbs. This is the new London of clerks and shopkeepers and teachers. They live in long rows of neat brick houses. Each house has a little front garden and a bay window and iron railings.

[long-pause]

London is noisy. Iron wheels and horses' hooves clatter on the stones. Street sellers shout. Carts rumble. Trains whistle and church bells ring.

[pause]

And London is dirty. The air is full of smoke from a million coal fires. The streets are full of mud and horse droppings. Little boys with brooms sweep a path across the road for ladies and gentlemen. In return they hope for a penny.

[long-pause]

But London is also magnificent and exciting and full of life. Every visitor who comes here, from anywhere in the world, is amazed by it.

[long-pause]

{img:st-pancras-station-train-shed-iron-glass-roof-steam-trains-1888}
Around the centre of London there is a ring of great railway stations.

[pause]

Each one was built by a different railway company, forty or fifty years ago. Each stands at the end of its own line into London. There is Euston and King's Cross and Saint Pancras in the north. There is Paddington in the west. There is Waterloo and Victoria in the south, and Liverpool Street in the east.

[long-pause]

They are like cathedrals of the new age of steam. The grandest of them all is Saint Pancras. In front stands a huge hotel of red brick. It looks like a palace, with towers and spires and a great clock tower. Behind it is the train shed. It is one enormous arch of iron and glass, more than seventy metres wide. When it was built, no roof like it in the world was wider. Under it are the platforms and the trains, filled with light and steam and smoke.

[pause]

Next door is King's Cross. It has two big plain arches of yellow brick, side by side. Trains leave from here for the north and for Scotland.

[pause]

{img:paddington-station-platform-crowds-porters-luggage-steam-locomotive}
Inside the stations there is always noise and bustle and excitement.

[long-pause]

The great steam engines stand at the platforms, hissing and puffing. They are painted in the bright colours of their companies. Green and red and blue and brown, with shining brass. The drivers lean out of the cabs and wait for the signal.

[pause]

Porters in uniform push carts piled with trunks and hatboxes and bags. Families hurry along the platforms looking for their carriage. Newspaper boys sell papers and magazines and cheap novels for the journey. And the clocks tick above the platforms. They all show exactly the same time.

[long-pause]

That is new. Before the railways, every town kept its own time, by the sun. In Bristol, the clocks were ten minutes behind London. But the railways needed timetables. A train could not leave at two different times. So the whole country agreed on one time, London time. A few years ago, the whole world agreed on something too. A line through Greenwich, in London, would be the starting line for the world's time and its maps. It is still called Greenwich Mean Time.

[pause]

{img:victorian-railway-carriage-compartment-passengers-window-countryside}
From the stations, trains go out to every part of the country. They go to the seaside, where Londoners go for a day out in their thousands. They go to the big cities of the north and to Scotland. And they go to the ports, for ships to France and Ireland and America.

[long-pause]

The station hotels are grand and comfortable. The station cafes serve tea and sandwiches and soup. The book stalls sell books for the journey.

[pause]

At night the great stations are lit by gas. The last trains come in from the country. The passengers are sleepy and tired. And the porters and the cab drivers wait by the platforms to take them home, through the dark quiet streets.

[long-pause]

{img:horse-omnibus-top-deck-passengers-knifeboard-seat-oxford-street}
Let us ride through London on top of an omnibus.

[pause]

An omnibus is a big wagon pulled by two horses. It is a kind of bus. It is painted in a bright colour, red or green or yellow or blue, depending on its route. The names of the places it goes to are painted on its side in big letters. And there are adverts for soap and tea and cocoa.

[long-pause]

Inside, two long benches face each other. Passengers sit there in the warm, a little squashed together. In winter, there is straw on the floor to keep their feet warm. But on top, on the roof, there is a long bench where the passengers sit back to back. From there you can see the whole city passing by below. It is cheaper, and much more fun.

[pause]

In the past, ladies did not sit on top. They would have had to climb a little iron ladder in long skirts. But the new buses have proper stairs at the back. And more and more ladies climb up.

[pause]

{img:busy-victorian-street-carriages-carts-hansom-cabs-crossing-sweeper}
From the top of the bus, we see all the traffic of London below us.

[long-pause]

There are hundreds of other buses. There are hansom cabs, small fast carriages with two wheels and one horse. The driver sits high up behind and holds the reins over the roof. They are the taxis of Victorian London. There are slow heavy cabs with four wheels for families with luggage. There are shiny private carriages with drivers in uniform. And there are carts and wagons full of beer barrels and coal and vegetables and furniture. Big heavy horses pull them.

[pause]

There are trams on rails, pulled by horses. There are barrows pushed by hand. And now and then a new bicycle weaves through the traffic.

[long-pause]

There are probably about three hundred thousand horses in London. They need stables and hay and oats and water. They need blacksmiths. And they fill the streets with their smell and their sound.

[pause]

The traffic is often terrible. At the busiest corners, the carts and cabs and buses get packed so tight that nothing can move. For minutes at a time, everyone is stuck. The drivers shout and argue. And the policemen in their tall helmets try to sort it out.

[pause]

{img:london-policeman-tall-helmet-directing-traffic-busy-junction}
The streets are lined with shops with big glass windows, lit by gas. There are signs and adverts painted on the walls. Men walk slowly along the gutter with one advert board hanging in front of them and another behind. People call them sandwich men.

[long-pause]

The pavements are crowded. There are clerks and shop girls. There are gentlemen in top hats and ladies in long dresses with little hats. There are workmen in caps and nursemaids pushing prams. Flower girls sell violets. Newspaper boys shout the headlines. And the muffin man rings his bell, with a tray of warm muffins on his head.

[pause]

And from the top of the bus, swaying gently, we watch it all go by.

[long-pause]

{img:victoria-embankment-thames-gas-lamps-trees-granite-wall-river-1888}
Beside the Thames, in the middle of London, there is a long wide new road along the river. It is called the Embankment.

[pause]

It has a wide pavement and plane trees. A granite wall runs along the river. On the wall stand lamps on iron posts, with dolphins twisting around them. Beside the road there are gardens with flowers and lawns and statues.

[long-pause]

It looks simple. But underneath it is one of the great wonders of Victorian London.

[pause]

Thirty years ago, there was a terrible summer. People called it the Great Stink. In those days, all the dirty water of London flowed straight into the Thames. Millions of people's waste. In the summer heat, the river smelled so bad that people could hardly breathe. The members of Parliament sit right next to the river. They hung curtains soaked in chemicals at their windows. But it did not help. At last they decided something had to be done.

[pause]

{img:bazalgette-engineer-inspecting-brick-sewer-tunnel-workers-lamps}
They chose an engineer called Joseph Bazalgette. He was a small quiet man with a big moustache. He planned a huge system of sewers under the whole of London. A sewer is a tunnel for dirty water. He planned more than a hundred and thirty kilometres of big brick tunnels. And many hundreds of kilometres of smaller ones under the streets. They would carry the dirty water far down the river, away from the houses.

[long-pause]

It took nearly twenty years to build. It used more than three hundred million bricks. Part of it was built under the new Embankment. Under the same road run the underground railway and pipes for gas and water. All hidden under the road and the gardens.

[pause]

There are big pumping stations along the way. They lift the water so it keeps flowing. And they are beautiful, like palaces or churches. One of them, by the river, is painted inside in red and green and gold. Its ironwork is so fine that visitors call it the cathedral on the marsh.

[pause]

{img:crossness-pumping-station-ornate-painted-ironwork-beam-engines}
Bazalgette's sewers changed London. Before, a sickness called cholera came back again and again. It spread through dirty water, and thousands of people died. Now it stopped. The river became cleaner. And the city became healthier.

[long-pause]

Bazalgette built his tunnels bigger than they needed to be. We will only do this once, he said. We must think of the future. Because of that, his sewers are still used today, more than a hundred and fifty years later. They serve a city of nearly nine million people.

[pause]

There is a monument to him on the Embankment. And the Embankment, with its plane trees and its dolphin lamps, is still one of the loveliest places in London to walk in the evening, beside the river.

[long-pause]

{img:victorian-underground-station-steam-locomotive-gas-lamps-platform-smoke}
Under the streets of London there is something no other city in the world has yet. A railway under the ground.

[pause]

The first underground railway in the world opened in London twenty-five years ago. It ran from Paddington to Farringdon, about six kilometres, under a big road.

[long-pause]

It was built in a simple way. The builders dug a big ditch along the middle of the street. They built a brick tunnel inside it. Then they covered it over and put the street back on top. For years the streets were a mess. But when it was finished, it was a great success. On the first day, nearly forty thousand people rode on it.

[pause]

By 1888, the lines have grown and joined together. They make a big loop around the centre of London. And there are branches running out to the suburbs.

[pause]

{img:victorian-underground-train-carriages-first-class-gaslit-passengers}
The trains are pulled by steam engines. They run through the tunnels, puffing out smoke and steam. The engines are built to make as little smoke as they can. But still the tunnels and stations are often full of smoke. They smell of sulphur, like rotten eggs. And passengers sometimes arrive with black smudges on their faces.

[long-pause]

Between the tunnels there are open parts where the smoke can escape. The stations have glass roofs to let the smoke out and the daylight in. Some people say the air in the tunnels is good for your lungs. Most people do not agree.

[pause]

The carriages have first, second and third class. They are lit by gas lamps. Every morning and evening they are full of clerks and workers. Many ride on cheap early tickets for workmen.

[pause]

{img:tunnel-digging-workers-shield-clay-candles-1880s-deep-tube}
And now, in 1888, something new is being built, deep under the ground.

[long-pause]

Under the river Thames and under the City, engineers are digging a new kind of railway. It is not just under the street. It is deep down, in round iron tunnels. The workers push a big iron shield forward through the clay, inch by inch. They work by candlelight, far below the busy streets. Above them, the river flows. Will the tunnel hold? Day after day, they push on.

[pause]

It will be called the City and South London Railway. Its trains will not be pulled by steam. They will run on electricity. It will open in two years' time. It will be the first deep electric underground railway in the world. And because of its round tunnels, Londoners will soon give it a name. The tube.

[long-pause]

From these beginnings will grow the London Underground. Today it is a great web of lines under the whole city. Millions of people use it every day. Some of the old stations, like Baker Street, can still be seen and used today, with their brick walls and arches.

[long-pause]

{img:victorian-london-docks-tall-ships-masts-warehouses-cranes-busy}
The heart of London's wealth is the river Thames and its great docks.

[pause]

All through the century, London's traders have built huge docks along the river. They lie to the east of the Tower of London. Each has its own name, after the places its ships trade with. There are the West India Docks and the East India Docks. And there are many more, further down the river.

[long-pause]

The docks are huge square lakes, dug out of the marshes. High walls surround them. Big brick warehouses stand around them, five or six floors high. The goods of the whole world are stored inside.

[pause]

Ships come up the Thames from the sea. They are let into the docks through gates and tied up along the quays. There are tall sailing ships with forests of masts. There are steamships with painted chimneys. There are the little Thames barges with their red-brown sails. And there are tugboats, puffing and whistling.

[pause]

{img:dockers-unloading-tea-chests-ship-hold-ropes-cranes-warehouse}
And everywhere there are dockers. Thousands of men unload and load the ships. They carry sacks and bales and barrels and chests on their backs. Or they use ropes and hand cranes and carts.

[long-pause]

The warehouses are full of wonderful things and wonderful smells. There is tea from India and China in wooden chests. There is coffee and cocoa and sugar. There are spices, like pepper and cinnamon and cloves. There is wine in great barrels in the cellars. There is wool from Australia and wood from Canada. And there is meat, frozen in the new cold ships.

[pause]

Visitors are sometimes taken down into the great wine cellars under the docks. They stretch for kilometres, lit by lamps. Thousands of barrels lie in long rows in the cool dark. It smells of wine and old wood.

[long-pause]

But the dockers have a hard life. Most of them have no steady work. Every morning hundreds of them gather at the dock gates. They hope to be picked for a day's work, or half a day. The foremen walk along and point. You, and you, and you. Those who are picked work hard all day for a few pennies an hour. Those who are not picked go home with nothing. And tomorrow they will stand at the gate again, and hope.

[pause]

{img:thames-river-traffic-barges-red-sails-steamboat-tower-of-london}
Next year the dockers will stop work together, peacefully. They will ask for a fair wage of sixpence an hour. Many people will support them. And they will win.

[long-pause]

On the river itself there is always traffic. Barges carry goods from the ships to the quays. Little passenger steamboats carry people up and down the river, from pier to pier. Boatmen row their small boats across. And the river police keep watch.

[pause]

At night, the lights of the ships and the quays shine on the dark water. And the river flows on quietly toward the sea.

[long-pause]

{img:tower-bridge-under-construction-1888-steel-frames-towers-scaffolding}
Beside the Tower of London, in 1888, something new is rising from the river.

[pause]

Two huge bases of granite and concrete have been sunk into the bottom of the Thames. On top of them, workers are building two tall towers of steel. It is a new bridge. It will be called Tower Bridge.

[long-pause]

For years, the people of the East End have needed a new bridge here. But there is a problem. A bridge must not stop the tall ships. They need to sail up the river to the quays beside the Tower.

[pause]

So the builders thought of something clever. The middle of the bridge will have two great arms. They can be lifted up, like a drawbridge, to let tall ships pass through. Then they can be lowered again for the traffic.

[pause]

{img:workers-riveting-steel-high-tower-bridge-river-below-boats}
The building began two years ago. It will take eight years. Hundreds of men are working on it. They join the steel together high above the river, with hot metal pins. There are no safety nets. One wrong step, and they would fall into the water far below. So they move slowly and carefully, every day.

[long-pause]

The towers are made of steel to make them strong. But they will be covered with stone. That way they will look like old towers, to match the Tower of London beside them. High up between the towers there will be two walkways. People can cross there even when the bridge is open.

[pause]

The great arms will be lifted by steam engines hidden inside the bases. They will rise smoothly and quickly, in about a minute.

[long-pause]

When it is finished, the prince will open it. And it will become the most famous bridge in the world, the symbol of London.

[pause]

{img:tower-of-london-white-tower-walls-river-thames-autumn-afternoon}
Beside it stands the Tower of London. It is already eight hundred years old. It has watched over everything since William the Conqueror built it. The ravens hop about on the grass. The guards in their dark blue uniforms show visitors around and tell them the old stories. And the crown jewels glitter behind their railings.

[long-pause]

In 1888, many Londoners come to watch the new bridge being built. They stand on the river bank and look at the great steel towers. Every month they rise a little higher. Will it really open in the middle? Will it work? People wonder what it will look like when it is done.

[pause]

Today, more than a hundred and thirty years later, Tower Bridge still opens several hundred times a year. It opens for tall ships and sailing boats. The traffic stops. People stop and watch. And the great arms rise slowly into the sky.

[long-pause]

{img:victorian-kensington-tall-white-stucco-houses-square-garden-railings}
Let us visit a family home in Kensington, in the west of London.

[pause]

The house stands on a quiet square. In the middle of the square is a private garden with tall plane trees and lawns and flowers. Iron railings surround it. Only the families who live on the square have a key. The houses around it are tall and narrow and white. They have pillars by the front doors and black iron railings. Steps lead down to a lower floor under the street.

[long-pause]

The family who live here are comfortable, but not very rich. The father is a lawyer in the City. There is the mother, four children and a grandmother. And like most families of their kind in London, they have servants. There is a cook and a housemaid. There is a nursemaid for the little children. And a boy comes in every day to clean the boots and carry the coal.

[pause]

The house has five floors. Down below the street are the kitchen and the cool store rooms. There is a coal cellar under the pavement. And there is a room where the servants eat.

[pause]

{img:victorian-parlour-drawing-room-fireplace-piano-ferns-gaslight}
On the ground floor are the dining room and the father's study. On the first floor is the drawing room. It is the best room in the house. Here the mother welcomes her visitors in the afternoon. And here the family sits together in the evening.

[long-pause]

The drawing room is full of things. Victorians love things. There are heavy velvet curtains with lace behind them. The wallpaper has patterns and so do the carpets. There is a big marble fireplace with a mirror above it. On the shelf stands a clock under a glass dome, with china figures and photos in silver frames. There are armchairs and a sofa and little tables. There is a piano. There are ferns and palms in pots. And a canary sings in its cage.

[pause]

In the evening, the room is lit by gas lamps on the walls. They give a soft warm golden light, with a gentle hiss. And a coal fire burns in the grate.

[pause]

{img:victorian-nursery-children-rocking-horse-toys-nanny-fireside}
Above are the bedrooms. And near the top of the house is the nursery. The children live there. They play and have lessons and eat their meals there, with the nursemaid. At the very top, under the roof, are little attic rooms where the servants sleep. They are cold in winter and hot in summer.

[long-pause]

The children do not see much of their parents during the day. After tea, they come down to the drawing room for an hour. They are washed and brushed and in their best clothes. They play quietly and talk. Perhaps their mother reads to them. Then they go back up to the nursery for supper and bed.

[pause]

In the nursery there is a rocking horse and a doll's house. There are tin soldiers and a wooden Noah's ark with pairs of animals. There are picture books. And there is a big guard in front of the fire, where the children's night clothes hang to warm before bed.

[pause]

{img:victorian-family-dinner-table-silver-candles-father-carving}
In the evening, the parents have dinner in the dining room, at seven or eight. There is soup and fish, and then roast beef carved by the father at the table. There are vegetables and potatoes. Then there is a pudding and fruit and cheese. The housemaid serves it in her black dress and white apron.

[long-pause]

After dinner they go up to the drawing room. The mother plays the piano and the eldest daughter sings. The father reads the newspaper. The grandmother sews by the fire. The gas lamps hiss softly. The clock ticks under its glass dome. And outside, in the square, the lamps are lit, and the rain falls gently on the trees.

[long-pause]

{img:young-housemaid-lighting-kitchen-range-dawn-basement-candle}
Let us spend a day with the housemaid of the house in Kensington.

[pause]

Her name is Annie. She is sixteen. She comes from a village in the country north of London. Her father works on a farm. Two years ago she came to London to work as a servant. Most village girls do. There is little other work for them.

[long-pause]

Her day begins at six or earlier, in the dark. She gets up in her little attic room at the top of the house. She shares it with the nursemaid. She washes in cold water from a jug. She puts on her work dress, her apron and her cap. And she creeps down all the stairs to the kitchen with a candle.

[pause]

First she lights the big iron kitchen stove, so the cook can make breakfast and heat water. Then she goes up to the other rooms. She cleans out every fireplace. She sweeps out the ashes. She polishes the grates with black polish until they shine. And she lays new fires and lights them. She must be quick. The family must not come down to a cold house.

[pause]

{img:housemaid-scrubbing-front-steps-whitening-morning-street}
Then she sweeps and dusts the downstairs rooms. She opens the curtains. She scrubs the front steps and whitens them with a special stone. Soon they gleam white in the morning sun, for everyone on the square to see.

[long-pause]

At half past seven she carries cans of hot water up the stairs to the bedrooms, so the family can wash. Then she helps the cook carry up breakfast. Porridge and eggs and bacon and toast and tea.

[pause]

When the family is at breakfast, she goes up to make the beds and clean the bedrooms. Many times a day she carries heavy buckets of coal from the cellar up to all the fires. She answers the front door. And she answers the bells. In the kitchen there is a row of bells on springs. Each one has the name of a room. When a bell rings, she must hurry to that room.

[pause]

{img:servants-hall-basement-tea-break-cook-housemaid-bells-wall}
In the afternoon she changes into her black dress and a white apron with frills. Now she is ready to open the door to visitors. She brings in the tea for the mother and her guests.

[long-pause]

In the evening she helps to serve dinner. She clears the table and washes up with the cook. She turns down the beds and fills the hot water bottles. She closes the shutters and puts out the lights.

[pause]

It is a long day. She works about fifteen hours, with only short rests. She eats her meals downstairs with the cook and the nursemaid. She has one afternoon off a week. Every other Sunday she has the afternoon free. And in summer she has one week's holiday. Then she goes home to her family in the village. She earns about fourteen pounds a year, plus her food and her bed.

[long-pause]

{img:housemaids-afternoon-off-kensington-gardens-best-hats-walking}
On her afternoon off, she walks in the park with the nursemaid, in her best hat. She looks in the shops. She goes to church. She writes letters home to her mother. And she sends home a little of her money.

[pause]

At night, at last, she climbs the stairs to her little room under the roof. She takes off her cap and her apron. She lies down in her narrow bed. She listens to the rain on the roof and the church bell striking eleven. And she falls asleep at once.

[long-pause]

{img:victorian-basement-kitchen-range-copper-pans-cook-table-window}
Let us go down the stairs to the kitchen.

[pause]

It is a big room under the street, with a stone floor and white walls. A window sits high up near the ceiling. It looks out at the pavement. So from the kitchen you can see the feet of people walking past outside.

[long-pause]

In the middle stands a long wooden table. It has been scrubbed so often that it has turned white. Here the cook prepares the food. Along one wall stands a huge black iron stove. It has a fire in the middle and ovens on each side. There is a tank for hot water. On top, pans bubble all day long. The stove burns coal from morning to night. It keeps the kitchen warm. In summer it makes it very hot.

[pause]

On the walls hang rows of copper pans and moulds for jellies. They are polished till they shine. There are shelves of plates and dishes. And there is the row of bells for the servants.

[pause]

{img:victorian-cook-making-pastry-flour-rolling-pin-kitchen-table}
The cook is a big kind woman of about fifty. She comes from Devon, in the south west. She has worked in kitchens since she was a girl. She is the most important of the servants. She rules the kitchen. And she knows how to make everything.

[long-pause]

Her day starts early and ends late. In the morning she makes breakfast and bakes bread and makes soup. The delivery men come to the little door under the street. The milkman comes with his cans. The baker brings bread. The butcher's boy brings meat. The cook checks everything and argues about the prices.

[pause]

In the afternoon she makes cakes and scones and sandwiches for tea. Then she starts on dinner. Soup and fish and a roast and vegetables. And a pudding steamed in a bowl wrapped in cloth. Or a jelly from a copper mould. It wobbles and shines on its plate. Will it come out of the mould in one piece? The cook holds her breath every time. And every time, it does.

[pause]

{img:victorian-larder-shelves-jars-preserves-cheese-hams-cool-room}
Next to the kitchen there is a room with a big stone sink. The dishes and pans are washed there. And there is a cool store room with stone shelves. The window is covered with fine wire, against the flies. Meat and milk and butter and cheese are kept there. There are no fridges yet. Some houses have an ice box, filled with blocks of ice brought by the ice man.

[long-pause]

The cook has a very famous book. It is called Mrs Beeton's Book of Household Management. It has more than a thousand pages. It tells you everything. How to make soup. How to run a house. What to do when the children are ill.

[pause]

And in the evening, when dinner is served and cleared and the dishes are washed, the cook sits down at last. She sits in her armchair by the kitchen fire with a cup of tea. She puts her feet up and reads the newspaper. The kettle sings. And the kitchen cat sleeps on the warm rug in front of the stove.

[long-pause]

{img:victorian-postman-red-pillar-box-uniform-delivering-letters-street}
In 1888, the post is wonderful.

[pause]

In the centre of London, postmen bring letters up to twelve times a day. A letter posted in the morning can arrive across town before lunch. And the answer can come back the same afternoon. People write to each other all day long, as we send messages today.

[long-pause]

It costs only a penny to send a letter anywhere in the country. You stick on a little stamp with the Queen's head on it. The first stamp in the world was made in England, about fifty years ago. And the red post boxes on the street corners have been there for more than thirty years.

[pause]

The postmen wear dark blue uniforms and caps. They carry big bags of letters. At each door they knock twice, sharp and quick. That way everyone knows it is the post.

[pause]

{img:telegraph-boy-uniform-bicycle-telegram-message-door}
For urgent news there is the telegraph. A message can be sent along wires in a few minutes, anywhere in the country. It can even go under the sea to America or India. A boy in uniform brings it to your door. When a telegraph boy knocks, people often feel a little nervous. A telegram usually means big news. Good or bad.

[long-pause]

And there is something new. The telephone. It was invented only twelve years ago. There are already a few thousand telephones in London, in offices and in rich homes. But most people have never used one.

[pause]

Every afternoon at about four, in the houses of London, there is tea.

[pause]

{img:victorian-afternoon-tea-drawing-room-ladies-china-cups-cakes}
The story goes that a duchess started it, about fifty years ago. She felt hungry in the long afternoons between lunch and dinner. So she asked for tea and a little bread and butter and cake. Soon she was inviting her friends to join her. And the custom spread.

[long-pause]

Now everyone has tea. In grand houses it is served in the drawing room. There is a silver teapot and fine china cups. There are thin slices of bread and butter, little sandwiches, scones with jam, and cake. The lady of the house welcomes her visitors at this hour. They leave their name cards in a little silver dish in the hall.

[pause]

In poorer homes, tea is a real meal. When the workers come home in the evening, there is bread and butter and jam. Perhaps a smoked fish or an egg. And lots of strong sweet tea with milk.

[pause]

{img:abc-tea-shop-ladies-waitresses-marble-tables-1880s}
And there are tea shops. About twenty-five years ago, a company opened a tea room at a railway station. Anyone could sit down there and have a cup of tea and a bun, cheaply.

[long-pause]

This was very important for the women of London. Before, there were very few places a woman could go alone to sit and rest and eat in the city. Only the homes of friends. Now there are tea shops all over London. They are full of shop girls and typists and ladies out shopping. They chat over their tea and buns.

[pause]

And so London drinks tea all day long, from morning till night. Tea from India and China, unloaded at the docks. Brewed in brown teapots and silver ones, in every house in London.

[long-pause]

{img:covent-garden-market-dawn-carts-vegetables-flowers-porters-baskets}
Before dawn, while most of London is still asleep, the markets are already awake.

[pause]

At Covent Garden, in the middle of the West End, the great market hall is full of light and noise and colour. All night long, carts and wagons have been coming in from the farms around London. They are piled high with vegetables and fruit and flowers.

[long-pause]

There are cabbages and carrots and onions and potatoes. There are big heaps of apples and pears and plums. In summer there are strawberries and cherries. From warmer countries come oranges and lemons and bananas and pineapples.

[pause]

The porters carry baskets on their heads. They pile up ten or twelve, one on top of another. And they walk fast through the crowds without dropping a single one.

[pause]

And there are flowers. Roses and violets and lilies. Daffodils in spring. Ferns and palms in pots. The flower market is full of their scent and colour.

[long-pause]

Flower girls in shawls come early to buy their flowers. Then they go out into the streets with their baskets. They sell little bunches of violets to gentlemen on their way to work, for a penny.

[pause]

By the river near London Bridge is the fish market, called Billingsgate. From five in the morning, fish comes in from the boats and the trains. Cod and herring and salmon and eels. Oysters and shrimps and crabs and lobsters. They lie in big piles in baskets and boxes, wet and silver.

[pause]

{img:billingsgate-fish-market-porters-leather-hats-fish-baskets-river}
The fish porters wear special flat leather hats with a wide brim. They carry the heavy boxes of fish on their heads. That way the water runs off and does not go down their necks.

[long-pause]

Billingsgate is famous for its noise. And for the language of its porters. People say it is the roughest in London.

[pause]

There is a great meat market in a new hall of iron and glass. And in the City there is a beautiful new market with a glass roof. Its iron arches are painted red and cream and green and gold. It sells chickens and game and cheese.

[pause]

{img:costermonger-barrow-fruit-street-market-gaslight-saturday-night}
All over London, in the poorer streets, there are street markets. Sellers stand by their barrows of fruit and vegetables and fish. On Saturday nights they work by the light of flickering lamps. Each one shouts out what he sells, in his own special cry.

[long-pause]

There are street sellers of everything. Hot potatoes and roasted chestnuts and pies. Pea soup and ginger beer. Coffee from little stalls on the corners, for the workers early in the morning. Matches and bootlaces and newspapers. Songs printed on sheets of paper. Toys, and birds in cages.

[pause]

And so every morning, the markets of London feed five and a half million people. With food from all over the country and all over the world.

[long-pause]

{img:victorian-department-store-interior-counters-ladies-shopping-gaslight}
In 1888, shopping in London is turning into something new.

[pause]

For hundreds of years, people bought things in small shops. Each shop sold just one kind of thing. Cloth here, buttons there, hats somewhere else. But now there are big new shops where you can buy almost everything under one roof. They are called department stores.

[long-pause]

One of them calls itself the Universal Provider. It says it can supply anything, from a pin to an elephant. In Knightsbridge there is Harrods. It started as a small grocer's shop. Now it grows bigger every year. In Oxford Street and Regent Street there are big shops with tall glass windows. Inside are rooms full of silk and lace and gloves and hats and coats.

[pause]

And in Regent Street there is Liberty's. It is full of beautiful things from the East. Silks from Japan and India and China. Fans and fine china and soft flowing cloth in lovely colours. Artists and fashionable ladies love it.

[pause]

{img:liberty-shop-silks-japanese-fans-oriental-goods-1880s-display}
The department stores are a new world for the ladies of London. They can come and walk around and look without having to buy. That was not the custom before. There are tea rooms inside, and rooms to rest and read. Ladies can sit down and meet their friends.

[long-pause]

The shop assistants are young men and young women in neat black clothes. They stand behind long counters. They measure out cloth and ribbons and show gloves and hats. Many of them live in rooms above the shop. They work from eight in the morning until nine or ten at night.

[pause]

When you pay, the money goes to the cashier in a little wooden ball or cup. It whizzes along a wire above the customers' heads. And a moment later it comes whizzing back with your change.

[pause]

{img:victorian-shop-window-display-hats-gloves-ribbons-gaslit-evening}
In the evening, the shop windows are lit by gas. They are full of wonderful displays. People walk along the streets just to look at them.

[long-pause]

And there are still thousands of small shops everywhere. The corner shops and grocers smell of tea and coffee and spices and cheese. There are bakers and butchers. There are chemists with big glass bottles of coloured water in their windows, glowing red and green and blue. And there are sweet shops with jars of toffee and peppermints. The children press their noses against the glass.

[pause]

And the shopkeepers stand in their doorways in their aprons. They greet their customers by name.

[long-pause]

{img:east-end-street-1888-crowded-houses-washing-lines-children-playing}
Let us go east, to the East End of London.

[pause]

Here, east of the City and north of the docks, live hundreds of thousands of people. They are the working people of London. Many of them are very poor.

[long-pause]

The streets are narrow and crowded. There are old houses and new blocks of flats. There are small workshops and factories, pubs and chapels and shops. Often a whole family lives in one room. They share one tap and one toilet in the yard with all the other families in the house.

[pause]

The people work at a thousand trades. They are dock workers and porters and carters. They are seamstresses and tailors and shoemakers. They make furniture and matches and brushes. They work long hours, in small workshops or at home, for very little money.

[pause]

{img:jewish-immigrant-family-arriving-london-docks-bundles-1880s}
The East End has always been a place where newcomers arrive. Two hundred years ago, people came from France and became silk weavers here. Then came people from Ireland. And now, in the 1880s, thousands of Jewish families are arriving from Russia and Poland. They are escaping danger and poverty. They come with their bundles and their children. They settle here and open shops and bakeries and workshops and synagogues. And in the streets you hear their language, Yiddish.

[long-pause]

Life in the East End is hard. But it is also full of life. There are markets and music and street games. There are families and neighbours who help each other.

[pause]

And in 1888, people elsewhere are starting to notice. Some are starting to help.

[pause]

{img:matchgirls-strike-1888-young-women-shawls-street-gathering-peaceful}
In the summer of 1888, the young women and girls at a match factory in the East End stop work.

[long-pause]

They work long hours for very low pay. They are fined for small things, like talking or dropping matches. And some get ill from the chemicals used to make the matches. A writer called Annie Besant wrote about this in her newspaper. The factory asked the girls to sign a paper saying it was not true. They would not sign. They knew they might lose their jobs. But they stood together. In July, fourteen hundred of them walked out at once.

[pause]

Within two weeks they won. The newspapers and the public were on their side. The fines stopped. Things got better. And they started a union, a group that would stand up for them. It was one of the first great wins for ordinary working women. And it gave courage to others, like the dock workers the next year.

[pause]

{img:charles-booth-researcher-notebook-walking-london-street-policeman}
That same year, a rich businessman called Charles Booth is walking through London's streets. His helpers walk with him. Street by street and house by house, they write down how people live and what they earn. He wants to know, for certain, how many Londoners are poor.

[long-pause]

He will make a great map of London. Every street gets a colour, depending on how rich or poor its people are. Yellow for the rich. Red and pink for those doing well. Blue for the poor. And black for the very poorest. He will find that about one in three Londoners lives in poverty. It shocks the country. And it helps to change how people think about the poor.

[pause]

In the East End there are places that try to help. In one big house, young men from the universities come to live among the poor. They teach classes and help where they can. There are missions and churches. And there is the Salvation Army, started right here in the East End. It gives soup and shelter and help.

[long-pause]

In the autumn of 1888, the East End is also in the newspapers every day. There is a dark mystery in the streets of Whitechapel that frightens the whole city. It will never be solved. We will leave it there, far away in the shadows of the past. And we will remember instead the ordinary people of the East End. Every day they went on working and raising their children and helping each other.

[long-pause]

{img:victorian-board-school-classroom-rows-desks-slates-teacher-blackboard}
In 1888, every child in London between five and ten must go to school.

[pause]

This is quite new. Not long ago, there were no schools for everyone. There were only church schools and charity schools. Many poor children never went to school at all. Then a new law said there must be a school near every child. And a few years later, another law said every child must go.

[long-pause]

So London has built hundreds of new schools all over the city. They are big tall buildings of yellow and red brick, three floors high. They have tall windows and a little bell tower. They stand out above the rows of small houses around them. Many of them are still there today. And many are still schools.

[pause]

They have separate doors for boys and for girls and for the little ones. The words are carved in stone above each door. Some even have playgrounds on the roof.

[pause]

In the classrooms there are rows of wooden desks. There is a blackboard and a big map of the world. The British Empire is coloured pink on it. There is a stove for winter. And the teacher's desk stands on a little platform at the front.

[long-pause]

The children learn reading and writing and sums. They learn some history and geography and singing. They do exercises in the playground. The girls learn sewing and cooking. They write on small slates with slate pencils that squeak. They rub out their work with a cloth, or with their sleeves. The older children write in copybooks with ink and steel pens.

[pause]

The classes are big. Sixty or seventy children, sometimes more. The teachers are strict. But many of them are kind and work hard. For many poor children, school is the warmest and brightest and safest place they know.

[pause]

{img:victorian-street-children-playing-marbles-hopscotch-pavement}
After school and on Saturdays, the children of London play in the streets.

[long-pause]

They play marbles and hopscotch, chalked on the pavement. They skip with a long rope and sing. They roll hoops along with a stick. They play leapfrog and tag and football with a ball of rags. When the organ grinder comes down the street with his monkey, they dance to the music. And they watch the Punch and Judy puppet show on the corner.

[pause]

Many poor children also work. They sell newspapers and matches and flowers. They run errands and mind babies. They help their parents in their workshops at home.

[long-pause]

For rich children it is different. They have teachers at home. Then the boys go away to boarding schools. More and more girls go to the new girls' schools. A very few even go to the new colleges for women at the universities.

[pause]

{img:victorian-toy-shop-window-dolls-tin-soldiers-children-looking}
And on special days, the children of London gaze into the toy shop windows. They see dolls with china faces and tin soldiers. They see rocking horses and toy theatres and wonderful wind-up toys. And they dream.

[long-pause]

{img:hyde-park-rotten-row-riders-carriages-sunday-afternoon-trees}
London is a city of parks.

[pause]

In the middle of the city are the great royal parks. Long ago they belonged to the kings and queens, for hunting and riding. Now they are open to everyone. There is Hyde Park and Kensington Gardens. There is Green Park and Saint James's Park and Regent's Park. They are wide and green and peaceful, with great trees and lawns and lakes.

[long-pause]

On a fine Sunday afternoon, all of London comes to the parks.

[pause]

In Hyde Park there is a wide sandy track. Fashionable ladies and gentlemen ride their horses slowly up and down it. They want to see and be seen. Beside it, the carriages of the rich drive slowly past. Ladies in their finest dresses and hats nod to their friends. And on the grass and the paths, the crowds walk and watch.

[pause]

In the middle of the park is a long lake. People row little boats on it. Children sail toy boats and feed the ducks and swans. In winter, when it freezes, thousands of people skate on the ice.

[long-pause]

In Kensington Gardens, nursemaids push prams along the long paths under the trees. Children play by the Round Pond, sailing boats and flying kites. And there is a great monument covered with gold and coloured stone. In it sits a statue of Prince Albert, the Queen's husband. He passed away more than twenty-five years ago. The Queen still misses him every day.

[pause]

In Regent's Park there is the Zoo. Londoners come to see the lions and tigers and bears. They see monkeys and giraffes and the hippo. And the children can ride on the elephant.

[pause]

{img:london-zoo-1888-children-elephant-ride-howdah-crowd}
In Saint James's Park, there is a lake with pelicans. Pelicans have lived there for two hundred years.

[long-pause]

There are newer parks too, all over London, for the people of the crowded streets. One is in the East End and one is south of the river. They have bandstands where bands play on summer afternoons. People sit in deckchairs and listen.

[pause]

On Sunday mornings, most of London goes to church or chapel. The shops are closed and it is quiet. In the afternoon, people walk in the parks or along the river, in their Sunday best. Or they take the train to the country or the seaside for the day.

[pause]

{img:hampstead-heath-view-over-london-families-walking-kites-autumn}
On the hills north of London lies Hampstead Heath. It is a wide wild open space of grass and woods and ponds. On holidays, thousands of Londoners come here. They walk and play games and fly kites and ride donkeys. And they look out over the whole city below them, under its haze of smoke. In the middle rises the great dome of Saint Paul's.

[long-pause]

In the summer evenings, as the sun sets, the parks grow quiet. The park keepers ring their bells and close the gates. The swans settle on the lakes. And the lamps come on along the paths, under the trees.

[long-pause]

{img:thames-passenger-steamboat-pier-westminster-families-summer-day}
On a summer day, many Londoners go up the river.

[pause]

Little steamboats leave from the piers in the middle of town every few minutes. They are crowded with families and couples and children. Everyone wears summer clothes and straw hats. The boats go up the river, away from the city, toward the green countryside.

[long-pause]

As the boat goes upriver, the city slowly falls behind. The great buildings and factories give way to houses with gardens. The gardens run down to the water. There are boathouses and willow trees and meadows. The river grows narrower and quieter and greener.

[pause]

At Kew there are the royal gardens. There are wide lawns and great trees from all over the world. There are flower beds and lakes. And there are huge glass houses of iron and glass. Inside the biggest one it is warm and steamy and full of tall palm trees. And a Chinese tower rises above the trees.

[pause]

{img:kew-gardens-palm-house-glasshouse-victorian-visitors-lawn-summer}
At Richmond there is a hill with a famous view over the bend of the river. Painters have painted it for a hundred years. There are boats to hire. Young men in striped jackets and flat straw hats row on the river. Young ladies in white sit with parasols. They trail their fingers in the cool water.

[long-pause]

This is the great age of boating on the Thames. Every summer weekend the river is crowded. There are rowing boats and flat boats pushed with long poles. There are canoes and little steam launches. Houseboats are tied up along the banks, with flower boxes on their roofs.

[pause]

Next year, a writer called Jerome K. Jerome will publish a funny book. It is about three young men and a dog on a boat trip up the Thames. It is called Three Men in a Boat. Things keep going wrong for them, one after another. It will become one of the best-loved funny books in English. People still read it today.

[pause]

{img:rowing-boats-punts-river-thames-willows-summer-afternoon-picnic}
Further up is Hampton Court, the great old palace of King Henry the Eighth. It has red brick and tall chimneys and gardens. And it has a famous maze of hedges. Visitors get lost in it. They laugh and try to find the way out.

[long-pause]

And in the evening, as the sun sets, the steamboats carry the tired happy families back down the river. They glide through the golden light, past the willows and the meadows. The children fall asleep on their mothers' laps. And ahead of them, in the dusk, the lights of London begin to shine.

[long-pause]

{img:natural-history-museum-1888-terracotta-facade-towers-visitors}
In South Kensington, in the west of London, there is a whole area of great museums.

[pause]

One museum is full of art and beautiful things. Furniture and silver and glass. Pots and tapestries and statues from all over the world. Later it will be called the Victoria and Albert Museum.

[long-pause]

And there is the new Natural History Museum. It opened only seven years ago. It is a wonderful building, like a great church of nature. It is covered with tiles of pale blue and honey colour. Carved animals and plants climb up the pillars and peer down from the walls.

[pause]

Inside is a great hall with a high roof. The glass cases are full of stuffed animals and birds and fish. There are fossils and skeletons and shining stones. And there are dinosaurs.

[pause]

The idea of dinosaurs is still quite new. The word dinosaur was made up only about forty-five years ago. The scientist who made it up also started this museum. In a park in south London, there are big models of dinosaurs beside a lake. They were made more than thirty years ago. Children love to visit them. They are not quite right, as we know now. But they are still there today.

[long-pause]

In the middle of London is the British Museum, with its great columns. It holds treasures from the ancient world. The famous Rosetta Stone, which helped people read old Egyptian writing. Egyptian mummies. Statues from Greece. Great stone bulls with wings, from the old lands of Iraq.

[pause]

And in the middle of the museum is the Reading Room. It is a great round room under a huge dome, painted blue and gold. Long desks are arranged like the spokes of a wheel. Books line the walls all around. Scholars and writers from all over the world come here to read and study and write. Many great books of this age were written under this dome.

[pause]

{img:british-museum-reading-room-dome-readers-desks-lamps-quiet}
It is very quiet in the Reading Room. There is only the sound of pages turning and pens scratching, and now and then a soft cough.

[long-pause]

The museums are free and open to everyone. On Sundays and holidays, crowds of working families come. They look at the treasures and the animals and the machines. The Victorians believe that museums and libraries and parks will make people's lives better and happier and wiser.

[pause]

New free libraries are opening all over London too. Anyone can borrow books there. Anyone can read the newspapers in warm bright reading rooms.

[pause]

{img:science-museum-steam-engines-machines-victorian-families-looking}
There are wonderful machines to see as well. Steam engines and trains and model ships. Clocks and telescopes. Among them is the Rocket, one of the very first steam trains, built nearly sixty years ago.

[long-pause]

And so on a rainy afternoon, Londoners can spend hours wandering through the warm halls. They walk among the dinosaurs and the mummies and the steam engines. It costs nothing. Then they step out into the gaslight and the rain, with their heads full of wonders.

[long-pause]

{img:victorian-music-hall-interior-stage-gaslight-audience-tables-chairman}
In the evenings, Londoners go out to the music hall.

[pause]

The music hall is the great fun of Victorian London. There are hundreds of them all over the city. Some are grand palaces in the West End. Some are small halls behind a pub in the East End.

[long-pause]

Inside there is a stage with lights along the front and painted scenery. There are red velvet seats and golden balconies. The ceiling is painted with clouds. A great sparkling gas chandelier hangs in the middle. In the older halls, people sit at tables and eat and drink while they watch. A chairman sits at a table beside the stage with a little hammer. He announces each act with long grand words. And he keeps order.

[pause]

The show goes on all evening, one act after another. Singers and comedians and dancers. Jugglers and acrobats and magicians. Dogs that do tricks and strong men. Each one does a few minutes, and then the next one comes on.

[pause]

{img:music-hall-singer-on-stage-audience-singing-along-chorus}
The great stars are the singers and comedians. They sing funny songs and sad songs about everyday life. Songs about landladies and lodgers. About trips to the seaside and falling in love. About the lives of ordinary Londoners.

[long-pause]

And the audience sing along with the chorus, hundreds of voices together. Everyone knows the songs. They sing them in the streets and at home around the piano. And the next day the barrel organs play them in the streets.

[pause]

One of the brightest young stars this year is Marie Lloyd. She is a girl from the East End, only eighteen. She will become the queen of the music hall, loved by everyone in London.

[pause]

{img:savoy-theatre-electric-light-elegant-audience-gilbert-sullivan-stage}
And in the West End there are the theatres.

[long-pause]

On the Strand stands the Savoy Theatre. When it opened seven years ago, it was the first public building in the world lit only by electric light. Many people were afraid. Would the new lights set the theatre on fire? So on the first night, the owner walked onto the stage holding a glowing light bulb. In front of everyone, he broke it. Nothing caught fire. The audience cheered.

[pause]

The most popular writers in London are Gilbert and Sullivan. Gilbert writes the words, clever and funny. Sullivan writes the music, beautiful and catchy. Their funny operas make everybody laugh. People sing and whistle their songs everywhere.

[long-pause]

And in October 1888, their new opera opens at the Savoy. It is set in the Tower of London, long ago. There are guards and a jester. And there is a young man in prison who must escape in disguise before it is too late. It is a little more serious than their other operas. And it has some of Sullivan's most beautiful music. The audience on the first night love it.

[pause]

{img:west-end-theatre-street-night-carriages-crowds-lights-strand}
There are many other theatres too. At one of them, the great actor Henry Irving plays Shakespeare, with the actress Ellen Terry. The audience hardly dare to breathe. The theatre's manager is a quiet Irishman called Bram Stoker. Some years later he will write a famous spooky book called Dracula.

[long-pause]

And at Christmas there are the pantomimes. They have wonderful scenery and fairies and giants. Every child in London wants to go.

[pause]

When the shows are over, late in the evening, the streets of the West End fill with people and carriages and cabs. The lights of the theatres shine on the wet streets. And the crowds go home through the gaslight, humming the songs.

[long-pause]

{img:cabmens-shelter-green-wooden-hut-cabmen-eating-hansom-cabs-night}
Late at night, when the theatres have closed and most of London is in bed, some people are still working in the streets.

[pause]

The cab drivers wait with their cabs in the cold for the last passengers. Policemen walk slowly through the dark streets with their lanterns. They try the shop doors to check they are locked. Night watchmen sit in little huts beside holes in the road, warming their hands by small fires.

[long-pause]

For the cab drivers there are little green wooden huts. They stand in the middle of the street, at the places where cabs wait. A charity built them. Inside, the drivers can get out of the cold and the rain. They can have a hot meal and a cup of tea. And they do not have to leave their horses. The huts may not be bigger than a horse and cart, because they stand in the road.

[pause]

Inside there is a tiny kitchen and a bench and a table for about ten men. A cook makes them bacon and eggs and tea. Some of these little green huts are still in London's streets today. They still serve tea and bacon sandwiches to the taxi drivers.

[pause]

{img:coffee-stall-street-corner-night-lamp-workers-steaming-mugs}
On the street corners there are coffee stalls. They are little carts with a lamp and a big pot of hot coffee and tea. They sell bread and butter and cake and boiled eggs. They stay open all night. Cab drivers come, and policemen, and market porters on their way to work before dawn. And people who have nowhere to sleep come too, for a little warmth.

[long-pause]

And there are the pubs.

[pause]

There are thousands of pubs in London, on almost every corner. Their names are painted in gold letters over the doors. Their windows are of coloured and patterned glass. Gas lamps hang outside.

[pause]

{img:victorian-pub-interior-mahogany-bar-etched-glass-mirrors-gaslight}
Inside they are warm and bright. There are polished wooden bars and brass rails and big mirrors. Wooden screens divide the room into little separate bars. One bar is for workers. One is a bit more comfortable and a bit dearer. And one is a tiny room for a few quiet people.

[long-pause]

For many working people, the pub is like their living room. It is warm and bright and friendly. They meet their friends there. They talk and sing and play darts or dominoes. They read the newspaper and get warm after work.

[pause]

At the end of the night, the landlord calls time. People go home through the quiet streets. The pub lamps are turned down and the doors are closed. And in their little green hut, the cab drivers drink one last cup of tea. They wait for one more passenger before they too go home.

[long-pause]

{img:baker-street-victorian-fog-gaslight-hansom-cab-door-evening}
A few months before our story, a new story appeared in a Christmas magazine. It was called A Study in Scarlet.

[pause]

It was written by a young doctor called Arthur Conan Doyle. He had a small practice by the sea in the south of England. In the evenings, when there were no patients, he wrote stories. And in this one he made up a new character. A detective called Sherlock Holmes.

[long-pause]

In the story, a young army doctor called John Watson comes back to London. He has been hurt in a war far away. He is tired and has little money. He needs a place to live. A friend tells him about a strange young man who wants to share his rooms. The rooms are at 221B Baker Street.

[pause]

The young man is Sherlock Holmes. He is tall and thin, with sharp eyes and a nose like a hawk. He is a detective. When the police are puzzled, they come to him. He can tell at a glance where a person has been and what they do. He looks at the mud on their boots and the marks on their hands. When he first meets Watson, he tells him where he has been. He has never met him before. And Watson is amazed.

[pause]

{img:victorian-sitting-room-221b-fireplace-armchairs-violin-chemistry-table}
Holmes's rooms are cosy and messy. There are two armchairs, one on each side of the fire. There is a table full of bottles and glass tubes for his experiments. There are papers and books everywhere. And there is his violin. He plays it late at night when he is thinking.

[long-pause]

Their landlady, Mrs Hudson, brings them breakfast and tea. Visitors come at all hours of the day and night to ask for help. Worried ladies. Puzzled policemen. Even kings in disguise. Each one brings a new mystery. And each time, Holmes leans forward in his chair and listens.

[pause]

In 1888, Holmes is not yet famous. Only a few readers have noticed him. This year the story comes out as a book. A few years later, his stories will appear every month in a new magazine, with pictures. And then Sherlock Holmes will become the most famous detective in the world.

[pause]

{img:holmes-watson-walking-foggy-london-street-gas-lamps-silhouettes}
The Holmes stories are full of the London of 1888. Cabs rattling through the fog. Gaslit streets and railway stations. Telegrams and the river and the docks. Great houses and poor streets and all the strange people of the great city.

[long-pause]

Holmes knows every street and alley in London. He can find his way even in the thickest fog. He has a gang of street boys who run errands for him and find things out. He pays them a shilling a day.

[pause]

And at the end of every story, after all the adventures and the puzzles, Holmes and Watson sit by the fire in their armchairs. The fog presses against the windows. The cabs go by in the street below. The fire glows. And Holmes picks up his violin and plays softly into the night.

[pause]

{img:sherlock-holmes-museum-baker-street-today-blue-plaque-visitors}
Today, in Baker Street, there is a Sherlock Holmes Museum at a house numbered 221B. It has the sitting room and the armchairs and the violin, just as in the stories. Visitors come from all over the world to see it. And letters still arrive, addressed to Mr Sherlock Holmes, asking for his help.

[long-pause]

{img:london-pea-souper-fog-street-gas-lamps-blurred-figures-yellow}
In 1888, London is famous for its fog.

[pause]

In autumn and winter, on still cold days, the fog comes. It rises from the river and the marshes. It mixes with the smoke of millions of coal fires and factories and trains. It turns thick and yellow and brown. And it settles over the city, sometimes for days.

[long-pause]

Londoners call it a pea souper, because it is as thick and yellow as pea soup. Sometimes you cannot see across the street. Sometimes you cannot even see your own hand in front of you.

[pause]

In the fog, the whole city changes. The gas lamps become small blurry yellow glows that light nothing. The buildings disappear. People appear out of the fog and vanish again, like ghosts. Sounds change too. Footsteps and hooves and voices sound soft and strange. They seem to come from everywhere and nowhere.

[pause]

In the thickest fog, the traffic stops. Cabs and buses crawl along. The drivers climb down and lead their horses by hand. Boys carry flaming torches. For a penny, they will guide you home through the fog. Trains go slowly. The railway men put little bangers on the tracks. When a train rolls over them they go bang. That warns the driver to be careful.

[long-pause]

On the river the boats stop. Their foghorns sound low and sad through the mist. And the church bells ring, muffled, through the fog.

[pause]

The fog is bad for people's health. It makes them cough. But it is also strangely beautiful. Writers and painters love it. The writer Charles Dickens began one of his books with fog. Fog everywhere, he wrote. Fog up the river, fog down the river. And a few years later, a French painter called Monet will come to London. He will paint the Houses of Parliament again and again, glowing in the fog in purple and gold and blue.

[pause]

{img:painterly-houses-of-parliament-fog-sunset-thames-purple-gold}
An American painter who lives by the river paints the Thames at night, in the mist. He paints in soft blues and greys and golds. The lights of the bridges and boats glow through the dark. He calls his paintings nocturnes, like pieces of music for the night.

[long-pause]

Inside the houses, on a foggy day, the gas lamps burn all day long. The fires are piled high with coal. People stay home if they can. They look out of the window at the blank yellow nothing outside. Then they draw the curtains and sit by the fire and read.

[pause]

{img:cosy-victorian-room-fire-window-fog-outside-cat-armchair-book}
After a day, or two, or three, a wind comes. The fog lifts and drifts away. The city appears again, wet and shining and grey, under a pale sky. And people come back out into the streets.

[long-pause]

Much later, after one terrible fog that lasted five days, new laws will stop people burning coal in the city. And slowly the great London fogs will disappear. But in 1888 they are still part of London. As much as the river and the Tower and the sound of Big Ben.

[long-pause]

{img:lamplighter-long-pole-lighting-gas-lamp-dusk-london-street}
Every evening at dusk, the lamplighters come into the streets of London.

[pause]

There are about five hundred of them. Each one has his own round of streets, with perhaps a hundred lamps or more. They carry a long pole with a little flame at the top and a hook. They walk quickly from lamp to lamp. They reach up and open the little glass door at the top. They turn on the gas and light it. And they move on to the next one, without stopping.

[long-pause]

Behind them, one by one, the lamps come on all along the street. Small soft yellow lights in the blue dusk, like a string of beads. Children run behind the lamplighters to watch. And the lamplighters wave to them.

[pause]

In the morning at dawn, they come again. They put the lamps out one by one. They clean the glass. And they go home to sleep.

[pause]

{img:gaslit-london-square-evening-lamps-glowing-rain-wet-pavement}
The gas comes from gasworks beside the river. There, coal is heated in big ovens to make the gas. The gas is stored in huge round iron tanks. They rise and sink as they fill and empty. From there, pipes under the streets carry the gas to every lamp and every house in London.

[long-pause]

Gaslight is soft and warm and golden. It flickers. It does not light up the whole street like electric light does today. It makes little pools of light under each lamp, with shadows in between. On a misty evening, each lamp has a soft halo around it. On a rainy evening, the light shimmers on the wet pavements.

[pause]

Inside the houses there is gaslight too. The lamps hiss gently. You light them with a match and turn them up or down with a little tap. In poorer homes there are oil lamps and candles.

[pause]

{img:victorian-bedroom-candle-washstand-window-gaslamp-street-below}
When people go up to bed, they carry a candle up the dark stairs. The little flame makes shadows dance on the walls. From the bedroom window they can look down into the street. The gas lamp glows softly below, all night long. A policeman walks slowly past. A cab clatters home in the distance. And the church clock strikes the hours.

[long-pause]

Electric light is coming. A few streets and theatres and grand houses already have it. It is bright and white and steady. But for many years yet, the soft golden gaslight will light the streets of London. And the lamplighters will walk their rounds at dusk and dawn.

[pause]

Even today, in some streets of London, old gas lamps still burn every evening, just as they did in 1888.

[long-pause]

{img:queen-victoria-elderly-black-dress-white-cap-windsor-castle-window}
Over all of this, there is the old Queen.

[pause]

Queen Victoria became queen when she was only eighteen. She was a small young girl with blue eyes and fair hair. One early morning she was woken up and told the news. The king, her uncle, had passed away. Now she was the queen.

[long-pause]

In 1888 she is sixty-nine. She has ruled for more than fifty years. She is small and round and always dressed in black, with a little white lace cap. In her photographs she looks stern. But people who know her say she laughs and has a lovely smile.

[pause]

She has worn black for more than twenty-five years. Her beloved husband, Prince Albert, passed away when he was only forty-two. She was heartbroken. For many years she hid away from the world. She stayed at her castles and houses far from London. People called her the Widow of Windsor.

[pause]

{img:golden-jubilee-1887-procession-open-carriage-crowds-flags-london}
But last year she came out again. She had been queen for fifty years. And the whole country wanted to celebrate.

[long-pause]

There was a great procession through the streets of London to Westminster Abbey. The Queen rode in an open carriage pulled by cream-coloured horses. Around her rode princes from all over Europe. Many of them were her own sons and grandsons. Soldiers came from all over the Empire. She would not wear a crown. She wore a bonnet with diamonds and lace instead.

[pause]

Hundreds of thousands of people lined the streets and cheered. There were flags and banners and flowers everywhere. In the evening, bonfires were lit on the hills all over the country, one after another. There were fireworks and parties. Poor people were given dinners. And children were given mugs and medals to remember the day.

[pause]

{img:jubilee-street-party-children-tables-bunting-mugs-1887}
The Queen wrote in her diary that she would never forget that day. The crowds had been so kind and so happy.

[long-pause]

The Queen has nine children and many grandchildren. They have married into the royal families of Europe. So people call her the grandmother of Europe. Her eldest daughter is the Empress of Germany. This year has been sad for her. In June, the daughter's husband passed away, after being emperor for only ninety-nine days. Now the Queen's grandson William is the new emperor of Germany. He is only twenty-nine.

[pause]

The Queen works hard every day. She reads her government papers in red boxes. She writes letters and her diary. She meets her ministers.

[pause]

{img:queen-victoria-indian-secretary-garden-tent-writing-letters}
And she is learning a new language. Last year two young men from India came to work for her. One of them, Abdul Karim, became her teacher. A little every day, he teaches her to speak and write a language of India. And he tells her about India. She rules it as Empress. But she has never seen it.

[long-pause]

To the people of London, the Queen is far away and a little mysterious. They rarely see her. But she is always there, like the river and the Tower. Her face is on every coin and every stamp. Her name is everywhere. Soon a whole age will be named after her. And when she passes away, years later, after nearly sixty-four years as queen, it will feel to many people as if the world itself has changed.

[long-pause]

{img:victorian-cricket-match-oval-gentlemen-whites-crowd-gasholder}
The Victorians love sport.

[pause]

Many of the great sports of the world got their rules in England in these years. Football and rugby. Tennis and boxing. And many more.

[long-pause]

Cricket is the great summer game. In 1888 a team from Australia is visiting England. They play England in three big matches. Australia wins the first one. Everyone in England is worried. Will they lose the series? But England wins the next two.

[pause]

Thousands of people come to watch. They sit in the sunshine in their straw hats. They watch the slow peaceful game all day long. There is the soft knock of bat on ball. And polite clapping drifts across the green grass.

[pause]

And football is becoming the great game of working people.

[long-pause]

The rules of football were written in London twenty-five years ago, at a meeting in a pub. And in 1888, the first football league begins, with twelve clubs. Most of them are from the north and the middle of England, where football is most loved. The first season starts in September.

[pause]

On Saturday afternoons, many workers now have time off. They go to watch the games in their thousands, in cloth caps and scarves. They cheer and shout. And they go home happy, or a little sad.

[pause]

{img:lawn-tennis-victorian-ladies-long-dresses-garden-net-summer}
Lawn tennis is very new and very fashionable. It was invented only about fifteen years ago. The first big tennis championship at Wimbledon was held soon after. Ladies and gentlemen play it in their gardens and at clubs. The ladies play in long dresses and hats.

[long-pause]

And there is the bicycle.

[pause]

For years there have been bicycles in London. But they were hard to ride and dangerous. The penny farthing had a huge front wheel and a tiny back wheel. The rider sat high on top. If he hit a stone, he would fly right over the handlebars. Only brave young men rode them.

[pause]

{img:safety-bicycle-1888-young-woman-riding-park-road-hat}
But three years ago, a new bicycle was invented. It has two wheels of the same size and a chain that drives the back wheel. It is just like the bicycles of today. People call it the safety bicycle. And in 1888, a Scottish animal doctor called Dunlop invents a tyre filled with air. He wants his little son's tricycle to be more comfortable on the bumpy roads.

[long-pause]

Together, these two inventions will start a great bicycle craze. Soon everyone will ride bicycles. Men and women, young and old. For women most of all, the bicycle will bring a new freedom. They can go where they like, alone or with friends. And they can wear lighter, simpler clothes.

[pause]

On a sunny Saturday afternoon in 1888, in a London park, the first young ladies and gentlemen wobble along the paths on their new bicycles. They ring their bells and laugh. And the older people watch and shake their heads.

[long-pause]

{img:london-1890s-electric-tram-motor-car-horse-cab-street-change}
In the years after 1888, London changes fast.

[pause]

Next year, a new council is created to run the whole great city for the first time. It will build new houses and schools and parks and roads. And later it will clear away the worst of the poor streets.

[long-pause]

In 1890, the first electric tube railway opens. Others follow, deep under the city. Tower Bridge is finished and opened. The first motor cars appear on the streets. They are noisy and smelly and slow, and they frighten the horses. Then come the first motor buses. Slowly, over the next thirty years, the horses disappear from the streets.

[pause]

Electric light spreads through the streets and houses. One by one, the gas lamps go out. But even today, about fifteen hundred gas lamps still burn in the streets of London every evening.

[pause]

{img:queen-victoria-diamond-jubilee-1897-procession-st-pauls-crowds}
Nine years later, the Queen celebrates sixty years on the throne, with an even bigger procession. And in January 1901 she passes away, peacefully, with her family around her. She is eighty-one. Her son becomes King Edward.

[long-pause]

The Victorian age ends. But the London of 1888 lives on. It lives on in its buildings and streets and parks. In its museums and railways and bridges and sewers. In its schools and markets. Many of them are still there today.

[pause]

And it lives on in the books of the time. In the stories of Sherlock Holmes. In the novels of Charles Dickens. In The Strange Case of Doctor Jekyll and Mister Hyde, a story about a man with two sides. In Oscar Wilde's fairy tale The Happy Prince, published this very year. In The Time Machine, about a Victorian gentleman who travels far into the future. And in Peter Pan. A few years from now, its writer will walk his big dog in Kensington Gardens. There he will meet the little boys who give him the idea.

[pause]

{img:victorian-london-book-illustration-style-library-reading-lamp-fire}
And it lives on in the memories of the people who lived then.

[long-pause]

The children of 1888 played hopscotch on the pavements. They sat in rows in the new schools. They rode on top of the horse buses. They will grow up and live through huge changes. Two world wars, cars and aeroplanes, radio and television. Some of them will even live to see men walk on the moon. And they will tell their grandchildren what London was like when they were young. About the gas lamps and the horses and the fog. And about the muffin man, ringing his bell in the street on a winter afternoon.

[long-pause]

{img:tower-bridge-today-raised-tall-ship-passing-sunset-london}
Today London is still one of the great cities of the world. Nearly nine million people live there, from every country on earth.

[pause]

Much of the London of 1888 is still there, if you know where to look.

[long-pause]

Tower Bridge still stands beside the Tower of London. It still opens for tall ships. Visitors can walk across the high walkways between the towers. Now they have glass floors. You can look down at the river and the traffic far below.

[pause]

The Houses of Parliament and Big Ben still stand beside the river. The great museums are still there, free and open to everyone. Children still come to see the dinosaurs in the great hall, under the arches with the carved animals peering down.

[pause]

{img:leadenhall-market-today-victorian-glass-roof-painted-iron-lunch-crowd}
The old market in the City, with its glass roof and painted iron arches, still stands. At lunchtime it is full of people eating and drinking, just like in 1888. The old Covent Garden market building is still there, full of shops and cafes and street performers.

[long-pause]

The Underground still runs under the streets. The old station at Baker Street, with its brick arches, is still used every day by thousands of people. And in Baker Street there is a statue of the great detective outside the station, in his famous hat.

[pause]

The old schools of yellow and red brick, with their tall windows and bell towers, still stand all over London. Many are still schools, full of children.

[pause]

{img:regents-park-london-today-autumn-trees-families-walking-path}
The parks are still there, green and peaceful in the middle of the city. The pelicans are still on the lake. Boats still float on the Serpentine. Children still sail toy boats on the Round Pond. And the Zoo is still in Regent's Park.

[long-pause]

And the river still flows through the middle of the city, toward the sea, as it has for thousands of years. The great docks are quiet now. They have become homes and offices and harbours for small boats. Tall towers stand where the ships once unloaded their tea.

[pause]

So we leave London tonight, in the year 1888, as the evening falls.

[pause]

{img:victorian-london-night-thames-embankment-gas-lamps-big-ben-quiet}
The lamplighters walk along the streets with their long poles. One by one the gas lamps come on along the river. Their soft yellow lights shine in the dark quiet water. The last horse buses are going home, tired and slow. In the houses of Kensington, families sit by the fire. The housemaids climb the stairs to their little attic rooms. In Baker Street, a light still burns in an upstairs window. And over the rooftops and chimneys and the river, Big Ben strikes the hour, deep and slow and soft, through the evening mist.
