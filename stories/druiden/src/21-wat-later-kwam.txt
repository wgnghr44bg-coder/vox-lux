After the conquest of Gaul, the last great stronghold of the druids was in Britain. And especially on the island of Mona, today called Anglesey, off the coast of north Wales. It was said to be the most sacred place of the druids, full of groves and altars. In the year 60 after Christ, more than a hundred years after our story, a Roman army crossed the narrow strait to the island. We will not follow what happened there. The sacred groves were cut down. And the great age of the druids came to an end.

--

But the druids did not vanish completely from memory. In Ireland, which the Romans never conquered, the druids lived on for several more centuries. In the old Irish stories, there are many druids. Wise men, advisers to kings, poets and magicians. And when Christianity came to Ireland, in the fifth century, with Saint Patrick, the stories say that the saint met the druids of the king, and won them over. Slowly, the druids gave way to the Christian monks.

Interestingly, the monks of Ireland kept many of the old traditions alive. They wrote down the old stories, which the druids and the bards had remembered for centuries. The tales of the heroes. The stories of the gods, now told as ancient kings and queens. The laws. The lists of kings. Without the monks, these stories would have been lost forever. And so, in a way, the memory of the druids passed into the books of the monks.

{img:irish-monk-writing-old-tales-manuscript-candle-celtic-knotwork-margin}
The Celtic languages also lived on. Today, there are six Celtic languages that are still spoken. Irish, Scottish Gaelic and Manx, in Ireland, Scotland and the Isle of Man. And Welsh, Cornish and Breton, in Wales, Cornwall and Brittany. They are the descendants of the languages that the Celts spoke in the time of our story. Gaulish itself disappeared, slowly, replaced by Latin, which later became French. But many French words, and many place names, still come from Gaulish.

In the seventeen and eighteen hundreds, people in Britain and France became fascinated by the druids again. They read Caesar and Pliny. They imagined the druids as wise old men with long white beards, in white robes, worshipping in stone circles. They wrongly believed that the druids had built Stonehenge. Poets wrote about them. Artists painted them. And people formed societies of modern druids, which still exist today.

--

{img:modern-druids-white-robes-stone-circle-summer-solstice-sunrise-later}
In Wales, there is a great festival of poetry and music every year, called the Eisteddfod. It was revived in the eighteen hundreds. In it, poets and musicians compete, and the winners are honoured in a ceremony of the Gorsedd, a society of bards, who wear robes of white, green and blue, and stand in a circle of stones. It is not an ancient druid ceremony. But it is inspired by the old Celtic love of poetry, music and memory.

The French have also always remembered the Gauls. They call them their ancestors. Vercingetorix is a national hero in France, with great statues in his honour, including one at Alesia. And there is a famous French comic book, about a little Gaulish village that resists the Romans, with a wise old druid who brews a magic potion. Millions of children all over the world have read it, and laughed at it, and learned a little about the Gauls.

{img:vercingetorix-statue-alesia-hilltop-bronze-warrior-sky-later}
Archaeologists have also learned a great deal about the Celts, in the last two hundred years. They have dug at many Celtic sites, all over Europe. They have found the towns, the houses, the workshops, the sacred places, the graves and the treasures of the Celts. And they have shown that the Celts were not wild barbarians, as the Romans said, but a rich and skilled civilisation, with its own art, its own knowledge and its own beauty.

--

So, in many ways, the world of the druids is still with us. In the Celtic languages, still spoken by millions of people. In the old stories of Ireland and Wales. In the mistletoe at Christmas, and the pumpkins at Halloween. In the names of rivers and cities. In the swirling patterns of Celtic art, which people still love. And in the old trees, the springs and the stars, which the druids watched and honoured, and which are still there, just as they were.
