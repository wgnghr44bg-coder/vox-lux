Who were the Celts? The name comes from the Greeks, who called them Keltoi. The Romans called them Galli, or Gauls. They themselves probably did not use one name for all their peoples. They called themselves by the names of their tribes. The Aedui, the Arverni, the Helvetii, the Belgae, and many others. In Britain, there were the Iceni, the Brigantes, the Catuvellauni. In Ireland, there were many more.

--

The Celts had lived in Europe for a very long time. Their culture grew up in the middle of Europe, in the lands that are now Austria, Switzerland and southern Germany, more than seven hundred years before our story. From there, it spread in all directions. Some Celts moved west, into Gaul and Spain. Some moved north, into Britain and Ireland. Some moved south, into Italy. And some moved east, into the Balkans and even into Turkey.

About three hundred years before our story, some Celts attacked Rome itself, and took the city. The Romans never forgot it. Other Celts went to Greece, and reached the famous temple of Delphi. Celtic warriors were feared all over the ancient world. They were tall and strong, with long hair and moustaches, and they went into battle with great courage, sometimes shouting and singing.

{img:la-tene-celtic-art-swirling-patterns-bronze-shield-close-gold-detail}
But the Celts were not only warriors. They were also wonderful farmers, craftsmen and artists. They were some of the best metalworkers in the ancient world. They made beautiful jewellery, weapons, shields and cauldrons, of bronze, iron, silver and gold, decorated with swirling patterns, spirals, curving lines and strange animals. This style of art is called La Tène, after a place in Switzerland where many beautiful objects were found.

The Celts did not use writing very much. They had no books. Their history, their laws, their poems and their religion were all kept in memory, and passed on by word of mouth, from teacher to student, from parent to child. That is why we know so little about them from their own words. Most of what we know comes from what the Greeks and Romans wrote about them. And from what archaeologists have found in the ground.

--

{img:gallic-farmers-fields-oxen-plough-wheat-cattle-countryside-summer}
In the time of our story, Gaul is a rich and busy land. There are many towns, called oppida, on hills, surrounded by strong walls. There are farms and villages, with fields of wheat and barley, and great herds of cattle, pigs and sheep. There are roads, and rivers full of boats, carrying goods. There are markets, where people buy and sell with coins. The Gauls trade with the Greeks and the Romans, to the south, and with the Britons, across the sea.

The tribes of Gaul are often rivals. They quarrel and sometimes fight with each other. The two most powerful tribes are the Aedui, in the middle of Gaul, and the Arverni, to the south-west. They have been rivals for a long time. The Aedui have made friends with the Romans, who rule the south of Gaul, by the Mediterranean. They are called brothers and kinsmen of the Roman people. It is a friendship that will have great consequences.

{img:gallic-river-boats-trade-amphorae-wine-barrels-riverbank-town}
In the south of Gaul, along the Mediterranean coast, the Romans have already ruled for about sixty years. They call it the Province, and today it is still called Provence. From there, Roman merchants come north, selling wine, oil and fine pottery. The Gauls love Roman wine. They pay for it with slaves, metals, grain and cattle. Some people say a Gaul would trade a slave for a single jar of wine.

--

But in the year 60 before Christ, most of Gaul is still free. The Romans are only in the south. The Celtic tribes rule themselves, with their own kings and nobles, their own laws, their own gods, and their own druids. Nobody knows that, in just two years, a Roman general called Julius Caesar will come north, with his legions. And that everything will change. But for now, life in Gaul goes on, as it has for centuries.
