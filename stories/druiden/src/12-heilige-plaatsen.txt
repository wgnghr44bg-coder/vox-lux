For the Celts, the whole world is full of the sacred. Gods and spirits live everywhere. In the forests and the groves. In the springs and the rivers. In the lakes and the marshes. On the tops of hills, and in caves. Every river has its goddess. Every spring has its spirit. And the people respect them, and bring them gifts, and ask for their help.

--

The Celts do not usually build great temples, like the Greeks and the Romans. Instead, they worship in the open air, in sacred places in nature. The most important are the sacred groves. These are clearings in the forests, surrounded by old trees, especially oaks, where nobody may cut wood. The Roman writers described them as dark and mysterious, silent places, where even the birds did not sing. But to the Celts, they were holy and peaceful.

Springs are especially sacred. Water that comes out of the earth, clear and pure, seems like a gift from the underworld. Many springs are believed to have healing powers. Sick people come to them, and bathe in the water, or drink it, and pray to the goddess of the spring to make them well. They leave offerings. Coins, brooches, little figures of clay or wood. And sometimes, carved models of the part of the body that is sick. A leg, an arm, an eye, a head.

{img:carved-wooden-figures-offerings-healing-spring-source-seine-legs-arms}
At the source of the river Seine, in Burgundy, not far from Bibracte, there is a famous healing spring, sacred to the goddess Sequana, the goddess of the river. Archaeologists found hundreds of wooden figures there, carved and offered by pilgrims. Figures of people, of heads, of legs and arms and bodies. They had been preserved for two thousand years, in the wet mud by the spring. They are like a crowd of silent pilgrims, asking for help.

Rivers and lakes are sacred too. The Celts throw precious things into them, as offerings to the gods. Swords, shields, spears, helmets, cauldrons, jewellery and coins. Archaeologists have found thousands of these objects, at the bottom of rivers and lakes all over Europe. In the river Thames, in London. In the lake at La Tène, in Switzerland. In lakes in Ireland and Wales. Some of the most beautiful Celtic objects in the world were found in this way.

At Bibracte, there is the sacred spring, near the druid's house, where the students wash every morning. The water comes out of the hillside, cold and clear, into a stone basin. People come here every day, to pray, and to leave small offerings. A coin. A flower. A ribbon tied to the branches of the tree above the spring. Senna comes often, and leaves a little piece of bread for the goddess, and asks her to look after the horses.

Some places are sacred because of the ancestors. On hills and in fields, there are old burial mounds, built by people who lived long before the Celts. The Celts believe that these are entrances to the Otherworld, the land of the gods and the dead. A beautiful land, beneath the hills or across the sea, where there is no sickness and no old age, where the feasting never ends, and where the apple trees always bear fruit.

{img:ancient-burial-mound-misty-evening-grass-standing-stone-otherworld}
The great stone monuments, like Stonehenge in Britain, were already thousands of years old, in the time of the druids. The druids did not build them. They were built by much earlier peoples, long forgotten. But the druids may have known them, and wondered about them, just as we do. And perhaps they used some of them for their own ceremonies. Later people believed that the druids built Stonehenge. But that is just a legend.

--

Lugos learns the names and the stories of all the sacred places in the land of the Aedui. Every spring, every grove, every hill and every river. And the old druid takes him to visit many of them. They walk through the forests together, for days, sleeping in villages or under the trees. And at each sacred place, the old druid stops, and prays, and tells the story of the place, and Lugos repeats it, until he knows it by heart.
