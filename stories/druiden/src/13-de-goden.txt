The Celts have many gods. Hundreds of them. Some are worshipped all over the Celtic world. Others belong to one tribe, or one place, one river or one spring. We know their names mostly from inscriptions, carved on stones in Roman times, and from the old stories of Ireland and Wales, written down much later. But the druids knew them all, and all their stories.

--

One of the greatest gods is called Lugus. He is the god of light, of skill and of all the crafts. He is the master of every art. A smith, a poet, a harper, a warrior, a healer and a magician, all at once. Julius Caesar said that the Gauls honoured him most of all, and that they thought he had invented all the arts. Many places are named after him. The city of Lyon, in France, was once called Lugdunum, the fort of Lugus. Lugos, our young student, is named after him.

Another important god is Taranis, the god of thunder. His symbol is a wheel, perhaps the wheel of the sun, or the wheel of his chariot, rolling across the sky and making the thunder. There is Belenos, the bright one, a god of the sun and of healing. And there is Cernunnos, a strange and ancient god, with the antlers of a stag on his head, who sits cross-legged, surrounded by animals. He is the god of nature, of animals and of plenty.

{img:cernunnos-antlered-god-sitting-cross-legged-animals-around-forest-carving}
There are goddesses too, and they are very important. Epona is the goddess of horses. She is shown riding a horse, or sitting between two horses, with a basket of fruit or grain. She is loved by everyone who works with horses, and the Roman cavalry later adopted her too. There are mother goddesses, often shown as three women sitting together, with babies, fruit and bread. They bring fertility, health and plenty to the land and the people.

And there are the goddesses of the rivers. Sequana, the goddess of the Seine. Matrona, the goddess of the Marne. Sabrina, the goddess of the Severn, in Britain. Every river has its goddess, who gives life to the land through which it flows. The Celts throw offerings into the rivers to honour them. And many rivers in Europe still have Celtic names today. The Thames. The Rhine. The Danube.

--

{img:epona-horse-goddess-carving-sitting-between-horses-basket-fruit}
One of the most famous Celtic objects in the world shows many of these gods. It is a great silver cauldron, found in a bog in Denmark, at a place called Gundestrup. It was made around the time of our story. It is decorated, inside and out, with pictures of gods and goddesses, animals, warriors and ceremonies. There is Cernunnos, with his antlers, holding a torc and a snake. There are goddesses, wheels, elephants, griffins, bulls and dogs. It is like a picture book of Celtic beliefs.

The Celts also believe in the Otherworld, the land of the gods and the dead. It is not a dark place, like the underworld of the Greeks. It is a beautiful land, of feasting and music, of eternal youth and endless summer. Sometimes, the stories say, living people can go there, by accident, through a mist, or a hole in a hill, or across the sea. And when they come back, they find that hundreds of years have passed, although they thought they were away for only a day.

{img:otherworld-island-mist-apple-trees-feast-hall-music-golden-light-celtic}
In the evenings, by the fire, the grandmother tells Senna stories about the gods and the Otherworld. About the god of light, who came to the hall of the gods, and was not allowed in until he showed that he had every skill. About the horse goddess, whose white mare could never be caught, however fast you rode. About the young hero who followed a white deer into the forest, and found himself in the Otherworld, at a feast that lasted for a hundred years.

--

Senna loves the story of Epona and her white mare best of all. She asks for it every night. And at night, before she goes to sleep, she holds her little clay figure of Epona, and whispers to it. She asks Epona to give her a horse of her own, one day. A white mare, as fast as the wind. And she falls asleep, and dreams of galloping across green fields, with her hair flying, and nobody able to catch her.
