There is one ceremony of the druids that has become very famous. It was described by a Roman writer called Pliny the Elder, more than a hundred years after our story. It is the cutting of the mistletoe. And it shows how deeply the druids loved and respected nature, especially the oak tree.

--

The oak was the most sacred tree of the druids. Some people think that the word druid itself comes from an old Celtic word for oak, combined with a word meaning to know. So a druid would be someone with the knowledge of the oak. The druids held their ceremonies in groves of oak trees, in the forests. They used oak leaves in their rituals. And they believed that the oak was the tree of the greatest of the gods.

Mistletoe is a strange plant. It does not grow in the ground. It grows on the branches of trees, high up, as a round, bushy ball of green leaves, with little white berries. It stays green all winter, when the tree is bare. It usually grows on apple trees, poplars and limes. But very rarely, it grows on an oak. And when the druids found mistletoe growing on an oak, Pliny wrote, they believed it was a gift from the gods, and treated it as something very holy.

{img:white-robed-druid-golden-sickle-cutting-mistletoe-oak-branch-white-cloth-below}
Pliny described the ceremony. It happened on the sixth day of the moon. The druids prepared a feast under the tree. Then a druid, dressed in a white robe, climbed up into the oak. With a sickle made of gold, he cut the mistletoe. And below the tree, other druids caught it in a white cloth, so that it did not touch the ground. Then the druids prayed that the god would make his gift bring good fortune to the people.

Pliny wrote that the druids called the mistletoe by a name that meant all-healing. They believed that a drink made from it could help animals have young, and could cure poisons. Some of this may be true, and some may not. Mistletoe is actually slightly poisonous. But the druids knew a great deal about plants and their uses. And mistletoe was clearly very special to them.

One winter morning, Diviciacus takes his students to see a mistletoe ceremony, in a sacred grove of oaks, deep in the forest below the hill. It is a cold, clear day. The ground is white with frost. The bare branches of the oaks are dark against the pale blue sky. And high in one ancient oak, there is a round green ball of mistletoe, with white berries shining in the low winter sun.

The druids of the region have gathered, in their white robes. The people of the nearby villages stand around the edge of the grove, quietly. The oldest druid climbs slowly up a ladder, into the tree. He reaches up with the golden sickle, and cuts the mistletoe. It falls, gently, into the white cloth held by the druids below. The people sigh, as if with relief. And then the druids begin to sing, a slow, old song, that rises up into the frosty air.

{img:gold-sickle-and-mistletoe-sprig-on-white-cloth-oak-leaves-close}
Lugos stands with the other students, at the edge of the grove, watching. He holds his breath. The singing, the cold air, the white robes, the green mistletoe, the golden sickle shining in the sun, and the ancient oak, reaching up into the sky. It all seems very old, and very holy. He feels as if he were standing at the beginning of the world.

--

Today, more than two thousand years later, people still hang mistletoe in their houses, at Christmas time. And there is a custom that, if two people stand under the mistletoe, they should kiss. Nobody knows exactly where this custom comes from. But perhaps, somewhere far back in time, it remembers the druids, and the sacred green plant on the oak, that brought good fortune and peace, in the middle of winter.
