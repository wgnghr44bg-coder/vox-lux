The Celts are some of the best metalworkers of the ancient world. And the blacksmiths are among the most respected people in the tribe. They work with fire and iron, which seem almost magical. They make the tools for farming, the weapons for war, and the beautiful objects for the nobles and the gods. Cunobel is proud of his craft. He learned it from his father, who learned it from his.

--

Iron comes from iron ore, a reddish rock, which is dug from the ground in the hills nearby. The ore is smelted in small clay furnaces, called bloomeries, with charcoal. The furnace is blown with bellows, made of animal skin, until it is very hot. The iron melts out of the ore, and forms a spongy lump, called a bloom. Then the smith heats the bloom again and again, in his forge, and hammers it, to drive out the impurities, until it becomes good, strong iron.

In his forge, Cunobel heats the iron in the fire, until it glows red, then orange, then yellow. Then he takes it out with long tongs, lays it on the anvil, and hammers it into shape. Clang, clang, clang. The sparks fly. The iron bends and stretches under the hammer. When it cools, he heats it again. Slowly, the shapeless lump becomes a ploughshare, an axe, a knife, a sickle or a sword.

{img:celtic-iron-tools-ploughshare-axe-sickle-hammer-workshop-wall}
The Celts invented many tools that are still used today. They made iron ploughs, which could plough the heavy soils of northern Europe. They made iron rims for wheels, fitted while hot, so that they shrank tight onto the wooden wheel. They made iron chain mail, a kind of armour made of thousands of little iron rings, linked together. The Romans copied it. They made scissors, and saws, and files, and barrels, and many other things.

But Cunobel's greatest pride is his fine work. Brooches, buckles and decorations, of bronze, with beautiful swirling patterns. And sometimes, for rich customers, a torc, or a sword hilt, decorated with gold, and with red enamel, made by melting powdered red glass onto the bronze. The Celtic craftsmen of Bibracte are famous for their red enamel. Visitors come from far away to buy it.

--

{img:enamel-brooch-red-glass-bronze-swirls-craftsman-hands-close-detail}
The Celts also make coins. Gold, silver and bronze coins, stamped with pictures. Usually, a head on one side, and a horse on the other. The Celts copied the idea from the Greek coins of King Philip of Macedonia. But over time, they changed the pictures, making them more and more Celtic. The horses become strange and wild, with swirling legs. The heads become patterns. Today, Celtic coins are some of the most beautiful coins of the ancient world.

Lugos has often watched his father at work. He loves the fire, and the sparks, and the sound of the hammer. He has learned to work the bellows, and to hold the tongs. But he has never wanted to be a smith. He prefers words to iron. His father understands. He says that every person has their own fire. His is in the forge. Lugos's is in his head.

{img:father-giving-son-small-bronze-brooch-gift-forge-glow-farewell}
On the evening before Lugos goes to the druid's house, Cunobel gives him a present. A small brooch of bronze, which he has made especially for him. It is shaped like an owl, with eyes of red enamel. The owl is the bird of wisdom, Cunobel says. And it can see in the dark. Lugos pins it onto his cloak, at the shoulder. He will wear it for the rest of his life.

--

The forge is the warmest place in the house. In winter, the family often sits there in the evenings, by the glowing coals, after Cunobel has finished his work. The light of the coals flickers on the walls, on the tools hanging there, and on the faces of the family. And Cunobel tells stories about the great smiths of the old days, and about the smith of the gods, who forged the weapons of the heroes, deep under the mountains.
