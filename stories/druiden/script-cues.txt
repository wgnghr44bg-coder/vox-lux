Tonight we travel back more than two thousand years, to the year 60 before Christ. We go to Gaul, the land that is now France. It is a land of great forests, wide rivers, green hills and rich farmland. And on top of a high, wooded hill, in the middle of Gaul, there stands a great walled town. It is called Bibracte. And it is the capital of a Celtic people, called the Aedui.

[long-pause]

It is early morning, in spring. A thick mist fills the valleys below the hill. Only the tops of the hills rise above it, dark with forests of beech and oak. On top of the highest hill, there is a long wall of stone and timber, running around the town. Inside the wall, there are hundreds of houses, with thatched roofs, and smoke rising from them into the cool morning air. A cock is crowing. Somewhere, a blacksmith's hammer is already ringing.

[pause]

The people of Bibracte are waking up. Farmers are leading their cattle out to the fields. Women are fetching water from the springs. Craftsmen are opening their workshops. And in a quiet house near the sacred spring, an old man with a long white beard, in a white robe, is standing at his door, looking at the sky. He is watching the moon fade, and the sun rise. He is a druid.

[pause]

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

{img:celtic-family-gaul-woollen-checked-cloaks-torc-doorway-morning-light}
The Celts were one of the great peoples of ancient Europe. They lived across a huge area, from Ireland and Britain in the west, through Gaul and Spain, to the lands along the Danube, and even as far as Turkey. They were not one country, but many different tribes, each with its own king or leaders. But they spoke related languages, believed in similar gods, and shared a way of life.

[pause]

Tonight, we will spend a year among the Celts of Gaul, just before the Romans came. We will follow a family in the town of Bibracte. A blacksmith, his wife, and their two children. Especially their son, a boy of twelve, who has been chosen to study with the druids. We will see how the Celts lived, worked, ate and celebrated. We will visit their workshops, their sacred groves and their great feasts.

[pause]

{img:white-robed-druid-oak-grove-sunlight-old-man-staff-calm-wise}
And we will meet the druids. The wise men of the Celts. Priests, judges, teachers, healers and keepers of knowledge. Very little is known about them, because they never wrote anything down. Everything we know comes from what Greek and Roman writers said about them, and from archaeology. And so, they are one of the great mysteries of the ancient world.

[long-pause]

The Romans told some dark stories about the druids, and about the Celts. Some of them may be true. Some were probably exaggerated, by enemies who wanted to make the Celts look wild. We will not dwell on them. Instead, we will look at the everyday life of the Celts, their skill, their art, their love of stories, and their deep connection with the natural world. The oak trees, the springs, the moon and the stars.

[pause]

{img:celtic-hillfort-sunrise-over-forested-hills-gaul-golden-light}
So let us begin, on a misty spring morning, on a hill in the middle of Gaul, in the year 60 before Christ. The sun is rising over the forests. The birds are singing in the oaks. And in the town of Bibracte, a boy of twelve is waking up, on the most important day of his life.

[long-pause]

{img:map-feeling-celtic-europe-tribes-hillforts-rivers-forests-ancient}
Who were the Celts? The name comes from the Greeks, who called them Keltoi. The Romans called them Galli, or Gauls. They themselves probably did not use one name for all their peoples. They called themselves by the names of their tribes. The Aedui, the Arverni, the Helvetii, the Belgae, and many others. In Britain, there were the Iceni, the Brigantes, the Catuvellauni. In Ireland, there were many more.

[long-pause]

The Celts had lived in Europe for a very long time. Their culture grew up in the middle of Europe, in the lands that are now Austria, Switzerland and southern Germany, more than seven hundred years before our story. From there, it spread in all directions. Some Celts moved west, into Gaul and Spain. Some moved north, into Britain and Ireland. Some moved south, into Italy. And some moved east, into the Balkans and even into Turkey.

[pause]

About three hundred years before our story, some Celts attacked Rome itself, and took the city. The Romans never forgot it. Other Celts went to Greece, and reached the famous temple of Delphi. Celtic warriors were feared all over the ancient world. They were tall and strong, with long hair and moustaches, and they went into battle with great courage, sometimes shouting and singing.

[pause]

{img:la-tene-celtic-art-swirling-patterns-bronze-shield-close-gold-detail}
But the Celts were not only warriors. They were also wonderful farmers, craftsmen and artists. They were some of the best metalworkers in the ancient world. They made beautiful jewellery, weapons, shields and cauldrons, of bronze, iron, silver and gold, decorated with swirling patterns, spirals, curving lines and strange animals. This style of art is called La Tène, after a place in Switzerland where many beautiful objects were found.

[pause]

The Celts did not use writing very much. They had no books. Their history, their laws, their poems and their religion were all kept in memory, and passed on by word of mouth, from teacher to student, from parent to child. That is why we know so little about them from their own words. Most of what we know comes from what the Greeks and Romans wrote about them. And from what archaeologists have found in the ground.

[long-pause]

{img:gallic-farmers-fields-oxen-plough-wheat-cattle-countryside-summer}
In the time of our story, Gaul is a rich and busy land. There are many towns, called oppida, on hills, surrounded by strong walls. There are farms and villages, with fields of wheat and barley, and great herds of cattle, pigs and sheep. There are roads, and rivers full of boats, carrying goods. There are markets, where people buy and sell with coins. The Gauls trade with the Greeks and the Romans, to the south, and with the Britons, across the sea.

[pause]

The tribes of Gaul are often rivals. They quarrel and sometimes fight with each other. The two most powerful tribes are the Aedui, in the middle of Gaul, and the Arverni, to the south-west. They have been rivals for a long time. The Aedui have made friends with the Romans, who rule the south of Gaul, by the Mediterranean. They are called brothers and kinsmen of the Roman people. It is a friendship that will have great consequences.

[pause]

{img:gallic-river-boats-trade-amphorae-wine-barrels-riverbank-town}
In the south of Gaul, along the Mediterranean coast, the Romans have already ruled for about sixty years. They call it the Province, and today it is still called Provence. From there, Roman merchants come north, selling wine, oil and fine pottery. The Gauls love Roman wine. They pay for it with slaves, metals, grain and cattle. Some people say a Gaul would trade a slave for a single jar of wine.

[long-pause]

But in the year 60 before Christ, most of Gaul is still free. The Romans are only in the south. The Celtic tribes rule themselves, with their own kings and nobles, their own laws, their own gods, and their own druids. Nobody knows that, in just two years, a Roman general called Julius Caesar will come north, with his legions. And that everything will change. But for now, life in Gaul goes on, as it has for centuries.

[long-pause]

{img:bibracte-murus-gallicus-wall-timber-stone-gate-oppidum-forest-hill}
Bibracte stands on top of a high, wooded hill, called Mont Beuvray today, in the middle of Burgundy, in France. It is about eight hundred metres high, one of the highest hills in the region. From the top, on a clear day, you can see for a very long way. Forests, valleys, rivers and other hills, stretching away into the blue distance. Even, people say, the snowy peaks of the Alps, far to the east.

[long-pause]

The town is surrounded by a great wall, more than five kilometres long. It is a special kind of wall, which the Romans called the Gallic wall. It is made of a frame of wooden beams, laid in layers, crossing each other, and nailed together with long iron nails. The spaces between the beams are filled with earth and stones. And the front is faced with stone blocks. It is very strong. Battering rams cannot break it, because the wood bends. And fire cannot burn it, because the stone protects it.

[pause]

There are several gates in the wall. The main gate is huge, with a wide passage, flanked by high walls, so that anyone coming in must walk between them, watched by guards above. Through the gates, every day, come farmers with carts of grain, herders with cattle, merchants with goods, and visitors from far away. Bibracte is a busy place. Several thousand people live here.

[pause]

{img:bibracte-street-workshops-smiths-bronze-workers-enamel-craftsmen-busy}
Inside the walls, the town is divided into different areas. Near the main gate, there are the workshops of the craftsmen. Blacksmiths, bronze workers, enamellers, potters, carpenters and leather workers. The street is noisy and smoky, with the sound of hammers and the glow of furnaces. The craftsmen of Bibracte are famous. They make beautiful brooches, decorated with bright red enamel, which are sold all over Gaul.

[pause]

Further up the hill, there are the houses of ordinary people. Simple houses of timber and wattle, with thatched roofs, along little streets and paths. And at the top of the hill, there are the bigger houses of the nobles, the rich and powerful families of the Aedui. Some of them are quite grand, with many rooms, and courtyards. A few of the richest even have houses built in the Roman style, with tiled roofs and painted walls.

[long-pause]

{img:sacred-spring-oppidum-stone-basin-water-flowing-offerings-trees}
In the middle of the town, there is a great open space, used for meetings and markets. Here, the people gather for assemblies, where the nobles and the druids discuss the affairs of the tribe. Here, too, there are markets, where farmers sell their produce, and merchants sell their goods. And not far away, there is a sacred spring, where water flows out of the hillside into a stone basin, and where people come to pray and leave offerings.

[pause]

The Aedui are ruled by a council of nobles. Every year, they elect a chief magistrate, called the vergobret, who rules the tribe for one year. He has great power, but only for that one year, and he may not leave the territory of the tribe during his rule. The druids help to choose him. It is a wise system, which prevents any one man from becoming too powerful.

[pause]

{img:view-from-oppidum-over-forested-hills-morrow-valleys-blue-distance}
From the edges of the town, the views are wonderful. The hillsides are covered with forests of beech and oak, which turn golden in autumn. In the valleys below, there are fields, pastures and little villages, with smoke rising from their roofs. Rivers wind through the valleys, shining in the sun. And over everything, the great sky, with its clouds and its stars, which the druids watch so carefully.

[long-pause]

In winter, Bibracte is a cold place. The wind blows hard over the high hill, and snow often covers the roofs and the streets. The people stay close to their fires. But in summer, it is beautiful. The air is fresh and cool, even on the hottest days. The forests are green and full of birds. And the people of Bibracte think that their town, on its high hill, is the best place in all of Gaul.

[long-pause]

{img:celtic-blacksmith-father-forge-leather-apron-hammer-glowing-iron-gaul}
Near the main gate of Bibracte, in the street of the craftsmen, there lives a family. The father is called Cunobel. He is a blacksmith. He is about forty years old, big and strong, with long reddish hair tied back, and a great moustache, as is the fashion among Gaulish men. His arms are covered with little burn marks from the sparks. He makes tools, weapons, nails, hinges, and sometimes beautiful things, like brooches and torcs.

[long-pause]

His wife is called Elva. She is a weaver. She weaves cloth on a big upright loom, in the house. The Celts love bright colours and patterns. Elva weaves cloth in checks and stripes, in red, blue, green and yellow. The Romans think that the Gauls' clothes are very colourful, even a little loud. Elva is famous in the street for her beautiful cloth. She is a calm, wise woman, with long braids, and a gentle laugh.

[pause]

Their son is called Lugos. He is twelve years old. He has his mother's calm grey eyes, and his father's red hair. He is quiet and thoughtful, and he has an extraordinary memory. He can remember every story he has ever heard, word for word. He can remember the names of all his ancestors, back seven generations. He knows all the songs of the bards. And he loves to watch the stars at night, and to ask questions about them.

[pause]

{img:celtic-boy-twelve-red-hair-listening-to-old-druid-under-oak-tree}
Their daughter is called Senna. She is nine. She is lively and cheerful, always running, always talking, always laughing. She loves animals, especially horses. She spends hours at the stables, near the gate, helping the grooms and feeding the horses. She dreams of having her own horse one day. Her favourite goddess is Epona, the goddess of horses, and she has a little clay figure of her, which she keeps by her bed.

[pause]

There is also a grandmother, Elva's mother, who is very old and wise. She knows all the old herbs and remedies, and all the old stories. And she is the one who first noticed Lugos's special memory. When he was only five, he could repeat her longest stories back to her, word for word, without a single mistake. She told her daughter that the boy had a gift. A gift that the druids would want.

[long-pause]

{img:celtic-family-around-hearth-cauldron-evening-firelight-grandmother-story}
On this spring morning, the family is up early. Today, an old druid is coming to the house. His name is Diviciacus. He is the most famous and most powerful druid of the Aedui. And he is coming to speak with Lugos, to see if the boy is suitable to become a student of the druids. It is a great honour. Elva has swept the house, and put out the best cups and bowls. Cunobel has put on his best tunic, and his bronze torc.

[pause]

Lugos is very nervous. He has been thinking about this day for months. To become a druid is the greatest thing a boy can hope for. Druids are respected by everyone, even the kings. They do not have to fight in wars, or pay taxes. They know the secrets of the gods, the stars and the earth. But it takes a very long time to become one. Twenty years of study, the people say. Lugos would be thirty-two before he finished.

[pause]

{img:old-druid-arriving-at-humble-house-family-bowing-respect-morning}
At midmorning, the druid arrives. He is a tall, thin old man, with a long white beard, and bright, kind eyes. He wears a white robe, and carries a staff of oak. He greets the family politely, and sits down by the fire. He drinks the milk that Elva offers him. And then he turns to Lugos, and asks him to recite something. Anything he knows.

[long-pause]

Lugos takes a deep breath, and begins. He recites the names of the kings of the Aedui, back for ten generations. Then a long poem about a hero who crossed the sea. Then the names of all the rivers of Gaul. The druid listens, with his eyes closed. When Lugos has finished, there is a long silence. Then the old man opens his eyes, and smiles. He says that he will take the boy as his student. Elva bursts into tears. Cunobel puts his big hand on his son's shoulder. And Senna jumps up and down, and runs out to tell the horses.

[long-pause]

{img:gallic-house-timber-wattle-daub-thatched-roof-yard-chickens-oppidum-street}
The house of Cunobel and Elva is made of timber, with walls of woven branches covered with clay, and a roof of thick straw thatch. It is rectangular, about ten metres long. At the front, facing the street, there is the forge, Cunobel's workshop, open to the street, with its furnace, anvil and tools. Behind it, there is the living room, where the family lives, eats and sleeps.

[long-pause]

In the middle of the living room, there is a hearth, a low square of stones, where the fire burns all the time. Over the fire, from a chain hanging from the roof beams, there is a big cauldron of bronze, in which Elva cooks the family's meals. The smoke rises up through the thatch. Along the walls, there are benches covered with furs and woollen blankets, where the family sits during the day, and sleeps at night. There is Elva's loom, standing against one wall. And there are big clay jars, for storing grain, beans and beer.

[pause]

The Gauls are good cooks, and they eat well. Their main food is bread and porridge, made from wheat, barley and millet. They eat a lot of meat, especially pork. The Gauls are famous for their pigs and their hams, which they even sell to Rome. They also eat beef, mutton, chicken, and game, like deer, boar and hare. And fish from the rivers. And there are vegetables, beans, lentils, peas, onions, cabbages and wild herbs. And cheese, butter and milk.

[pause]

{img:celtic-meal-wooden-table-bread-pork-cheese-beer-cups-family-eating}
The Gauls sit on the floor, or on low benches, on animal skins, to eat. They eat from wooden or pottery bowls, and use knives and their fingers. They drink beer, made from barley or wheat, which they call cervesia. Beer is very popular. The Romans think that drinking beer is a little strange, because they drink wine. Rich Gauls also drink wine, imported from Italy at great expense, and mead, made from honey.

[pause]

Elva makes a special dish for the druid's visit. Pork, cooked slowly in the cauldron, with beans, onions and herbs. And fresh bread, baked on a flat stone by the fire. There is also a little honey cake, for dessert. And for the druid, a cup of the best beer, which Elva brewed herself. The old man eats with pleasure, and says that it is the best meal he has had in a long time. Elva blushes with pride.

[long-pause]

{img:celtic-clothing-checked-trousers-tunic-cloak-brooch-woman-long-dress}
The Gauls dress in a way that surprises the Romans. The men wear trousers, called bracae, made of wool, often in bright checks and stripes. The Romans, who wear tunics and togas, think that trousers are very strange and barbarian. Over the trousers, the men wear a tunic, with a belt. And over that, a cloak, fastened at the shoulder with a brooch. The women wear long dresses, of wool or linen, with a belt, and a cloak.

[pause]

Both men and women love jewellery. Brooches, bracelets, rings, necklaces of glass and amber beads. And the most famous Celtic jewellery of all, the torc. A torc is a thick neck ring, made of twisted metal, gold, silver or bronze, open at the front, with decorated ends. It is a sign of rank and importance. Warriors and nobles wear gold torcs. Cunobel wears a bronze one, which he made himself. And the gods are often shown wearing torcs too.

[pause]

{img:gold-celtic-torc-twisted-neck-ring-decorated-terminals-close-light}
The Gauls take great care of their appearance. They wash often, and they use soap, which they invented. The Romans learned soap from the Gauls. The men often wear long moustaches, and some bleach their hair with lime water, to make it lighter and stiffer. The women braid their hair, in long, beautiful braids. Everyone is proud of their looks. Being clean, neat and well dressed is very important.

[long-pause]

In the evenings, the family sits around the hearth. Cunobel mends his tools. Elva spins. The grandmother tells stories. Senna plays with a puppy. And Lugos thinks about his new life, with the druids, which will start tomorrow. He will leave home, and go to live in the druid's house, near the sacred spring. He will come home to visit. But it will never be quite the same. He looks around the warm, smoky room, at his family, by the fire. And he tries to remember everything, the way he remembers stories. So that he can keep it forever.

[long-pause]

{img:celtic-smith-forging-iron-sword-anvil-sparks-bellows-dark-workshop-glow}
The Celts are some of the best metalworkers of the ancient world. And the blacksmiths are among the most respected people in the tribe. They work with fire and iron, which seem almost magical. They make the tools for farming, the weapons for war, and the beautiful objects for the nobles and the gods. Cunobel is proud of his craft. He learned it from his father, who learned it from his.

[long-pause]

Iron comes from iron ore, a reddish rock, which is dug from the ground in the hills nearby. The ore is smelted in small clay furnaces, called bloomeries, with charcoal. The furnace is blown with bellows, made of animal skin, until it is very hot. The iron melts out of the ore, and forms a spongy lump, called a bloom. Then the smith heats the bloom again and again, in his forge, and hammers it, to drive out the impurities, until it becomes good, strong iron.

[pause]

In his forge, Cunobel heats the iron in the fire, until it glows red, then orange, then yellow. Then he takes it out with long tongs, lays it on the anvil, and hammers it into shape. Clang, clang, clang. The sparks fly. The iron bends and stretches under the hammer. When it cools, he heats it again. Slowly, the shapeless lump becomes a ploughshare, an axe, a knife, a sickle or a sword.

[pause]

{img:celtic-iron-tools-ploughshare-axe-sickle-hammer-workshop-wall}
The Celts invented many tools that are still used today. They made iron ploughs, which could plough the heavy soils of northern Europe. They made iron rims for wheels, fitted while hot, so that they shrank tight onto the wooden wheel. They made iron chain mail, a kind of armour made of thousands of little iron rings, linked together. The Romans copied it. They made scissors, and saws, and files, and barrels, and many other things.

[pause]

But Cunobel's greatest pride is his fine work. Brooches, buckles and decorations, of bronze, with beautiful swirling patterns. And sometimes, for rich customers, a torc, or a sword hilt, decorated with gold, and with red enamel, made by melting powdered red glass onto the bronze. The Celtic craftsmen of Bibracte are famous for their red enamel. Visitors come from far away to buy it.

[long-pause]

{img:enamel-brooch-red-glass-bronze-swirls-craftsman-hands-close-detail}
The Celts also make coins. Gold, silver and bronze coins, stamped with pictures. Usually, a head on one side, and a horse on the other. The Celts copied the idea from the Greek coins of King Philip of Macedonia. But over time, they changed the pictures, making them more and more Celtic. The horses become strange and wild, with swirling legs. The heads become patterns. Today, Celtic coins are some of the most beautiful coins of the ancient world.

[pause]

Lugos has often watched his father at work. He loves the fire, and the sparks, and the sound of the hammer. He has learned to work the bellows, and to hold the tongs. But he has never wanted to be a smith. He prefers words to iron. His father understands. He says that every person has their own fire. His is in the forge. Lugos's is in his head.

[pause]

{img:father-giving-son-small-bronze-brooch-gift-forge-glow-farewell}
On the evening before Lugos goes to the druid's house, Cunobel gives him a present. A small brooch of bronze, which he has made especially for him. It is shaped like an owl, with eyes of red enamel. The owl is the bird of wisdom, Cunobel says. And it can see in the dark. Lugos pins it onto his cloak, at the shoulder. He will wear it for the rest of his life.

[long-pause]

The forge is the warmest place in the house. In winter, the family often sits there in the evenings, by the glowing coals, after Cunobel has finished his work. The light of the coals flickers on the walls, on the tools hanging there, and on the faces of the family. And Cunobel tells stories about the great smiths of the old days, and about the smith of the gods, who forged the weapons of the heroes, deep under the mountains.

[long-pause]

{img:druids-gathered-oak-grove-white-robes-sunlight-through-leaves-council}
Who were the druids? It is one of the great questions of ancient history. The druids were the wise men of the Celts. And sometimes wise women too. They were priests, who performed the sacred ceremonies and spoke with the gods. But they were much more than that. They were also judges, teachers, scholars, healers, astronomers, poets and advisers to kings.

[long-pause]

The most detailed description of the druids comes from Julius Caesar, the Roman general, who conquered Gaul a few years after our story. He wrote a book about his wars, and in it, he described the druids. He said that they were one of the two most important groups of people in Gaul, together with the nobles. They looked after the religion, the sacrifices and the questions of right and wrong. And they had great power.

[pause]

Caesar wrote that the druids were the judges of almost all disputes, both between tribes and between people. If there was a quarrel about land, or an inheritance, or a crime, the druids decided. And everyone accepted their decision. If someone refused to accept it, the druids could forbid them from taking part in the sacred ceremonies. That was the worst punishment of all. Everyone avoided such a person, as if they were cursed.

[pause]

{img:druid-judge-listening-to-two-men-dispute-council-circle-tribe-gathered}
The druids did not have to go to war, and they did not pay taxes. Because of these privileges, and because of their great prestige, many young men wanted to become druids. Some were sent by their families. Others chose it themselves. But it was not easy. Caesar wrote that some students studied for twenty years. And during all that time, they learned everything by heart. Thousands of verses of poetry, containing all the knowledge of the druids.

[pause]

The druids did not write their knowledge down. Caesar thought this was for two reasons. Because they did not want their sacred knowledge to become known to ordinary people. And because they believed that writing weakens the memory. If you write something down, you do not bother to remember it. Strangely, the druids did know how to write. For other purposes, like accounts and letters, they used Greek letters. But never for sacred things.

[long-pause]

{img:druid-teaching-students-sitting-in-circle-forest-glade-reciting-verses}
What did the druids teach? Caesar wrote that they taught about the stars and their movements, about the size of the world and of the earth, about nature, and about the power of the gods. They taught that the soul does not die, but passes from one body to another after death. Some Greek writers thought that this was why the Celts were so brave. They did not fear death, because they believed they would live again.

[pause]

There were different kinds of wise people among the Celts. Greek writers described three groups. The druids, who were the philosophers and priests. The bards, who were the poets and singers, who sang the praises of heroes and the history of the tribe. And the vates, or seers, who studied nature and foretold the future, by watching the flight of birds, the stars and other signs. Sometimes, one person could belong to more than one group.

[pause]

{img:seer-watching-birds-flight-hilltop-dawn-staff-reading-signs-sky}
The druids had a leader, a chief druid, chosen from among them, who held office for life. When he died, the next most important druid took his place. If there were several candidates, they voted. And once a year, the druids of all Gaul met together, in a sacred place in the land of a tribe called the Carnutes, which was thought to be the centre of Gaul. There, they held a great assembly, and judged disputes from all over the country.

[long-pause]

Caesar also wrote that the druids' teaching came originally from Britain. And that those who wanted to learn it most deeply went to Britain to study. Perhaps Britain was the heart of the druid religion. Perhaps there were famous druid schools there. Nobody knows for sure. But it shows how connected the Celtic world was, across the sea. And how much respect the druids had, from Ireland to the Danube.

[long-pause]

{img:boy-arriving-druid-house-sacred-spring-oak-trees-other-students-waiting}
On a spring morning, Lugos leaves his family's house, and walks up the hill to the house of the druid Diviciacus, near the sacred spring. He carries a small bundle, with a spare tunic, a cloak and a blanket. And on his shoulder, he wears the owl brooch that his father made. His mother kisses him goodbye at the door. Senna walks with him all the way, holding his hand, talking without stopping.

[long-pause]

The druid's house is bigger than his family's. It has several rooms, and a courtyard, with an old oak tree in the middle. There are already five other students living there. Boys from noble families, from Bibracte and the villages around. Some are younger than Lugos, some are older. The oldest is a young man of twenty-five, who has been studying for thirteen years, and is almost a druid himself. He is called Cotus, and he is kind to the new boy.

[pause]

The students' life is simple and strict. They get up before dawn, and greet the rising sun. They wash in the cold water of the sacred spring. They eat simple food, bread, porridge, milk, fruit and a little meat. They help with the work of the house. And they study, all day long, from morning until evening. Not from books, because there are no books. But by listening, and repeating, and remembering.

[pause]

{img:students-reciting-verses-in-unison-druid-listening-courtyard-oak}
Every day, the old druid teaches them verses. Long poems, full of knowledge. About the gods and their stories. About the laws of the tribe. About the history of the Aedui, and the names of their kings and heroes. About the stars, the moon and the seasons. About the plants and their uses, for food and medicine. About the land, its rivers, hills and sacred places. All in verse, because verse is easier to remember than ordinary speech.

[pause]

The students repeat the verses after him, again and again, all together, in a kind of chant. Then each student must repeat them alone. If he makes a mistake, he must start again. And they repeat the verses they learned yesterday, and last week, and last month, and last year, so that they never forget them. Day after day, year after year, the verses pile up in their memories, like the rings of an oak tree.

[long-pause]

{img:young-student-alone-under-oak-tree-memorizing-eyes-closed-evening}
For Lugos, with his wonderful memory, the learning is easy, at first. He learns the verses faster than any of the other boys. But the old druid does not only want him to remember. He wants him to understand. So after each verse, he asks questions. What does it mean? Why is it so? How does it connect with what you learned before? These questions are much harder. And Lugos often has to think about them for days.

[pause]

The students also learn by watching. They go with the druid when he visits the sick, and learn how to heal them with herbs. They watch him judge disputes, in the assembly. They go with him to the sacred groves and springs, and watch him perform the ceremonies. And at night, they sit with him on the hill, and watch the stars, and learn their names and their movements.

[pause]

{img:druid-and-boy-gathering-herbs-meadow-dawn-dew-basket-teaching-plants}
The old druid is strict, but kind. He has a dry sense of humour. When a student makes a silly mistake, he does not get angry. He just raises one eyebrow, and waits. And the student, red in the face, corrects himself. He tells the students that memory is like a field. If you look after it, and plant it, and tend it, it will give you a harvest all your life. But if you neglect it, it will be covered with weeds.

[long-pause]

In the evenings, Lugos sometimes goes home, to visit his family, and eat supper with them. Senna wants to know everything. What did he learn today? Is the druid strict? Can he do magic? Lugos laughs, and says no, druids cannot do magic. But they can remember things that nobody else remembers. And that, he says, is a kind of magic too.

[long-pause]

{img:druid-diviciacus-elderly-noble-gaulish-cloak-torc-dignified-portrait}
The druid who teaches Lugos is a real person, from history. His name is Diviciacus. He is the only druid from ancient Gaul whose name we know. We know about him because two famous Romans wrote about him. One was Julius Caesar. The other was Cicero, the greatest speaker and writer of Rome. And Cicero knew him personally. Diviciacus stayed in his house.

[long-pause]

Diviciacus is a nobleman of the Aedui, from one of the most important families of the tribe. He is also a druid, and one of the most powerful men in the tribe. He has a younger brother, called Dumnorix, who is ambitious and proud, and very popular with the ordinary people. The two brothers are rivals. Diviciacus is a friend of Rome. Dumnorix does not trust the Romans. Their rivalry will play an important part in the coming years.

[pause]

About a year before our story, Diviciacus made a long journey. To Rome. The Aedui were in trouble. Their rivals had brought in a German king, with his warriors, from across the river Rhine. The Germans had settled in Gaul, and were taking land from the Aedui. So Diviciacus went to Rome, to ask the Senate for help, because the Aedui were friends and allies of Rome. He stood before the Senate, leaning on his shield, and spoke to them.

[pause]

{img:gaulish-druid-standing-before-roman-senate-marble-hall-senators-listening}
The Senate listened politely. But they did not send help. Not yet. Diviciacus stayed in Rome for some time. And while he was there, he stayed at the house of Cicero's brother, Quintus. And there, he met Cicero, who was very interested in him. Cicero was curious about everything, especially about religion, philosophy and the gods. And here was a real druid, a priest of the mysterious Celts, sitting at his dinner table.

[pause]

Many years later, Cicero wrote about Diviciacus, in a book about divination, about how people try to know the future. He wrote that he had known a druid, Diviciacus the Aeduan, who said that he knew the laws of nature, which the Greeks call physiologia, and who could predict the future, partly by watching the flight of birds, and partly by reasoning. It is a short passage. But it is one of the very few real descriptions we have of a druid, by someone who met him.

[long-pause]

{img:roman-villa-dinner-cicero-and-gaulish-guest-conversation-lamps}
Imagine that dinner. In a fine Roman house, with marble floors and painted walls, lit by oil lamps. Roman senators, in their white togas, lying on couches. And among them, a tall Gaulish nobleman, with a long moustache, a gold torc, and checked trousers. Talking about the stars, the soul, the gods and the future. Two worlds, meeting at one table. The Roman world of writing and law. And the Celtic world of memory and nature.

[pause]

Now, Diviciacus has come home to Bibracte. He is tired from his long journey, and worried about the future of his tribe. But he is also glad to be home, among his own people, in the hills and the forests. He tells his students about Rome. About its enormous size, its marble temples and palaces, its crowds, its noise, its dirt. About the Senate and the forum. About the Roman way of doing things, with written laws and endless arguments.

[pause]

{img:old-druid-telling-students-about-rome-courtyard-oak-evening-firelight}
Lugos listens, fascinated. He has never been further than the next valley. And here is his teacher, who has crossed the Alps, and seen Rome, and talked with its greatest men. He asks a hundred questions. What do the Romans eat? What gods do they pray to? Do they really write everything down? The old druid answers patiently. And then, he says something that Lugos will remember. The Romans are very clever, and very strong, he says. And one day, they will come here.

[long-pause]

Diviciacus is right. In just two years, Julius Caesar will come to Gaul, with his legions, at the request of the Aedui, to help them against their enemies. Diviciacus will become Caesar's friend and adviser. And Caesar will stay in Gaul for eight years. And at the end, all of Gaul will be part of the Roman Empire. Diviciacus does not know this yet. He only knows that the world is changing, and that his people must be wise.

[long-pause]

{img:druid-and-students-hilltop-night-watching-stars-moon-milky-way-gaul}
The druids are great watchers of the sky. On clear nights, Diviciacus takes his students up to the highest point of the hill, outside the town walls, where nothing blocks the view. They sit on the grass, wrapped in their cloaks, and look up at the stars. And the old druid teaches them their names, their shapes and their movements.

[long-pause]

He shows them the bright stars that rise in the east, and set in the west, every night. The great shapes in the sky, which the Celts see as their own heroes, animals and gods. He shows them the planets, the wandering stars, which move slowly among the others, from night to night. He shows them the Milky Way, the great band of pale light across the sky, which some Celts call the road of the souls. And he shows them the moon.

[pause]

The moon is the most important thing in the sky for the druids. Their calendar is based on the moon. Each month begins with a new moon, or perhaps with the first quarter. The Greek and Roman writers said that the Celts count time not by days but by nights. So a festival begins in the evening, and lasts until the next evening. And a period of two weeks is called fourteen nights. This custom lives on in English, in the word fortnight.

[pause]

{img:coligny-calendar-bronze-plate-engraved-holes-months-symbols-close}
The druids have created an amazing calendar. We know about it because a bronze plate was found in France, more than a hundred years ago, in a place called Coligny. It was broken into many pieces, but it has been put back together. It shows a calendar of five years, with sixty-two months, each with twenty-nine or thirty days. There are little holes, for putting a peg into, to mark the day. And there are special words and signs, showing which days are lucky and which are unlucky.

[pause]

The calendar is very clever. A year of twelve moon months is shorter than a real year, by about eleven days. So the druids added an extra month, every two and a half years, to keep the calendar in step with the sun and the seasons. This is a difficult calculation. It shows that the druids were very good astronomers and mathematicians. The Coligny calendar is written in the Gaulish language, using Roman letters. It was made a little after our story, in Roman times. But it shows the knowledge of the druids.

[long-pause]

{img:moon-phases-over-oak-forest-crescent-full-druid-silhouette-watching}
The druids also watch the sun. They know the days when the sun rises furthest to the north, in midsummer, and furthest to the south, in midwinter. They know the days in spring and autumn when day and night are equal. These are important days, for the festivals and for the farmers. The druids tell the people when to plough, when to sow, and when to harvest. Their knowledge of the sky is useful to everyone.

[pause]

Lugos loves the nights on the hill. He lies on his back in the grass, looking up at the thousands of stars, and listening to the old druid's quiet voice, naming them. He tries to remember every name. He tries to imagine how far away they are. The druid says that nobody knows. Some say the stars are fires in the sky. Some say they are the souls of heroes. Some say they are holes in the great cloak of the night, with light shining through.

[pause]

{img:boy-lying-on-grass-looking-up-at-starry-sky-cloak-owl-brooch-wonder}
One night, a shooting star streaks across the sky, leaving a bright line of light behind it, for a moment. Lugos gasps. The old druid smiles. He says that the Celts believe a shooting star is a sign from the gods. Some say it means that a soul has passed on to the Otherworld. Some say it means that a wish will come true. Lugos quickly makes a wish. He wishes that he will become a good druid, as wise as his teacher. He does not tell anyone.

[long-pause]

When the moon sets, and the night grows cold, the old druid and his students walk back down to the town, through the dark, by the light of the stars. The town is asleep. The fires are covered. Only a dog barks, at the gate. And Lugos lies down on his blanket, in the druid's house, and closes his eyes, and sees the stars still, behind his eyelids, turning slowly, as they have turned forever.

[long-pause]

{img:mistletoe-growing-on-old-oak-tree-winter-sunlight-white-berries-sacred}
There is one ceremony of the druids that has become very famous. It was described by a Roman writer called Pliny the Elder, more than a hundred years after our story. It is the cutting of the mistletoe. And it shows how deeply the druids loved and respected nature, especially the oak tree.

[long-pause]

The oak was the most sacred tree of the druids. Some people think that the word druid itself comes from an old Celtic word for oak, combined with a word meaning to know. So a druid would be someone with the knowledge of the oak. The druids held their ceremonies in groves of oak trees, in the forests. They used oak leaves in their rituals. And they believed that the oak was the tree of the greatest of the gods.

[pause]

Mistletoe is a strange plant. It does not grow in the ground. It grows on the branches of trees, high up, as a round, bushy ball of green leaves, with little white berries. It stays green all winter, when the tree is bare. It usually grows on apple trees, poplars and limes. But very rarely, it grows on an oak. And when the druids found mistletoe growing on an oak, Pliny wrote, they believed it was a gift from the gods, and treated it as something very holy.

[pause]

{img:white-robed-druid-golden-sickle-cutting-mistletoe-oak-branch-white-cloth-below}
Pliny described the ceremony. It happened on the sixth day of the moon. The druids prepared a feast under the tree. Then a druid, dressed in a white robe, climbed up into the oak. With a sickle made of gold, he cut the mistletoe. And below the tree, other druids caught it in a white cloth, so that it did not touch the ground. Then the druids prayed that the god would make his gift bring good fortune to the people.

[pause]

Pliny wrote that the druids called the mistletoe by a name that meant all-healing. They believed that a drink made from it could help animals have young, and could cure poisons. Some of this may be true, and some may not. Mistletoe is actually slightly poisonous. But the druids knew a great deal about plants and their uses. And mistletoe was clearly very special to them.

[long-pause]

{img:druids-procession-forest-oak-grove-winter-white-robes-quiet-ceremony}
One winter morning, Diviciacus takes his students to see a mistletoe ceremony, in a sacred grove of oaks, deep in the forest below the hill. It is a cold, clear day. The ground is white with frost. The bare branches of the oaks are dark against the pale blue sky. And high in one ancient oak, there is a round green ball of mistletoe, with white berries shining in the low winter sun.

[pause]

The druids of the region have gathered, in their white robes. The people of the nearby villages stand around the edge of the grove, quietly. The oldest druid climbs slowly up a ladder, into the tree. He reaches up with the golden sickle, and cuts the mistletoe. It falls, gently, into the white cloth held by the druids below. The people sigh, as if with relief. And then the druids begin to sing, a slow, old song, that rises up into the frosty air.

[pause]

{img:gold-sickle-and-mistletoe-sprig-on-white-cloth-oak-leaves-close}
Lugos stands with the other students, at the edge of the grove, watching. He holds his breath. The singing, the cold air, the white robes, the green mistletoe, the golden sickle shining in the sun, and the ancient oak, reaching up into the sky. It all seems very old, and very holy. He feels as if he were standing at the beginning of the world.

[long-pause]

Today, more than two thousand years later, people still hang mistletoe in their houses, at Christmas time. And there is a custom that, if two people stand under the mistletoe, they should kiss. Nobody knows exactly where this custom comes from. But perhaps, somewhere far back in time, it remembers the druids, and the sacred green plant on the oak, that brought good fortune and peace, in the middle of winter.

[long-pause]

{img:sacred-spring-forest-clear-water-pool-ribbons-tied-branches-offerings}
For the Celts, the whole world is full of the sacred. Gods and spirits live everywhere. In the forests and the groves. In the springs and the rivers. In the lakes and the marshes. On the tops of hills, and in caves. Every river has its goddess. Every spring has its spirit. And the people respect them, and bring them gifts, and ask for their help.

[long-pause]

The Celts do not usually build great temples, like the Greeks and the Romans. Instead, they worship in the open air, in sacred places in nature. The most important are the sacred groves. These are clearings in the forests, surrounded by old trees, especially oaks, where nobody may cut wood. The Roman writers described them as dark and mysterious, silent places, where even the birds did not sing. But to the Celts, they were holy and peaceful.

[pause]

Springs are especially sacred. Water that comes out of the earth, clear and pure, seems like a gift from the underworld. Many springs are believed to have healing powers. Sick people come to them, and bathe in the water, or drink it, and pray to the goddess of the spring to make them well. They leave offerings. Coins, brooches, little figures of clay or wood. And sometimes, carved models of the part of the body that is sick. A leg, an arm, an eye, a head.

[pause]

{img:carved-wooden-figures-offerings-healing-spring-source-seine-legs-arms}
At the source of the river Seine, in Burgundy, not far from Bibracte, there is a famous healing spring, sacred to the goddess Sequana, the goddess of the river. Archaeologists found hundreds of wooden figures there, carved and offered by pilgrims. Figures of people, of heads, of legs and arms and bodies. They had been preserved for two thousand years, in the wet mud by the spring. They are like a crowd of silent pilgrims, asking for help.

[pause]

Rivers and lakes are sacred too. The Celts throw precious things into them, as offerings to the gods. Swords, shields, spears, helmets, cauldrons, jewellery and coins. Archaeologists have found thousands of these objects, at the bottom of rivers and lakes all over Europe. In the river Thames, in London. In the lake at La Tène, in Switzerland. In lakes in Ireland and Wales. Some of the most beautiful Celtic objects in the world were found in this way.

[long-pause]

{img:celtic-warrior-throwing-sword-into-misty-lake-offering-dawn-reeds}
At Bibracte, there is the sacred spring, near the druid's house, where the students wash every morning. The water comes out of the hillside, cold and clear, into a stone basin. People come here every day, to pray, and to leave small offerings. A coin. A flower. A ribbon tied to the branches of the tree above the spring. Senna comes often, and leaves a little piece of bread for the goddess, and asks her to look after the horses.

[pause]

Some places are sacred because of the ancestors. On hills and in fields, there are old burial mounds, built by people who lived long before the Celts. The Celts believe that these are entrances to the Otherworld, the land of the gods and the dead. A beautiful land, beneath the hills or across the sea, where there is no sickness and no old age, where the feasting never ends, and where the apple trees always bear fruit.

[pause]

{img:ancient-burial-mound-misty-evening-grass-standing-stone-otherworld}
The great stone monuments, like Stonehenge in Britain, were already thousands of years old, in the time of the druids. The druids did not build them. They were built by much earlier peoples, long forgotten. But the druids may have known them, and wondered about them, just as we do. And perhaps they used some of them for their own ceremonies. Later people believed that the druids built Stonehenge. But that is just a legend.

[long-pause]

Lugos learns the names and the stories of all the sacred places in the land of the Aedui. Every spring, every grove, every hill and every river. And the old druid takes him to visit many of them. They walk through the forests together, for days, sleeping in villages or under the trees. And at each sacred place, the old druid stops, and prays, and tells the story of the place, and Lugos repeats it, until he knows it by heart.

[long-pause]

{img:celtic-god-lugus-bright-figure-spear-light-ancient-carving-style}
The Celts have many gods. Hundreds of them. Some are worshipped all over the Celtic world. Others belong to one tribe, or one place, one river or one spring. We know their names mostly from inscriptions, carved on stones in Roman times, and from the old stories of Ireland and Wales, written down much later. But the druids knew them all, and all their stories.

[long-pause]

One of the greatest gods is called Lugus. He is the god of light, of skill and of all the crafts. He is the master of every art. A smith, a poet, a harper, a warrior, a healer and a magician, all at once. Julius Caesar said that the Gauls honoured him most of all, and that they thought he had invented all the arts. Many places are named after him. The city of Lyon, in France, was once called Lugdunum, the fort of Lugus. Lugos, our young student, is named after him.

[pause]

Another important god is Taranis, the god of thunder. His symbol is a wheel, perhaps the wheel of the sun, or the wheel of his chariot, rolling across the sky and making the thunder. There is Belenos, the bright one, a god of the sun and of healing. And there is Cernunnos, a strange and ancient god, with the antlers of a stag on his head, who sits cross-legged, surrounded by animals. He is the god of nature, of animals and of plenty.

[pause]

{img:cernunnos-antlered-god-sitting-cross-legged-animals-around-forest-carving}
There are goddesses too, and they are very important. Epona is the goddess of horses. She is shown riding a horse, or sitting between two horses, with a basket of fruit or grain. She is loved by everyone who works with horses, and the Roman cavalry later adopted her too. There are mother goddesses, often shown as three women sitting together, with babies, fruit and bread. They bring fertility, health and plenty to the land and the people.

[pause]

And there are the goddesses of the rivers. Sequana, the goddess of the Seine. Matrona, the goddess of the Marne. Sabrina, the goddess of the Severn, in Britain. Every river has its goddess, who gives life to the land through which it flows. The Celts throw offerings into the rivers to honour them. And many rivers in Europe still have Celtic names today. The Thames. The Rhine. The Danube.

[long-pause]

{img:epona-horse-goddess-carving-sitting-between-horses-basket-fruit}
One of the most famous Celtic objects in the world shows many of these gods. It is a great silver cauldron, found in a bog in Denmark, at a place called Gundestrup. It was made around the time of our story. It is decorated, inside and out, with pictures of gods and goddesses, animals, warriors and ceremonies. There is Cernunnos, with his antlers, holding a torc and a snake. There are goddesses, wheels, elephants, griffins, bulls and dogs. It is like a picture book of Celtic beliefs.

[pause]

The Celts also believe in the Otherworld, the land of the gods and the dead. It is not a dark place, like the underworld of the Greeks. It is a beautiful land, of feasting and music, of eternal youth and endless summer. Sometimes, the stories say, living people can go there, by accident, through a mist, or a hole in a hill, or across the sea. And when they come back, they find that hundreds of years have passed, although they thought they were away for only a day.

[pause]

{img:otherworld-island-mist-apple-trees-feast-hall-music-golden-light-celtic}
In the evenings, by the fire, the grandmother tells Senna stories about the gods and the Otherworld. About the god of light, who came to the hall of the gods, and was not allowed in until he showed that he had every skill. About the horse goddess, whose white mare could never be caught, however fast you rode. About the young hero who followed a white deer into the forest, and found himself in the Otherworld, at a feast that lasted for a hundred years.

[long-pause]

Senna loves the story of Epona and her white mare best of all. She asks for it every night. And at night, before she goes to sleep, she holds her little clay figure of Epona, and whispers to it. She asks Epona to give her a horse of her own, one day. A white mare, as fast as the wind. And she falls asleep, and dreams of galloping across green fields, with her hair flying, and nobody able to catch her.

[long-pause]

{img:celtic-feast-great-hall-long-fire-roasting-boar-warriors-nobles-cups}
The Celts love to feast. Feasting is one of the most important parts of Celtic life. The nobles hold great feasts in their halls, for their warriors, their friends and their guests. They are a way of showing wealth and generosity, of making alliances, and of celebrating victories and festivals. A noble who gives great feasts is admired. A noble who is stingy is despised.

[long-pause]

The Greek writers described the Celtic feasts. The guests sit on the floor, on animal skins, around a great fire, with low tables in front of them. The most important man sits in the middle, and the others sit in a circle around him, according to their rank. Behind each warrior stands his shield bearer, and his spear bearer. And the servants bring in the food. Great quantities of roast meat, especially pork, from the cauldrons and the spits. Bread, and fish, with salt, vinegar and cumin.

[pause]

There is plenty to drink. Beer, made from wheat or barley, sometimes with honey. Mead. And for the rich, Roman wine, imported in great clay amphorae, which they drink without mixing it with water. The Greeks and Romans were shocked by this, because they always mixed their wine with water. They drink from a shared cup, passed around the circle, a little at a time, but very often.

[pause]

{img:bard-with-harp-singing-at-feast-firelight-celtic-hall-listeners}
There is a famous custom at Celtic feasts. The champion's portion. The best piece of meat, the thigh of the roast pig, is given to the bravest warrior at the feast. Sometimes, two warriors both claim it, and argue about who is braver. In the old Irish stories, these arguments sometimes lead to contests and quarrels. But usually, the host settles it, or the druids, or the bards, with a clever word. And everyone laughs, and goes on eating.

[pause]

And there is music. The bards sing at the feasts, playing their harps or lyres. They sing the praises of the host, and of his ancestors, and of his generosity. They sing the stories of the old heroes. They also sing funny songs, and make up verses about the guests, sometimes teasing them. The Celts love clever words and quick wit. A good bard can make the whole hall roar with laughter. Or he can make it weep.

[long-pause]

{img:noble-host-giving-gifts-guests-feast-gold-torc-brooch-generosity}
One evening in the summer, the vergobret, the chief magistrate of the Aedui, gives a great feast in his hall, at the top of the town, to celebrate the midsummer festival. Everyone important is invited. The nobles, the warriors, the druids and their students. Lugos goes with Diviciacus and the other students. It is the first great feast he has ever been to. And he is amazed by everything.

[pause]

The hall is enormous, with a long fire burning in the middle, and torches on the walls. Whole pigs are turning on spits over the fire. The smell of roasting meat fills the air. Hundreds of people are sitting around the walls, talking and laughing. Servants are carrying great jars of wine and beer. And at the far end, in the place of honour, sits the vergobret, a big, grey-bearded man, wearing a magnificent gold torc.

[pause]

{img:young-student-watching-feast-wide-eyes-druid-beside-him-hall-torches}
Diviciacus sits near the vergobret, in a place of honour. The students sit behind him. Lugos watches everything. The warriors, with their long moustaches and their bright cloaks. The women, with their braided hair and their jewellery. The bard, an old man with a harp, singing the history of the Aedui. And the gifts that the vergobret gives to his guests. Brooches, rings, cups and even horses. Being generous is a sign of greatness.

[long-pause]

Late in the night, the old bard sings a song about a hero who travelled to the Otherworld. Everyone becomes quiet. The fire crackles. The harp plays softly. And Lugos listens, and memorises every word. Afterwards, the old bard notices him, and asks him who he is. Lugos says he is a student of the druid Diviciacus. And then, shyly, he repeats the bard's song back to him, every word, without a mistake. The old bard laughs with delight, and gives him a little silver ring. He says that the boy has a bard's memory, as well as a druid's heart.

[long-pause]

{img:roman-wine-amphorae-unloaded-river-boat-gaulish-traders-riverbank}
Bibracte is not cut off from the world. It is part of a great network of trade, reaching across all of Europe. Along the rivers and the roads, merchants come and go, carrying goods from far away. And the most famous trade of all is the trade in Roman wine.

[long-pause]

The Gauls love wine. But they cannot grow much of it themselves, in the north. So it is brought from Italy, by Roman merchants. It is carried in amphorae, big clay jars with two handles and a pointed bottom, each holding about twenty-five litres. The amphorae are carried by ship from Italy to the port of Marseille, on the Mediterranean. Then up the river Rhône, on boats. Then up the river Saône. And then on carts, over the hills, to Bibracte.

[pause]

At Bibracte, archaeologists have found the remains of thousands and thousands of broken amphorae. Huge amounts. Some people have calculated that, over the century before the Roman conquest, millions of amphorae of wine were brought into Gaul. The Gauls paid for the wine with many things. Grain, cattle, leather, wool, metals, salt, and sadly, slaves. The Roman merchants grew rich. And so did the Gaulish nobles, who controlled the trade.

[pause]

{img:gaulish-market-coins-trade-goods-salt-iron-cloth-amphorae-busy}
But wine is not the only thing that comes from the south. There is fine pottery, with a shiny red surface, from Italy. Bronze jugs and cups, for serving wine. Olive oil. Dates and figs. Glass beads and bracelets. And there are new ideas. Some rich Gauls begin to live in the Roman way. They build houses with tiled roofs and painted walls. They drink wine from Roman cups. They learn to speak a little Latin. They even begin to write, in Greek letters.

[pause]

Other goods come from the north and the west. Tin from Britain, which is needed to make bronze. Amber from the shores of the Baltic Sea, a golden stone made from ancient tree resin, which is used for beads and jewellery. Salt from the salt mines in the mountains. Iron from the hills. Furs from the forests. And from Gaul itself, the merchants take fine woollen cloth, enamelled brooches, iron tools, and the famous hams and sausages of the Gauls, which are popular in Rome.

[long-pause]

{img:merchant-roman-gaulish-bargaining-fire-camp-roadside-goods-ponies}
The roads of Gaul are busy with traders. There are no paved Roman roads yet. Those will come later. But there are many good tracks, through the forests and over the hills, used for centuries. And there are the great rivers, the Loire, the Seine, the Rhône and the Saône, on which boats carry goods long distances. Along the rivers, there are trading towns, where merchants stop to rest, and to buy and sell.

[pause]

Cunobel, the blacksmith, trades too. He sells his tools and brooches to merchants who come to Bibracte. And he buys iron, bronze and tin from them, for his work. Sometimes, a Roman merchant comes to his forge. A man with a short Roman haircut, a tunic, and a little Latin, mixed with a few words of Gaulish. He buys enamel brooches, to sell in Italy, where people think they are exotic and beautiful. And he pays with Roman silver coins.

[pause]

{img:senna-girl-looking-at-roman-glass-beads-merchant-stall-wonder}
Senna loves the Roman merchants. They bring such strange and wonderful things. Glass beads, in colours she has never seen. Little bronze figures of Roman gods. Dried figs and dates, which taste like honey. One merchant, a kind old man from Italy, gives her a glass bead, deep blue, like the sky at evening. She threads it on a string, and wears it around her neck. She says that it is a little piece of the faraway world.

[long-pause]

But not everyone is happy about the Roman trade. Some of the Gauls, like Dumnorix, the brother of Diviciacus, think that the Romans are dangerous. That their wine and their gold are making the Gauls weak and greedy. That one day, the Romans will want more than trade. Diviciacus listens to these worries. He knows Rome. He thinks that friendship with Rome is the best way to protect his people. Time will tell who is right.

[long-pause]

{img:great-druid-assembly-forest-clearing-carnutes-hundreds-white-robes-tribes}
Once a year, at a fixed time, the druids of all Gaul meet together. Caesar wrote that they gather in a sacred place, in the land of a tribe called the Carnutes, which is believed to be the centre of all Gaul. The land of the Carnutes is near the river Loire, in the middle of France, around the city that is now called Chartres. And there, in a great forest, the druids hold their assembly.

[long-pause]

In the summer, Diviciacus travels to the assembly, with his students. It is a long journey, of many days, through the forests and across the rivers of Gaul. They travel on horseback, and on foot, with a few servants and pack horses. They sleep in villages, where they are welcomed with great respect, because they are druids. Lugos has never travelled so far. He sees new rivers, new hills, new towns, new tribes.

[pause]

At last, they reach the forest of the Carnutes. It is a vast, ancient forest, of oak and beech, so thick that the sunlight hardly reaches the ground. Paths wind through it, between huge old trees. Birds sing in the branches. Deer move silently among the trunks. And in the middle of the forest, there is a great clearing, open to the sky, surrounded by the most ancient oaks of all. This is the sacred place.

[pause]

{img:ancient-oak-forest-paths-sunbeams-druids-walking-deep-woods-summer}
Druids have come from all over Gaul. From the Arverni and the Helvetii, from the Belgae in the north and the Aquitani in the south-west, from the coasts of the Atlantic and the banks of the Rhine. Hundreds of them, in their white robes, with their students and their servants. They camp around the clearing, in tents and huts made of branches. And for several days, the forest is full of the sound of voices, in many Gaulish dialects.

[pause]

The assembly has several purposes. First, the druids perform sacred ceremonies, to honour the gods. Second, they meet together, to discuss their knowledge, to share news, and to choose new leaders if needed. And third, they act as judges, for disputes from all over Gaul. People who have quarrels, which cannot be settled at home, come to the assembly, and present their case to the druids. And the druids decide.

[long-pause]

{img:druids-judging-dispute-assembly-people-presenting-case-oak-circle}
Lugos watches the judgements, fascinated. A quarrel between two villages, about a field. A quarrel between two families, about a marriage. A quarrel between two tribes, about a stolen herd of cattle. Each side speaks, and the druids listen. Then they discuss among themselves, quietly. And then the eldest druid announces the decision. And both sides accept it, because it is the judgement of the druids, and nobody would dare to refuse.

[pause]

In the evenings, there are long conversations around the fires. The old druids talk about the stars, the gods, the soul and the future. They exchange verses and stories, and compare their knowledge. Some of them have studied in Britain, across the sea, where the most famous druid schools are. They tell stories about the island of Mona, in the far west of Britain, which is the most sacred place of all, full of groves and druids.

[pause]

{img:druids-talking-around-campfire-night-forest-stars-sharing-wisdom}
Lugos meets students from other tribes. Boys of his own age, from all over Gaul, who are also learning to be druids. They speak with different accents, and know different stories, and different gods. They sit together in the evenings, and recite their verses to each other, and compare them. Some verses are the same everywhere. Others are different. Lugos learns many new stories. And he makes friends, who he will meet again at the assembly, every year, for the rest of his life.

[long-pause]

On the last night of the assembly, all the druids gather in the great clearing, under the stars, for a final ceremony. They stand in a great circle, hundreds of them, in their white robes. A fire burns in the middle. The eldest druid speaks a prayer, for peace and good fortune for all the peoples of Gaul. And then all the druids sing together, a slow, ancient song, which rises up from the clearing, into the dark sky, and the stars. Lugos sings with them. And he feels, for the first time, that he is truly part of something old and great.

[long-pause]

{img:beltane-may-eve-bonfires-hilltop-cattle-driven-between-fires-celtic-night}
The Celts divide the year into two halves. The dark half, which begins at the start of winter, and the light half, which begins at the start of summer. And at the beginning of each half, there is a great festival. In the old Irish stories, the summer festival is called Beltane. It is held at the beginning of May, when the cattle are taken out to the summer pastures, and the world turns green and full of life.

[long-pause]

The name Beltane probably means bright fire. On the eve of the festival, all the fires in the houses are put out. And then, on the hilltops, the druids light two great new fires. The cattle of the tribe are driven between the two fires, to protect them from sickness, and to bless them for the summer. The people walk between the fires too, and jump over the smaller ones, for luck. And then they carry burning branches home, to light their own fires again, from the sacred flame.

[pause]

We know most about Beltane from Ireland, where the old stories were written down by monks, many centuries later. The Gauls probably had a similar festival, at the same time of year, perhaps with a different name. Everywhere, the beginning of summer was a time of joy and celebration. The long, dark winter was over. The grass was growing. The cows would give milk again. And the young people would go out into the fields and the woods, to dance and to gather flowers.

[pause]

{img:young-people-gathering-hawthorn-blossom-dawn-may-morning-meadow-celtic}
At Bibracte, on the eve of the summer festival, everyone climbs to the highest point of the hill, at sunset. The druids are there, in their white robes, with the wood for the two great fires, piled high. As the sun goes down, and the stars come out, the eldest druid lights the fires, with a flame made by rubbing two pieces of oak wood together. The flames leap up into the darkness, and everyone cheers.

[pause]

Then the cattle are brought up the hill, by the herders, lowing and stamping. One by one, the herds are driven between the two fires, through the smoke, while the druids sing and pray. Senna helps the herders with the horses, which are also led between the fires. She leads a beautiful white mare, which belongs to the vergobret. The mare is nervous, but Senna talks to her softly, and she walks calmly between the flames.

[long-pause]

{img:girl-leading-white-mare-between-two-bonfires-night-crowd-watching}
After the cattle, the people walk between the fires too. Families, holding hands. Old people, leaning on sticks. Children, running and laughing. Cunobel carries the grandmother on his back, because she is too old to walk up the hill. Elva holds Senna's hand. And Lugos walks with the druids, for the first time, as one of them, singing the old songs, which he now knows by heart.

[pause]

Then there is music, and dancing, around the fires, all night long. The young men and women dance in great circles, holding hands, faster and faster. The musicians play pipes, drums and harps. Food is roasted over the fires, and beer and mead are shared. The night is short, at this time of year. And at dawn, the young people go down into the woods and the fields, to gather the first flowers of summer, hawthorn blossom, bluebells and primroses, and bring them back to decorate the houses.

[pause]

{img:celtic-family-carrying-torch-home-from-beltane-fire-dawn-path}
Elva carries a burning branch home from the sacred fire, very carefully, cupping the flame with her hand. At home, she uses it to light the fire in the hearth again. Now the house has a new fire, blessed by the druids, which will burn all summer. She puts a branch of hawthorn blossom above the door, for luck. And the whole family goes to bed, very tired and very happy, as the sun rises over the green forests.

[long-pause]

The vergobret was watching, at the fire, as Senna led his white mare between the flames. He noticed how calm the horse was with her, and how gently she spoke to it. A few days later, a servant comes to the blacksmith's house. The vergobret has a young foal, a white filly, born that spring, from the white mare. And he wants Senna to help train her. Senna cannot believe it. She runs around the house, shouting, until her grandmother tells her to calm down. Epona, it seems, has heard her prayers.

[long-pause]

{img:celtic-noblewoman-dignified-gold-torc-long-cloak-council-speaking}
In the ancient world, women often had few rights. In Greece, women were mostly kept at home, and could not own property or take part in public life. In Rome, they had a little more freedom, but were still under the control of their fathers and husbands. Among the Celts, things were different. Celtic women had more freedom and more power than most women in the ancient world.

[long-pause]

Celtic women could own property, and keep it when they married. Caesar wrote that, when a Gaulish couple married, the wife brought a dowry, and the husband added an equal amount from his own property. The money was kept together, and the profits were saved. And whichever of them lived longer received both shares. This was quite unusual for the ancient world. It meant that husband and wife were almost partners.

[pause]

Celtic women could be very powerful. Some were queens and leaders. In Britain, about a hundred years after our story, there were two famous queens. Cartimandua, who ruled the Brigantes, a large tribe in the north of England, for many years. And Boudica, the queen of the Iceni, who led a great rebellion against the Romans. The Roman writers were amazed that the Britons would follow a woman into battle.

[pause]

{img:celtic-women-weaving-spinning-talking-courtyard-sunlight-children-playing}
The Greek and Roman writers described the Celtic women as tall, strong and beautiful, with long, braided hair. They said that a Gaulish woman was as strong as her husband, and that when a Gaul quarrelled with someone, his wife would often come to help him, and was even more formidable. Perhaps they exaggerated. But it shows that Celtic women were not shy or timid.

[pause]

Some women were also druids, or seers. The Roman writers mention druidesses, in later times, who could foretell the future. And in the old Irish stories, there are many wise women, prophetesses and poets. Women were also healers, with great knowledge of herbs and remedies, like Elva's mother, the grandmother. Everyone in the street comes to her when they are ill.

[long-pause]

{img:grandmother-healer-herbs-drying-teaching-granddaughter-remedies-hut}
In everyday life, the women of the Celts work very hard. They run the house, cook, brew beer, look after the children, and care for the animals. They spin and weave all the cloth for the family. They work in the fields at harvest time. Many of them also run businesses, like Elva, who sells her beautiful cloth at the market, and keeps the money herself. And they take part in the decisions of the family.

[pause]

Elva is a respected woman in the street of the craftsmen. Her cloth is the finest in Bibracte. Nobles come to her to order cloth for their cloaks, in special colours and patterns. She is proud of her work. And she is teaching Senna to weave, although Senna would rather be with the horses. Elva laughs, and says that even a horse-trainer needs a good cloak, and that it is better to make it yourself.

[pause]

{img:mother-and-daughter-at-upright-loom-bright-checked-cloth-weaving-together}
The grandmother teaches Senna about herbs. Which plants are good for coughs, which for wounds, which for sleep, which for fever. She takes her into the meadows and the forest, in the early morning, to gather them, with the dew still on them. She tells her that a woman who knows the herbs will always be welcome everywhere. And that knowledge is a treasure that nobody can steal.

[long-pause]

Senna listens to her grandmother, and learns. But her heart is with the horses. Every day, she goes to the vergobret's stables, to work with the little white filly. She brushes her, talks to her, leads her around the field. The filly follows her everywhere, like a puppy. Senna has named her Gwenn, which means white, or blessed. And everyone in Bibracte smiles when they see them together, the little girl and the little white horse.

[long-pause]

{img:samhain-autumn-night-bonfire-hilltop-mist-forest-turning-leaves-celtic}
At the end of the year, there is another great festival. In the old Irish stories, it is called Samhain. It is held at the beginning of November, when the summer ends and the winter begins. It is the start of the dark half of the year. And for the Celts, it is also the beginning of the new year. Because, just as the day begins at night, the year begins with the dark.

[long-pause]

Samhain is the time when the cattle are brought back from the summer pastures, into the shelter of the farms. The harvest is finished, and stored away. Some animals are killed, because there is not enough food to keep them all through the winter. Their meat is salted and smoked, to last until spring. And there is a great feast, with plenty of meat, bread and beer, before the hard months of winter.

[pause]

But Samhain is also a mysterious time. The Celts believe that, on this night, the border between this world and the Otherworld becomes very thin. The spirits of the dead can come back, to visit their families. And people from this world can sometimes slip into the Otherworld, by accident. So it is a night of wonder, and a little fear. People stay close to the fire, with their families, and tell stories of the Otherworld, and remember those who have passed away.

[pause]

{img:family-hearth-samhain-night-empty-place-set-for-ancestors-candles}
In the house of the blacksmith, on Samhain eve, the family sits around the hearth. The grandmother has set an extra place, with a bowl of food and a cup of beer, for the ancestors, in case they come to visit. She tells the children the names of all their ancestors, back for many generations. Their great-grandfather, the smith. Their great-great-grandmother, the healer. And many more. Lugos already knows them all. Senna is still learning.

[pause]

Outside, on the hilltop, the druids have lit a great fire, as they did at Beltane. All the fires in the town are put out, and then lit again from the sacred fire. The new fire, for the new year. The people come up the hill in the darkness, carrying lanterns, to fetch the flame. And the druids pray for the souls of the dead, and for a gentle winter, and for the return of the sun in the spring.

[long-pause]

{img:children-carrying-carved-turnip-lanterns-dark-path-autumn-night-glow}
Many of the customs of Samhain have lasted for more than two thousand years. In Ireland and Scotland, people continued to celebrate it, long after the druids were gone. They lit bonfires. They told stories of ghosts and fairies. They put food out for the spirits. And children dressed up in disguises, and went from house to house. In the Christian era, the festival became All Saints' Day, and the evening before became All Hallows' Eve. Today, we call it Halloween.

[pause]

The children of Ireland and Scotland carved lanterns out of turnips, with faces, to scare away the spirits. When Irish and Scottish people went to America, they found that pumpkins were bigger and easier to carve. And so the Halloween pumpkin lantern was born. But its roots go back, through the centuries, to the fires of Samhain, on the hilltops of the Celts.

[pause]

{img:quiet-autumn-night-oppidum-houses-glowing-hearths-mist-stars}
On the night of Samhain, Lugos goes up the hill with Diviciacus, to the great fire. The old druid is very quiet. He stands by the fire for a long time, looking into the flames. Then he tells Lugos that he is getting old, and that this may be one of his last Samhains. Lugos does not want to hear it. But the old druid smiles, and says that there is nothing to fear. The soul does not die. It goes on, to another life. That is what the druids teach.

[long-pause]

Then the old druid asks Lugos to recite the verses of the dead. A long, slow poem, about the journey of the soul to the Otherworld, across the sea, to the islands in the west, where the sun sets. Lugos recites it, all of it, by the light of the fire, in the cold November night. And when he has finished, the old druid puts his hand on his shoulder, and says that he is proud of him. That one day, Lugos will be a great druid. And that he will teach these verses to his own students, long after the old man is gone.

[long-pause]

{img:roman-legions-marching-into-gaul-58bc-distant-dust-hills-forest-distance}
Two years after our story, in the year 58 before Christ, the Romans came. A Roman general called Julius Caesar arrived in Gaul, with his legions. At first, he came to help the Aedui, against their enemies, at the request of Diviciacus. But he did not go home again. For eight years, he fought in Gaul, against tribe after tribe. And in the end, all of Gaul became part of the Roman Empire.

[long-pause]

We will not follow those wars. They were long and hard, and many people suffered. It is enough to say that in the year 52 before Christ, the tribes of Gaul united against Caesar, under a young nobleman of the Arverni, called Vercingetorix. And at a great meeting at Bibracte, the tribes chose him as their leader. Even the Aedui joined him, at last. But after a long siege, at a place called Alesia, Vercingetorix surrendered to Caesar. And Gaul was conquered.

[pause]

Diviciacus did not see the end. He passed away a few years after Caesar arrived, peacefully, at Bibracte. He had tried, all his life, to protect his people, through friendship with Rome. Whether he was right or wrong, historians still argue. But his students remembered him with great love. And Lugos sang the verses of the dead for him, by the great fire on the hill, as the old man had asked.

[pause]

{img:older-druid-lugos-teaching-young-students-oak-grove-owl-brooch}
Lugos became a druid, after many years of study. He was a wise and gentle druid, much loved by the people of Bibracte. He judged their disputes, healed their sick, watched the stars and kept the calendar. He wore his father's owl brooch on his cloak, all his life. And he taught many students, as his teacher had taught him, sitting under the old oak tree in the courtyard, repeating the verses, again and again, until they knew them by heart.

[pause]

But the world was changing. After the Roman conquest, the druids slowly lost their power. The Romans did not like the druids. They saw them as dangerous, because they were respected by the people, and could unite the tribes. Roman emperors made laws against the druids. And slowly, the druids disappeared, or became ordinary priests of the new Roman temples, or hid in the countryside, keeping the old ways in secret.

[long-pause]

{img:bibracte-abandoned-forest-growing-over-ruins-new-roman-town-autun-valley}
Bibracte itself was abandoned, a few decades after the conquest. The Romans founded a new town in the valley below, called Augustodunum, today the town of Autun, with straight streets, stone houses, a theatre and temples, in the Roman style. The people moved down from the hill, to the new town. And slowly, the old town on the hill was swallowed up by the forest, and forgotten.

[pause]

Senna grew up to be a famous horse trainer, the best in the land of the Aedui. Her white mare, Gwenn, lived to a great age. Senna married a young warrior, who later became a cavalryman in the Roman army, like many young Gauls. And their children grew up speaking both Gaulish and Latin, and worshipping both the old gods and the new. They still honoured Epona, the horse goddess. And so did the Roman soldiers, who carried her worship all over the empire.

[pause]

{img:epona-shrine-roman-stable-horses-small-statue-flowers-later-times}
Elva and Cunobel lived out their lives in Bibracte, and then in the new town of Augustodunum, where Cunobel opened a new forge, and Elva went on weaving her beautiful checked cloth, which even the Romans admired. The grandmother passed away peacefully, in her bed, on a winter night, and Lugos sang the old verses for her too. And her place by the fire was set, every Samhain, for many years after.

[long-pause]

The world of the Celts did not disappear completely. It mixed with the Roman world, and changed, and lived on in new forms. The language, the gods, the customs, the festivals, the art. Some of it lasted in Gaul for centuries. And much more of it lasted in the places where the Romans never came, or came only briefly. In Ireland, in Scotland, in Wales and in Brittany. Where the old stories were still told, by the fire, for two thousand years.

[long-pause]

{img:island-of-mona-anglesey-sacred-groves-coast-mist-druids-last-refuge}
After the conquest of Gaul, the last great stronghold of the druids was in Britain. And especially on the island of Mona, today called Anglesey, off the coast of north Wales. It was said to be the most sacred place of the druids, full of groves and altars. In the year 60 after Christ, more than a hundred years after our story, a Roman army crossed the narrow strait to the island. We will not follow what happened there. The sacred groves were cut down. And the great age of the druids came to an end.

[long-pause]

But the druids did not vanish completely from memory. In Ireland, which the Romans never conquered, the druids lived on for several more centuries. In the old Irish stories, there are many druids. Wise men, advisers to kings, poets and magicians. And when Christianity came to Ireland, in the fifth century, with Saint Patrick, the stories say that the saint met the druids of the king, and won them over. Slowly, the druids gave way to the Christian monks.

[pause]

Interestingly, the monks of Ireland kept many of the old traditions alive. They wrote down the old stories, which the druids and the bards had remembered for centuries. The tales of the heroes. The stories of the gods, now told as ancient kings and queens. The laws. The lists of kings. Without the monks, these stories would have been lost forever. And so, in a way, the memory of the druids passed into the books of the monks.

[pause]

{img:irish-monk-writing-old-tales-manuscript-candle-celtic-knotwork-margin}
The Celtic languages also lived on. Today, there are six Celtic languages that are still spoken. Irish, Scottish Gaelic and Manx, in Ireland, Scotland and the Isle of Man. And Welsh, Cornish and Breton, in Wales, Cornwall and Brittany. They are the descendants of the languages that the Celts spoke in the time of our story. Gaulish itself disappeared, slowly, replaced by Latin, which later became French. But many French words, and many place names, still come from Gaulish.

[pause]

In the seventeen and eighteen hundreds, people in Britain and France became fascinated by the druids again. They read Caesar and Pliny. They imagined the druids as wise old men with long white beards, in white robes, worshipping in stone circles. They wrongly believed that the druids had built Stonehenge. Poets wrote about them. Artists painted them. And people formed societies of modern druids, which still exist today.

[long-pause]

{img:modern-druids-white-robes-stone-circle-summer-solstice-sunrise-later}
In Wales, there is a great festival of poetry and music every year, called the Eisteddfod. It was revived in the eighteen hundreds. In it, poets and musicians compete, and the winners are honoured in a ceremony of the Gorsedd, a society of bards, who wear robes of white, green and blue, and stand in a circle of stones. It is not an ancient druid ceremony. But it is inspired by the old Celtic love of poetry, music and memory.

[pause]

The French have also always remembered the Gauls. They call them their ancestors. Vercingetorix is a national hero in France, with great statues in his honour, including one at Alesia. And there is a famous French comic book, about a little Gaulish village that resists the Romans, with a wise old druid who brews a magic potion. Millions of children all over the world have read it, and laughed at it, and learned a little about the Gauls.

[pause]

{img:vercingetorix-statue-alesia-hilltop-bronze-warrior-sky-later}
Archaeologists have also learned a great deal about the Celts, in the last two hundred years. They have dug at many Celtic sites, all over Europe. They have found the towns, the houses, the workshops, the sacred places, the graves and the treasures of the Celts. And they have shown that the Celts were not wild barbarians, as the Romans said, but a rich and skilled civilisation, with its own art, its own knowledge and its own beauty.

[long-pause]

So, in many ways, the world of the druids is still with us. In the Celtic languages, still spoken by millions of people. In the old stories of Ireland and Wales. In the mistletoe at Christmas, and the pumpkins at Halloween. In the names of rivers and cities. In the swirling patterns of Celtic art, which people still love. And in the old trees, the springs and the stars, which the druids watched and honoured, and which are still there, just as they were.

[long-pause]

{img:mont-beuvray-forest-today-excavated-bibracte-walls-visitors-paths}
Today, the hill of Mont Beuvray, where Bibracte stood, is covered with a beautiful forest of beech trees. For almost two thousand years, it was forgotten. Then, in the eighteen hundreds, archaeologists began to dig there. And since the nineteen eighties, there have been large excavations, every summer, by archaeologists and students from all over Europe. Slowly, the old town is coming back to light.

[long-pause]

Visitors can walk through the forest, on the hill, and see the remains. Parts of the great Gallic wall, with its timber frame, have been rebuilt, so that people can see how it looked. There is the great gate. There are the foundations of the houses, the workshops of the craftsmen, the streets and the great open square. There is the sacred spring, which still flows, after two thousand years. And there is a great stone basin, carved from a single block, which may have been used for ceremonies.

[pause]

At the foot of the hill, there is a museum, called the Museum of Celtic Civilisation. It shows the life of the Celts, with objects found at Bibracte and other places. The enamelled brooches. The amphorae of wine. The tools of the smiths. The coins. The jewellery. And models and reconstructions, showing how the town looked, with its houses, its workshops and its people.

[pause]

{img:museum-celtic-civilisation-display-brooches-coins-amphorae-glass-cases-today}
In other museums, all over Europe, there are wonderful Celtic treasures. In the National Museum in Copenhagen, in Denmark, there is the great Gundestrup cauldron, with its gods and its animals. In the British Museum, in London, there are beautiful shields, helmets and torcs, found in rivers and fields. In the National Museum of Ireland, in Dublin, there are golden torcs and collars, of extraordinary beauty. And in France, in the museum at Saint-Germain-en-Laye, near Paris, there are thousands of Celtic objects from Gaul.

[pause]

In the Lyon museum, there is the Coligny calendar, the great bronze plate with the druids' calendar of five years, sixty-two months, and lucky and unlucky days. Visitors stand in front of it, and look at the little holes, where the pegs once marked the days. It is one of the very few writings in the Gaulish language that survive. And it is the best evidence of the druids' knowledge of the sky.

[long-pause]

{img:ancient-oak-forest-today-sunbeams-moss-quiet-sacred-grove-feeling}
And there are the old forests. In France, Britain and Ireland, there are still ancient woods of oak and beech, where the trees are hundreds of years old. Walking through them, on a quiet morning, with the sunlight falling through the leaves, and the birds singing, it is easy to understand why the Celts thought that the forests were sacred. They feel like places full of life and spirit. Like great green temples, built by nature.

[pause]

At the source of the river Seine, in Burgundy, there is still a little spring, in a quiet valley, among the trees. A statue of the goddess Sequana stands there, in a little grotto, made in the eighteen hundreds. And people still sometimes leave coins in the water, for luck. Just as the Celtic pilgrims did, two thousand years ago, when they came to ask the goddess for healing.

[pause]

{img:source-of-seine-spring-grotto-statue-goddess-coins-in-water-quiet-today}
In Ireland, in Scotland, in Wales and in Brittany, the old Celtic festivals are still remembered. At Beltane, on the first of May, some people still light bonfires on the hills. At Samhain, at Halloween, children dress up and carry lanterns. And at Christmas, mistletoe hangs over the doors. Most people do not know where these customs come from. But they are there, quietly, carrying the past into the present.

[long-pause]

And in the night sky, the same stars still shine, that the druids watched from the hill of Bibracte. The same moon still grows and shrinks, marking the months of the Celtic calendar. The same shooting stars still flash across the sky. If you go out on a clear night, far from the city lights, and lie in the grass, and look up, you are seeing exactly what Lugos saw, lying on the hill, more than two thousand years ago.

The mist is beginning to rise in the valleys. And on the high hill of Bibracte, the smoke is rising from the thatched roofs, straight up into the still evening air.

The moon rises over the eastern forests, a thin silver crescent, the beginning of a new month.
