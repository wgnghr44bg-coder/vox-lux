In 1933, the middle of America was a sea of cornfields and small farm towns.

[pause]

Here we're in the state of Iowa, in the great flat heart of the country, where the cornfields stretch away to the horizon, in every direction, under a huge, wide sky. It was a hard time.

Let's walk down the gravel road, between the fields, to the white farmhouse with the red barn. And let's meet the Millers, as they begin another day, in the hard, quiet years of the Depression.

To understand the Great Depression, we must go back a few years.

[pause]

To the 1920s. You might think the trouble began on a farm. It didn't. For many Americans, the 1920s were a wonderful time. People called them the Roaring Twenties. There were jobs, and money, and new things to buy. Cars, and radios, and refrigerators, and washing machines. There was jazz music, and dancing, and movies. Everything seemed to be getting better and better. Many people began to buy shares in companies, on the stock market, in New York. The prices of the shares kept going up, and up, and up. People thought they'd go up for ever. Ordinary people, shopkeepers, and teachers, and even farmers, borrowed money, to buy shares. They thought they'd get rich, quickly and easily. Some of them did, for a while.

[long-pause]

{img:06-newspaper-headline-1929-crash-man-reading-on-street-corner-shocked}

Then, in October 1929, the bubble burst.

[pause]

The prices of the shares began to fall. You'd have seen worried faces on every street corner. At first slowly, then faster and faster. People panicked, and tried to sell their shares, all at once. On one terrible day, which became known as Black Tuesday, the stock market crashed. Billions of dollars disappeared, in a few hours. Many people lost everything they had. The crash was only the beginning. Over the next few years, things got worse, and worse. People had less money, so they bought less. So the factories made less, and needed fewer workers. So more people lost their jobs, and had even less money. It was like a wheel, turning downward, faster and faster. Nobody seemed to know how to stop it.

[long-pause]

{img:07-people-lined-up-outside-closed-bank-1931-small-town-anxious-crowd}

Then the banks began to fail.

[pause]

When people were afraid, they rushed to the banks, to take out their savings. You'd walk to your bank and find the doors locked. But the banks had lent the money to other people, and didn't have it in the vaults. So the banks ran out of money, and closed their doors. And the people lost their savings. Thousands of banks failed, all over America. In the small towns, it was a disaster. The savings of a whole lifetime could disappear in a day. On the farms, the trouble had begun even earlier. After the Great War, in the early 1920s, the prices for crops had already fallen. Many farmers had borrowed money, to buy more land, and machines, when prices were high. Now, they couldn't pay it back. And when the Depression came, prices fell even further. By 1932, many farmers were in despair. The president, Herbert Hoover, tried to help. But many people felt that he didn't do enough, or didn't understand. Shanty towns of homeless people, built from boxes, and scrap metal, and old cars, appeared on the edges of the cities. People called them Hoovervilles. It wasn't quite fair to Hoover. But it showed how people felt.

[long-pause]

{img:08-shanty-town-hooverville-tin-shacks-river-bank-smoke-cooking-fires}

In November 1932, there was an election.

[pause]

The American people chose a new president. You'd see posters nailed to fence posts all along the road. His name was Franklin Delano Roosevelt. He was the governor of New York. He came from a rich family, and as a young man he had been struck by polio, which left him unable to walk without help. But he was cheerful, and confident, and he promised the American people a New Deal. People didn't know exactly what that meant. But they hoped it meant change. In those days, a new president didn't start work until March. So for four long months, the country waited. And during that winter, the worst winter of the Depression, things got worse than ever. More banks failed. More people lost their jobs. And in the farmhouse near Harlan, Iowa, the Millers waited too, and hoped, and prayed, for better times.

[long-pause]

{img:09-iowa-farmer-1933-overalls-straw-hat-leaning-on-fence-cornfield-thoughtful}

Now that you know what kind of year this is, let's go and meet the people who live in that white farmhouse. Shall we knock on the door?

[pause]

Walt Miller is forty-two years old.

[pause]

He was born on this farm, in the white farmhouse, in 1891. If you shook his hand, you'd feel how rough it was from years of work. His father came here from Ohio, as a young man, and bought the land, a hundred and sixty acres, when it was still prairie grass. Walt has worked on it all his life. He knows every field, every fence post, every corner of it. He is tall, and thin, and quiet, with a face burned brown by the sun, and big, rough hands. Walt grows corn, and oats, and hay, and he raises hogs, and a few cattle. He has four horses, for pulling the plough, and the wagon. He bought a tractor in 1928, on credit, when times were good. Now, he can hardly afford the fuel for it. So the tractor sits in the shed, most of the time, and the horses do the work again, as they always did. Walt doesn't mind. He likes the horses better anyway.

[long-pause]

{img:10-farm-wife-1933-flour-sack-apron-feeding-chickens-yard-morning-sun}

Ruth Miller is thirty-nine.

[pause]

She grew up on a farm too, a few miles away. You'd find her in the kitchen, almost always, with her sleeves rolled up. She met Walt at a church social, when she was eighteen, and they were married a year later. She is a strong, practical, cheerful woman, who never stops working. She cooks, and cleans, and washes, and sews, and mends. She keeps a big vegetable garden, and a flock of chickens, and sells the eggs in town, for a little money. She can make something out of nothing. June is twelve. She is a serious, bookish girl, with brown braids, and freckles. She loves to read, more than anything. She has read every book in the little school library, twice. She wants to be a teacher, or a writer, or perhaps a newspaper reporter, in a big city. She helps her mother in the house, and the garden. I've always liked her. But her head is always somewhere else, in a story.

[long-pause]

{img:11-boy-nine-overalls-barefoot-with-farm-dog-collie-barn-door-summer}

Eddie is nine.

[pause]

He is the opposite of his sister. You'd hear him long before you saw him. He hates sitting still. He loves being outside, with the animals, and the machines, and his dog, Rusty, a big, friendly collie. He helps his father with the chores, and follows him everywhere. He wants to be a farmer, like his father, and his grandfather. Or perhaps a baseball player. Or perhaps a pilot. He changes his mind every week. And there's Grandma Hattie. She is seventy-one. She came to Iowa with her husband, in a covered wagon, in 1880, when she was eighteen. She has seen many hard times. Droughts, and grasshoppers, and blizzards, and the hard years of the 1890s, when prices fell, and many farmers lost their land. She says that the country has been through hard times before, and it will get through this one too. She is the calm centre of the family. The Millers are luckier than many. They own their farm, and they still have it. They have a small mortgage, which Walt took out to buy the tractor. And they are behind with the payments. But the bank has been patient, so far. They grow most of their own food, so they never go hungry. They have a roof over their heads, and a warm stove, and each other.

[long-pause]

{img:12-farmhouse-kitchen-1933-cast-iron-stove-oilcloth-table-calendar-wall}

But there's almost no money.

[pause]

In a good year, the farm used to earn a thousand dollars or more. You'd count the coins in the sugar tin, and count them again. Last year, it earned less than two hundred. There's nothing left, after the mortgage payments, and the taxes. No money for new clothes, or new shoes, or a new coat for the winter. No money for the dentist, or the doctor. No money for anything that isn't absolutely necessary. So the Millers make do. They wear their clothes until they fall apart, and then mend them, and wear them again. They save everything. String, and paper, and buttons, and old nails, and bits of wood. Nothing is ever thrown away. As people said in those days, use it up, wear it out, make it do, or do without. It isn't easy. But they aren't alone. Everyone they know is in the same boat.

[long-pause]

{img:13-farm-dawn-1933-farmer-with-lantern-walking-to-barn-frost-windmill}

On the farm, the day begins before dawn.

[pause]

At five o'clock, the alarm clock rings, in the cold bedroom. You'd wake to a cold, dark room and the sound of the rooster. Walt gets up first, and gets dressed, in the dark. Overalls, and a flannel shirt, and heavy boots. He goes down to the kitchen, and lights the kerosene lamp. There's no electricity on the farm. Only about one farm in ten in America has electricity, in 1933. The rest use kerosene lamps, and wood stoves, and hand pumps. Walt lights the kitchen stove, with corncobs and kindling. Then he takes a lantern, and goes out to the barn, to do the morning chores. Eddie follows him, rubbing his eyes, with Rusty at his heels. The air is cold, and fresh, and smells of hay, and animals, and wet grass. The sky in the east is just beginning to grow pale. The windmill creaks, in the breeze.

[long-pause]

{img:14-father-and-son-milking-cows-by-lantern-light-barn-stalls-steam-breath}

In the barn, the animals are waiting.

[pause]

The horses stamp, and snort, in their stalls, wanting their breakfast. You'd smell hay and warm animals as soon as you opened the door. The cows low, wanting to be milked. Walt and Eddie give the horses their oats and hay. Then they sit on little stools, and milk the cows, by hand, into tin buckets. The milk squirts into the buckets, with a soft, rhythmic sound. The barn cats gather round, hoping for a squirt of warm milk. Eddie always gives them one. Then they feed the hogs. The hogs are in a pen, behind the barn, and they squeal and push, when they see the buckets of feed. Corn, and skim milk, and scraps from the kitchen. The hogs are the most important animals on the farm. They turn the corn into pork, which is worth more than the corn. Or it was, before the Depression. Now, even pork prices have fallen.

[long-pause]

{img:15-farm-kitchen-breakfast-1933-pancakes-eggs-bacon-family-at-table-lamp}

Meanwhile, in the kitchen, Ruth is making breakfast.

[pause]

The stove is hot now, and the coffee is boiling. You'd smell bacon and coffee drifting up the stairs. She fries eggs, from her own chickens, and bacon, from their own hogs. She makes pancakes, or biscuits, or fried potatoes. She heats the milk, fresh from the cows. June sets the table, and Grandma Hattie slices the bread, which Ruth baked yesterday. When Walt and Eddie come in from the barn, breakfast is ready. They wash their hands, at the sink, with water from the pump. And the family sits down together. Before they eat, Walt says grace. He thanks God for the food, and for the family, and for the farm. And he asks for help, in the hard times. Then they eat. A big breakfast, because there's a long day of work ahead. Nobody on the farm goes hungry, even in the Depression. There's always enough to eat. It's money they lack, not food. After breakfast, the day's work begins. There's always work on a farm. In the spring, ploughing, and planting. In the summer, cultivating the corn, and cutting the hay, and the oats. In the autumn, the harvest. In the winter, mending fences, and tools, and harness, and cutting wood. And every day, all year round, feeding and milking and caring for the animals, morning and evening.

[long-pause]

{img:16-farmer-ploughing-field-team-of-horses-spring-1933-black-soil-gulls}

Ruth's work never ends either.

[pause]

Washing, on Monday, with a washboard, and a big tub, and water heated on the stove. Ironing, on Tuesday, with heavy irons, heated on the stove too. Baking, and cleaning, and churning butter, and making soap, from fat and lye. And in the summer, the garden, and canning the vegetables, for the winter. Grandma Hattie helps as much as she can, and so does June, before and after school. In the evening, after supper, and the evening chores, the family sits in the kitchen, or on the porch, if it's warm. They are tired, but content. The day's work is done. The animals are fed, and settled for the night. The sun goes down, over the cornfields, in a blaze of orange and red. Could you have kept up with her for a single day? And the first stars come out, over the wide, quiet land.

[long-pause]

{img:17-harlan-iowa-1933-main-street-brick-shops-model-a-fords-courthouse-square}

The chores are done, and it's Saturday. Climb into the back of the old car with the children, and let's drive into town.

[pause]

On Saturday afternoon, the Millers go to town.

[pause]

They drive in their old car, a Model T Ford, which Walt bought second-hand, years ago. You'd squeeze into the back seat, between the egg baskets. It's black, and rattly, and slow, and it smells of oil. But it still runs, most of the time. They drive along the gravel road, between the fields, with a cloud of dust behind them, for about four miles, into Harlan. Harlan is a small town, of about three thousand people. It has a courthouse, in the middle of a square, with a big clock tower, and green lawns, and benches, under tall elm trees. Around the square, there are shops, and offices, in brick buildings, two or three storeys high. A grocery store, a hardware store, a drugstore, a dry goods store, a bank, a barber, a cafe, and a movie theatre. And churches, on the side streets, with tall white steeples.

[long-pause]

{img:18-general-store-interior-1933-shelves-cans-barrels-counter-farmers-wives-trading}

On Saturday, the town is busy.

[pause]

All the farm families come in, to buy what they need, and to sell what they can, and to see their friends. You'd see wagons and old cars parked side by side along the street. Ruth takes her eggs, and her butter, to the grocery store, and trades them for flour, and sugar, and coffee, and salt. The grocer, Mr Anderson, gives her a little credit, too, when she needs it. He gives credit to many families. He doesn't know if they will ever be able to pay him back. The men stand around on the corners, in their overalls and their hats, and talk. About the weather, and the crops, and the prices, and the bank, and the new president, and the farmers who have lost their farms. The talk is often gloomy. But there are jokes too, and laughter. Walt says that he isn't worried about the Depression. You'd stop to say hello to someone every few steps. He says that the Millers have been depressed for years, and nobody noticed.

[long-pause]

{img:19-children-outside-drugstore-soda-fountain-window-1933-looking-in-nickels}

The children love Saturdays in town.

[pause]

June goes to the little library, above the courthouse, and borrows a new book. You'd feel a penny burning a hole in your pocket. Eddie meets his friends, on the square, and they play marbles, and talk about baseball. If they are lucky, their mother gives them each a nickel. Then they go to the drugstore, which has a soda fountain, and buy an ice cream cone, or a bottle of soda pop. It's the best moment of the week. But there are signs of the hard times, everywhere. Some of the shops on the square are empty, with dusty windows, and signs saying closed, or for rent. The men who used to work in them are standing on the corners, with nothing to do. There are more people asking for help, at the churches. And there's the bank. The First National Bank, on the corner of the square, with its stone columns, and its big clock. Its doors are closed. The bank closed two years ago, in 1931. One morning, people came to take out their money, and found the doors locked, and a notice on the door. The bank had failed. All the money in it was gone. Many families in Harlan lost their savings. The Millers lost three hundred dollars. It was all the money they had saved, over many years, for June's education, and for a rainy day. What would you have bought with your penny? They have never got it back.

[long-pause]

{img:20-shuttered-bank-building-stone-columns-notice-on-door-pigeons-quiet-square}

Walt doesn't talk about it much.

[pause]

But June knows that he still thinks about it, sometimes. You'd see it in his face, though, when he thinks nobody is looking. He walks past the bank on Saturday, and looks at the closed doors, and shakes his head. Grandma Hattie says that the money is gone, and there's no use crying over it. She says that what you have in your head, and in your heart, nobody can take away. And she makes sure that June keeps reading her books. At the end of the afternoon, the family gets back into the old car, with their groceries, and drives home, along the gravel road, as the sun goes down. The car rattles, and bumps, and once, it stops, and Walt has to get out and crank it, to start it again. But they get home, in the end. And Ruth puts away the groceries, carefully, and makes supper. And they sit down together, to eat, as the lamps are lit.

[long-pause]

{img:21-fdr-inauguration-march-1933-capitol-steps-crowd-grey-sky-washington}

On the fourth of March, 1933, Franklin Delano Roosevelt became president of the United States.

[pause]

It was a cold, grey day in Washington. You'd gather around the radio with your neighbours to listen. A great crowd stood in front of the Capitol, to watch him take the oath. And all over America, millions of people sat by their radios, to listen. The Millers were among them, in their kitchen, in Iowa. The country was in a terrible state, that morning. In the weeks before, there had been panic. People everywhere had rushed to the banks, to take out their money, afraid that the banks would close. And many banks did close. In state after state, the governors had ordered all the banks to shut, to stop the panic. By the morning of the inauguration, almost every bank in America was closed, or limited. The whole money system of the country had almost stopped.

[long-pause]

{img:22-family-listening-to-radio-kitchen-march-1933-faces-hopeful-quiet}

Roosevelt stood up, at the front of the platform, holding tightly to the arm of his son, because of his weak legs.

[pause]

He took the oath. You'd see the crowd go quiet as he gripped the railing. Then he gave his speech. His voice was strong, and clear, and calm, and confident. And he said the words that everyone remembered, for the rest of their lives. He said, the only thing we have to fear is fear itself. In the Millers' kitchen, everyone was silent, listening. Grandma Hattie nodded. Ruth wiped her eyes, with her apron. Walt sat very still. And when the speech was over, he said, quietly, well, that's a man who means to do something. For the first time in a long time, the Millers felt a little hope.

[long-pause]

{img:23-closed-bank-doors-notice-bank-holiday-march-1933-townspeople-reading}

Roosevelt acted at once.

[pause]

Two days later, he ordered every bank in America to close, for a few days. He called it a bank holiday. It wasn't a holiday, of course. But it gave the government time to check all the banks, and to find out which ones were sound, and which ones weren't. The healthy banks would be allowed to open again. The unhealthy ones would stay closed, or be helped, or be closed for good. For a week, there was almost no money in America. People couldn't take money out of the banks, or put money in. Shops gave credit, or accepted promises, or IOUs. In some towns, people made their own money, out of paper, or wood, or even leather. Farmers traded eggs for flour, and butter for coffee, and chickens for shoes. For the Millers, it didn't make much difference. They had no money in the bank anyway. Then, on the twelfth of March, a Sunday evening, Roosevelt spoke to the nation again, on the radio. It wasn't a formal speech. It was a talk, as if he were sitting in their living room, by the fire. He explained, in simple words, what the banks were, and how they worked, and why they had failed, and what the government was doing about it. Can you imagine the relief in so many kitchens that week? And he told the people that it was safe to put their money back in the banks.

[long-pause]

{img:24-people-queuing-to-deposit-money-reopened-bank-march-1933-relieved-faces}

Millions of people listened.

[pause]

And they believed him. You'd lean closer to the speaker so you didn't miss a word. The next day, when the banks began to open again, the people didn't rush to take their money out. Instead, they came to put it back in. The panic was over. It was the first of many radio talks that Roosevelt gave, over the next twelve years. People called them the fireside chats. Later that year, a new law began to protect people's savings in the banks, so that they wouldn't lose them again, even if a bank failed. It was too late for the Millers' three hundred dollars. But Walt said that at least, it wouldn't happen to anyone else. And that was something.

[long-pause]

{img:25-wooden-cathedral-radio-1933-kitchen-shelf-battery-glowing-dial}

Back home, the evening light is fading. Can you hear that crackle from the parlour? Someone has switched on the radio.

[pause]

The most precious thing in the Millers' house is the radio.

[pause]

It stands on a shelf in the kitchen, a big wooden box, with a rounded top, like a little church window, and a cloth front, and two knobs. You'd see it standing in the parlour like a small wooden church. Because there's no electricity on the farm, it runs on a battery. A big, heavy battery, which must be taken to town, to be recharged, every few weeks. So the radio isn't on all the time. Only for the most important programmes. Walt bought the radio in 1927, when times were good. It cost a lot of money. Now, they could never afford it. But it has become the family's window on the world. Through it, they hear the news, from Washington, and New York, and Europe. They hear the farm prices, and the weather forecasts. They hear music, and comedy, and drama, and sports. And they hear the president, speaking to them, as if he were sitting at their kitchen table.

[long-pause]

{img:26-father-adjusting-radio-dial-family-gathered-close-evening-lamplight}

Every day at noon, Walt comes in from the fields, and turns on the radio, for the farm report.

[pause]

A man in Des Moines reads out the prices for corn, and wheat, and hogs, and cattle, in Chicago. You'd hear the prices read out, slowly and clearly. And the weather forecast. Walt listens carefully, while he eats his dinner, and shakes his head at the prices. Then he turns the radio off, to save the battery, and goes back to work. In the evening, after supper, the family gathers around the radio, for an hour or two. They pull their chairs close, because the sound isn't very loud. Grandma Hattie sits in the rocking chair, with her knitting. Ruth sits at the table, with her mending. Walt sits in his chair, with his pipe. And June and Eddie sit on the floor, as close to the radio as they can get. Rusty lies by the stove, with his head on his paws.

[long-pause]

{img:27-children-sitting-on-floor-close-to-radio-rapt-attention-dog-by-stove}

They listen to comedy shows, which make them laugh, until they cry.

[pause]

Two funny men, who are always getting into trouble, and always getting out of it again. A couple who argue, and joke, and tease each other, every week. They listen to music. Dance bands, from the great hotels of Chicago and New York. Country music, from Nashville. Church music, on Sunday. And Grandma Hattie's favourite, the old songs, from her youth. On clear nights, the radio can pick up stations far away. And Eddie's favourite is a new programme, from a station in Detroit, which began in 1933. It's about a masked rider of the plains, in the old West, who fights for justice, with his faithful friend, and his white horse. Every episode begins with exciting music, and the sound of galloping hooves, and a cry of Hi-yo, Silver. Eddie gallops around the kitchen, after every episode, until his mother tells him to calm down. June prefers the stories. Dramas, and mysteries, and adventures, read by actors, with sound effects. Doors creaking, footsteps, thunder, rain, horses, trains. She closes her eyes, and listens, and sees it all in her imagination, more clearly than any movie. She says that the radio is better than the movies, because you can make the pictures yourself. Eddie says that's silly. I love that little detail. But he closes his eyes too.

[long-pause]

{img:28-radio-listening-whole-family-laughing-comedy-show-kitchen-warm-night}

The radio brings the family together, every evening.

[pause]

And it brings the whole country together too. You'd pull your chair a little closer to the warm glow of the dial. All over America, in farmhouses, and city apartments, and small town homes, millions of people are listening to the same programmes, at the same time. Laughing at the same jokes, and worrying about the same news. In the hard times of the Depression, it makes people feel a little less alone. When the programmes are over, Walt turns off the radio, and the dial slowly fades, from orange to dark. The house is quiet again. Just the ticking of the clock, and the crackling of the stove, and the wind outside. The family says goodnight, and goes up to bed, with candles, and lamps. And the radio sits on its shelf, silent, until tomorrow.

[long-pause]

{img:29-mother-sewing-dress-from-printed-flour-sacks-treadle-machine-kitchen}

In the Depression, nothing is wasted.

[pause]

Not a scrap of food, not a piece of cloth, not a bit of string. You'd find a use for every scrap, every string and every nail. Everything is saved, and used, and reused, until it's completely worn out. And then it's used for something else. Ruth is very good at this. Like most farm wives, she has learned to make something useful out of almost anything. Flour, and sugar, and animal feed, come in big cotton sacks. In the old days, the sacks were plain white. But the companies that make them have noticed that the farm wives use the sacks to make clothes. So now, the sacks are made of pretty printed cotton, with flowers, and checks, and stripes. Ruth chooses the flour carefully, at the store, by the pattern on the sack. When she has enough sacks of the same pattern, she makes a dress for June.

[long-pause]

{img:30-girl-in-flour-sack-print-dress-twirling-farmyard-proud-smile}

June has three dresses made from flour sacks.

[pause]

One with little blue flowers, one with red checks, and one with yellow daisies. You'd never guess, looking at her, that they'd once held flour. Many of the girls at school wear flour sack dresses too. Nobody is ashamed of them. They are pretty, and practical, and free. The boys wear shirts made from feed sacks, and the babies wear diapers made from sugar sacks. And Ruth makes towels, and aprons, and pillowcases, and curtains, from the plain ones. When clothes wear out, they are mended. Patches are sewn over the holes, in the elbows, and the knees. Socks are darned, again and again, until they are more darn than sock. Collars are turned around, so that the worn side is hidden. When a coat is too small for June, it's cut down, and remade, for Eddie. Isn't that clever? When the clothes are too worn to wear, they are cut into strips, and braided into rugs, or sewn into quilts.

[long-pause]

{img:31-women-quilting-bee-large-quilt-frame-church-basement-1933-talking}

Food is never wasted either.

[pause]

Leftovers are eaten the next day, or turned into soup, or fed to the hogs. You'd see jars of beans and tomatoes lined up on the cellar shelves. Ruth bakes her own bread, and churns her own butter, and makes her own soap. In the summer, she spends weeks canning the vegetables, and fruit, from her garden. Beans, and tomatoes, and corn, and peas, and pickles, and peaches, and jam. Hundreds of glass jars, lined up on the shelves in the cellar, shining like jewels. Enough food for the whole winter. Many people in the towns plant gardens too, in their backyards, and in empty lots. Even in the cities, people grow vegetables, on rooftops, and in window boxes. Everyone keeps chickens, if they can. Meals are simple. Beans, and potatoes, and cornbread, and soup. Some meals are made from almost nothing. Bread, and gravy. Fried cornmeal mush. Potato pancakes. Water soup, the children call it, when there's very little in it. Things that break are repaired, not replaced. Walt fixes the harness, and the tools, and the machines, with wire, and nails, and pieces of old metal, and great patience. He makes new handles for the tools, from wood he cuts himself. He resoles the family's shoes, with pieces of old tyres. When the car breaks down, he fixes it himself. You'd learn to love bread pudding, I think. He says that there's almost nothing that can't be fixed, with a bit of baling wire, and a bit of thinking.

[long-pause]

{img:32-farmer-repairing-shoes-old-tyre-rubber-workbench-shed-lantern-evening}

The children make their own toys.

[pause]

Eddie makes a kite, from newspaper, and sticks, and string. You'd find a doll made from a corncob, or a top carved from a spool. He makes a slingshot, from a forked stick, and an old inner tube. He plays marbles, and mumblety-peg, with his pocketknife, and baseball, with a ball made of string, wrapped tightly, and covered with tape. June makes paper dolls, cut from old catalogues, with paper clothes. And together, they make a swing, from a rope, and an old tyre, hanging from the big tree in the yard. Many years later, when June is a grandmother, she will still save string, and paper bags, and rubber bands, and old jars. Her grandchildren will laugh at her. But she will just smile, and say that they don't know what it was like. And she will be right.

[long-pause]

{img:33-hobos-riding-freight-train-boxcar-open-door-prairie-1933-young-men}

Not everyone in 1933 had a farm, or a radio, or even a home. Some people were always on the road. Let's sit on the porch and watch them pass.

[pause]

In the Depression, America is full of travellers.

[pause]

Not tourists, or holidaymakers. You'd see them walking along the road with a bundle on a stick. But people looking for work, or for food, or for a new beginning. Men, mostly, and some women, and many young people, boys and girls, sometimes as young as fourteen or fifteen. They have left their homes, because there was no work there, and no food, and they didn't want to be a burden on their families. Many of them travel by freight train. They climb into empty boxcars, or onto the roofs, or underneath, and ride for free, from town to town, from state to state, across the whole country. It's dangerous, and uncomfortable, and illegal. But it's the only way they can afford to travel. People call them hobos, or tramps, or transients. Perhaps a quarter of a million young people are riding the rails, in 1933. Near the railway tracks, outside every town, there are hobo camps, which they call jungles. Places where the travellers can rest, and sleep, and cook, and wash, and share news. They build little fires, and cook a stew, in an old tin can, from whatever they have. A potato, an onion, a bit of meat, if they are lucky. Everyone puts something in. And everyone shares. There are unwritten rules, in the jungles, about sharing, and cleanliness, and respect. The railway line runs past the end of the Millers' farm, about half a mile away. Several times a week, a long freight train rumbles past, with its whistle blowing, and its smoke trailing across the fields. Eddie loves to watch the trains. Sometimes, he sees men sitting in the open doors of the boxcars, with their legs dangling, watching the fields go by. Sometimes, they wave to him. And he waves back.

[long-pause]

{img:34-stranger-at-farmhouse-back-door-hat-in-hand-farm-wife-offering-plate}

And sometimes, the travellers come to the farmhouse.

[pause]

They walk up the lane, from the railway, tired, and dusty, and hungry. You'd see a tired figure standing at the end of the lane. They knock at the back door, politely, with their hats in their hands. And they ask if there's any work they could do, in exchange for a meal. Ruth never turns them away. She gives them a plate of food. Bread, and beans, and coffee, or whatever there's. And Walt finds them a job to do. Chopping wood, or mending a fence, or cleaning out the barn. Most of the travellers are polite, and grateful, and honest. They are ordinary people, who have fallen on hard times. A carpenter from Chicago. A factory worker from Detroit. A young man from Kansas, whose family lost their farm. A schoolteacher, who lost her job when the school closed. They sit on the back step, and eat, and talk a little, about where they have come from, and where they are going. Then they thank Ruth, and walk back down the lane, to the railway.

[long-pause]

{img:35-chalk-symbol-on-fence-post-hobo-sign-cat-drawing-farm-gate}

On the gatepost, at the end of the Millers' lane, there's a little mark, drawn in chalk.

[pause]

A simple drawing, like a cat. You'd walk right past it, unless you knew what to look for. It's a hobo sign. The travellers have their own secret code, of little signs, which they draw on fences, and gateposts, and walls, to tell each other about the places they pass. This house has a dog. This town has police who arrest hobos. This farmer will give you work. And the cat means, a kind lady lives here. Ruth doesn't know about the sign. But Eddie has seen it. He asked one of the travellers what it meant, and the man told him. Eddie is very proud of his mother. He decides not to rub it out. Would you have known what it meant? Even though, he thinks, his mother isn't much like a cat.

[long-pause]

{img:36-young-traveller-chopping-wood-farmyard-boy-watching-afternoon-light}

One day in the spring, a young man comes to the door.

[pause]

He is only seventeen, thin, and pale, and shy. You'd hear a soft knock at the kitchen door. His name is Tom. He comes from a farm in Nebraska, which his family lost to the bank. He has been riding the trains for six months, looking for work. Walt gives him a job, for a week, helping with the planting. He sleeps in the barn, and eats with the family. He is a good worker, and quiet, and polite, and the children like him. At the end of the week, Walt gives him two dollars, which is all he can spare, and a bag of food. Tom thanks him, and shakes his hand, and walks away down the lane, toward the railway. Ruth watches him go, from the kitchen window. She thinks about her own children, and hopes that they will never have to do what he is doing. You'd probably give him a plate of food too, wouldn't you? And she says a little prayer for him, that he will find his way home.

[long-pause]

{img:37-farm-auction-1933-crowd-of-farmers-in-farmyard-auctioneer-on-wagon}

In 1932 and 1933, many farmers in Iowa lost their farms.

[pause]

They couldn't pay their mortgages, or their taxes. You can imagine how it felt to see your own farm on a sale poster. So the banks, or the insurance companies, which had lent them the money, took the farms, and sold them, at auction. Everything was sold. The land, the house, the barn, the animals, the machines, the furniture. And the family had to leave, with nothing. These auctions were very sad occasions. A family that had worked the land for generations, perhaps fifty years, or more, had to stand and watch as their home, and everything they owned, was sold to strangers. Their neighbours came to watch too. And many of them were angry. They thought it was wrong. The farmers hadn't done anything wrong. They had worked hard. It was the Depression that had ruined them. So, in some places, the farmers began to fight back, in their own way. They organised what were called penny auctions. When a farm was being sold, all the neighbours came to the auction, in a big crowd. They stood together, silently, around the auctioneer. And when the bidding began, they made it very clear, without saying a word, that nobody should bid more than a few pennies.

[long-pause]

{img:38-farmers-standing-silently-together-auction-crowd-arms-folded-serious}

So the horse was sold for ten cents.

[pause]

The cow, for five cents. The plough, for a quarter. The tractor, for a dollar. And all of it was bought by the neighbours. Then, when the auction was over, the neighbours gave everything back to the family. The bank got only a few dollars. And the family kept their things, and sometimes, even their farm. It wasn't exactly legal. But it worked, sometimes. In the spring of 1933, there's a penny auction, on a farm a few miles from the Millers. The farm belongs to the Petersens, a Danish family, who are friends of the Millers, from church. Karl Petersen is a good farmer, and a hard worker. But he borrowed money, to buy more land, in the good years. And now he can't pay it back. Can you believe it? The insurance company has decided to sell his farm.

[long-pause]

{img:39-old-farmer-and-wife-standing-on-porch-watching-auction-neighbours-crowd}

On the morning of the auction, Walt puts on his hat, and his good coat, and drives over to the Petersen farm.

[pause]

Many other farmers are there already, standing in the yard, in their overalls, and their hats. You'd see dozens of trucks and wagons parked along the lane. Hundreds of them. They are quiet, and serious. Nobody is shouting, or making trouble. They just stand there, together, with their arms folded, and watch. The auctioneer stands on a wagon, and begins. He describes the first horse, a fine brown mare, and asks for a bid. There's silence. Then one farmer says, five cents. The auctioneer asks for more. Nobody says anything. They just look at him. The auctioneer looks at the crowd. Then he says, sold, for five cents. And so it goes on, all morning. Everything sold, for a few pennies, and all of it given back to the Petersens. Karl Petersen stands on his porch, with his wife, and watches. At first, he doesn't understand. Then he does. And he can't speak. His wife cries, quietly, into her handkerchief. When the auction is over, the farmers come up to the porch, one by one, and shake Karl's hand, and say a few words. Then they get into their cars and their wagons, and drive home.

[long-pause]

{img:40-neighbours-shaking-hands-on-porch-after-auction-relief-gratitude-spring}

The penny auctions didn't save every farm.

[pause]

And in some places, there was trouble, and anger, and even violence. You might think the neighbours could save everyone. Sadly, you'd be wrong. In the spring of 1933, some farmers in Iowa became so angry that they threatened a judge, who was handling the farm sales. The governor sent in soldiers, to keep order. Most farmers didn't approve of this. They wanted justice, but peacefully. Then, slowly, things began to change. The government passed new laws, to help the farmers. The banks were told to be more patient. New loans were made available, at lower interest, so that farmers could keep their farms. And the Petersens kept theirs. Years later, Karl's grandson still farmed the same land. And on the wall of the farmhouse, there was a framed receipt, from the auction, for a brown mare, sold for five cents.

[long-pause]

{img:41-one-room-country-schoolhouse-1933-white-wooden-bell-tower-children-walking}

The road outside is quiet again. Tomorrow is a school day, so you'll want to be up early with June and Eddie.

[pause]

June and Eddie go to a one-room school, about a mile and a half from the farm.

[pause]

It's a small white wooden building, standing alone, at a crossroads, between the cornfields. You'd walk with them along the road, swinging your lunch pail. It has a little bell tower, on the roof, and a porch, and a pump, and two outhouses at the back. One for boys, and one for girls. There are hundreds of little schools like this, all over Iowa. One for every few square miles of farmland. Every morning, June and Eddie walk to school, along the gravel road, with their lunch pails. A lunch pail is an old tin bucket, which once held lard, or syrup, with a lid. Inside, there's their lunch. A sandwich, of homemade bread, with butter, and jam, or a slice of meat. An apple, or a cookie. And a little jar of milk. In the winter, when the snow is deep, Walt takes them in the sleigh, pulled by the horses.

[long-pause]

{img:42-schoolroom-interior-1933-wooden-desks-pot-bellied-stove-blackboard-teacher}

Inside the school, there's one big room.

[pause]

At the front, there's the teacher's desk, and a blackboard, and a map of the world, and a picture of George Washington. You'd smell chalk dust and wood smoke from the stove. There are rows of wooden desks, with inkwells, and lids that open. In the middle of the room, there's a big iron stove, which heats the school in the winter. And at the back, there are hooks for coats, and a shelf for the lunch pails, and a water bucket, with a dipper. There are eighteen children in the school. From the youngest, who are six, to the oldest, who are fourteen. All in the same room, with one teacher. The teacher must teach all the grades, at the same time. While she teaches the little ones to read, the older ones do their arithmetic, or their writing. Then she teaches the older ones, and the little ones practise their letters. You'd sit near the stove on the coldest mornings, if you were lucky. The older children often help the younger ones.

[long-pause]

{img:43-young-woman-teacher-1933-helping-small-child-reading-older-girl-assisting}

The teacher is Miss Clara Holm.

[pause]

She is twenty-two years old, and she has been teaching here for two years. If you'd been her pupil, you'd have liked her, I'm sure. She lives with a farm family nearby, and walks to school every morning, very early, to light the stove, and sweep the floor, before the children arrive. She is kind, and patient, and strict, and the children love her. June wants to be just like her. Miss Holm's pay has been cut, because of the Depression. She now earns forty dollars a month, for eight months of the year. And sometimes, the school board can't pay her at all, and gives her a note, promising to pay her later. In some places, the schools have closed completely, because there's no money to pay the teachers. But the families around here have decided to keep the school open, whatever happens. They bring the teacher food, and firewood, and whatever else they can. The school day begins at nine o'clock, when Miss Holm rings the bell, on the porch. The children line up, and go in, and stand by their desks, and say the Pledge of Allegiance, to the flag. Then they sing a song. Then the lessons begin. Reading, and writing, and arithmetic, and spelling, and geography, and history. There are spelling bees, on Friday afternoons, when the children stand in a line, and spell difficult words, until only one is left. June often wins.

[long-pause]

{img:44-schoolchildren-playing-recess-schoolyard-ball-game-fields-around-1933}

At recess, in the middle of the morning, and the afternoon, the children go outside, to play.

[pause]

In the schoolyard, and the fields around. They play baseball, and tag, and hide and seek, and Annie Over, throwing a ball over the roof of the school. In the winter, they build snowmen, and have snowball fights, and slide on the ice. At lunchtime, they eat their lunches, sitting on the steps, or by the stove, and swap their sandwiches. Some of the children are very poor. Their families have lost their farms, or have no money at all. They come to school in thin, worn clothes, and broken shoes, and sometimes, without any lunch. Miss Holm notices. And quietly, without saying anything, she makes sure that they get something to eat. She brings an extra sandwich. Or she asks June, and some of the other children, to share. June always shares. What was your favourite game at school? She knows that her family is lucky.

[long-pause]

{img:45-white-country-church-1933-steeple-cars-and-wagons-parked-sunday-morning}

On Sunday morning, the Millers go to church.

[pause]

A white wooden church, with a tall steeple, about two miles from the farm, at the edge of a little village. You'd put on your best clothes and climb into the car. It's a Methodist church. The Millers have gone there for three generations. Grandma Hattie was married there. Walt and Ruth were married there. June and Eddie were baptised there. It's the centre of the community, for all the farm families around. The church is full on Sundays. The families come in their best clothes, which are often their only good clothes. The men in their dark suits, a little shiny with age. The women in their best dresses, and hats. The children scrubbed clean, with their hair brushed flat, and wet. You'd hear the bell from a long way off, across the fields. They sit in the wooden pews, and sing the old hymns, and listen to the sermon, and pray.

[long-pause]

{img:46-church-basement-potluck-supper-long-tables-casseroles-pies-families-1933}

After the service, the families stand outside, in the churchyard, and talk.

[pause]

This is almost as important as the service. They share the news of the week. Who is ill, who has had a baby, who has lost their farm, who has found work. Who needs help. In the Depression, there's always someone who needs help. And the church families help each other, in every way they can. Once a month, there's a church supper, in the basement. Every family brings a dish. Casseroles, and salads, and baked beans, and fried chicken, and potatoes, and bread, and pies, and cakes. It's called a potluck, because you never know what you'll get. The food is laid out on long tables, and everyone helps themselves. Even the poorest families, who bring only a little, can eat as much as they want. Wouldn't you like to stand there with them, listening? Nobody asks what anyone brought.

[long-pause]

{img:47-barn-raising-neighbours-lifting-wall-frame-together-ropes-summer-1933}

The neighbours help each other with the work, too.

[pause]

When a farmer is ill, or injured, the neighbours come, and do his chores, and bring in his crops, without being asked, and without being paid. You'd see five or six men working together in one field. When a barn burns down, the neighbours come together, and build a new one, in a day or two. The men do the building, and the women cook a great feast, for everyone. They call it a barn raising. The women have their own gatherings. Every week, on Wednesday afternoon, some of them meet at one of their houses, for a quilting bee. They sit around a big wooden frame, with a quilt stretched across it, and sew, and talk, and drink coffee, and eat cake. The quilts are made from scraps of old clothes, and flour sacks, in beautiful patterns. Each one takes many weeks. When it's finished, it's given to someone who needs it. You'd never have to ask twice for help out here. A new bride, or a new baby, or a family who has lost everything.

[long-pause]

{img:48-women-delivering-food-basket-to-poor-family-farmhouse-door-kind}

Ruth is one of the leaders of the church women.

[pause]

She organises the help, for the families in need. You'd find her organising quilts, suppers and baskets for families in need. She knows who is struggling, and what they need. Food, or clothes, or coal, or a doctor. And she makes sure they get it, quietly, without fuss, and without making anyone feel ashamed. She says that the Lord didn't put us on this earth to look after only ourselves. One family, the Dunns, are in very great trouble. The father passed away last winter, and the mother has five children, and no money, and no land. They live in a little rented house, at the edge of the village. Every week, Ruth takes them a basket of food. Eggs, and butter, and bread, and vegetables, and sometimes a chicken. June goes with her, and plays with the Dunn children, while Ruth talks to their mother. On the way home, June asks her mother why she does it, when they have so little themselves. Ruth thinks for a moment. Then she says, because one day, it might be us. And because, when you share what you have, there's always enough. June doesn't quite understand. But she remembers it, all her life.

[long-pause]

{img:49-ccc-camp-1933-young-men-planting-trees-hillside-shovels-uniforms}

Far away from the little schoolhouse, in Washington, big plans were being made. What do you think they meant for a farm family like the Millers?

[pause]

In the first hundred days after he became president, Roosevelt and the Congress passed more new laws than any government in American history, in such a short time.

[pause]

People called it the Hundred Days. You'd open the newspaper and find something new almost every day. The new laws were part of the New Deal, which Roosevelt had promised. They were meant to help the people who were suffering, and to get the economy working again, and to make sure that such a disaster could never happen again. There were many new programmes, with many new names. So many that people couldn't keep track of them. They were known by their initials, like a kind of alphabet soup. The AAA, for the farmers. The CCC, for the young men. The FERA, to give money to the states, for the poor. The TVA, to build dams, and bring electricity, to the poor lands of the south. And many more.

[long-pause]

{img:50-young-men-arriving-ccc-camp-tents-barracks-forest-truck-1933}

One of the most popular programmes was the Civilian Conservation Corps, the CCC.

[pause]

It gave work to young men, between eighteen and twenty-five, from poor families. You'd see young men in work clothes climbing into army trucks. They lived in camps, in the countryside, in the forests, and the parks, and worked on projects to protect nature. Planting trees, building roads and trails, stopping soil from washing away, fighting forest fires, and building parks. They were paid thirty dollars a month. Twenty-five of those dollars were sent home, to their families. Ruth's nephew, Jimmy, joins the CCC in the summer of 1933. He is nineteen, and he has been out of work for two years. He goes to a camp in the forests of Minnesota, in the north. He writes letters home, which Ruth reads aloud, in the evenings. He says the work is hard, but good. He has never eaten so well in his life. Three big meals a day. He has put on fifteen pounds. And he has learned to use an axe, and a saw, and to drive a truck.

[long-pause]

{img:51-ccc-boys-around-campfire-evening-forest-letters-harmonica-tired-happy}

Jimmy says that he has planted thousands of trees, on hillsides where the old forest was cut down.

[pause]

He says that one day, there will be a great forest there again, and he will take his children to see it. You can picture him, proud and sunburnt, telling you all about it. He says he is proud of his work. And his mother, Ruth's sister, is proud of him. The twenty-five dollars a month that he sends home keeps her family going. The CCC was a great success. Over the next nine years, about three million young men worked in it. They planted about three billion trees. They built thousands of parks, and trails, and bridges, and roads, all over America. Many of them are still used today. And many of the young men said, later, that the CCC changed their lives, and gave them hope, and pride, when they needed it most. For the farmers, there was the AAA, the Agricultural Adjustment Act. It tried to raise the prices of crops, by paying farmers to grow less. The idea was that if there was less corn, and less pork, the prices would go up. It was controversial. In the summer of 1933, the government paid farmers to slaughter millions of young pigs, to reduce the number of hogs. Many people thought this was shocking, when so many people were hungry.

[long-pause]

{img:52-farmer-reading-government-letter-kitchen-table-wife-looking-over-shoulder}

Walt doesn't like the idea of being paid not to farm.

[pause]

It goes against everything he believes in. But he needs the money. So in the autumn, he signs up, and agrees to plant fewer acres of corn next year. And in return, he gets a cheque from the government. It isn't much. But it helps him pay the mortgage, and keep the farm. Many farmers do the same. Not all of the New Deal worked. Some of it was badly planned, and some of it was wasteful, and some of it was later stopped by the courts. People argued about it, then, and they still argue about it today. But it gave many people hope, in the darkest time. It showed that the government cared about them. Do you think he was right? And slowly, very slowly, things began to get a little better.

[long-pause]

{img:53-iowa-cornfield-july-tall-green-corn-blue-sky-white-clouds-farm-boy}

The new programmes take time to reach the farm. Meanwhile, the summer arrives, hot and bright. Can you feel the sun on your arms?

[pause]

The summer of 1933 comes to Iowa, hot and bright.

[pause]

The corn grows tall and green, in the long, warm days. You'd feel the heat rising off the dusty road. Walt says you can almost hear it growing, on a still, hot night. A soft, crackling, rustling sound, all around the farm, in the dark. By the Fourth of July, the corn is supposed to be knee high, according to the old saying. This year, it's waist high, and more. Walt is pleased. But the summer is also a time of hard work. The oats must be harvested, and the hay cut, and stacked. In July, there's threshing. A big threshing machine, driven by a steam engine or a tractor, goes from farm to farm. All the neighbours come together, to help each other. The men feed the bundles of oats into the machine, which separates the grain from the straw. It's hot, dusty, noisy work.

[long-pause]

{img:54-threshing-dinner-long-table-under-trees-farmers-eating-women-serving}

And at midday, there's the threshing dinner.

[pause]

The women of the neighbourhood cook a great feast, for all the workers. You'd see long tables in the shade, piled high with food. Fried chicken, and roast beef, and mashed potatoes, and gravy, and corn on the cob, and green beans, and sliced tomatoes, and fresh bread, and pies. Apple, and cherry, and rhubarb. The men eat at long tables, set up under the trees, in the yard. They eat enormous amounts. Then they go back to work, until the evening. Even in the hard times, the summer has its pleasures. On hot afternoons, when the chores are done, the children go to the swimming hole. A deep, cool pool, in the creek, under the trees, about half a mile from the farm. They swim, and splash, and dive from a rope swing, hanging from a big branch. Eddie can do a backward somersault, into the water. June prefers to float on her back, and look at the sky, through the leaves.

[long-pause]

{img:55-children-swimming-creek-rope-swing-summer-afternoon-sunlight-trees}

On summer evenings, there's baseball.

[pause]

The town has a team, made up of farmers, and shopkeepers, and young men, and they play against the teams from other towns, on Sunday afternoons, and summer evenings. In the evening, they stay to see the lights.

In 1933, Walt enters the county contest, for the first time.

[pause]

[pause]

In December, the winter comes to Iowa.

[pause]

{img:77-rural-electrification-1930s-power-lines-reaching-farmhouse-workers-poles}

Let's step out of 1933 for a little while, you and I, and look ahead. What became of the Millers, and of the country around them?

[pause]

The Great Depression didn't end in 1933.

[pause]

It went on for several more years. You might think one good year would end it. It didn't. Things got slowly better, from 1933 to 1937. Then they got worse again, for a while. Unemployment stayed high, through the whole of the 1930s. And the Dust Bowl, in the southern plains, brought terrible suffering to many farm families, in the middle of the decade. But slowly, step by step, the country recovered. The New Deal brought many changes, which lasted. In 1935, a new law created Social Security, which gave pensions to old people, and help to the unemployed, and the disabled. For the first time, old people like Grandma Hattie would have a little money of their own, from the government, every month, when they could no longer work.

[long-pause]

In 1935, another programme began, which changed the lives of the farm families more than almost anything else.

[pause]

It was called the Rural Electrification Administration. You'd see men putting up tall poles along the gravel roads. It brought electricity to the farms, which the private power companies hadn't wanted to do, because it didn't make enough money. The government lent money to the farmers, to form cooperatives, and build their own power lines. Electricity came to the Millers' farm in 1939. June never forgot the day. A truck came along the gravel road, with men, and poles, and wire. They put up the poles, and strung the wires, all the way to the house. And that evening, Ruth pulled a little chain, in the kitchen, and a light bulb came on, over the table. The whole family stood and stared at it. Grandma Hattie cried. Soon, they had an electric radio, and an electric iron, and a refrigerator. And Ruth's life became a great deal easier. Then, in December 1941, America entered the Second World War. The war finally ended the Depression. Suddenly, there was work for everyone. The factories ran day and night, making ships, and planes, and tanks. The farmers were asked to grow as much food as they possibly could, to feed the soldiers, and the allies. And the prices for crops went up, and up. Walt paid off the mortgage, at last, in 1943. Eddie joined the army in 1942, when he was eighteen. He served in Europe, as a mechanic, fixing trucks and tanks. He came home safely, in 1945, and took over the farm from his father. In the evenings, Walt and Ruth sometimes talked about the Depression.

[pause]

{img:81-old-woman-grandmother-1980s-saving-string-ball-kitchen-drawer-jars}

The people who lived through the Great Depression never forgot it.

[pause]

It shaped them for the rest of their lives. You might know someone like that yourself, a grandparent perhaps. They were called, later, the Depression generation. And many of them kept the habits they learned in those years, long after the hard times were over. Even when they were comfortable, and even rich, they couldn't bear to waste anything. They saved string, and rubber bands, and paper bags, and twist ties, and bits of aluminium foil, smoothed out and folded. They kept old jars, and old buttons, and old clothes, just in case. They mended things, instead of throwing them away. They grew vegetables in their gardens, and canned them, every summer. They ate every scrap of food on their plates, and told their grandchildren to do the same.

[long-pause]

{img:82-grandmother-teaching-grandchild-to-darn-sock-wooden-egg-armchair}

They were careful with money.

[pause]

Very careful. You might have a grandparent who saved string and paper bags, just like them. Many of them never trusted banks again, and kept some of their money hidden at home, under the mattress, or in a coffee can, buried in the garden. They didn't like to borrow money, or to buy things on credit. If they couldn't afford something, they did without it, until they could. And they saved, every week, a little, for a rainy day. Because they knew that rainy days could come. But the Depression also taught them other things. It taught them to help each other. To share what they had, with people who had less. To be kind to strangers. To work together, as a community, as neighbours, as a country. Many of them said that the Depression made people closer to each other. In the hard times, they found out who their friends were. And they found out that most people are good.

[long-pause]

{img:83-neighbours-helping-each-other-1930s-sharing-vegetables-over-fence}

It taught them to be grateful, for small things.

[pause]

A warm house. A full stomach. A job. A family. A friend. A good harvest. A song on the radio. Things that people in good times often take for granted. And it taught them to be patient. To get through hard times, one day at a time, without giving up. To believe that things would get better, even when there was no sign of it. The Depression also changed America. After it, the government took on a much bigger role in people's lives. Protecting their savings, and their jobs, and their old age. Helping the farmers, and the poor, and the unemployed. Building roads, and dams, and power lines, and parks. People argued then, and they still argue now, about whether this was right. Isn't that a lesson worth keeping? But most people agreed that the country should never again let its people suffer as they had in the early 1930s.

[long-pause]

{img:84-photograph-album-1930s-family-pictures-old-hands-turning-pages-memories}

Many of the photographs that we have of the Depression were taken by photographers who worked for the government, in the later 1930s.

[pause]

They travelled all over America, with their cameras, and took pictures of ordinary people. You've probably seen some of those pictures yourself. Farmers, and their families, and migrant workers, and children, and old people. Their photographs show the poverty, and the hardship. But they also show the dignity, and the strength, and the courage of the people. They are some of the most famous photographs ever taken. The stories of the Depression were handed down, in families, from grandparents to grandchildren. Stories of flour sack dresses, and hobos at the back door, and penny auctions, and the first electric light. Many of those grandchildren are grandparents themselves, today. And they still tell the stories. Of how great-grandma saved string. And how great-grandpa fixed everything with baling wire. And how, in the hardest of times, people shared what they had. June kept her diary, the one her grandmother gave her, for Christmas, in 1933, all her life. Years later, her granddaughter found it, in a box, in the attic. She read it, sitting on the attic floor, all afternoon. And on the last page of 1933, she found these words, in June's careful, childish handwriting. We didn't have much this year. But we had enough. And we had each other.

[long-pause]

{img:85-today-iowa-farmland-aerial-cornfields-grain-bins-wind-turbines-summer}

The years roll on, and the old farm changes. So what would you find if you drove down that same gravel road today?

[pause]

Today, Iowa is still farm country.

[pause]

The cornfields still stretch away to the horizon, in every direction, under the wide sky. If you drive there today, you'll still see cornfields to the horizon. Iowa grows more corn than almost anywhere else in the world. And more soybeans, and more hogs. In the summer, the corn still grows tall and green, and on a still, hot night, you can still hear it crackling, as it grows. But farming has changed enormously since 1933. The farms are much bigger now. Where there were once four or five farms, of a hundred and sixty acres each, there's now one farm, of a thousand acres or more. The work that once took a farmer and his horses a whole autumn, picking corn by hand, is now done by a great combine harvester, in a few days. One farmer, with modern machines, can do the work of dozens of people, in the old days.

[long-pause]

{img:86-today-old-one-room-schoolhouse-preserved-museum-prairie-grass-visitors}

So there are far fewer people living on the farms.

[pause]

Many of the old farmhouses are empty, or gone. The little one-room schools have closed, and the children go by bus, to big consolidated schools in the towns. Some of the old schoolhouses have been preserved, as museums. Visitors can sit at the old wooden desks, and see the old blackboard, and the iron stove, and imagine the children of the 1930s, learning their lessons. Harlan is still there, with its courthouse square, and its brick buildings, and its shady streets. It's a quiet, friendly town, of about five thousand people. The old courthouse still stands, in the middle of the square, with its clock tower. Would you like to live out there, so far from town? On Saturday, the farm families still come into town, to shop, and to see their friends, although now they come in pickup trucks, not Model Ts.

[long-pause]

{img:87-today-small-iowa-town-courthouse-square-clock-tower-summer-evening}

Across Iowa, there are reminders of the Depression, and the New Deal.

[pause]

Parks, and lodges, and trails, built by the young men of the CCC, in the 1930s, of stone and wood. You could stop at an old stone shelter in a park and read the little sign. Many of them are still used, by families, for picnics, and walks, and camping. There are post offices, with murals, painted by artists who were given work by the government, in the Depression. Murals showing farmers, and corn, and the history of the towns. The county fairs still take place, every summer, in every county in Iowa. The 4-H children still show their calves, and their pigs, and their chickens, and get ribbons, just as Eddie did. There are still pie contests, and quilts, and canning, and the midway, with its rides, and its lights. And the Iowa State Fair, in Des Moines, is one of the biggest and most famous fairs in America. Every year, more than a million people come.

[long-pause]

{img:88-today-iowa-state-fair-crowds-ferris-wheel-livestock-barn-blue-ribbons}

In some parts of Iowa, the old prairie is being restored.

[pause]

Before the farmers came, Iowa was covered with tall grass prairie. You'd walk through tall grass and wildflowers up to your waist. Grasses as tall as a man, and wildflowers of every colour, stretching away for hundreds of miles. Almost all of it was ploughed up, for farming. Now, in some places, people are planting the prairie again, with the old grasses, and flowers. And the birds, and the butterflies, are coming back. And in many families, in Iowa, and all over America, the old stories are still told. About the Depression, and the dust storms, and the flour sack dresses, and the penny auctions, and the travellers at the back door. Some families still have an old quilt, made from flour sacks, by a grandmother, or a great-grandmother. Or an old red ribbon, from a county fair, in 1933. Or a little metal model of the Sky Ride, from the Chicago world's fair. Treasured, and kept, and handed down. If you drive along the gravel roads of Iowa today, on an autumn evening, between the golden cornfields, you might see an old white farmhouse, with a red barn, and a windmill, standing alone, as the sun goes down. And you might imagine a family, long ago, sitting on the porch, in the evening light, after a long day of work, grateful for what they had.

[long-pause]

{img:89-father-pipe-mother-mending-grandmother-rocking-chair-porch-dusk}

Walt sits in his chair, with his pipe, looking out at the fields.

[pause]

The corn harvest is almost done. Sit down on the step beside him, and you'll see what he sees. It has been a good year, in the end. Better than last year. Better than they had feared. Ruth sits beside him, with her mending basket, sewing a button onto Eddie's shirt, in the last of the light. Grandma Hattie rocks slowly in her rocking chair, with a shawl around her shoulders, humming an old hymn, very softly. June sits on the steps, with a book, reading, until it's too dark to see the words. Then she closes it, and just looks at the sky. Eddie sits beside her, with his arm around Rusty. He is tired, from helping with the corn. His hands are sore. But he is proud. His father said, today, that he did a man's work. He will remember that, all his life.

Up in the little bedrooms under the roof, June and Eddie are already asleep, and the farmhouse settles into the quiet of an Iowa night.
