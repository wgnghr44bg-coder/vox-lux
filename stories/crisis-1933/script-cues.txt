In the early 1930s, the United States fell into the worst economic crisis of its history. Banks closed, factories went silent, and prices on the farms collapsed. Yet in small towns and farmhouses across the Midwest, daily life carried on. Families mended, saved and shared, and listened to the radio in the evening. In this story, we will look at how ordinary people lived through the year 1933.

[long-pause]

In 1933, the middle of America was a sea of cornfields and small farm towns. Iowa lay in the broad, gently rolling heart of the country. Its cornfields stretched to the horizon under a wide open sky. Every ten miles or so stood a small town, with a main street, church steeples, a grain elevator and a water tower. The soil was rich and black, among the best farmland in the world. In summer, the corn grew taller than a man. In autumn, it turned golden brown and rustled in the wind. White farmhouses, red barns and turning windmills were scattered across the land. Long gravel roads ran straight between the fields. Yet by 1933, this peaceful landscape was caught in a national disaster. It was the time of the Great Depression, the worst economic crisis of the modern age. Banks, factories and shops had closed across the country. About one American worker in four had no job at all. Even in the quiet farm country, almost no family escaped its effects.

[long-pause]

{img:02-main-street-small-town-1933-closed-bank-sign-men-standing-waiting}

For farmers, the early 1930s were among the hardest years they had ever known. Farm prices fell so low that many families could no longer make a living. A bushel of corn that had once sold for close to a dollar now fetched as little as ten cents. In many cases, it cost more to grow a crop than it could be sold for. Some farmers burned corn in their stoves, because it was cheaper than buying coal. Debts were the greatest danger. Many farmers had borrowed money for land and machinery in better years. When prices collapsed, they could not keep up their payments. Thousands lost farms that their families had worked for generations. In some places, angry farmers gathered at foreclosure sales and tried to stop them. Others organised strikes and refused to send their produce to market. Yet most families simply worked harder and spent less. The crisis tested a way of life that had seemed secure for decades. It changed how a whole generation thought about money and land.

[long-pause]

{img:03-farm-family-1933-porch-father-overalls-mother-apron-girl-boy-grandmother}

In 1933, about a quarter of all Americans still lived on farms. Farming was not a small part of American life in the 1930s. It was the daily world of millions of families. A typical Midwestern farm covered about a hundred and sixty acres. It was usually run by a family of several generations living under one roof. The father worked the fields and cared for the larger animals. The mother ran the house, the garden and the chickens. Children helped with chores from an early age, before and after school. Grandparents often lived with the family and passed on skills and memories. Many of them had arrived as pioneers in covered wagons, when the land was still open prairie. That shared history mattered during the Depression. Older people had lived through droughts, grasshopper plagues and earlier collapses in prices. Their experience offered calm when younger people felt afraid. The farm family was therefore both a household and a small working business, held together by habit, faith and necessity.

[long-pause]

{img:04-family-gathered-around-radio-kitchen-lamp-evening-1933-listening}

Many people who lived through the Depression later remembered it as a time of great kindness. The Depression brought real hardship. Yet it also brought out generosity in many communities. Neighbours shared food, tools and labour. Churches collected clothing and organised meals. Families took in relatives who had lost their homes or jobs. Shopkeepers gave credit, often knowing it might never be repaid. People learned to be patient, careful and grateful for small things. In later years, many survivors described the period with mixed feelings. They remembered fear and worry, but also closeness and shared effort. Historians have collected hundreds of these memories through interviews and letters. They show that hardship did not affect everyone in the same way. Some families suffered terribly, while others managed to hold on. Even so, a common thread runs through many accounts. People who lived through those years often said the experience taught them what really mattered. For the rest of their lives, many of them saved string, avoided debt and never wasted food.

[long-pause]

{img:05-new-york-stock-exchange-1929-crowds-wall-street-worried-men-hats}

The roots of the Great Depression lay in the booming years of the 1920s. For many Americans, the 1920s were a time of growth and optimism. People called them the Roaring Twenties. Jobs were plentiful in the cities, and wages were rising. New products filled the shops, from cars and radios to refrigerators and washing machines. Jazz, dancing and films became part of everyday life. Many people began to buy shares in companies on the stock market in New York. Share prices rose year after year, and many investors believed they would keep rising. Ordinary people, from shopkeepers to teachers, borrowed money to buy shares. They hoped to grow rich quickly and easily. Some did, at least for a while. Yet the prosperity was uneven. Farmers had struggled with low prices for most of the decade. Many workers earned too little to buy all the goods being produced. Beneath the excitement, the economy rested on borrowed money and confidence. Both could disappear very quickly.

[long-pause]

{img:06-newspaper-headline-1929-crash-man-reading-on-street-corner-shocked}

In October 1929, the American stock market crashed. Share prices began to fall in the autumn of 1929. At first the decline was slow, then it became faster. Investors panicked and tried to sell their shares all at once. On the twenty-ninth of October, a day remembered as Black Tuesday, the market collapsed. Billions of dollars in value disappeared within hours. Many people lost everything they had invested, including money they had borrowed. Yet the crash was only the beginning. Over the next few years, the economy spiralled downward. People had less money, so they bought less. Factories produced less and needed fewer workers. Unemployed workers then had even less to spend. It was like a wheel turning downward, faster and faster. Economists still debate exactly why the downturn became so severe. Bank failures, falling trade and government policies all played a part. What is clear is that a crisis on Wall Street became a crisis in almost every American town.

[long-pause]

{img:07-people-lined-up-outside-closed-bank-1931-small-town-anxious-crowd}

Between 1930 and 1933, thousands of American banks failed. When people grew afraid, they rushed to their banks to withdraw their savings. Yet banks did not keep all their deposits in the vault. Much of the money had been lent to others. When too many people asked for their money at once, a bank could run out of cash and close its doors. Its depositors then lost their savings. In those days, there was no national insurance to protect them. Small towns suffered especially badly. A local bank might hold the savings of almost every family in the district. The money of a lifetime could vanish in a single morning. On the farms, trouble had begun even earlier. Many farmers had borrowed heavily when prices were high during and after the Great War. Now they could not repay their loans, and their failures weakened the rural banks further. By 1932, despair was spreading across the countryside. Shanty towns built from boxes and scrap metal appeared near the cities. People bitterly called them Hoovervilles, after President Herbert Hoover.

[long-pause]

{img:08-shanty-town-hooverville-tin-shacks-river-bank-smoke-cooking-fires}

In November 1932, Americans elected Franklin Delano Roosevelt as their new president. Roosevelt had been governor of New York. He came from a wealthy family, and as a young man he had been struck by polio. The illness left him unable to walk without help. Yet he was cheerful and confident, and he promised the American people a New Deal. Few people knew exactly what that would mean. But many hoped it meant change. Roosevelt won by a large margin. In those days, a new president did not take office until March. The country therefore waited through four long months. That winter was the worst of the Depression. More banks failed, and more people lost their jobs. In many cities, soup kitchens and breadlines grew longer. Hoovervilles of tin and timber spread along riverbanks and railway lines. Smoke from cooking fires rose over them on cold mornings. The long wait showed the danger of a slow handover of power. A later change to the Constitution moved the start of a presidential term to January.

[long-pause]

{img:09-iowa-farmer-1933-overalls-straw-hat-leaning-on-fence-cornfield-thoughtful}

Many Iowa farmers in 1933 worked land their fathers had bought when it was still open prairie. The farms of Iowa were often only one or two generations old. Settlers had bought them in the late nineteenth century, when tall prairie grass still covered the land. By 1933, many sons farmed the same quarter section their fathers had broken with a plough. They knew every field, fence post and corner of it. A typical farm grew corn, oats and hay, and raised hogs and a few cattle. Horses still pulled ploughs and wagons on most farms. Some farmers had bought tractors on credit during the good years of the 1920s. When prices collapsed, many could barely afford the fuel. Tractors stood idle in sheds, and horses returned to the fields. Horses had one great advantage. They ate oats grown on the farm itself, while tractors needed fuel bought with cash. During the Depression, that difference mattered enormously. Older methods returned because they cost less, and many farmers were glad to fall back on them.

[long-pause]

{img:10-farm-wife-1933-flour-sack-apron-feeding-chickens-yard-morning-sun}

On many farms, the wife's eggs and butter brought in the only steady cash. Farm wives did far more than housework. They cooked, cleaned, washed, sewed and mended. They also kept large vegetable gardens and flocks of chickens. Eggs and butter could be sold or traded in town every week. In the worst years, this small income often paid for flour, sugar, coffee and salt. Historians call it the egg money, and it kept many households afloat. Farm women were also skilled managers. They planned meals around what the garden produced. They stored food for winter and found uses for every scrap. Many of them had grown up on farms themselves and had learned these skills as girls. Daughters helped from an early age, before and after school. Some dreamed of becoming teachers, writers or nurses in the cities. The Depression made such dreams harder to reach. Yet it also showed how much depended on women's work. Without it, many farm families could not have survived those years.

[long-pause]

{img:11-boy-nine-overalls-barefoot-with-farm-dog-collie-barn-door-summer}

Farm children in the 1930s did real work from an early age. On a family farm, every pair of hands mattered. Boys of eight or nine fed animals, carried water and brought in wood for the stove. Girls helped with cooking, washing and caring for younger children. Both worked in the fields at harvest time. Chores came before school in the morning and again after school in the evening. Yet farm childhood was not only work. Children roamed the fields with their dogs, swam in creeks and climbed trees. They played baseball with home-made balls and invented games of their own. Grandparents often lived with the family and told stories of earlier hardships. Many had seen droughts, blizzards and grasshopper plagues. They remembered the hard years of the 1890s, when prices also collapsed. Their message to the young was often the same. The country had come through hard times before, and it would do so again. That long view gave families patience in a time when the future seemed uncertain.

[long-pause]

{img:12-farmhouse-kitchen-1933-cast-iron-stove-oilcloth-table-calendar-wall}

During the Depression, many farm incomes fell to a small fraction of what they had been. In good years, a Midwestern farm might earn a thousand dollars or more. By 1932, many earned only a few hundred, and some earned almost nothing. After mortgage payments and taxes, little or no money remained. There was nothing for new clothes, shoes or winter coats. Visits to the dentist or the doctor were put off for as long as possible. Families spent money only on what was absolutely necessary. Clothes were worn until they fell apart, then mended and worn again. String, paper, buttons, old nails and bits of wood were all saved. A popular saying captured the spirit of the time. Use it up, wear it out, make it do, or do without. Yet farm families had one important advantage over many city workers. They could grow most of their own food. Money was scarce, but hunger was less common on working farms than in the cities. What they lacked was cash, not bread.

[long-pause]

{img:13-farm-dawn-1933-farmer-with-lantern-walking-to-barn-frost-windmill}

In 1933, only about one American farm in ten had electricity. Power lines had reached most American cities by the 1930s. Yet the countryside was largely left in the dark. Electric companies considered it too expensive to run lines to scattered farms. As a result, most farm families lived much as their grandparents had. They lit kerosene lamps in the evening and cooked on wood or coal stoves. They pumped water by hand or relied on windmills. Work began before dawn. Farmers rose around five o'clock, dressed in the dark and lit the kitchen lamp. Then they carried lanterns to the barn for the morning chores. The lack of electricity also meant no refrigerators, no electric irons and no washing machines. Farm work was therefore physically harder than city work in almost every way. This gap became a major political issue. In 1935, a new federal agency began to bring power to rural America. Within a generation, most farms were connected.

[long-pause]

{img:14-father-and-son-milking-cows-by-lantern-light-barn-stalls-steam-breath}

On Midwestern farms, hogs turned corn into something worth more than corn. Morning chores began in the barn. Horses were fed oats and hay. Cows were milked by hand into tin buckets, a task done twice every day. Barn cats often gathered nearby, hoping for a little warm milk. Then the hogs were fed, usually with corn, skim milk and kitchen scraps. Hogs were among the most important animals on many Iowa farms. Farmers often said that a hog was simply corn on four legs. Feeding corn to hogs turned a bulky, cheap crop into pork, which could be sold for more. This system had made the Corn Belt prosperous in earlier decades. During the Depression, however, even pork prices collapsed. In 1933, the government took a drastic step to raise prices. It paid farmers to slaughter millions of young pigs. Many people were shocked that food was destroyed while others went hungry. Some of the meat was given to families on relief, but the programme remained deeply controversial.

[long-pause]

{img:15-farm-kitchen-breakfast-1933-pancakes-eggs-bacon-family-at-table-lamp}

Farm breakfasts in the 1930s were large, because the working day was long and hard. Breakfast on a Midwestern farm was a substantial meal. It often included eggs from the farm's own chickens and bacon from its own hogs. There might be pancakes, biscuits or fried potatoes, with fresh milk and strong coffee. Bread was baked at home, usually once or twice a week. Water came from a hand pump at the sink or in the yard. Many families said grace before eating, thanking God for food and asking for help in hard times. After breakfast, the day's work began. It followed the rhythm of the seasons. Spring brought ploughing and planting. Summer meant cultivating corn and cutting hay and oats. Autumn was harvest time. Winter was spent mending fences, tools and harness, and cutting firewood. All year round, animals had to be fed and milked morning and evening. Farm life left little room for rest. Yet a good breakfast gave families the strength for days that often lasted from dawn until dark.

[long-pause]

{img:16-farmer-ploughing-field-team-of-horses-spring-1933-black-soil-gulls}

A farm wife's week in the 1930s followed a fixed pattern of washing, ironing and baking. Housework on a farm without electricity was heavy labour. Monday was usually washing day. Water was heated on the stove and clothes were scrubbed on a washboard in a large tub. Tuesday was for ironing, with heavy irons warmed on the same stove. Other days brought baking, cleaning, churning butter and making soap from fat and lye. In summer, gardens had to be weeded and vegetables preserved for winter. Grandmothers and daughters often shared the work. Out in the fields, men ploughed behind teams of horses, turning the black soil in long straight furrows. Gulls and blackbirds followed the plough, searching for worms. Every task took time, strength and skill. Historians estimate that farm women in this period often worked longer hours than anyone else in the household. Yet their labour rarely appeared in official statistics. It was simply expected. In the evening, families often sat on the porch to rest as the sun went down over the fields.

[long-pause]

{img:17-harlan-iowa-1933-main-street-brick-shops-model-a-fords-courthouse-square}

Saturday was town day for farm families across the Midwest. On Saturday afternoons, farm families travelled into the nearest town. Many drove old Model T Fords, bought second-hand years earlier. Others still came by horse and wagon. Small towns such as Harlan, Iowa, with about three thousand people, served as centres for the surrounding countryside. Many had a courthouse in the middle of a square, with a clock tower, lawns and benches under tall elm trees. Around the square stood shops and offices in brick buildings two or three storeys high. There was usually a grocery, a hardware store, a drugstore, a bank, a barber and a café. Larger towns also had a movie theatre. Churches with white steeples stood on the side streets. The weekly trip was more than shopping. It was the main social event of the week. For people who spent most days alone in the fields, town offered news, conversation and company. Even in hard times, Saturday in town remained a cherished routine.

[long-pause]

{img:18-general-store-interior-1933-shelves-cans-barrels-counter-farmers-wives-trading}

In the Depression, many rural shops survived by trading goods and giving credit. On Saturdays, small-town streets filled with wagons and old cars. Farm wives brought eggs and butter to the grocery store. Instead of cash, they often traded them directly for flour, sugar, coffee and salt. Grocers also gave credit to families they knew. Many kept long lists of debts that might never be paid. Refusing credit could mean that a neighbour's children went hungry, and most shopkeepers found that hard to do. Men gathered on street corners in overalls and hats. They talked about the weather, crops, prices and the latest farm foreclosures. The mood was often gloomy, yet humour remained. Jokes about the Depression were common in newspapers and on the radio. Farmers sometimes said they had been in a depression for years before the cities noticed. There was truth in that remark. Farm prices had been weak for most of the 1920s. The national crisis simply made an old rural problem far worse.

[long-pause]

{img:19-children-outside-drugstore-soda-fountain-window-1933-looking-in-nickels}

For many children in the 1930s, a nickel at the soda fountain was the best treat of the week. Saturday in town was a highlight for farm children. Some visited the small public library to borrow books. Others met friends on the square to play marbles or talk about baseball. If they were lucky, a parent gave them a nickel. Many spent it at the drugstore soda fountain, on an ice cream cone or a bottle of soda. Drugstore counters were popular meeting places in small towns across America. Yet signs of hard times were everywhere. Some shops stood empty, with dusty windows and signs reading closed or for rent. Unemployed men stood on corners with nothing to do. Churches saw more people asking for help. In many towns, the bank building itself had become a reminder of loss. Thousands of rural banks had failed since 1930. Their stone columns and locked doors stood on the square for everyone to see. For a whole generation, a simple nickel came to represent real value.

[long-pause]

{img:20-shuttered-bank-building-stone-columns-notice-on-door-pigeons-quiet-square}

When a small-town bank failed, many families lost savings they had built up over decades. Bank failures struck rural America especially hard. In 1931 alone, more than two thousand banks closed across the country. In a small town, depositors often learned the news in the simplest way. They arrived in the morning and found the doors locked and a notice posted on them. Savings set aside for education, emergencies or old age disappeared overnight. Some depositors later recovered part of their money, but often years later and only in part. Many never recovered anything. The emotional effect was lasting. People who had trusted banks all their lives became suspicious of them. Some kept their money hidden at home for decades afterwards. Older relatives often offered a different kind of comfort. They said that knowledge and skills could not be taken away, unlike money. Families who had lost savings often became even more determined to educate their children. The empty bank on the square remained a quiet symbol of the crisis long after it ended.

[long-pause]

{img:21-fdr-inauguration-march-1933-capitol-steps-crowd-grey-sky-washington}

On the fourth of March, 1933, Franklin Delano Roosevelt became president of the United States. It was a cold, grey day in Washington. A large crowd gathered in front of the Capitol to watch the new president take the oath of office. Across the country, millions of people listened on the radio. The nation was in a dangerous state that morning. In the weeks before, banking panic had spread from state to state. Depositors rushed to withdraw their money, afraid that more banks would fail. Governors responded by ordering banks to close or to limit withdrawals. By the day of the inauguration, almost every bank in America was closed or restricted. The financial system of the country had nearly stopped. Business could barely function, and many people had no access to their money at all. Few presidents have taken office at a more anxious moment. The atmosphere was tense and uncertain. Everyone waited to hear what the new leader would say, and whether he had any real plan to stop the collapse.

[long-pause]

{img:22-family-listening-to-radio-kitchen-march-1933-faces-hopeful-quiet}

In his first inaugural address, Roosevelt said that the only thing to fear was fear itself. Roosevelt stood at the front of the platform, holding the arm of his son for support. Polio had left his legs weak, and he could not stand unaided. He took the oath and then began his speech. His voice was strong, clear and confident. Early in the address came the sentence that people remembered for the rest of their lives. The only thing we have to fear, he said, is fear itself. He spoke of nameless, unreasoning terror that paralysed efforts to recover. He promised action, and quickly. In farmhouses and city apartments, people listened in silence. Many later recalled the speech as a turning point in their feelings. Nothing had yet changed in the economy. Yet the tone of the government had changed. After years of discouraging news, a leader seemed ready to act. Confidence matters a great deal in a financial crisis. Roosevelt's words helped to restore some of it, even before his policies took effect.

[long-pause]

{img:23-closed-bank-doors-notice-bank-holiday-march-1933-townspeople-reading}

Two days after taking office, Roosevelt closed every bank in the country for a national bank holiday. On the sixth of March, 1933, Roosevelt ordered all American banks to close. He called it a bank holiday, though it was nothing of the kind. The pause gave the government time to inspect the banks. Sound banks would be allowed to reopen. Weak ones would be helped, reorganised or closed for good. Congress quickly passed an emergency law to support the plan. For about a week, very little money moved through the country. Shops gave credit or accepted written promises. Some towns printed their own temporary money on paper, wood or even leather. Farmers traded eggs for flour and chickens for shoes. Then, on the twelfth of March, Roosevelt spoke to the nation on the radio. He explained in simple words how banks worked and why they had failed. He told listeners that the reopened banks would be safe. It was a calm, direct talk, almost like a conversation, and it reached millions of homes.

[long-pause]

{img:24-people-queuing-to-deposit-money-reopened-bank-march-1933-relieved-faces}

When the banks reopened in March 1933, people lined up to deposit money rather than withdraw it. Millions of Americans heard Roosevelt's radio talk on the banks, and many believed him. When the banks reopened the next day, the feared rush did not come. Instead, people began bringing money back. Within weeks, deposits exceeded withdrawals, and the panic was over. This was the first of many radio talks Roosevelt gave over the next twelve years. They became known as the fireside chats. Their simple, friendly tone helped him connect with ordinary listeners in a new way. Later in 1933, Congress passed a law creating federal insurance for bank deposits. From the beginning of 1934, ordinary savings were protected even if a bank failed. That protection still exists today. It came too late for those who had already lost their savings. Yet it transformed public trust in banks. Waves of bank failures had been common in American history. After deposit insurance was introduced, such panics became far less frequent.

[long-pause]

{img:25-wooden-cathedral-radio-1933-kitchen-shelf-battery-glowing-dial}

In the 1930s, the radio became the most precious possession in many American homes. Many radios of the period were large wooden sets with rounded tops. Their shape reminded people of church windows, and they were often called cathedral radios. On farms without electricity, radios ran on heavy batteries. These had to be taken to town to be recharged every few weeks. Families therefore listened carefully and saved the battery for important programmes. Many radios had been bought during the prosperous 1920s, when credit was easy. During the Depression, few families could have afforded a new one. Yet those who had a set rarely gave it up. Radio became a window on the world. It brought news from Washington, New York and Europe. It carried farm prices, weather forecasts, music, comedy and drama. By the late 1930s, most American households owned a radio. Historians often describe the decade as the golden age of radio. For isolated rural families, the impact was especially great. The wider world suddenly entered their kitchens every evening.

[long-pause]

{img:26-father-adjusting-radio-dial-family-gathered-close-evening-lamplight}

Farmers relied on daily radio market reports to follow prices for corn, wheat and hogs. Agricultural radio programmes became essential in the 1920s and 1930s. Stations across the Midwest broadcast market reports at midday. An announcer read out the prices for grain and livestock in Chicago and other markets. Weather forecasts followed, which were vital for planting and harvesting. Many farmers came in from the fields at noon to listen while they ate. The reports helped them decide when to sell and when to wait. In the evening, families often gathered around the set for an hour or two. Chairs were pulled close, because battery radios were not very loud. Older relatives might knit or mend while listening. Children often sat on the floor, as near to the speaker as possible. Listening together became a family ritual in millions of homes. It replaced older evening habits such as reading aloud. Yet it served a similar purpose. At the end of a hard day, it gave families a shared moment of rest and attention.

[long-pause]

{img:27-children-sitting-on-floor-close-to-radio-rapt-attention-dog-by-stove}

Radio drama in the 1930s depended on sound effects and the listener's imagination. Comedy shows were among the most popular programmes of the decade. Comic duos and quarrelling married couples made millions of listeners laugh each week. Music filled many evening hours, from dance bands in big-city hotels to country songs and church hymns. On clear nights, distant stations could be heard across hundreds of miles. Children loved adventure serials. One famous programme began on a Detroit station in 1933. It told of a masked rider of the old West who fought for justice with a faithful companion and a white horse. Each episode opened with stirring music and galloping hooves. Radio dramas used sound effects to create entire worlds. Studio technicians made doors creak, footsteps echo and thunder roll. Horses were often imitated with coconut shells. Many listeners said radio was better than the movies, because they could imagine the pictures themselves. That remark captures something important. Radio asked its audience to take part, and millions gladly did.

[long-pause]

{img:28-radio-listening-whole-family-laughing-comedy-show-kitchen-warm-night}

Radio gave Americans in the Depression a shared national experience. By the 1930s, national networks broadcast the same programmes across the country. Families in farmhouses, city apartments and small-town homes listened at the same time. They laughed at the same jokes and followed the same news. This was something new in American life. Before radio, regional differences in daily experience had been much stronger. During the Depression, this shared listening had special value. It made people feel less alone in their difficulties. Comedy offered relief, and music offered comfort. Presidential talks gave reassurance and a sense of direction. Advertisers also discovered radio's power. Many programmes were sponsored by soap and food companies, which is why later dramas came to be called soap operas. When the evening's programmes ended, sets were switched off and the glowing dials slowly faded. Homes became quiet again. Yet the experience left a lasting mark. Radio helped to create a common culture that later television would continue.

[long-pause]

{img:29-mother-sewing-dress-from-printed-flour-sacks-treadle-machine-kitchen}

Flour and feed companies began printing their cotton sacks with pretty patterns, because women made clothes from them. In the Depression, nothing was wasted. Farm wives had long made towels, aprons and pillowcases from plain cotton flour sacks. As money grew scarce, they used the sacks for clothing as well. Manufacturers noticed this habit and saw an opportunity. By the late 1930s, many companies sold flour and animal feed in sacks printed with flowers, checks and stripes. Women chose their flour partly by the pattern on the sack. When they had collected enough sacks of the same design, they could make a dress or a shirt. Some sacks even came with printed patterns for dolls or quilts. Many women sewed on treadle machines powered by their feet, since farms often had no electricity. The practice continued well into the 1940s. Today, surviving feed sack dresses are collected by museums. They show how ordinary families turned necessity into something practical and even beautiful. Businesses, in turn, learned to follow what their customers were really doing.

[long-pause]

{img:30-girl-in-flour-sack-print-dress-twirling-farmyard-proud-smile}

In rural schools of the 1930s, many children wore clothes sewn from flour and feed sacks. Flour sack clothing became common across the American countryside. Girls wore dresses with little flowers, checks or daisies. Boys wore shirts made from feed sacks. Babies wore diapers made from sugar sacks. Because so many families used them, there was little shame attached to them. Many people remembered them later with affection. Clothes that wore out were mended rather than replaced. Patches covered holes at the elbows and knees. Socks were darned again and again. Collars were turned so that the worn side was hidden. Coats outgrown by one child were cut down and remade for a younger brother or sister. When clothes could no longer be worn, they were cut into strips. The strips were braided into rugs or sewn into quilts. Quilting bees brought women together to share work and conversation. Many quilts from this period survive today. Their patchwork patterns preserve tiny pieces of everyday clothing from an entire generation.

[long-pause]

{img:31-women-quilting-bee-large-quilt-frame-church-basement-1933-talking}

During the Depression, many families preserved hundreds of jars of vegetables and fruit each summer. Food was never wasted in the 1930s. Leftovers were eaten the next day, turned into soup or fed to the hogs. Bread was baked at home, butter was churned and soap was made from fat and lye. In summer, women spent weeks canning food from their gardens. Beans, tomatoes, corn, peas, pickles, peaches and jam were sealed in glass jars. Hundreds of jars were stored on cellar shelves for the winter. Government extension agents taught safe canning methods in rural communities. Many town families also planted gardens in backyards and empty lots. City dwellers grew vegetables on rooftops and in window boxes. Meals were simple, built around beans, potatoes, cornbread and soup. Some dishes were made from almost nothing, such as bread with gravy or fried cornmeal mush. Things that broke were repaired with wire, nails and patience. Shoes were resoled with pieces of old tyres. The Depression made thrift a skill, and many families practised it for the rest of their lives.

[long-pause]

{img:32-farmer-repairing-shoes-old-tyre-rubber-workbench-shed-lantern-evening}

Depression-era children made many of their own toys from scraps. Few families could afford shop-bought toys in the 1930s. Children therefore made their own. Kites were built from newspaper, sticks and string. Slingshots came from forked branches and old inner tubes. Baseballs were made from tightly wound string covered with tape. Girls cut paper dolls from old mail-order catalogues and made paper clothes for them. Corncobs became dolls, and empty thread spools became spinning tops. Tyre swings hung from the branches of farmyard trees. Marbles were a favourite game, and a good set of marbles was a real treasure. Many adults who grew up in the Depression later remembered these simple games with pleasure. They also carried lifelong habits from those years. They saved string, paper bags, rubber bands and old jars. Their grandchildren sometimes found these habits strange. Yet they reflected experience rather than eccentricity. People who had once lived with very little never quite forgot how valuable small things could be.

[long-pause]

{img:33-hobos-riding-freight-train-boxcar-open-door-prairie-1933-young-men}

During the Depression, around a quarter of a million young Americans travelled the country by freight train. The roads and railways of the 1930s carried an unusual kind of traveller. They were not tourists, but people searching for work, food or a new beginning. Most were men, though some were women, and many were teenagers. Some left home simply so that their families would have one less mouth to feed. Many rode freight trains, climbing into empty boxcars or onto the roofs. It was free, but it was also dangerous, uncomfortable and illegal. Railway police chased them away, and thousands were injured or killed in accidents. Such travellers were called hobos, tramps or transients. Near the tracks outside many towns were camps known as jungles. Travellers rested there, washed, cooked and shared news. Stews were made in old tin cans from whatever people could contribute. A potato, an onion, perhaps a little meat. The camps had unwritten rules about sharing, cleanliness and respect. Even among the poorest, a sense of order and fairness often survived.

[long-pause]

{img:34-stranger-at-farmhouse-back-door-hat-in-hand-farm-wife-offering-plate}

Travellers often knocked at farmhouse doors, offering to work in exchange for a meal. Farms near railway lines saw many travellers during the Depression. Men walked up the lane, tired and dusty, and knocked politely at the back door. Most asked not for charity, but for work. In return for chopping wood, mending a fence or cleaning a barn, they hoped for a plate of food. Many farm families agreed, even when they had little to spare. Bread, beans and coffee were common offerings. The travellers came from every background. There were carpenters from Chicago, factory workers from Detroit and young men from farms that had been lost. Some had been teachers or clerks before the crash. Accounts from the period describe most of them as polite, grateful and honest. They were ordinary people who had fallen on hard times. After eating, they usually thanked the family and walked back to the railway. Such meetings left deep impressions. Many children of the period later remembered these strangers at the back door for the rest of their lives.

[long-pause]

{img:35-chalk-symbol-on-fence-post-hobo-sign-cat-drawing-farm-gate}

Hobos were said to use a secret code of chalk signs to tell each other which houses were friendly. Stories about a hidden language of signs were common in the 1930s. According to these accounts, travellers drew small symbols on fences, gateposts and walls. Each sign carried a message for those who came later. One might warn of a dangerous dog. Another might mean that local police arrested travellers. Some signs meant that a farmer would give work. A simple drawing of a cat was said to mean that a kind woman lived there. Historians are not certain how widely these signs were really used. Some lists of symbols were probably collected or embellished later by writers and journalists. Yet many travellers did describe marking places in this way. The idea itself reveals something important. People on the road depended on each other for information. A small chalk mark could mean a meal, a night of shelter or a warning. In a world without telephones, a gatepost could carry news from one stranger to the next.

[long-pause]

{img:36-young-traveller-chopping-wood-farmyard-boy-watching-afternoon-light}

Many young drifters of the 1930s came from farms that their own families had lost. Among those on the road were many teenagers from the countryside. Some came from Nebraska, Kansas or the Dakotas, where drought and debt had ruined farms. When the bank took the land, older children often left to seek work elsewhere. Some were only fifteen or sixteen. Farmers sometimes hired them for a week during planting or harvest. They slept in barns, ate with the family and were paid a dollar or two if money allowed. Many farm parents saw their own children in these young travellers. They hoped their sons and daughters would never face the same journey. Reports from the time describe young drifters as thin, tired and often shy. Many sent letters home whenever they could. Government agencies grew concerned about the number of young people on the road. That concern helped shape programmes created in 1933 to give young men work and shelter. For many, those programmes offered the first steady place to stay in months.

[long-pause]

{img:37-farm-auction-1933-crowd-of-farmers-in-farmyard-auctioneer-on-wagon}

In 1932 and 1933, thousands of Midwestern farm families lost their land at foreclosure auctions. When farmers could not pay their mortgages or taxes, lenders could take the farm and sell it at auction. Banks and insurance companies held many of these loans. Everything could be sold, including the land, the house, the animals, the machinery and the furniture. Families who had worked the same land for fifty years or more had to watch their possessions sold to strangers. Neighbours often came to these auctions, and many were angry. They felt that the families had done nothing wrong. They had worked hard and lost everything to forces beyond their control. In some places, farmers began to resist in an unusual way. They organised what became known as penny auctions. When a farm was put up for sale, large crowds of neighbours gathered around the auctioneer. They stood silently together. When the bidding began, they made it clear that nobody should offer more than a few pennies. Outside bidders were quietly discouraged from taking part.

[long-pause]

{img:38-farmers-standing-silently-together-auction-crowd-arms-folded-serious}

At penny auctions, neighbours bought a family's possessions for a few cents and then gave them back. At a penny auction, a horse might sell for ten cents and a cow for five. A plough could go for a quarter. The buyers were the family's own neighbours. When the sale was over, they returned everything to its original owners. The lender received only a few dollars. The family kept its animals and tools, and sometimes even its farm. The method was not strictly illegal, but it was certainly unwelcome to banks and insurance companies. It depended entirely on community pressure. Outsiders who might have bid more were made to understand that they were not welcome. Penny auctions spread through Iowa, Nebraska and other farm states in 1932 and 1933. Newspapers reported on them across the country. They showed how far rural communities were willing to go to protect their own. They also revealed how deeply many farmers distrusted distant financial institutions. For a short time, a silent crowd could be more powerful than a mortgage.

[long-pause]

{img:39-old-farmer-and-wife-standing-on-porch-watching-auction-neighbours-crowd}

Penny auctions were usually quiet, orderly and deeply emotional. Accounts of penny auctions often describe the same scene. Dozens of trucks and wagons lined the lane. Hundreds of farmers stood in the yard in overalls and hats. They were quiet and serious. Nobody shouted or caused trouble. They simply stood with folded arms and watched. The auctioneer climbed onto a wagon and described the first animal for sale. Silence followed. Then one farmer offered five cents. The auctioneer asked for more, but nobody spoke. After a pause, he declared the animal sold. The same thing happened again and again throughout the morning. The family often watched from the porch. At first, some did not understand what was happening. When they did, many were overcome. Afterwards, neighbours came forward one by one to shake hands and offer a few words. Then they drove home. These events showed the strength of rural communities. They also showed that even in desperate times, many farmers preferred quiet solidarity to violence.

[long-pause]

{img:40-neighbours-shaking-hands-on-porch-after-auction-relief-gratitude-spring}

In April 1933, the governor of Iowa sent troops into farm country after angry farmers attacked a judge. Penny auctions did not save every farm. In some places, anger turned into violence. In April 1933, a crowd of farmers near Le Mars, Iowa, dragged a judge from his courtroom. He had refused to stop hearing foreclosure cases. They threatened him with a rope before letting him go. The governor responded by declaring martial law in several counties and sending in the National Guard. Most farmers did not approve of such actions. They wanted justice, but peacefully. Meanwhile, state and federal governments began to change the rules. Several states passed laws that delayed foreclosures. In 1933, Congress created new programmes to refinance farm mortgages at lower interest. Many families were able to keep their land as a result. The crisis had shown how close parts of rural America had come to open rebellion. Lawmakers took notice. Over the following years, farm debt relief became a lasting part of national policy.

[long-pause]

{img:41-one-room-country-schoolhouse-1933-white-wooden-bell-tower-children-walking}

In the 1930s, Iowa still had thousands of one-room country schools. Rural America in the 1930s was dotted with small schoolhouses. Iowa alone had several thousand of them, roughly one for every few square miles of farmland. A typical school was a small white wooden building standing alone at a crossroads. It often had a bell tower, a porch, a pump and two outhouses at the back. Children walked to school along gravel roads, often a mile or two each way. They carried lunch pails, which were often old tin buckets that had once held lard or syrup. Inside might be a sandwich of homemade bread, an apple or a cookie, and a small jar of milk. In deep winter snow, some fathers drove their children to school by horse-drawn sleigh. These schools were the heart of many rural districts. They hosted meetings, plays and holiday programmes. Over the following decades, most were closed and replaced by larger consolidated schools. Many of the old buildings still stand in fields today.

[long-pause]

{img:42-schoolroom-interior-1933-wooden-desks-pot-bellied-stove-blackboard-teacher}

In a one-room school, one teacher taught every grade at the same time. Inside the typical country school was a single large room. At the front stood the teacher's desk, a blackboard and a map of the world. A portrait of George Washington often hung on the wall. Rows of wooden desks had inkwells and hinged lids. In the middle of the room stood an iron stove that heated the school in winter. At the back were hooks for coats, a shelf for lunch pails and a water bucket with a dipper. A school might have fifteen or twenty pupils, aged from six to fourteen. One teacher taught all of them. While the youngest learned to read, older pupils worked on arithmetic or writing. Then the roles were reversed. Older children often helped the younger ones. Educators later recognised the value of this arrangement. Younger pupils heard advanced lessons, while older ones reinforced their knowledge by teaching. It was a demanding system, yet many former pupils remembered it with great fondness.

[long-pause]

{img:43-young-woman-teacher-1933-helping-small-child-reading-older-girl-assisting}

During the Depression, many rural teachers worked for reduced pay, and some were paid in promises. Country school teachers in the 1930s were often young women in their early twenties. Many boarded with local farm families. They arrived early each morning to light the stove and sweep the floor before the pupils came. As tax revenues collapsed, school boards cut salaries sharply. A teacher might earn forty dollars a month for eight months of the year. Some districts could not pay at all and issued written promises instead. In parts of the country, schools closed completely for lack of money. In many farming communities, however, families were determined to keep their schools open. They brought teachers food, firewood and whatever else they could spare. The school day usually began at nine o'clock with the ringing of a bell. Pupils recited the Pledge of Allegiance and often sang a song. Lessons covered reading, writing, arithmetic, spelling, geography and history. Friday spelling bees were a favourite event. For many rural children, the teacher was the most educated person they knew.

[long-pause]

{img:44-schoolchildren-playing-recess-schoolyard-ball-game-fields-around-1933}

In Depression-era schools, teachers often quietly made sure that hungry children were fed. Recess was an important part of the country school day. Children played baseball, tag and hide and seek in the schoolyard. A popular game called Annie Over involved throwing a ball over the school roof to a team on the other side. In winter, pupils built snowmen, held snowball fights and slid on frozen ponds. At lunchtime, they ate on the steps or around the stove and swapped sandwiches. Yet poverty was visible in every classroom. Some children came from families that had lost their farms. They wore thin, worn clothes and broken shoes. Some arrived with no lunch at all. Many teachers noticed and acted quietly. They brought extra food or asked other children to share. In some areas, the government later supported school lunch programmes. Surplus farm products were used to feed hungry pupils. These efforts laid the foundations for the national school lunch programme created after the Depression. It still feeds millions of children today.

[long-pause]

{img:45-white-country-church-1933-steeple-cars-and-wagons-parked-sunday-morning}

In rural America, the country church was the centre of community life. On Sunday mornings, farm families across the Midwest drove to church. Many churches were simple white wooden buildings with tall steeples, standing at the edge of small villages. Families often attended the same church for generations. Grandparents had been married there, and children were baptised there. Methodist, Lutheran, Catholic and Baptist congregations were all common, often reflecting the origins of local settlers. On Sundays, people wore their best clothes, which were often their only good clothes. Men wore dark suits, a little shiny with age. Women wore their best dresses and hats. Children were scrubbed clean, with hair combed flat. The service included hymns, a sermon and prayers. Church bells could be heard across the fields from miles away. In the Depression, attendance in many rural churches remained strong. Religion offered comfort, meaning and a sense of continuity. The church was also one of the few places where scattered farm families regularly met.

[long-pause]

{img:46-church-basement-potluck-supper-long-tables-casseroles-pies-families-1933}

Church potluck suppers allowed poor families to eat well without feeling ashamed. After the service, families often stood outside and talked. These conversations were almost as important as the service itself. People shared news about illness, births, lost farms and new jobs. Above all, they learned who needed help. Churches organised much of the charity in rural communities during the Depression. One popular tradition was the potluck supper, usually held in the church basement. Every family brought a dish. There might be casseroles, salads, baked beans, fried chicken, potatoes, bread, pies and cakes. The food was laid out on long tables, and everyone helped themselves. The name came from the idea that nobody knew in advance what they would get. Its great value lay in its fairness. Wealthier families might bring more, and poorer families less. Yet everyone ate as much as they needed. Nobody asked who had brought what. In this way, the potluck shared food without embarrassment, and many such suppers continue in American churches today.

[long-pause]

{img:47-barn-raising-neighbours-lifting-wall-frame-together-ropes-summer-1933}

When a farmer's barn burned down, neighbours often built a new one together in a day or two. Rural communities depended on shared work. When a farmer was ill or injured, neighbours often did his chores and brought in his crops. They did this without being asked and without being paid. When a barn burned down, neighbours gathered for a barn raising. Men prepared timber, raised the frames with ropes and poles, and nailed everything into place. Women cooked a great feast for all the workers. A barn that would take one family months could be built by a community in a day or two. Women also had their own gatherings. Quilting bees brought groups together around a large wooden frame. They sewed, talked, drank coffee and ate cake. Quilts made from scraps and flour sacks took weeks to finish. They were often given to brides, new mothers or families who had lost everything. Such traditions were already old in the 1930s. During the Depression, they became more important than ever.

[long-pause]

{img:48-women-delivering-food-basket-to-poor-family-farmhouse-door-kind}

Church women's groups organised much of the local relief for struggling families. Before large government programmes began, local charity carried much of the burden of the Depression. In rural areas, church women's groups played a central role. They knew which families were struggling and what they needed. It might be food, clothing, coal or a visit from a doctor. Members delivered baskets of eggs, butter, bread and vegetables. Sometimes they added a chicken or a jar of preserves. They tried to give help quietly, without making anyone feel ashamed. Widows with young children were among the most vulnerable. Without land or a wage earner, they could quickly fall into deep poverty. Women's groups also organised clothing drives, sewing circles and community suppers. Much of this work went unrecorded in official histories. Yet letters and memoirs from the period describe it again and again. A common belief lay behind it. Hard times could strike anyone, and those who had a little were expected to share with those who had less.

[long-pause]

{img:49-ccc-camp-1933-young-men-planting-trees-hillside-shovels-uniforms}

In Roosevelt's first hundred days, Congress passed an unprecedented wave of new laws. Roosevelt took office in March 1933 and immediately began to act. Over the next hundred days, Congress passed a remarkable series of major laws. The period became known as the Hundred Days. Newspapers reported new programmes almost every week. Together they formed the New Deal that Roosevelt had promised. The laws aimed to help people who were suffering, to restart the economy and to prevent such a disaster from happening again. There were so many new agencies that people struggled to keep track of them. They were known by their initials, like a kind of alphabet soup. The AAA dealt with farming. The CCC gave work to young men. FERA sent federal money to the states for relief. The TVA built dams and brought electricity to a poor region of the South. Not all of these programmes succeeded. Yet the speed and scale of action changed what Americans expected from their government. Later presidents have often been measured against those first hundred days.

[long-pause]

{img:50-young-men-arriving-ccc-camp-tents-barracks-forest-truck-1933}

The Civilian Conservation Corps gave young men work in forests and parks, and sent most of their pay home. The Civilian Conservation Corps was one of the most popular programmes of the New Deal. It was created in the spring of 1933. It offered work to unemployed young men, usually between eighteen and twenty-five, from poor families. Recruits lived in camps run with help from the army. The camps were set up in forests, parks and other rural areas. The men planted trees, built roads and trails, stopped soil erosion and fought forest fires. They were paid thirty dollars a month. Twenty-five dollars of that was sent directly to their families. For many households, this money made a real difference. Many recruits had been out of work for months or years. In the camps, they received three meals a day, clothing, medical care and basic education. Many gained weight and learned practical skills, from using an axe to driving a truck. Letters home often described hard but satisfying work. The programme gave young men structure and dignity at a time when both were scarce.

[long-pause]

{img:51-ccc-boys-around-campfire-evening-forest-letters-harmonica-tired-happy}

Over nine years, the Civilian Conservation Corps planted around three billion trees. The Civilian Conservation Corps operated from 1933 to 1942. During that time, about three million young men served in it. Together they planted roughly three billion trees, many on hillsides where earlier forests had been cut down. They built thousands of miles of trails and roads. They constructed bridges, campgrounds and buildings in national and state parks. Many of these structures are still in use today. Former members often said that the experience changed their lives. It gave them hope and pride when they needed them most. Other New Deal programmes were more controversial. For farmers, the Agricultural Adjustment Act tried to raise prices by reducing production. The government paid farmers to plant fewer acres. In the summer of 1933, it also paid them to slaughter about six million young pigs. Many people were shocked that food was destroyed while others went hungry. The programme raised prices, but the debate over its methods continued for years.

[long-pause]

{img:52-farmer-reading-government-letter-kitchen-table-wife-looking-over-shoulder}

Many farmers disliked being paid to grow less, but signed up because they needed the money. The Agricultural Adjustment Act asked farmers to reduce the acreage they planted. In return, they received payments from the government. For many farmers, the idea felt deeply wrong. Their whole way of life was built on growing as much as possible. Yet farm incomes had collapsed, and the payments offered real help. Many signed contracts in 1933 and 1934, agreeing to plant fewer acres of corn or cotton. The cheques were not large, but they helped families pay mortgages and taxes. Farm prices gradually began to rise. The programme had serious problems, however. In the South, landowners often kept the payments and evicted the tenant farmers who worked their land. In 1936, the Supreme Court struck down the original law. Congress replaced it with new legislation. Historians still debate how well the New Deal worked overall. Yet for many rural families, it marked the first time the national government had offered them direct help.

[long-pause]

{img:53-iowa-cornfield-july-tall-green-corn-blue-sky-white-clouds-farm-boy}

An old Midwestern saying held that corn should be knee high by the Fourth of July. Summer in Iowa brought long, hot days, and the corn grew quickly. On still nights, farmers sometimes said they could almost hear it growing. Leaves rustled and stalks creaked softly in the dark. The old saying about the Fourth of July gave farmers a simple measure of progress. In good years, the corn was often much taller than that. Summer was also a time of heavy work. Oats had to be harvested, and hay cut and stacked. In July came threshing. A large threshing machine, powered by a steam engine or tractor, travelled from farm to farm. Neighbours formed threshing rings and worked together on each farm in turn. The men fed bundles of oats into the machine, which separated grain from straw. The work was hot, dusty and noisy. Yet it was also social. Threshing rings brought neighbours together for days at a time. Many people later remembered threshing season as one of the high points of the farm year.

[long-pause]

{img:54-threshing-dinner-long-table-under-trees-farmers-eating-women-serving}

Threshing dinners were among the largest meals of the Midwestern farm year. At midday during threshing, women of the neighbourhood served a great feast to the workers. Long tables were set up under the trees. They held fried chicken, roast beef, mashed potatoes and gravy. There were green beans, corn on the cob, sliced tomatoes and fresh bread. Pies of apple, cherry and rhubarb followed. Hostesses took pride in their cooking, and friendly competition between farm kitchens was common. The men ate enormous amounts before returning to work until evening. Summer also had its quieter pleasures, even in hard times. Children swam in creeks and swimming holes on hot afternoons. Rope swings hung from branches over deep, cool pools. Children dived, splashed and floated beneath the leaves. Such simple pleasures cost nothing, which made them especially precious during the Depression. Memoirs from the period often return to these summer scenes. They suggest that childhood joy survived even when adult worries were great.

[long-pause]

{img:55-children-swimming-creek-rope-swing-summer-afternoon-sunlight-trees}

In small Midwestern towns, local baseball teams and Fourth of July celebrations brought everyone together. Baseball was one of the great pleasures of rural summer life. Many small towns had teams made up of farmers, shopkeepers and young men. They played against teams from neighbouring towns on Sunday afternoons and summer evenings. Spectators sat on the grass or on wooden benches, cheering and eating popcorn. Boys dreamed of playing for the town team one day. The Fourth of July was the biggest public celebration of the year. Towns held parades with brass bands, old soldiers, decorated floats and children on bicycles. Picnics in the park followed, with speeches, games and races. Sack races, three-legged races and egg-and-spoon races were favourites. Spelling contests and pie competitions were also common. In the evening, fireworks lit the sky above the town square. Even during the Depression, communities found ways to hold these events. They were cheap, cheerful and shared by everyone. They reminded people that community life continued despite hard times.

[long-pause]

{img:56-ice-cream-social-church-lawn-evening-paper-lanterns-families-1933}

Church ice cream socials raised money for the poor, one nickel at a time. Summer evenings in rural America often brought ice cream socials. They were usually held on church lawns. Men took turns cranking large wooden ice cream makers packed with ice and salt. Vanilla, strawberry and peach were popular flavours. A dish of ice cream and a slice of cake might cost a nickel. The money often went to help poor families in the community. Children ran across the lawns in the twilight, catching fireflies in glass jars. Fireflies were one of the great sights of a Midwestern summer. On warm evenings after dark, thousands rose from the grass and fields, blinking with soft green light. Children watched them glow and then let them go. For farmers, warm summer nights also brought cautious hope. A good harvest would not make them rich at low prices. Yet it could feed the animals and the family, and pay something on the mortgage. In 1933, many farmers felt hopeful for the first time in years.

[long-pause]

{img:57-century-of-progress-chicago-1933-worlds-fair-art-deco-pavilions-lake-michigan}

In 1933, Chicago held a world's fair called A Century of Progress. The fair celebrated the hundredth anniversary of Chicago and the achievements of science and industry. It was built on the shore of Lake Michigan, partly on land created by filling in the lake. Its buildings were modern and bold, painted in bright colours of red, blue, yellow and orange. At night, electric lights in every colour transformed the grounds. The organisers wanted to show that science and technology could lead to a better future. That message had special meaning in the middle of the Depression. Millions of visitors came from across the country. Many saved for months to afford the trip by train, car or bus. The fair was so successful that it reopened for a second season in 1934. Altogether, it attracted more than forty million visits. Remarkably, it also paid for itself. For many rural visitors, it was their first experience of a great city. It offered a glimpse of modern life that most had only heard about on the radio.

[long-pause]

{img:58-grandmother-and-girl-on-train-window-seat-1933-fields-passing-excited}

Railways offered special cheap fares so that ordinary families could visit the Chicago world's fair. Travel was expensive in the 1930s, and few farm families could afford long journeys. To attract visitors, railways offered reduced fares to the Chicago fair. Many people used small savings set aside over many years. After so many bank failures, some older people kept their money hidden at home rather than in banks. Coins in an old sock or a tin box could pay for a once-in-a-lifetime trip. For many rural travellers, the journey itself was an adventure. Trains crossed broad farmland, small towns and great rivers. Travellers from Iowa crossed the Mississippi on long iron bridges. Arrival in Chicago could be overwhelming. It was one of the largest cities in the world, with tall buildings, crowds, noise and lights everywhere. Many visitors stayed with relatives in small city apartments to save money. Older travellers who had crossed the prairie by wagon in their youth often marvelled at the change. In a single lifetime, travel had been transformed.

[long-pause]

{img:59-sky-ride-towers-cable-cars-worlds-fair-1933-crowds-below-sunshine}

The Sky Ride at the 1933 world's fair carried visitors high above Lake Michigan between two tall towers. The Century of Progress was filled with wonders. Great halls displayed machines, inventions and scientific exhibits. One exhibit, the House of Tomorrow, had glass walls and even a hangar for a private aeroplane. Visitors saw the newest cars, trains and engines. A display of life-sized moving dinosaurs attracted large crowds. The most famous attraction was the Sky Ride. Two steel towers more than six hundred feet tall stood on either side of a lagoon. Small rocket-shaped cars travelled along cables between them, high above the fairgrounds. From the cars, riders could look down on the crowds, the lake and the city. For many visitors, it was the highest they had ever been. The towers were among the tallest structures in Chicago at the time. The Sky Ride captured the spirit of the fair. It mixed engineering, excitement and optimism. It offered ordinary people a brief experience of a future that seemed fast, modern and full of possibility.

[long-pause]

{img:60-worlds-fair-night-1933-colored-lights-buildings-reflected-lagoon-fireworks}

At night, the Chicago fair glowed with coloured electric light, at a time when most farms still had none. As evening fell over the fairgrounds, the buildings lit up in brilliant colours. Red, blue, green and gold reflected in the lagoons. Searchlights swept across the sky, and music played. The fair even began with light. Its opening lights were switched on using starlight from the star Arcturus, captured by telescopes and turned into an electrical signal. For visitors from farms without electricity, the effect was overwhelming. Many had lived their whole lives by kerosene lamps. Older visitors could remember a world without electric lights, cars, aeroplanes, radios or telephones. In a single lifetime, they had seen more technological change than any earlier generation. Many visitors kept small souvenirs for the rest of their lives, such as metal models of the Sky Ride towers. Others wrote letters and school essays about what they had seen. The fair offered a vision of progress during a time of hardship. For millions, it was a reminder that the future might still be brighter than the present.

[long-pause]

{img:61-county-fair-1933-iowa-midway-ferris-wheel-tents-crowds-summer-evening}

The county fair was the biggest event of the year for Midwestern farm families. Every summer, each county in Iowa held its own fair. For a week, the fairground at the edge of town filled with people, animals, tents and music. There were livestock shows, horse races and a midway with rides, games and food stands. Families came from every corner of the county. The heart of the fair was the livestock show. Farmers brought their best cattle, hogs, sheep, horses and chickens to be judged. The animals were washed, brushed and groomed until they shone. Judges examined each one carefully, feeling its build and comparing it with the others. The best received ribbons, blue for first place, red for second and white for third. County fairs had begun in the nineteenth century as a way to improve farming. They allowed farmers to compare animals and learn new methods. By the 1930s, they had also become festivals. Even in the Depression, most counties found a way to keep their fair going.

[long-pause]

{img:62-boy-with-calf-show-ring-4h-county-fair-judge-looking-proud-nervous}

Through 4-H clubs, farm children raised their own animals and showed them at the county fair. The 4-H movement began in the early twentieth century. Its four H's stood for head, heart, hands and health. Clubs taught farm children practical skills in farming, gardening, cooking and sewing. Each member took on a project for the year. Many boys and girls raised a calf, a pig or a lamb from birth. They fed it, watered it and groomed it every day. The project taught responsibility as well as farming skills. At the county fair, members led their animals into the show ring. Calves were washed and brushed until their coats shone. Young exhibitors wore their cleanest clothes and tried to stay calm in front of the judges. For many children, it was the most exciting day of the year. Extension agents from the state agricultural colleges often supported the clubs. 4-H grew rapidly in the 1930s. It became one of the largest youth organisations in America, and it remains active today.

[long-pause]

{img:63-prize-ribbon-red-pinned-on-calf-halter-boy-grinning-family-cheering}

At county fairs, farm women competed for ribbons with their jams, pies, quilts and sewing. The livestock show was only one part of the fair. In the women's building, long tables displayed the skills of farm households. There were jars of canned vegetables and fruit, glowing in the light. There were loaves of bread, cakes and pies. Quilts, embroidery and dresses hung on display. Judges tasted, examined and compared each entry. Winners received the same coloured ribbons as the best animals. A blue ribbon for a pie or a quilt brought real pride. Girls often entered their first jam or apron, made under the guidance of mothers and grandmothers. These contests honoured work that was otherwise rarely recognised. Canning, baking and sewing were essential to a farm's survival, especially during the Depression. At the fair, they became public achievements. Many families kept their ribbons for generations. Some can still be found in old trunks and albums, small reminders of a proud day at the county fair.

[long-pause]

{img:64-fair-midway-night-lights-carousel-cotton-candy-children-coins}

In the evening, the fair's midway glowed with coloured lights, rides and music. When the judging ended, families often went to the midway. It was noisy, bright and exciting. There was a merry-go-round with painted horses and a Ferris wheel rising high above the fairground. Game booths offered prizes for throwing a ball or a ring. Stands sold popcorn, cotton candy, hot dogs and lemonade. Travelling carnival companies supplied many of these attractions, moving from fair to fair through the summer. During the Depression, money for such pleasures was scarce. Many children received only a quarter or a dime to spend. They chose carefully between rides and treats. Some saved part of it for later. From the top of the Ferris wheel, riders could see the whole fairground lit up. Beyond lay the town and the dark fields stretching away under the stars. For farm children who rarely saw electric light, the midway at night felt almost magical. It was a small, local version of the wonders shown at the great world's fairs.

[long-pause]

{img:65-corn-husking-by-hand-1933-farmer-throwing-ears-into-wagon-bangboard-frost}

In 1933, most Iowa corn was still harvested by hand, one ear at a time. In October, the cornfields turned brown and dry. The leaves rustled like paper in the wind, and the heavy ears hung down in their husks. Harvest time had come. On most Iowa farms, corn was still picked by hand, as it had been for a century. It was the hardest work of the year. Farmers harnessed their horses before dawn and drove a wagon into the field. One side of the wagon had a high board called a bangboard. The farmer walked beside the wagon, grabbed an ear and stripped the husk with a small metal hook strapped to his hand. Then he snapped the ear from the stalk and threw it against the bangboard without looking. It bounced off and fell into the wagon. The rhythm was steady and repetitive. Mechanical corn pickers already existed, but few farmers could afford them during the Depression. Hand husking remained common until the 1940s.

[long-pause]

{img:66-horses-pulling-corn-wagon-slowly-along-rows-autumn-field-golden-light}

A skilled corn husker could pick around a hundred bushels in a single day. Well-trained horses knew the work of husking. They walked slowly along the row, stopping and starting at a word from the farmer. The farmer kept up a steady rhythm. Grab, husk, snap, throw, bang. Again and again, from dawn until dusk. In autumn, the sound of ears striking bangboards could be heard across the countryside from farm to farm. A good husker might pick about a hundred bushels a day, or several thousand ears. When the wagon was full, the farmer drove it back to the farmyard. There he shovelled the corn into a corn crib. This was a long, narrow building with slatted sides, so that air could blow through and dry the corn. Full cribs of golden ears were a sign of a successful year. The corn would feed hogs, cattle and horses through the winter. Some would be sold. In a hard decade, a full crib brought a rare feeling of security.

[long-pause]

{img:67-corn-crib-filled-golden-ears-slatted-walls-farmyard-autumn-sun}

Corn husking contests drew crowds of tens of thousands across the Corn Belt. Harvest was a family effort. Children helped after school and on Saturdays. They picked the low ears their fathers missed and drove the horses back to the farm. Dry husks cut and chapped their hands, and mothers treated the cuts with salve in the evening. Out of this hard work grew a popular sport. In the 1920s and 1930s, corn husking contests were held throughout the Corn Belt. The best huskers in each county competed to see who could pick the most corn in eighty minutes. Winners advanced to state contests and then to a national championship. Huge crowds came to watch, sometimes more than a hundred thousand people at the national event. Radio stations broadcast the contests live. National champions became famous across farm country, and their names appeared in newspapers everywhere. The contests turned an exhausting chore into a source of pride. They celebrated strength, skill and endurance, qualities that farm families valued highly.

[long-pause]

{img:68-corn-husking-contest-crowd-watching-huskers-field-judges-1933}

At a husking contest, judges deducted points for husks left on the ears and corn left in the field. County husking contests were usually held on a cold, bright morning in early November. Each competitor had his own row of corn, his own wagon and his own team of horses. A crowd gathered at the edge of the field. When the starting gun sounded, ears flew through the air and struck the bangboards like drums. Huskers worked as fast as they could for eighty minutes. Arms ached and hands often bled. When time was called, judges weighed the corn in each wagon. They deducted points for husks left on the ears and for ears missed in the field. Speed alone was not enough. Clean, careful work mattered too. By mid-November, most harvests were complete. In 1933, many Iowa farms brought in a good crop. Prices remained low, but there was enough corn to feed the animals through winter. Some could be sold to pay a little on the mortgage. For many families, that alone was a quiet victory.

[long-pause]

{img:69-thanksgiving-1933-farmhouse-dining-table-roast-turkey-family-saying-grace}

In 1933, Thanksgiving held special meaning for families who had come through hard years. Thanksgiving fell at the end of November, after the harvest was in. Americans traced the holiday back to the Pilgrims' harvest feast more than three centuries earlier. For farm families, it was one of the most important days of the year. Even in the Depression, many believed there was much to be thankful for. Preparations began days in advance. Women baked pumpkin, apple and mince pies. They made cranberry sauce, bread and rolls. On farms, the turkey was often raised at home. On Thanksgiving morning, it was put in the oven early and roasted slowly for hours. The smell filled the whole house. In the cities, many families could not afford such a meal. Charities and churches organised Thanksgiving dinners for the poor. Newspapers reported long lines at soup kitchens on the holiday. Farm families, with their own animals and gardens, were often better placed. Their feasts were simple by later standards, yet generous for the time.

[long-pause]

{img:70-relatives-arriving-farmhouse-old-car-greetings-cold-november-porch}

Depression-era Thanksgiving dinners often included neighbours and guests who had nowhere else to go. On Thanksgiving, relatives travelled from nearby towns and counties. Old cars bumped along the gravel roads to farmhouses. Young men home from Civilian Conservation Corps camps often looked healthier than they had in years. Their families noticed the difference at once. Many households also invited guests. Widows and their children, lonely neighbours and teachers far from home were common guests at Depression-era tables. In a period of shared hardship, hospitality was a strong rural value. A farmhouse might hold twenty people or more on the holiday. Extra chairs were carried in from every room. Tables were joined together and covered with white cloths. Children often ate at a smaller table in the corner. Memoirs of the period describe these gatherings as crowded, noisy and warm. For poorer guests, the meal could be the best they had eaten all year. The custom of opening the table to others reflected a simple belief. Food shared was food multiplied.

[long-pause]

{img:71-long-table-extended-into-parlour-children-at-small-table-crowded-happy}

A traditional farm Thanksgiving dinner in the 1930s included turkey, stuffing, potatoes, pies and much more. Before eating, families often stood around the table and said grace. Prayers gave thanks for the harvest, the farm, family and friends. Many also asked for help for those who were hungry or alone. Then the meal began. There was roast turkey with stuffing, mashed potatoes and gravy. There were sweet potatoes, green beans, corn and cranberry sauce. Bread, butter, pickles and jam covered the table. Then came the pies. After dinner, men often sat in the parlour talking or dozing. Women washed dishes in the kitchen. Children played outside in the cold air until dark. In the evening, families gathered around the radio. College football games were already a Thanksgiving tradition on the air. Older relatives told stories of earlier times. Some remembered Thanksgivings in sod houses on the open prairie in the 1870s and 1880s. Those stories reminded younger listeners how much had already changed, and how much hardship earlier generations had survived.

[long-pause]

{img:72-family-and-guests-singing-around-piano-parlour-lamplight-evening}

On the fifth of December, 1933, Prohibition came to an end in the United States. For thirteen years, it had been illegal to make, transport or sell alcoholic drinks across most of America. Prohibition had begun in 1920 with high hopes of reducing crime and improving family life. Instead, it encouraged smuggling, secret bars and organised crime. By the early 1930s, many Americans had turned against it. The Depression strengthened the case for repeal. Legal alcohol would create jobs and bring in tax revenue. In 1933, the states ratified a new amendment to the Constitution. On the fifth of December, Utah became the last state needed, and Prohibition ended. In the cities, there were large celebrations. In many rural areas, the change made far less difference. Strong temperance traditions remained, especially in churches. Some counties and states kept their own local bans for decades. In many farmhouses, life went on as before. The end of Prohibition was a national event, yet its effect varied greatly from place to place.

[long-pause]

{img:73-iowa-farm-winter-1933-snow-covered-fields-farmhouse-smoke-chimney-blue-dusk}

During Iowa blizzards, farmers tied a rope between house and barn so they would not get lost. Winter came to Iowa in December. The first snow often fell softly in the night. By morning, fields, roads, barns and trees lay under a thick white blanket. The air was sharp, cold and clean. The only sounds were the creak of the windmill and the crunch of boots in the snow. Iowa winters could be severe. Temperatures sometimes stayed far below freezing for weeks. Blizzards swept down from the north with fierce wind and blinding snow. In the worst storms, visibility could fall almost to nothing. People who stepped outside could become lost within a few yards of their own door. Many farmers therefore stretched a rope from the house to the barn. Holding it, they could reach the animals safely to feed and milk them. Older settlers remembered deadly blizzards from the nineteenth century. Those memories made rural families cautious and well prepared. In winter, survival depended on planning done months in advance.

[long-pause]

{img:74-father-carrying-hay-to-cattle-snowstorm-rope-line-to-barn-lantern}

Farm families prepared for winter with full corn cribs, hay lofts, cellars and woodpiles. By December, a well-run farm was ready for the cold months. The corn crib was full, and so was the hay loft. The cellar held jars of canned food, sacks of potatoes, apples and onions. Woodpiles and corncobs were stacked for the stoves. Animals rested in barns bedded with thick straw. The kitchen stove burned day and night. Winter changed the rhythm of farm life. Daily chores continued, including feeding and milking animals, cleaning barns, chopping wood and carrying water. Yet there was no work in the fields. This gave families more time than at any other season. They mended tools and harness, made clothing and repaired furniture. They read, rested and visited neighbours. Long winter evenings were spent around the stove and the lamp. Many farm families looked forward to this quieter season. It offered rest after the heavy labour of harvest. It was also a time when families were together more than at any other point in the year.

[long-pause]

{img:75-children-sledding-down-snowy-hill-farm-dog-running-beside-laughing}

In the early 1930s, great dust storms began to blow across the southern and western plains. Winter brought simple pleasures for farm children. They went sledding on wooden sleds, built snow forts and skated on frozen creeks. They came home red-cheeked and soaked, to warm themselves by the stove. Yet news from further west was troubling. Over earlier decades, farmers on the plains had ploughed up vast areas of native grass to plant wheat. When drought arrived in the early 1930s, the exposed soil dried out. Strong winds lifted it into enormous black clouds. In November 1933, severe dust storms struck parts of the Dakotas and nearby states. Witnesses described skies darkening in the middle of the day. Over the next few years, the storms grew worse. The southern plains became known as the Dust Bowl. Hundreds of thousands of people eventually left, many heading to California in search of work. Iowa received more rain and suffered less. Yet the disaster showed how fragile the land could be when it was not protected.

[long-pause]

{img:76-family-reading-by-lamplight-winter-evening-kitchen-frost-on-window}

Christmas presents during the Depression were often an orange, a few sweets and one small gift. Christmas in the 1930s was modest for most rural families. Stockings might hold mittens, a book, an orange and a few pieces of candy. Oranges were a real treat in the Midwest, because they came from far away and were expensive. Many children received one practical present, such as a pocketknife or a diary. Gifts were often handmade. Mothers knitted sweaters from the wool of old ones, carefully unravelled. Fathers carved toys or built sleds in their workshops. Winter evenings were spent around the kitchen lamp. Adults read farm newspapers or mended clothes. Children read library books or drew pictures. On Christmas Eve, many families attended church services lit by candles and sang familiar carols. Later generations sometimes found these accounts surprisingly modest. Yet many people who grew up in the Depression described their childhood Christmases with great warmth. What they remembered most was not the gifts. It was the feeling of being together, warm and safe.

[long-pause]

{img:77-rural-electrification-1930s-power-lines-reaching-farmhouse-workers-poles}

The Great Depression did not end in 1933, but continued for most of the decade. Roosevelt's first year brought hope, yet recovery was slow. The economy improved gradually from 1933 to 1937. Then a new downturn struck in 1937 and 1938, and unemployment rose again. Throughout the decade, millions of Americans remained without work. The Dust Bowl brought terrible suffering to farm families on the southern plains in the mid-1930s. Even so, the New Deal created institutions that lasted. One of the most important was Social Security, established in 1935. It provided pensions for older people when they could no longer work. It also created unemployment insurance and assistance for some disabled people and children. For the first time, many elderly Americans could expect a small regular income from the national government. The programme was limited at first and excluded many workers, including farm labourers. Later laws expanded it greatly. Social Security became one of the most lasting legacies of the Depression era. It still shapes American life today.

[long-pause]

{img:78-farm-wife-switching-on-first-electric-light-kitchen-family-amazed-1940}

From 1935, a new federal agency brought electricity to millions of American farms. Private power companies had long refused to serve most rural areas. Building lines across scattered farms was expensive and promised little profit. In 1935, Roosevelt created the Rural Electrification Administration. It lent money to groups of farmers who formed cooperatives and built their own power lines. Across the countryside, crews set up tall poles along gravel roads and strung wires to farmhouses. Many people remembered the first evening the lights came on. A single bulb over the kitchen table could bring a family to tears. Soon came electric radios, irons, pumps and refrigerators. Women's work in particular became far easier. In 1935, about one farm in ten had electricity. By the early 1950s, most farms were connected. Then came the Second World War. American entry in December 1941 finally ended the Depression. Factories ran day and night, and farmers were asked to grow as much food as possible. Crop prices rose, and many families finally paid off old debts.

[long-pause]

{img:79-woman-teacher-1950s-classroom-children-desks-sunlight-chalkboard}

Many children of the Depression later became the first in their families to attend college. The generation that grew up in the 1930s saw enormous change during their adult lives. Many farm daughters became teachers, nurses or office workers. Some went on to write about their childhood. Memoirs of growing up on farms during the Depression became a popular kind of American writing. They described flour sack dresses, strangers at the back door, penny auctions and evenings around the radio. After the Second World War, many young men attended college through government support for returning soldiers. Higher education, once rare for farm families, became far more common. Older relatives who had pioneered the prairie lived to see electric lights, radios and cars. Some even rode aeroplanes. Many parents who had farmed through the hard years eventually retired to small houses in nearby towns. From their porches, they watched a very different world go by. The changes they witnessed in one lifetime were greater than those of many earlier centuries combined.

[long-pause]

{img:80-old-couple-porch-small-town-house-1960s-rocking-chairs-evening-peace}

Many people who lived through the Depression later spoke of it without bitterness. Historians who interviewed survivors of the Depression often noticed something unexpected. Many spoke of the hard years calmly, and sometimes even with affection. They remembered worry, lost savings and low prices. Yet they also remembered neighbours, church suppers and the help they gave and received. Some described the period as one of the most meaningful times of their lives. They said it had taught them what really mattered. Such memories were shaped by time, of course. People who suffered most sometimes did not live to tell their stories, or did not wish to. Families who lost everything in the Dust Bowl or the city breadlines often told darker accounts. Even so, the pattern appears in many collections of oral history. Shared hardship created strong bonds. Communities that pulled together left lasting impressions. For many survivors, the Depression was remembered not only as a crisis, but as a time when ordinary kindness made a great difference.

[long-pause]

{img:81-old-woman-grandmother-1980s-saving-string-ball-kitchen-drawer-jars}

The habits learned in the Depression lasted for the rest of many people's lives. People who grew up in the 1930s were later called the Depression generation. Many kept the habits of those years long after hard times had passed. Even when they became comfortable, they could not bear to waste anything. They saved string, rubber bands, paper bags and twist ties. They smoothed out pieces of aluminium foil and folded them for reuse. Old jars, buttons and clothes were kept just in case. Broken things were mended rather than thrown away. Many grew and canned vegetables every summer. They finished every scrap of food and expected their grandchildren to do the same. Younger family members sometimes found these habits puzzling or amusing. Yet they reflected deep experience. Researchers who study childhood poverty note that early hardship often shapes lifelong attitudes towards money and possessions. The Depression generation is a clear example. Their caution and thrift were lessons learned in childhood, and they never quite let them go.

[long-pause]

{img:82-grandmother-teaching-grandchild-to-darn-sock-wooden-egg-armchair}

After the bank failures, many Americans of the Depression generation never fully trusted banks again. The collapse of thousands of banks left a deep mark. Many survivors kept part of their money at home for the rest of their lives. Some hid it under mattresses, in coffee cans or buried in gardens. They disliked borrowing and avoided buying on credit. If they could not afford something, they waited until they could. They saved a little every week for emergencies. These habits made them cautious with money in later decades. Yet the Depression also taught other lessons. Many survivors spoke of learning to help each other. They shared with those who had less and showed kindness to strangers. They valued working together as neighbours and as a country. Some said that the hard years showed them who their real friends were. Many concluded that most people, when tested, behaved well. Darning socks over a wooden egg and saving every coin were outward signs of that generation. The deeper lesson was a belief in thrift, patience and mutual help.

[long-pause]

{img:83-neighbours-helping-each-other-1930s-sharing-vegetables-over-fence}

The Depression permanently changed the role of the American government in everyday life. Before the 1930s, the national government played a limited role in most people's lives. Relief for the poor was largely left to families, churches and local communities. The Depression changed that. The New Deal created programmes to protect savings, support farmers and help the unemployed. It provided pensions for older people. It built roads, dams, power lines, schools and parks across the country. Not everyone welcomed these changes. Critics argued that government was growing too large and costly. Supporters believed it had a duty to protect citizens from disaster. That debate has continued in American politics ever since. Yet one broad conclusion was widely shared. Most people agreed that the country should never again allow its citizens to suffer as they had in the early 1930s. The experience also left personal lessons. Survivors spoke of being grateful for small things, such as a warm house, a full stomach and a job. Many said those years taught them patience and endurance.

[long-pause]

{img:84-photograph-album-1930s-family-pictures-old-hands-turning-pages-memories}

Many of the most famous photographs of the Depression were taken by photographers working for the government. In the later 1930s, a federal agency hired photographers to document rural America. They travelled across the country with their cameras. They photographed farmers, migrant workers, children and elderly people. Their goal was partly to show why government programmes were needed. Their pictures captured poverty and hardship. Yet they also showed dignity, strength and courage. Some of these images became among the most famous photographs ever taken. Dorothea Lange's portrait of a migrant mother in California is perhaps the best known. Walker Evans photographed tenant farm families in the South. Today, the collection is held by the Library of Congress and contains well over a hundred thousand images. Alongside these public records, family stories carried the memory forward. Grandparents told grandchildren about flour sack dresses, strangers at the back door and the first electric light. Diaries, letters and old photographs were kept in attics and drawers. Together, they preserve the experience of ordinary people through an extraordinary decade.

[long-pause]

{img:85-today-iowa-farmland-aerial-cornfields-grain-bins-wind-turbines-summer}

Today, Iowa remains one of the most productive farming regions in the world. Iowa's cornfields still stretch to the horizon under a wide sky. The state usually grows more corn than any other in the United States. It also leads the nation in hog production and is a major producer of soybeans. In summer, the corn still grows tall and green. Yet farming has changed enormously since 1933. Farms are much larger. Where four or five farms of a hundred and sixty acres once stood, there may now be a single farm of a thousand acres or more. Work that once took a farmer and his horses an entire autumn is now done by a combine harvester in a few days. One farmer with modern machinery can do the work of dozens of people from the 1930s. Wind turbines now rise above many fields, and grain bins have replaced wooden corn cribs. The landscape looks familiar from a distance. Yet up close, almost every part of farm work has been transformed.

[long-pause]

{img:86-today-old-one-room-schoolhouse-preserved-museum-prairie-grass-visitors}

Far fewer people live on Iowa's farms today than in the 1930s. As farms grew larger and more mechanised, fewer families were needed to work the land. Many old farmhouses stand empty or have disappeared. The little one-room schools closed in the decades after the Second World War. Children now travel by bus to larger consolidated schools in nearby towns. Some old schoolhouses have been preserved as museums. Visitors can sit at the wooden desks, see the blackboard and the iron stove, and picture the pupils of the 1930s. Many small towns have survived, though often with fewer shops than before. Harlan, for example, still has its courthouse square, brick buildings and shady streets, with about five thousand residents. Its old courthouse still stands with its clock tower. Farm families still come into town to shop and meet friends. Now they arrive in pickup trucks rather than Model T Fords. The rhythm of rural life has changed, yet many of its familiar places remain.

[long-pause]

{img:87-today-small-iowa-town-courthouse-square-clock-tower-summer-evening}

Across Iowa, parks, shelters and post office murals still remind visitors of the New Deal. The young men of the Civilian Conservation Corps left lasting marks on the Iowa landscape. They built stone and timber lodges, shelters, bridges and trails in state parks. Many are still used today by families for picnics, hiking and camping. Small signs often explain who built them and when. Post offices in many small towns contain murals painted during the 1930s. Artists were commissioned by the government to create them. They show farmers, corn, local history and the life of the towns. County fairs still take place every summer in every county in Iowa. 4-H members still show calves, pigs and chickens and win ribbons. There are still pie contests, quilts, canned goods and brightly lit midways. The Iowa State Fair in Des Moines has become one of the largest and most famous fairs in America. Every year, it attracts more than a million visitors. Traditions shaped in hard times have become part of the state's identity.

[long-pause]

{img:88-today-iowa-state-fair-crowds-ferris-wheel-livestock-barn-blue-ribbons}

The Iowa State Fair continued through the hard years of the Great Depression. The Iowa State Fair in Des Moines was first held in 1854, and by the 1930s it was one of the largest agricultural fairs in the country. During the Depression, many families had very little money to spend. Yet the fair carried on almost every summer. Farm families travelled to Des Moines by car, truck or train, and some camped on the fairgrounds to save money. In the great livestock barns, young members of 4-H clubs showed the calves, pigs and sheep they had raised, hoping for a blue ribbon. Women entered pies, preserves, quilts and canned vegetables in competitions. Newspapers of the time reported smaller crowds in the worst years, and prize money was sometimes reduced. Even so, the fair remained a highlight of the farming year. It offered a break from worry, a chance to meet friends, and pride in good work. Today the fair still welcomes more than a million visitors each summer, with the same barns, ribbons and Ferris wheel.

[long-pause]

{img:89-father-pipe-mother-mending-grandmother-rocking-chair-porch-dusk}

For many farm families, 1933 ended better than the year before, and better than they had feared. By the end of 1933, the worst of the banking panic was over. Farm prices remained low, but they had begun to recover from the depths of 1932. New federal programmes offered help with mortgages and debts. Many families had brought in a good harvest. They had full cribs, full cellars and enough to last the winter. The future remained uncertain. Dust storms, a second downturn and war still lay ahead. Yet many people sensed that the country had turned a corner. Letters and diaries from the time often express cautious relief. Evenings on the porch gave families a moment to rest after the harvest. Parents mended clothes in the last of the light. Older relatives hummed old hymns. Children read until it was too dark to see the page. For a generation that had lived through so much fear, such quiet evenings mattered greatly. They were reminders that ordinary life could continue, even in the hardest of times.

[long-pause]

We have followed the hard years of the Depression, from closed banks and penny auctions to radio evenings, county fairs and the first electric lights. Through it all, families held on, and neighbours helped each other.

Now the lamps in the farmhouse windows go dark. The cornfields lie silent under the stars, and the windmill stands still. None of it needs to be remembered.
