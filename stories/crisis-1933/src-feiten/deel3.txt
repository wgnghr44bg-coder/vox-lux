TOPIC: The county fair, and the harvest
===
IMG: county-fair-1933-iowa-midway-ferris-wheel-tents-crowds-summer-evening | county fair 1933 iowa midway ferris wheel tents crowds summer evening
HEAD: The county fair was the biggest event of the year for Midwestern farm families.
BODY: Every summer, each county in Iowa held its own fair. For a week, the fairground at the edge of town filled with people, animals, tents and music. There were livestock shows, horse races and a midway with rides, games and food stands. Families came from every corner of the county. The heart of the fair was the livestock show. Farmers brought their best cattle, hogs, sheep, horses and chickens to be judged. The animals were washed, brushed and groomed until they shone. Judges examined each one carefully, feeling its build and comparing it with the others. The best received ribbons, blue for first place, red for second and white for third. County fairs had begun in the nineteenth century as a way to improve farming. They allowed farmers to compare animals and learn new methods. By the 1930s, they had also become festivals. Even in the Depression, most counties found a way to keep their fair going.
===
IMG: boy-with-calf-show-ring-4h-county-fair-judge-looking-proud-nervous | boy with calf show ring 4h county fair judge looking proud nervous
HEAD: Through 4-H clubs, farm children raised their own animals and showed them at the county fair.
BODY: The 4-H movement began in the early twentieth century. Its four H's stood for head, heart, hands and health. Clubs taught farm children practical skills in farming, gardening, cooking and sewing. Each member took on a project for the year. Many boys and girls raised a calf, a pig or a lamb from birth. They fed it, watered it and groomed it every day. The project taught responsibility as well as farming skills. At the county fair, members led their animals into the show ring. Calves were washed and brushed until their coats shone. Young exhibitors wore their cleanest clothes and tried to stay calm in front of the judges. For many children, it was the most exciting day of the year. Extension agents from the state agricultural colleges often supported the clubs. 4-H grew rapidly in the 1930s. It became one of the largest youth organisations in America, and it remains active today.
===
IMG: prize-ribbon-red-pinned-on-calf-halter-boy-grinning-family-cheering | prize ribbon red pinned on calf halter boy grinning family cheering
HEAD: At county fairs, farm women competed for ribbons with their jams, pies, quilts and sewing.
BODY: The livestock show was only one part of the fair. In the women's building, long tables displayed the skills of farm households. There were jars of canned vegetables and fruit, glowing in the light. There were loaves of bread, cakes and pies. Quilts, embroidery and dresses hung on display. Judges tasted, examined and compared each entry. Winners received the same coloured ribbons as the best animals. A blue ribbon for a pie or a quilt brought real pride. Girls often entered their first jam or apron, made under the guidance of mothers and grandmothers. These contests honoured work that was otherwise rarely recognised. Canning, baking and sewing were essential to a farm's survival, especially during the Depression. At the fair, they became public achievements. Many families kept their ribbons for generations. Some can still be found in old trunks and albums, small reminders of a proud day at the county fair.
===
IMG: fair-midway-night-lights-carousel-cotton-candy-children-coins | fair midway night lights carousel cotton candy children coins
HEAD: In the evening, the fair's midway glowed with coloured lights, rides and music.
BODY: When the judging ended, families often went to the midway. It was noisy, bright and exciting. There was a merry-go-round with painted horses and a Ferris wheel rising high above the fairground. Game booths offered prizes for throwing a ball or a ring. Stands sold popcorn, cotton candy, hot dogs and lemonade. Travelling carnival companies supplied many of these attractions, moving from fair to fair through the summer. During the Depression, money for such pleasures was scarce. Many children received only a quarter or a dime to spend. They chose carefully between rides and treats. Some saved part of it for later. From the top of the Ferris wheel, riders could see the whole fairground lit up. Beyond lay the town and the dark fields stretching away under the stars. For farm children who rarely saw electric light, the midway at night felt almost magical. It was a small, local version of the wonders shown at the great world's fairs.
===
IMG: corn-husking-by-hand-1933-farmer-throwing-ears-into-wagon-bangboard-frost | corn husking by hand 1933 farmer throwing ears into wagon bangboard frost
HEAD: In 1933, most Iowa corn was still harvested by hand, one ear at a time.
BODY: In October, the cornfields turned brown and dry. The leaves rustled like paper in the wind, and the heavy ears hung down in their husks. Harvest time had come. On most Iowa farms, corn was still picked by hand, as it had been for a century. It was the hardest work of the year. Farmers harnessed their horses before dawn and drove a wagon into the field. One side of the wagon had a high board called a bangboard. The farmer walked beside the wagon, grabbed an ear and stripped the husk with a small metal hook strapped to his hand. Then he snapped the ear from the stalk and threw it against the bangboard without looking. It bounced off and fell into the wagon. The rhythm was steady and repetitive. Mechanical corn pickers already existed, but few farmers could afford them during the Depression. Hand husking remained common until the 1940s.
===
IMG: horses-pulling-corn-wagon-slowly-along-rows-autumn-field-golden-light | horses pulling corn wagon slowly along rows autumn field golden light
HEAD: A skilled corn husker could pick around a hundred bushels in a single day.
BODY: Well-trained horses knew the work of husking. They walked slowly along the row, stopping and starting at a word from the farmer. The farmer kept up a steady rhythm. Grab, husk, snap, throw, bang. Again and again, from dawn until dusk. In autumn, the sound of ears striking bangboards could be heard across the countryside from farm to farm. A good husker might pick about a hundred bushels a day, or several thousand ears. When the wagon was full, the farmer drove it back to the farmyard. There he shovelled the corn into a corn crib. This was a long, narrow building with slatted sides, so that air could blow through and dry the corn. Full cribs of golden ears were a sign of a successful year. The corn would feed hogs, cattle and horses through the winter. Some would be sold. In a hard decade, a full crib brought a rare feeling of security.
===
IMG: corn-crib-filled-golden-ears-slatted-walls-farmyard-autumn-sun | corn crib filled golden ears slatted walls farmyard autumn sun
HEAD: Corn husking contests drew crowds of tens of thousands across the Corn Belt.
BODY: Harvest was a family effort. Children helped after school and on Saturdays. They picked the low ears their fathers missed and drove the horses back to the farm. Dry husks cut and chapped their hands, and mothers treated the cuts with salve in the evening. Out of this hard work grew a popular sport. In the 1920s and 1930s, corn husking contests were held throughout the Corn Belt. The best huskers in each county competed to see who could pick the most corn in eighty minutes. Winners advanced to state contests and then to a national championship. Huge crowds came to watch, sometimes more than a hundred thousand people at the national event. Radio stations broadcast the contests live. National champions became famous across farm country, and their names appeared in newspapers everywhere. The contests turned an exhausting chore into a source of pride. They celebrated strength, skill and endurance, qualities that farm families valued highly.
===
IMG: corn-husking-contest-crowd-watching-huskers-field-judges-1933 | corn husking contest crowd watching huskers field judges 1933
HEAD: At a husking contest, judges deducted points for husks left on the ears and corn left in the field.
BODY: County husking contests were usually held on a cold, bright morning in early November. Each competitor had his own row of corn, his own wagon and his own team of horses. A crowd gathered at the edge of the field. When the starting gun sounded, ears flew through the air and struck the bangboards like drums. Huskers worked as fast as they could for eighty minutes. Arms ached and hands often bled. When time was called, judges weighed the corn in each wagon. They deducted points for husks left on the ears and for ears missed in the field. Speed alone was not enough. Clean, careful work mattered too. By mid-November, most harvests were complete. In 1933, many Iowa farms brought in a good crop. Prices remained low, but there was enough corn to feed the animals through winter. Some could be sold to pay a little on the mortgage. For many families, that alone was a quiet victory.

TOPIC: Thanksgiving, and winter
===
IMG: thanksgiving-1933-farmhouse-dining-table-roast-turkey-family-saying-grace | thanksgiving 1933 farmhouse dining table roast turkey family saying grace
HEAD: In 1933, Thanksgiving held special meaning for families who had come through hard years.
BODY: Thanksgiving fell at the end of November, after the harvest was in. Americans traced the holiday back to the Pilgrims' harvest feast more than three centuries earlier. For farm families, it was one of the most important days of the year. Even in the Depression, many believed there was much to be thankful for. Preparations began days in advance. Women baked pumpkin, apple and mince pies. They made cranberry sauce, bread and rolls. On farms, the turkey was often raised at home. On Thanksgiving morning, it was put in the oven early and roasted slowly for hours. The smell filled the whole house. In the cities, many families could not afford such a meal. Charities and churches organised Thanksgiving dinners for the poor. Newspapers reported long lines at soup kitchens on the holiday. Farm families, with their own animals and gardens, were often better placed. Their feasts were simple by later standards, yet generous for the time.
===
IMG: relatives-arriving-farmhouse-old-car-greetings-cold-november-porch | relatives arriving farmhouse old car greetings cold november porch
HEAD: Depression-era Thanksgiving dinners often included neighbours and guests who had nowhere else to go.
BODY: On Thanksgiving, relatives travelled from nearby towns and counties. Old cars bumped along the gravel roads to farmhouses. Young men home from Civilian Conservation Corps camps often looked healthier than they had in years. Their families noticed the difference at once. Many households also invited guests. Widows and their children, lonely neighbours and teachers far from home were common guests at Depression-era tables. In a period of shared hardship, hospitality was a strong rural value. A farmhouse might hold twenty people or more on the holiday. Extra chairs were carried in from every room. Tables were joined together and covered with white cloths. Children often ate at a smaller table in the corner. Memoirs of the period describe these gatherings as crowded, noisy and warm. For poorer guests, the meal could be the best they had eaten all year. The custom of opening the table to others reflected a simple belief. Food shared was food multiplied.
===
IMG: long-table-extended-into-parlour-children-at-small-table-crowded-happy | long table extended into parlour children at small table crowded happy
HEAD: A traditional farm Thanksgiving dinner in the 1930s included turkey, stuffing, potatoes, pies and much more.
BODY: Before eating, families often stood around the table and said grace. Prayers gave thanks for the harvest, the farm, family and friends. Many also asked for help for those who were hungry or alone. Then the meal began. There was roast turkey with stuffing, mashed potatoes and gravy. There were sweet potatoes, green beans, corn and cranberry sauce. Bread, butter, pickles and jam covered the table. Then came the pies. After dinner, men often sat in the parlour talking or dozing. Women washed dishes in the kitchen. Children played outside in the cold air until dark. In the evening, families gathered around the radio. College football games were already a Thanksgiving tradition on the air. Older relatives told stories of earlier times. Some remembered Thanksgivings in sod houses on the open prairie in the 1870s and 1880s. Those stories reminded younger listeners how much had already changed, and how much hardship earlier generations had survived.
===
IMG: family-and-guests-singing-around-piano-parlour-lamplight-evening | family and guests singing around piano parlour lamplight evening
HEAD: On the fifth of December, 1933, Prohibition came to an end in the United States.
BODY: For thirteen years, it had been illegal to make, transport or sell alcoholic drinks across most of America. Prohibition had begun in 1920 with high hopes of reducing crime and improving family life. Instead, it encouraged smuggling, secret bars and organised crime. By the early 1930s, many Americans had turned against it. The Depression strengthened the case for repeal. Legal alcohol would create jobs and bring in tax revenue. In 1933, the states ratified a new amendment to the Constitution. On the fifth of December, Utah became the last state needed, and Prohibition ended. In the cities, there were large celebrations. In many rural areas, the change made far less difference. Strong temperance traditions remained, especially in churches. Some counties and states kept their own local bans for decades. In many farmhouses, life went on as before. The end of Prohibition was a national event, yet its effect varied greatly from place to place.
===
IMG: iowa-farm-winter-1933-snow-covered-fields-farmhouse-smoke-chimney-blue-dusk | iowa farm winter 1933 snow covered fields farmhouse smoke chimney blue dusk
HEAD: During Iowa blizzards, farmers tied a rope between house and barn so they would not get lost.
BODY: Winter came to Iowa in December. The first snow often fell softly in the night. By morning, fields, roads, barns and trees lay under a thick white blanket. The air was sharp, cold and clean. The only sounds were the creak of the windmill and the crunch of boots in the snow. Iowa winters could be severe. Temperatures sometimes stayed far below freezing for weeks. Blizzards swept down from the north with fierce wind and blinding snow. In the worst storms, visibility could fall almost to nothing. People who stepped outside could become lost within a few yards of their own door. Many farmers therefore stretched a rope from the house to the barn. Holding it, they could reach the animals safely to feed and milk them. Older settlers remembered deadly blizzards from the nineteenth century. Those memories made rural families cautious and well prepared. In winter, survival depended on planning done months in advance.
===
IMG: father-carrying-hay-to-cattle-snowstorm-rope-line-to-barn-lantern | father carrying hay to cattle snowstorm rope line to barn lantern
HEAD: Farm families prepared for winter with full corn cribs, hay lofts, cellars and woodpiles.
BODY: By December, a well-run farm was ready for the cold months. The corn crib was full, and so was the hay loft. The cellar held jars of canned food, sacks of potatoes, apples and onions. Woodpiles and corncobs were stacked for the stoves. Animals rested in barns bedded with thick straw. The kitchen stove burned day and night. Winter changed the rhythm of farm life. Daily chores continued, including feeding and milking animals, cleaning barns, chopping wood and carrying water. Yet there was no work in the fields. This gave families more time than at any other season. They mended tools and harness, made clothing and repaired furniture. They read, rested and visited neighbours. Long winter evenings were spent around the stove and the lamp. Many farm families looked forward to this quieter season. It offered rest after the heavy labour of harvest. It was also a time when families were together more than at any other point in the year.
===
IMG: children-sledding-down-snowy-hill-farm-dog-running-beside-laughing | children sledding down snowy hill farm dog running beside laughing
HEAD: In the early 1930s, great dust storms began to blow across the southern and western plains.
BODY: Winter brought simple pleasures for farm children. They went sledding on wooden sleds, built snow forts and skated on frozen creeks. They came home red-cheeked and soaked, to warm themselves by the stove. Yet news from further west was troubling. Over earlier decades, farmers on the plains had ploughed up vast areas of native grass to plant wheat. When drought arrived in the early 1930s, the exposed soil dried out. Strong winds lifted it into enormous black clouds. In November 1933, severe dust storms struck parts of the Dakotas and nearby states. Witnesses described skies darkening in the middle of the day. Over the next few years, the storms grew worse. The southern plains became known as the Dust Bowl. Hundreds of thousands of people eventually left, many heading to California in search of work. Iowa received more rain and suffered less. Yet the disaster showed how fragile the land could be when it was not protected.
===
IMG: family-reading-by-lamplight-winter-evening-kitchen-frost-on-window | family reading by lamplight winter evening kitchen frost on window
HEAD: Christmas presents during the Depression were often an orange, a few sweets and one small gift.
BODY: Christmas in the 1930s was modest for most rural families. Stockings might hold mittens, a book, an orange and a few pieces of candy. Oranges were a real treat in the Midwest, because they came from far away and were expensive. Many children received one practical present, such as a pocketknife or a diary. Gifts were often handmade. Mothers knitted sweaters from the wool of old ones, carefully unravelled. Fathers carved toys or built sleds in their workshops. Winter evenings were spent around the kitchen lamp. Adults read farm newspapers or mended clothes. Children read library books or drew pictures. On Christmas Eve, many families attended church services lit by candles and sang familiar carols. Later generations sometimes found these accounts surprisingly modest. Yet many people who grew up in the Depression described their childhood Christmases with great warmth. What they remembered most was not the gifts. It was the feeling of being together, warm and safe.

TOPIC: What came later, and what they learned
===
IMG: rural-electrification-1930s-power-lines-reaching-farmhouse-workers-poles | rural electrification 1930s power lines reaching farmhouse workers poles
HEAD: The Great Depression did not end in 1933, but continued for most of the decade.
BODY: Roosevelt's first year brought hope, yet recovery was slow. The economy improved gradually from 1933 to 1937. Then a new downturn struck in 1937 and 1938, and unemployment rose again. Throughout the decade, millions of Americans remained without work. The Dust Bowl brought terrible suffering to farm families on the southern plains in the mid-1930s. Even so, the New Deal created institutions that lasted. One of the most important was Social Security, established in 1935. It provided pensions for older people when they could no longer work. It also created unemployment insurance and assistance for some disabled people and children. For the first time, many elderly Americans could expect a small regular income from the national government. The programme was limited at first and excluded many workers, including farm labourers. Later laws expanded it greatly. Social Security became one of the most lasting legacies of the Depression era. It still shapes American life today.
===
IMG: farm-wife-switching-on-first-electric-light-kitchen-family-amazed-1940 | farm wife switching on first electric light kitchen family amazed 1940
HEAD: From 1935, a new federal agency brought electricity to millions of American farms.
BODY: Private power companies had long refused to serve most rural areas. Building lines across scattered farms was expensive and promised little profit. In 1935, Roosevelt created the Rural Electrification Administration. It lent money to groups of farmers who formed cooperatives and built their own power lines. Across the countryside, crews set up tall poles along gravel roads and strung wires to farmhouses. Many people remembered the first evening the lights came on. A single bulb over the kitchen table could bring a family to tears. Soon came electric radios, irons, pumps and refrigerators. Women's work in particular became far easier. In 1935, about one farm in ten had electricity. By the early 1950s, most farms were connected. Then came the Second World War. American entry in December 1941 finally ended the Depression. Factories ran day and night, and farmers were asked to grow as much food as possible. Crop prices rose, and many families finally paid off old debts.
===
IMG: woman-teacher-1950s-classroom-children-desks-sunlight-chalkboard | woman teacher 1950s classroom children desks sunlight chalkboard
HEAD: Many children of the Depression later became the first in their families to attend college.
BODY: The generation that grew up in the 1930s saw enormous change during their adult lives. Many farm daughters became teachers, nurses or office workers. Some went on to write about their childhood. Memoirs of growing up on farms during the Depression became a popular kind of American writing. They described flour sack dresses, strangers at the back door, penny auctions and evenings around the radio. After the Second World War, many young men attended college through government support for returning soldiers. Higher education, once rare for farm families, became far more common. Older relatives who had pioneered the prairie lived to see electric lights, radios and cars. Some even rode aeroplanes. Many parents who had farmed through the hard years eventually retired to small houses in nearby towns. From their porches, they watched a very different world go by. The changes they witnessed in one lifetime were greater than those of many earlier centuries combined.
===
IMG: old-couple-porch-small-town-house-1960s-rocking-chairs-evening-peace | old couple porch small town house 1960s rocking chairs evening peace
HEAD: Many people who lived through the Depression later spoke of it without bitterness.
BODY: Historians who interviewed survivors of the Depression often noticed something unexpected. Many spoke of the hard years calmly, and sometimes even with affection. They remembered worry, lost savings and low prices. Yet they also remembered neighbours, church suppers and the help they gave and received. Some described the period as one of the most meaningful times of their lives. They said it had taught them what really mattered. Such memories were shaped by time, of course. People who suffered most sometimes did not live to tell their stories, or did not wish to. Families who lost everything in the Dust Bowl or the city breadlines often told darker accounts. Even so, the pattern appears in many collections of oral history. Shared hardship created strong bonds. Communities that pulled together left lasting impressions. For many survivors, the Depression was remembered not only as a crisis, but as a time when ordinary kindness made a great difference.
===
IMG: old-woman-grandmother-1980s-saving-string-ball-kitchen-drawer-jars | old woman grandmother 1980s saving string ball kitchen drawer jars
HEAD: The habits learned in the Depression lasted for the rest of many people's lives.
BODY: People who grew up in the 1930s were later called the Depression generation. Many kept the habits of those years long after hard times had passed. Even when they became comfortable, they could not bear to waste anything. They saved string, rubber bands, paper bags and twist ties. They smoothed out pieces of aluminium foil and folded them for reuse. Old jars, buttons and clothes were kept just in case. Broken things were mended rather than thrown away. Many grew and canned vegetables every summer. They finished every scrap of food and expected their grandchildren to do the same. Younger family members sometimes found these habits puzzling or amusing. Yet they reflected deep experience. Researchers who study childhood poverty note that early hardship often shapes lifelong attitudes towards money and possessions. The Depression generation is a clear example. Their caution and thrift were lessons learned in childhood, and they never quite let them go.
===
IMG: grandmother-teaching-grandchild-to-darn-sock-wooden-egg-armchair | grandmother teaching grandchild to darn sock wooden egg armchair
HEAD: After the bank failures, many Americans of the Depression generation never fully trusted banks again.
BODY: The collapse of thousands of banks left a deep mark. Many survivors kept part of their money at home for the rest of their lives. Some hid it under mattresses, in coffee cans or buried in gardens. They disliked borrowing and avoided buying on credit. If they could not afford something, they waited until they could. They saved a little every week for emergencies. These habits made them cautious with money in later decades. Yet the Depression also taught other lessons. Many survivors spoke of learning to help each other. They shared with those who had less and showed kindness to strangers. They valued working together as neighbours and as a country. Some said that the hard years showed them who their real friends were. Many concluded that most people, when tested, behaved well. Darning socks over a wooden egg and saving every coin were outward signs of that generation. The deeper lesson was a belief in thrift, patience and mutual help.
===
IMG: neighbours-helping-each-other-1930s-sharing-vegetables-over-fence | neighbours helping each other 1930s sharing vegetables over fence
HEAD: The Depression permanently changed the role of the American government in everyday life.
BODY: Before the 1930s, the national government played a limited role in most people's lives. Relief for the poor was largely left to families, churches and local communities. The Depression changed that. The New Deal created programmes to protect savings, support farmers and help the unemployed. It provided pensions for older people. It built roads, dams, power lines, schools and parks across the country. Not everyone welcomed these changes. Critics argued that government was growing too large and costly. Supporters believed it had a duty to protect citizens from disaster. That debate has continued in American politics ever since. Yet one broad conclusion was widely shared. Most people agreed that the country should never again allow its citizens to suffer as they had in the early 1930s. The experience also left personal lessons. Survivors spoke of being grateful for small things, such as a warm house, a full stomach and a job. Many said those years taught them patience and endurance.
===
IMG: photograph-album-1930s-family-pictures-old-hands-turning-pages-memories | photograph album 1930s family pictures old hands turning pages memories
HEAD: Many of the most famous photographs of the Depression were taken by photographers working for the government.
BODY: In the later 1930s, a federal agency hired photographers to document rural America. They travelled across the country with their cameras. They photographed farmers, migrant workers, children and elderly people. Their goal was partly to show why government programmes were needed. Their pictures captured poverty and hardship. Yet they also showed dignity, strength and courage. Some of these images became among the most famous photographs ever taken. Dorothea Lange's portrait of a migrant mother in California is perhaps the best known. Walker Evans photographed tenant farm families in the South. Today, the collection is held by the Library of Congress and contains well over a hundred thousand images. Alongside these public records, family stories carried the memory forward. Grandparents told grandchildren about flour sack dresses, strangers at the back door and the first electric light. Diaries, letters and old photographs were kept in attics and drawers. Together, they preserve the experience of ordinary people through an extraordinary decade.

TOPIC: Iowa today
===
IMG: today-iowa-farmland-aerial-cornfields-grain-bins-wind-turbines-summer | LATER: A calm, warmly lit scene: today iowa farmland aerial cornfields grain bins wind turbines summer
HEAD: Today, Iowa remains one of the most productive farming regions in the world.
BODY: Iowa's cornfields still stretch to the horizon under a wide sky. The state usually grows more corn than any other in the United States. It also leads the nation in hog production and is a major producer of soybeans. In summer, the corn still grows tall and green. Yet farming has changed enormously since 1933. Farms are much larger. Where four or five farms of a hundred and sixty acres once stood, there may now be a single farm of a thousand acres or more. Work that once took a farmer and his horses an entire autumn is now done by a combine harvester in a few days. One farmer with modern machinery can do the work of dozens of people from the 1930s. Wind turbines now rise above many fields, and grain bins have replaced wooden corn cribs. The landscape looks familiar from a distance. Yet up close, almost every part of farm work has been transformed.
===
IMG: today-old-one-room-schoolhouse-preserved-museum-prairie-grass-visitors | LATER: A calm, warmly lit scene: today old one room schoolhouse preserved museum prairie grass visitors
HEAD: Far fewer people live on Iowa's farms today than in the 1930s.
BODY: As farms grew larger and more mechanised, fewer families were needed to work the land. Many old farmhouses stand empty or have disappeared. The little one-room schools closed in the decades after the Second World War. Children now travel by bus to larger consolidated schools in nearby towns. Some old schoolhouses have been preserved as museums. Visitors can sit at the wooden desks, see the blackboard and the iron stove, and picture the pupils of the 1930s. Many small towns have survived, though often with fewer shops than before. Harlan, for example, still has its courthouse square, brick buildings and shady streets, with about five thousand residents. Its old courthouse still stands with its clock tower. Farm families still come into town to shop and meet friends. Now they arrive in pickup trucks rather than Model T Fords. The rhythm of rural life has changed, yet many of its familiar places remain.
===
IMG: today-small-iowa-town-courthouse-square-clock-tower-summer-evening | LATER: A calm, warmly lit scene: today small iowa town courthouse square clock tower summer evening
HEAD: Across Iowa, parks, shelters and post office murals still remind visitors of the New Deal.
BODY: The young men of the Civilian Conservation Corps left lasting marks on the Iowa landscape. They built stone and timber lodges, shelters, bridges and trails in state parks. Many are still used today by families for picnics, hiking and camping. Small signs often explain who built them and when. Post offices in many small towns contain murals painted during the 1930s. Artists were commissioned by the government to create them. They show farmers, corn, local history and the life of the towns. County fairs still take place every summer in every county in Iowa. 4-H members still show calves, pigs and chickens and win ribbons. There are still pie contests, quilts, canned goods and brightly lit midways. The Iowa State Fair in Des Moines has become one of the largest and most famous fairs in America. Every year, it attracts more than a million visitors. Traditions shaped in hard times have become part of the state's identity.
===
IMG: today-iowa-state-fair-crowds-ferris-wheel-livestock-barn-blue-ribbons | LATER: A calm, warmly lit scene: today iowa state fair crowds ferris wheel livestock barn blue ribbons
HEAD: In parts of Iowa, the original tallgrass prairie is slowly being restored.
BODY: Before settlement, most of Iowa was covered with tallgrass prairie. Grasses could grow taller than a person, mixed with wildflowers of every colour. This prairie stretched for hundreds of miles. Almost all of it was ploughed for farming. Today, only a tiny fraction of the original prairie survives. In recent decades, conservation groups, farmers and public agencies have begun planting prairie again. They use native grasses and flowers grown from local seed. Birds, bees and butterflies return to these restored areas. Prairie strips are even planted inside some farm fields to reduce soil erosion. Meanwhile, the stories of the 1930s live on in many families. Some still own quilts made from flour sacks by grandmothers or great-grandmothers. Others keep old fair ribbons or souvenirs from the Chicago world's fair. Such objects are treasured and handed down. They connect present generations with a time when families survived through thrift, hard work and shared effort.
===
IMG: father-pipe-mother-mending-grandmother-rocking-chair-porch-dusk | father pipe mother mending grandmother rocking chair porch dusk
HEAD: For many farm families, 1933 ended better than the year before, and better than they had feared.
BODY: By the end of 1933, the worst of the banking panic was over. Farm prices remained low, but they had begun to recover from the depths of 1932. New federal programmes offered help with mortgages and debts. Many families had brought in a good harvest. They had full cribs, full cellars and enough to last the winter. The future remained uncertain. Dust storms, a second downturn and war still lay ahead. Yet many people sensed that the country had turned a corner. Letters and diaries from the time often express cautious relief. Evenings on the porch gave families a moment to rest after the harvest. Parents mended clothes in the last of the light. Older relatives hummed old hymns. Children read until it was too dark to see the page. For a generation that had lived through so much fear, such quiet evenings mattered greatly. They were reminders that ordinary life could continue, even in the hardest of times.
