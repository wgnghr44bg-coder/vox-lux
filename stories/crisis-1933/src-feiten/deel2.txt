HEAD: In the Depression, America is full of travellers.
BODY: Not tourists, or holidaymakers. You'd see them walking along the road with a bundle on a stick. But people looking for work, or for food, or for a new beginning. Men, mostly, and some women, and many young people, boys and girls, sometimes as young as fourteen or fifteen. They have left their homes, because there was no work there, and no food, and they didn't want to be a burden on their families. Many of them travel by freight train. They climb into empty boxcars, or onto the roofs, or underneath, and ride for free, from town to town, from state to state, across the whole country. It's dangerous, and uncomfortable, and illegal. But it's the only way they can afford to travel. People call them hobos, or tramps, or transients. Perhaps a quarter of a million young people are riding the rails, in 1933. Near the railway tracks, outside every town, there are hobo camps, which they call jungles. Places where the travellers can rest, and sleep, and cook, and wash, and share news. They build little fires, and cook a stew, in an old tin can, from whatever they have. A potato, an onion, a bit of meat, if they are lucky. Everyone puts something in. And everyone shares. There are unwritten rules, in the jungles, about sharing, and cleanliness, and respect. The railway line runs past the end of the Millers' farm, about half a mile away. Several times a week, a long freight train rumbles past, with its whistle blowing, and its smoke trailing across the fields. Eddie loves to watch the trains. Sometimes, he sees men sitting in the open doors of the boxcars, with their legs dangling, watching the fields go by. Sometimes, they wave to him. And he waves back.

HEAD: And sometimes, the travellers come to the farmhouse.
BODY: They walk up the lane, from the railway, tired, and dusty, and hungry. You'd see a tired figure standing at the end of the lane. They knock at the back door, politely, with their hats in their hands. And they ask if there's any work they could do, in exchange for a meal. Ruth never turns them away. She gives them a plate of food. Bread, and beans, and coffee, or whatever there's. And Walt finds them a job to do. Chopping wood, or mending a fence, or cleaning out the barn. Most of the travellers are polite, and grateful, and honest. They are ordinary people, who have fallen on hard times. A carpenter from Chicago. A factory worker from Detroit. A young man from Kansas, whose family lost their farm. A schoolteacher, who lost her job when the school closed. They sit on the back step, and eat, and talk a little, about where they have come from, and where they are going. Then they thank Ruth, and walk back down the lane, to the railway.

HEAD: On the gatepost, at the end of the Millers' lane, there's a little mark, drawn in chalk.
BODY: A simple drawing, like a cat. You'd walk right past it, unless you knew what to look for. It's a hobo sign. The travellers have their own secret code, of little signs, which they draw on fences, and gateposts, and walls, to tell each other about the places they pass. This house has a dog. This town has police who arrest hobos. This farmer will give you work. And the cat means, a kind lady lives here. Ruth doesn't know about the sign. But Eddie has seen it. He asked one of the travellers what it meant, and the man told him. Eddie is very proud of his mother. He decides not to rub it out. Would you have known what it meant? HEAD: One day in the spring, a young man comes to the door.
BODY: He is only seventeen, thin, and pale, and shy. You'd hear a soft knock at the kitchen door. His name is Tom. He comes from a farm in Nebraska, which his family lost to the bank. She thinks about her own children, and hopes that they will never have to do what he is doing. You'd probably give him a plate of food too, wouldn't you? HEAD: In 1932 and 1933, many farmers in Iowa lost their farms.
BODY: They couldn't pay their mortgages, or their taxes. You can imagine how it felt to see your own farm on a sale poster. So the banks, or the insurance companies, which had lent them the money, took the farms, and sold them, at auction. Everything was sold. The land, the house, the barn, the animals, the machines, the furniture. And the family had to leave, with nothing. These auctions were very sad occasions. A family that had worked the land for generations, perhaps fifty years, or more, had to stand and watch as their home, and everything they owned, was sold to strangers. Their neighbours came to watch too. And many of them were angry. They thought it was wrong. The farmers hadn't done anything wrong. They had worked hard. It was the Depression that had ruined them. So, in some places, the farmers began to fight back, in their own way. They organised what were called penny auctions. When a farm was being sold, all the neighbours came to the auction, in a big crowd. They stood together, silently, around the auctioneer. And when the bidding began, they made it very clear, without saying a word, that nobody should bid more than a few pennies.

HEAD: So the horse was sold for ten cents.
BODY: The cow, for five cents. The plough, for a quarter. The tractor, for a dollar. And all of it was bought by the neighbours. Then, when the auction was over, the neighbours gave everything back to the family. The bank got only a few dollars. And the family kept their things, and sometimes, even their farm. It wasn't exactly legal. But it worked, sometimes. In the spring of 1933, there's a penny auction, on a farm a few miles from the Millers. The farm belongs to the Petersens, a Danish family, who are friends of the Millers, from church. Karl Petersen is a good farmer, and a hard worker. But he borrowed money, to buy more land, in the good years. And now he can't pay it back. Can you believe it? The insurance company has decided to sell his farm.

HEAD: On the morning of the auction, Walt puts on his hat, and his good coat, and drives over to the Petersen farm.
BODY: Many other farmers are there already, standing in the yard, in their overalls, and their hats. You'd see dozens of trucks and wagons parked along the lane. Hundreds of them. He describes the first horse, a fine brown mare, and asks for a bid. There's silence. Then one farmer says, five cents. The auctioneer asks for more. Nobody says anything. They just look at him. The auctioneer looks at the crowd. Then he says, sold, for five cents. And so it goes on, all morning. Everything sold, for a few pennies, and all of it given back to the Petersens. Karl Petersen stands on his porch, with his wife, and watches. At first, he doesn't understand. Then he does. And he can't speak. His wife cries, quietly, into her handkerchief. When the auction is over, the farmers come up to the porch, one by one, and shake Karl's hand, and say a few words. Then they get into their cars and their wagons, and drive home.

HEAD: The penny auctions didn't save every farm.
BODY: And in some places, there was trouble, and anger, and even violence. You might think the neighbours could save everyone. Sadly, you'd be wrong. In the spring of 1933, some farmers in Iowa became so angry that they threatened a judge, who was handling the farm sales. The governor sent in soldiers, to keep order. Most farmers didn't approve of this. They wanted justice, but peacefully. Then, slowly, things began to change. The government passed new laws, to help the farmers. The banks were told to be more patient. New loans were made available, at lower interest, so that farmers could keep their farms. And the Petersens kept theirs. Years later, Karl's grandson still farmed the same land. And on the wall of the farmhouse, there was a framed receipt, from the auction, for a brown mare, sold for five cents.

TOPIC: The New Deal
BRIDGE: Far away from the little schoolhouse, in Washington, big plans were being made. What do you think they meant for a farm family like the Millers?
===
IMG: ccc-camp-1933-young-men-planting-trees-hillside-shovels-uniforms | ccc camp 1933 young men planting trees hillside shovels uniforms
HEAD: In the first hundred days after he became president, Roosevelt and the Congress passed more new laws than any government in American history, in such a short time.
BODY: People called it the Hundred Days. You'd open the newspaper and find something new almost every day. The new laws were part of the New Deal, which Roosevelt had promised. They were meant to help the people who were suffering, and to get the economy working again, and to make sure that such a disaster could never happen again. There were many new programmes, with many new names. So many that people couldn't keep track of them. They were known by their initials, like a kind of alphabet soup. The AAA, for the farmers. The CCC, for the young men. The FERA, to give money to the states, for the poor. The TVA, to build dams, and bring electricity, to the poor lands of the south. And many more.
===
IMG: young-men-arriving-ccc-camp-tents-barracks-forest-truck-1933 | young men arriving ccc camp tents barracks forest truck 1933
HEAD: One of the most popular programmes was the Civilian Conservation Corps, the CCC.
BODY: It gave work to young men, between eighteen and twenty-five, from poor families. You'd see young men in work clothes climbing into army trucks. They lived in camps, in the countryside, in the forests, and the parks, and worked on projects to protect nature. Planting trees, building roads and trails, stopping soil from washing away, fighting forest fires, and building parks. They were paid thirty dollars a month. Twenty-five of those dollars were sent home, to their families. Ruth's nephew, Jimmy, joins the CCC in the summer of 1933. He is nineteen, and he has been out of work for two years. He goes to a camp in the forests of Minnesota, in the north. He writes letters home, which Ruth reads aloud, in the evenings. He says the work is hard, but good. He has never eaten so well in his life. Three big meals a day. He has put on fifteen pounds. And he has learned to use an axe, and a saw, and to drive a truck.
===
IMG: ccc-boys-around-campfire-evening-forest-letters-harmonica-tired-happy | ccc boys around campfire evening forest letters harmonica tired happy
HEAD: Jimmy says that he has planted thousands of trees, on hillsides where the old forest was cut down.
BODY: He says that one day, there will be a great forest there again, and he will take his children to see it. You can picture him, proud and sunburnt, telling you all about it. He says he is proud of his work. And his mother, Ruth's sister, is proud of him. The twenty-five dollars a month that he sends home keeps her family going. The CCC was a great success. Over the next nine years, about three million young men worked in it. They planted about three billion trees. They built thousands of parks, and trails, and bridges, and roads, all over America. Many of them are still used today. And many of the young men said, later, that the CCC changed their lives, and gave them hope, and pride, when they needed it most. For the farmers, there was the AAA, the Agricultural Adjustment Act. It tried to raise the prices of crops, by paying farmers to grow less. The idea was that if there was less corn, and less pork, the prices would go up. It was controversial. In the summer of 1933, the government paid farmers to slaughter millions of young pigs, to reduce the number of hogs. Many people thought this was shocking, when so many people were hungry.

Some of it was badly planned, and some of it was wasteful, and some of it was later stopped by the courts. People argued about it, then, and they still argue about it today. But it gave many people hope, in the darkest time. It showed that the government cared about them. Do you think he was right? And slowly, very slowly, things began to get a little better.

HEAD: They stay with a cousin of Grandma Hattie, in a small apartment, on the south side of the city.
BODY: And the next day, they go to the fair. You'd hear the trains rumbling past the window all night. It's like a dream. There are great halls of science, with machines, and inventions, and exhibitions. There's a house of the future, with walls of glass, and a garage for an airplane. There are cars, and trains, and the newest engines. There's a model of a dinosaur, which moves its head and tail. There's a great ride, called the Sky Ride, with little cars, hanging from cables, between two tall towers, high above the fair, and the lake. June and Grandma Hattie ride across it, and look down at the crowds below, and the blue lake, and the city. June holds her grandmother's hand, tightly. Grandma Hattie laughs, and says that it's better than a covered wagon, any day.

HEAD: In the evening, they stay to see the lights.
BODY: As the sun goes down, all the buildings light up, in brilliant colours. You'd look up, and the whole sky would seem to glow. Red, and blue, and green, and gold, reflected in the lagoons. Searchlights sweep the sky. Music plays. June has never seen anything like it. She has never seen electric light like this. On the farm, there are only kerosene lamps. She stands still, and stares, for a long time. Grandma Hattie says that when she was a girl, there were no electric lights at all, anywhere. No cars, no airplanes, no radios, no telephones. People travelled by horse, and lit their homes with candles. She says that she has seen more change in her life than anyone, in all of history. And she wonders what June will see, in hers. They go home to Iowa three days later, tired, and happy, with their heads full of wonders. June has a little souvenir, a small metal model of the Sky Ride towers, which she keeps on her shelf, for the rest of her life. And she writes a long essay about the fair, for school, which Miss Holm reads aloud to the whole class. Eddie is very jealous. But June promises to take him, one day, to see the next world's fair. And many years later, she does.
