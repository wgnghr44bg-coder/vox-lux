TOPIC: Drifters, and the farm auction
===
IMG: hobos-riding-freight-train-boxcar-open-door-prairie-1933-young-men | hobos riding freight train boxcar open door prairie 1933 young men
HEAD: During the Depression, around a quarter of a million young Americans travelled the country by freight train.
BODY: The roads and railways of the 1930s carried an unusual kind of traveller. They were not tourists, but people searching for work, food or a new beginning. Most were men, though some were women, and many were teenagers. Some left home simply so that their families would have one less mouth to feed. Many rode freight trains, climbing into empty boxcars or onto the roofs. It was free, but it was also dangerous, uncomfortable and illegal. Railway police chased them away, and thousands were injured or killed in accidents. Such travellers were called hobos, tramps or transients. Near the tracks outside many towns were camps known as jungles. Travellers rested there, washed, cooked and shared news. Stews were made in old tin cans from whatever people could contribute. A potato, an onion, perhaps a little meat. The camps had unwritten rules about sharing, cleanliness and respect. Even among the poorest, a sense of order and fairness often survived.
===
IMG: stranger-at-farmhouse-back-door-hat-in-hand-farm-wife-offering-plate | stranger at farmhouse back door hat in hand farm wife offering plate
HEAD: Travellers often knocked at farmhouse doors, offering to work in exchange for a meal.
BODY: Farms near railway lines saw many travellers during the Depression. Men walked up the lane, tired and dusty, and knocked politely at the back door. Most asked not for charity, but for work. In return for chopping wood, mending a fence or cleaning a barn, they hoped for a plate of food. Many farm families agreed, even when they had little to spare. Bread, beans and coffee were common offerings. The travellers came from every background. There were carpenters from Chicago, factory workers from Detroit and young men from farms that had been lost. Some had been teachers or clerks before the crash. Accounts from the period describe most of them as polite, grateful and honest. They were ordinary people who had fallen on hard times. After eating, they usually thanked the family and walked back to the railway. Such meetings left deep impressions. Many children of the period later remembered these strangers at the back door for the rest of their lives.
===
IMG: chalk-symbol-on-fence-post-hobo-sign-cat-drawing-farm-gate | chalk symbol on fence post hobo sign cat drawing farm gate
HEAD: Hobos were said to use a secret code of chalk signs to tell each other which houses were friendly.
BODY: Stories about a hidden language of signs were common in the 1930s. According to these accounts, travellers drew small symbols on fences, gateposts and walls. Each sign carried a message for those who came later. One might warn of a dangerous dog. Another might mean that local police arrested travellers. Some signs meant that a farmer would give work. A simple drawing of a cat was said to mean that a kind woman lived there. Historians are not certain how widely these signs were really used. Some lists of symbols were probably collected or embellished later by writers and journalists. Yet many travellers did describe marking places in this way. The idea itself reveals something important. People on the road depended on each other for information. A small chalk mark could mean a meal, a night of shelter or a warning. In a world without telephones, a gatepost could carry news from one stranger to the next.
===
IMG: young-traveller-chopping-wood-farmyard-boy-watching-afternoon-light | young traveller chopping wood farmyard boy watching afternoon light
HEAD: Many young drifters of the 1930s came from farms that their own families had lost.
BODY: Among those on the road were many teenagers from the countryside. Some came from Nebraska, Kansas or the Dakotas, where drought and debt had ruined farms. When the bank took the land, older children often left to seek work elsewhere. Some were only fifteen or sixteen. Farmers sometimes hired them for a week during planting or harvest. They slept in barns, ate with the family and were paid a dollar or two if money allowed. Many farm parents saw their own children in these young travellers. They hoped their sons and daughters would never face the same journey. Reports from the time describe young drifters as thin, tired and often shy. Many sent letters home whenever they could. Government agencies grew concerned about the number of young people on the road. That concern helped shape programmes created in 1933 to give young men work and shelter. For many, those programmes offered the first steady place to stay in months.
===
IMG: farm-auction-1933-crowd-of-farmers-in-farmyard-auctioneer-on-wagon | farm auction 1933 crowd of farmers in farmyard auctioneer on wagon
HEAD: In 1932 and 1933, thousands of Midwestern farm families lost their land at foreclosure auctions.
BODY: When farmers could not pay their mortgages or taxes, lenders could take the farm and sell it at auction. Banks and insurance companies held many of these loans. Everything could be sold, including the land, the house, the animals, the machinery and the furniture. Families who had worked the same land for fifty years or more had to watch their possessions sold to strangers. Neighbours often came to these auctions, and many were angry. They felt that the families had done nothing wrong. They had worked hard and lost everything to forces beyond their control. In some places, farmers began to resist in an unusual way. They organised what became known as penny auctions. When a farm was put up for sale, large crowds of neighbours gathered around the auctioneer. They stood silently together. When the bidding began, they made it clear that nobody should offer more than a few pennies. Outside bidders were quietly discouraged from taking part.
===
IMG: farmers-standing-silently-together-auction-crowd-arms-folded-serious | farmers standing silently together auction crowd arms folded serious
HEAD: At penny auctions, neighbours bought a family's possessions for a few cents and then gave them back.
BODY: At a penny auction, a horse might sell for ten cents and a cow for five. A plough could go for a quarter. The buyers were the family's own neighbours. When the sale was over, they returned everything to its original owners. The lender received only a few dollars. The family kept its animals and tools, and sometimes even its farm. The method was not strictly illegal, but it was certainly unwelcome to banks and insurance companies. It depended entirely on community pressure. Outsiders who might have bid more were made to understand that they were not welcome. Penny auctions spread through Iowa, Nebraska and other farm states in 1932 and 1933. Newspapers reported on them across the country. They showed how far rural communities were willing to go to protect their own. They also revealed how deeply many farmers distrusted distant financial institutions. For a short time, a silent crowd could be more powerful than a mortgage.
===
IMG: old-farmer-and-wife-standing-on-porch-watching-auction-neighbours-crowd | old farmer and wife standing on porch watching auction neighbours crowd
HEAD: Penny auctions were usually quiet, orderly and deeply emotional.
BODY: Accounts of penny auctions often describe the same scene. Dozens of trucks and wagons lined the lane. Hundreds of farmers stood in the yard in overalls and hats. They were quiet and serious. Nobody shouted or caused trouble. They simply stood with folded arms and watched. The auctioneer climbed onto a wagon and described the first animal for sale. Silence followed. Then one farmer offered five cents. The auctioneer asked for more, but nobody spoke. After a pause, he declared the animal sold. The same thing happened again and again throughout the morning. The family often watched from the porch. At first, some did not understand what was happening. When they did, many were overcome. Afterwards, neighbours came forward one by one to shake hands and offer a few words. Then they drove home. These events showed the strength of rural communities. They also showed that even in desperate times, many farmers preferred quiet solidarity to violence.
===
IMG: neighbours-shaking-hands-on-porch-after-auction-relief-gratitude-spring | neighbours shaking hands on porch after auction relief gratitude spring
HEAD: In April 1933, the governor of Iowa sent troops into farm country after angry farmers attacked a judge.
BODY: Penny auctions did not save every farm. In some places, anger turned into violence. In April 1933, a crowd of farmers near Le Mars, Iowa, dragged a judge from his courtroom. He had refused to stop hearing foreclosure cases. They threatened him with a rope before letting him go. The governor responded by declaring martial law in several counties and sending in the National Guard. Most farmers did not approve of such actions. They wanted justice, but peacefully. Meanwhile, state and federal governments began to change the rules. Several states passed laws that delayed foreclosures. In 1933, Congress created new programmes to refinance farm mortgages at lower interest. Many families were able to keep their land as a result. The crisis had shown how close parts of rural America had come to open rebellion. Lawmakers took notice. Over the following years, farm debt relief became a lasting part of national policy.

TOPIC: The country school, and the neighbours
===
IMG: one-room-country-schoolhouse-1933-white-wooden-bell-tower-children-walking | one room country schoolhouse 1933 white wooden bell tower children walking
HEAD: In the 1930s, Iowa still had thousands of one-room country schools.
BODY: Rural America in the 1930s was dotted with small schoolhouses. Iowa alone had several thousand of them, roughly one for every few square miles of farmland. A typical school was a small white wooden building standing alone at a crossroads. It often had a bell tower, a porch, a pump and two outhouses at the back. Children walked to school along gravel roads, often a mile or two each way. They carried lunch pails, which were often old tin buckets that had once held lard or syrup. Inside might be a sandwich of homemade bread, an apple or a cookie, and a small jar of milk. In deep winter snow, some fathers drove their children to school by horse-drawn sleigh. These schools were the heart of many rural districts. They hosted meetings, plays and holiday programmes. Over the following decades, most were closed and replaced by larger consolidated schools. Many of the old buildings still stand in fields today.
===
IMG: schoolroom-interior-1933-wooden-desks-pot-bellied-stove-blackboard-teacher | schoolroom interior 1933 wooden desks pot bellied stove blackboard teacher
HEAD: In a one-room school, one teacher taught every grade at the same time.
BODY: Inside the typical country school was a single large room. At the front stood the teacher's desk, a blackboard and a map of the world. A portrait of George Washington often hung on the wall. Rows of wooden desks had inkwells and hinged lids. In the middle of the room stood an iron stove that heated the school in winter. At the back were hooks for coats, a shelf for lunch pails and a water bucket with a dipper. A school might have fifteen or twenty pupils, aged from six to fourteen. One teacher taught all of them. While the youngest learned to read, older pupils worked on arithmetic or writing. Then the roles were reversed. Older children often helped the younger ones. Educators later recognised the value of this arrangement. Younger pupils heard advanced lessons, while older ones reinforced their knowledge by teaching. It was a demanding system, yet many former pupils remembered it with great fondness.
===
IMG: young-woman-teacher-1933-helping-small-child-reading-older-girl-assisting | young woman teacher 1933 helping small child reading older girl assisting
HEAD: During the Depression, many rural teachers worked for reduced pay, and some were paid in promises.
BODY: Country school teachers in the 1930s were often young women in their early twenties. Many boarded with local farm families. They arrived early each morning to light the stove and sweep the floor before the pupils came. As tax revenues collapsed, school boards cut salaries sharply. A teacher might earn forty dollars a month for eight months of the year. Some districts could not pay at all and issued written promises instead. In parts of the country, schools closed completely for lack of money. In many farming communities, however, families were determined to keep their schools open. They brought teachers food, firewood and whatever else they could spare. The school day usually began at nine o'clock with the ringing of a bell. Pupils recited the Pledge of Allegiance and often sang a song. Lessons covered reading, writing, arithmetic, spelling, geography and history. Friday spelling bees were a favourite event. For many rural children, the teacher was the most educated person they knew.
===
IMG: schoolchildren-playing-recess-schoolyard-ball-game-fields-around-1933 | schoolchildren playing recess schoolyard ball game fields around 1933
HEAD: In Depression-era schools, teachers often quietly made sure that hungry children were fed.
BODY: Recess was an important part of the country school day. Children played baseball, tag and hide and seek in the schoolyard. A popular game called Annie Over involved throwing a ball over the school roof to a team on the other side. In winter, pupils built snowmen, held snowball fights and slid on frozen ponds. At lunchtime, they ate on the steps or around the stove and swapped sandwiches. Yet poverty was visible in every classroom. Some children came from families that had lost their farms. They wore thin, worn clothes and broken shoes. Some arrived with no lunch at all. Many teachers noticed and acted quietly. They brought extra food or asked other children to share. In some areas, the government later supported school lunch programmes. Surplus farm products were used to feed hungry pupils. These efforts laid the foundations for the national school lunch programme created after the Depression. It still feeds millions of children today.
===
IMG: white-country-church-1933-steeple-cars-and-wagons-parked-sunday-morning | white country church 1933 steeple cars and wagons parked sunday morning
HEAD: In rural America, the country church was the centre of community life.
BODY: On Sunday mornings, farm families across the Midwest drove to church. Many churches were simple white wooden buildings with tall steeples, standing at the edge of small villages. Families often attended the same church for generations. Grandparents had been married there, and children were baptised there. Methodist, Lutheran, Catholic and Baptist congregations were all common, often reflecting the origins of local settlers. On Sundays, people wore their best clothes, which were often their only good clothes. Men wore dark suits, a little shiny with age. Women wore their best dresses and hats. Children were scrubbed clean, with hair combed flat. The service included hymns, a sermon and prayers. Church bells could be heard across the fields from miles away. In the Depression, attendance in many rural churches remained strong. Religion offered comfort, meaning and a sense of continuity. The church was also one of the few places where scattered farm families regularly met.
===
IMG: church-basement-potluck-supper-long-tables-casseroles-pies-families-1933 | church basement potluck supper long tables casseroles pies families 1933
HEAD: Church potluck suppers allowed poor families to eat well without feeling ashamed.
BODY: After the service, families often stood outside and talked. These conversations were almost as important as the service itself. People shared news about illness, births, lost farms and new jobs. Above all, they learned who needed help. Churches organised much of the charity in rural communities during the Depression. One popular tradition was the potluck supper, usually held in the church basement. Every family brought a dish. There might be casseroles, salads, baked beans, fried chicken, potatoes, bread, pies and cakes. The food was laid out on long tables, and everyone helped themselves. The name came from the idea that nobody knew in advance what they would get. Its great value lay in its fairness. Wealthier families might bring more, and poorer families less. Yet everyone ate as much as they needed. Nobody asked who had brought what. In this way, the potluck shared food without embarrassment, and many such suppers continue in American churches today.
===
IMG: barn-raising-neighbours-lifting-wall-frame-together-ropes-summer-1933 | barn raising neighbours lifting wall frame together ropes summer 1933
HEAD: When a farmer's barn burned down, neighbours often built a new one together in a day or two.
BODY: Rural communities depended on shared work. When a farmer was ill or injured, neighbours often did his chores and brought in his crops. They did this without being asked and without being paid. When a barn burned down, neighbours gathered for a barn raising. Men prepared timber, raised the frames with ropes and poles, and nailed everything into place. Women cooked a great feast for all the workers. A barn that would take one family months could be built by a community in a day or two. Women also had their own gatherings. Quilting bees brought groups together around a large wooden frame. They sewed, talked, drank coffee and ate cake. Quilts made from scraps and flour sacks took weeks to finish. They were often given to brides, new mothers or families who had lost everything. Such traditions were already old in the 1930s. During the Depression, they became more important than ever.
===
IMG: women-delivering-food-basket-to-poor-family-farmhouse-door-kind | women delivering food basket to poor family farmhouse door kind
HEAD: Church women's groups organised much of the local relief for struggling families.
BODY: Before large government programmes began, local charity carried much of the burden of the Depression. In rural areas, church women's groups played a central role. They knew which families were struggling and what they needed. It might be food, clothing, coal or a visit from a doctor. Members delivered baskets of eggs, butter, bread and vegetables. Sometimes they added a chicken or a jar of preserves. They tried to give help quietly, without making anyone feel ashamed. Widows with young children were among the most vulnerable. Without land or a wage earner, they could quickly fall into deep poverty. Women's groups also organised clothing drives, sewing circles and community suppers. Much of this work went unrecorded in official histories. Yet letters and memoirs from the period describe it again and again. A common belief lay behind it. Hard times could strike anyone, and those who had a little were expected to share with those who had less.

TOPIC: The New Deal
===
IMG: ccc-camp-1933-young-men-planting-trees-hillside-shovels-uniforms | ccc camp 1933 young men planting trees hillside shovels uniforms
HEAD: In Roosevelt's first hundred days, Congress passed an unprecedented wave of new laws.
BODY: Roosevelt took office in March 1933 and immediately began to act. Over the next hundred days, Congress passed a remarkable series of major laws. The period became known as the Hundred Days. Newspapers reported new programmes almost every week. Together they formed the New Deal that Roosevelt had promised. The laws aimed to help people who were suffering, to restart the economy and to prevent such a disaster from happening again. There were so many new agencies that people struggled to keep track of them. They were known by their initials, like a kind of alphabet soup. The AAA dealt with farming. The CCC gave work to young men. FERA sent federal money to the states for relief. The TVA built dams and brought electricity to a poor region of the South. Not all of these programmes succeeded. Yet the speed and scale of action changed what Americans expected from their government. Later presidents have often been measured against those first hundred days.
===
IMG: young-men-arriving-ccc-camp-tents-barracks-forest-truck-1933 | young men arriving ccc camp tents barracks forest truck 1933
HEAD: The Civilian Conservation Corps gave young men work in forests and parks, and sent most of their pay home.
BODY: The Civilian Conservation Corps was one of the most popular programmes of the New Deal. It was created in the spring of 1933. It offered work to unemployed young men, usually between eighteen and twenty-five, from poor families. Recruits lived in camps run with help from the army. The camps were set up in forests, parks and other rural areas. The men planted trees, built roads and trails, stopped soil erosion and fought forest fires. They were paid thirty dollars a month. Twenty-five dollars of that was sent directly to their families. For many households, this money made a real difference. Many recruits had been out of work for months or years. In the camps, they received three meals a day, clothing, medical care and basic education. Many gained weight and learned practical skills, from using an axe to driving a truck. Letters home often described hard but satisfying work. The programme gave young men structure and dignity at a time when both were scarce.
===
IMG: ccc-boys-around-campfire-evening-forest-letters-harmonica-tired-happy | ccc boys around campfire evening forest letters harmonica tired happy
HEAD: Over nine years, the Civilian Conservation Corps planted around three billion trees.
BODY: The Civilian Conservation Corps operated from 1933 to 1942. During that time, about three million young men served in it. Together they planted roughly three billion trees, many on hillsides where earlier forests had been cut down. They built thousands of miles of trails and roads. They constructed bridges, campgrounds and buildings in national and state parks. Many of these structures are still in use today. Former members often said that the experience changed their lives. It gave them hope and pride when they needed them most. Other New Deal programmes were more controversial. For farmers, the Agricultural Adjustment Act tried to raise prices by reducing production. The government paid farmers to plant fewer acres. In the summer of 1933, it also paid them to slaughter about six million young pigs. Many people were shocked that food was destroyed while others went hungry. The programme raised prices, but the debate over its methods continued for years.
===
IMG: farmer-reading-government-letter-kitchen-table-wife-looking-over-shoulder | farmer reading government letter kitchen table wife looking over shoulder
HEAD: Many farmers disliked being paid to grow less, but signed up because they needed the money.
BODY: The Agricultural Adjustment Act asked farmers to reduce the acreage they planted. In return, they received payments from the government. For many farmers, the idea felt deeply wrong. Their whole way of life was built on growing as much as possible. Yet farm incomes had collapsed, and the payments offered real help. Many signed contracts in 1933 and 1934, agreeing to plant fewer acres of corn or cotton. The cheques were not large, but they helped families pay mortgages and taxes. Farm prices gradually began to rise. The programme had serious problems, however. In the South, landowners often kept the payments and evicted the tenant farmers who worked their land. In 1936, the Supreme Court struck down the original law. Congress replaced it with new legislation. Historians still debate how well the New Deal worked overall. Yet for many rural families, it marked the first time the national government had offered them direct help.

TOPIC: Summer, and the world's fair
===
IMG: iowa-cornfield-july-tall-green-corn-blue-sky-white-clouds-farm-boy | iowa cornfield july tall green corn blue sky white clouds farm boy
HEAD: An old Midwestern saying held that corn should be knee high by the Fourth of July.
BODY: Summer in Iowa brought long, hot days, and the corn grew quickly. On still nights, farmers sometimes said they could almost hear it growing. Leaves rustled and stalks creaked softly in the dark. The old saying about the Fourth of July gave farmers a simple measure of progress. In good years, the corn was often much taller than that. Summer was also a time of heavy work. Oats had to be harvested, and hay cut and stacked. In July came threshing. A large threshing machine, powered by a steam engine or tractor, travelled from farm to farm. Neighbours formed threshing rings and worked together on each farm in turn. The men fed bundles of oats into the machine, which separated grain from straw. The work was hot, dusty and noisy. Yet it was also social. Threshing rings brought neighbours together for days at a time. Many people later remembered threshing season as one of the high points of the farm year.
===
IMG: threshing-dinner-long-table-under-trees-farmers-eating-women-serving | threshing dinner long table under trees farmers eating women serving
HEAD: Threshing dinners were among the largest meals of the Midwestern farm year.
BODY: At midday during threshing, women of the neighbourhood served a great feast to the workers. Long tables were set up under the trees. They held fried chicken, roast beef, mashed potatoes and gravy. There were green beans, corn on the cob, sliced tomatoes and fresh bread. Pies of apple, cherry and rhubarb followed. Hostesses took pride in their cooking, and friendly competition between farm kitchens was common. The men ate enormous amounts before returning to work until evening. Summer also had its quieter pleasures, even in hard times. Children swam in creeks and swimming holes on hot afternoons. Rope swings hung from branches over deep, cool pools. Children dived, splashed and floated beneath the leaves. Such simple pleasures cost nothing, which made them especially precious during the Depression. Memoirs from the period often return to these summer scenes. They suggest that childhood joy survived even when adult worries were great.
===
IMG: children-swimming-creek-rope-swing-summer-afternoon-sunlight-trees | children swimming creek rope swing summer afternoon sunlight trees
HEAD: In small Midwestern towns, local baseball teams and Fourth of July celebrations brought everyone together.
BODY: Baseball was one of the great pleasures of rural summer life. Many small towns had teams made up of farmers, shopkeepers and young men. They played against teams from neighbouring towns on Sunday afternoons and summer evenings. Spectators sat on the grass or on wooden benches, cheering and eating popcorn. Boys dreamed of playing for the town team one day. The Fourth of July was the biggest public celebration of the year. Towns held parades with brass bands, old soldiers, decorated floats and children on bicycles. Picnics in the park followed, with speeches, games and races. Sack races, three-legged races and egg-and-spoon races were favourites. Spelling contests and pie competitions were also common. In the evening, fireworks lit the sky above the town square. Even during the Depression, communities found ways to hold these events. They were cheap, cheerful and shared by everyone. They reminded people that community life continued despite hard times.
===
IMG: ice-cream-social-church-lawn-evening-paper-lanterns-families-1933 | ice cream social church lawn evening paper lanterns families 1933
HEAD: Church ice cream socials raised money for the poor, one nickel at a time.
BODY: Summer evenings in rural America often brought ice cream socials. They were usually held on church lawns. Men took turns cranking large wooden ice cream makers packed with ice and salt. Vanilla, strawberry and peach were popular flavours. A dish of ice cream and a slice of cake might cost a nickel. The money often went to help poor families in the community. Children ran across the lawns in the twilight, catching fireflies in glass jars. Fireflies were one of the great sights of a Midwestern summer. On warm evenings after dark, thousands rose from the grass and fields, blinking with soft green light. Children watched them glow and then let them go. For farmers, warm summer nights also brought cautious hope. A good harvest would not make them rich at low prices. Yet it could feed the animals and the family, and pay something on the mortgage. In 1933, many farmers felt hopeful for the first time in years.
===
IMG: century-of-progress-chicago-1933-worlds-fair-art-deco-pavilions-lake-michigan | century of progress chicago 1933 worlds fair art deco pavilions lake michigan
HEAD: In 1933, Chicago held a world's fair called A Century of Progress.
BODY: The fair celebrated the hundredth anniversary of Chicago and the achievements of science and industry. It was built on the shore of Lake Michigan, partly on land created by filling in the lake. Its buildings were modern and bold, painted in bright colours of red, blue, yellow and orange. At night, electric lights in every colour transformed the grounds. The organisers wanted to show that science and technology could lead to a better future. That message had special meaning in the middle of the Depression. Millions of visitors came from across the country. Many saved for months to afford the trip by train, car or bus. The fair was so successful that it reopened for a second season in 1934. Altogether, it attracted more than forty million visits. Remarkably, it also paid for itself. For many rural visitors, it was their first experience of a great city. It offered a glimpse of modern life that most had only heard about on the radio.
===
IMG: grandmother-and-girl-on-train-window-seat-1933-fields-passing-excited | grandmother and girl on train window seat 1933 fields passing excited
HEAD: Railways offered special cheap fares so that ordinary families could visit the Chicago world's fair.
BODY: Travel was expensive in the 1930s, and few farm families could afford long journeys. To attract visitors, railways offered reduced fares to the Chicago fair. Many people used small savings set aside over many years. After so many bank failures, some older people kept their money hidden at home rather than in banks. Coins in an old sock or a tin box could pay for a once-in-a-lifetime trip. For many rural travellers, the journey itself was an adventure. Trains crossed broad farmland, small towns and great rivers. Travellers from Iowa crossed the Mississippi on long iron bridges. Arrival in Chicago could be overwhelming. It was one of the largest cities in the world, with tall buildings, crowds, noise and lights everywhere. Many visitors stayed with relatives in small city apartments to save money. Older travellers who had crossed the prairie by wagon in their youth often marvelled at the change. In a single lifetime, travel had been transformed.
===
IMG: sky-ride-towers-cable-cars-worlds-fair-1933-crowds-below-sunshine | sky ride towers cable cars worlds fair 1933 crowds below sunshine
HEAD: The Sky Ride at the 1933 world's fair carried visitors high above Lake Michigan between two tall towers.
BODY: The Century of Progress was filled with wonders. Great halls displayed machines, inventions and scientific exhibits. One exhibit, the House of Tomorrow, had glass walls and even a hangar for a private aeroplane. Visitors saw the newest cars, trains and engines. A display of life-sized moving dinosaurs attracted large crowds. The most famous attraction was the Sky Ride. Two steel towers more than six hundred feet tall stood on either side of a lagoon. Small rocket-shaped cars travelled along cables between them, high above the fairgrounds. From the cars, riders could look down on the crowds, the lake and the city. For many visitors, it was the highest they had ever been. The towers were among the tallest structures in Chicago at the time. The Sky Ride captured the spirit of the fair. It mixed engineering, excitement and optimism. It offered ordinary people a brief experience of a future that seemed fast, modern and full of possibility.
===
IMG: worlds-fair-night-1933-colored-lights-buildings-reflected-lagoon-fireworks | worlds fair night 1933 colored lights buildings reflected lagoon fireworks
HEAD: At night, the Chicago fair glowed with coloured electric light, at a time when most farms still had none.
BODY: As evening fell over the fairgrounds, the buildings lit up in brilliant colours. Red, blue, green and gold reflected in the lagoons. Searchlights swept across the sky, and music played. The fair even began with light. Its opening lights were switched on using starlight from the star Arcturus, captured by telescopes and turned into an electrical signal. For visitors from farms without electricity, the effect was overwhelming. Many had lived their whole lives by kerosene lamps. Older visitors could remember a world without electric lights, cars, aeroplanes, radios or telephones. In a single lifetime, they had seen more technological change than any earlier generation. Many visitors kept small souvenirs for the rest of their lives, such as metal models of the Sky Ride towers. Others wrote letters and school essays about what they had seen. The fair offered a vision of progress during a time of hardship. For millions, it was a reminder that the future might still be brighter than the present.
