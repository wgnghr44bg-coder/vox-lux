TOPIC: America in 1933, and the crash
===
IMG: iowa-farm-1933-white-farmhouse-red-barn-windmill-cornfields-autumn-sky | iowa farm 1933 white farmhouse red barn windmill cornfields autumn sky
HEAD: In 1933, the middle of America was a sea of cornfields and small farm towns.
BODY: Iowa lay in the broad, gently rolling heart of the country. Its cornfields stretched to the horizon under a wide open sky. Every ten miles or so stood a small town, with a main street, church steeples, a grain elevator and a water tower. The soil was rich and black, among the best farmland in the world. In summer, the corn grew taller than a man. In autumn, it turned golden brown and rustled in the wind. White farmhouses, red barns and turning windmills were scattered across the land. Long gravel roads ran straight between the fields. Yet by 1933, this peaceful landscape was caught in a national disaster. It was the time of the Great Depression, the worst economic crisis of the modern age. Banks, factories and shops had closed across the country. About one American worker in four had no job at all. Even in the quiet farm country, almost no family escaped its effects.
===
IMG: main-street-small-town-1933-closed-bank-sign-men-standing-waiting | main street small town 1933 closed bank sign men standing waiting
HEAD: For farmers, the early 1930s were among the hardest years they had ever known.
BODY: Farm prices fell so low that many families could no longer make a living. A bushel of corn that had once sold for close to a dollar now fetched as little as ten cents. In many cases, it cost more to grow a crop than it could be sold for. Some farmers burned corn in their stoves, because it was cheaper than buying coal. Debts were the greatest danger. Many farmers had borrowed money for land and machinery in better years. When prices collapsed, they could not keep up their payments. Thousands lost farms that their families had worked for generations. In some places, angry farmers gathered at foreclosure sales and tried to stop them. Others organised strikes and refused to send their produce to market. Yet most families simply worked harder and spent less. The crisis tested a way of life that had seemed secure for decades. It changed how a whole generation thought about money and land.
===
IMG: farm-family-1933-porch-father-overalls-mother-apron-girl-boy-grandmother | farm family 1933 porch father overalls mother apron girl boy grandmother
HEAD: In 1933, about a quarter of all Americans still lived on farms.
BODY: Farming was not a small part of American life in the 1930s. It was the daily world of millions of families. A typical Midwestern farm covered about a hundred and sixty acres. It was usually run by a family of several generations living under one roof. The father worked the fields and cared for the larger animals. The mother ran the house, the garden and the chickens. Children helped with chores from an early age, before and after school. Grandparents often lived with the family and passed on skills and memories. Many of them had arrived as pioneers in covered wagons, when the land was still open prairie. That shared history mattered during the Depression. Older people had lived through droughts, grasshopper plagues and earlier collapses in prices. Their experience offered calm when younger people felt afraid. The farm family was therefore both a household and a small working business, held together by habit, faith and necessity.
===
IMG: family-gathered-around-radio-kitchen-lamp-evening-1933-listening | family gathered around radio kitchen lamp evening 1933 listening
HEAD: Many people who lived through the Depression later remembered it as a time of great kindness.
BODY: The Depression brought real hardship. Yet it also brought out generosity in many communities. Neighbours shared food, tools and labour. Churches collected clothing and organised meals. Families took in relatives who had lost their homes or jobs. Shopkeepers gave credit, often knowing it might never be repaid. People learned to be patient, careful and grateful for small things. In later years, many survivors described the period with mixed feelings. They remembered fear and worry, but also closeness and shared effort. Historians have collected hundreds of these memories through interviews and letters. They show that hardship did not affect everyone in the same way. Some families suffered terribly, while others managed to hold on. Even so, a common thread runs through many accounts. People who lived through those years often said the experience taught them what really mattered. For the rest of their lives, many of them saved string, avoided debt and never wasted food.
===
IMG: new-york-stock-exchange-1929-crowds-wall-street-worried-men-hats | new york stock exchange 1929 crowds wall street worried men hats
HEAD: The roots of the Great Depression lay in the booming years of the 1920s.
BODY: For many Americans, the 1920s were a time of growth and optimism. People called them the Roaring Twenties. Jobs were plentiful in the cities, and wages were rising. New products filled the shops, from cars and radios to refrigerators and washing machines. Jazz, dancing and films became part of everyday life. Many people began to buy shares in companies on the stock market in New York. Share prices rose year after year, and many investors believed they would keep rising. Ordinary people, from shopkeepers to teachers, borrowed money to buy shares. They hoped to grow rich quickly and easily. Some did, at least for a while. Yet the prosperity was uneven. Farmers had struggled with low prices for most of the decade. Many workers earned too little to buy all the goods being produced. Beneath the excitement, the economy rested on borrowed money and confidence. Both could disappear very quickly.
===
IMG: newspaper-headline-1929-crash-man-reading-on-street-corner-shocked | newspaper headline 1929 crash man reading on street corner shocked
HEAD: In October 1929, the American stock market crashed.
BODY: Share prices began to fall in the autumn of 1929. At first the decline was slow, then it became faster. Investors panicked and tried to sell their shares all at once. On the twenty-ninth of October, a day remembered as Black Tuesday, the market collapsed. Billions of dollars in value disappeared within hours. Many people lost everything they had invested, including money they had borrowed. Yet the crash was only the beginning. Over the next few years, the economy spiralled downward. People had less money, so they bought less. Factories produced less and needed fewer workers. Unemployed workers then had even less to spend. It was like a wheel turning downward, faster and faster. Economists still debate exactly why the downturn became so severe. Bank failures, falling trade and government policies all played a part. What is clear is that a crisis on Wall Street became a crisis in almost every American town.
===
IMG: people-lined-up-outside-closed-bank-1931-small-town-anxious-crowd | people lined up outside closed bank 1931 small town anxious crowd
HEAD: Between 1930 and 1933, thousands of American banks failed.
BODY: When people grew afraid, they rushed to their banks to withdraw their savings. Yet banks did not keep all their deposits in the vault. Much of the money had been lent to others. When too many people asked for their money at once, a bank could run out of cash and close its doors. Its depositors then lost their savings. In those days, there was no national insurance to protect them. Small towns suffered especially badly. A local bank might hold the savings of almost every family in the district. The money of a lifetime could vanish in a single morning. On the farms, trouble had begun even earlier. Many farmers had borrowed heavily when prices were high during and after the Great War. Now they could not repay their loans, and their failures weakened the rural banks further. By 1932, despair was spreading across the countryside. Shanty towns built from boxes and scrap metal appeared near the cities. People bitterly called them Hoovervilles, after President Herbert Hoover.
===
IMG: shanty-town-hooverville-tin-shacks-river-bank-smoke-cooking-fires | shanty town hooverville tin shacks river bank smoke cooking fires
HEAD: In November 1932, Americans elected Franklin Delano Roosevelt as their new president.
BODY: Roosevelt had been governor of New York. He came from a wealthy family, and as a young man he had been struck by polio. The illness left him unable to walk without help. Yet he was cheerful and confident, and he promised the American people a New Deal. Few people knew exactly what that would mean. But many hoped it meant change. Roosevelt won by a large margin. In those days, a new president did not take office until March. The country therefore waited through four long months. That winter was the worst of the Depression. More banks failed, and more people lost their jobs. In many cities, soup kitchens and breadlines grew longer. Hoovervilles of tin and timber spread along riverbanks and railway lines. Smoke from cooking fires rose over them on cold mornings. The long wait showed the danger of a slow handover of power. A later change to the Constitution moved the start of a presidential term to January.

TOPIC: Farm families, and the farm
===
IMG: iowa-farmer-1933-overalls-straw-hat-leaning-on-fence-cornfield-thoughtful | iowa farmer 1933 overalls straw hat leaning on fence cornfield thoughtful
HEAD: Many Iowa farmers in 1933 worked land their fathers had bought when it was still open prairie.
BODY: The farms of Iowa were often only one or two generations old. Settlers had bought them in the late nineteenth century, when tall prairie grass still covered the land. By 1933, many sons farmed the same quarter section their fathers had broken with a plough. They knew every field, fence post and corner of it. A typical farm grew corn, oats and hay, and raised hogs and a few cattle. Horses still pulled ploughs and wagons on most farms. Some farmers had bought tractors on credit during the good years of the 1920s. When prices collapsed, many could barely afford the fuel. Tractors stood idle in sheds, and horses returned to the fields. Horses had one great advantage. They ate oats grown on the farm itself, while tractors needed fuel bought with cash. During the Depression, that difference mattered enormously. Older methods returned because they cost less, and many farmers were glad to fall back on them.
===
IMG: farm-wife-1933-flour-sack-apron-feeding-chickens-yard-morning-sun | farm wife 1933 flour sack apron feeding chickens yard morning sun
HEAD: On many farms, the wife's eggs and butter brought in the only steady cash.
BODY: Farm wives did far more than housework. They cooked, cleaned, washed, sewed and mended. They also kept large vegetable gardens and flocks of chickens. Eggs and butter could be sold or traded in town every week. In the worst years, this small income often paid for flour, sugar, coffee and salt. Historians call it the egg money, and it kept many households afloat. Farm women were also skilled managers. They planned meals around what the garden produced. They stored food for winter and found uses for every scrap. Many of them had grown up on farms themselves and had learned these skills as girls. Daughters helped from an early age, before and after school. Some dreamed of becoming teachers, writers or nurses in the cities. The Depression made such dreams harder to reach. Yet it also showed how much depended on women's work. Without it, many farm families could not have survived those years.
===
IMG: boy-nine-overalls-barefoot-with-farm-dog-collie-barn-door-summer | boy nine overalls barefoot with farm dog collie barn door summer
HEAD: Farm children in the 1930s did real work from an early age.
BODY: On a family farm, every pair of hands mattered. Boys of eight or nine fed animals, carried water and brought in wood for the stove. Girls helped with cooking, washing and caring for younger children. Both worked in the fields at harvest time. Chores came before school in the morning and again after school in the evening. Yet farm childhood was not only work. Children roamed the fields with their dogs, swam in creeks and climbed trees. They played baseball with home-made balls and invented games of their own. Grandparents often lived with the family and told stories of earlier hardships. Many had seen droughts, blizzards and grasshopper plagues. They remembered the hard years of the 1890s, when prices also collapsed. Their message to the young was often the same. The country had come through hard times before, and it would do so again. That long view gave families patience in a time when the future seemed uncertain.
===
IMG: farmhouse-kitchen-1933-cast-iron-stove-oilcloth-table-calendar-wall | farmhouse kitchen 1933 cast iron stove oilcloth table calendar wall
HEAD: During the Depression, many farm incomes fell to a small fraction of what they had been.
BODY: In good years, a Midwestern farm might earn a thousand dollars or more. By 1932, many earned only a few hundred, and some earned almost nothing. After mortgage payments and taxes, little or no money remained. There was nothing for new clothes, shoes or winter coats. Visits to the dentist or the doctor were put off for as long as possible. Families spent money only on what was absolutely necessary. Clothes were worn until they fell apart, then mended and worn again. String, paper, buttons, old nails and bits of wood were all saved. A popular saying captured the spirit of the time. Use it up, wear it out, make it do, or do without. Yet farm families had one important advantage over many city workers. They could grow most of their own food. Money was scarce, but hunger was less common on working farms than in the cities. What they lacked was cash, not bread.
===
IMG: farm-dawn-1933-farmer-with-lantern-walking-to-barn-frost-windmill | farm dawn 1933 farmer with lantern walking to barn frost windmill
HEAD: In 1933, only about one American farm in ten had electricity.
BODY: Power lines had reached most American cities by the 1930s. Yet the countryside was largely left in the dark. Electric companies considered it too expensive to run lines to scattered farms. As a result, most farm families lived much as their grandparents had. They lit kerosene lamps in the evening and cooked on wood or coal stoves. They pumped water by hand or relied on windmills. Work began before dawn. Farmers rose around five o'clock, dressed in the dark and lit the kitchen lamp. Then they carried lanterns to the barn for the morning chores. The lack of electricity also meant no refrigerators, no electric irons and no washing machines. Farm work was therefore physically harder than city work in almost every way. This gap became a major political issue. In 1935, a new federal agency began to bring power to rural America. Within a generation, most farms were connected.
===
IMG: father-and-son-milking-cows-by-lantern-light-barn-stalls-steam-breath | father and son milking cows by lantern light barn stalls steam breath
HEAD: On Midwestern farms, hogs turned corn into something worth more than corn.
BODY: Morning chores began in the barn. Horses were fed oats and hay. Cows were milked by hand into tin buckets, a task done twice every day. Barn cats often gathered nearby, hoping for a little warm milk. Then the hogs were fed, usually with corn, skim milk and kitchen scraps. Hogs were among the most important animals on many Iowa farms. Farmers often said that a hog was simply corn on four legs. Feeding corn to hogs turned a bulky, cheap crop into pork, which could be sold for more. This system had made the Corn Belt prosperous in earlier decades. During the Depression, however, even pork prices collapsed. In 1933, the government took a drastic step to raise prices. It paid farmers to slaughter millions of young pigs. Many people were shocked that food was destroyed while others went hungry. Some of the meat was given to families on relief, but the programme remained deeply controversial.
===
IMG: farm-kitchen-breakfast-1933-pancakes-eggs-bacon-family-at-table-lamp | farm kitchen breakfast 1933 pancakes eggs bacon family at table lamp
HEAD: Farm breakfasts in the 1930s were large, because the working day was long and hard.
BODY: Breakfast on a Midwestern farm was a substantial meal. It often included eggs from the farm's own chickens and bacon from its own hogs. There might be pancakes, biscuits or fried potatoes, with fresh milk and strong coffee. Bread was baked at home, usually once or twice a week. Water came from a hand pump at the sink or in the yard. Many families said grace before eating, thanking God for food and asking for help in hard times. After breakfast, the day's work began. It followed the rhythm of the seasons. Spring brought ploughing and planting. Summer meant cultivating corn and cutting hay and oats. Autumn was harvest time. Winter was spent mending fences, tools and harness, and cutting firewood. All year round, animals had to be fed and milked morning and evening. Farm life left little room for rest. Yet a good breakfast gave families the strength for days that often lasted from dawn until dark.
===
IMG: farmer-ploughing-field-team-of-horses-spring-1933-black-soil-gulls | farmer ploughing field team of horses spring 1933 black soil gulls
HEAD: A farm wife's week in the 1930s followed a fixed pattern of washing, ironing and baking.
BODY: Housework on a farm without electricity was heavy labour. Monday was usually washing day. Water was heated on the stove and clothes were scrubbed on a washboard in a large tub. Tuesday was for ironing, with heavy irons warmed on the same stove. Other days brought baking, cleaning, churning butter and making soap from fat and lye. In summer, gardens had to be weeded and vegetables preserved for winter. Grandmothers and daughters often shared the work. Out in the fields, men ploughed behind teams of horses, turning the black soil in long straight furrows. Gulls and blackbirds followed the plough, searching for worms. Every task took time, strength and skill. Historians estimate that farm women in this period often worked longer hours than anyone else in the household. Yet their labour rarely appeared in official statistics. It was simply expected. In the evening, families often sat on the porch to rest as the sun went down over the fields.

TOPIC: The small town, and the bank holiday
===
IMG: harlan-iowa-1933-main-street-brick-shops-model-a-fords-courthouse-square | harlan iowa 1933 main street brick shops model a fords courthouse square
HEAD: Saturday was town day for farm families across the Midwest.
BODY: On Saturday afternoons, farm families travelled into the nearest town. Many drove old Model T Fords, bought second-hand years earlier. Others still came by horse and wagon. Small towns such as Harlan, Iowa, with about three thousand people, served as centres for the surrounding countryside. Many had a courthouse in the middle of a square, with a clock tower, lawns and benches under tall elm trees. Around the square stood shops and offices in brick buildings two or three storeys high. There was usually a grocery, a hardware store, a drugstore, a bank, a barber and a café. Larger towns also had a movie theatre. Churches with white steeples stood on the side streets. The weekly trip was more than shopping. It was the main social event of the week. For people who spent most days alone in the fields, town offered news, conversation and company. Even in hard times, Saturday in town remained a cherished routine.
===
IMG: general-store-interior-1933-shelves-cans-barrels-counter-farmers-wives-trading | general store interior 1933 shelves cans barrels counter farmers wives trading
HEAD: In the Depression, many rural shops survived by trading goods and giving credit.
BODY: On Saturdays, small-town streets filled with wagons and old cars. Farm wives brought eggs and butter to the grocery store. Instead of cash, they often traded them directly for flour, sugar, coffee and salt. Grocers also gave credit to families they knew. Many kept long lists of debts that might never be paid. Refusing credit could mean that a neighbour's children went hungry, and most shopkeepers found that hard to do. Men gathered on street corners in overalls and hats. They talked about the weather, crops, prices and the latest farm foreclosures. The mood was often gloomy, yet humour remained. Jokes about the Depression were common in newspapers and on the radio. Farmers sometimes said they had been in a depression for years before the cities noticed. There was truth in that remark. Farm prices had been weak for most of the 1920s. The national crisis simply made an old rural problem far worse.
===
IMG: children-outside-drugstore-soda-fountain-window-1933-looking-in-nickels | children outside drugstore soda fountain window 1933 looking in nickels
HEAD: For many children in the 1930s, a nickel at the soda fountain was the best treat of the week.
BODY: Saturday in town was a highlight for farm children. Some visited the small public library to borrow books. Others met friends on the square to play marbles or talk about baseball. If they were lucky, a parent gave them a nickel. Many spent it at the drugstore soda fountain, on an ice cream cone or a bottle of soda. Drugstore counters were popular meeting places in small towns across America. Yet signs of hard times were everywhere. Some shops stood empty, with dusty windows and signs reading closed or for rent. Unemployed men stood on corners with nothing to do. Churches saw more people asking for help. In many towns, the bank building itself had become a reminder of loss. Thousands of rural banks had failed since 1930. Their stone columns and locked doors stood on the square for everyone to see. For a whole generation, a simple nickel came to represent real value.
===
IMG: shuttered-bank-building-stone-columns-notice-on-door-pigeons-quiet-square | shuttered bank building stone columns notice on door pigeons quiet square
HEAD: When a small-town bank failed, many families lost savings they had built up over decades.
BODY: Bank failures struck rural America especially hard. In 1931 alone, more than two thousand banks closed across the country. In a small town, depositors often learned the news in the simplest way. They arrived in the morning and found the doors locked and a notice posted on them. Savings set aside for education, emergencies or old age disappeared overnight. Some depositors later recovered part of their money, but often years later and only in part. Many never recovered anything. The emotional effect was lasting. People who had trusted banks all their lives became suspicious of them. Some kept their money hidden at home for decades afterwards. Older relatives often offered a different kind of comfort. They said that knowledge and skills could not be taken away, unlike money. Families who had lost savings often became even more determined to educate their children. The empty bank on the square remained a quiet symbol of the crisis long after it ended.
===
IMG: fdr-inauguration-march-1933-capitol-steps-crowd-grey-sky-washington | fdr inauguration march 1933 capitol steps crowd grey sky washington
HEAD: On the fourth of March, 1933, Franklin Delano Roosevelt became president of the United States.
BODY: It was a cold, grey day in Washington. A large crowd gathered in front of the Capitol to watch the new president take the oath of office. Across the country, millions of people listened on the radio. The nation was in a dangerous state that morning. In the weeks before, banking panic had spread from state to state. Depositors rushed to withdraw their money, afraid that more banks would fail. Governors responded by ordering banks to close or to limit withdrawals. By the day of the inauguration, almost every bank in America was closed or restricted. The financial system of the country had nearly stopped. Business could barely function, and many people had no access to their money at all. Few presidents have taken office at a more anxious moment. The atmosphere was tense and uncertain. Everyone waited to hear what the new leader would say, and whether he had any real plan to stop the collapse.
===
IMG: family-listening-to-radio-kitchen-march-1933-faces-hopeful-quiet | family listening to radio kitchen march 1933 faces hopeful quiet
HEAD: In his first inaugural address, Roosevelt said that the only thing to fear was fear itself.
BODY: Roosevelt stood at the front of the platform, holding the arm of his son for support. Polio had left his legs weak, and he could not stand unaided. He took the oath and then began his speech. His voice was strong, clear and confident. Early in the address came the sentence that people remembered for the rest of their lives. The only thing we have to fear, he said, is fear itself. He spoke of nameless, unreasoning terror that paralysed efforts to recover. He promised action, and quickly. In farmhouses and city apartments, people listened in silence. Many later recalled the speech as a turning point in their feelings. Nothing had yet changed in the economy. Yet the tone of the government had changed. After years of discouraging news, a leader seemed ready to act. Confidence matters a great deal in a financial crisis. Roosevelt's words helped to restore some of it, even before his policies took effect.
===
IMG: closed-bank-doors-notice-bank-holiday-march-1933-townspeople-reading | closed bank doors notice bank holiday march 1933 townspeople reading
HEAD: Two days after taking office, Roosevelt closed every bank in the country for a national bank holiday.
BODY: On the sixth of March, 1933, Roosevelt ordered all American banks to close. He called it a bank holiday, though it was nothing of the kind. The pause gave the government time to inspect the banks. Sound banks would be allowed to reopen. Weak ones would be helped, reorganised or closed for good. Congress quickly passed an emergency law to support the plan. For about a week, very little money moved through the country. Shops gave credit or accepted written promises. Some towns printed their own temporary money on paper, wood or even leather. Farmers traded eggs for flour and chickens for shoes. Then, on the twelfth of March, Roosevelt spoke to the nation on the radio. He explained in simple words how banks worked and why they had failed. He told listeners that the reopened banks would be safe. It was a calm, direct talk, almost like a conversation, and it reached millions of homes.
===
IMG: people-queuing-to-deposit-money-reopened-bank-march-1933-relieved-faces | people queuing to deposit money reopened bank march 1933 relieved faces
HEAD: When the banks reopened in March 1933, people lined up to deposit money rather than withdraw it.
BODY: Millions of Americans heard Roosevelt's radio talk on the banks, and many believed him. When the banks reopened the next day, the feared rush did not come. Instead, people began bringing money back. Within weeks, deposits exceeded withdrawals, and the panic was over. This was the first of many radio talks Roosevelt gave over the next twelve years. They became known as the fireside chats. Their simple, friendly tone helped him connect with ordinary listeners in a new way. Later in 1933, Congress passed a law creating federal insurance for bank deposits. From the beginning of 1934, ordinary savings were protected even if a bank failed. That protection still exists today. It came too late for those who had already lost their savings. Yet it transformed public trust in banks. Waves of bank failures had been common in American history. After deposit insurance was introduced, such panics became far less frequent.

TOPIC: The radio, and living on very little
===
IMG: wooden-cathedral-radio-1933-kitchen-shelf-battery-glowing-dial | wooden cathedral radio 1933 kitchen shelf battery glowing dial
HEAD: In the 1930s, the radio became the most precious possession in many American homes.
BODY: Many radios of the period were large wooden sets with rounded tops. Their shape reminded people of church windows, and they were often called cathedral radios. On farms without electricity, radios ran on heavy batteries. These had to be taken to town to be recharged every few weeks. Families therefore listened carefully and saved the battery for important programmes. Many radios had been bought during the prosperous 1920s, when credit was easy. During the Depression, few families could have afforded a new one. Yet those who had a set rarely gave it up. Radio became a window on the world. It brought news from Washington, New York and Europe. It carried farm prices, weather forecasts, music, comedy and drama. By the late 1930s, most American households owned a radio. Historians often describe the decade as the golden age of radio. For isolated rural families, the impact was especially great. The wider world suddenly entered their kitchens every evening.
===
IMG: father-adjusting-radio-dial-family-gathered-close-evening-lamplight | father adjusting radio dial family gathered close evening lamplight
HEAD: Farmers relied on daily radio market reports to follow prices for corn, wheat and hogs.
BODY: Agricultural radio programmes became essential in the 1920s and 1930s. Stations across the Midwest broadcast market reports at midday. An announcer read out the prices for grain and livestock in Chicago and other markets. Weather forecasts followed, which were vital for planting and harvesting. Many farmers came in from the fields at noon to listen while they ate. The reports helped them decide when to sell and when to wait. In the evening, families often gathered around the set for an hour or two. Chairs were pulled close, because battery radios were not very loud. Older relatives might knit or mend while listening. Children often sat on the floor, as near to the speaker as possible. Listening together became a family ritual in millions of homes. It replaced older evening habits such as reading aloud. Yet it served a similar purpose. At the end of a hard day, it gave families a shared moment of rest and attention.
===
IMG: children-sitting-on-floor-close-to-radio-rapt-attention-dog-by-stove | children sitting on floor close to radio rapt attention dog by stove
HEAD: Radio drama in the 1930s depended on sound effects and the listener's imagination.
BODY: Comedy shows were among the most popular programmes of the decade. Comic duos and quarrelling married couples made millions of listeners laugh each week. Music filled many evening hours, from dance bands in big-city hotels to country songs and church hymns. On clear nights, distant stations could be heard across hundreds of miles. Children loved adventure serials. One famous programme began on a Detroit station in 1933. It told of a masked rider of the old West who fought for justice with a faithful companion and a white horse. Each episode opened with stirring music and galloping hooves. Radio dramas used sound effects to create entire worlds. Studio technicians made doors creak, footsteps echo and thunder roll. Horses were often imitated with coconut shells. Many listeners said radio was better than the movies, because they could imagine the pictures themselves. That remark captures something important. Radio asked its audience to take part, and millions gladly did.
===
IMG: radio-listening-whole-family-laughing-comedy-show-kitchen-warm-night | radio listening whole family laughing comedy show kitchen warm night
HEAD: Radio gave Americans in the Depression a shared national experience.
BODY: By the 1930s, national networks broadcast the same programmes across the country. Families in farmhouses, city apartments and small-town homes listened at the same time. They laughed at the same jokes and followed the same news. This was something new in American life. Before radio, regional differences in daily experience had been much stronger. During the Depression, this shared listening had special value. It made people feel less alone in their difficulties. Comedy offered relief, and music offered comfort. Presidential talks gave reassurance and a sense of direction. Advertisers also discovered radio's power. Many programmes were sponsored by soap and food companies, which is why later dramas came to be called soap operas. When the evening's programmes ended, sets were switched off and the glowing dials slowly faded. Homes became quiet again. Yet the experience left a lasting mark. Radio helped to create a common culture that later television would continue.
===
IMG: mother-sewing-dress-from-printed-flour-sacks-treadle-machine-kitchen | mother sewing dress from printed flour sacks treadle machine kitchen
HEAD: Flour and feed companies began printing their cotton sacks with pretty patterns, because women made clothes from them.
BODY: In the Depression, nothing was wasted. Farm wives had long made towels, aprons and pillowcases from plain cotton flour sacks. As money grew scarce, they used the sacks for clothing as well. Manufacturers noticed this habit and saw an opportunity. By the late 1930s, many companies sold flour and animal feed in sacks printed with flowers, checks and stripes. Women chose their flour partly by the pattern on the sack. When they had collected enough sacks of the same design, they could make a dress or a shirt. Some sacks even came with printed patterns for dolls or quilts. Many women sewed on treadle machines powered by their feet, since farms often had no electricity. The practice continued well into the 1940s. Today, surviving feed sack dresses are collected by museums. They show how ordinary families turned necessity into something practical and even beautiful. Businesses, in turn, learned to follow what their customers were really doing.
===
IMG: girl-in-flour-sack-print-dress-twirling-farmyard-proud-smile | girl in flour sack print dress twirling farmyard proud smile
HEAD: In rural schools of the 1930s, many children wore clothes sewn from flour and feed sacks.
BODY: Flour sack clothing became common across the American countryside. Girls wore dresses with little flowers, checks or daisies. Boys wore shirts made from feed sacks. Babies wore diapers made from sugar sacks. Because so many families used them, there was little shame attached to them. Many people remembered them later with affection. Clothes that wore out were mended rather than replaced. Patches covered holes at the elbows and knees. Socks were darned again and again. Collars were turned so that the worn side was hidden. Coats outgrown by one child were cut down and remade for a younger brother or sister. When clothes could no longer be worn, they were cut into strips. The strips were braided into rugs or sewn into quilts. Quilting bees brought women together to share work and conversation. Many quilts from this period survive today. Their patchwork patterns preserve tiny pieces of everyday clothing from an entire generation.

HEAD: During the Depression, many families preserved hundreds of jars of vegetables and fruit each summer.
BODY: Food was never wasted in the 1930s. Leftovers were eaten the next day, turned into soup or fed to the hogs. Bread was baked at home, butter was churned and soap was made from fat and lye. In summer, women spent weeks canning food from their gardens. Beans, tomatoes, corn, peas, pickles, peaches and jam were sealed in glass jars. Hundreds of jars were stored on cellar shelves for the winter. Government extension agents taught safe canning methods in rural communities. Many town families also planted gardens in backyards and empty lots. City dwellers grew vegetables on rooftops and in window boxes. Meals were simple, built around beans, potatoes, cornbread and soup. Some dishes were made from almost nothing, such as bread with gravy or fried cornmeal mush. Things that broke were repaired with wire, nails and patience. Shoes were resoled with pieces of old tyres. The Depression made thrift a skill, and many families practised it for the rest of their lives.

HEAD: Depression-era children made many of their own toys from scraps.
BODY: Few families could afford shop-bought toys in the 1930s. Children therefore made their own. Kites were built from newspaper, sticks and string. Slingshots came from forked branches and old inner tubes. Baseballs were made from tightly wound string covered with tape. Girls cut paper dolls from old mail-order catalogues and made paper clothes for them. Corncobs became dolls, and empty thread spools became spinning tops. Tyre swings hung from the branches of farmyard trees. Marbles were a favourite game, and a good set of marbles was a real treasure. Many adults who grew up in the Depression later remembered these simple games with pleasure. They also carried lifelong habits from those years. They saved string, paper bags, rubber bands and old jars. Their grandchildren sometimes found these habits strange. Yet they reflected experience rather than eccentricity. People who had once lived with very little never quite forgot how valuable small things could be.
