TOPIC: America in 1933, and the crash
===
IMG: iowa-farm-1933-white-farmhouse-red-barn-windmill-cornfields-autumn-sky | iowa farm 1933 white farmhouse red barn windmill cornfields autumn sky
HEAD: In 1933, the middle of America was a sea of cornfields and small farm towns.
BODY: Here we're in the state of Iowa, in the great flat heart of the country, where the cornfields stretch away to the horizon, in every direction, under a huge, wide sky. Picture yourself standing at the edge of a cornfield, with the wind in your hair. Here, in the gently rolling farmland, there are small towns, every ten miles or so, each with its main street, its church steeples, its grain elevator, and its water tower. It's a beautiful land. Rich, black soil, some of the best farmland in the world. In the summer, the corn grows tall and green, higher than a man's head. In the autumn, it turns golden brown, and rustles in the wind. There are white farmhouses, and red barns, and windmills, turning slowly, pumping water for the animals. And there are long, straight gravel roads, running between the fields, with a cloud of dust behind every car. But in 1933, this land is in trouble. All of America is in trouble. The whole world is in trouble. It's the time of the Great Depression. The worst economic crisis in modern history. Banks have closed. Factories have closed. Shops have closed. Millions of people have lost their jobs, and their savings, and their homes. Can you see it, stretching away in every direction? One worker in every four has no job at all.
===
IMG: main-street-small-town-1933-closed-bank-sign-men-standing-waiting | main street small town 1933 closed bank sign men standing waiting
HEAD: For the farmers, it's a very hard time.
BODY: The prices for their crops have fallen so low that they can't make a living. If you'd been a farmer then, you'd have watched every penny. A bushel of corn, which sold for almost a dollar a few years ago, now sells for ten cents. It costs more to grow it than you can sell it for. Some farmers burn their corn in their stoves, for heat, because it's cheaper than coal. And many farmers can't pay their debts, and lose their farms. If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you're warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let's begin.
===
IMG: farm-family-1933-porch-father-overalls-mother-apron-girl-boy-grandmother | farm family 1933 porch father overalls mother apron girl boy grandmother
HEAD: In this story, we'll follow one family through the year 1933.
BODY: The Millers, who have a farm a few miles outside a small town called Harlan, in western Iowa. You'll get to know them well, I think. There's Walt, the father, who has farmed this land all his life, like his father before him. Ruth, the mother, who keeps the house, and the garden, and the chickens, and the family together. June, their daughter, who is twelve. Eddie, their son, who is nine. And Grandma Hattie, Walt's mother, who has seen hard times before. We'll see how they live, and how they manage, in the hardest of times. How they save, and mend, and make do, and share. We'll listen to the radio with them, as a new president speaks to the nation. We'll meet the travellers who knock at the back door, asking for a meal. We'll go to the county fair, and help with the harvest, and gather around the table at Thanksgiving. And we'll see how a whole country slowly began to find its way, out of the dark.
===
IMG: family-gathered-around-radio-kitchen-lamp-evening-1933-listening | family gathered around radio kitchen lamp evening 1933 listening
HEAD: It was a hard time.
BODY: But it was also a time of great kindness, and courage, and community. If you'd lived through it, you'd never forget it. People helped each other, in a way they hadn't done before. They shared what little they had. They learned to be patient, and careful, and grateful for small things. And many of the people who lived through it said, later, that it taught them what really mattered in life. So let's go to Iowa, in the year 1933. Let's walk down the gravel road, between the fields, to the white farmhouse with the red barn. And let's meet the Millers, as they begin another day, in the hard, quiet years of the Depression.
===
IMG: new-york-stock-exchange-1929-crowds-wall-street-worried-men-hats | new york stock exchange 1929 crowds wall street worried men hats
HEAD: To understand the Great Depression, we must go back a few years.
BODY: To the 1920s. You might think the trouble began on a farm. It didn't. For many Americans, the 1920s were a wonderful time. People called them the Roaring Twenties. There were jobs, and money, and new things to buy. Cars, and radios, and refrigerators, and washing machines. There was jazz music, and dancing, and movies. Everything seemed to be getting better and better. Many people began to buy shares in companies, on the stock market, in New York. The prices of the shares kept going up, and up, and up. People thought they'd go up for ever. Ordinary people, shopkeepers, and teachers, and even farmers, borrowed money, to buy shares. They thought they'd get rich, quickly and easily. Some of them did, for a while.
===
IMG: newspaper-headline-1929-crash-man-reading-on-street-corner-shocked | newspaper headline 1929 crash man reading on street corner shocked
HEAD: Then, in October 1929, the bubble burst.
BODY: The prices of the shares began to fall. You'd have seen worried faces on every street corner. At first slowly, then faster and faster. People panicked, and tried to sell their shares, all at once. On one terrible day, which became known as Black Tuesday, the stock market crashed. Billions of dollars disappeared, in a few hours. Many people lost everything they had. The crash was only the beginning. Over the next few years, things got worse, and worse. People had less money, so they bought less. So the factories made less, and needed fewer workers. So more people lost their jobs, and had even less money. It was like a wheel, turning downward, faster and faster. Nobody seemed to know how to stop it.
===
IMG: people-lined-up-outside-closed-bank-1931-small-town-anxious-crowd | people lined up outside closed bank 1931 small town anxious crowd
HEAD: Then the banks began to fail.
BODY: When people were afraid, they rushed to the banks, to take out their savings. You'd walk to your bank and find the doors locked. But the banks had lent the money to other people, and didn't have it in the vaults. So the banks ran out of money, and closed their doors. And the people lost their savings. Thousands of banks failed, all over America. In the small towns, it was a disaster. The savings of a whole lifetime could disappear in a day. On the farms, the trouble had begun even earlier. After the Great War, in the early 1920s, the prices for crops had already fallen. Many farmers had borrowed money, to buy more land, and machines, when prices were high. Now, they couldn't pay it back. And when the Depression came, prices fell even further. By 1932, many farmers were in despair. The president, Herbert Hoover, tried to help. But many people felt that he didn't do enough, or didn't understand. Shanty towns of homeless people, built from boxes, and scrap metal, and old cars, appeared on the edges of the cities. People called them Hoovervilles. It wasn't quite fair to Hoover. But it showed how people felt.
===
IMG: shanty-town-hooverville-tin-shacks-river-bank-smoke-cooking-fires | shanty town hooverville tin shacks river bank smoke cooking fires
HEAD: In November 1932, there was an election.
BODY: The American people chose a new president. You'd see posters nailed to fence posts all along the road. His name was Franklin Delano Roosevelt. He was the governor of New York. He came from a rich family, and as a young man he had been struck by polio, which left him unable to walk without help. But he was cheerful, and confident, and he promised the American people a New Deal. People didn't know exactly what that meant. But they hoped it meant change. In those days, a new president didn't start work until March. So for four long months, the country waited. And during that winter, the worst winter of the Depression, things got worse than ever. More banks failed. More people lost their jobs. And in the farmhouse near Harlan, Iowa, the Millers waited too, and hoped, and prayed, for better times.

TOPIC: The family, and the farm
BRIDGE: Now that you know what kind of year this is, let's go and meet the people who live in that white farmhouse. Shall we knock on the door?
===
IMG: iowa-farmer-1933-overalls-straw-hat-leaning-on-fence-cornfield-thoughtful | iowa farmer 1933 overalls straw hat leaning on fence cornfield thoughtful
HEAD: Walt Miller is forty-two years old.
BODY: He was born on this farm, in the white farmhouse, in 1891. If you shook his hand, you'd feel how rough it was from years of work. His father came here from Ohio, as a young man, and bought the land, a hundred and sixty acres, when it was still prairie grass. Walt has worked on it all his life. He knows every field, every fence post, every corner of it. He is tall, and thin, and quiet, with a face burned brown by the sun, and big, rough hands. Walt grows corn, and oats, and hay, and he raises hogs, and a few cattle. He has four horses, for pulling the plough, and the wagon. He bought a tractor in 1928, on credit, when times were good. Now, he can hardly afford the fuel for it. So the tractor sits in the shed, most of the time, and the horses do the work again, as they always did. Walt doesn't mind. He likes the horses better anyway.
===
IMG: farm-wife-1933-flour-sack-apron-feeding-chickens-yard-morning-sun | farm wife 1933 flour sack apron feeding chickens yard morning sun
HEAD: Ruth Miller is thirty-nine.
BODY: She grew up on a farm too, a few miles away. You'd find her in the kitchen, almost always, with her sleeves rolled up. She met Walt at a church social, when she was eighteen, and they were married a year later. She is a strong, practical, cheerful woman, who never stops working. She cooks, and cleans, and washes, and sews, and mends. She keeps a big vegetable garden, and a flock of chickens, and sells the eggs in town, for a little money. She can make something out of nothing. June is twelve. She is a serious, bookish girl, with brown braids, and freckles. She loves to read, more than anything. She has read every book in the little school library, twice. She wants to be a teacher, or a writer, or perhaps a newspaper reporter, in a big city. She helps her mother in the house, and the garden. I've always liked her. But her head is always somewhere else, in a story.
===
IMG: boy-nine-overalls-barefoot-with-farm-dog-collie-barn-door-summer | boy nine overalls barefoot with farm dog collie barn door summer
HEAD: Eddie is nine.
BODY: He is the opposite of his sister. You'd hear him long before you saw him. He hates sitting still. He loves being outside, with the animals, and the machines, and his dog, Rusty, a big, friendly collie. He helps his father with the chores, and follows him everywhere. He wants to be a farmer, like his father, and his grandfather. Or perhaps a baseball player. Or perhaps a pilot. He changes his mind every week. And there's Grandma Hattie. She is seventy-one. She came to Iowa with her husband, in a covered wagon, in 1880, when she was eighteen. She has seen many hard times. Droughts, and grasshoppers, and blizzards, and the hard years of the 1890s, when prices fell, and many farmers lost their land. She says that the country has been through hard times before, and it will get through this one too. She is the calm centre of the family. The Millers are luckier than many. They own their farm, and they still have it. They have a small mortgage, which Walt took out to buy the tractor. And they are behind with the payments. But the bank has been patient, so far. They grow most of their own food, so they never go hungry. They have a roof over their heads, and a warm stove, and each other.
===
IMG: farmhouse-kitchen-1933-cast-iron-stove-oilcloth-table-calendar-wall | farmhouse kitchen 1933 cast iron stove oilcloth table calendar wall
HEAD: But there's almost no money.
BODY: In a good year, the farm used to earn a thousand dollars or more. You'd count the coins in the sugar tin, and count them again. Last year, it earned less than two hundred. There's nothing left, after the mortgage payments, and the taxes. No money for new clothes, or new shoes, or a new coat for the winter. No money for the dentist, or the doctor. No money for anything that isn't absolutely necessary. So the Millers make do. They wear their clothes until they fall apart, and then mend them, and wear them again. They save everything. String, and paper, and buttons, and old nails, and bits of wood. Nothing is ever thrown away. As people said in those days, use it up, wear it out, make it do, or do without. It isn't easy. But they aren't alone. Everyone they know is in the same boat.
===
IMG: farm-dawn-1933-farmer-with-lantern-walking-to-barn-frost-windmill | farm dawn 1933 farmer with lantern walking to barn frost windmill
HEAD: On the farm, the day begins before dawn.
BODY: At five o'clock, the alarm clock rings, in the cold bedroom. You'd wake to a cold, dark room and the sound of the rooster. Walt gets up first, and gets dressed, in the dark. Overalls, and a flannel shirt, and heavy boots. He goes down to the kitchen, and lights the kerosene lamp. There's no electricity on the farm. Only about one farm in ten in America has electricity, in 1933. The rest use kerosene lamps, and wood stoves, and hand pumps. Walt lights the kitchen stove, with corncobs and kindling. Then he takes a lantern, and goes out to the barn, to do the morning chores. Eddie follows him, rubbing his eyes, with Rusty at his heels. The air is cold, and fresh, and smells of hay, and animals, and wet grass. The sky in the east is just beginning to grow pale. The windmill creaks, in the breeze.
===
IMG: father-and-son-milking-cows-by-lantern-light-barn-stalls-steam-breath | father and son milking cows by lantern light barn stalls steam breath
HEAD: In the barn, the animals are waiting.
BODY: The horses stamp, and snort, in their stalls, wanting their breakfast. You'd smell hay and warm animals as soon as you opened the door. The cows low, wanting to be milked. Walt and Eddie give the horses their oats and hay. Then they sit on little stools, and milk the cows, by hand, into tin buckets. The milk squirts into the buckets, with a soft, rhythmic sound. The barn cats gather round, hoping for a squirt of warm milk. Eddie always gives them one. Then they feed the hogs. The hogs are in a pen, behind the barn, and they squeal and push, when they see the buckets of feed. Corn, and skim milk, and scraps from the kitchen. The hogs are the most important animals on the farm. They turn the corn into pork, which is worth more than the corn. Or it was, before the Depression. Now, even pork prices have fallen.
===
IMG: farm-kitchen-breakfast-1933-pancakes-eggs-bacon-family-at-table-lamp | farm kitchen breakfast 1933 pancakes eggs bacon family at table lamp
HEAD: Meanwhile, in the kitchen, Ruth is making breakfast.
BODY: The stove is hot now, and the coffee is boiling. You'd smell bacon and coffee drifting up the stairs. She fries eggs, from her own chickens, and bacon, from their own hogs. She makes pancakes, or biscuits, or fried potatoes. She heats the milk, fresh from the cows. June sets the table, and Grandma Hattie slices the bread, which Ruth baked yesterday. When Walt and Eddie come in from the barn, breakfast is ready. They wash their hands, at the sink, with water from the pump. And the family sits down together. Before they eat, Walt says grace. He thanks God for the food, and for the family, and for the farm. And he asks for help, in the hard times. Then they eat. A big breakfast, because there's a long day of work ahead. Nobody on the farm goes hungry, even in the Depression. There's always enough to eat. It's money they lack, not food. After breakfast, the day's work begins. There's always work on a farm. In the spring, ploughing, and planting. In the summer, cultivating the corn, and cutting the hay, and the oats. In the autumn, the harvest. In the winter, mending fences, and tools, and harness, and cutting wood. And every day, all year round, feeding and milking and caring for the animals, morning and evening.
===
IMG: farmer-ploughing-field-team-of-horses-spring-1933-black-soil-gulls | farmer ploughing field team of horses spring 1933 black soil gulls
HEAD: Ruth's work never ends either.
BODY: Washing, on Monday, with a washboard, and a big tub, and water heated on the stove. Ironing, on Tuesday, with heavy irons, heated on the stove too. Baking, and cleaning, and churning butter, and making soap, from fat and lye. And in the summer, the garden, and canning the vegetables, for the winter. Grandma Hattie helps as much as she can, and so does June, before and after school. In the evening, after supper, and the evening chores, the family sits in the kitchen, or on the porch, if it's warm. They are tired, but content. The day's work is done. The animals are fed, and settled for the night. The sun goes down, over the cornfields, in a blaze of orange and red. Could you have kept up with her for a single day? And the first stars come out, over the wide, quiet land.

TOPIC: The small town, and the bank holiday
BRIDGE: The chores are done, and it's Saturday. Climb into the back of the old car with the children, and let's drive into town.
===
IMG: harlan-iowa-1933-main-street-brick-shops-model-a-fords-courthouse-square | harlan iowa 1933 main street brick shops model a fords courthouse square
HEAD: On Saturday afternoon, the Millers go to town.
BODY: They drive in their old car, a Model T Ford, which Walt bought second-hand, years ago. You'd squeeze into the back seat, between the egg baskets. It's black, and rattly, and slow, and it smells of oil. But it still runs, most of the time. They drive along the gravel road, between the fields, with a cloud of dust behind them, for about four miles, into Harlan. Harlan is a small town, of about three thousand people. It has a courthouse, in the middle of a square, with a big clock tower, and green lawns, and benches, under tall elm trees. Around the square, there are shops, and offices, in brick buildings, two or three storeys high. A grocery store, a hardware store, a drugstore, a dry goods store, a bank, a barber, a cafe, and a movie theatre. And churches, on the side streets, with tall white steeples.
===
IMG: general-store-interior-1933-shelves-cans-barrels-counter-farmers-wives-trading | general store interior 1933 shelves cans barrels counter farmers wives trading
HEAD: On Saturday, the town is busy.
BODY: All the farm families come in, to buy what they need, and to sell what they can, and to see their friends. You'd see wagons and old cars parked side by side along the street. Ruth takes her eggs, and her butter, to the grocery store, and trades them for flour, and sugar, and coffee, and salt. The grocer, Mr Anderson, gives her a little credit, too, when she needs it. He gives credit to many families. He doesn't know if they will ever be able to pay him back. The men stand around on the corners, in their overalls and their hats, and talk. About the weather, and the crops, and the prices, and the bank, and the new president, and the farmers who have lost their farms. The talk is often gloomy. But there are jokes too, and laughter. Walt says that he isn't worried about the Depression. You'd stop to say hello to someone every few steps. He says that the Millers have been depressed for years, and nobody noticed.
===
IMG: children-outside-drugstore-soda-fountain-window-1933-looking-in-nickels | children outside drugstore soda fountain window 1933 looking in nickels
HEAD: The children love Saturdays in town.
BODY: June goes to the little library, above the courthouse, and borrows a new book. You'd feel a penny burning a hole in your pocket. Eddie meets his friends, on the square, and they play marbles, and talk about baseball. If they are lucky, their mother gives them each a nickel. Then they go to the drugstore, which has a soda fountain, and buy an ice cream cone, or a bottle of soda pop. It's the best moment of the week. But there are signs of the hard times, everywhere. Some of the shops on the square are empty, with dusty windows, and signs saying closed, or for rent. The men who used to work in them are standing on the corners, with nothing to do. There are more people asking for help, at the churches. And there's the bank. The First National Bank, on the corner of the square, with its stone columns, and its big clock. Its doors are closed. The bank closed two years ago, in 1931. One morning, people came to take out their money, and found the doors locked, and a notice on the door. The bank had failed. All the money in it was gone. Many families in Harlan lost their savings. The Millers lost three hundred dollars. It was all the money they had saved, over many years, for June's education, and for a rainy day. What would you have bought with your penny? They have never got it back.
===
IMG: shuttered-bank-building-stone-columns-notice-on-door-pigeons-quiet-square | shuttered bank building stone columns notice on door pigeons quiet square
HEAD: Walt doesn't talk about it much.
BODY: But June knows that he still thinks about it, sometimes. You'd see it in his face, though, when he thinks nobody is looking. He walks past the bank on Saturday, and looks at the closed doors, and shakes his head. Grandma Hattie says that the money is gone, and there's no use crying over it. She says that what you have in your head, and in your heart, nobody can take away. And she makes sure that June keeps reading her books. At the end of the afternoon, the family gets back into the old car, with their groceries, and drives home, along the gravel road, as the sun goes down. The car rattles, and bumps, and once, it stops, and Walt has to get out and crank it, to start it again. But they get home, in the end. And Ruth puts away the groceries, carefully, and makes supper. And they sit down together, to eat, as the lamps are lit.
===
IMG: fdr-inauguration-march-1933-capitol-steps-crowd-grey-sky-washington | fdr inauguration march 1933 capitol steps crowd grey sky washington
HEAD: On the fourth of March, 1933, Franklin Delano Roosevelt became president of the United States.
BODY: It was a cold, grey day in Washington. You'd gather around the radio with your neighbours to listen. A great crowd stood in front of the Capitol, to watch him take the oath. And all over America, millions of people sat by their radios, to listen. The Millers were among them, in their kitchen, in Iowa. The country was in a terrible state, that morning. In the weeks before, there had been panic. People everywhere had rushed to the banks, to take out their money, afraid that the banks would close. And many banks did close. In state after state, the governors had ordered all the banks to shut, to stop the panic. By the morning of the inauguration, almost every bank in America was closed, or limited. The whole money system of the country had almost stopped.
===
IMG: family-listening-to-radio-kitchen-march-1933-faces-hopeful-quiet | family listening to radio kitchen march 1933 faces hopeful quiet
HEAD: Roosevelt stood up, at the front of the platform, holding tightly to the arm of his son, because of his weak legs.
BODY: He took the oath. You'd see the crowd go quiet as he gripped the railing. Then he gave his speech. His voice was strong, and clear, and calm, and confident. And he said the words that everyone remembered, for the rest of their lives. He said, the only thing we have to fear is fear itself. In the Millers' kitchen, everyone was silent, listening. Grandma Hattie nodded. Ruth wiped her eyes, with her apron. Walt sat very still. And when the speech was over, he said, quietly, well, that's a man who means to do something. For the first time in a long time, the Millers felt a little hope.
===
IMG: closed-bank-doors-notice-bank-holiday-march-1933-townspeople-reading | closed bank doors notice bank holiday march 1933 townspeople reading
HEAD: Roosevelt acted at once.
BODY: Two days later, he ordered every bank in America to close, for a few days. He called it a bank holiday. It wasn't a holiday, of course. But it gave the government time to check all the banks, and to find out which ones were sound, and which ones weren't. The healthy banks would be allowed to open again. The unhealthy ones would stay closed, or be helped, or be closed for good. For a week, there was almost no money in America. People couldn't take money out of the banks, or put money in. Shops gave credit, or accepted promises, or IOUs. In some towns, people made their own money, out of paper, or wood, or even leather. Farmers traded eggs for flour, and butter for coffee, and chickens for shoes. For the Millers, it didn't make much difference. They had no money in the bank anyway. Then, on the twelfth of March, a Sunday evening, Roosevelt spoke to the nation again, on the radio. It wasn't a formal speech. It was a talk, as if he were sitting in their living room, by the fire. He explained, in simple words, what the banks were, and how they worked, and why they had failed, and what the government was doing about it. Can you imagine the relief in so many kitchens that week? And he told the people that it was safe to put their money back in the banks.
===
IMG: people-queuing-to-deposit-money-reopened-bank-march-1933-relieved-faces | people queuing to deposit money reopened bank march 1933 relieved faces
HEAD: Millions of people listened.
BODY: And they believed him. You'd lean closer to the speaker so you didn't miss a word. The next day, when the banks began to open again, the people didn't rush to take their money out. Instead, they came to put it back in. The panic was over. It was the first of many radio talks that Roosevelt gave, over the next twelve years. People called them the fireside chats. Later that year, a new law began to protect people's savings in the banks, so that they wouldn't lose them again, even if a bank failed. It was too late for the Millers' three hundred dollars. But Walt said that at least, it wouldn't happen to anyone else. And that was something.

TOPIC: The radio, and living on very little
BRIDGE: Back home, the evening light is fading. Can you hear that crackle from the parlour? Someone has switched on the radio.
===
IMG: wooden-cathedral-radio-1933-kitchen-shelf-battery-glowing-dial | wooden cathedral radio 1933 kitchen shelf battery glowing dial
HEAD: The most precious thing in the Millers' house is the radio.
BODY: It stands on a shelf in the kitchen, a big wooden box, with a rounded top, like a little church window, and a cloth front, and two knobs. You'd see it standing in the parlour like a small wooden church. Because there's no electricity on the farm, it runs on a battery. A big, heavy battery, which must be taken to town, to be recharged, every few weeks. So the radio isn't on all the time. Only for the most important programmes. Walt bought the radio in 1927, when times were good. It cost a lot of money. Now, they could never afford it. But it has become the family's window on the world. Through it, they hear the news, from Washington, and New York, and Europe. They hear the farm prices, and the weather forecasts. They hear music, and comedy, and drama, and sports. And they hear the president, speaking to them, as if he were sitting at their kitchen table.
===
IMG: father-adjusting-radio-dial-family-gathered-close-evening-lamplight | father adjusting radio dial family gathered close evening lamplight
HEAD: Every day at noon, Walt comes in from the fields, and turns on the radio, for the farm report.
BODY: A man in Des Moines reads out the prices for corn, and wheat, and hogs, and cattle, in Chicago. You'd hear the prices read out, slowly and clearly. And the weather forecast. Walt listens carefully, while he eats his dinner, and shakes his head at the prices. Then he turns the radio off, to save the battery, and goes back to work. In the evening, after supper, the family gathers around the radio, for an hour or two. They pull their chairs close, because the sound isn't very loud. Grandma Hattie sits in the rocking chair, with her knitting. Ruth sits at the table, with her mending. Walt sits in his chair, with his pipe. And June and Eddie sit on the floor, as close to the radio as they can get. Rusty lies by the stove, with his head on his paws.
===
IMG: children-sitting-on-floor-close-to-radio-rapt-attention-dog-by-stove | children sitting on floor close to radio rapt attention dog by stove
HEAD: They listen to comedy shows, which make them laugh, until they cry.
BODY: Two funny men, who are always getting into trouble, and always getting out of it again. A couple who argue, and joke, and tease each other, every week. They listen to music. Dance bands, from the great hotels of Chicago and New York. Country music, from Nashville. Church music, on Sunday. And Grandma Hattie's favourite, the old songs, from her youth. On clear nights, the radio can pick up stations far away. And Eddie's favourite is a new programme, from a station in Detroit, which began in 1933. It's about a masked rider of the plains, in the old West, who fights for justice, with his faithful friend, and his white horse. Every episode begins with exciting music, and the sound of galloping hooves, and a cry of Hi-yo, Silver. Eddie gallops around the kitchen, after every episode, until his mother tells him to calm down. June prefers the stories. Dramas, and mysteries, and adventures, read by actors, with sound effects. Doors creaking, footsteps, thunder, rain, horses, trains. She closes her eyes, and listens, and sees it all in her imagination, more clearly than any movie. She says that the radio is better than the movies, because you can make the pictures yourself. Eddie says that's silly. I love that little detail. But he closes his eyes too.
===
IMG: radio-listening-whole-family-laughing-comedy-show-kitchen-warm-night | radio listening whole family laughing comedy show kitchen warm night
HEAD: The radio brings the family together, every evening.
BODY: And it brings the whole country together too. You'd pull your chair a little closer to the warm glow of the dial. All over America, in farmhouses, and city apartments, and small town homes, millions of people are listening to the same programmes, at the same time. Laughing at the same jokes, and worrying about the same news. In the hard times of the Depression, it makes people feel a little less alone. When the programmes are over, Walt turns off the radio, and the dial slowly fades, from orange to dark. The house is quiet again. HEAD: In the Depression, nothing is wasted.
BODY: Not a scrap of food, not a piece of cloth, not a bit of string. You'd find a use for every scrap, every string and every nail. Everything is saved, and used, and reused, until it's completely worn out. And then it's used for something else. Ruth is very good at this. Like most farm wives, she has learned to make something useful out of almost anything. Flour, and sugar, and animal feed, come in big cotton sacks. In the old days, the sacks were plain white. But the companies that make them have noticed that the farm wives use the sacks to make clothes. So now, the sacks are made of pretty printed cotton, with flowers, and checks, and stripes. Ruth chooses the flour carefully, at the store, by the pattern on the sack. When she has enough sacks of the same pattern, she makes a dress for June.

HEAD: June has three dresses made from flour sacks.
BODY: One with little blue flowers, one with red checks, and one with yellow daisies. You'd never guess, looking at her, that they'd once held flour. Many of the girls at school wear flour sack dresses too. Nobody is ashamed of them. They are pretty, and practical, and free. The boys wear shirts made from feed sacks, and the babies wear diapers made from sugar sacks. And Ruth makes towels, and aprons, and pillowcases, and curtains, from the plain ones. When clothes wear out, they are mended. Patches are sewn over the holes, in the elbows, and the knees. Socks are darned, again and again, until they are more darn than sock. Collars are turned around, so that the worn side is hidden. When a coat is too small for June, it's cut down, and remade, for Eddie. Isn't that clever? When the clothes are too worn to wear, they are cut into strips, and braided into rugs, or sewn into quilts.

HEAD: Food is never wasted either.
BODY: Leftovers are eaten the next day, or turned into soup, or fed to the hogs. You'd see jars of beans and tomatoes lined up on the cellar shelves. Ruth bakes her own bread, and churns her own butter, and makes her own soap. In the summer, she spends weeks canning the vegetables, and fruit, from her garden. Beans, and tomatoes, and corn, and peas, and pickles, and peaches, and jam. Hundreds of glass jars, lined up on the shelves in the cellar, shining like jewels. Enough food for the whole winter. Many people in the towns plant gardens too, in their backyards, and in empty lots. Even in the cities, people grow vegetables, on rooftops, and in window boxes. Everyone keeps chickens, if they can. Meals are simple. Beans, and potatoes, and cornbread, and soup. Some meals are made from almost nothing. Bread, and gravy. Fried cornmeal mush. Potato pancakes. Water soup, the children call it, when there's very little in it. Things that break are repaired, not replaced. Walt fixes the harness, and the tools, and the machines, with wire, and nails, and pieces of old metal, and great patience. He makes new handles for the tools, from wood he cuts himself. He resoles the family's shoes, with pieces of old tyres. When the car breaks down, he fixes it himself. You'd learn to love bread pudding, I think. He says that there's almost nothing that can't be fixed, with a bit of baling wire, and a bit of thinking.

HEAD: The children make their own toys.
BODY: Eddie makes a kite, from newspaper, and sticks, and string. You'd find a doll made from a corncob, or a top carved from a spool. He makes a slingshot, from a forked stick, and an old inner tube. He plays marbles, and mumblety-peg, with his pocketknife, and baseball, with a ball made of string, wrapped tightly, and covered with tape. June makes paper dolls, cut from old catalogues, with paper clothes. And together, they make a swing, from a rope, and an old tyre, hanging from the big tree in the yard. Many years later, when June is a grandmother, she will still save string, and paper bags, and rubber bands, and old jars. Her grandchildren will laugh at her. But she will just smile, and say that they don't know what it was like. And she will be right.
