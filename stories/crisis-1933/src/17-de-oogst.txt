In October, the corn is ripe. The tall green stalks have turned brown, and dry, and the leaves rustle in the wind, like paper. The ears of corn hang down, heavy, in their dry husks. It is time for the corn harvest. In 1933, on most Iowa farms, the corn is still picked by hand, ear by ear, as it has been for a hundred years. It is the hardest work of the whole year.

Every morning, before dawn, Walt harnesses the horses to the wagon, and drives out to the cornfield. The wagon has a high board on one side, called a bangboard. Walt walks along the row of corn, beside the wagon. He grabs an ear of corn, and strips off the husk, with a little metal hook, strapped to his hand. Then he snaps off the ear, and throws it, without looking, against the bangboard. It bounces off, and falls into the wagon. Bang.

--

{img:horses-pulling-corn-wagon-slowly-along-rows-autumn-field-golden-light}
The horses walk slowly along the row, by themselves, pulling the wagon. They know the work, and they stop and start, when Walt calls to them. Grab, husk, snap, throw, bang. Grab, husk, snap, throw, bang. Again, and again, and again. All day long, from dawn until dusk. The sound of the ears of corn hitting the bangboards can be heard all over the countryside, in the autumn, from farm to farm.

A good corn husker can pick about a hundred bushels a day. That is about five thousand ears of corn. Walt is a good husker. He can fill a wagon in the morning, and another in the afternoon. When the wagon is full, he drives it back to the farm, and shovels the corn into the corn crib, a long, narrow building, with slatted sides, so that the air can blow through, and dry the corn.

{img:corn-crib-filled-golden-ears-slatted-walls-farmyard-autumn-sun}
Eddie helps, after school, and on Saturdays. He picks the ears from the bottom of the stalks, which his father misses. And he drives the horses, back to the farm, when the wagon is full. His hands get sore, and cut, from the dry, sharp husks. Ruth puts salve on them, in the evening. June helps too, sometimes, and so does Ruth, when the other work is done. Everyone helps, at harvest time.

In the autumn, there are corn husking contests, all over the Corn Belt. The best huskers in each county compete, to see who can pick the most corn, in eighty minutes. The winners go on to the state contest, and then to the national contest. Thousands of people come to watch. It is like a sporting event. The national champion is a famous man, in the farm country. His name is in all the newspapers.

--

{img:corn-husking-contest-crowd-watching-huskers-field-judges-1933}
In 1933, Walt enters the county contest, for the first time. It is held on a farm near Harlan, on a cold, bright morning in early November. There are twenty huskers, each with his own row of corn, and his own wagon, and his own team of horses. A crowd of people stands at the edge of the field, to watch. When the gun goes off, the huskers start, and the ears of corn fly through the air, and bang against the bangboards, like drums.

Walt husks as fast as he can, for eighty minutes. His arms ache, and his hands bleed. But he does not stop. When the time is up, the judges weigh each wagon of corn, and deduct points for any husks left on the ears, and any ears left in the field. Walt comes fourth. He is disappointed, a little. But Eddie says that fourth out of twenty is very good. And Walt laughs, and agrees.

By the middle of November, the harvest is finished. The corn crib is full, of golden corn. It is a good harvest, the best in years. The prices are still low. But there is enough corn to feed the hogs, and the cattle, and the horses, all winter, and to sell some, and to pay a little more on the mortgage. Walt stands by the corn crib, in the evening, and looks at it, and is content.
