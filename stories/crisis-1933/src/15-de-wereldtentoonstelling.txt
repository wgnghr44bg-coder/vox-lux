In the summer of 1933, there is a great event in Chicago. A world's fair, called A Century of Progress. It celebrates the hundredth birthday of the city, and the wonders of science and industry, and the future. It is built on the shore of Lake Michigan, on land made by filling in the lake. It has huge, modern buildings, painted in bright colours, red and blue and yellow and orange. And at night, it is lit up, with electric lights, in every colour.

People come from all over America to see it. Millions of them. Even in the Depression, they save their pennies, and come by train, and by car, and by bus. It is a moment of hope, and wonder, in the hard times. A glimpse of a better future. Grandma Hattie has decided that she wants to see it. She is seventy-one, and she has never been to a big city. And she wants to take June.

--

{img:grandmother-and-girl-on-train-window-seat-1933-fields-passing-excited}
Grandma Hattie has a little money of her own. A few dollars, which she has saved, for years, in an old sock, in her dresser drawer. She did not put it in the bank, because she never trusted banks. And she was right. Now, she uses it to buy two train tickets, to Chicago, at a special cheap price for the fair. Walt and Ruth are worried. Chicago is a big, dangerous city. But Grandma Hattie says that she has crossed the prairie in a covered wagon, and she is not afraid of Chicago.

So, one morning in August, Grandma Hattie and June take the train, from Harlan, to Chicago. It is June's first train journey. She sits by the window, all day, watching the fields, and the towns, and the rivers, go by. They cross the great Mississippi River, on a long iron bridge. And in the evening, they arrive in Chicago. The biggest city June has ever seen. Tall buildings, and crowds, and noise, and lights, everywhere.

{img:sky-ride-towers-cable-cars-worlds-fair-1933-crowds-below-sunshine}
They stay with a cousin of Grandma Hattie, in a small apartment, on the south side of the city. And the next day, they go to the fair. It is like a dream. There are great halls of science, with machines, and inventions, and exhibitions. There is a house of the future, with walls of glass, and a garage for an airplane. There are cars, and trains, and the newest engines. There is a model of a dinosaur, which moves its head and tail.

There is a great ride, called the Sky Ride, with little cars, hanging from cables, between two tall towers, high above the fair, and the lake. June and Grandma Hattie ride across it, and look down at the crowds below, and the blue lake, and the city. June holds her grandmother's hand, tightly. Grandma Hattie laughs, and says that it is better than a covered wagon, any day.

--

{img:worlds-fair-night-1933-colored-lights-buildings-reflected-lagoon-fireworks}
In the evening, they stay to see the lights. As the sun goes down, all the buildings light up, in brilliant colours. Red, and blue, and green, and gold, reflected in the lagoons. Searchlights sweep the sky. Music plays. June has never seen anything like it. She has never seen electric light like this. On the farm, there are only kerosene lamps. She stands still, and stares, for a long time.

Grandma Hattie says that when she was a girl, there were no electric lights at all, anywhere. No cars, no airplanes, no radios, no telephones. People travelled by horse, and lit their homes with candles. She says that she has seen more change in her life than anyone, in all of history. And she wonders what June will see, in hers.

They go home to Iowa three days later, tired, and happy, with their heads full of wonders. June has a little souvenir, a small metal model of the Sky Ride towers, which she keeps on her shelf, for the rest of her life. And she writes a long essay about the fair, for school, which Miss Holm reads aloud to the whole class. Eddie is very jealous. But June promises to take him, one day, to see the next world's fair. And many years later, she does.
