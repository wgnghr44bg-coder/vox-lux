The most precious thing in the Millers' house is the radio. It stands on a shelf in the kitchen, a big wooden box, with a rounded top, like a little church window, and a cloth front, and two knobs. Because there is no electricity on the farm, it runs on a battery. A big, heavy battery, which must be taken to town, to be recharged, every few weeks. So the radio is not on all the time. Only for the most important programmes.

Walt bought the radio in 1927, when times were good. It cost a lot of money. Now, they could never afford it. But it has become the family's window on the world. Through it, they hear the news, from Washington, and New York, and Europe. They hear the farm prices, and the weather forecasts. They hear music, and comedy, and drama, and sports. And they hear the president, speaking to them, as if he were sitting at their kitchen table.

--

{img:father-adjusting-radio-dial-family-gathered-close-evening-lamplight}
Every day at noon, Walt comes in from the fields, and turns on the radio, for the farm report. A man in Des Moines reads out the prices for corn, and wheat, and hogs, and cattle, in Chicago. And the weather forecast. Walt listens carefully, while he eats his dinner, and shakes his head at the prices. Then he turns the radio off, to save the battery, and goes back to work.

In the evening, after supper, the family gathers around the radio, for an hour or two. They pull their chairs close, because the sound is not very loud. Grandma Hattie sits in the rocking chair, with her knitting. Ruth sits at the table, with her mending. Walt sits in his chair, with his pipe. And June and Eddie sit on the floor, as close to the radio as they can get. Rusty lies by the stove, with his head on his paws.

{img:children-sitting-on-floor-close-to-radio-rapt-attention-dog-by-stove}
They listen to comedy shows, which make them laugh, until they cry. Two funny men, who are always getting into trouble, and always getting out of it again. A couple who argue, and joke, and tease each other, every week. They listen to music. Dance bands, from the great hotels of Chicago and New York. Country music, from Nashville. Church music, on Sunday. And Grandma Hattie's favourite, the old songs, from her youth.

On clear nights, the radio can pick up stations far away. And Eddie's favourite is a new programme, from a station in Detroit, which began in 1933. It is about a masked rider of the plains, in the old West, who fights for justice, with his faithful friend, and his white horse. Every episode begins with exciting music, and the sound of galloping hooves, and a cry of Hi-yo, Silver. Eddie gallops around the kitchen, after every episode, until his mother tells him to calm down.

--

June prefers the stories. Dramas, and mysteries, and adventures, read by actors, with sound effects. Doors creaking, footsteps, thunder, rain, horses, trains. She closes her eyes, and listens, and sees it all in her imagination, more clearly than any movie. She says that the radio is better than the movies, because you can make the pictures yourself. Eddie says that is silly. But he closes his eyes too.

{img:radio-listening-whole-family-laughing-comedy-show-kitchen-warm-night}
The radio brings the family together, every evening. And it brings the whole country together too. All over America, in farmhouses, and city apartments, and small town homes, millions of people are listening to the same programmes, at the same time. Laughing at the same jokes, and worrying about the same news. In the hard times of the Depression, it makes people feel a little less alone.

When the programmes are over, Walt turns off the radio, and the dial slowly fades, from orange to dark. The house is quiet again. Just the ticking of the clock, and the crackling of the stove, and the wind outside. The family says goodnight, and goes up to bed, with candles, and lamps. And the radio sits on its shelf, silent, until tomorrow.
