In December, the winter comes to Iowa. The first snow falls, softly, in the night, and in the morning, the whole world is white. The fields, the roads, the barn, the trees, all covered in a thick white blanket. The air is sharp, and cold, and clean. The sky is a pale, clear blue. And the only sound is the creak of the windmill, and the crunch of boots in the snow.

The winters in Iowa are very cold. Sometimes, the temperature falls far below freezing, and stays there, for weeks. Blizzards come roaring down from the north, with wind, and snow, so thick that you cannot see your own hand in front of your face. The farmers tie a rope between the house and the barn, so that they can find their way, in a blizzard, to feed the animals, and not get lost.

--

{img:father-carrying-hay-to-cattle-snowstorm-rope-line-to-barn-lantern}
But the farm is ready for the winter. The corn crib is full. The hay loft is full. The cellar is full of jars of canned food, and sacks of potatoes, and apples, and onions. There is a big pile of wood, and corncobs, for the stoves. The animals are warm, in the barn, with thick straw. And the house is snug, with the kitchen stove burning all day, and all night.

In the winter, life on the farm slows down. There is still the daily work. Feeding the animals, and milking the cows, and cleaning out the barn, and chopping wood, and carrying water. But there is no work in the fields. So there is more time. Time to mend things, and make things, and read, and rest, and visit the neighbours. The long winter evenings are spent around the stove, in the kitchen, with the lamp, and the radio.

{img:children-sledding-down-snowy-hill-farm-dog-running-beside-laughing}
The children love the winter. They go sledding, down the hill behind the barn, on an old wooden sled, which Walt made, years ago. They build snowmen, and snow forts, and have snowball fights. They skate on the frozen creek, where they swam in the summer. And they come home, red-cheeked, and frozen, and soaking wet, and stand by the stove, to warm up, while Ruth makes them hot cocoa.

In November, there was strange news from the west. Great dust storms had blown across the plains, in South Dakota, and Nebraska, and Kansas. The farmers there had ploughed up the prairie grass, years before, to plant wheat. Now, after years of drought, the dry soil was blowing away, in great black clouds. People said that in some places, the sky went dark in the middle of the day. And there were reports that the snow, further east, had fallen brown, with dust.

--

Walt reads about it in the newspaper, and shakes his head. He says that the land must be cared for, or it will not care for you. In Iowa, the rains have been better, and the soil has held. But Walt is worried about the future. Over the next few years, the dust storms would get worse, and worse, and the southern plains would become known as the Dust Bowl. Many families there would lose everything, and travel west, to California, looking for work.

{img:family-reading-by-lamplight-winter-evening-kitchen-frost-on-window}
But for now, in the Millers' kitchen, it is warm, and quiet. Snow is falling outside, softly, against the windows. The lamp is lit, on the table. Ruth is knitting a sweater for Eddie, from the wool of an old one, unravelled. Walt is reading the farm newspaper. Grandma Hattie is dozing in her rocking chair. June is reading a book, from the library, about a girl who goes to the big city, and becomes a writer. And Eddie is lying on the floor, with Rusty, drawing pictures of airplanes.

At Christmas, there are not many presents. A new pair of mittens, a book, an orange, and a few pieces of candy, in each stocking. Eddie gets a pocketknife, from his father. June gets a diary, with a lock, from her grandmother. But the family is together, and warm, and well. And on Christmas Eve, they go to the little white church, and sing the old carols, by candlelight, while the snow falls outside.
