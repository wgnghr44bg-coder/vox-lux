Tonight, we travel back to the year 1933, to the middle of America. To the state of Iowa, in the great flat heart of the country, where the cornfields stretch away to the horizon, in every direction, under a huge, wide sky. Here, in the gently rolling farmland, there are small towns, every ten miles or so, each with its main street, its church steeples, its grain elevator, and its water tower.

It is a beautiful land. Rich, black soil, some of the best farmland in the world. In the summer, the corn grows tall and green, higher than a man's head. In the autumn, it turns golden brown, and rustles in the wind. There are white farmhouses, and red barns, and windmills, turning slowly, pumping water for the animals. And there are long, straight gravel roads, running between the fields, with a cloud of dust behind every car.

--

But in 1933, this land is in trouble. All of America is in trouble. The whole world is in trouble. It is the time of the Great Depression. The worst economic crisis in modern history. Banks have closed. Factories have closed. Shops have closed. Millions of people have lost their jobs, and their savings, and their homes. One worker in every four has no job at all.

{img:main-street-small-town-1933-closed-bank-sign-men-standing-waiting}
For the farmers, it is a very hard time. The prices for their crops have fallen so low that they cannot make a living. A bushel of corn, which sold for almost a dollar a few years ago, now sells for ten cents. It costs more to grow it than you can sell it for. Some farmers burn their corn in their stoves, for heat, because it is cheaper than coal. And many farmers cannot pay their debts, and lose their farms.

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

--

In this story, we will follow one family through the year 1933. The Millers, who have a farm a few miles outside a small town called Harlan, in western Iowa. There is Walt, the father, who has farmed this land all his life, like his father before him. Ruth, the mother, who keeps the house, and the garden, and the chickens, and the family together. June, their daughter, who is twelve. Eddie, their son, who is nine. And Grandma Hattie, Walt's mother, who has seen hard times before.

We will see how they live, and how they manage, in the hardest of times. How they save, and mend, and make do, and share. We will listen to the radio with them, as a new president speaks to the nation. We will meet the travellers who knock at the back door, asking for a meal. We will go to the county fair, and help with the harvest, and gather around the table at Thanksgiving. And we will see how a whole country slowly began to find its way, out of the dark.

{img:family-gathered-around-radio-kitchen-lamp-evening-1933-listening}
It was a hard time. But it was also a time of great kindness, and courage, and community. People helped each other, in a way they had not done before. They shared what little they had. They learned to be patient, and careful, and grateful for small things. And many of the people who lived through it said, later, that it taught them what really mattered in life.

So let us go to Iowa, in the year 1933. Let us walk down the gravel road, between the fields, to the white farmhouse with the red barn. And let us meet the Millers, as they begin another day, in the hard, quiet years of the Depression.
