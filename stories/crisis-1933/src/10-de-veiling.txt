In 1932 and 1933, many farmers in Iowa lost their farms. They could not pay their mortgages, or their taxes. So the banks, or the insurance companies, which had lent them the money, took the farms, and sold them, at auction. Everything was sold. The land, the house, the barn, the animals, the machines, the furniture. And the family had to leave, with nothing.

These auctions were very sad occasions. A family that had worked the land for generations, perhaps fifty years, or more, had to stand and watch as their home, and everything they owned, was sold to strangers. Their neighbours came to watch too. And many of them were angry. They thought it was wrong. The farmers had not done anything wrong. They had worked hard. It was the Depression that had ruined them.

--

So, in some places, the farmers began to fight back, in their own way. They organised what were called penny auctions. When a farm was being sold, all the neighbours came to the auction, in a big crowd. They stood together, silently, around the auctioneer. And when the bidding began, they made it very clear, without saying a word, that nobody should bid more than a few pennies.

{img:farmers-standing-silently-together-auction-crowd-arms-folded-serious}
So the horse was sold for ten cents. The cow, for five cents. The plough, for a quarter. The tractor, for a dollar. And all of it was bought by the neighbours. Then, when the auction was over, the neighbours gave everything back to the family. The bank got only a few dollars. And the family kept their things, and sometimes, even their farm. It was not exactly legal. But it worked, sometimes.

In the spring of 1933, there is a penny auction, on a farm a few miles from the Millers. The farm belongs to the Petersens, a Danish family, who are friends of the Millers, from church. Karl Petersen is a good farmer, and a hard worker. But he borrowed money, to buy more land, in the good years. And now he cannot pay it back. The insurance company has decided to sell his farm.

{img:old-farmer-and-wife-standing-on-porch-watching-auction-neighbours-crowd}
On the morning of the auction, Walt puts on his hat, and his good coat, and drives over to the Petersen farm. Many other farmers are there already, standing in the yard, in their overalls, and their hats. Hundreds of them. They are quiet, and serious. Nobody is shouting, or making trouble. They just stand there, together, with their arms folded, and watch.

The auctioneer stands on a wagon, and begins. He describes the first horse, a fine brown mare, and asks for a bid. There is silence. Then one farmer says, five cents. The auctioneer asks for more. Nobody says anything. They just look at him. The auctioneer looks at the crowd. Then he says, sold, for five cents. And so it goes on, all morning. Everything sold, for a few pennies, and all of it given back to the Petersens.

--

Karl Petersen stands on his porch, with his wife, and watches. At first, he does not understand. Then he does. And he cannot speak. His wife cries, quietly, into her handkerchief. When the auction is over, the farmers come up to the porch, one by one, and shake Karl's hand, and say a few words. Then they get into their cars and their wagons, and drive home.

{img:neighbours-shaking-hands-on-porch-after-auction-relief-gratitude-spring}
The penny auctions did not save every farm. And in some places, there was trouble, and anger, and even violence. In the spring of 1933, some farmers in Iowa became so angry that they threatened a judge, who was handling the farm sales. The governor sent in soldiers, to keep order. Most farmers did not approve of this. They wanted justice, but peacefully.

Then, slowly, things began to change. The government passed new laws, to help the farmers. The banks were told to be more patient. New loans were made available, at lower interest, so that farmers could keep their farms. And the Petersens kept theirs. Years later, Karl's grandson still farmed the same land. And on the wall of the farmhouse, there was a framed receipt, from the auction, for a brown mare, sold for five cents.
