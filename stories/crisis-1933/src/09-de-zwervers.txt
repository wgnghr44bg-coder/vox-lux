In the Depression, America is full of travellers. Not tourists, or holidaymakers. But people looking for work, or for food, or for a new beginning. Men, mostly, and some women, and many young people, boys and girls, sometimes as young as fourteen or fifteen. They have left their homes, because there was no work there, and no food, and they did not want to be a burden on their families.

Many of them travel by freight train. They climb into empty boxcars, or onto the roofs, or underneath, and ride for free, from town to town, from state to state, across the whole country. It is dangerous, and uncomfortable, and illegal. But it is the only way they can afford to travel. People call them hobos, or tramps, or transients. Perhaps a quarter of a million young people are riding the rails, in 1933.

--

Near the railway tracks, outside every town, there are hobo camps, which they call jungles. Places where the travellers can rest, and sleep, and cook, and wash, and share news. They build little fires, and cook a stew, in an old tin can, from whatever they have. A potato, an onion, a bit of meat, if they are lucky. Everyone puts something in. And everyone shares. There are unwritten rules, in the jungles, about sharing, and cleanliness, and respect.

The railway line runs past the end of the Millers' farm, about half a mile away. Several times a week, a long freight train rumbles past, with its whistle blowing, and its smoke trailing across the fields. Eddie loves to watch the trains. Sometimes, he sees men sitting in the open doors of the boxcars, with their legs dangling, watching the fields go by. Sometimes, they wave to him. And he waves back.

{img:stranger-at-farmhouse-back-door-hat-in-hand-farm-wife-offering-plate}
And sometimes, the travellers come to the farmhouse. They walk up the lane, from the railway, tired, and dusty, and hungry. They knock at the back door, politely, with their hats in their hands. And they ask if there is any work they could do, in exchange for a meal. Ruth never turns them away. She gives them a plate of food. Bread, and beans, and coffee, or whatever there is. And Walt finds them a job to do. Chopping wood, or mending a fence, or cleaning out the barn.

Most of the travellers are polite, and grateful, and honest. They are ordinary people, who have fallen on hard times. A carpenter from Chicago. A factory worker from Detroit. A young man from Kansas, whose family lost their farm. A schoolteacher, who lost her job when the school closed. They sit on the back step, and eat, and talk a little, about where they have come from, and where they are going. Then they thank Ruth, and walk back down the lane, to the railway.

--

{img:chalk-symbol-on-fence-post-hobo-sign-cat-drawing-farm-gate}
On the gatepost, at the end of the Millers' lane, there is a little mark, drawn in chalk. A simple drawing, like a cat. It is a hobo sign. The travellers have their own secret code, of little signs, which they draw on fences, and gateposts, and walls, to tell each other about the places they pass. This house has a dog. This town has police who arrest hobos. This farmer will give you work. And the cat means, a kind lady lives here.

Ruth does not know about the sign. But Eddie has seen it. He asked one of the travellers what it meant, and the man told him. Eddie is very proud of his mother. He decides not to rub it out. Even though, he thinks, his mother is not much like a cat.

{img:young-traveller-chopping-wood-farmyard-boy-watching-afternoon-light}
One day in the spring, a young man comes to the door. He is only seventeen, thin, and pale, and shy. His name is Tom. He comes from a farm in Nebraska, which his family lost to the bank. He has been riding the trains for six months, looking for work. Walt gives him a job, for a week, helping with the planting. He sleeps in the barn, and eats with the family. He is a good worker, and quiet, and polite, and the children like him.

At the end of the week, Walt gives him two dollars, which is all he can spare, and a bag of food. Tom thanks him, and shakes his hand, and walks away down the lane, toward the railway. Ruth watches him go, from the kitchen window. She thinks about her own children, and hopes that they will never have to do what he is doing. And she says a little prayer for him, that he will find his way home.
