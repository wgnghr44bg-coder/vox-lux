Walt Miller is forty-two years old. He was born on this farm, in the white farmhouse, in 1891. His father came here from Ohio, as a young man, and bought the land, a hundred and sixty acres, when it was still prairie grass. Walt has worked on it all his life. He knows every field, every fence post, every corner of it. He is tall, and thin, and quiet, with a face burned brown by the sun, and big, rough hands.

Walt grows corn, and oats, and hay, and he raises hogs, and a few cattle. He has four horses, for pulling the plough, and the wagon. He bought a tractor in 1928, on credit, when times were good. Now, he can hardly afford the fuel for it. So the tractor sits in the shed, most of the time, and the horses do the work again, as they always did. Walt does not mind. He likes the horses better anyway.

{img:farm-wife-1933-flour-sack-apron-feeding-chickens-yard-morning-sun}
Ruth Miller is thirty-nine. She grew up on a farm too, a few miles away. She met Walt at a church social, when she was eighteen, and they were married a year later. She is a strong, practical, cheerful woman, who never stops working. She cooks, and cleans, and washes, and sews, and mends. She keeps a big vegetable garden, and a flock of chickens, and sells the eggs in town, for a little money. She can make something out of nothing.

June is twelve. She is a serious, bookish girl, with brown braids, and freckles. She loves to read, more than anything. She has read every book in the little school library, twice. She wants to be a teacher, or a writer, or perhaps a newspaper reporter, in a big city. She helps her mother in the house, and the garden. But her head is always somewhere else, in a story.

{img:boy-nine-overalls-barefoot-with-farm-dog-collie-barn-door-summer}
Eddie is nine. He is the opposite of his sister. He hates sitting still. He loves being outside, with the animals, and the machines, and his dog, Rusty, a big, friendly collie. He helps his father with the chores, and follows him everywhere. He wants to be a farmer, like his father, and his grandfather. Or perhaps a baseball player. Or perhaps a pilot. He changes his mind every week.

And there is Grandma Hattie. She is seventy-one. She came to Iowa with her husband, in a covered wagon, in 1880, when she was eighteen. She has seen many hard times. Droughts, and grasshoppers, and blizzards, and the hard years of the 1890s, when prices fell, and many farmers lost their land. She says that the country has been through hard times before, and it will get through this one too. She is the calm centre of the family.

--

The Millers are luckier than many. They own their farm, and they still have it. They have a small mortgage, which Walt took out to buy the tractor. And they are behind with the payments. But the bank has been patient, so far. They grow most of their own food, so they never go hungry. They have a roof over their heads, and a warm stove, and each other.

{img:farmhouse-kitchen-1933-cast-iron-stove-oilcloth-table-calendar-wall}
But there is almost no money. In a good year, the farm used to earn a thousand dollars or more. Last year, it earned less than two hundred. There is nothing left, after the mortgage payments, and the taxes. No money for new clothes, or new shoes, or a new coat for the winter. No money for the dentist, or the doctor. No money for anything that is not absolutely necessary.

So the Millers make do. They wear their clothes until they fall apart, and then mend them, and wear them again. They save everything. String, and paper, and buttons, and old nails, and bits of wood. Nothing is ever thrown away. As people said in those days, use it up, wear it out, make it do, or do without. It is not easy. But they are not alone. Everyone they know is in the same boat.
