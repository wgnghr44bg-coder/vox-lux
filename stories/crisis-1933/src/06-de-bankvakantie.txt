On the fourth of March, 1933, Franklin Delano Roosevelt became president of the United States. It was a cold, grey day in Washington. A great crowd stood in front of the Capitol, to watch him take the oath. And all over America, millions of people sat by their radios, to listen. The Millers were among them, in their kitchen, in Iowa.

The country was in a terrible state, that morning. In the weeks before, there had been panic. People everywhere had rushed to the banks, to take out their money, afraid that the banks would close. And many banks did close. In state after state, the governors had ordered all the banks to shut, to stop the panic. By the morning of the inauguration, almost every bank in America was closed, or limited. The whole money system of the country had almost stopped.

--

{img:family-listening-to-radio-kitchen-march-1933-faces-hopeful-quiet}
Roosevelt stood up, at the front of the platform, holding tightly to the arm of his son, because of his weak legs. He took the oath. Then he gave his speech. His voice was strong, and clear, and calm, and confident. And he said the words that everyone remembered, for the rest of their lives. He said, the only thing we have to fear is fear itself.

In the Millers' kitchen, everyone was silent, listening. Grandma Hattie nodded. Ruth wiped her eyes, with her apron. Walt sat very still. And when the speech was over, he said, quietly, well, that is a man who means to do something. For the first time in a long time, the Millers felt a little hope.

{img:closed-bank-doors-notice-bank-holiday-march-1933-townspeople-reading}
Roosevelt acted at once. Two days later, he ordered every bank in America to close, for a few days. He called it a bank holiday. It was not a holiday, of course. But it gave the government time to check all the banks, and to find out which ones were sound, and which ones were not. The healthy banks would be allowed to open again. The unhealthy ones would stay closed, or be helped, or be closed for good.

For a week, there was almost no money in America. People could not take money out of the banks, or put money in. Shops gave credit, or accepted promises, or IOUs. In some towns, people made their own money, out of paper, or wood, or even leather. Farmers traded eggs for flour, and butter for coffee, and chickens for shoes. For the Millers, it did not make much difference. They had no money in the bank anyway.

--

Then, on the twelfth of March, a Sunday evening, Roosevelt spoke to the nation again, on the radio. It was not a formal speech. It was a talk, as if he were sitting in their living room, by the fire. He explained, in simple words, what the banks were, and how they worked, and why they had failed, and what the government was doing about it. And he told the people that it was safe to put their money back in the banks.

{img:people-queuing-to-deposit-money-reopened-bank-march-1933-relieved-faces}
Millions of people listened. And they believed him. The next day, when the banks began to open again, the people did not rush to take their money out. Instead, they came to put it back in. The panic was over. It was the first of many radio talks that Roosevelt gave, over the next twelve years. People called them the fireside chats.

Later that year, a new law began to protect people's savings in the banks, so that they would not lose them again, even if a bank failed. It was too late for the Millers' three hundred dollars. But Walt said that at least, it would not happen to anyone else. And that was something.
