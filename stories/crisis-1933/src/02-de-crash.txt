To understand the Great Depression, we must go back a few years. To the 1920s. For many Americans, the 1920s were a wonderful time. People called them the Roaring Twenties. There were jobs, and money, and new things to buy. Cars, and radios, and refrigerators, and washing machines. There was jazz music, and dancing, and movies. Everything seemed to be getting better and better.

Many people began to buy shares in companies, on the stock market, in New York. The prices of the shares kept going up, and up, and up. People thought they would go up for ever. Ordinary people, shopkeepers, and teachers, and even farmers, borrowed money, to buy shares. They thought they would get rich, quickly and easily. Some of them did, for a while.

--

{img:newspaper-headline-1929-crash-man-reading-on-street-corner-shocked}
Then, in October 1929, the bubble burst. The prices of the shares began to fall. At first slowly, then faster and faster. People panicked, and tried to sell their shares, all at once. On one terrible day, which became known as Black Tuesday, the stock market crashed. Billions of dollars disappeared, in a few hours. Many people lost everything they had.

The crash was only the beginning. Over the next few years, things got worse, and worse. People had less money, so they bought less. So the factories made less, and needed fewer workers. So more people lost their jobs, and had even less money. It was like a wheel, turning downward, faster and faster. Nobody seemed to know how to stop it.

{img:people-lined-up-outside-closed-bank-1931-small-town-anxious-crowd}
Then the banks began to fail. When people were afraid, they rushed to the banks, to take out their savings. But the banks had lent the money to other people, and did not have it in the vaults. So the banks ran out of money, and closed their doors. And the people lost their savings. Thousands of banks failed, all over America. In the small towns, it was a disaster. The savings of a whole lifetime could disappear in a day.

On the farms, the trouble had begun even earlier. After the Great War, in the early 1920s, the prices for crops had already fallen. Many farmers had borrowed money, to buy more land, and machines, when prices were high. Now, they could not pay it back. And when the Depression came, prices fell even further. By 1932, many farmers were in despair.

--

The president, Herbert Hoover, tried to help. But many people felt that he did not do enough, or did not understand. Shanty towns of homeless people, built from boxes, and scrap metal, and old cars, appeared on the edges of the cities. People called them Hoovervilles. It was not quite fair to Hoover. But it showed how people felt.

{img:shanty-town-hooverville-tin-shacks-river-bank-smoke-cooking-fires}
In November 1932, there was an election. The American people chose a new president. His name was Franklin Delano Roosevelt. He was the governor of New York. He came from a rich family, and as a young man he had been struck by polio, which left him unable to walk without help. But he was cheerful, and confident, and he promised the American people a New Deal. People did not know exactly what that meant. But they hoped it meant change.

In those days, a new president did not start work until March. So for four long months, the country waited. And during that winter, the worst winter of the Depression, things got worse than ever. More banks failed. More people lost their jobs. And in the farmhouse near Harlan, Iowa, the Millers waited too, and hoped, and prayed, for better times.
