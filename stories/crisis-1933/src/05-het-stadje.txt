On Saturday afternoon, the Millers go to town. They drive in their old car, a Model T Ford, which Walt bought second-hand, years ago. It is black, and rattly, and slow, and it smells of oil. But it still runs, most of the time. They drive along the gravel road, between the fields, with a cloud of dust behind them, for about four miles, into Harlan.

Harlan is a small town, of about three thousand people. It has a courthouse, in the middle of a square, with a big clock tower, and green lawns, and benches, under tall elm trees. Around the square, there are shops, and offices, in brick buildings, two or three storeys high. A grocery store, a hardware store, a drugstore, a dry goods store, a bank, a barber, a cafe, and a movie theatre. And churches, on the side streets, with tall white steeples.

--

{img:general-store-interior-1933-shelves-cans-barrels-counter-farmers-wives-trading}
On Saturday, the town is busy. All the farm families come in, to buy what they need, and to sell what they can, and to see their friends. Ruth takes her eggs, and her butter, to the grocery store, and trades them for flour, and sugar, and coffee, and salt. The grocer, Mr Anderson, gives her a little credit, too, when she needs it. He gives credit to many families. He does not know if they will ever be able to pay him back.

The men stand around on the corners, in their overalls and their hats, and talk. About the weather, and the crops, and the prices, and the bank, and the new president, and the farmers who have lost their farms. The talk is often gloomy. But there are jokes too, and laughter. Walt says that he is not worried about the Depression. He says that the Millers have been depressed for years, and nobody noticed.

{img:children-outside-drugstore-soda-fountain-window-1933-looking-in-nickels}
The children love Saturdays in town. June goes to the little library, above the courthouse, and borrows a new book. Eddie meets his friends, on the square, and they play marbles, and talk about baseball. If they are lucky, their mother gives them each a nickel. Then they go to the drugstore, which has a soda fountain, and buy an ice cream cone, or a bottle of soda pop. It is the best moment of the week.

But there are signs of the hard times, everywhere. Some of the shops on the square are empty, with dusty windows, and signs saying closed, or for rent. The men who used to work in them are standing on the corners, with nothing to do. There are more people asking for help, at the churches. And there is the bank. The First National Bank, on the corner of the square, with its stone columns, and its big clock. Its doors are closed.

--

The bank closed two years ago, in 1931. One morning, people came to take out their money, and found the doors locked, and a notice on the door. The bank had failed. All the money in it was gone. Many families in Harlan lost their savings. The Millers lost three hundred dollars. It was all the money they had saved, over many years, for June's education, and for a rainy day. They have never got it back.

{img:shuttered-bank-building-stone-columns-notice-on-door-pigeons-quiet-square}
Walt does not talk about it much. But June knows that he still thinks about it, sometimes. He walks past the bank on Saturday, and looks at the closed doors, and shakes his head. Grandma Hattie says that the money is gone, and there is no use crying over it. She says that what you have in your head, and in your heart, nobody can take away. And she makes sure that June keeps reading her books.

At the end of the afternoon, the family gets back into the old car, with their groceries, and drives home, along the gravel road, as the sun goes down. The car rattles, and bumps, and once, it stops, and Walt has to get out and crank it, to start it again. But they get home, in the end. And Ruth puts away the groceries, carefully, and makes supper. And they sit down together, to eat, as the lamps are lit.
