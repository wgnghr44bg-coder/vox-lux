In the Depression, nothing is wasted. Not a scrap of food, not a piece of cloth, not a bit of string. Everything is saved, and used, and reused, until it is completely worn out. And then it is used for something else. Ruth is very good at this. Like most farm wives, she has learned to make something useful out of almost anything.

Flour, and sugar, and animal feed, come in big cotton sacks. In the old days, the sacks were plain white. But the companies that make them have noticed that the farm wives use the sacks to make clothes. So now, the sacks are made of pretty printed cotton, with flowers, and checks, and stripes. Ruth chooses the flour carefully, at the store, by the pattern on the sack. When she has enough sacks of the same pattern, she makes a dress for June.

--

{img:girl-in-flour-sack-print-dress-twirling-farmyard-proud-smile}
June has three dresses made from flour sacks. One with little blue flowers, one with red checks, and one with yellow daisies. Many of the girls at school wear flour sack dresses too. Nobody is ashamed of them. They are pretty, and practical, and free. The boys wear shirts made from feed sacks, and the babies wear diapers made from sugar sacks. And Ruth makes towels, and aprons, and pillowcases, and curtains, from the plain ones.

When clothes wear out, they are mended. Patches are sewn over the holes, in the elbows, and the knees. Socks are darned, again and again, until they are more darn than sock. Collars are turned around, so that the worn side is hidden. When a coat is too small for June, it is cut down, and remade, for Eddie. When the clothes are too worn to wear, they are cut into strips, and braided into rugs, or sewn into quilts.

{img:women-quilting-bee-large-quilt-frame-church-basement-1933-talking}
Food is never wasted either. Leftovers are eaten the next day, or turned into soup, or fed to the hogs. Ruth bakes her own bread, and churns her own butter, and makes her own soap. In the summer, she spends weeks canning the vegetables, and fruit, from her garden. Beans, and tomatoes, and corn, and peas, and pickles, and peaches, and jam. Hundreds of glass jars, lined up on the shelves in the cellar, shining like jewels. Enough food for the whole winter.

Many people in the towns plant gardens too, in their backyards, and in empty lots. Even in the cities, people grow vegetables, on rooftops, and in window boxes. Everyone keeps chickens, if they can. Meals are simple. Beans, and potatoes, and cornbread, and soup. Some meals are made from almost nothing. Bread, and gravy. Fried cornmeal mush. Potato pancakes. Water soup, the children call it, when there is very little in it.

--

Things that break are repaired, not replaced. Walt fixes the harness, and the tools, and the machines, with wire, and nails, and pieces of old metal, and great patience. He makes new handles for the tools, from wood he cuts himself. He resoles the family's shoes, with pieces of old tyres. When the car breaks down, he fixes it himself. He says that there is almost nothing that cannot be fixed, with a bit of baling wire, and a bit of thinking.

{img:farmer-repairing-shoes-old-tyre-rubber-workbench-shed-lantern-evening}
The children make their own toys. Eddie makes a kite, from newspaper, and sticks, and string. He makes a slingshot, from a forked stick, and an old inner tube. He plays marbles, and mumblety-peg, with his pocketknife, and baseball, with a ball made of string, wrapped tightly, and covered with tape. June makes paper dolls, cut from old catalogues, with paper clothes. And together, they make a swing, from a rope, and an old tyre, hanging from the big tree in the yard.

Many years later, when June is a grandmother, she will still save string, and paper bags, and rubber bands, and old jars. Her grandchildren will laugh at her. But she will just smile, and say that they do not know what it was like. And she will be right.
