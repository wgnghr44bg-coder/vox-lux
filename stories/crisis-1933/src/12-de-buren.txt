On Sunday morning, the Millers go to church. A white wooden church, with a tall steeple, about two miles from the farm, at the edge of a little village. It is a Methodist church. The Millers have gone there for three generations. Grandma Hattie was married there. Walt and Ruth were married there. June and Eddie were baptised there. It is the centre of the community, for all the farm families around.

The church is full on Sundays. The families come in their best clothes, which are often their only good clothes. The men in their dark suits, a little shiny with age. The women in their best dresses, and hats. The children scrubbed clean, with their hair brushed flat, and wet. They sit in the wooden pews, and sing the old hymns, and listen to the sermon, and pray.

--

{img:church-basement-potluck-supper-long-tables-casseroles-pies-families-1933}
After the service, the families stand outside, in the churchyard, and talk. This is almost as important as the service. They share the news of the week. Who is ill, who has had a baby, who has lost their farm, who has found work. Who needs help. In the Depression, there is always someone who needs help. And the church families help each other, in every way they can.

Once a month, there is a church supper, in the basement. Every family brings a dish. Casseroles, and salads, and baked beans, and fried chicken, and potatoes, and bread, and pies, and cakes. It is called a potluck, because you never know what you will get. The food is laid out on long tables, and everyone helps themselves. Even the poorest families, who bring only a little, can eat as much as they want. Nobody asks what anyone brought.

{img:barn-raising-neighbours-lifting-wall-frame-together-ropes-summer-1933}
The neighbours help each other with the work, too. When a farmer is ill, or injured, the neighbours come, and do his chores, and bring in his crops, without being asked, and without being paid. When a barn burns down, the neighbours come together, and build a new one, in a day or two. The men do the building, and the women cook a great feast, for everyone. They call it a barn raising.

The women have their own gatherings. Every week, on Wednesday afternoon, some of them meet at one of their houses, for a quilting bee. They sit around a big wooden frame, with a quilt stretched across it, and sew, and talk, and drink coffee, and eat cake. The quilts are made from scraps of old clothes, and flour sacks, in beautiful patterns. Each one takes many weeks. When it is finished, it is given to someone who needs it. A new bride, or a new baby, or a family who has lost everything.

--

{img:women-delivering-food-basket-to-poor-family-farmhouse-door-kind}
Ruth is one of the leaders of the church women. She organises the help, for the families in need. She knows who is struggling, and what they need. Food, or clothes, or coal, or a doctor. And she makes sure they get it, quietly, without fuss, and without making anyone feel ashamed. She says that the Lord did not put us on this earth to look after only ourselves.

One family, the Dunns, are in very great trouble. The father passed away last winter, and the mother has five children, and no money, and no land. They live in a little rented house, at the edge of the village. Every week, Ruth takes them a basket of food. Eggs, and butter, and bread, and vegetables, and sometimes a chicken. June goes with her, and plays with the Dunn children, while Ruth talks to their mother.

On the way home, June asks her mother why she does it, when they have so little themselves. Ruth thinks for a moment. Then she says, because one day, it might be us. And because, when you share what you have, there is always enough. June does not quite understand. But she remembers it, all her life.
