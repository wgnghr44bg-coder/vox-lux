At the end of November, it is Thanksgiving. The day when Americans give thanks for the harvest, and for their blessings, as the Pilgrims did, three hundred years before. For the Millers, it is one of the most important days of the year. Even in the hard times, especially in the hard times, they believe that there is a lot to be thankful for.

Ruth begins cooking days before. She bakes pies, pumpkin, and apple, and mince. She makes cranberry sauce, and bread, and rolls. On Thanksgiving morning, she gets up very early, and puts the turkey in the oven. It is a big turkey, which Walt raised on the farm. It roasts slowly, all morning, and the smell fills the whole house. The children keep coming into the kitchen, to sniff, and Ruth keeps sending them out again.

--

{img:relatives-arriving-farmhouse-old-car-greetings-cold-november-porch}
In the late morning, the relatives arrive. Ruth's sister, and her husband, and their children, from the next county. Walt's brother, and his family, from Council Bluffs. And Jimmy, Ruth's nephew, who has come home from the CCC camp, for a few days. He is tall, and brown, and strong, and much heavier than he was in the summer. Everyone says how well he looks. His mother cannot stop smiling.

And there are guests. The Dunns, the poor family from the village, whom Ruth helps. Mrs Dunn, and her five children, in their best clothes, shy, and quiet. Ruth invited them, and would not take no for an answer. And Miss Holm, the teacher, who cannot go home to her own family, because it is too far, and too expensive. The house is very full. Twenty-two people, in a small farmhouse. But nobody minds.

{img:long-table-extended-into-parlour-children-at-small-table-crowded-happy}
They set up tables, in the kitchen, and the parlour, joined together, and covered with white cloths. The grown-ups sit at the big table. The children sit at a smaller table, in the corner. Then everyone stands, and holds hands, around the tables, and Walt says grace. He thanks God for the harvest, and the farm, and the family, and the friends, and the food. He thanks him for the new president, and for the hope. And he asks him to remember all the people who are hungry, and alone, today.

Then they eat. Turkey, and stuffing, and mashed potatoes, and gravy, and sweet potatoes, and green beans, and corn, and cranberry sauce, and bread, and butter, and pickles, and jam. And then the pies. The Dunn children have never seen so much food. They eat, and eat, until they can eat no more. Mrs Dunn has tears in her eyes. She holds Ruth's hand, under the table, and does not say anything.

--

After dinner, the men sit in the parlour, and talk, and smoke, and doze. The women wash the dishes, in the kitchen, and talk. The children play outside, in the cold, crisp air, until it gets dark. Then they all gather around the radio, and listen to a football game, and then music. And Grandma Hattie tells stories, about the old days, about the first Thanksgiving she spent in Iowa, in a sod house, on the prairie, in 1880.

{img:family-and-guests-singing-around-piano-parlour-lamplight-evening}
On Thanksgiving evening, when all the guests have gone home, Ruth sits down at the kitchen table, tired, and happy. Walt brings her a cup of coffee. They sit together, and look at the empty plates, and the crumbs, and the dirty tablecloth. And Ruth says that it was the best Thanksgiving she can remember. And Walt agrees.

Less than a week later, on the fifth of December, 1933, there is other news, on the radio. Prohibition has ended. For thirteen years, it has been against the law to make, or sell, alcohol in America. Now, the law has been repealed. In the cities, there are great celebrations. In the Millers' house, it makes little difference. Ruth does not approve of drinking. And Walt only has a glass of beer, now and then, at a wedding.
