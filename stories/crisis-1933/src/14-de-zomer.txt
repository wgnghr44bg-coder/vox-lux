The summer of 1933 comes to Iowa, hot and bright. The corn grows tall and green, in the long, warm days. Walt says you can almost hear it growing, on a still, hot night. A soft, crackling, rustling sound, all around the farm, in the dark. By the Fourth of July, the corn is supposed to be knee high, according to the old saying. This year, it is waist high, and more. Walt is pleased.

But the summer is also a time of hard work. The oats must be harvested, and the hay cut, and stacked. In July, there is threshing. A big threshing machine, driven by a steam engine or a tractor, goes from farm to farm. All the neighbours come together, to help each other. The men feed the bundles of oats into the machine, which separates the grain from the straw. It is hot, dusty, noisy work.

--

{img:threshing-dinner-long-table-under-trees-farmers-eating-women-serving}
And at midday, there is the threshing dinner. The women of the neighbourhood cook a great feast, for all the workers. Fried chicken, and roast beef, and mashed potatoes, and gravy, and corn on the cob, and green beans, and sliced tomatoes, and fresh bread, and pies. Apple, and cherry, and rhubarb. The men eat at long tables, set up under the trees, in the yard. They eat enormous amounts. Then they go back to work, until the evening.

Even in the hard times, the summer has its pleasures. On hot afternoons, when the chores are done, the children go to the swimming hole. A deep, cool pool, in the creek, under the trees, about half a mile from the farm. They swim, and splash, and dive from a rope swing, hanging from a big branch. Eddie can do a backward somersault, into the water. June prefers to float on her back, and look at the sky, through the leaves.

{img:children-swimming-creek-rope-swing-summer-afternoon-sunlight-trees}
On summer evenings, there is baseball. The town has a team, made up of farmers, and shopkeepers, and young men, and they play against the teams from other towns, on Sunday afternoons, and summer evenings. Everyone comes to watch. They sit on the grass, or on wooden benches, and cheer, and shout, and eat popcorn. Walt played for the team, when he was young. Now, Eddie dreams of playing for it, one day.

On the Fourth of July, there is a big celebration in Harlan. A parade, down the main street, with a brass band, and old soldiers, and floats, and children on decorated bicycles. A picnic, in the park, with speeches, and games, and races. A sack race, and a three-legged race, and an egg and spoon race. Eddie wins the sack race. June comes second in the spelling contest. And in the evening, there are fireworks, over the courthouse square.

There is also an ice cream social, at the church. The men take turns turning the handle of the big wooden ice cream makers, packed with ice and salt, until the ice cream is frozen. Vanilla, and strawberry, and peach. Everyone pays a nickel for a dish of ice cream, and a slice of cake, and the money goes to help the poor families. The children run around on the lawn, in the twilight, catching fireflies, in glass jars.

Fireflies are one of the magic things of an Iowa summer. On warm evenings, after dark, thousands of them rise up from the grass, and the fields, blinking on and off, with little green lights. Like stars, come down to earth. June and Eddie catch them, and hold them in their hands, and watch them glow. Then they let them go, and watch them float away, into the dark.

Late at night, when everyone else is asleep, Walt sometimes sits on the porch, alone, and looks out at the cornfields, in the moonlight. The corn is growing well. If the weather holds, it will be a good harvest. Even if the prices are low, there will be enough to feed the animals, and the family, and to pay something on the mortgage. He breathes in the warm night air, and feels, for the first time in a long time, a little hopeful.
