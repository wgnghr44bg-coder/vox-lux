The Great Depression did not end in 1933. It went on for several more years. Things got slowly better, from 1933 to 1937. Then they got worse again, for a while. Unemployment stayed high, through the whole of the 1930s. And the Dust Bowl, in the southern plains, brought terrible suffering to many farm families, in the middle of the decade.

But slowly, step by step, the country recovered. The New Deal brought many changes, which lasted. In 1935, a new law created Social Security, which gave pensions to old people, and help to the unemployed, and the disabled. For the first time, old people like Grandma Hattie would have a little money of their own, from the government, every month, when they could no longer work.

--

{img:farm-wife-switching-on-first-electric-light-kitchen-family-amazed-1940}
In 1935, another programme began, which changed the lives of the farm families more than almost anything else. It was called the Rural Electrification Administration. It brought electricity to the farms, which the private power companies had not wanted to do, because it did not make enough money. The government lent money to the farmers, to form cooperatives, and build their own power lines.

Electricity came to the Millers' farm in 1939. June never forgot the day. A truck came along the gravel road, with men, and poles, and wire. They put up the poles, and strung the wires, all the way to the house. And that evening, Ruth pulled a little chain, in the kitchen, and a light bulb came on, over the table. The whole family stood and stared at it. Grandma Hattie cried. Soon, they had an electric radio, and an electric iron, and a refrigerator. And Ruth's life became a great deal easier.

Then, in December 1941, America entered the Second World War. The war finally ended the Depression. Suddenly, there was work for everyone. The factories ran day and night, making ships, and planes, and tanks. The farmers were asked to grow as much food as they possibly could, to feed the soldiers, and the allies. And the prices for crops went up, and up. Walt paid off the mortgage, at last, in 1943.

Eddie joined the army in 1942, when he was eighteen. He served in Europe, as a mechanic, fixing trucks and tanks. He came home safely, in 1945, and took over the farm from his father. He farmed it for forty years, with tractors, and machines, and electricity, and all the things that Walt had never had. But he still kept a team of horses, for many years, for the love of them.

--

{img:woman-teacher-1950s-classroom-children-desks-sunlight-chalkboard}
June went to college, in Iowa, with a scholarship. She became a teacher, as she had dreamed, and then a writer. She wrote articles, for newspapers, and magazines, and later, a book, about growing up on a farm in the Depression. In it, she wrote about her mother, and her father, and her grandmother, and the flour sack dresses, and the travellers at the back door, and the penny auction, and the radio. The book was read by many people, all over America.

Grandma Hattie passed away peacefully in 1940, at the age of seventy-eight, in her rocking chair, by the stove. She had lived long enough to see the electric light, and to listen to the president on the radio, and to ride the Sky Ride in Chicago. Walt and Ruth lived on the farm until they were old, and then moved to a little house in Harlan, near the courthouse square, where they could sit on the porch, and watch the town go by.

{img:old-couple-porch-small-town-house-1960s-rocking-chairs-evening-peace}
In the evenings, Walt and Ruth sometimes talked about the Depression. About the hard years, and the worry, and the lost savings, and the low prices. But they did not talk about it with bitterness. They talked about the neighbours, and the church suppers, and the help they gave, and the help they received. And Ruth said, once, that in a strange way, those had been some of the best years of their lives. Because they had learned what really mattered.
