June and Eddie go to a one-room school, about a mile and a half from the farm. It is a small white wooden building, standing alone, at a crossroads, between the cornfields. It has a little bell tower, on the roof, and a porch, and a pump, and two outhouses at the back. One for boys, and one for girls. There are hundreds of little schools like this, all over Iowa. One for every few square miles of farmland.

Every morning, June and Eddie walk to school, along the gravel road, with their lunch pails. A lunch pail is an old tin bucket, which once held lard, or syrup, with a lid. Inside, there is their lunch. A sandwich, of homemade bread, with butter, and jam, or a slice of meat. An apple, or a cookie. And a little jar of milk. In the winter, when the snow is deep, Walt takes them in the sleigh, pulled by the horses.

--

{img:schoolroom-interior-1933-wooden-desks-pot-bellied-stove-blackboard-teacher}
Inside the school, there is one big room. At the front, there is the teacher's desk, and a blackboard, and a map of the world, and a picture of George Washington. There are rows of wooden desks, with inkwells, and lids that open. In the middle of the room, there is a big iron stove, which heats the school in the winter. And at the back, there are hooks for coats, and a shelf for the lunch pails, and a water bucket, with a dipper.

There are eighteen children in the school. From the youngest, who are six, to the oldest, who are fourteen. All in the same room, with one teacher. The teacher must teach all the grades, at the same time. While she teaches the little ones to read, the older ones do their arithmetic, or their writing. Then she teaches the older ones, and the little ones practise their letters. The older children often help the younger ones.

{img:young-woman-teacher-1933-helping-small-child-reading-older-girl-assisting}
The teacher is Miss Clara Holm. She is twenty-two years old, and she has been teaching here for two years. She lives with a farm family nearby, and walks to school every morning, very early, to light the stove, and sweep the floor, before the children arrive. She is kind, and patient, and strict, and the children love her. June wants to be just like her.

Miss Holm's pay has been cut, because of the Depression. She now earns forty dollars a month, for eight months of the year. And sometimes, the school board cannot pay her at all, and gives her a note, promising to pay her later. In some places, the schools have closed completely, because there is no money to pay the teachers. But the families around here have decided to keep the school open, whatever happens. They bring the teacher food, and firewood, and whatever else they can.

--

The school day begins at nine o'clock, when Miss Holm rings the bell, on the porch. The children line up, and go in, and stand by their desks, and say the Pledge of Allegiance, to the flag. Then they sing a song. Then the lessons begin. Reading, and writing, and arithmetic, and spelling, and geography, and history. There are spelling bees, on Friday afternoons, when the children stand in a line, and spell difficult words, until only one is left. June often wins.

{img:schoolchildren-playing-recess-schoolyard-ball-game-fields-around-1933}
At recess, in the middle of the morning, and the afternoon, the children go outside, to play. In the schoolyard, and the fields around. They play baseball, and tag, and hide and seek, and Annie Over, throwing a ball over the roof of the school. In the winter, they build snowmen, and have snowball fights, and slide on the ice. At lunchtime, they eat their lunches, sitting on the steps, or by the stove, and swap their sandwiches.

Some of the children are very poor. Their families have lost their farms, or have no money at all. They come to school in thin, worn clothes, and broken shoes, and sometimes, without any lunch. Miss Holm notices. And quietly, without saying anything, she makes sure that they get something to eat. She brings an extra sandwich. Or she asks June, and some of the other children, to share. June always shares. She knows that her family is lucky.
