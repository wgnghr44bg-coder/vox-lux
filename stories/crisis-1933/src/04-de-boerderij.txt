On the farm, the day begins before dawn. At five o'clock, the alarm clock rings, in the cold bedroom. Walt gets up first, and gets dressed, in the dark. Overalls, and a flannel shirt, and heavy boots. He goes down to the kitchen, and lights the kerosene lamp. There is no electricity on the farm. Only about one farm in ten in America has electricity, in 1933. The rest use kerosene lamps, and wood stoves, and hand pumps.

Walt lights the kitchen stove, with corncobs and kindling. Then he takes a lantern, and goes out to the barn, to do the morning chores. Eddie follows him, rubbing his eyes, with Rusty at his heels. The air is cold, and fresh, and smells of hay, and animals, and wet grass. The sky in the east is just beginning to grow pale. The windmill creaks, in the breeze.

--

{img:father-and-son-milking-cows-by-lantern-light-barn-stalls-steam-breath}
In the barn, the animals are waiting. The horses stamp, and snort, in their stalls, wanting their breakfast. The cows low, wanting to be milked. Walt and Eddie give the horses their oats and hay. Then they sit on little stools, and milk the cows, by hand, into tin buckets. The milk squirts into the buckets, with a soft, rhythmic sound. The barn cats gather round, hoping for a squirt of warm milk. Eddie always gives them one.

Then they feed the hogs. The hogs are in a pen, behind the barn, and they squeal and push, when they see the buckets of feed. Corn, and skim milk, and scraps from the kitchen. The hogs are the most important animals on the farm. They turn the corn into pork, which is worth more than the corn. Or it was, before the Depression. Now, even pork prices have fallen.

{img:farm-kitchen-breakfast-1933-pancakes-eggs-bacon-family-at-table-lamp}
Meanwhile, in the kitchen, Ruth is making breakfast. The stove is hot now, and the coffee is boiling. She fries eggs, from her own chickens, and bacon, from their own hogs. She makes pancakes, or biscuits, or fried potatoes. She heats the milk, fresh from the cows. June sets the table, and Grandma Hattie slices the bread, which Ruth baked yesterday. When Walt and Eddie come in from the barn, breakfast is ready. They wash their hands, at the sink, with water from the pump. And the family sits down together.

Before they eat, Walt says grace. He thanks God for the food, and for the family, and for the farm. And he asks for help, in the hard times. Then they eat. A big breakfast, because there is a long day of work ahead. Nobody on the farm goes hungry, even in the Depression. There is always enough to eat. It is money they lack, not food.

--

After breakfast, the day's work begins. There is always work on a farm. In the spring, ploughing, and planting. In the summer, cultivating the corn, and cutting the hay, and the oats. In the autumn, the harvest. In the winter, mending fences, and tools, and harness, and cutting wood. And every day, all year round, feeding and milking and caring for the animals, morning and evening.

{img:farmer-ploughing-field-team-of-horses-spring-1933-black-soil-gulls}
Ruth's work never ends either. Washing, on Monday, with a washboard, and a big tub, and water heated on the stove. Ironing, on Tuesday, with heavy irons, heated on the stove too. Baking, and cleaning, and churning butter, and making soap, from fat and lye. And in the summer, the garden, and canning the vegetables, for the winter. Grandma Hattie helps as much as she can, and so does June, before and after school.

In the evening, after supper, and the evening chores, the family sits in the kitchen, or on the porch, if it is warm. They are tired, but content. The day's work is done. The animals are fed, and settled for the night. The sun goes down, over the cornfields, in a blaze of orange and red. And the first stars come out, over the wide, quiet land.
