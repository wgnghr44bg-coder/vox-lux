Today, Iowa is still farm country. The cornfields still stretch away to the horizon, in every direction, under the wide sky. Iowa grows more corn than almost anywhere else in the world. And more soybeans, and more hogs. In the summer, the corn still grows tall and green, and on a still, hot night, you can still hear it crackling, as it grows.

But farming has changed enormously since 1933. The farms are much bigger now. Where there were once four or five farms, of a hundred and sixty acres each, there is now one farm, of a thousand acres or more. The work that once took a farmer and his horses a whole autumn, picking corn by hand, is now done by a great combine harvester, in a few days. One farmer, with modern machines, can do the work of dozens of people, in the old days.

So there are far fewer people living on the farms. Many of the old farmhouses are empty, or gone. The little one-room schools have closed, and the children go by bus, to big consolidated schools in the towns. Some of the old schoolhouses have been preserved, as museums. Visitors can sit at the old wooden desks, and see the old blackboard, and the iron stove, and imagine the children of the 1930s, learning their lessons.

Harlan is still there, with its courthouse square, and its brick buildings, and its shady streets. It is a quiet, friendly town, of about five thousand people. The old courthouse still stands, in the middle of the square, with its clock tower. On Saturday, the farm families still come into town, to shop, and to see their friends, although now they come in pickup trucks, not Model Ts.

{img:today-small-iowa-town-courthouse-square-clock-tower-summer-evening}
Across Iowa, there are reminders of the Depression, and the New Deal. Parks, and lodges, and trails, built by the young men of the CCC, in the 1930s, of stone and wood. Many of them are still used, by families, for picnics, and walks, and camping. There are post offices, with murals, painted by artists who were given work by the government, in the Depression. Murals showing farmers, and corn, and the history of the towns.

The county fairs still take place, every summer, in every county in Iowa. The 4-H children still show their calves, and their pigs, and their chickens, and get ribbons, just as Eddie did. There are still pie contests, and quilts, and canning, and the midway, with its rides, and its lights. And the Iowa State Fair, in Des Moines, is one of the biggest and most famous fairs in America. Every year, more than a million people come.

In some parts of Iowa, the old prairie is being restored. Before the farmers came, Iowa was covered with tall grass prairie. Grasses as tall as a man, and wildflowers of every colour, stretching away for hundreds of miles. Almost all of it was ploughed up, for farming. Now, in some places, people are planting the prairie again, with the old grasses, and flowers. And the birds, and the butterflies, are coming back.

And in many families, in Iowa, and all over America, the old stories are still told. About the Depression, and the dust storms, and the flour sack dresses, and the penny auctions, and the travellers at the back door. Some families still have an old quilt, made from flour sacks, by a grandmother, or a great-grandmother. Or an old red ribbon, from a county fair, in 1933. Or a little metal model of the Sky Ride, from the Chicago world's fair. Treasured, and kept, and handed down.

If you drive along the gravel roads of Iowa today, on an autumn evening, between the golden cornfields, you might see an old white farmhouse, with a red barn, and a windmill, standing alone, as the sun goes down. And you might imagine a family, long ago, sitting on the porch, in the evening light, after a long day of work, grateful for what they had.
