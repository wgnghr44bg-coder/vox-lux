The people who lived through the Great Depression never forgot it. It shaped them for the rest of their lives. They were called, later, the Depression generation. And many of them kept the habits they learned in those years, long after the hard times were over. Even when they were comfortable, and even rich, they could not bear to waste anything.

They saved string, and rubber bands, and paper bags, and twist ties, and bits of aluminium foil, smoothed out and folded. They kept old jars, and old buttons, and old clothes, just in case. They mended things, instead of throwing them away. They grew vegetables in their gardens, and canned them, every summer. They ate every scrap of food on their plates, and told their grandchildren to do the same.

--

{img:grandmother-teaching-grandchild-to-darn-sock-wooden-egg-armchair}
They were careful with money. Very careful. Many of them never trusted banks again, and kept some of their money hidden at home, under the mattress, or in a coffee can, buried in the garden. They did not like to borrow money, or to buy things on credit. If they could not afford something, they did without it, until they could. And they saved, every week, a little, for a rainy day. Because they knew that rainy days could come.

But the Depression also taught them other things. It taught them to help each other. To share what they had, with people who had less. To be kind to strangers. To work together, as a community, as neighbours, as a country. Many of them said that the Depression made people closer to each other. In the hard times, they found out who their friends were. And they found out that most people are good.

{img:neighbours-helping-each-other-1930s-sharing-vegetables-over-fence}
It taught them to be grateful, for small things. A warm house. A full stomach. A job. A family. A friend. A good harvest. A song on the radio. Things that people in good times often take for granted. And it taught them to be patient. To get through hard times, one day at a time, without giving up. To believe that things would get better, even when there was no sign of it.

The Depression also changed America. After it, the government took on a much bigger role in people's lives. Protecting their savings, and their jobs, and their old age. Helping the farmers, and the poor, and the unemployed. Building roads, and dams, and power lines, and parks. People argued then, and they still argue now, about whether this was right. But most people agreed that the country should never again let its people suffer as they had in the early 1930s.

Many of the photographs that we have of the Depression were taken by photographers who worked for the government, in the later 1930s. They travelled all over America, with their cameras, and took pictures of ordinary people. Farmers, and their families, and migrant workers, and children, and old people. Their photographs show the poverty, and the hardship. But they also show the dignity, and the strength, and the courage of the people. They are some of the most famous photographs ever taken.

The stories of the Depression were handed down, in families, from grandparents to grandchildren. Stories of flour sack dresses, and hobos at the back door, and penny auctions, and the first electric light. Many of those grandchildren are grandparents themselves, today. And they still tell the stories. Of how great-grandma saved string. And how great-grandpa fixed everything with baling wire. And how, in the hardest of times, people shared what they had.

June kept her diary, the one her grandmother gave her, for Christmas, in 1933, all her life. Years later, her granddaughter found it, in a box, in the attic. She read it, sitting on the attic floor, all afternoon. And on the last page of 1933, she found these words, in June's careful, childish handwriting. We did not have much this year. But we had enough. And we had each other.
