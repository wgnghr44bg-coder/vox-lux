Over the centuries, many stories and legends grew up around Saint Patrick, which are told all over the world today.

The most famous is the story of the shamrock. The legend says that Patrick, when he was explaining the Holy Trinity, how God can be three persons, the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit, and yet only one God, picked a little shamrock, a small clover, with three leaves on one stem, and held it up, and said: just as this little plant has three leaves, and yet is one plant, so God is three persons, and yet one God.

--

There is no record of this story until more than a thousand years after Patrick's death. But the shamrock has become the symbol of Ireland, and of Saint Patrick, and on Saint Patrick's Day, people all over the world wear a little sprig of shamrock, or something green.

Another famous legend says that Patrick drove all the snakes out of Ireland. He stood on a mountain, the story says, and rang his bell, and banished every snake on the island into the sea, and they never came back.

In fact, there have never been any snakes in Ireland, as far as scientists know, since the last Ice Age. After the ice melted, the sea rose, and cut Ireland off from Britain, before the snakes could get there. But the legend may be a way of saying that Patrick drove out the old religion, the old gods, from Ireland.

--

The mountain where Patrick is said to have done this is Croagh Patrick, Patrick's mountain, a tall, beautiful, cone-shaped mountain, in County Mayo, in the far west of Ireland, rising steeply from the shore of Clew Bay, a bay full of little green islands.

The legend says that Patrick climbed it, and spent forty days and forty nights on the top, fasting, and praying, for the people of Ireland, in the time of Lent, like Moses on Mount Sinai. And that the demons came to him, in the form of black birds, to frighten him, and he drove them away, by ringing his bell, and throwing it at them.

{img:pilgrims-climbing-stony-path-croagh-patrick-summer-morning}
Every year, on the last Sunday of July, called Reek Sunday, thousands of pilgrims, from all over Ireland, and the world, climb Croagh Patrick, in honour of Saint Patrick, some of them barefoot, up the steep, stony path, to the little chapel on the top, as people have done for more than a thousand years. On a clear day, from the top, you can see the whole of Clew Bay, with its islands, and the Atlantic Ocean, stretching away to the west.

--

Another legend tells of Patrick's Purgatory, on a little island, in a lake, called Lough Derg, in County Donegal, where, the story says, Christ showed Patrick a cave, which was the entrance to Purgatory. In the Middle Ages, it became one of the most famous pilgrimages in Europe, and knights came from all over Europe to visit it.

Today, pilgrims still go to Lough Derg, every summer, for three days, of fasting, and prayer, and walking barefoot around the old stone circles, the beds of the saints, and staying awake all night, in the church on the island.

{img:lough-derg-island-basilica-lake-grey-sky-boats-pilgrims}
And there are many other stories: of Patrick's bell, and his staff, and his tooth, and his arguments with the druids, and his conversations with the old heroes of Ireland, Oisín, and Caílte, who, in one medieval story, came back from the Otherworld, after hundreds of years, and walked with Patrick through Ireland, and told him the old stories of the places they passed.
