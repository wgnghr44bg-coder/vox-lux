What were the first churches in Ireland like?

They were small, and simple: little rectangular buildings, of wood, oak planks, or wattle and daub, with a thatched roof, or wooden shingles, often no bigger than a single room, with a little altar at the east end, and a door at the west end, and perhaps one small window.

--

Beside the church, there was a little house for the priest, and a graveyard, and perhaps a well, and a cross: at first, a simple wooden cross, or a cross carved on a standing stone. Later, the Irish would carve great stone crosses, the high crosses, covered with pictures from the Bible, and spirals, and knots, with a ring around the centre of the cross, the famous Celtic cross.

The priests wore simple robes of wool, and carried a staff, a crozier, and a bell: a little iron handbell, which they rang, to call the people to prayer, and which became the symbol of the Irish saints. Many of these old bells survive, kept in beautiful shrines.

The Irish monks had their own special haircut, the tonsure: they shaved the front of their heads, from ear to ear, and let the hair at the back grow long, perhaps in the way the druids had done. This was different from the tonsure of the monks of Rome, who shaved a round patch on the top of their heads, and later caused great arguments.

--

The services were in Latin, which the people did not understand, but the priests explained them, in Irish. The people came on Sundays, and on feast days, to hear Mass, and to be baptized, and married, and buried. And the priests went around the countryside, visiting the ringforts, and blessing the houses, and the cattle, and the fields, and the wells.

Later, in the seventh and eighth centuries, the Irish would build their churches in stone, small, and steep-roofed, and very strong, some of which still stand today: little stone oratories, like the Gallarus Oratory, in County Kerry, shaped like an upturned boat, built without mortar, of carefully fitted stones, which has kept out the rain for more than a thousand years.

{img:gallarus-oratory-dry-stone-upturned-boat-shape-kerry-green}
And later still, the tall, slender, round towers, beside the monasteries, with their conical stone caps, and their doors high above the ground, which were bell towers, and lookout towers, and places of safety, where the monks could hide their treasures, and themselves, when raiders came.
