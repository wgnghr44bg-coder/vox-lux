Before Patrick and the monks brought books, and the Latin alphabet, to Ireland, the Irish had only one kind of writing: ogham.

Ogham is a strange and beautiful alphabet, of straight lines and notches, cut along the edge of a stone, or a stick of wood. A long line, the edge of the stone, is the base. And the letters are groups of one to five short strokes, cut across the line, or to the left of it, or to the right of it, or notches on the edge itself. It looks a little like the marks on a tally stick, or a barcode.

--

Each letter has a name, often the name of a tree: beith, the birch, for B. Luis, the rowan, for L. Fearn, the alder, for F. Saille, the willow, for S. Nuin, the ash, for N. Duir, the oak, for D. And so on. So, in a way, it is an alphabet of trees.

Nobody knows exactly when it was invented, or by whom. Probably in the third or fourth century after Christ, by someone in Ireland who knew the Latin alphabet, and made a new kind of writing, which suited the Irish language, and was easy to carve.

{img:ogham-letters-tree-names-birch-rowan-oak-forest-calm}
The legend says that it was invented by Ogma, the god of eloquence, and learning, who was very strong, and very wise.

--

Most of the ogham inscriptions that survive are on standing stones: tall, rough, upright stones, in the fields, or by the roads, or in churchyards. There are about four hundred of them in Ireland, and others in Wales, and Scotland, and the Isle of Man, and Cornwall, where Irish settlers went.

They usually have only a few words: a name, and the name of a father, or an ancestor. Like this: of Cunacena, son of Muco. Or: of Dalagnos, son of Dalos. They are probably memorials to people who had died, or boundary markers, to show who owned the land.

{img:roadside-ogham-stone-green-hedges-country-lane-evening}
The stories also say that the druids and the poets used ogham, carved on sticks of yew, or hazel, or aspen, for magic, and messages, and to record genealogies. In the old story of the Cattle Raid of Cooley, the hero Cú Chulainn cuts an ogham message on a hoop of oak, and leaves it on a standing stone, as a challenge to the invading army.

--

When Christianity came, with Patrick, and the monks, they brought the Latin alphabet, and books, and parchment, and ink. And within a few generations, the Irish monks became some of the finest scribes in Europe, writing in Latin, and in Irish, with the Latin alphabet, in beautiful handwriting, and making the most beautiful books in the world.

But ogham was not forgotten. The scholars of the Middle Ages wrote about it, and listed its letters, and its names. And today, you can still see the old ogham stones, standing in the green fields of Ireland, with the rain running down their edges, and the moss and lichen growing on their notches, keeping the names of people who lived, and died, sixteen hundred years ago.
