The most famous story about Saint Patrick is the story of the Easter fire.

It was written down about two hundred years after his death, by a monk, called Muirchú, and it is probably more legend than history. But it is the story that every Irish child knows.

--

In the spring of the year 433, the story says, Patrick came to the hill of Slane, in Meath, a few kilometres from Tara, on Easter Eve.

That same night, at Tara, King Lóegaire, the High King, was holding a great festival, with all the kings, and nobles, and druids, of Ireland. And the law was, that on that night, no fire could be lit anywhere in the land, until the king had lit the great fire at Tara.

But Patrick, on the hill of Slane, lit the Easter fire, the fire of the resurrection of Christ, as Christians do, on Easter Eve. And the king, and his druids, at Tara, saw the fire, burning, across the dark plain, before the king's fire had been lit.

The king was furious. And the druids said to him: O king, unless this fire, which we see, is put out this very night, it will never be put out, and it will burn over all of Ireland, and the man who has lit it will overcome us all, and all the people of your kingdom.

--

So the king drove with his chariots, and his druids, to Slane, to arrest Patrick. But Patrick, the story says, came out to meet them, singing a psalm, and the king's men could not harm him. And there was a contest, between Patrick, and the druids, of miracles, and in the end, Patrick won.

The king, the story says, did not become a Christian himself. But he allowed Patrick to preach, and to travel, freely, through his kingdom.

{img:patrick-before-king-chariots-druids-dawn-hill-calm-meeting}
And the druids' words came true: the fire that Patrick lit on the hill of Slane, that Easter night, was never put out. It spread over all of Ireland.

--

There is a famous prayer, called Saint Patrick's Breastplate, or the Deer's Cry, which, the legend says, Patrick sang, on the way to Tara, to protect himself and his companions, and which made the king's soldiers, waiting in ambush, see only a deer, and its fawns, passing by. The prayer was probably written later, but it is still sung today:

Christ with me, Christ before me, Christ behind me. Christ in me, Christ beneath me, Christ above me. Christ on my right, Christ on my left. Christ when I lie down, Christ when I sit down, Christ when I arise.

{img:deer-and-fawns-misty-forest-path-dawn-light-quiet}
Today, on the hill of Slane, there are the ruins of an old friary, and a church, and a graveyard, and a statue of Saint Patrick. And every year, at Easter, a fire is lit there again.
