The old Irish year has four great festivals that mark the turning of the seasons.

The year begins with Samhain at the start of November. Summer ends. The cattle are brought down from the summer meadows and gathered in for winter. Some are killed for meat, because there is not enough food to keep them all through the winter.

--

Samhain is the most mysterious of the festivals. The Irish believe that on the night of Samhain, the border between this world and the Otherworld grows very thin. The Otherworld is the world of the gods, the fairies and the dead. On that night, spirits can come out and walk among the living. Great fires are lit on the hills. People feast and tell stories, and are careful not to go out alone in the dark.

Many centuries later, the Church will hold its feast of All Saints at the same time. And Samhain will become Halloween, with its ghosts, bonfires and carved lanterns. The Irish will take it with them to America.

{img:imbolc-february-lambs-snowdrops-farm-woman-milking-ewe-dawn}
At the start of February comes the next festival. It celebrates the first signs of spring. The ewes begin to give milk, the first lambs are born, and the days grow longer. It is the festival of the goddess Brigid, goddess of poetry, healing and fire. Later it will become the day of Saint Brigid, the great woman saint of Ireland.

--

At the start of May comes the festival of summer. Great fires are lit on the hills. The cattle are driven between two fires to protect them from sickness, before they go up to the summer meadows. People hang yellow May flowers on their doors and wash their faces in the May morning dew, for beauty and good luck.

The legend says that on this night, no fire could be lit anywhere in Ireland until the king lit the great fire at Tara. Remember that. It will matter very much in the story of Patrick.

{img:bealtaine-cattle-driven-between-two-fires-hillside-may-evening}
And at the start of August comes the harvest festival of the bright god Lugh, the god of all skills. People climb hills and mountains together, pick the first wild berries, and hold fairs and horse races, games and feasts and dances.

--

Many of these customs lived on in the Irish countryside for more than a thousand years, long after the old gods were forgotten. Some were still kept in the twentieth century. The bonfires on the hills, the mountain climbing in August, the May flowers on the doors.

And the August mountain climb became a great pilgrimage. Every year, on the last Sunday of July, thousands of people still climb Croagh Patrick, the holy mountain of Saint Patrick in the west of Ireland.
