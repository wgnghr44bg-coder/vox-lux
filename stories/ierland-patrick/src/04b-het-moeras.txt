In the middle of Ireland, and in the west, there are great bogs. They are wide, flat, brown lands of peat, heather and moss, with little pools of dark water, stretching to the horizon under an enormous sky.

A bog is made of plants, mostly moss, that grow and die but do not rot, because the ground is so wet and sour and cold. Over thousands of years the dead plants pile up and are pressed down into a thick, soft, dark brown layer called peat, or turf. It can be many metres deep.

--

For the Irish, the bog is precious. When turf is cut and dried, it burns slowly and warmly, with a sweet, smoky smell. It is the main fuel of the Irish countryside. Even today, for many Irish people, the smell of turf smoke on a cold evening is the smell of home.

The bog also preserves things. Anything that falls into it is kept almost perfectly for thousands of years, because there is no air and the water is sour. Wood, leather, cloth, even butter.

Archaeologists have found wooden roads in Irish bogs, made of planks and logs laid across the soft ground thousands of years ago, so people could cross. One of them is more than two thousand years old. Part of it can still be seen today.

--

They have found gold and bronze, wooden bowls and shoes, wooden figures of the old gods, and boats. They have even found big lumps of butter, buried in the bog long ago to keep it fresh, and then forgotten.

For the people of Patrick's time, the bogs were mysterious places. They were beautiful and lonely, and a little dangerous, because you could sink into the soft places and be lost. People believed the bogs were at the edge of the Otherworld, the land of spirits. Sometimes they left gifts there for the gods.

{img:skylark-over-bog-cotton-grass-white-tufts-summer-breeze}
In summer, the bogs are full of life. White tufts of bog cotton wave in the wind. There is purple heather, little orchids and dragonflies. Curlews call with their long, sad, bubbling cry. And skylarks sing high in the sky, all day long.
