We do not know exactly when Patrick died. The old Irish annals give two dates: 461, and 493. Many historians think it was some time in the second half of the fifth century, when he was an old man.

But the tradition says that he died on the seventeenth of March. And that is why Saint Patrick's Day is held on the seventeenth of March, every year, all over the world.

--

The tradition also says that he died at Saul, in County Down, near the place where he first landed in Ireland, and founded his first church, in the barn of the chieftain Dichu, so many years before.

And that, when he died, there was a dispute, about where he should be buried. So his body was put on a cart, pulled by two untamed oxen, and they let the oxen go wherever they wished. And the oxen walked, and walked, and stopped, at last, at a place called Dún Pádraig, which became Downpatrick, a few kilometres away. And there, he was buried.

{img:downpatrick-cathedral-hill-granite-stone-grave-green-grass}
Today, on the hill of Down Cathedral, in Downpatrick, there is a great rough stone of granite, lying on the grass, with a cross, and the name Patric carved on it, which marks the place where, tradition says, he is buried. It was put there in 1900. People come from all over the world to visit it, and leave flowers, and shamrocks, especially on the seventeenth of March.

The tradition also says that Saint Brigid, and Saint Columba, two of the other great saints of Ireland, were later buried in the same grave, beside him. There is an old rhyme: in Down, three saints one grave do fill: Patrick, Brigid, and Columcille.

--

By the time Patrick died, Ireland was changing.

There were Christian churches, and priests, and bishops, all over the north, and the middle of Ireland. And in the following generations, Christianity spread over the whole island, peacefully, without war, and without martyrs, which was very unusual in the history of the world.

{img:early-church-site-round-tower-stone-crosses-green-valley-later}
The old kings remained, and the old laws, and the old poets, and the old way of life, with its cattle, and its ringforts, and its feasts. But now, beside the ringforts, there were churches, and soon, monasteries, which became the centres of learning, and art, and books, in Ireland.

And in the following centuries, Ireland would become the island of saints and scholars, and send its own missionaries, to Scotland, and England, and France, and Switzerland, and Italy, to bring Christianity, and learning, back to the rest of Europe, in the dark centuries after the fall of Rome.
