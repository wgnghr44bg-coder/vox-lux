For about thirty years, from 432, Patrick travels all over Ireland, mostly in the north, and the west, and the middle of the island.

He travels on foot, mostly, or sometimes in a chariot, with a small group of companions: priests, and helpers, and young men he is training, and some of the sons of kings, who travel with him. They walk along the old tracks, through the forests, and across the bogs, and over the mountains, from kingdom to kingdom, from ringfort to ringfort.

--

Patrick writes, in his Confession, about how hard it was. He was often in danger. He was attacked, and robbed, and put in chains, and held prisoner, for fourteen days, once, and threatened with death, many times. He had to pay the kings, and their sons, to let him travel safely through their lands, and to protect him.

He writes: every day, I expect to be killed, or betrayed, or taken back into slavery. But I fear none of these things, because of the promises of heaven.

{img:bishop-baptizing-people-holy-well-crowd-spring-morning}
He preaches to the people, in Irish, which he learned as a slave, and baptizes them, in rivers, and springs, and holy wells, thousands of them, he says. He ordains priests, and founds churches, small ones, of wood, or wattle, often near the ringforts of the kings who welcome him.

--

He is especially proud of the young people, the sons and daughters of the Irish, who become monks, and nuns, giving their lives to God. He tells the story of a beautiful young Irish noblewoman, whom he baptized, who came to him a few days later, and told him that an angel had told her to become a nun. And she did, six days later, eagerly, and fervently, against the wishes of her family.

Patrick does not destroy the old ways. He respects much of the old Irish culture, and uses it. According to later stories, he blessed the old holy wells, which had been sacred to the old gods, and made them Christian holy wells, which they still are today. He built churches near the old sacred places. He made friends with some of the poets, and the lawyers.

{img:holy-well-stone-surround-ribbons-tree-rags-green-hollow}
There are still holy wells all over Ireland, by the roads, and in the fields, often with an old tree beside them, hung with ribbons, and rags, and rosaries, left by pilgrims, who come to pray there, and to ask for healing, as they have done for more than fifteen hundred years.

--

Patrick also writes, angrily, a letter, which still survives, to the soldiers of a British king, called Coroticus, who had raided the coast of Ireland, and killed, and captured, some of Patrick's newly baptized Christians, still in their white baptismal robes, with the oil of baptism still on their foreheads, and sold them as slaves. Patrick, who had been a slave himself, is furious, and demands that they be set free.

It is one of the first letters in history written against slavery.
