After Patrick, the most beloved saint of Ireland is a woman: Saint Brigid of Kildare.

She was born, according to tradition, around the year 451, about twenty years after Patrick came to Ireland, in Leinster, the daughter of a chieftain, Dubthach, and a slave woman, Broicsech, who, some stories say, had been baptized by Patrick himself. She grew up as a servant, in her father's house, working in the dairy, and looking after the cows.

--

The stories about her are full of kindness, and generosity, and milk, and butter, and cows. As a girl, she gave away everything in her father's house to the poor: the butter, and the milk, and the food, and even her father's precious sword, to a leper, so that he could sell it and buy food. Her father was furious, and tried to sell her to a king. But the king was so impressed by her goodness that he refused to buy her, and told her father to let her be.

She became a nun, and founded a great monastery, at Kildare, in the middle of Ireland, under a great oak tree. The name Kildare comes from Cill Dara, the church of the oak.

{img:kildare-monastery-wooden-church-round-houses-oak-tree-sacred-fire}
It was a double monastery, for both nuns and monks, and Brigid was its abbess, and became one of the most powerful and respected people in Ireland. Kildare became a great centre of learning, and art, and charity, with a famous school of metalwork, and manuscript painting.

--

At Kildare, the nuns kept a sacred fire burning, day and night, for hundreds of years, tended by nineteen nuns, in turn, and on the twentieth day, by Brigid herself, after her death. Perhaps the fire went back to the old goddess Brigid, whose festival, Imbolc, became the feast of Saint Brigid, on the first of February.

There are many stories of her miracles: that she could turn water into beer, for the poor, and that her cows gave milk three times a day, and that a single basket of butter never ran out. That she hung her wet cloak, to dry, on a sunbeam. And that, when she asked a king for land for her monastery, he laughed, and said she could have as much as her cloak would cover. So she spread out her cloak, and it grew, and grew, and covered the whole of the Curragh, the great plain of Kildare.

{img:green-plain-curragh-kildare-horses-grazing-wide-open-morning}
On Saint Brigid's Day, the first of February, in Ireland, people still make Saint Brigid's crosses, small crosses woven from rushes, with four arms, and a square in the middle, and hang them in their houses, and their barns, for protection, for the year. The legend says that Brigid made the first one, at the bedside of a dying pagan chieftain, to explain the cross of Christ to him, and he was baptized before he died.

--

Brigid died, around the year 525, at Kildare. And she is one of the three patron saints of Ireland, with Patrick, and Columba. Since 2023, her feast day, the first of February, has been a public holiday in Ireland, the first Irish public holiday named after a woman.
