Most of what we know for certain about Patrick comes from two short documents, which he wrote himself, in Latin, when he was an old man: his Confession, and his Letter to the soldiers of Coroticus.

They are the oldest documents written in Ireland that survive. And they are very personal, and moving.

--

The Confession begins: I, Patrick, a sinner, a most simple countryman, the least of all the faithful, and most contemptible to many.

He writes it, he says, because some people, in Britain, and perhaps in the Church, have criticized him, and accused him of something, and he wants to defend himself, and to explain what he has done, and why. And he wants to thank God, for all that God has done for him.

He apologizes, again and again, for his bad Latin. He says that, because he was captured as a boy, and lived as a slave, instead of going to school, he never learned to write well, like the educated men of Britain. He says that he is ashamed, and afraid, to write, because his words do not come easily. But he writes anyway, because he wants the truth to be known.

--

And then he tells his story: the capture, the slavery, the prayers on the mountain, the escape, the dream, the return to Ireland, and the long years of work, and danger, and the thousands of people he has baptized.

He writes with great humility, and great faith, and great love for the Irish people. He says that he would gladly give his life for them. He says that he never took money, or gifts, from the people he baptized, and that when the women gave him their jewellery, as gifts, he gave it back to them, so that no one could say that he had done it for gain.

{img:simple-church-wooden-small-ireland-evening-candles-door-open}
He writes about the Irish, who had never known God, and worshipped idols, and now have become a people of the Lord, and are called children of God.

--

And he ends: I pray that none of those who believe in God, and fear Him, who read this, or receive this writing, which Patrick, a sinner, without learning, wrote in Ireland, will say that it was I, in my ignorance, who did any small thing that I did, or showed, according to God's will. But let them judge, and believe, that it was the gift of God. And this is my confession, before I die.

Two copies of these documents survive in the Book of Armagh, a manuscript written about four hundred years later, around the year 807, by an Irish monk, which is now kept in the library of Trinity College, in Dublin.

{img:trinity-college-dublin-long-room-library-old-books-arches}
Reading them today, sixteen hundred years later, you can still hear the voice of a real man: humble, and brave, and stubborn, and a little anxious, and deeply sincere, who was once a lonely slave boy on an Irish mountain, and came back, as an old man, to give his life to the people who had enslaved him.
