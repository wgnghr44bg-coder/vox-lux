Ireland, in the fifth century, is a land of kings.

There are perhaps a hundred and fifty little kingdoms, each called a tuath, a people, ruled by its own king, a rí. A tuath is small: perhaps a few thousand people, living in a few valleys, or around a lake, or along a river. Everyone knows everyone else, and everyone knows the king.

--

Above the kings of the tuatha, there are greater kings, who rule over several kingdoms, and receive tribute, and hostages, from the lesser kings, to make sure that they stay loyal. And above them, there are the kings of the provinces: Ulster, in the north, and Connacht, in the west, and Leinster, in the east, and Munster, in the south.

And in the middle, in the green plain of Meath, there is Tara, the seat of the High King of Ireland.

Tara is a long, low hill, with wide views over the green plains of Meath, in every direction. On it, there are ancient mounds, and earthworks, and circles of banks and ditches, some of them already thousands of years old, in the time of Patrick. There is an old passage tomb, called the Mound of the Hostages. And there is a standing stone, the Lia Fáil, the Stone of Destiny, which, the legend says, would cry out, with a roar, when the rightful king of Ireland touched it.

--

In the fifth century, the most powerful king in the north of Ireland is Lóegaire, the son of Niall of the Nine Hostages, a famous warrior king, who, the stories say, raided Britain, and Gaul, and took hostages from all the provinces of Ireland, and from Britain, and Scotland. Some even say that it was Niall's raiders who captured the young Patricius.

Lóegaire is the king of Tara, at the time of Patrick's mission, according to tradition.

{img:royal-feast-hall-tara-long-house-fires-warriors-poets-evening}
Being a king, in early Ireland, is a serious thing. A king must be perfect: without any injury, or blemish, on his body. He must be generous, and just, and brave, and wise. If he is unjust, the old law says, the land itself will suffer: the crops will fail, the cows will give no milk, the rivers will have no fish, and there will be storms, and plagues.

--

A king is married, in a way, to the land, and to its goddess. At Tara, in the old days, there was a great feast, called the feast of Tara, at which the new king was married to the goddess of sovereignty, the goddess of the land, and drank from her cup.

But a king is not above the law. He must obey the law, like everyone else. He has a great house, a hall, where he feasts, and receives guests, and holds court, and judges disputes, with the help of the lawyers. He leads his people in war, and in the great assemblies, called the óenach, where the people of the kingdom gather, once a year, for horse races, and markets, and games, and the making of laws, and the settling of disputes, and feasting, and music.

{img:oenach-fair-hilltop-horse-races-crowds-stalls-summer-day}
And above all, he is expected to be generous: to give feasts, and gifts, and to welcome every guest, and never to refuse hospitality to anyone who comes to his door.
