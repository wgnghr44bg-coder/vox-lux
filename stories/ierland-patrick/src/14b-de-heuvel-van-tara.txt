The hill of Tara is the most sacred place in ancient Ireland.

It is not very high: only about a hundred and fifty metres above the sea. But it stands alone, in the middle of the wide, flat, green plains of Meath, so that from the top, on a clear day, you can see, it is said, half of the counties of Ireland: the mountains of Wicklow, in the south, and the hills of Ulster, in the north, and the plains stretching away to the west.

--

People have come to Tara for five thousand years. The oldest monument on the hill, the Mound of the Hostages, is a passage tomb, built by the first farmers, around 3000 before Christ, older than the pyramids of Egypt. After that, people built great circles of banks, and ditches, and wooden posts, and earthworks, and mounds, and burials, over thousands of years.

In the time of Patrick, there are great earthworks on the hill: the Ráith na Ríg, the fort of the kings, a great oval enclosure, with a bank, and a ditch, and inside it, two ringforts, joined together, called the Royal Seat, and Cormac's House. And the long, sunken avenue, called the Banqueting Hall, where, the stories say, the great feasts of the High Kings were held, with thousands of guests, each in their proper place.

{img:lia-fail-stone-of-destiny-upright-stone-tara-mound-grass}
On the top of one of the mounds stands the Lia Fáil, the Stone of Destiny: a tall, smooth, grey, upright stone. The legend says that the gods, the Tuatha Dé Danann, brought it to Ireland, from one of their four magical cities, and that when the true king of Ireland stood on it, or touched it, it roared, so that the whole of Ireland could hear it.

--

The stories say that the feast of Tara was held every three years, or every seven years, at Samhain, and that all the kings, and nobles, and poets, and lawyers, of Ireland came, and the laws were proclaimed, and the history of the kingdoms was recited, and the genealogies of the families were checked, by the poets, to make sure they were correct, and disputes were settled, and peace was kept, for the feast. Anyone who broke the peace of Tara, by fighting, was put to death.

{img:great-assembly-tara-tents-banners-kings-poets-autumn-evening}
Whether all this is true, or how much of it is legend, nobody knows. But for the Irish, Tara was, and still is, the symbol of the ancient glory of Ireland, the seat of the High Kings, the heart of the land.
