In early Ireland, wealth is measured in cattle.

There is no money, no coins. Instead, people count their wealth, and pay their debts, and their fines, and their rents, and the price of a wife, in cows. A milch cow, a cow that gives milk, is the basic unit of value. A female slave, a cumal, is worth three cows. A sword might be worth a cow, or two.

--

Cattle are everything. They give milk, and butter, and cheese, all summer long, which is the main food of the Irish. They give meat, and leather, and horn, and bone. They pull the ploughs. And a man's rank, and his place in society, and his honour, depend on how many cattle he has.

The greatest of all the old Irish stories, the Táin Bó Cúailnge, the Cattle Raid of Cooley, is about a war, between the two great provinces of Ulster and Connacht, over a single famous bull, the Brown Bull of Cooley. It is the Irish Iliad.

{img:great-brown-bull-legend-misty-hill-dramatic-calm-sky}
In summer, the cattle are taken up to the hills, and the mountains, to graze on the summer pastures, while the grass grows in the fields below for hay. The young people of the family go with them, and live in little huts, on the mountain, and milk the cows, and make butter, and cheese, and sing, and dance, in the long summer evenings. This custom is called booleying, and it went on in Ireland until less than two hundred years ago.

--

The Irish make butter in great quantities, and store it in wooden barrels, or in skin bags, and sometimes bury it in the bogs, to keep it cool, and fresh, for months. Archaeologists still find these lumps of bog butter, hundreds or thousands of years old, sometimes in their wooden containers, in the peat. Some of them weigh many kilograms. And sometimes, they are still, it is said, quite edible, though nobody recommends it.

Besides milk and butter and cheese, the Irish eat oats, and barley, and wheat, made into porridge, and bread, and flat cakes, baked on hot stones by the fire. They eat meat, beef, and pork, and mutton, especially in winter, and venison, and wild boar, from the forests, and fish, salmon, and trout, and eels, from the rivers, and shellfish, from the shore. They eat wild garlic, and watercress, and nettles, and seaweed, and berries, and hazelnuts, and apples, and honey.

{img:woman-churning-butter-wooden-churn-ringfort-yard-morning}
And they drink milk, and buttermilk, and ale, made from barley, and mead, made from honey, at feasts.

--

Cattle raiding is the favourite sport of the young Irish warriors: going into a neighbouring kingdom, at night, and driving off its cattle, and bringing them home, in triumph. It is dangerous, and causes many quarrels, and feuds, between the kingdoms. But it is also a kind of game, with rules, and the old stories are full of it.

Later, when Patricius writes about his own times, he complains that the Irish kings and warriors are always fighting, and raiding, and that he had to pay them, and give them gifts, to be allowed to travel safely through their lands.
