{img:ireland-432ad-green-hills-ringfort-mist-river-morning}
Tonight we are going about sixteen hundred years back, to the green island of Ireland, in the year 432 after Christ.

[long-pause]

At the far western edge of the known world, beyond Britain, lies an island of soft rain and green fields. Round farms sit behind earth banks. Cattle graze in the mist. There are kings and poets and druids, and stories told around the fire on long winter nights.

[pause]

The Romans never came here. And this year, a man is coming back to the island where, long ago, he was a slave. He is a bishop from Britain called Patricius. We call him Saint Patrick. Will the people who once enslaved him listen to him now? Tonight we will spend a little time in his Ireland.

[pause]

If you like these slow evenings in the distant past, you are welcome to subscribe to the channel, so that the next one is easy to find.

[long-pause]

Ireland in the fifth century is very different from the rest of Europe.

[pause]

All around the Mediterranean, and in France, Spain and Britain, the great Roman Empire has ruled for hundreds of years. It brought cities and roads, laws and the Latin language. But Ireland was never conquered by Rome. The Romans called it the land of winter. They looked at it across the sea from Britain, and traded with it, but never came.

[long-pause]

So Ireland has no cities, no towns, no roads, no coins and no stone buildings. There is almost no writing. It is a land of farms and forests, bogs and lakes, rivers and mountains, with the sea all around.

[pause]

The island is green because it rains so often. The rain is soft and misty, coming in from the Atlantic Ocean. Then the sun shines through, and rainbows stand over the hills. Winters are mild and summers are cool. The grass grows almost all year, which is good for cattle.

[pause]

There are great forests of oak, ash and hazel, with wolves, wild boar and red deer. There are wide brown bogs in the middle and the west. There are lakes, and rivers full of salmon. And around the coast there are cliffs and beaches and islands, with seals and thousands of seabirds.

[long-pause]

Perhaps half a million people live in Ireland in 432. They are the Irish. They speak Irish, a language related to the old languages of Britain and France.

[pause]

They live on farms scattered all over the island. Each family has its cattle and its fields of oats and barley, and little round houses behind a ring-shaped bank of earth, called a ringfort.

[pause]

{img:circular-ringfort-earth-bank-round-houses-smoke-cattle-field}
They are ruled by many little kings, perhaps a hundred and fifty of them, each with his own small kingdom. Above them are greater kings of the provinces. And above all, in the old stories, there is the High King of Ireland at Tara.

[pause]

They have their own laws, their own gods and their own priests, called druids. They have poets, and great stories of heroes, gods, battles, cattle raids and magic, told by the fire for hundreds of years.

[long-pause]

And they have something else, something their neighbours across the sea fear very much. They are raiders.

[pause]

For many years, Irish warriors have crossed the sea in their boats to the coast of Britain. They raided the farms and villages there, and carried away cattle, silver and people, to be slaves in Ireland.

[pause]

{img:western-coast-britain-villa-evening-sea-mist-quiet-farm}
One of those slaves, about forty years before our story, was a boy of sixteen from a Roman family in Britain. His name was Patricius.

[long-pause]

{img:romano-british-villa-farm-boy-teenager-fields-sea-distance}
Patricius was born around the year 390 in Roman Britain, somewhere near the west coast. Nobody knows exactly where. He says his home was near a little country house that belonged to his father.

[pause]

His family were Romans and Christians, and fairly well off. His father was a deacon of the church and a town councillor. His grandfather was a priest. They spoke Latin and lived the Roman way.

[long-pause]

When he was old, Patricius wrote a little book about his life, called his Confession. In it he tells us that as a boy he did not care much about God or his lessons. He was careless and lazy and did not listen to his teachers. Later he was sorry, because his Latin was never very good.

[pause]

And then, when he was sixteen, everything changed.

[pause]

Irish raiders came to his father's farm in their boats from across the sea. They took him, along with many others, and carried him away to Ireland as a slave.

[long-pause]

In Ireland he was sold to a master and sent to look after sheep or pigs on a mountain in the north. Tradition says it was a strange, rounded hill rising out of the plain, in what is now Northern Ireland.

[pause]

For six years he looked after the animals, alone on the mountain, in all weathers.

[pause]

{img:slemish-mountain-rounded-hill-sheep-boy-shepherd-mist-alone}
He was cold and hungry and lonely, far from home. And in his loneliness he began to pray.

[pause]

He writes: every day I was looking after the flocks, and I prayed many times a day. My faith grew. In one day I would say a hundred prayers, and almost as many at night. I would wake before dawn to pray, in the snow and the frost and the rain. And I felt no harm.

[long-pause]

Then one night, after six years, he heard a voice in his sleep. You have fasted well, it said. Soon you will go to your own country. And a little later: look, your ship is ready.

[pause]

So he ran away. Running away was dangerous. A slave who was caught could be punished harshly. But he walked, he says, about two hundred miles across Ireland, to a place he did not know, where a ship was about to sail. At first the captain refused to take him. Patricius turned away and began to pray. Then the sailors called him back, and he went with them.

[pause]

{img:small-ship-leaving-irish-coast-young-man-looking-back-dawn}
After a long, hard journey, he came home to his family in Britain. They welcomed him with joy, and begged him never to leave them again.

[pause]

But he could not forget Ireland.

[long-pause]

{img:roman-britain-410-villa-mosaic-floor-family-dining-room-quiet}
What was the world like that Patricius came from?

[pause]

He grew up in Roman Britain, in the last years of Roman rule. For more than three hundred years, Britain had been part of the Roman Empire, with Roman towns, roads, baths and churches, and Roman soldiers and laws. Rich families like his lived in country houses in the Roman style. They had floors made of tiny coloured stones in pictures, painted walls, heating under the floors, and gardens and farms.

[long-pause]

The educated ones spoke Latin, as well as the old British language, the ancestor of Welsh. They read Latin books. By the end of the fourth century, many of them were Christians, with churches, bishops and priests.

[pause]

But while Patricius was a boy, the Roman world was falling apart. Enemies were attacking the empire on every side. In Britain, raiders came from the north, from across the North Sea, and from Ireland in the west.

[pause]

About four years after Patricius was taken, the Roman emperor wrote to the cities of Britain. You must defend yourselves, he told them. Rome can no longer send soldiers to help you. The Roman army left. And slowly, over the following years, Roman Britain crumbled. The towns emptied, the country houses were abandoned, the coins stopped and the roads fell into ruin.

[long-pause]

By the time Patrick wrote his little book as an old man, the Britain of his childhood was gone. Newcomers from Germany and Denmark were settling in the east of the island. They would become the English. The British retreated to the west, to Wales and Cornwall, and across the sea to Brittany.

[pause]

It was the beginning of the time later people called the Dark Ages. And in those dark years, the light of learning, of Latin books and of the Christian faith that Patrick carried to Ireland, would be kept burning in the monasteries of Ireland. Later, Irish monks would carry it back again to Britain and Europe.

[pause]

{img:abandoned-roman-villa-overgrown-ivy-mosaic-grass-evening}
Archaeologists have found many Roman country houses in Britain, with beautiful stone-picture floors of gods and dolphins and the seasons. They show how families like Patricius's lived, in the last golden years before the end.

[long-pause]

{img:young-man-asleep-villa-room-dream-figure-letters-moonlight}
Back home in Britain, Patricius had a dream.

[pause]

He writes: I saw in a vision of the night a man coming from Ireland with many letters. He gave me one of them. I read the beginning, and it said: the voice of the Irish. And as I read, I seemed to hear the voices of the people who lived beside a wood near the western sea. They cried out as with one voice: we ask you, holy youth, to come and walk among us once more.

[long-pause]

He writes that he was so moved that he could not read any more, and woke up.

[pause]

He decided that God wanted him to go back to Ireland. Not as a slave this time, but as a missionary, to teach the Irish about Christianity. It was a frightening thought. He would be going back to the land of the people who had taken him.

[pause]

But first he had to study and become a priest. Tradition says he went to France and studied in the monasteries there for many years.

[long-pause]

There were already a few Christians in Ireland at that time. Traders, slaves from Britain, and some Irish people who had learned about Christianity from their neighbours across the sea. In the year 431, the Pope sent a bishop called Palladius to the Christians in Ireland. But Palladius did not stay long, or did not succeed.

[pause]

And in the year 432, according to tradition, Patricius was made a bishop and sent to Ireland in his place.

[pause]

{img:ship-arriving-irish-coast-strangford-lough-bishop-companions}
He was about forty-two. Older, wiser and stronger than the frightened boy who had escaped from the mountain twenty years before. He crossed the sea by ship with a few companions, back to the island where he had been a slave.

[long-pause]

Tradition says he landed in the northeast of Ireland, on the shore of a long sea lake. The first person he won over was a local chieftain, who gave him a barn to hold services in. The little town there is still called Saul, from the Irish word for barn.

[pause]

It was the start of thirty years of work. Patricius would travel all over Ireland on foot, preaching, baptising and founding churches. Some kings would welcome him. Others would not. And he would never know, from one day to the next, which kind he would meet.

[pause]

{img:barn-church-saul-county-down-rolling-hills-lough-morning}
But before we follow him, let us look at the Ireland he came back to. Its people and their homes, their kings and poets, their gods and their way of life.

[long-pause]

{img:ringfort-interior-round-houses-wattle-thatch-cattle-pen-morning}
Most Irish families in the fifth century live in ringforts.

[pause]

A ringfort is a round farm, about thirty or forty metres across. A circular bank of earth surrounds it, with a ditch outside, and often a wooden fence or a hedge on top. There are tens of thousands of them all over Ireland. Many can still be seen today in the fields, as round grassy rings, often with trees growing on them.

[long-pause]

The bank is not really for war. It keeps the cattle in at night, safe from wolves and from cattle thieves. And it shows everyone that a free family lives here.

[pause]

Inside the bank there are one or two round houses, a few small buildings for storage and animals, and a yard.

[pause]

The round houses are made of wattle. Wooden stakes are driven into the ground in a circle, and thin branches of hazel or willow are woven between them, like a basket. Sometimes the walls are covered with clay. Sometimes there are two woven walls with moss or straw packed between them, to keep out the cold. The roof is a pointed cone of poles, thatched with straw or reeds. There is no chimney, so the smoke from the fire seeps out through the thatch.

[long-pause]

Inside, the house is dim and smoky and warm. It smells of turf, wood smoke, food and animals.

[pause]

In the middle is the hearth, a fire of wood or turf that burns day and night and is never allowed to go out. Around the walls are beds, little woven corners with straw, rushes, hides and woollen blankets. There are wooden tubs and buckets and bowls, a pair of stones for grinding grain, a loom for weaving, and leather bags hanging from the roof.

[pause]

{img:inside-round-house-central-hearth-family-beds-wattle-walls-dim}
Richer houses have a few precious things. A bronze cauldron hanging over the fire on a chain, a wooden chest, brooches, a fine cloak, perhaps a board game.

[long-pause]

A bigger family might have a few servants, and perhaps a slave or two, often taken in raids on Britain, just as Patricius was.

[pause]

In the evening, the whole household gathers around the fire to eat and talk, tell stories, sing and play games. The cattle are safe in the yard inside the bank, and the dogs guard the gate.

[pause]

{img:crannog-lake-dwelling-island-round-houses-causeway-mist}
Some richer families live on little islands they have built in lakes, out of stones, timber and earth, with round houses on top. They can be reached only by a narrow path or by boat. Raiders cannot easily get to them. On a misty morning, smoke rises from the thatch over the still water, ducks swim around the island, and a fisherman sets out in his little boat.

[long-pause]

{img:irish-bog-brown-heather-pools-sky-reflections-wide-flat-evening}
In the middle of Ireland, and in the west, there are great bogs. They are wide, flat, brown lands of peat, heather and moss, with little pools of dark water, stretching to the horizon under an enormous sky.

[pause]

A bog is made of plants, mostly moss, that grow and die but do not rot, because the ground is so wet and sour and cold. Over thousands of years the dead plants pile up and are pressed down into a thick, soft, dark brown layer called peat, or turf. It can be many metres deep.

[long-pause]

For the Irish, the bog is precious. When turf is cut and dried, it burns slowly and warmly, with a sweet, smoky smell. It is the main fuel of the Irish countryside. Even today, for many Irish people, the smell of turf smoke on a cold evening is the smell of home.

[pause]

The bog also preserves things. Anything that falls into it is kept almost perfectly for thousands of years, because there is no air and the water is sour. Wood, leather, cloth, even butter.

[pause]

Archaeologists have found wooden roads in Irish bogs, made of planks and logs laid across the soft ground thousands of years ago, so people could cross. One of them is more than two thousand years old. Part of it can still be seen today.

[long-pause]

They have found gold and bronze, wooden bowls and shoes, wooden figures of the old gods, and boats. They have even found big lumps of butter, buried in the bog long ago to keep it fresh, and then forgotten.

[pause]

For the people of Patrick's time, the bogs were mysterious places. They were beautiful and lonely, and a little dangerous, because you could sink into the soft places and be lost. People believed the bogs were at the edge of the Otherworld, the land of spirits. Sometimes they left gifts there for the gods.

[pause]

{img:skylark-over-bog-cotton-grass-white-tufts-summer-breeze}
In summer, the bogs are full of life. White tufts of bog cotton wave in the wind. There is purple heather, little orchids and dragonflies. Curlews call with their long, sad, bubbling cry. And skylarks sing high in the sky, all day long.

[long-pause]

{img:cattle-herd-green-pasture-herder-boy-stick-irish-hills}
In early Ireland, wealth is counted in cattle.

[pause]

There is no money and there are no coins. People count their wealth, and pay their debts, fines and rents, in cows. A cow that gives milk is the basic measure of value. A female slave is worth three cows. A sword might be worth one or two.

[long-pause]

Cattle are everything. All summer they give milk, butter and cheese, the main food of the Irish. They give meat, leather and horn. They pull the ploughs. And a man's rank and honour depend on how many cattle he owns.

[pause]

The greatest of all the old Irish stories is about a war between two great provinces over one famous bull, the Brown Bull of Cooley. It is the Irish Iliad.

[pause]

{img:great-brown-bull-legend-misty-hill-dramatic-calm-sky}
In summer, the cattle are taken up to the hills to graze, while the grass grows in the fields below for hay. The young people of the family go with them. They live in little huts on the mountain, milk the cows and make butter and cheese. In the long summer evenings they sing and dance. This custom went on in Ireland until less than two hundred years ago.

[long-pause]

The Irish make lots of butter. They store it in wooden barrels or skin bags. Sometimes they bury it in the bog to keep it cool and fresh for months. Archaeologists still find these lumps of bog butter, hundreds or thousands of years old, in the peat. Some weigh many kilos. It is said they are sometimes still almost edible, though nobody recommends trying.

[pause]

Besides milk, butter and cheese, the Irish eat porridge and flat cakes of oats and barley, baked on hot stones by the fire. They eat beef and pork, deer and wild boar, and salmon and eels from the rivers. They gather wild garlic and nettles, seaweed and berries, hazelnuts and apples and honey.

[pause]

{img:woman-churning-butter-wooden-churn-ringfort-yard-morning}
They drink milk and buttermilk, ale made from barley, and at feasts, mead made from honey.

[long-pause]

Cattle raiding is the favourite sport of young Irish warriors. They sneak into a neighbouring kingdom at night, drive off its cattle and bring them home in triumph. It is dangerous and causes many quarrels between kingdoms. But it is also a kind of game, with its own rules, and the old stories are full of it.

[pause]

Later, Patrick writes that the Irish kings and warriors are always fighting and raiding. He had to pay them and give them gifts to be allowed to travel safely through their lands. Even with gifts, he was never quite safe.

[long-pause]

{img:currach-leather-boat-rowers-atlantic-waves-west-coast-ireland}
The Irish are a people of the sea.

[pause]

All around the coast of Ireland there are fishermen, sailors, traders and raiders who go out on the sea in their boats. The most famous Irish boat is the currach.

[long-pause]

A currach has a light wooden frame of thin, bent strips of ash or willow, like a basket. It is covered with cowhides sewn together and stretched tight. The hides are rubbed with butter or fat or tar to keep the water out. It is so light that a few men can carry it over land. It floats on top of the waves like a seabird. In the hands of good rowers, it handles rough seas very well.

[pause]

Small currachs carry two or three men, for fishing. Large ones carry twenty or thirty men, with oars and a sail. They can cross to Britain and Scotland, and even further.

[pause]

{img:fishermen-carrying-upturned-currach-beach-evening-seaweed}
It was in currachs like these that Irish raiders crossed the sea to the coast of Britain, and carried away young Patricius and many others.

[long-pause]

And in the centuries after Patrick, it was in currachs like these that Irish monks set out on the ocean. They looked for lonely islands where they could live and pray in peace, and they carried Christianity to other lands.

[pause]

The most famous story is the voyage of Saint Brendan. The story says he sailed out into the Atlantic Ocean in a great leather boat with seventeen monks, for seven years, looking for a promised land. They saw many wonderful islands. An island of sheep. An island of birds that sang hymns. A great crystal pillar rising out of the sea, which may have been an iceberg. And an island that turned out to be a huge whale. The monks lit a fire on its back to cook their dinner, and the whale felt the heat, and swam away.

[pause]

{img:monks-in-leather-boat-iceberg-ocean-calm-mist-wonder}
In 1976, a British explorer built a copy of a large leather currach. He sailed it with a small crew from Ireland across the Atlantic, by way of Iceland and Greenland, all the way to Canada. He wanted to show that Irish monks could have reached America long before Columbus.

[long-pause]

Currachs are still built and rowed today on the west coast of Ireland, though now they are covered with tarred canvas instead of hides. In summer there are currach races in the bays, with the rowers pulling hard and the crowds cheering from the shore.

[long-pause]

{img:hill-of-tara-green-mounds-earthworks-standing-stone-sky}
Ireland in the fifth century is a land of kings.

[pause]

There are perhaps a hundred and fifty little kingdoms, each ruled by its own king. A kingdom is small, perhaps a few thousand people living in a few valleys or around a lake. Everyone knows everyone, and everyone knows the king.

[long-pause]

Above these little kings are greater kings. They rule over several kingdoms and take tribute and hostages from the lesser kings, to keep them loyal. Above them are the kings of the four great provinces, in the north, the west, the east and the south.

[pause]

And in the middle, on the green plain of Meath, lies Tara, the seat of the High King of Ireland.

[pause]

Tara is a long, low hill with wide views over the green plains in every direction. On it there are ancient mounds and rings of banks and ditches. Some of them were already thousands of years old in Patrick's time. And there is a standing stone called the Stone of Destiny. The legend says it would cry out with a roar when the rightful king of Ireland touched it.

[long-pause]

In the fifth century, the most powerful king in the north of Ireland is Lóegaire. He is the son of a famous warrior king who, the stories say, raided Britain and France. Some even say it was his father's raiders who captured young Patricius.

[pause]

According to tradition, Lóegaire is the king of Tara when Patrick begins his mission. What will such a king think of a stranger who comes to change the old ways?

[pause]

{img:royal-feast-hall-tara-long-house-fires-warriors-poets-evening}
Being a king in early Ireland is a serious thing. A king must have no injury or flaw on his body. He must be generous, just, brave and wise. If he is unjust, the old law says, the land itself will suffer. The crops will fail, the cows will give no milk, and there will be storms and sickness.

[long-pause]

A king is married, in a way, to the land and its goddess. In the old days at Tara, a new king was married to the goddess of the land at a great feast, and drank from her cup.

[pause]

But a king is not above the law. He must obey it like everyone else. He has a great hall where he feasts, welcomes guests and judges quarrels with the help of the lawyers. He leads his people in war, and at the great yearly gathering. There, the people of the kingdom come together for horse races and markets, games and law-making, feasting and music.

[pause]

{img:oenach-fair-hilltop-horse-races-crowds-stalls-summer-day}
And above all, he must be generous. He must give feasts and gifts, welcome every guest, and never turn anyone away from his door.

[long-pause]

{img:royal-hall-feast-long-tables-fires-cauldrons-harper-warriors}
Let us go to a feast in the hall of an Irish king, on a winter evening in the fifth century.

[pause]

The hall is a great house of timber and wattle with a high thatched roof. A big fire burns in the middle, and there are torches and candles. Shields and spears hang on the walls, and the antlered heads of deer.

[long-pause]

Everyone has a place according to their rank, as the law says. The king sits in the middle, with his queen, his chief poet, his judge and his druid close by. Then come the nobles and warriors and guests. And then the lesser people, further away.

[pause]

And everyone gets a portion of meat according to their rank. The very best portion, the champion's portion, goes to the bravest warrior in the hall. In the old stories there are great quarrels, and even fights, over who deserves it.

[pause]

{img:great-bronze-cauldron-meat-boiling-fire-hall-servants-ladles}
The meat is boiled in huge bronze cauldrons over the fire. Beef, pork, mutton and deer. A whole pig is roasted. There is salmon from the river, bread and butter, cheese and honey, apples and hazelnuts. To drink there is ale, and mead made from honey, in cups of wood and horn. The king drinks from a silver cup.

[long-pause]

While they eat and drink, the poets recite and the harpers play.

[pause]

The harp is the great instrument of Ireland. It is a small harp, held in the lap, with strings of bronze wire, plucked with the fingernails. It makes a bright, ringing, shimmering sound. The old stories say a master harper must be able to play three kinds of music. The music of laughter, which makes everyone laugh. The music of sorrow, which makes everyone weep. And the music of sleep, which makes everyone fall asleep.

[pause]

{img:harper-playing-small-harp-bronze-strings-firelight-faces}
Then a storyteller tells a long story of the heroes of old. It takes hours, and the whole hall listens in silence. The fire crackles, the rain falls on the thatch outside, and the dogs lie by the fire.

[long-pause]

The feast goes on late into the night. Then the guests lie down to sleep on the benches and the floor, on rushes and furs by the fire. The king and his family go to their own house. And the hall is quiet, except for the sleeping guests and the crackle of the dying fire. Perhaps the music of sleep is still echoing in their dreams.

[long-pause]

{img:druid-white-robe-oak-grove-staff-mist-dawn-ancient}
Before Patrick came, the religion of Ireland was the old religion, with many gods and spirits and sacred places.

[pause]

The priests of the old religion are the druids. They are the wise men, and sometimes women, of Ireland. They are priests and teachers, judges and advisers to the kings. They know the old knowledge, the gods and the stars, and the right times to do things. People believe they can read the future in the flight of birds, the clouds and the stars.

[long-pause]

The druids train for many years. They learn everything by heart, because they never write their knowledge down. They are greatly respected and feared, and they have great power. A king must listen to his druid. In the old stories, the druid even speaks before the king at the feast.

[pause]

In the stories of Patrick, the druids are his great opponents. They try to stop him with magic, arguments and threats. They have the most to lose if the new faith wins. And over the following centuries, as Christianity spreads, they do lose their power.

[pause]

{img:filid-poet-reciting-harp-feast-hall-firelight-listeners-rapt}
But there is another learned group in Ireland that does not disappear. They are the poets.

[long-pause]

The poets of early Ireland do much more than write poems. They are the historians and the keepers of family trees and old stories. They know the history of every family, the deeds of every king, and thousands of lines of stories about gods and heroes, all by heart.

[pause]

Becoming a master poet takes twelve years of training. It is said the students lay in dark rooms with a stone on their bellies, making up poems in their heads in the dark. Then they came out into the light and recited them.

[pause]

{img:dark-room-student-poets-lying-composing-candle-stone-quiet}
The poets are honoured and paid by the kings. They are given land and cattle and treated almost like kings themselves. They travel from court to court with their followers and are welcome everywhere.

[pause]

And they are feared. A poet can make a mocking poem about a king or lord who is mean or unjust. People believe such a poem can shame him so badly that it raises blisters on his face. No king wants to be mocked by a poet.

[long-pause]

When Christianity came to Ireland, the poets did not disappear. Many became Christians and kept their learning and their stories. Later, monks wrote those stories down. So the old tales of Ireland were saved, and can still be read today. Tales of heroes like Cú Chulainn and Finn, and of the Children of Lir.

[pause]

And there are the storytellers who tell stories by the fire in ordinary homes on winter evenings. In the Irish countryside, such storytellers went on telling the old tales until the twentieth century.

[long-pause]

{img:four-white-swans-misty-lake-irish-hills-dawn-legend}
One of the most beautiful old Irish stories, told by the fire for centuries and still known by every Irish child, is the story of the Children of Lir.

[pause]

Lir was a king of the old gods who lived in Ireland long ago. He had four children, a daughter called Fionnuala and three sons. Their mother died, and Lir married her sister, Aoife.

[long-pause]

At first Aoife loved the children. But then she grew jealous, because Lir loved them so much. One day she took them to a lake and told them to bathe in the water. When they were in the water, she struck them with a magic wand, and turned them into four white swans.

[pause]

She said they would stay swans for nine hundred years. Three hundred years on that lake. Three hundred years on the cold, stormy sea between Ireland and Scotland. And three hundred years on the wild Atlantic Ocean in the far west. The spell would only be broken when they heard the sound of a Christian bell.

[pause]

But she let them keep their human voices and their minds, and the gift of music. So the swans could speak and sing.

[long-pause]

When Lir found out, he was heartbroken. He sent Aoife away forever. And every day he came to the lake with his people to talk to his children and listen to them sing. It was the most beautiful music ever heard in Ireland. All who heard it forgot their troubles and slept peacefully.

[pause]

But after three hundred years, the swans had to leave the lake and fly to the cold, stormy sea. There they suffered terribly from storms and ice. Often they were separated. And Fionnuala gathered her brothers under her wings to keep them warm.

[pause]

{img:white-swan-sheltering-three-smaller-under-wings-ice-sea-night}
Then for three hundred years more they lived on the Atlantic in the far west. And when at last the nine hundred years were over, they flew home to their father's palace. But they found it in ruins, overgrown with grass and nettles. Everyone they had known was long gone.

[long-pause]

Then one morning they heard the sound of a bell. It was the little bell of a Christian monk who had built a small church by the lake. They came to him, and he cared for them, and they listened to his prayers.

[pause]

And the spell was broken. The swans turned back into people. But now they were very old, nine hundred years old, withered and frail. They asked the monk to baptise them, and he did. Then they died peacefully, and were buried together in one grave, as Fionnuala had asked. Her brothers lay around her, just as she had sheltered them under her wings.

[pause]

{img:small-stone-church-lakeside-monk-bell-swans-flying-dawn}
It is said the monk saw four white swans fly up into the sky toward heaven. And to this day, in Ireland, it is thought to be very bad luck to harm a swan.

[long-pause]

{img:brehon-judge-seated-outdoors-hill-people-dispute-listening}
Ireland in the fifth century has no police, no prisons and no courts as we know them. But it has a very careful and detailed system of law. It is called Brehon law, after the brehons, the judges who know it and explain it.

[pause]

The laws were passed down by memory for many generations. Later, monks wrote them down in long books that still exist. They are among the oldest written laws in Europe.

[long-pause]

Brehon law is not about punishment, but about making things right. If someone harms another person or their property, they must pay a fine to the victim or the victim's family. The fine depends on the harm done, and on the rank of the person harmed. Every person has an honour price. A king's is high, a farmer's lower, a slave's very low. If you insult or injure someone, you must pay part of their honour price.

[pause]

The laws are wonderfully detailed. There are laws about bees, and who owns the honey if a swarm settles in a neighbour's tree. There are laws about cats and dogs, and what happens if a dog bites someone. There are laws about trees, with a different fine for cutting down a neighbour's oak, apple tree or hazel.

[pause]

{img:bees-hive-straw-skep-neighbour-tree-swarm-orchard-sunny}
There are even laws about caring for the sick. If you injure someone, you must pay for their care while they get better. Their food, their doctor, and someone to do their work. The laws say a sick person must be kept in a house with four doors, so they can see out, with clean water running nearby. And there must be no noise near them, no barking dogs, no fighting and no shouting.

[long-pause]

Under Brehon law, women have more rights than in many other lands at the time. A woman can own property and keep it after marriage. She can divorce her husband for many reasons. If he is cruel to her, or neglects her, or tells everyone her secrets. And she can take her property with her.

[pause]

{img:woman-owner-cattle-ringfort-managing-farm-servants-morning}
The judges are learned men, and sometimes women, trained for many years. Anyone can come to them with a quarrel. They listen to both sides and decide according to the law. Their decisions are respected, because everyone knows that without them, the whole kingdom would fall into disorder.

[long-pause]

Brehon law lasted in Ireland for more than a thousand years, until about four hundred years ago, when English law finally replaced it.

[long-pause]

{img:irish-noble-cloak-brat-colourful-penannular-brooch-tunic-leine}
What do the Irish wear in the fifth century?

[pause]

The main garment is a long tunic of linen or wool, reaching to the knees or the ankles, with long sleeves. It is often dyed yellow or white, and tied at the waist. Over it goes a big rectangular woollen cloak, wrapped around the shoulders and pinned at the chest with a brooch.

[long-pause]

The cloak is the most important garment. It tells everyone who you are. The law says which colours each rank may wear. A king may wear seven colours, a poet six, a lord four, and so on down to the poor, who wear only one colour, or plain undyed wool.

[pause]

The cloaks are woven from the family's own sheep's wool and dyed with plants. Some have fringes and coloured borders. The rich wear cloaks of purple, crimson, blue and green, with borders of gold thread.

[pause]

{img:woman-dyeing-wool-vats-plants-colours-yellow-blue-red-yard}
The brooch that holds the cloak is a little work of art. It is usually a ring with a small gap, and a long pin that goes through the cloak and the ring, and is turned to lock it in place. Ordinary people have brooches of bronze or iron. The rich have brooches of silver or gold, decorated with spirals and animals and coloured enamel.

[long-pause]

In the centuries after Patrick, Irish craftsmen will make some of the most beautiful brooches in the world. The most famous was made around the year 700, of silver and gold. It is covered in tiny patterns of birds, animals, spirals and knots, so fine it is hard to believe it was made by hand. It is now in the National Museum in Dublin.

[pause]

The men wear their hair long, often with beards or long moustaches. The women wear their hair long in braids, sometimes with little golden balls at the ends.

[pause]

{img:ornate-silver-gold-brooch-filigree-spirals-museum-close-light}
They wear leather shoes made from one piece, laced up. In summer, many go barefoot, even nobles.

[pause]

And everyone, rich and poor, has a cloak against the rain and wind, and sleeps in it at night, wrapped up by the fire. For centuries, the Irish cloak was famous all over Europe. It was big, thick, warm and shaggy, and it kept out even the Irish rain.

[long-pause]

{img:ogham-stone-standing-field-notches-edge-lichen-soft-light}
Before Patrick and the monks brought books and the Latin alphabet to Ireland, the Irish had only one kind of writing. It is called ogham.

[pause]

Ogham is a strange and beautiful alphabet made of straight lines and notches, cut along the edge of a stone or a stick. The edge is the base line. The letters are groups of one to five short strokes, cut across the line, or to one side of it, or as notches on the edge itself. It looks a little like a barcode.

[long-pause]

Each letter has a name, often the name of a tree. The birch for B, the rowan for L, the willow for S, the oak for D, and so on. So in a way, it is an alphabet of trees.

[pause]

Nobody knows exactly when it was invented, or by whom. Probably a few hundred years after Christ, by someone in Ireland who knew the Latin alphabet, and made a new kind of writing that suited the Irish language and was easy to carve.

[pause]

{img:ogham-letters-tree-names-birch-rowan-oak-forest-calm}
The legend says it was invented by Ogma, the god of fine speech and learning.

[long-pause]

Most ogham writing that survives is on standing stones. They are tall, rough, upright stones in the fields, by the roads or in churchyards. There are about four hundred in Ireland, and more in Wales and Scotland, where Irish settlers went.

[pause]

They usually have only a few words. A name, and the name of a father or ancestor. Something like: of Cunacena, son of Muco. They were probably memorials to people who had died, or markers to show who owned the land.

[pause]

{img:roadside-ogham-stone-green-hedges-country-lane-evening}
The stories say that druids and poets also cut ogham on sticks of yew or hazel, for magic and messages. In one old story, the hero Cú Chulainn cuts an ogham message on a ring of oak and leaves it on a standing stone, as a challenge to an invading army.

[long-pause]

When Patrick and the monks came, they brought the Latin alphabet, books, parchment and ink. Within a few generations, Irish monks became some of the finest writers of books in Europe. They wrote in Latin and in Irish, in beautiful handwriting, and made some of the most beautiful books in the world.

[pause]

But ogham was not forgotten. Scholars later wrote down its letters and their names. And today you can still see the old ogham stones standing in the green fields of Ireland. The rain runs down their edges, and moss grows on their notches. They still keep the names of people who lived and died sixteen hundred years ago.

[long-pause]

{img:samhain-bonfire-hilltop-night-people-gathered-autumn-stars}
The old Irish year has four great festivals that mark the turning of the seasons.

[pause]

The year begins with Samhain at the start of November. Summer ends. The cattle are brought down from the summer meadows and gathered in for winter. Some are killed for meat, because there is not enough food to keep them all through the winter.

[long-pause]

Samhain is the most mysterious of the festivals. The Irish believe that on the night of Samhain, the border between this world and the Otherworld grows very thin. The Otherworld is the world of the gods, the fairies and the dead. On that night, spirits can come out and walk among the living. Great fires are lit on the hills. People feast and tell stories, and are careful not to go out alone in the dark.

[pause]

Many centuries later, the Church will hold its feast of All Saints at the same time. And Samhain will become Halloween, with its ghosts, bonfires and carved lanterns. The Irish will take it with them to America.

[pause]

{img:imbolc-february-lambs-snowdrops-farm-woman-milking-ewe-dawn}
At the start of February comes the next festival. It celebrates the first signs of spring. The ewes begin to give milk, the first lambs are born, and the days grow longer. It is the festival of the goddess Brigid, goddess of poetry, healing and fire. Later it will become the day of Saint Brigid, the great woman saint of Ireland.

[long-pause]

At the start of May comes the festival of summer. Great fires are lit on the hills. The cattle are driven between two fires to protect them from sickness, before they go up to the summer meadows. People hang yellow May flowers on their doors and wash their faces in the May morning dew, for beauty and good luck.

[pause]

The legend says that on this night, no fire could be lit anywhere in Ireland until the king lit the great fire at Tara. Remember that. It will matter very much in the story of Patrick.

[pause]

{img:bealtaine-cattle-driven-between-two-fires-hillside-may-evening}
And at the start of August comes the harvest festival of the bright god Lugh, the god of all skills. People climb hills and mountains together, pick the first wild berries, and hold fairs and horse races, games and feasts and dances.

[long-pause]

Many of these customs lived on in the Irish countryside for more than a thousand years, long after the old gods were forgotten. Some were still kept in the twentieth century. The bonfires on the hills, the mountain climbing in August, the May flowers on the doors.

[pause]

And the August mountain climb became a great pilgrimage. Every year, on the last Sunday of July, thousands of people still climb Croagh Patrick, the holy mountain of Saint Patrick in the west of Ireland.

[long-pause]

{img:dawn-ringfort-woman-lighting-fire-hearth-smoke-thatch-mist}
Let us spend an ordinary day in a ringfort in Ireland, in the spring of the year 432.

[pause]

The family are free farmers. They own about twenty cows, some sheep and pigs and a horse, and fields of oats and barley. They share a plough with the neighbours, and a mill.

[long-pause]

The day begins at dawn. Grey light comes in through the doorway. Birds sing in the hedges, and the cows low in the yard, waiting to be milked.

[pause]

The mother is up first, as always. She blows the embers back into flame and adds turf and wood. The girls go out to milk the cows into wooden pails. They sit on little stools in the yard and sing to the cows to keep them calm.

[pause]

Breakfast is simple. Oat porridge from the cauldron over the fire, with milk or butter, and perhaps a little honey.

[long-pause]

Then the work of the day begins.

[pause]

The men and boys drive the cattle out to graze. They plough the fields with oxen for the spring sowing. They mend the fences and the banks, cut wood, and dig turf from the bog for the fire. The turf is cut in long slices with a special spade, and laid out on the bog to dry in the wind and sun.

[pause]

{img:men-cutting-turf-peat-bog-slean-spade-drying-stacks-spring}
The women and girls make butter and cheese. They grind oats and barley on the grinding stones and bake bread. They spin and weave wool, and dye it with plants. Yellow from one plant, blue from another, red from a root. They look after the hens and geese, the calves and the little children.

[long-pause]

At midday they eat bread, butter and cheese, and drink buttermilk.

[pause]

In the afternoon, the work goes on. Then a traveller comes to the gate, a man on foot with a staff, a bag and a cloak. He is welcomed in, as all travellers are, and given food, drink and a place by the fire for the night. In Ireland, welcoming guests is sacred. The law says a free farmer must feed his guests and give them a bed.

[pause]

{img:traveller-welcomed-ringfort-gate-dog-family-greeting-evening}
In the evening, the cattle are brought back into the yard, safe from the wolves. The family gathers by the fire with the traveller for the main meal. Perhaps meat or fish, with bread, milk and ale. And the traveller tells them the news from the far side of the kingdom. Who has died, who has married, which king has raided which. And one more thing. A strange bishop from Britain has come to Ireland, he says, and is going around telling people about a new god. The family looks at each other. What will the king say about that?

[long-pause]

Then the grandmother tells an old story by the fire. A story about heroes of long ago, or about the fairies who live inside the green mounds under the hills. The children listen wide-eyed until they fall asleep.

[pause]

The fire is covered with ashes for the night so it will not go out. And the family goes to sleep in their beds around the walls, with the dogs by the door, the cattle breathing in the yard, and the rain whispering on the thatch.

[long-pause]

{img:fairy-mound-green-hill-sidhe-mist-moonlight-hawthorn}
In the old Irish stories, there is another world beside this one. It is the Otherworld, the land of the fairy people.

[pause]

The fairy people live inside the hills, the ancient mounds and the old ringforts. They live in beautiful halls of gold and crystal, where it is always summer, there is always music and feasting, and nobody grows old or sick or dies.

[long-pause]

The stories say the fairy people were once the gods of Ireland. Long ago, before the ancestors of the Irish came across the sea, these gods ruled Ireland. When the Irish defeated them, the old gods went into the hills and mounds. They became the fairies. And they still live there, unseen, right beside us.

[pause]

Some of the greatest of these mounds are the great stone tombs of the Boyne valley. They were built by the first farmers of Ireland five thousand years ago, before the pyramids. The stories say they were the homes of the gods. The greatest, Newgrange, was the house of the god of love and youth.

[pause]

{img:newgrange-white-quartz-front-stone-mound-boyne-valley-winter-sunrise}
And at Newgrange, on the shortest day of the year in December, something wonderful happens at dawn. The rising sun shines through a little opening above the entrance. A beam of golden light travels along the long, dark passage, nineteen metres, right into the heart of the tomb. It lights up the room inside for about seventeen minutes, and then fades away. It has done this every year for five thousand years.

[long-pause]

The fairies can be dangerous, the old people say, if you are not careful. They can steal a child and leave a fairy child in its place. They can lead travellers astray at night with lights in the bog. They can take people into their mounds to dance for what seems like one night. But when the person comes out, a hundred years have passed.

[pause]

But the fairies can also be kind to those who respect them. People leave a little milk or butter on the doorstep for them at night. And nobody ever cuts down a lone hawthorn tree in a field, because it belongs to the fairies.

[pause]

{img:lone-hawthorn-tree-field-white-blossom-may-stone-wall}
On the night of Samhain, and the night before May, the fairies come out of their hills and ride through the countryside in great processions, with music and lights. In the old days, people sometimes heard the fairy music coming from inside the hills on those nights. It was the most beautiful music in the world. And those who heard it never forgot it.

[long-pause]

{img:fosterage-children-arriving-new-household-ringfort-welcome}
One of the most remarkable customs of early Ireland is fostering.

[pause]

Children of noble families, and of free farming families too, are not raised by their own parents. At about seven, they are sent to live with another family. It might be relatives, or friends, or the family of a lord, a poet or a king. There they are raised and taught until they are fourteen or seventeen.

[long-pause]

The real parents pay the foster parents in cattle. And the law says exactly what the foster parents must give the child and teach the child, according to the child's rank.

[pause]

A farmer's son learns to look after calves, lambs and pigs, to dry grain and to split wood. A farmer's daughter learns to grind grain and knead dough. A lord's son learns to ride, swim and shoot, and to play a board game a bit like chess. A lord's daughter learns to sew and embroider.

[pause]

{img:foster-father-teaching-boy-swimming-river-summer-calm}
The law even says what food the children must get. Oat porridge with buttermilk or water for the children of ordinary people. With fresh milk and butter for the children of lords. And with fresh milk and honey for the children of kings.

[pause]

It says what clothes they must wear too. Plain brown and grey for ordinary children. Red and brown for the children of lords. And purple and blue, with silver and gold brooches, for the children of kings.

[long-pause]

The bond between a child and its foster family is very strong. Often it is even stronger than the bond with its own family. In the old stories, a hero's foster brother is his closest friend. His foster mother and father are loved as deeply as his own parents.

[pause]

Fostering ties the families of Ireland together in a web of friendship and loyalty. The kingdoms are held together not only by kings, but by thousands of personal bonds between families.

[pause]

{img:children-playing-hurling-sticks-ball-green-field-summer}
Irish children play many games. The most famous is hurling. It is a fast, wild game played with curved wooden sticks and a small ball, across a big field. In the old stories, the young hero Cú Chulainn was the best hurler in Ireland. He could hit the ball so hard and run so fast that he caught it again before it landed. Hurling is still played in Ireland today, and it is the fastest field game in the world.

[long-pause]

Children also play board games, swim and fish in the rivers, climb trees, and play with toy boats, dolls and wooden horses. They listen to stories and learn the old songs.

[pause]

And when they are grown, at seventeen, they go home to their own families, with gifts from their foster parents. All their lives, they remember the family who raised them.

[long-pause]

{img:irish-wolfhound-tall-grey-shaggy-ringfort-gate-calm-guard}
In the time of Patrick, the Irish share their island with many animals.

[pause]

The most famous Irish dog is the wolfhound. It is huge, tall, shaggy and grey, the tallest dog in the world. It is strong enough to hunt wolves, boar and deer, yet gentle, loyal and calm at home. Kings and nobles keep them for hunting and as guards. Roman writers tell of Irish wolfhounds sent as gifts to Rome, where people were amazed at their size.

[long-pause]

In the old stories, the hero Cú Chulainn got his name because of a dog. As a boy, he was attacked by the fierce guard dog of a smith called Culann, and killed it to save himself. Then he felt sorry. So he offered to take the dog's place, and guard the smith's house himself until a new dog could be raised. His name means the Hound of Culann.

[pause]

There are wolves in the forests, and wild boar and red deer with great antlers. There are badgers, foxes, otters, hares and red squirrels. And in the rivers there are salmon, trout and eels.

[pause]

{img:red-deer-stag-antlers-misty-oak-forest-autumn-morning}
The salmon is the most honoured fish of all. In an old story, there was a Salmon of Knowledge. It lived in a pool in the river Boyne, under a hazel tree. It ate the hazelnuts that fell into the water, and so it learned all the knowledge in the world. An old poet spent seven years trying to catch it, because whoever ate it would know everything.

[long-pause]

At last he caught it. He gave it to his young servant, a boy called Fionn, to cook. And he told him not to eat any of it. But while Fionn was cooking, he burned his thumb on the hot skin of the fish, and put his thumb in his mouth to cool it. And at that moment, all the knowledge of the salmon passed into him.

[pause]

From then on, whenever Fionn wanted to know something, he only had to put his thumb in his mouth. He grew up to be Fionn mac Cumhaill, the great hero who led a band of warriors that protected Ireland in the old stories.

[pause]

{img:boy-cooking-salmon-fire-riverbank-hazel-tree-thumb-legend}
On the coasts there are seals. The people of the west believe some seals are really enchanted people, who can take off their sealskins and come ashore as beautiful men and women. And on the cliffs, millions of seabirds nest, puffins and gannets and gulls, as they still do today.

[long-pause]

{img:saint-patrick-walking-irish-road-staff-companions-hills-rain}
For about thirty years from 432, Patrick travels all over Ireland, mostly in the north, the west and the middle of the island.

[pause]

He usually travels on foot, sometimes in a chariot, with a small group of companions. Priests and helpers, young men he is training, and some sons of kings. They walk along the old tracks, through forests, across bogs and over mountains, from kingdom to kingdom.

[long-pause]

In his little book, Patrick writes about how hard it was. He was often in danger. He was attacked and robbed. Once he was put in chains and held prisoner for fourteen days. Many times he was threatened with death. He had to pay the kings and their sons to let him travel safely through their lands.

[pause]

He writes: every day I expect to be killed, or betrayed, or taken back into slavery. But I fear none of these things, because of the promises of heaven.

[pause]

{img:bishop-baptizing-people-holy-well-crowd-spring-morning}
He preaches to the people in Irish, which he learned as a slave. He baptises them in rivers and springs and holy wells, thousands of them, he says. He trains priests, and builds small churches of wood or wattle, often near the homes of kings who welcome him.

[long-pause]

He is especially proud of the young Irish men and women who give their lives to God as monks and nuns. He tells of a beautiful young noblewoman he baptised. A few days later she came to him and said an angel had told her to become a nun. And she did, eagerly, even though her family did not want it.

[pause]

Patrick does not destroy the old ways. He respects much of the old Irish culture. Later stories say he blessed the old holy wells, which had been sacred to the old gods, and made them Christian wells. He built churches near old sacred places. He made friends with some of the poets and lawyers.

[pause]

{img:holy-well-stone-surround-ribbons-tree-rags-green-hollow}
There are still holy wells all over Ireland, by the roads and in the fields. Often there is an old tree beside them, hung with ribbons left by pilgrims. People still come to pray there and ask for healing, as they have for more than fifteen hundred years.

[long-pause]

Patrick also wrote an angry letter, which still survives. It was to the soldiers of a British king who had raided the Irish coast. They had captured some of Patrick's newly baptised Christians, still in their white robes, and sold them as slaves. Patrick, who had been a slave himself, was furious. He demanded that they be set free.

[pause]

It is one of the first letters in history written against slavery.

[long-pause]

{img:small-wooden-church-oak-planks-thatch-ireland-churchyard-cross}
What were the first churches in Ireland like?

[pause]

They were small and simple. Little rectangular buildings of oak planks, or woven branches covered in clay, with a thatched roof. Often they were no bigger than a single room, with a small altar at the east end, a door at the west end, and perhaps one little window.

[long-pause]

Beside the church there was a small house for the priest, a graveyard, perhaps a well, and a cross. At first the crosses were simple wooden ones, or crosses carved on a standing stone. Later, the Irish would carve great stone crosses, covered with pictures from the Bible and spirals and knots, with a ring around the middle. That is the famous Celtic cross.

[pause]

The priests wore simple woollen robes and carried a staff and a bell. It was a little iron handbell, rung to call people to prayer. It became the sign of the Irish saints. Many of these old bells still survive.

[pause]

The Irish monks had their own special haircut. They shaved the front of their heads from ear to ear, and let the hair at the back grow long. It was different from the haircut of the monks of Rome, who shaved a round patch on top. Later this little difference would cause great arguments.

[long-pause]

The services were in Latin, which the people did not understand. But the priests explained them in Irish. People came on Sundays and feast days to hear Mass, and to be baptised, married and buried. And the priests went around the countryside blessing the houses, the cattle, the fields and the wells.

[pause]

A few hundred years later, the Irish began to build their churches in stone. They were small, steep-roofed and very strong. Some still stand today. One little stone chapel in the far southwest is shaped like an upturned boat. It was built without mortar, from carefully fitted stones, and it has kept out the rain for more than a thousand years.

[pause]

{img:gallarus-oratory-dry-stone-upturned-boat-shape-kerry-green}
And later still came the tall, slim round towers beside the monasteries, with pointed stone caps and doors high above the ground. They were bell towers and lookout towers. And they were safe places, where the monks could hide their treasures, and themselves, when raiders came.

[long-pause]

{img:hill-of-slane-easter-fire-night-small-figures-distant-tara}
The most famous story about Saint Patrick is the story of the Easter fire.

[pause]

It was written down about two hundred years after his death by a monk. It is probably more legend than history. But it is the story every Irish child knows.

[long-pause]

In the spring of 433, the story says, Patrick came to the hill of Slane, a few kilometres from Tara, on the night before Easter.

[pause]

That same night, at Tara, King Lóegaire was holding a great festival with all the kings, nobles and druids of Ireland. And the law said that on that night, no fire could be lit anywhere in the land until the king had lit the great fire at Tara.

[pause]

But on the hill of Slane, Patrick lit the Easter fire, as Christians do on the night before Easter. And at Tara, the king and his druids saw the fire burning across the dark plain, before the king's own fire was lit.

[pause]

The king was furious. And the druids said to him: O king, unless this fire is put out tonight, it will never be put out. It will burn over all of Ireland. And the man who lit it will overcome us all.

[long-pause]

So the king rode with his chariots and his druids to Slane, to arrest Patrick. It must have been a tense moment. One bishop and a few companions on a dark hill, and the most powerful king in Ireland coming toward them. But Patrick came out to meet them, singing a psalm, and the king's men could not harm him. There was a contest of wonders between Patrick and the druids. And in the end, the story says, Patrick won.

[pause]

The king did not become a Christian himself. But he allowed Patrick to preach and to travel freely through his kingdom.

[pause]

{img:patrick-before-king-chariots-druids-dawn-hill-calm-meeting}
And the druids' words came true. The fire Patrick lit on the hill of Slane that Easter night was never put out. It spread over all of Ireland.

[long-pause]

There is a famous prayer called Saint Patrick's Breastplate. The legend says Patrick sang it on the way to Tara to protect himself and his friends. Soldiers were waiting in hiding to catch them. But when the prayer was sung, the soldiers saw only a deer and its fawns passing by. The prayer was probably written later, but it is still sung today:

[pause]

Christ with me, Christ before me, Christ behind me. Christ in me, Christ beneath me, Christ above me. Christ on my right, Christ on my left. Christ when I lie down, Christ when I sit down, Christ when I arise.

[pause]

{img:deer-and-fawns-misty-forest-path-dawn-light-quiet}
Today, on the hill of Slane, there are the ruins of an old church and a statue of Saint Patrick. And every year at Easter, a fire is lit there again.

[long-pause]

{img:tara-hill-today-like-ancient-banks-ditches-view-plains-meath}
The hill of Tara is the most sacred place in ancient Ireland.

[pause]

It is not very high, only about a hundred and fifty metres above the sea. But it stands alone in the middle of the wide, flat, green plains. From the top, on a clear day, people say you can see half of Ireland. Mountains in the south, hills in the north, and plains stretching away to the west.

[long-pause]

People have come to Tara for five thousand years. The oldest monument on the hill is a stone tomb built by the first farmers, older than the pyramids of Egypt. After that, over thousands of years, people built great circles of banks and ditches, wooden posts and mounds.

[pause]

In Patrick's time there are great earthworks on the hill. There is a big oval enclosure called the fort of the kings, with two ring-shaped forts inside it. And there is a long, sunken avenue. The stories say the great feasts of the High Kings were held there, with thousands of guests, each in their proper place.

[pause]

{img:lia-fail-stone-of-destiny-upright-stone-tara-mound-grass}
On top of one mound stands the Stone of Destiny, a tall, smooth, grey stone. The legend says the old gods brought it to Ireland from one of their magical cities. When the true king of Ireland stood on it, it roared so loudly that all of Ireland could hear.

[long-pause]

The stories say a great feast was held at Tara every few years, in autumn. All the kings, nobles, poets and lawyers of Ireland came. The laws were announced, and the history of the kingdoms was recited. The poets checked the family trees to make sure they were correct. Quarrels were settled, and peace was kept for the feast. Anyone who broke the peace of Tara by fighting was put to death.

[pause]

{img:great-assembly-tara-tents-banners-kings-poets-autumn-evening}
Nobody knows how much of this is true and how much is legend. But for the Irish, Tara was, and still is, the symbol of the ancient glory of Ireland. The seat of the High Kings, and the heart of the land.

[long-pause]

{img:shamrock-three-leaves-green-close-dew-morning-grass}
Over the centuries, many stories and legends grew up around Saint Patrick. They are told all over the world today.

[pause]

The most famous is the story of the shamrock. Patrick was trying to explain how God can be three, the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit, and yet still be one God. So the legend says he picked a shamrock, a little clover with three leaves on one stem. He held it up and said: this little plant has three leaves, and yet it is one plant. In the same way, God is three, and yet one.

[long-pause]

Nobody wrote this story down until more than a thousand years after Patrick died. But the shamrock has become the symbol of Ireland and of Saint Patrick. On Saint Patrick's Day, people all over the world wear a sprig of shamrock, or something green.

[pause]

Another famous legend says Patrick drove all the snakes out of Ireland. He stood on a mountain, the story says, rang his bell, and sent every snake on the island into the sea, and they never came back.

[pause]

In fact, scientists think there have been no snakes in Ireland since the last Ice Age. When the ice melted, the sea rose and cut Ireland off from Britain before the snakes could get there. But perhaps the legend is really a way of saying that Patrick drove out the old religion.

[long-pause]

The mountain in that story is Croagh Patrick, Patrick's mountain. It is a tall, beautiful, cone-shaped mountain in the far west of Ireland, rising steeply from a bay full of little green islands.

[pause]

The legend says Patrick climbed it and stayed on top for forty days and forty nights, fasting and praying for the people of Ireland. Demons came to him in the form of black birds to frighten him. But he drove them away by ringing his bell, and throwing it at them.

[pause]

{img:pilgrims-climbing-stony-path-croagh-patrick-summer-morning}
Every year, on the last Sunday of July, thousands of pilgrims climb Croagh Patrick in his honour. Some climb barefoot up the steep, stony path to the little chapel on top, as people have done for more than a thousand years. On a clear day, from the top, you can see the whole bay with its islands, and the Atlantic Ocean stretching away to the west.

[long-pause]

Another legend tells of a cave on a little island in a lake in the north. The story says Christ showed Patrick that this cave was the entrance to Purgatory. In the Middle Ages, it became one of the most famous pilgrimages in Europe. Knights came from far away to visit it.

[pause]

Pilgrims still go to that island every summer, for three days of fasting and prayer. They walk barefoot around old stone circles, and stay awake all night in the church on the island.

[pause]

{img:lough-derg-island-basilica-lake-grey-sky-boats-pilgrims}
And there are many more stories. About Patrick's bell and his staff, his contests with the druids. In one old tale, two heroes of ancient Ireland come back from the Otherworld after hundreds of years. They walk with Patrick through Ireland, telling him the old stories of every place they pass.

[long-pause]

{img:old-bishop-writing-confession-quill-parchment-candle-cell}
Most of what we know for certain about Patrick comes from two short pieces of writing. He wrote them himself in Latin when he was an old man. One is his Confession. The other is his angry letter against the slave raiders.

[pause]

They are the oldest writings from Ireland that survive. And they are very personal and moving.

[long-pause]

The Confession begins: I, Patrick, a sinner, a very simple country man, the least of all the faithful.

[pause]

He wrote it, he says, because some people had criticised him and accused him of something. He wanted to defend himself and explain what he had done and why. And he wanted to thank God for everything God had done for him. We do not know exactly what he was accused of. But we can feel how much it hurt him.

[pause]

He says sorry again and again for his bad Latin. Because he was captured as a boy and lived as a slave instead of going to school, he never learned to write well. He says he is ashamed and afraid to write, because the words do not come easily. But he writes anyway, because he wants the truth to be known.

[long-pause]

Then he tells his story. The capture and the slavery. The prayers on the mountain and the escape. The dream, the return to Ireland, and the long years of work and danger, and the thousands of people he baptised.

[pause]

He writes with great humility, great faith and great love for the Irish people. He says he would gladly give his life for them. He says he never took money or gifts from the people he baptised. When women gave him their jewellery, he gave it back to them, so nobody could say he had done it for gain.

[pause]

{img:simple-church-wooden-small-ireland-evening-candles-door-open}
He writes about the Irish, who had never known God, and now have become a people of the Lord.

[long-pause]

And he ends: let nobody say that it was I, in my ignorance, who did any small thing I did. Let them believe that it was the gift of God. And this is my confession, before I die.

[pause]

Copies of his writings survive in an old book written by an Irish monk about four hundred years later. Today it is kept in a famous library in Dublin.

[pause]

{img:trinity-college-dublin-long-room-library-old-books-arches}
Reading his words today, sixteen hundred years later, you can still hear the voice of a real man. Humble and brave, stubborn and a little anxious, and deeply sincere. Once he was a lonely slave boy on an Irish mountain. And he came back as a grown man to give his life to the people who had enslaved him.

[long-pause]

{img:saul-county-down-small-church-hill-evening-old-man-companions}
We do not know exactly when Patrick died. The old Irish records give two different dates. Many historians think it was sometime in the second half of the fifth century, when he was an old man.

[pause]

But tradition says he died on the seventeenth of March. That is why Saint Patrick's Day is held on the seventeenth of March every year, all over the world.

[long-pause]

Tradition also says he died at Saul, near the place where he first landed in Ireland, and built his first church in a chieftain's barn, so many years before.

[pause]

When he died, the story goes, people argued about where he should be buried. So his body was put on a cart pulled by two untamed oxen, and the oxen were left to go wherever they wished. They walked and walked, and at last they stopped at a place a few kilometres away. That place became the town of Downpatrick. And there he was buried.

[pause]

{img:downpatrick-cathedral-hill-granite-stone-grave-green-grass}
Today, on the cathedral hill in Downpatrick, there is a great rough granite stone lying on the grass, with a cross and the name Patric carved on it. It marks the place where tradition says he lies. People come from all over the world to visit it and leave flowers and shamrocks, especially on the seventeenth of March.

[pause]

Tradition says that Saint Brigid and Saint Columba, two other great saints of Ireland, were later buried in the same grave beside him.

[long-pause]

By the time Patrick died, Ireland was changing.

[pause]

There were churches, priests and bishops all over the north and middle of Ireland. In the generations that followed, Christianity spread over the whole island peacefully, without war. That was very unusual in the history of the world.

[pause]

{img:early-church-site-round-tower-stone-crosses-green-valley-later}
The old kings remained, and the old laws, the old poets and the old way of life with its cattle, ringforts and feasts. But now there were churches beside the ringforts. And soon there were monasteries, which became the centres of learning, art and books in Ireland.

[pause]

In the centuries that followed, Ireland would become the island of saints and scholars. It would send its own missionaries to Scotland and England, France, Switzerland and Italy. They would bring faith and learning back to the rest of Europe, in the dark centuries after the fall of Rome.

[long-pause]

{img:iona-island-abbey-white-sand-turquoise-sea-mull-hills}
About a hundred years after Patrick, another great Irish saint carried the faith across the sea to Scotland. His name was Columba. In Irish he was called Colm Cille, the dove of the church.

[pause]

He was born in the northwest of Ireland, a prince of a royal family. He could have been a king. But he became a monk and a scholar instead, and founded monasteries in Ireland.

[long-pause]

There is a famous story about him. He secretly copied a beautiful book of psalms that belonged to another monk. That monk said the copy belonged to him, because the book was his. The quarrel went all the way to the High King, who said: to every cow its calf, and to every book its copy. So the copy belonged to the other monk. Some people call it the first copyright case in history.

[pause]

The story goes on to say that the quarrel grew and led to a battle. Columba was full of sorrow. He decided to leave Ireland as a penance, and never see it again.

[pause]

{img:currach-twelve-monks-sailing-to-iona-sea-islands-dawn}
In 563 he sailed with twelve companions in a leather boat across the sea, to the little island of Iona off the west coast of Scotland. There he founded a monastery that became one of the most famous in Europe.

[long-pause]

Iona is a small, beautiful island, with white sand beaches, clear turquoise sea, green fields and wide views of the mountains on the nearby island of Mull. From Iona, Columba and his monks brought Christianity to the north of Scotland. Later his monks went south to England and founded another famous monastery there.

[pause]

Columba was a great poet and writer of books, and a strong, sometimes fierce leader. All his life he loved Ireland and missed it. A poem said to be by him speaks of a grey eye that looks back on Ireland, and will never see its people again.

[pause]

{img:old-monk-white-horse-weeping-head-on-shoulder-iona-evening}
A monk who wrote his life tells a beautiful story about his last day. The old saint was walking slowly, and sat down to rest beside the road. The old white horse that carried the milk pails came up to him. It laid its head on his chest and wept, because it knew its master was going to leave it. Columba blessed the horse. And that night, in the church, he died peacefully among his monks.

[long-pause]

{img:saint-brigid-woman-cloak-kildare-oak-church-cows-dawn}
After Patrick, the most loved saint of Ireland is a woman, Saint Brigid of Kildare.

[pause]

Tradition says she was born about twenty years after Patrick came to Ireland. Her father was a chieftain and her mother was a slave woman. She grew up as a servant in her father's house, working in the dairy and looking after the cows.

[long-pause]

The stories about her are full of kindness and generosity, and full of milk and butter and cows. As a girl, she gave away everything in her father's house to the poor. The butter, the milk, the food, and even her father's precious sword, which she gave to a sick man so he could sell it and buy food. Her father was furious and tried to sell her to a king. But the king was so impressed by her goodness that he refused to buy her. He told her father to let her be.

[pause]

She became a nun and founded a great monastery at Kildare, in the middle of Ireland, under a great oak tree. The name Kildare means the church of the oak.

[pause]

{img:kildare-monastery-wooden-church-round-houses-oak-tree-sacred-fire}
It was a monastery for both nuns and monks, and Brigid was its leader. She became one of the most powerful and respected people in Ireland. Kildare became a great centre of learning and art and care for the poor.

[long-pause]

At Kildare, the nuns kept a sacred fire burning day and night for hundreds of years. Perhaps it went back to the old goddess Brigid, whose spring festival became the feast of Saint Brigid on the first of February.

[pause]

There are many stories of her wonders. Her cows gave milk three times a day. A single basket of her butter never ran out. She hung her wet cloak to dry on a sunbeam. And when she asked a king for land for her monastery, he laughed. You can have as much as your cloak will cover, he said. So she spread out her cloak, and it grew and grew, until it covered the whole great plain of Kildare.

[pause]

{img:green-plain-curragh-kildare-horses-grazing-wide-open-morning}
On Saint Brigid's Day, people in Ireland still make Brigid's crosses. They are small crosses woven from rushes, with four arms and a square in the middle. People hang them in their houses and barns for protection for the coming year. The legend says Brigid made the first one at the bedside of a dying chieftain, to explain the cross of Christ to him.

[long-pause]

Brigid died at Kildare around the year 525. She is one of the three patron saints of Ireland, with Patrick and Columba. Since 2023, her feast day, the first of February, has been a public holiday in Ireland. It is the first Irish public holiday named after a woman.

[long-pause]

{img:early-irish-monastery-beehive-huts-stone-island-sea-cliffs}
In the century after Patrick, monasteries spread all over Ireland.

[pause]

Some were great, busy places, like little towns, with hundreds or even thousands of monks, students, craftsmen and farmers living around them. One stood on the river Shannon in the middle of Ireland. Another lay in a beautiful valley between two lakes in the mountains south of Dublin.

[long-pause]

Others were tiny, remote hermitages on islands or mountains, where a few monks lived alone in prayer, as far from the world as they could get.

[pause]

The most remarkable of these is Skellig Michael. It is a great, steep, jagged rock rising out of the Atlantic Ocean, twelve kilometres off the coast. A little community of monks lived there on a narrow ledge near the top, more than a hundred and seventy metres above the waves. They lived in little round stone huts shaped like beehives, built without mortar. There was a little church, a graveyard and tiny terraced gardens. To reach it, you climb more than six hundred stone steps cut into the rock.

[pause]

They lived there for hundreds of years, in the wind and the spray and the storms, with the seabirds and the seals. They fished, grew a few vegetables and prayed. Their beehive huts are still there today. In recent years the island became famous all over the world, because it appeared in the Star Wars films.

[long-pause]

In the monasteries, the Irish monks learned Latin and Greek, and studied the Bible and the old Roman writers. And they copied books by hand, on parchment made from calfskin, with feather pens, inks and colours.

[pause]

And they made some of the most beautiful books in the world.

[pause]

{img:monk-illuminating-manuscript-intricate-knotwork-colours-desk}
The most famous is the Book of Kells, made around the year 800. It is a copy of the four Gospels in Latin, decorated with wonderful pictures and patterns in brilliant colours. There are spirals and knots, strange animals and birds, angels and saints. Some patterns are so tiny you can only see them properly with a magnifying glass. A visitor who saw a book like it long ago said it must have been made by angels, not by people.

[long-pause]

The Book of Kells is now kept in a library in Dublin. Every year, more than half a million people come to see it in its glass case. A different page is shown each day.

[pause]

The Irish monks also wrote down the old Irish stories, laws, poems and history in the Irish language, so they would not be lost. And they wrote little poems of their own in the margins of the books they were copying. One monk wrote a poem about his white cat, Pangur. The cat hunted mice, he wrote, while the monk hunted for the meaning of words.

[pause]

{img:monk-and-white-cat-scriptorium-candle-mouse-hole-quiet-evening}
Another monk wrote a tiny poem in the margin about a blackbird. It whistles from the tip of its bright yellow beak, he wrote, and sends its song across the water, from a yellow-tipped branch.

[long-pause]

{img:irish-monks-walking-alps-pass-staffs-satchels-books-snow}
In the centuries after Patrick, Ireland sent its monks out all over Europe.

[pause]

They called it going into exile for the love of God. They left their beloved homeland and travelled as pilgrims and missionaries to other lands, knowing they would never return.

[long-pause]

One of the most famous was Columbanus, a monk from the north of Ireland. Around the year 590 he sailed to France with twelve companions. He founded monasteries in the wild forests of the east of France. Later he quarrelled with the kings there and had to leave. So he crossed the Alps into Switzerland and Italy, and founded a great monastery in the mountains of northern Italy, where he died.

[pause]

One of his companions, Gallus, stayed behind in Switzerland by a lake. He lived as a hermit in the forest. After his death, a monastery grew up around his little hut. It became one of the greatest centres of learning in Europe, with a wonderful library that still exists today. It still holds books written by Irish monks twelve hundred years ago.

[pause]

Irish monks went to England, Scotland, France, Belgium, Germany, Austria and Italy. They founded monasteries and schools. They taught Latin and Greek, copied books, and kept learning alive in the centuries after the fall of Rome, when much of Europe had forgotten it.

[long-pause]

They carried their books in leather bags on their journeys. Many of the oldest books in Europe's libraries were written by Irish monks, with little notes in Irish in the margins. They grumble about the cold, the bad ink and the poor light. They write little poems about birds and cats and the weather.

[pause]

One monk wrote in the margin: the wind is fierce tonight. It tosses the white hair of the sea. I do not fear the wild Vikings tonight. Because on stormy nights, the Viking raiders who had begun to come to Ireland could not sail. So a storm meant a safe night's sleep.

[pause]

{img:monk-at-window-stormy-night-sea-waves-candle-writing-relief}
And so the faith and the learning that the slave boy from Britain had brought back to Ireland went out again from Ireland, all over Europe.

[long-pause]

{img:st-patricks-day-parade-dublin-green-crowds-music-sunny}
Today, Saint Patrick is remembered all over the world.

[pause]

On the seventeenth of March every year, Saint Patrick's Day is celebrated not only in Ireland, but wherever the Irish have gone. In America and Canada, Australia and Britain and far beyond. There are parades with bands and dancers, people dressed in green with shamrocks, and music and dancing.

[long-pause]

The first Saint Patrick's Day parades were not in Ireland, but in America, more than two hundred and fifty years ago. They were started by Irish soldiers and immigrants who wanted to remember their homeland. The New York parade is now one of the biggest parades in the world. In Chicago they even dye the river green.

[pause]

In Ireland, Saint Patrick's Day is a national holiday, with a great festival in Dublin. And there are pilgrimages and church services at the places linked to the saint.

[pause]

And the old Ireland of Patrick's time can still be seen, if you know where to look.

[long-pause]

The ringforts are still there, tens of thousands of them, in fields all over Ireland. They are round, grassy banks, often with hawthorn trees growing on them. For centuries, Irish farmers would not touch them or plough them or cut down their trees. They believed fairies lived in them, and that disturbing them would bring bad luck. And so the ringforts survived.

[pause]

The ogham stones still stand in the fields and churchyards with their mysterious notches. The holy wells are still visited and decorated with ribbons and flowers.

[pause]

On the hill of Tara, the ancient mounds and the Stone of Destiny are still there, with the wind blowing and the wide view over the plains. On the hill of Slane, the ruins of an old church stand where Patrick lit his Easter fire. And Croagh Patrick still rises above the bay in the west, with pilgrims climbing it every summer.

[long-pause]

There are rebuilt ringforts and round houses in open-air museums, where you can walk into a round house with its woven walls, its thatch and its fire, and imagine life in Patrick's time.

[pause]

And in the National Museum in Dublin are the beautiful treasures of early Christian Ireland. Among them is an old iron bell. Tradition says it belonged to Saint Patrick himself. It is kept in a beautiful case of bronze, gold, silver and jewels, made for it about a thousand years ago.

[pause]

{img:ancient-iron-handbell-jewelled-shrine-museum-case-light}
So we leave Ireland tonight, as the soft rain falls on the green fields, and the evening mist settles over the round grassy banks of the old ringforts, where the fires once burned, and the stories were told.

[pause]

The slave boy on the mountain, the bishop on the long roads, the poets by the fire, the cattle in the summer meadows, and the fire on the hill of Slane that was never put out.

[pause]

{img:irish-countryside-dusk-mist-green-fields-stone-walls-rain}
If you enjoyed this evening in old Ireland, you are very welcome to subscribe to the channel, and to come back for another quiet story.

[pause]

There should be another quiet story waiting on the screen now, if you are still awake.
