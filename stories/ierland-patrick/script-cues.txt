{img:ireland-432ad-green-hills-ringfort-mist-river-morning}
Tonight we are going about sixteen hundred years back, to the green island of Ireland, in the year 432 after Christ.

[long-pause]

At the far western edge of the known world, beyond Britain, there is an island of soft rain and green fields, of round farmsteads behind earthen banks, of cattle grazing in the mist, of kings and poets and druids, and of stories told around the fire on long winter nights.

[pause]

The Romans never came here. And in this year, a man is coming back to the island where, long ago, he was a slave: a bishop, from Britain, called Patricius. We call him Saint Patrick. And tonight, we will spend a little time in his Ireland.

[pause]

If you like these slow evenings in the distant past, you are welcome to subscribe to the channel, so that the next one is easy to find.

[long-pause]

Ireland, in the fifth century, is very different from the rest of Europe.

[pause]

All around the Mediterranean, and in Gaul, and Spain, and Britain, the great Roman Empire has ruled for hundreds of years, with its cities, and its roads, and its villas, and its laws, and its Latin language. But Ireland was never conquered by Rome. The Romans called it Hibernia, the land of winter, and looked at it across the sea from Britain, and traded with it, but never came.

[long-pause]

So Ireland has no cities, no towns, no roads, no coins, no stone buildings, and almost no writing. It is a land of farms, and forests, and bogs, and lakes, and rivers, and mountains, and the sea all around.

[pause]

The island is green, because it rains so often: soft, gentle, misty rain, coming in from the Atlantic Ocean, in the west, and then the sun shining through, and rainbows over the hills. It is mild, not very cold in winter, and not very hot in summer. The grass grows almost all year round, which is good for cattle.

[pause]

There are great forests of oak, and ash, and hazel, and elm, and yew, where there are wolves, and wild boar, and red deer. There are wide brown bogs, of peat, and heather, in the middle of the island, and in the west. There are lakes, and rivers full of salmon. And around the coasts, there are cliffs, and beaches, and islands, with seals, and seabirds, by the thousand.

[long-pause]

Perhaps half a million people live in Ireland, in the year 432, perhaps fewer. They are the Irish, the Gaels, who speak Irish, a Celtic language, related to the languages of the ancient Britons, and the Gauls.

[pause]

They live on scattered farms, all over the island, each family with its cattle, and its fields of oats and barley, and its little round houses, behind a circular bank of earth, called a ringfort.

[pause]

{img:circular-ringfort-earth-bank-round-houses-smoke-cattle-field}
They are ruled by many little kings, perhaps a hundred and fifty of them, each with his own small kingdom, called a tuath. Above them, there are greater kings, of the provinces. And above all, in legend, there is the High King of Ireland, at Tara.

[pause]

They have their own laws, and their own gods, and their own priests, the druids, and their own poets, and their own great stories, of heroes, and gods, and battles, and cattle raids, and magic, which they have told, and retold, by the fire, for hundreds of years.

[long-pause]

And they have something else, which their neighbours across the sea fear very much: they are raiders.

[pause]

For many years, Irish warriors have crossed the sea, in their boats, to the coast of Britain, and raided the farms and villages there, and carried away cattle, and silver, and people, to be slaves, in Ireland.

[pause]

{img:western-coast-britain-villa-evening-sea-mist-quiet-farm}
And one of those slaves, about forty years before our story, was a boy of sixteen, from a Roman family in Britain. His name was Patricius.

[long-pause]

{img:romano-british-villa-farm-boy-teenager-fields-sea-distance}
Patricius was born, around the year 390, in Roman Britain, somewhere near the west coast, perhaps in what is now Wales, or the north west of England, or Scotland. Nobody knows exactly where. He says that his home was at a place called Bannavem Taburniae, near a little villa, which belonged to his father.

[pause]

His family were Romans, and Christians, and quite well off. His father, Calpornius, was a deacon of the Church, and a town councillor. His grandfather, Potitus, was a priest. They spoke Latin, and British, and lived in the Roman way.

[long-pause]

Patricius tells us, in a little book he wrote when he was an old man, his Confession, that as a boy he did not care much about God, or about his studies. He was careless, and lazy, and did not pay attention to his teachers, which he later regretted, because his Latin was never very good.

[pause]

And then, when he was sixteen, everything changed.

[pause]

Irish raiders came to his father's villa, in their boats, from across the sea, and took him, with many thousands of other people, he says, and carried him away to Ireland, as a slave.

[long-pause]

In Ireland, he was sold to a master, and sent to look after his sheep, or his pigs, on a mountain, in the north of Ireland. Tradition says that it was the mountain of Slemish, in what is now County Antrim, in Northern Ireland, a strange, rounded hill, rising out of the plain. Others think it was in the far west, near the wood of Foclut, by the western sea.

[pause]

For six years, he looked after the animals, alone, on the mountain, in all weathers.

[pause]

{img:slemish-mountain-rounded-hill-sheep-boy-shepherd-mist-alone}
He was cold, and hungry, and lonely, and far from home. And in his loneliness, he began to pray.

[pause]

He writes: after I came to Ireland, I was tending flocks every day, and I prayed many times a day. More and more, the love of God, and fear of Him, grew in me, and my faith grew, and my spirit was moved, so that in a single day I would say as many as a hundred prayers, and almost as many at night. I would even stay in the woods, and on the mountain, and before dawn, I would wake up to pray, in the snow, and the frost, and the rain. And I felt no harm.

[long-pause]

Then, one night, after six years, he heard a voice, in his sleep, saying: you have fasted well. Soon you will go to your own country. And a little later: look, your ship is ready.

[pause]

So he ran away. He walked, he says, about two hundred miles, across Ireland, to a place he did not know, where there was a ship, about to sail. At first, the captain refused to take him. But then the sailors called him back, and he went with them.

[pause]

{img:small-ship-leaving-irish-coast-young-man-looking-back-dawn}
After a long and difficult journey, he came home, to his family, in Britain. They welcomed him, and begged him never to leave them again.

[pause]

But he could not forget Ireland.

[long-pause]

{img:roman-britain-410-villa-mosaic-floor-family-dining-room-quiet}
What was the world like, that Patricius came from?

[pause]

He grew up in Roman Britain, in the last years of Roman rule. For more than three hundred years, Britain had been a province of the Roman Empire, with Roman towns, and roads, and villas, and baths, and temples, and churches, and soldiers, and laws, and coins. Rich families, like Patricius's, lived in villas, country houses in the Roman style, with mosaic floors, and painted walls, and underfloor heating, and baths, and gardens, and farms, worked by slaves, and tenants.

[long-pause]

They spoke Latin, at least the educated ones, and British, the Celtic language of the island, the ancestor of Welsh. They read Latin books. They were Christians, many of them, by the end of the fourth century, with churches, and bishops, and priests.

[pause]

But in the years when Patricius was a boy, the Roman world was falling apart. Barbarians were attacking the empire on every side: Goths, and Vandals, and Huns, in Europe, and in Britain, Picts, from the north, and Saxons, from across the North Sea, and Scots, which was the Roman name for the Irish, from the west.

[pause]

In the year 410, about four years after Patricius was captured, the Roman emperor wrote to the cities of Britain, telling them that they must defend themselves, because Rome could no longer send soldiers to help them. The Roman army left. And slowly, over the following decades, Roman Britain crumbled: the towns emptied, the villas were abandoned, the coins stopped, the roads fell into ruin.

[long-pause]

By the time Patrick wrote his Confession, as an old man, the Britain of his childhood was gone. The Saxons, and the Angles, from Germany and Denmark, were settling in the east of the island, and would become the English. The British were retreating to the west, to Wales, and Cornwall, and Cumbria, and Brittany, across the sea.

[pause]

It was the beginning of the time that later people would call the Dark Ages. And in those dark years, the light of learning, and of Latin books, and of the Christian faith, which Patrick carried back to Ireland, would be kept burning, in the monasteries of Ireland, and later carried back again, by Irish monks, to Britain, and Europe.

[pause]

{img:abandoned-roman-villa-overgrown-ivy-mosaic-grass-evening}
Archaeologists have found many Roman villas in Britain, with their beautiful mosaic floors, of gods, and dolphins, and the seasons, and Christian symbols, which tell us how families like Patricius's lived, in the last golden years before the end.

[long-pause]

{img:young-man-asleep-villa-room-dream-figure-letters-moonlight}
Back home in Britain, Patricius had a dream.

[pause]

He writes: I saw, in a vision of the night, a man coming, as it were from Ireland, whose name was Victoricus, with countless letters. And he gave me one of them, and I read the beginning of the letter, which said: The voice of the Irish. And as I read, I seemed to hear the voices of the people who were beside the wood of Foclut, which is near the western sea, and they cried out, as with one voice: we ask you, holy youth, to come and walk among us once more.

[long-pause]

He writes that he was deeply moved, and could not read any more, and woke up.

[pause]

He decided that God wanted him to go back to Ireland: not as a slave, but as a missionary, to teach the Irish about Christianity.

[pause]

But first, he had to study, and become a priest. Tradition says that he went to Gaul, to France, and studied in the monasteries there, perhaps at Auxerre, with a famous bishop, Germanus, or on the island of Lérins, off the south coast of France. He was there for many years.

[long-pause]

There were already some Christians in Ireland, at that time: traders, and slaves from Britain, and some Irish people who had become Christians, through contact with Britain and Gaul. In the year 431, the Pope, Celestine, sent a bishop, called Palladius, to the Christians in Ireland. But Palladius, it seems, did not stay long, or did not succeed.

[pause]

And in the year 432, according to tradition, Patricius was made a bishop, and sent to Ireland, to take Palladius's place.

[pause]

{img:ship-arriving-irish-coast-strangford-lough-bishop-companions}
He was about forty-two. Older, and wiser, and stronger, than the frightened boy who had escaped from the mountain, twenty years before. And he came back, with a few companions, by ship, across the sea, to the island where he had been a slave.

[long-pause]

Tradition says that he landed in the north east of Ireland, at Strangford Lough, near the little town now called Saul, in what is now County Down. And that the first person he converted was a local chieftain, called Dichu, who gave him a barn, in which to hold services. The name Saul comes from the Irish word sabhall, which means barn.

[pause]

It was the beginning of thirty years of work, travelling all over Ireland, on foot, preaching, and baptizing, and founding churches.

[pause]

{img:barn-church-saul-county-down-rolling-hills-lough-morning}
But before we follow him, let us look at the Ireland he came back to: its people, and their homes, and their kings, and their poets, and their gods, and their way of life.

[long-pause]

{img:ringfort-interior-round-houses-wattle-thatch-cattle-pen-morning}
Most Irish families, in the fifth century, live in ringforts.

[pause]

A ringfort is a round farmstead, about thirty or forty metres across, surrounded by a circular bank of earth, with a ditch outside it, and often a fence of wooden stakes, or a hedge, on top of the bank. There are tens of thousands of them, all over Ireland. Many can still be seen today, in the fields, as round, grassy rings, often with trees growing on them. The Irish call them raths, or lios.

[long-pause]

The bank is not for war. It is to keep the cattle in, at night, safe from wolves, and from cattle thieves, and to show everyone that this is the home of a free family.

[pause]

Inside the bank, there are one or two round houses, and a few smaller buildings, for storage, and for animals, and a yard.

[pause]

The round houses are built of wattle: stakes driven into the ground, in a circle, with thin branches of hazel, or willow, woven between them, like a basket. The walls are sometimes plastered with clay, or left as they are, and sometimes there are two walls of wattle, with moss, or straw, packed between them, to keep out the cold. The roof is conical, made of poles, and thatched with straw, or reeds, or heather, with no chimney, so the smoke from the fire seeps out through the thatch.

[long-pause]

Inside, the house is dim, and smoky, and warm, and smells of peat, and wood smoke, and food, and animals.

[pause]

In the middle, there is the hearth, a fire, of wood, or peat, which burns all day and all night, and is never allowed to go out. Around the walls, there are beds, little compartments of wattle, with straw, and rushes, and hides, and woollen blankets. There are wooden tubs, and buckets, and bowls, and pots, and a quern, a pair of stones, for grinding grain, and a loom, for weaving, and baskets, and leather bags, hanging from the roof.

[pause]

{img:inside-round-house-central-hearth-family-beds-wattle-walls-dim}
In wealthier houses, there are a few precious things: a bronze cauldron, hanging over the fire, on a chain, and a wooden chest, and brooches, and a fine cloak, and perhaps a board game.

[long-pause]

A larger family might have a few servants, and perhaps a slave or two, often captured in raids on Britain, as Patricius was.

[pause]

In the evening, the whole household gathers around the fire, to eat, and talk, and tell stories, and sing, and play games, while the cattle are safe in the yard, inside the bank, and the dogs guard the gate.

[pause]

{img:crannog-lake-dwelling-island-round-houses-causeway-mist}
Some families, especially the wealthier ones, live in crannógs: little artificial islands, built in lakes, of stones, and timber, and brushwood, and earth, with round houses on them, reached by a narrow causeway, or by boat. They are safer from raiders. And there, on a misty morning, the smoke rises from the thatch, over the still water, and the ducks swim around the island, and the fisherman sets out in his little boat.

[long-pause]

{img:irish-bog-brown-heather-pools-sky-reflections-wide-flat-evening}
In the middle of Ireland, and in the west, there are great bogs: wide, flat, brown lands, of peat, and heather, and moss, and little pools of dark water, stretching away to the horizon, under the enormous sky.

[pause]

A bog is made of plants, mostly sphagnum moss, which grow, and die, and do not rot, because the ground is so wet, and so acid, and so cold. Over thousands of years, the dead plants pile up, and are pressed down, into a thick, soft, dark brown layer, called peat, or turf, many metres deep.

[long-pause]

For the Irish, the bog is precious. Because peat, when it is cut, and dried, burns slowly, and warmly, with a sweet, smoky smell, and is the main fuel of the Irish countryside, in a land where wood is becoming scarce. The smell of turf smoke, drifting from the chimneys, on a cold evening, is still, for many Irish people, the smell of home.

[pause]

The bog also preserves things. Anything that falls into the bog, or is buried in it, is kept, almost perfectly, for thousands of years, because there is no air, and the water is acid. Wood, and leather, and cloth, and butter, and even bodies.

[pause]

Archaeologists have found, in the Irish bogs, wooden roads, called toghers, made of planks, and logs, laid across the bog, thousands of years ago, so that people could cross. The longest, at Corlea, in County Longford, is more than two thousand years old, and part of it can still be seen today, preserved in a special building.

[long-pause]

They have found gold ornaments, and bronze cauldrons, and wooden bowls, and shoes, and clothes, and the lumps of bog butter, which we heard about, and wooden figures of the old gods, and boats.

[pause]

For the people of Patrick's time, the bogs were mysterious places: beautiful, and lonely, and a little dangerous, because you could sink into the soft places, and be lost. They believed that the bogs were the edges of the Otherworld, and they sometimes left offerings there, to the gods: gold, and weapons, and butter.

[pause]

{img:skylark-over-bog-cotton-grass-white-tufts-summer-breeze}
In the summer, the bogs are full of life: the white tufts of bog cotton, waving in the wind, and purple heather, and little orchids, and the insect-eating sundew plants, and dragonflies, and frogs, and curlews, calling, with their long, sad, bubbling cry, and skylarks, singing, high in the sky, all day long.

[long-pause]

{img:cattle-herd-green-pasture-herder-boy-stick-irish-hills}
In early Ireland, wealth is measured in cattle.

[pause]

There is no money, no coins. Instead, people count their wealth, and pay their debts, and their fines, and their rents, and the price of a wife, in cows. A milch cow, a cow that gives milk, is the basic unit of value. A female slave, a cumal, is worth three cows. A sword might be worth a cow, or two.

[long-pause]

Cattle are everything. They give milk, and butter, and cheese, all summer long, which is the main food of the Irish. They give meat, and leather, and horn, and bone. They pull the ploughs. And a man's rank, and his place in society, and his honour, depend on how many cattle he has.

[pause]

The greatest of all the old Irish stories, the Táin Bó Cúailnge, the Cattle Raid of Cooley, is about a war, between the two great provinces of Ulster and Connacht, over a single famous bull, the Brown Bull of Cooley. It is the Irish Iliad.

[pause]

{img:great-brown-bull-legend-misty-hill-dramatic-calm-sky}
In summer, the cattle are taken up to the hills, and the mountains, to graze on the summer pastures, while the grass grows in the fields below for hay. The young people of the family go with them, and live in little huts, on the mountain, and milk the cows, and make butter, and cheese, and sing, and dance, in the long summer evenings. This custom is called booleying, and it went on in Ireland until less than two hundred years ago.

[long-pause]

The Irish make butter in great quantities, and store it in wooden barrels, or in skin bags, and sometimes bury it in the bogs, to keep it cool, and fresh, for months. Archaeologists still find these lumps of bog butter, hundreds or thousands of years old, sometimes in their wooden containers, in the peat. Some of them weigh many kilograms. And sometimes, they are still, it is said, quite edible, though nobody recommends it.

[pause]

Besides milk and butter and cheese, the Irish eat oats, and barley, and wheat, made into porridge, and bread, and flat cakes, baked on hot stones by the fire. They eat meat, beef, and pork, and mutton, especially in winter, and venison, and wild boar, from the forests, and fish, salmon, and trout, and eels, from the rivers, and shellfish, from the shore. They eat wild garlic, and watercress, and nettles, and seaweed, and berries, and hazelnuts, and apples, and honey.

[pause]

{img:woman-churning-butter-wooden-churn-ringfort-yard-morning}
And they drink milk, and buttermilk, and ale, made from barley, and mead, made from honey, at feasts.

[long-pause]

Cattle raiding is the favourite sport of the young Irish warriors: going into a neighbouring kingdom, at night, and driving off its cattle, and bringing them home, in triumph. It is dangerous, and causes many quarrels, and feuds, between the kingdoms. But it is also a kind of game, with rules, and the old stories are full of it.

[pause]

Later, when Patricius writes about his own times, he complains that the Irish kings and warriors are always fighting, and raiding, and that he had to pay them, and give them gifts, to be allowed to travel safely through their lands.

[long-pause]

{img:currach-leather-boat-rowers-atlantic-waves-west-coast-ireland}
The Irish are a people of the sea.

[pause]

All around the coast of Ireland, there are fishermen, and sailors, and traders, and raiders, who go out on the sea in their boats. And the most famous Irish boat is the currach.

[long-pause]

A currach is made of a light wooden frame, of thin, bent laths of ash, or willow, like a basket, covered with the hides of cattle, sewn together, and stretched tight, and rubbed with butter, or tallow, or tar, to make them waterproof. It is very light, and can be carried by a few men, over the land. It floats on top of the waves, like a seabird, and is very good in rough seas, if the rowers are skilful.

[pause]

Small currachs carry two or three men, for fishing. Large ones, for the sea, carry twenty or thirty men, with oars, and a sail, made of leather, or cloth, and can cross to Britain, and Scotland, and even further.

[pause]

{img:fishermen-carrying-upturned-currach-beach-evening-seaweed}
It was in currachs like these that the Irish raiders crossed the sea, to raid the coasts of Britain, and carried away the young Patricius, and many others.

[long-pause]

And it was in currachs like these that the Irish monks, in the following centuries, set out on the ocean, to find lonely islands, where they could live in peace, and pray, and to bring Christianity to other lands.

[pause]

The most famous story is the Voyage of Saint Brendan, an Irish monk of the sixth century, who, the story says, sailed out into the Atlantic Ocean, in a great currach, covered with ox hides, with seventeen monks, for seven years, looking for the Promised Land of the Saints, and visited many wonderful islands: an island of sheep, and an island of birds, which sang hymns, and a great crystal pillar, rising out of the sea, which may have been an iceberg, and an island which turned out to be a great whale, on whose back they lit a fire, to cook their dinner, and which swam away, when it felt the heat.

[pause]

{img:monks-in-leather-boat-iceberg-ocean-calm-mist-wonder}
In 1976, a British explorer, Tim Severin, built a copy of a large currach, of ox hides, and sailed it, with a small crew, from Ireland, across the Atlantic, by way of the Faroe Islands, and Iceland, and Greenland, to Newfoundland, to show that the Irish monks could have reached America, long before Columbus.

[long-pause]

Currachs are still made, and rowed, today, on the west coast of Ireland, especially on the Aran Islands, and in Connemara, and Kerry, though now they are covered with tarred canvas, instead of hides. And in the summer, there are currach races, in the bays, with the rowers pulling hard, and the crowds cheering, from the shore.

[long-pause]

{img:hill-of-tara-green-mounds-earthworks-standing-stone-sky}
Ireland, in the fifth century, is a land of kings.

[pause]

There are perhaps a hundred and fifty little kingdoms, each called a tuath, a people, ruled by its own king, a rí. A tuath is small: perhaps a few thousand people, living in a few valleys, or around a lake, or along a river. Everyone knows everyone else, and everyone knows the king.

[long-pause]

Above the kings of the tuatha, there are greater kings, who rule over several kingdoms, and receive tribute, and hostages, from the lesser kings, to make sure that they stay loyal. And above them, there are the kings of the provinces: Ulster, in the north, and Connacht, in the west, and Leinster, in the east, and Munster, in the south.

[pause]

And in the middle, in the green plain of Meath, there is Tara, the seat of the High King of Ireland.

[pause]

Tara is a long, low hill, with wide views over the green plains of Meath, in every direction. On it, there are ancient mounds, and earthworks, and circles of banks and ditches, some of them already thousands of years old, in the time of Patrick. There is an old passage tomb, called the Mound of the Hostages. And there is a standing stone, the Lia Fáil, the Stone of Destiny, which, the legend says, would cry out, with a roar, when the rightful king of Ireland touched it.

[long-pause]

In the fifth century, the most powerful king in the north of Ireland is Lóegaire, the son of Niall of the Nine Hostages, a famous warrior king, who, the stories say, raided Britain, and Gaul, and took hostages from all the provinces of Ireland, and from Britain, and Scotland. Some even say that it was Niall's raiders who captured the young Patricius.

[pause]

Lóegaire is the king of Tara, at the time of Patrick's mission, according to tradition.

[pause]

{img:royal-feast-hall-tara-long-house-fires-warriors-poets-evening}
Being a king, in early Ireland, is a serious thing. A king must be perfect: without any injury, or blemish, on his body. He must be generous, and just, and brave, and wise. If he is unjust, the old law says, the land itself will suffer: the crops will fail, the cows will give no milk, the rivers will have no fish, and there will be storms, and plagues.

[long-pause]

A king is married, in a way, to the land, and to its goddess. At Tara, in the old days, there was a great feast, called the feast of Tara, at which the new king was married to the goddess of sovereignty, the goddess of the land, and drank from her cup.

[pause]

But a king is not above the law. He must obey the law, like everyone else. He has a great house, a hall, where he feasts, and receives guests, and holds court, and judges disputes, with the help of the lawyers. He leads his people in war, and in the great assemblies, called the óenach, where the people of the kingdom gather, once a year, for horse races, and markets, and games, and the making of laws, and the settling of disputes, and feasting, and music.

[pause]

{img:oenach-fair-hilltop-horse-races-crowds-stalls-summer-day}
And above all, he is expected to be generous: to give feasts, and gifts, and to welcome every guest, and never to refuse hospitality to anyone who comes to his door.

[long-pause]

{img:royal-hall-feast-long-tables-fires-cauldrons-harper-warriors}
Let us go to a feast, in the hall of an Irish king, on a winter evening, in the fifth century.

[pause]

The hall is a great round, or long, house, of timber, and wattle, with a high thatched roof, and a great fire burning in the middle, and torches, and candles, of tallow, and rushlights. The walls are hung with shields, and spears, and the heads of deer, with their antlers.

[long-pause]

Everyone has a place, according to their rank, as the law sets out: the king, in the middle, with his queen, and the chief poet, and the judge, and the druid, near him. Then the nobles, and the warriors, and the guests. And then the lesser people, further away.

[pause]

And everyone gets a portion of meat, according to their rank. The best portion of all, the hero's portion, the champion's portion, goes to the bravest warrior in the hall. In the old stories, there are great quarrels, and even fights, over who deserves the champion's portion.

[pause]

{img:great-bronze-cauldron-meat-boiling-fire-hall-servants-ladles}
The meat is boiled in huge bronze cauldrons, hanging over the fire: beef, and pork, and mutton, and venison. A pig, roasted whole. Salmon, from the river. Bread, and butter, and cheese, and honey, and apples, and hazelnuts. And to drink, ale, and mead, made from honey, in great vats, served in cups of wood, and horn, and, for the king, of silver.

[long-pause]

While they eat, and drink, the poets recite, and the harpers play.

[pause]

The harp is the great instrument of Ireland: a small harp, held in the lap, with strings of bronze wire, plucked with the fingernails, which makes a bright, ringing, shimmering sound. The old stories say that there are three kinds of music that a master harper must be able to play: the music of laughter, which makes everyone laugh, the music of sorrow, which makes everyone weep, and the music of sleep, which makes everyone fall asleep.

[pause]

{img:harper-playing-small-harp-bronze-strings-firelight-faces}
And then the storyteller tells a story: a long story, of the heroes of old, which takes hours to tell, and the whole hall listens, in silence, while the fire crackles, and the rain falls on the thatch outside, and the dogs lie by the fire.

[long-pause]

The feast goes on late into the night. And then, the guests lie down to sleep, on the benches, and the floor, on rushes, and furs, by the fire, in the hall, and the king and his family go to their own house, and the hall is quiet, except for the sleeping guests, and the crackling of the dying fire, and the music of sleep, still echoing, perhaps, in their dreams.

[long-pause]

{img:druid-white-robe-oak-grove-staff-mist-dawn-ancient}
Before Patrick came, the religion of Ireland was the old religion, of many gods, and spirits, and sacred places.

[pause]

The priests of the old religion are the druids. They are the wise men, and sometimes women, of Ireland: priests, and teachers, and judges, and advisers to the kings, and prophets, and magicians, who know the old lore, and the gods, and the stars, and the right times for things, and can read the future, in the flight of birds, and the clouds, and the stars.

[long-pause]

The druids train for many years, learning everything by heart, because they do not write their knowledge down. They are greatly respected, and feared, and they have great power. A king must listen to his druid. In the old stories, the druid speaks before the king, at the feast.

[pause]

In the stories of Patrick, the druids are his great opponents, who try to stop him, with magic, and argument, and threats. And in the end, they lose their power, as Christianity spreads, over the following centuries.

[pause]

{img:filid-poet-reciting-harp-feast-hall-firelight-listeners-rapt}
But there is another learned class in Ireland, which does not disappear: the filid, the poets.

[long-pause]

The poets of early Ireland are not just people who write poems. They are the historians, and the genealogists, and the keepers of the stories, and the praise singers, and the satirists, of the kingdom. They know the history of every family, and the deeds of every king, and the old stories of the gods and heroes, by heart, thousands of lines of them.

[pause]

To become a master poet, an ollamh, takes twelve years of training, in schools, where the students lie in dark rooms, with a stone on their bellies, it is said, composing poems in their heads, in the dark, and then come out into the light, and recite them.

[pause]

{img:dark-room-student-poets-lying-composing-candle-stone-quiet}
The poets are honoured, and paid, by the kings, and given land, and cattle, and treated almost like kings themselves. They travel from court to court, with their followers, and are welcomed everywhere.

[pause]

And they are feared. Because a poet can make a satire: a mocking poem, which can shame a king, or a lord, so badly, if he is mean, or unjust, or ungenerous, that, the people believe, it can raise blisters on his face, or even kill him. No king wants to be satirized.

[long-pause]

When Christianity came to Ireland, the poets did not disappear. Many of them became Christians, and kept their learning, and their stories, and later, the monks wrote them down, so that the old stories of Ireland, of the gods, and the heroes, Cú Chulainn, and Finn mac Cumhaill, and Deirdre of the Sorrows, and the Children of Lir, were saved, and can still be read today.

[pause]

And there are the harpers, and the musicians, who play at the feasts of the kings, and the storytellers, the seanchaí, who tell stories by the fire, in the houses of ordinary people, on winter evenings. The seanchaí went on telling stories, in the Irish countryside, until the twentieth century.

[long-pause]

{img:four-white-swans-misty-lake-irish-hills-dawn-legend}
One of the most beautiful of the old Irish stories, which the poets told by the fire, and which every Irish child still knows, is the story of the Children of Lir.

[pause]

Lir was a king of the old gods, the Tuatha Dé Danann, who lived in Ireland long ago. He had four children: a daughter, Fionnuala, and three sons, Aodh, and Fiachra, and Conn. Their mother died, and Lir married her sister, Aoife.

[long-pause]

At first, Aoife loved the children. But then she grew jealous of them, because Lir loved them so much. And one day, she took them to a lake, called Lough Derravaragh, and told them to bathe in the water. And when they were in the water, she struck them with a magic wand, and turned them into four white swans.

[pause]

She said that they would remain swans for nine hundred years: three hundred years on Lough Derravaragh, three hundred years on the cold, stormy sea of Moyle, between Ireland and Scotland, and three hundred years on the wild Atlantic Ocean, off the west coast, at Inis Glora. And that the spell would only be broken when they heard the sound of a Christian bell.

[pause]

But she let them keep their human voices, and their minds, and the gift of music. So the swans could speak, and sing.

[long-pause]

When Lir found out, he was heartbroken, and he banished Aoife, and turned her into a demon of the air. And he came to the lake, every day, with his people, to talk to his children, and listen to their singing, which was the most beautiful music ever heard in Ireland, so beautiful that all who heard it forgot their troubles, and slept peacefully.

[pause]

But after three hundred years, the swans had to leave the lake, and fly to the cold sea of Moyle, where they suffered terribly, from the storms, and the cold, and the ice, and were often separated, and Fionnuala gathered her brothers under her wings, to keep them warm.

[pause]

{img:white-swan-sheltering-three-smaller-under-wings-ice-sea-night}
And then, for three hundred years more, they lived on the Atlantic, in the far west. And when, at last, the nine hundred years were over, they flew home, to their father's palace. But they found it in ruins, overgrown with grass, and nettles, and everyone they had known was long gone.

[long-pause]

And then, one morning, they heard the sound of a bell: a little bell, of a Christian monk, called Mo Chaomhóg, who had come to Ireland, and built a little church, by the lake. They came to him, and he looked after them, and they listened to his prayers.

[pause]

And then the spell was broken. The swans changed back into human form: but they were now very old, nine hundred years old, withered and frail. They asked the monk to baptize them, and he did. And then they passed away, peacefully, and were buried together, in one grave, as Fionnuala had asked, with Conn on her right, and Fiachra on her left, and Aodh in front of her, as she had sheltered them under her wings.

[pause]

{img:small-stone-church-lakeside-monk-bell-swans-flying-dawn}
It is said that the monk saw four white swans flying up into the sky, toward heaven. And to this day, in Ireland, it is considered very bad luck to harm a swan.

[long-pause]

{img:brehon-judge-seated-outdoors-hill-people-dispute-listening}
Ireland, in the fifth century, has no police, no prisons, and no courts, as we know them. But it has a very detailed and careful system of law: the old Irish law, called the Brehon law, after the brehons, the judges, who know it, and interpret it.

[pause]

The laws were passed down by memory, for many generations, and later, in the seventh and eighth centuries, were written down, in long books, which still exist. They are among the oldest written laws in Europe.

[long-pause]

The Brehon law is not about punishment, but about compensation. If someone harms another person, or their property, they must pay a fine, to the victim, or the victim's family, according to the harm done, and the rank of the person harmed. Every person has an honour price, which depends on their rank: a king's is high, a farmer's lower, a slave's very low. If you insult someone, or injure them, you must pay part of their honour price.

[pause]

The laws are wonderfully detailed. There are laws about bees, and about who owns the honey, if a swarm of bees settles in a neighbour's tree. Laws about cats, and dogs, and what happens if a dog bites someone. Laws about trees, and how much the fine is, for cutting down a neighbour's oak, or apple tree, or hazel. Laws about mills, and fences, and boundaries, and water, and cattle trespassing in a neighbour's field.

[pause]

{img:bees-hive-straw-skep-neighbour-tree-swarm-orchard-sunny}
There are laws about the care of the sick. If you injure someone, you must pay for their care, while they are recovering: their food, and their doctor, and someone to do their work for them. And the laws say exactly what food a sick person must be given, depending on their rank, and that they must be kept in a house with four doors, so that they can see out, and with clean water running nearby, and no noise, no barking dogs, and no fighting, and no shouting, near the sick person.

[long-pause]

Women, under the Brehon law, have more rights than in many other countries of the time. A woman can own property, and keep her own property after marriage. She can divorce her husband, for many reasons: if he is cruel to her, or neglects her, or tells everyone her secrets, or becomes too fat to be a good husband, or does not provide for her. And she can take her property with her.

[pause]

There are many kinds of marriage, under the old laws, and the rights of the husband and wife depend on how much property each of them brought to the marriage.

[pause]

{img:woman-owner-cattle-ringfort-managing-farm-servants-morning}
The judges, the brehons, are learned men, and sometimes women, trained for many years. Anyone can come to them, with a dispute, and they listen to both sides, and decide, according to the law. And their decisions are respected, because everyone knows that, if they are not, the whole kingdom will fall into disorder.

[long-pause]

The Brehon law lasted in Ireland for more than a thousand years, until the seventeenth century, when the English finally replaced it with English law.

[long-pause]

{img:irish-noble-cloak-brat-colourful-penannular-brooch-tunic-leine}
What do the Irish wear, in the fifth century?

[pause]

The main garments are the léine, a long tunic, of linen, or wool, reaching to the knees, or the ankles, with long sleeves, often dyed yellow, or saffron-coloured, or white, and belted at the waist. And over it, the brat: a large, rectangular cloak, of wool, wrapped around the shoulders, and fastened at the breast with a brooch.

[long-pause]

The cloak is the most important garment. It tells everyone who you are. The law says what colours people of different ranks may wear: a king may wear seven colours, a poet six, a lord four, and so on, down to the poor, who wear only one colour, or plain undyed wool.

[pause]

The cloaks are woven from the wool of the family's own sheep, and dyed with plants, and sometimes have fringes, and borders, woven in, of different colours. The rich wear cloaks of purple, and crimson, and blue, and green, with borders of gold thread.

[pause]

{img:woman-dyeing-wool-vats-plants-colours-yellow-blue-red-yard}
The brooch which fastens the cloak is a work of art. It is usually a penannular brooch: a ring, with a small gap in it, and a long pin, which passes through the cloak, and through the ring, and is turned to lock it in place. For ordinary people, the brooches are of bronze, or iron. For the rich, they are of silver, or gold, decorated with spirals, and animals, and enamel, in red, and yellow, and later, with gold filigree, and amber, and glass.

[long-pause]

In the following centuries, the Irish craftsmen will make some of the most beautiful brooches in the world. The most famous is the Tara Brooch, made around the year 700, of silver, and gold, with tiny, intricate patterns, of birds, and animals, and spirals, and knots, so fine that it is hard to believe it was made by hand. It is now in the National Museum, in Dublin.

[pause]

The men wear their hair long, and often have beards, or long moustaches. The women wear their hair long, in braids, or plaits, sometimes with golden balls, or ornaments, at the ends.

[pause]

{img:ornate-silver-gold-brooch-filigree-spirals-museum-close-light}
On their feet, they wear shoes of leather, made in one piece, and laced, or they go barefoot, in summer, which is common, even for nobles.

[pause]

And everyone, rich and poor, wears a cloak, against the rain, and the wind, and sleeps in it, at night, wrapped up, by the fire. The cloak of an Irishman was famous, all over Europe, for centuries: a great, thick, warm, shaggy woollen cloak, which kept out the Irish rain.

[long-pause]

{img:ogham-stone-standing-field-notches-edge-lichen-soft-light}
Before Patrick and the monks brought books, and the Latin alphabet, to Ireland, the Irish had only one kind of writing: ogham.

[pause]

Ogham is a strange and beautiful alphabet, of straight lines and notches, cut along the edge of a stone, or a stick of wood. A long line, the edge of the stone, is the base. And the letters are groups of one to five short strokes, cut across the line, or to the left of it, or to the right of it, or notches on the edge itself. It looks a little like the marks on a tally stick, or a barcode.

[long-pause]

Each letter has a name, often the name of a tree: beith, the birch, for B. Luis, the rowan, for L. Fearn, the alder, for F. Saille, the willow, for S. Nuin, the ash, for N. Duir, the oak, for D. And so on. So, in a way, it is an alphabet of trees.

[pause]

Nobody knows exactly when it was invented, or by whom. Probably in the third or fourth century after Christ, by someone in Ireland who knew the Latin alphabet, and made a new kind of writing, which suited the Irish language, and was easy to carve.

[pause]

{img:ogham-letters-tree-names-birch-rowan-oak-forest-calm}
The legend says that it was invented by Ogma, the god of eloquence, and learning, who was very strong, and very wise.

[long-pause]

Most of the ogham inscriptions that survive are on standing stones: tall, rough, upright stones, in the fields, or by the roads, or in churchyards. There are about four hundred of them in Ireland, and others in Wales, and Scotland, and the Isle of Man, and Cornwall, where Irish settlers went.

[pause]

They usually have only a few words: a name, and the name of a father, or an ancestor. Like this: of Cunacena, son of Muco. Or: of Dalagnos, son of Dalos. They are probably memorials to people who had died, or boundary markers, to show who owned the land.

[pause]

{img:roadside-ogham-stone-green-hedges-country-lane-evening}
The stories also say that the druids and the poets used ogham, carved on sticks of yew, or hazel, or aspen, for magic, and messages, and to record genealogies. In the old story of the Cattle Raid of Cooley, the hero Cú Chulainn cuts an ogham message on a hoop of oak, and leaves it on a standing stone, as a challenge to the invading army.

[long-pause]

When Christianity came, with Patrick, and the monks, they brought the Latin alphabet, and books, and parchment, and ink. And within a few generations, the Irish monks became some of the finest scribes in Europe, writing in Latin, and in Irish, with the Latin alphabet, in beautiful handwriting, and making the most beautiful books in the world.

[pause]

But ogham was not forgotten. The scholars of the Middle Ages wrote about it, and listed its letters, and its names. And today, you can still see the old ogham stones, standing in the green fields of Ireland, with the rain running down their edges, and the moss and lichen growing on their notches, keeping the names of people who lived, and died, sixteen hundred years ago.

[long-pause]

{img:samhain-bonfire-hilltop-night-people-gathered-autumn-stars}
The old Irish year has four great festivals, which mark the turning of the seasons.

[pause]

The year begins with Samhain, at the beginning of November, when the summer ends, and the cattle are brought down from the summer pastures, and gathered in for the winter, and some of them are killed, for meat, because there is not enough food to keep them all through the winter.

[long-pause]

Samhain is the most mysterious of the festivals. The Irish believe that on the night of Samhain, the boundary between this world and the Otherworld, the world of the gods, and the fairies, and the dead, is very thin, and that the spirits can come out, and wander among the living, and that people can go into the Otherworld. Great fires are lit, on the hills, and the people feast, and tell stories, and are careful not to go out alone in the dark.

[pause]

Many centuries later, the Church will place the feast of All Saints, and All Souls, at the same time. And Samhain will become Halloween, with its ghosts, and its spirits, and its bonfires, and its turnip lanterns, which the Irish will take with them to America.

[pause]

{img:imbolc-february-lambs-snowdrops-farm-woman-milking-ewe-dawn}
Then, at the beginning of February, there is Imbolc, the festival of the first stirrings of spring, when the ewes begin to give milk, and the first lambs are born, and the days begin to grow longer. It is the festival of the goddess Brigid, the goddess of poetry, and healing, and smithcraft, and fire, and later, of Saint Brigid, the great woman saint of Ireland.

[long-pause]

At the beginning of May, there is Bealtaine, the festival of the beginning of summer. Great fires are lit, on the hills, and the cattle are driven between two fires, to protect them from disease, before they are taken up to the summer pastures, on the mountains. People decorate their doors with yellow May flowers, and wash their faces in the May morning dew, for beauty, and good luck.

[pause]

The legend says that on the night of Bealtaine, no fire could be lit anywhere in Ireland, until the king lit the great fire at Tara. That will be important, in the story of Patrick.

[pause]

{img:bealtaine-cattle-driven-between-two-fires-hillside-may-evening}
And at the beginning of August, there is Lughnasa, the festival of the god Lugh, the bright god of all the skills, at the beginning of the harvest. People climb the hills, and the mountains, together, and pick the first wild bilberries, and hold fairs, and horse races, and games, and matchmaking, and feasting, and dancing.

[long-pause]

Many of these customs survived, in the Irish countryside, for more than a thousand years, long after the old gods had been forgotten, and were still kept, in some places, in the twentieth century: the bonfires on the hills, the climbing of the mountains in August, the May flowers on the doors.

[pause]

And the climbing of the mountain, at Lughnasa, became the great pilgrimage to Croagh Patrick, the holy mountain of Saint Patrick, in the west of Ireland, which thousands of people still climb, every year, on the last Sunday of July.

[long-pause]

{img:dawn-ringfort-woman-lighting-fire-hearth-smoke-thatch-mist}
Let us spend an ordinary day in a ringfort, in Ireland, in the spring of the year 432.

[pause]

The family is a free farming family, a bóaire, a cow lord, as the law calls him, with about twenty cows, and some sheep, and pigs, and a horse, and fields of oats and barley, and a share in a plough, and a kiln for drying grain, and a mill, shared with the neighbours.

[long-pause]

The day begins at dawn, with the grey light coming through the doorway, and the birds singing in the hedges, and the cattle lowing in the yard, waiting to be milked.

[pause]

The mother is up first, as always. She blows the embers of the fire back into flame, and puts on more peat, and wood. The girls go out to milk the cows, in wooden pails, sitting on little stools, in the yard, singing to the cows, to keep them calm.

[pause]

Breakfast is simple: porridge, of oats, cooked in the cauldron over the fire, with milk, or butter, and perhaps a little honey. And fresh milk to drink.

[long-pause]

Then the work of the day begins.

[pause]

The men and boys drive the cattle out to the pasture, and look after them, and plough, with oxen, the fields for the spring sowing, and mend the fences, and the banks, and cut wood, and dig peat, from the bog, for the fire. The peat is cut in long slices, with a special spade, and laid out on the bog, to dry, in the wind and the sun.

[pause]

{img:men-cutting-turf-peat-bog-slean-spade-drying-stacks-spring}
The women and girls make butter, and cheese, and grind the oats and barley, on the quern, and bake bread, and spin wool, and weave cloth, on the loom, and dye it, with plants: yellow, from weld, and blue, from woad, and red, from madder, and brown, and green. They look after the hens, and the geese, and the calves, and the little children.

[long-pause]

At midday, they eat bread, and butter, and cheese, and drink buttermilk.

[pause]

In the afternoon, the work goes on. And a traveller comes to the gate of the ringfort: a man on foot, with a staff, and a bag, and a cloak. He is welcomed in, as all travellers are, and given food, and drink, and a place by the fire, for the night. Because hospitality is sacred, in Ireland, and the law says that a free farmer must welcome guests, and feed them, and give them a bed.

[pause]

{img:traveller-welcomed-ringfort-gate-dog-family-greeting-evening}
In the evening, the cattle are brought back into the yard, inside the bank, for the night, safe from the wolves. The family gathers by the fire, with the traveller, and eats the main meal of the day: meat, perhaps, or fish, and bread, and vegetables, and milk, and ale. And the traveller tells them the news, from the other side of the kingdom: who has died, and who has married, and which king has raided which, and that a strange bishop, from Britain, has come to Ireland, and is going around, telling the people about a new god.

[long-pause]

And then, the grandmother, or the father, tells a story, by the fire, an old story, about the heroes of long ago, or the fairies, who live in the green mounds, the sídhe, under the hills, and the children listen, wide-eyed, until they fall asleep.

[pause]

The fire is banked down, with ashes, for the night, so that it will not go out. And the family goes to sleep, in their beds around the walls, with the dogs by the door, and the cattle breathing in the yard, and the rain whispering on the thatch.

[long-pause]

{img:fairy-mound-green-hill-sidhe-mist-moonlight-hawthorn}
In the old Irish stories, there is another world, beside this one: the Otherworld, the land of the sídhe, the fairy people.

[pause]

The sídhe live inside the hills, and the ancient mounds, and the old ringforts, in beautiful halls, of gold, and crystal, where it is always summer, and there is always music, and feasting, and nobody grows old, or sick, or dies.

[long-pause]

The old stories say that the sídhe were once the gods of Ireland, the Tuatha Dé Danann, the people of the goddess Danu, a race of gods, and magicians, who ruled Ireland, long ago, before the ancestors of the Irish came, from Spain, across the sea. And when the Irish defeated them, the Tuatha Dé Danann went into the hills, and the mounds, and became the people of the hills, the fairies, and still live there, invisible, beside us.

[pause]

Some of the greatest of these mounds are the great passage tombs of the Boyne valley: Newgrange, and Knowth, and Dowth, built by the first farmers of Ireland, five thousand years ago, older than the pyramids, which, the stories say, were the homes of the gods: Newgrange was the house of the god Aengus, the god of love, and youth, and beauty.

[pause]

{img:newgrange-white-quartz-front-stone-mound-boyne-valley-winter-sunrise}
And at Newgrange, on the shortest day of the year, the winter solstice, in December, at dawn, the rising sun shines through a little opening, above the entrance, called the roof box, and sends a beam of golden light, along the long, dark passage, for nineteen metres, into the very heart of the tomb, and lights up the chamber, for seventeen minutes, and then fades away. It has done so, every year, for five thousand years.

[long-pause]

The fairies can be dangerous, the old people say, if you are not careful. They can steal children, and leave a fairy child, a changeling, in their place. They can lead travellers astray, at night, with lights in the bog. They can take people into their mounds, to dance, for what seems like one night, but when the person comes out, a hundred years have passed.

[pause]

But they can also be kind, and helpful, to those who respect them. People leave a little milk, or butter, or food, for them, on the doorstep, at night. And they never cut down a lone hawthorn tree, in a field, because it belongs to the fairies.

[pause]

{img:lone-hawthorn-tree-field-white-blossom-may-stone-wall}
On the night of Samhain, and the eve of May, the fairies come out of their hills, and ride through the countryside, in great processions, with music, and lights. And sometimes, on those nights, people in the old days would hear the music of the fairies, coming from inside the hills: the most beautiful music in the world.

[long-pause]

{img:fosterage-children-arriving-new-household-ringfort-welcome}
One of the most remarkable customs of early Ireland is fosterage.

[pause]

The children of noble families, and of free farming families too, are not brought up by their own parents. At about the age of seven, they are sent away, to live with another family, often relatives, or friends, or the family of a lord, or a poet, or a king, who bring them up, and teach them, until they are fourteen, or seventeen.

[long-pause]

The foster parents are paid a fee, in cattle, by the real parents. And the law says exactly what the foster parents must give the child, and teach the child, according to the child's rank.

[pause]

The son of a farmer is taught to look after calves, and lambs, and pigs, and to dry corn in the kiln, and to comb wool, and to split wood. The daughter of a farmer is taught to use the quern, and the kneading trough, and the sieve. The son of a lord is taught to ride, and to swim, and to shoot, and to play the board game, fidchell, a kind of chess. The daughter of a lord is taught to sew, and to cut out cloth, and to embroider.

[pause]

{img:foster-father-teaching-boy-swimming-river-summer-calm}
The law even says what food the foster children must be given: porridge, made of oatmeal, with buttermilk, or water, for the children of ordinary people. With fresh milk, and butter, for the children of lords. And with fresh milk, and honey, for the children of kings.

[pause]

And what clothes they must wear: for the children of ordinary families, plain clothes, of brown, and grey, and black. For the children of lords, clothes of red, and grey, and brown. And for the children of kings, purple, and blue, with brooches of silver, and gold.

[long-pause]

The bond between a child and its foster parents, and its foster brothers and sisters, is very strong, often stronger than the bond with its own family. In the old stories, a hero's foster brother is his closest friend, and his foster father, and foster mother, are loved as deeply as his own parents.

[pause]

Fosterage binds the families of Ireland together, in a web of friendship, and loyalty, and obligation, so that the kingdoms are held together, not only by the kings, but by thousands of personal bonds, between families.

[pause]

{img:children-playing-hurling-sticks-ball-green-field-summer}
Irish children play many games. The most famous is hurling: a fast, wild game, played with curved wooden sticks, and a small ball, which the players hit, and carry, and throw, across a great field. The young hero Cú Chulainn, in the old stories, was the best hurler in Ireland, and could hit the ball so hard, and run so fast, that he could catch it again before it landed. Hurling is still played in Ireland today, the fastest field game in the world.

[long-pause]

Children also play fidchell, on a wooden board, with pegs, and swim in the rivers, and fish, and climb trees, and play with toy boats, and dolls, and wooden horses, and listen to stories, and learn the old songs.

[pause]

And when they are grown, at seventeen, they go home, to their own families, with gifts from their foster parents. And they remember, all their lives, the family who brought them up.

[long-pause]

{img:irish-wolfhound-tall-grey-shaggy-ringfort-gate-calm-guard}
The Irish, in the time of Patrick, share their island with many animals.

[pause]

The most famous Irish dog is the wolfhound: a huge, tall, shaggy, grey dog, the tallest dog in the world, strong enough to hunt wolves, and wild boar, and deer, and gentle, and loyal, and calm, at home. The kings and nobles keep them for hunting, and as guards, and they are greatly prized. Roman writers tell of Irish wolfhounds being sent as gifts to Rome, where the people were amazed at their size.

[long-pause]

In the old stories, the hero Cú Chulainn got his name, which means the hound of Culann, because, as a boy, he killed the fierce guard dog of the smith Culann, in self-defence, and then, feeling sorry, offered to take its place, and guard Culann's house himself, until a new dog could be raised.

[pause]

There are wolves in the forests, and wild boar, and red deer, the largest wild animals of Ireland, with their great antlers, and badgers, and foxes, and otters, and pine martens, and hares, and red squirrels. And in the rivers, there are salmon, and trout, and eels.

[pause]

{img:red-deer-stag-antlers-misty-oak-forest-autumn-morning}
The salmon is the most respected of all the fish. In the old story, there is a salmon, the Salmon of Knowledge, which lived in a pool, in the river Boyne, under a hazel tree, and ate the hazelnuts which fell into the water, and so gained all the knowledge in the world. An old poet, Finnegas, spent seven years trying to catch it, because whoever ate it would know everything.

[long-pause]

At last, he caught it, and gave it to his young servant, a boy called Fionn, to cook, and told him not to eat any of it. But while Fionn was cooking it, he burned his thumb on the hot skin of the fish, and put his thumb in his mouth, to cool it. And at that moment, all the knowledge of the salmon passed into him.

[pause]

And ever after, whenever Fionn wanted to know something, he only had to put his thumb in his mouth, and he would know it. He grew up to be Fionn mac Cumhaill, the great hero, the leader of the Fianna, the band of warriors who protected Ireland, and hunted, and fought, and feasted, in the old stories.

[pause]

{img:boy-cooking-salmon-fire-riverbank-hazel-tree-thumb-legend}
And on the coasts, there are seals, which the people of the west believe are sometimes enchanted people, the selkies, who can take off their sealskins, and come ashore, as beautiful men and women. And the seabirds, puffins, and gannets, and gulls, and guillemots, nesting on the cliffs, in their millions, as they still do today.

[long-pause]

{img:saint-patrick-walking-irish-road-staff-companions-hills-rain}
For about thirty years, from 432, Patrick travels all over Ireland, mostly in the north, and the west, and the middle of the island.

[pause]

He travels on foot, mostly, or sometimes in a chariot, with a small group of companions: priests, and helpers, and young men he is training, and some of the sons of kings, who travel with him. They walk along the old tracks, through the forests, and across the bogs, and over the mountains, from kingdom to kingdom, from ringfort to ringfort.

[long-pause]

Patrick writes, in his Confession, about how hard it was. He was often in danger. He was attacked, and robbed, and put in chains, and held prisoner, for fourteen days, once, and threatened with death, many times. He had to pay the kings, and their sons, to let him travel safely through their lands, and to protect him.

[pause]

He writes: every day, I expect to be killed, or betrayed, or taken back into slavery. But I fear none of these things, because of the promises of heaven.

[pause]

{img:bishop-baptizing-people-holy-well-crowd-spring-morning}
He preaches to the people, in Irish, which he learned as a slave, and baptizes them, in rivers, and springs, and holy wells, thousands of them, he says. He ordains priests, and founds churches, small ones, of wood, or wattle, often near the ringforts of the kings who welcome him.

[long-pause]

He is especially proud of the young people, the sons and daughters of the Irish, who become monks, and nuns, giving their lives to God. He tells the story of a beautiful young Irish noblewoman, whom he baptized, who came to him a few days later, and told him that an angel had told her to become a nun. And she did, six days later, eagerly, and fervently, against the wishes of her family.

[pause]

Patrick does not destroy the old ways. He respects much of the old Irish culture, and uses it. According to later stories, he blessed the old holy wells, which had been sacred to the old gods, and made them Christian holy wells, which they still are today. He built churches near the old sacred places. He made friends with some of the poets, and the lawyers.

[pause]

{img:holy-well-stone-surround-ribbons-tree-rags-green-hollow}
There are still holy wells all over Ireland, by the roads, and in the fields, often with an old tree beside them, hung with ribbons, and rags, and rosaries, left by pilgrims, who come to pray there, and to ask for healing, as they have done for more than fifteen hundred years.

[long-pause]

Patrick also writes, angrily, a letter, which still survives, to the soldiers of a British king, called Coroticus, who had raided the coast of Ireland, and killed, and captured, some of Patrick's newly baptized Christians, still in their white baptismal robes, with the oil of baptism still on their foreheads, and sold them as slaves. Patrick, who had been a slave himself, is furious, and demands that they be set free.

[pause]

It is one of the first letters in history written against slavery.

[long-pause]

{img:small-wooden-church-oak-planks-thatch-ireland-churchyard-cross}
What were the first churches in Ireland like?

[pause]

They were small, and simple: little rectangular buildings, of wood, oak planks, or wattle and daub, with a thatched roof, or wooden shingles, often no bigger than a single room, with a little altar at the east end, and a door at the west end, and perhaps one small window.

[long-pause]

Beside the church, there was a little house for the priest, and a graveyard, and perhaps a well, and a cross: at first, a simple wooden cross, or a cross carved on a standing stone. Later, the Irish would carve great stone crosses, the high crosses, covered with pictures from the Bible, and spirals, and knots, with a ring around the centre of the cross, the famous Celtic cross.

[pause]

The priests wore simple robes of wool, and carried a staff, a crozier, and a bell: a little iron handbell, which they rang, to call the people to prayer, and which became the symbol of the Irish saints. Many of these old bells survive, kept in beautiful shrines.

[pause]

The Irish monks had their own special haircut, the tonsure: they shaved the front of their heads, from ear to ear, and let the hair at the back grow long, perhaps in the way the druids had done. This was different from the tonsure of the monks of Rome, who shaved a round patch on the top of their heads, and later caused great arguments.

[long-pause]

The services were in Latin, which the people did not understand, but the priests explained them, in Irish. The people came on Sundays, and on feast days, to hear Mass, and to be baptized, and married, and buried. And the priests went around the countryside, visiting the ringforts, and blessing the houses, and the cattle, and the fields, and the wells.

[pause]

Later, in the seventh and eighth centuries, the Irish would build their churches in stone, small, and steep-roofed, and very strong, some of which still stand today: little stone oratories, like the Gallarus Oratory, in County Kerry, shaped like an upturned boat, built without mortar, of carefully fitted stones, which has kept out the rain for more than a thousand years.

[pause]

{img:gallarus-oratory-dry-stone-upturned-boat-shape-kerry-green}
And later still, the tall, slender, round towers, beside the monasteries, with their conical stone caps, and their doors high above the ground, which were bell towers, and lookout towers, and places of safety, where the monks could hide their treasures, and themselves, when raiders came.

[long-pause]

{img:hill-of-slane-easter-fire-night-small-figures-distant-tara}
The most famous story about Saint Patrick is the story of the Easter fire.

[pause]

It was written down about two hundred years after his death, by a monk, called Muirchú, and it is probably more legend than history. But it is the story that every Irish child knows.

[long-pause]

In the spring of the year 433, the story says, Patrick came to the hill of Slane, in Meath, a few kilometres from Tara, on Easter Eve.

[pause]

That same night, at Tara, King Lóegaire, the High King, was holding a great festival, with all the kings, and nobles, and druids, of Ireland. And the law was, that on that night, no fire could be lit anywhere in the land, until the king had lit the great fire at Tara.

[pause]

But Patrick, on the hill of Slane, lit the Easter fire, the fire of the resurrection of Christ, as Christians do, on Easter Eve. And the king, and his druids, at Tara, saw the fire, burning, across the dark plain, before the king's fire had been lit.

[pause]

The king was furious. And the druids said to him: O king, unless this fire, which we see, is put out this very night, it will never be put out, and it will burn over all of Ireland, and the man who has lit it will overcome us all, and all the people of your kingdom.

[long-pause]

So the king drove with his chariots, and his druids, to Slane, to arrest Patrick. But Patrick, the story says, came out to meet them, singing a psalm, and the king's men could not harm him. And there was a contest, between Patrick, and the druids, of miracles, and in the end, Patrick won.

[pause]

The king, the story says, did not become a Christian himself. But he allowed Patrick to preach, and to travel, freely, through his kingdom.

[pause]

{img:patrick-before-king-chariots-druids-dawn-hill-calm-meeting}
And the druids' words came true: the fire that Patrick lit on the hill of Slane, that Easter night, was never put out. It spread over all of Ireland.

[long-pause]

There is a famous prayer, called Saint Patrick's Breastplate, or the Deer's Cry, which, the legend says, Patrick sang, on the way to Tara, to protect himself and his companions, and which made the king's soldiers, waiting in ambush, see only a deer, and its fawns, passing by. The prayer was probably written later, but it is still sung today:

[pause]

Christ with me, Christ before me, Christ behind me. Christ in me, Christ beneath me, Christ above me. Christ on my right, Christ on my left. Christ when I lie down, Christ when I sit down, Christ when I arise.

[pause]

{img:deer-and-fawns-misty-forest-path-dawn-light-quiet}
Today, on the hill of Slane, there are the ruins of an old friary, and a church, and a graveyard, and a statue of Saint Patrick. And every year, at Easter, a fire is lit there again.

[long-pause]

{img:tara-hill-today-like-ancient-banks-ditches-view-plains-meath}
The hill of Tara is the most sacred place in ancient Ireland.

[pause]

It is not very high: only about a hundred and fifty metres above the sea. But it stands alone, in the middle of the wide, flat, green plains of Meath, so that from the top, on a clear day, you can see, it is said, half of the counties of Ireland: the mountains of Wicklow, in the south, and the hills of Ulster, in the north, and the plains stretching away to the west.

[long-pause]

People have come to Tara for five thousand years. The oldest monument on the hill, the Mound of the Hostages, is a passage tomb, built by the first farmers, around 3000 before Christ, older than the pyramids of Egypt. After that, people built great circles of banks, and ditches, and wooden posts, and earthworks, and mounds, and burials, over thousands of years.

[pause]

In the time of Patrick, there are great earthworks on the hill: the Ráith na Ríg, the fort of the kings, a great oval enclosure, with a bank, and a ditch, and inside it, two ringforts, joined together, called the Royal Seat, and Cormac's House. And the long, sunken avenue, called the Banqueting Hall, where, the stories say, the great feasts of the High Kings were held, with thousands of guests, each in their proper place.

[pause]

{img:lia-fail-stone-of-destiny-upright-stone-tara-mound-grass}
On the top of one of the mounds stands the Lia Fáil, the Stone of Destiny: a tall, smooth, grey, upright stone. The legend says that the gods, the Tuatha Dé Danann, brought it to Ireland, from one of their four magical cities, and that when the true king of Ireland stood on it, or touched it, it roared, so that the whole of Ireland could hear it.

[long-pause]

The stories say that the feast of Tara was held every three years, or every seven years, at Samhain, and that all the kings, and nobles, and poets, and lawyers, of Ireland came, and the laws were proclaimed, and the history of the kingdoms was recited, and the genealogies of the families were checked, by the poets, to make sure they were correct, and disputes were settled, and peace was kept, for the feast. Anyone who broke the peace of Tara, by fighting, was put to death.

[pause]

{img:great-assembly-tara-tents-banners-kings-poets-autumn-evening}
Whether all this is true, or how much of it is legend, nobody knows. But for the Irish, Tara was, and still is, the symbol of the ancient glory of Ireland, the seat of the High Kings, the heart of the land.

[long-pause]

{img:shamrock-three-leaves-green-close-dew-morning-grass}
Over the centuries, many stories and legends grew up around Saint Patrick, which are told all over the world today.

[pause]

The most famous is the story of the shamrock. The legend says that Patrick, when he was explaining the Holy Trinity, how God can be three persons, the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit, and yet only one God, picked a little shamrock, a small clover, with three leaves on one stem, and held it up, and said: just as this little plant has three leaves, and yet is one plant, so God is three persons, and yet one God.

[long-pause]

There is no record of this story until more than a thousand years after Patrick's death. But the shamrock has become the symbol of Ireland, and of Saint Patrick, and on Saint Patrick's Day, people all over the world wear a little sprig of shamrock, or something green.

[pause]

Another famous legend says that Patrick drove all the snakes out of Ireland. He stood on a mountain, the story says, and rang his bell, and banished every snake on the island into the sea, and they never came back.

[pause]

In fact, there have never been any snakes in Ireland, as far as scientists know, since the last Ice Age. After the ice melted, the sea rose, and cut Ireland off from Britain, before the snakes could get there. But the legend may be a way of saying that Patrick drove out the old religion, the old gods, from Ireland.

[long-pause]

The mountain where Patrick is said to have done this is Croagh Patrick, Patrick's mountain, a tall, beautiful, cone-shaped mountain, in County Mayo, in the far west of Ireland, rising steeply from the shore of Clew Bay, a bay full of little green islands.

[pause]

The legend says that Patrick climbed it, and spent forty days and forty nights on the top, fasting, and praying, for the people of Ireland, in the time of Lent, like Moses on Mount Sinai. And that the demons came to him, in the form of black birds, to frighten him, and he drove them away, by ringing his bell, and throwing it at them.

[pause]

{img:pilgrims-climbing-stony-path-croagh-patrick-summer-morning}
Every year, on the last Sunday of July, called Reek Sunday, thousands of pilgrims, from all over Ireland, and the world, climb Croagh Patrick, in honour of Saint Patrick, some of them barefoot, up the steep, stony path, to the little chapel on the top, as people have done for more than a thousand years. On a clear day, from the top, you can see the whole of Clew Bay, with its islands, and the Atlantic Ocean, stretching away to the west.

[long-pause]

Another legend tells of Patrick's Purgatory, on a little island, in a lake, called Lough Derg, in County Donegal, where, the story says, Christ showed Patrick a cave, which was the entrance to Purgatory. In the Middle Ages, it became one of the most famous pilgrimages in Europe, and knights came from all over Europe to visit it.

[pause]

Today, pilgrims still go to Lough Derg, every summer, for three days, of fasting, and prayer, and walking barefoot around the old stone circles, the beds of the saints, and staying awake all night, in the church on the island.

[pause]

{img:lough-derg-island-basilica-lake-grey-sky-boats-pilgrims}
And there are many other stories: of Patrick's bell, and his staff, and his tooth, and his arguments with the druids, and his conversations with the old heroes of Ireland, Oisín, and Caílte, who, in one medieval story, came back from the Otherworld, after hundreds of years, and walked with Patrick through Ireland, and told him the old stories of the places they passed.

[long-pause]

{img:old-bishop-writing-confession-quill-parchment-candle-cell}
Most of what we know for certain about Patrick comes from two short documents, which he wrote himself, in Latin, when he was an old man: his Confession, and his Letter to the soldiers of Coroticus.

[pause]

They are the oldest documents written in Ireland that survive. And they are very personal, and moving.

[long-pause]

The Confession begins: I, Patrick, a sinner, a most simple countryman, the least of all the faithful, and most contemptible to many.

[pause]

He writes it, he says, because some people, in Britain, and perhaps in the Church, have criticized him, and accused him of something, and he wants to defend himself, and to explain what he has done, and why. And he wants to thank God, for all that God has done for him.

[pause]

He apologizes, again and again, for his bad Latin. He says that, because he was captured as a boy, and lived as a slave, instead of going to school, he never learned to write well, like the educated men of Britain. He says that he is ashamed, and afraid, to write, because his words do not come easily. But he writes anyway, because he wants the truth to be known.

[long-pause]

And then he tells his story: the capture, the slavery, the prayers on the mountain, the escape, the dream, the return to Ireland, and the long years of work, and danger, and the thousands of people he has baptized.

[pause]

He writes with great humility, and great faith, and great love for the Irish people. He says that he would gladly give his life for them. He says that he never took money, or gifts, from the people he baptized, and that when the women gave him their jewellery, as gifts, he gave it back to them, so that no one could say that he had done it for gain.

[pause]

{img:simple-church-wooden-small-ireland-evening-candles-door-open}
He writes about the Irish, who had never known God, and worshipped idols, and now have become a people of the Lord, and are called children of God.

[long-pause]

And he ends: I pray that none of those who believe in God, and fear Him, who read this, or receive this writing, which Patrick, a sinner, without learning, wrote in Ireland, will say that it was I, in my ignorance, who did any small thing that I did, or showed, according to God's will. But let them judge, and believe, that it was the gift of God. And this is my confession, before I die.

[pause]

Two copies of these documents survive in the Book of Armagh, a manuscript written about four hundred years later, around the year 807, by an Irish monk, which is now kept in the library of Trinity College, in Dublin.

[pause]

{img:trinity-college-dublin-long-room-library-old-books-arches}
Reading them today, sixteen hundred years later, you can still hear the voice of a real man: humble, and brave, and stubborn, and a little anxious, and deeply sincere, who was once a lonely slave boy on an Irish mountain, and came back, as an old man, to give his life to the people who had enslaved him.

[long-pause]

{img:saul-county-down-small-church-hill-evening-old-man-companions}
We do not know exactly when Patrick died. The old Irish annals give two dates: 461, and 493. Many historians think it was some time in the second half of the fifth century, when he was an old man.

[pause]

But the tradition says that he died on the seventeenth of March. And that is why Saint Patrick's Day is held on the seventeenth of March, every year, all over the world.

[long-pause]

The tradition also says that he died at Saul, in County Down, near the place where he first landed in Ireland, and founded his first church, in the barn of the chieftain Dichu, so many years before.

[pause]

And that, when he died, there was a dispute, about where he should be buried. So his body was put on a cart, pulled by two untamed oxen, and they let the oxen go wherever they wished. And the oxen walked, and walked, and stopped, at last, at a place called Dún Pádraig, which became Downpatrick, a few kilometres away. And there, he was buried.

[pause]

{img:downpatrick-cathedral-hill-granite-stone-grave-green-grass}
Today, on the hill of Down Cathedral, in Downpatrick, there is a great rough stone of granite, lying on the grass, with a cross, and the name Patric carved on it, which marks the place where, tradition says, he is buried. It was put there in 1900. People come from all over the world to visit it, and leave flowers, and shamrocks, especially on the seventeenth of March.

[pause]

The tradition also says that Saint Brigid, and Saint Columba, two of the other great saints of Ireland, were later buried in the same grave, beside him. There is an old rhyme: in Down, three saints one grave do fill: Patrick, Brigid, and Columcille.

[long-pause]

By the time Patrick died, Ireland was changing.

[pause]

There were Christian churches, and priests, and bishops, all over the north, and the middle of Ireland. And in the following generations, Christianity spread over the whole island, peacefully, without war, and without martyrs, which was very unusual in the history of the world.

[pause]

{img:early-church-site-round-tower-stone-crosses-green-valley-later}
The old kings remained, and the old laws, and the old poets, and the old way of life, with its cattle, and its ringforts, and its feasts. But now, beside the ringforts, there were churches, and soon, monasteries, which became the centres of learning, and art, and books, in Ireland.

[pause]

And in the following centuries, Ireland would become the island of saints and scholars, and send its own missionaries, to Scotland, and England, and France, and Switzerland, and Italy, to bring Christianity, and learning, back to the rest of Europe, in the dark centuries after the fall of Rome.

[long-pause]

{img:iona-island-abbey-white-sand-turquoise-sea-mull-hills}
About a hundred years after Patrick, another great Irish saint carried the faith across the sea, to Scotland: Columba, called in Irish Colm Cille, the dove of the church.

[pause]

He was born in 521, in Donegal, in the north west of Ireland, a prince of the royal family of the Uí Néill, the descendants of Niall of the Nine Hostages. He could have been a king. But he became a monk, and a scholar, and founded monasteries in Ireland, at Derry, and Durrow, and perhaps Kells.

[long-pause]

There is a famous story about him. He secretly copied a beautiful book of psalms, belonging to another monk, Finnian. Finnian said that the copy belonged to him, because the book was his. The quarrel went to the High King, who judged: to every cow its calf, and to every book its copy. The copy belonged to Finnian. It is sometimes called the first copyright case in history.

[pause]

The story goes on to say that the quarrel led to a battle, and that Columba, full of sorrow, decided to leave Ireland, as a penance, and never to see it again.

[pause]

{img:currach-twelve-monks-sailing-to-iona-sea-islands-dawn}
In 563, he sailed, with twelve companions, in a currach, across the sea, to the little island of Iona, off the west coast of Scotland. And there, he founded a monastery, which became one of the most famous in Europe.

[long-pause]

Iona is a small, beautiful island, with white sand beaches, and clear turquoise sea, and green fields, and rocky hills, and wide views over the sea, to the mountains of the island of Mull. From Iona, Columba and his monks brought Christianity to the Picts, in the north of Scotland, and later, his monks went south, to Northumbria, in England, and founded the monastery of Lindisfarne.

[pause]

Columba was a great poet, and scribe, and a strong, and sometimes fierce, leader, who loved Ireland, and missed it, all his life. A poem, said to be by him, says: there is a grey eye, which looks back on Ireland, and it will never see again the men of Ireland, nor her women.

[pause]

{img:old-monk-white-horse-weeping-head-on-shoulder-iona-evening}
His biographer, Adomnán, tells a beautiful story about his last day, in 597. The old saint, walking slowly, sat down to rest, beside the road, and the old white horse, which carried the milk pails from the cow pasture to the monastery, came up to him, and laid its head on his breast, and wept, because it knew that its master was going to leave it. And Columba blessed the horse. And that night, in the church, he passed away, peacefully, among his monks.

[long-pause]

{img:saint-brigid-woman-cloak-kildare-oak-church-cows-dawn}
After Patrick, the most beloved saint of Ireland is a woman: Saint Brigid of Kildare.

[pause]

She was born, according to tradition, around the year 451, about twenty years after Patrick came to Ireland, in Leinster, the daughter of a chieftain, Dubthach, and a slave woman, Broicsech, who, some stories say, had been baptized by Patrick himself. She grew up as a servant, in her father's house, working in the dairy, and looking after the cows.

[long-pause]

The stories about her are full of kindness, and generosity, and milk, and butter, and cows. As a girl, she gave away everything in her father's house to the poor: the butter, and the milk, and the food, and even her father's precious sword, to a leper, so that he could sell it and buy food. Her father was furious, and tried to sell her to a king. But the king was so impressed by her goodness that he refused to buy her, and told her father to let her be.

[pause]

She became a nun, and founded a great monastery, at Kildare, in the middle of Ireland, under a great oak tree. The name Kildare comes from Cill Dara, the church of the oak.

[pause]

{img:kildare-monastery-wooden-church-round-houses-oak-tree-sacred-fire}
It was a double monastery, for both nuns and monks, and Brigid was its abbess, and became one of the most powerful and respected people in Ireland. Kildare became a great centre of learning, and art, and charity, with a famous school of metalwork, and manuscript painting.

[long-pause]

At Kildare, the nuns kept a sacred fire burning, day and night, for hundreds of years, tended by nineteen nuns, in turn, and on the twentieth day, by Brigid herself, after her death. Perhaps the fire went back to the old goddess Brigid, whose festival, Imbolc, became the feast of Saint Brigid, on the first of February.

[pause]

There are many stories of her miracles: that she could turn water into beer, for the poor, and that her cows gave milk three times a day, and that a single basket of butter never ran out. That she hung her wet cloak, to dry, on a sunbeam. And that, when she asked a king for land for her monastery, he laughed, and said she could have as much as her cloak would cover. So she spread out her cloak, and it grew, and grew, and covered the whole of the Curragh, the great plain of Kildare.

[pause]

{img:green-plain-curragh-kildare-horses-grazing-wide-open-morning}
On Saint Brigid's Day, the first of February, in Ireland, people still make Saint Brigid's crosses, small crosses woven from rushes, with four arms, and a square in the middle, and hang them in their houses, and their barns, for protection, for the year. The legend says that Brigid made the first one, at the bedside of a dying pagan chieftain, to explain the cross of Christ to him, and he was baptized before he died.

[long-pause]

Brigid died, around the year 525, at Kildare. And she is one of the three patron saints of Ireland, with Patrick, and Columba. Since 2023, her feast day, the first of February, has been a public holiday in Ireland, the first Irish public holiday named after a woman.

[long-pause]

{img:early-irish-monastery-beehive-huts-stone-island-sea-cliffs}
In the century after Patrick, monasteries spread all over Ireland.

[pause]

Some were great, busy places, like little towns, with hundreds, or even thousands, of monks, and students, and craftsmen, and farmers, living around them: Clonmacnoise, on the river Shannon, in the middle of Ireland. Glendalough, in a beautiful valley, between two lakes, in the Wicklow mountains. Kildare. Armagh. Durrow. Kells. Bangor.

[long-pause]

Others were tiny, remote hermitages, on islands, or mountains, where a few monks lived alone, in prayer, as far from the world as possible.

[pause]

The most remarkable of these is Skellig Michael: a great, steep, jagged rock, rising out of the Atlantic Ocean, twelve kilometres off the coast of County Kerry, where, from the sixth or seventh century, a little community of monks lived, on a narrow ledge, near the top, more than a hundred and seventy metres above the waves, in little round huts of stone, shaped like beehives, built without mortar, with a little church, and a graveyard, and terraced gardens, reached by more than six hundred stone steps, cut into the rock.

[pause]

They lived there, for hundreds of years, in the wind, and the spray, and the storms, with the seabirds, and the seals, fishing, and growing a few vegetables, and praying. Their beehive huts are still there today, perfectly preserved. And in recent years, the island became famous all over the world, because it appeared in the Star Wars films.

[long-pause]

In the monasteries, the Irish monks learned Latin, and Greek, and studied the Bible, and the writings of the Church Fathers, and the old Roman writers. And they copied books, by hand, on parchment, made from calfskin, with quills, and inks, and colours.

[pause]

And they made the most beautiful books in the world.

[pause]

{img:monk-illuminating-manuscript-intricate-knotwork-colours-desk}
The most famous is the Book of Kells, made around the year 800, three and a half centuries after Patrick, by the monks of the monastery of Iona, in Scotland, founded by the Irish saint Columba, and perhaps finished at Kells, in Ireland. It is a copy of the four Gospels, in Latin, decorated with wonderful pictures, and patterns, in brilliant colours: spirals, and knots, and interlacing, and strange animals, and birds, and angels, and saints, so intricate, and so fine, that some of the patterns can only be seen properly with a magnifying glass. Giraldus of Wales, who saw a book like it, in the twelfth century, said that it must have been the work of angels, not of men.

[long-pause]

The Book of Kells is now kept in the library of Trinity College, in Dublin, and every year, more than half a million people come to see it, in its glass case. A different page is shown each day.

[pause]

The Irish monks also wrote down the old Irish stories, and laws, and poems, and history, in the Irish language, so that they were not lost. And they wrote little poems of their own, in the margins of the books they were copying, about the things around them. One of them, a monk in the ninth century, wrote a poem about his cat, called Pangur Bán, white Pangur, who hunted mice, while the monk hunted for the meaning of words.

[pause]

{img:monk-and-white-cat-scriptorium-candle-mouse-hole-quiet-evening}
Another monk wrote, in the margin of his manuscript, a little poem about a blackbird: the little bird which has whistled from the end of a bright yellow beak, it sends its note over Belfast Lough, a blackbird from a yellow-tipped branch.

[long-pause]

{img:irish-monks-walking-alps-pass-staffs-satchels-books-snow}
In the centuries after Patrick, Ireland sent its monks out, all over Europe.

[pause]

They called it peregrinatio: going into exile, for the love of God, leaving their beloved homeland, and travelling, as pilgrims, and missionaries, to other lands, never to return.

[long-pause]

One of the most famous was Columbanus, a monk from Bangor, in County Down, who, around the year 590, sailed to Gaul, with twelve companions, and founded monasteries in the wild forests of the Vosges mountains, in the east of France, at Luxeuil, and later, after quarrelling with the kings there, crossed the Alps, into Switzerland, and Italy, and founded a great monastery at Bobbio, in the mountains of northern Italy, where he passed away, in 615.

[pause]

One of his companions, Gallus, stayed in Switzerland, by a lake, and lived as a hermit, in the forest, and after his death, a monastery grew up around his cell: Saint Gall, which became one of the greatest centres of learning in Europe, with a wonderful library, which still exists today, in the city of Sankt Gallen, with books written by Irish monks, twelve hundred years ago.

[pause]

Irish monks went to England, and Scotland, and Wales, and France, and Belgium, and Germany, and Switzerland, and Austria, and Italy. They founded monasteries, and schools, and taught Latin, and Greek, and copied books, and kept learning alive, in the centuries after the fall of Rome, when much of Europe had forgotten it.

[long-pause]

They carried their books with them, in leather satchels, on their journeys. And many of the oldest books in the libraries of Europe were written by Irish monks, in their beautiful handwriting, with little notes in Irish in the margins: complaints about the cold, and the bad ink, and the poor light, and little poems about birds, and cats, and the weather.

[pause]

One monk wrote, in the margin of a book, in Irish: the wind is fierce tonight, it tosses the sea's white hair. I fear no wild Vikings, sailing the quiet main. Because, on stormy nights, the Viking raiders, who came to Ireland from the eighth century, could not sail.

[pause]

{img:monk-at-window-stormy-night-sea-waves-candle-writing-relief}
And so the faith, and the learning, that the slave boy from Britain had brought back to Ireland, went out again, from Ireland, all over Europe.

[long-pause]

{img:st-patricks-day-parade-dublin-green-crowds-music-sunny}
Today, Saint Patrick is remembered all over the world.

[pause]

On the seventeenth of March, every year, Saint Patrick's Day is celebrated, not only in Ireland, but wherever the Irish have gone: in America, and Canada, and Australia, and Britain, and Argentina, and all over the world. There are parades, with bands, and dancers, and floats, and people dressed in green, with shamrocks, and music, and dancing.

[long-pause]

The first Saint Patrick's Day parades were not in Ireland, but in America, in the eighteenth century, organized by Irish soldiers, and immigrants, in Boston, and New York, who wanted to remember their homeland. The New York parade, which began in 1762, is now one of the largest parades in the world. In Chicago, they even dye the river green.

[pause]

In Ireland, Saint Patrick's Day is a national holiday. In Dublin, there is a great festival, for several days. And in Downpatrick, and Armagh, and Saul, there are pilgrimages, and church services, and processions, to the places associated with the saint.

[pause]

And the old Ireland of Patrick's time can still be seen, if you know where to look.

[long-pause]

The ringforts are still there, tens of thousands of them, in the fields, all over Ireland: round, grassy banks, often with hawthorn trees growing on them. For centuries, the Irish farmers would not touch them, or plough them, or cut down their trees, because they believed that they were fairy forts, where the fairies lived, and that it would bring bad luck to disturb them. And so they survived.

[pause]

The ogham stones still stand, in the fields, and the churchyards, with their mysterious notches. The holy wells are still visited, and decorated with ribbons, and flowers.

[pause]

On the hill of Tara, the ancient mounds, and the earthworks, and the Stone of Destiny, are still there, on the green hill, with the wind blowing, and the wide view over the plains of Meath. On the hill of Slane, the ruins of the old friary stand where Patrick lit his Easter fire. And Croagh Patrick still rises above Clew Bay, with the pilgrims climbing it every summer.

[long-pause]

There are reconstructions of the old ringforts, and crannógs, and round houses, in open-air museums, like the Irish National Heritage Park, near Wexford, and Craggaunowen, in County Clare, where you can walk into a round house, with its wattle walls, and its thatch, and its fire, and imagine the life of the people, in Patrick's time.

[pause]

And in the National Museum, in Dublin, there are the beautiful things of early Christian Ireland: the Ardagh Chalice, and the Tara Brooch, and the bell of Saint Patrick, an old iron bell, which, tradition says, belonged to the saint himself, kept in a beautiful shrine, of bronze, and gold, and silver, and jewels, made for it in the eleventh century.

[pause]

The slave boy on the mountain, the bishop on the long roads, the poets by the fire, the cattle in the summer pastures, and the fire on the hill of Slane that was never put out.
