Today, you can still visit the place where gold was first found in California. It is called Coloma, on the American River.

It is a quiet and peaceful place now, a state park. There is a copy of the old sawmill, built just like the original, beside the river. There is a little museum, with tools and pictures from the Gold Rush. There are old buildings from the 1850s: a little store, a schoolhouse, a church. And there is a statue of James Marshall, the man who found the first gold, on a hill above the valley.

--

The river still flows through the valley, clear and fast, over the rocks. In summer, people swim in it and float down it in rafts and kayaks. And some people still look for gold, with pans, just like the forty-niners. At Coloma, visitors can try it for themselves. Children crouch at the edge of a little water trough with a pan, swirling and swirling. And sometimes, they find a tiny flake of gold at the bottom. Their faces light up, just like the faces of the miners long ago.

{img:children-panning-gold-trough-park-today-pans-smiling-summer-today}
There are other old Gold Rush towns too, in the foothills of the Sierra Nevada. Some of them have been carefully kept, just as they were. In a town called Columbia, the main street looks almost exactly as it did in the 1850s. There are wooden sidewalks and old brick shops. There is a stagecoach pulled by horses, and a blacksmith working at his forge. People in old-fashioned clothes work in the shops and tell visitors about the past.

--

The road that runs through the old Gold Rush towns is called Highway 49, after the forty-niners. It winds through the foothills, past oak trees and pine forests, rivers and old mining towns. Along the way, there are old cabins and stone buildings, little cemeteries and old mines. In spring, the hills are green and covered with orange poppies. The poppy is now the state flower of California.

{img:highway-49-foothills-spring-poppies-oak-trees-old-town-today}
In Sacramento, there is a part of the old town that has been rebuilt, beside the river. There are wooden buildings with raised sidewalks, and old shops and saloons. There is a big railway museum, with the old steam locomotives that crossed the mountains. And there is an old riverboat, tied up at the wharf, like the ones that carried the forty-niners up the river.

--

California is still called the Golden State. That name comes from the Gold Rush. But today, people say it is golden for other reasons too. Golden sunshine, golden hills in summer, golden beaches and golden poppies. And San Francisco has a famous bridge across the narrow entrance to its bay. It is called the Golden Gate Bridge, after the old name of the strait that the ships of 1849 sailed through.

{img:golden-gate-bridge-sunset-fog-san-francisco-bay-today}
In the evenings, when the sun sets over the Pacific Ocean, the Golden Gate Bridge glows red and gold. The fog rolls in from the sea, slowly, around the towers of the bridge. Ships sail in and out of the bay, as they have done for almost two hundred years. And somewhere, perhaps, a family is sitting on a porch in the warm evening, telling stories about the great-great-grandparents who came to California, long ago, to look for gold.

In the foothills, there are also little museums in old stone buildings, full of things from the Gold Rush. There are pans and rockers, picks and shovels. There are old letters and diaries, photographs and maps. There are little bottles of gold dust and real gold nuggets, behind glass. And there are the stories of the people who came here, from all over the world, long ago.

In the old Gold Rush towns, there are also little festivals every year. People dress up in clothes from the 1850s. There are parades and fiddle music and dancing. There are gold panning contests, where people race to find the most gold in a pan. Children ride in old stagecoaches. And in the evenings, people sit together on the wooden sidewalks and tell stories of the old days.
