Life on board the Orion is crowded and simple. There are about a hundred and thirty passengers, and a crew of about twenty sailors. The ship is only about forty metres long.

The passengers sleep in a big room below deck, called the steerage. There are rows of narrow wooden bunks, two or three on top of each other. Each bunk is just big enough for one man to lie down. There is no privacy at all. The room is dark and stuffy, and it smells of tar and wet wool. When the sea is rough, everything moves and creaks, and the men roll around in their bunks.

--

The food is simple and the same every day. There is salted beef or salted pork, hard ship's biscuits, beans and rice, and dried apples. There is coffee and tea, with a little molasses for sweetness. On Sundays, there is a plum pudding, made with flour and raisins. The men eat at long tables below deck, or sitting on the deck in good weather. They grumble about the food, but they eat it all.

{img:passengers-eating-long-table-below-deck-tin-plates-lantern-swaying}
To pass the time, the passengers find many things to do. They read books and newspapers, again and again. They play cards and checkers and dominoes. They write letters and diaries. They sing songs and play instruments: fiddles and banjos, flutes and harmonicas. One of the most popular songs is about California, set to the tune of an old song called Oh, Susanna. Oh, California, they sing, that's the land for me.

--

Some of the passengers hold classes, to teach each other useful things. Dr. Hale gives talks about staying healthy. A man who has read about mining explains how to wash gold. Another man teaches a little Spanish, because many people in California speak it. Eli goes to all the classes. He learns a few Spanish words. Hola means hello. Gracias means thank you. Oro means gold.

{img:men-on-deck-singing-banjo-fiddle-evening-sun-sails-above}
After a few weeks, the ship sails into warm waters, near the equator. The weather becomes hot and sunny. The men take off their coats and sit in the shade of the sails. They see flying fish leaping out of the water, gliding over the waves on their wing-like fins. Sometimes one lands on the deck, and the cook grills it for supper. They see dolphins swimming beside the ship, playing in the bow wave.

--

When the ship crosses the equator, there is a funny old ceremony. One of the sailors dresses up as King Neptune, the king of the sea, with a crown and a long beard made of rope. All the passengers who have never crossed the equator before must come before him. He pretends to shave them with a big wooden razor, and then they are dunked in a tub of seawater. Everyone laughs and cheers. Eli is dunked too, and comes up spluttering and grinning.

{img:equator-crossing-ceremony-sailor-neptune-crown-passengers-laughing-tub}
In the evenings, Samuel and Eli often sit together on the deck, looking at the stars. In the south, there are new stars they have never seen before. The sailors show them the Southern Cross, four bright stars in the shape of a cross. Samuel tells Eli about his own father, who was a sailor too. Eli tells his father about his hopes for California. They talk for a long time, as the ship sails on through the warm night.

Some of the passengers keep diaries of the voyage. They write about the weather, the food and the other passengers. They write about the fish and the birds they see. They write about their hopes for California. Many of these diaries have survived, and they are kept in libraries today. They tell us a lot about the long voyage around Cape Horn.

Eli makes friends with the cabin boy, a boy called Jack, who is about his age. Jack has been at sea for two years already. He teaches Eli to tie sailors' knots and to climb a little way up the rigging. He tells him stories of whales and storms and faraway ports. In return, Eli helps Jack peel potatoes in the galley. The cook gives them both an extra piece of pudding on Sundays.
