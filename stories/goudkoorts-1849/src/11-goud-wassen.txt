How do you find gold in a river? It is simpler than you might think, but it takes a lot of patience.

Gold is very heavy, much heavier than sand and gravel. Over thousands of years, the rivers have carried little bits of gold down from the mountains. Because it is so heavy, the gold sinks down and settles in quiet places in the river. It collects in the gravel at the bottom, behind rocks and in cracks. Sometimes it is in flakes, sometimes in tiny grains like dust. And sometimes, very rarely, it is in bigger lumps, called nuggets.

--

The simplest way to find gold is with a pan. A gold pan is a flat metal dish, a little like a frying pan without a handle. The miner fills it with gravel and sand from the river. Then he crouches at the edge of the water, and dips the pan into the river. He swirls it around and around, gently, tipping it a little. The water washes the light sand and gravel over the edge. The heavy gold sinks to the bottom of the pan.

{img:close-pan-bottom-black-sand-gold-flakes-shining-water}
After many swirls, there is only a little dark sand left at the bottom of the pan. And if you are lucky, among the dark sand, there are little flecks of gold, shining yellow in the sunlight. The miner picks them out carefully with his fingers, or with a little piece of tin. He puts them in a little bottle or a leather bag. It takes about ten minutes to wash one pan. On a good day, a miner might wash fifty pans.

--

Panning is very hard work. The miner must crouch at the edge of the river all day long, with his hands in the cold water. His back aches and his hands go numb. And most pans have only a few tiny flecks of gold in them, or none at all. So the miners soon start to use a better tool, called a rocker, or a cradle.

{img:wooden-rocker-cradle-two-miners-rocking-pouring-water-gravel}
A rocker is a wooden box, about the size of a baby's cradle. It sits on two curved pieces of wood, so it can rock from side to side. At the top, there is a metal sheet with holes in it. One man shovels gravel onto the sheet. Another pours water over it with a bucket, and rocks the box back and forth. The water and the small stones wash through the holes and down the box. On the bottom of the box, there are little wooden bars, called riffles. The heavy gold gets caught behind them.

--

The company works with two rockers and several pans. They work in teams. Some men dig the gravel from the riverbank with picks and shovels. Some carry it to the rockers in buckets. Some rock the rockers and pour the water. Eli carries the water, up and down from the river, all day long. At the end of each day, the men clean out the riffles very carefully. They wash the last sand in a pan, and pick out the gold.

{img:miners-evening-weighing-gold-dust-small-scales-cabin-lamp-hopeful}
On the first day, they find only a little gold, a few pinches of dust. On the second day, a little more. By the end of the first week, they have about two ounces between them, worth about thirty dollars. It is not a fortune, shared between twelve men. But it is a start. Dr. Hale weighs it on a little pair of scales, and writes it down in his notebook. And Eli takes the tiniest flake, with his father's permission, and puts it in an envelope for Ruth.

Gold is not found everywhere in the river. Experienced miners know where to look. They look on the inside of bends in the river, where the water slows down. They look behind big rocks, and in cracks in the bedrock, the solid rock at the bottom of the river. They look in old dried-up riverbeds, high on the hillsides. Learning where to look is the most important skill of all.

Later, the miners build an even bigger tool, called a long tom. It is a long wooden trough, many metres long, with riffles at the bottom. A stream of water runs through it all the time. Several men shovel gravel into it at once. It can wash much more gravel than a rocker. The company builds one in the autumn, with Samuel in charge of the carpentry.
