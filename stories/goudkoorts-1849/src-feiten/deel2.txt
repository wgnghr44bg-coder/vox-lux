TOPIC: By sea to California
===
IMG: sailing-ship-cape-horn-grey-waves-1849 | a three-masted wooden sailing ship with reefed sails rounding Cape Horn in heavy grey seas in 1849, dark rocky cliffs in the distance, dramatic clouds, soft muted light, calm overall atmosphere
HEAD: Many gold seekers sailed around Cape Horn, a voyage of about eighteen thousand miles.
BODY: Not everyone went overland. Many gold seekers from the eastern cities chose to travel by sea. The longest route went all the way around South America. Ships sailed south from New York or Boston, down past Brazil and Argentina. Then they rounded Cape Horn, at the stormy southern tip of the continent. From there, they sailed north along the coast of Chile and Peru, and on to San Francisco. The whole voyage was about eighteen thousand miles. It usually took five to eight months. Passengers were crowded together below deck. The food was poor, and fresh water was limited. Many suffered from seasickness. The waters around Cape Horn were famous for storms, huge waves and icy winds. Even so, the sea route had advantages. Passengers could carry heavy supplies and machinery. They did not have to walk or drive oxen. Yet the long months at sea tested everyone's patience. Most were overjoyed to see the hills of California at last.
===
IMG: ship-deck-passengers-1849-games-calm-sea | passengers in 1840s clothes on the deck of a wooden sailing ship on a calm tropical sea, some playing cards, some reading, a few looking out at the horizon, warm afternoon sunshine
HEAD: On the long sea voyage, passengers passed the time with games, music, newspapers and lectures.
BODY: Months at sea could be very boring. So passengers found ways to fill their time. They played cards, chess and dominoes. They sang songs and played instruments. Some ships had small libraries. Passengers wrote in diaries and composed letters to send home from the next port. On some ships, passengers even published their own handwritten newspapers. They were full of jokes, poems and news of life on board. Some passengers gave lectures on science, history or mining. Others held debates. On Sundays, there were church services on deck. Even so, tempers could grow short. People argued about food, space and the slow progress of the ship. Yet friendships also formed. Many passengers stayed together after they reached California. They joined forces to work in the mines. For some, the long voyage was a strange kind of school. They learned to live closely with strangers from very different backgrounds.
===
IMG: valparaiso-harbour-chile-colourful-houses-hills | a busy harbour town in Chile in 1849 with colourful houses climbing steep green hills above a bay full of sailing ships, small boats near the shore, warm afternoon light
HEAD: Ships bound for California often stopped at Valparaíso in Chile, where passengers enjoyed fresh fruit and solid ground.
BODY: After rounding Cape Horn, most ships stopped at a port on the coast of Chile. The most popular was Valparaíso. Its colourful houses climbed up steep hills around a wide bay. After months at sea, passengers were delighted to go ashore. They walked through the markets and ate fresh fruit, bread and meat. They wrote letters and sent them home. They bought supplies for the rest of the voyage. Valparaíso became very busy during the gold rush years. Many Chileans also caught gold fever. They had experience of mining in their own country. Thousands of them sailed north to California. Chilean miners brought useful skills and methods to the gold fields. Even so, they later faced hostility from some American miners. Yet the link between Chile and California was strong. Ships carried people, flour and goods between the two coasts. For a few years, Valparaíso was an important stop on the road to the gold.
===
IMG: chagres-river-canoes-jungle-panama-1849 | long dugout canoes carrying travellers in 1840s clothes up a winding jungle river in Panama, local boatmen poling the canoes, thick green rainforest on both banks, warm humid light
HEAD: A faster route to California went across the narrow jungle of Panama, by canoe and on mule-back.
BODY: There was a quicker way to reach California by sea. Instead of sailing around South America, travellers could cross the narrow strip of land at Panama. They sailed from New York to the mouth of the Chagres River, on the Caribbean coast. There, they hired local boatmen to take them up the river in long dugout canoes. The journey took several days through thick jungle. The boatmen poled the canoes against the current. Travellers saw monkeys, parrots and strange plants. At night, they slept in small villages along the river. Then they continued on mules along rough mountain trails to Panama City, on the Pacific coast. There, they waited for a steamer to San Francisco. Even so, the route had its dangers. Tropical diseases like malaria and yellow fever were common. Yet if all went well, the whole journey could take just six to eight weeks. In 1855, a railway was built across Panama, making the crossing much easier.
===
IMG: panama-city-waiting-crowds-old-walls-steamer | crowds of travellers in 1840s clothes waiting in the old walled streets of Panama City in 1849, looking towards a steamship anchored in the bay, palm trees and colonial buildings, warm afternoon light
HEAD: In Panama City, thousands of gold seekers waited for weeks for a place on a steamship to San Francisco.
BODY: When the gold seekers reached Panama City, they often faced a long wait. There were not enough steamships to carry everyone to California. Thousands of people crowded into the old city. They stayed in hotels, boarding houses and tents. Prices rose quickly. Tickets for the steamers were sold for high prices. Some people waited for weeks or even months. Many fell ill in the heat. Others ran out of money. The old Spanish city, with its stone walls and churches, was suddenly full of Americans. Local merchants did good business. Even so, the waiting was frustrating. Every day, people watched the bay, hoping a ship would arrive. When one did, there was a rush to get on board. Ships were often dangerously overcrowded. Yet once aboard, the voyage north to San Francisco took only a few weeks. For those who had waited, the sight of the steamer's smoke on the horizon was a great relief.
===
IMG: steamship-golden-gate-entrance-1849-hills | a wooden paddle steamship sailing through the narrow entrance of the Golden Gate into San Francisco Bay in 1849, green and golden hills on both sides, soft morning fog lifting, gentle light
HEAD: Ships entered San Francisco Bay through a narrow strait that had recently been named the Golden Gate.
BODY: The entrance to San Francisco Bay is a narrow strait between two headlands. In 1846, an American officer named John Frémont gave it the name Chrysopylae, or Golden Gate. He chose the name because it reminded him of the Golden Horn, the harbour of Istanbul. He also expected it to become a gateway for rich trade. He could not have known how fitting the name would become. Just two years later, gold was discovered. Ships carrying thousands of gold seekers sailed through the Golden Gate. Often, fog rolled in through the strait in the morning. As it lifted, passengers saw the green hills and the wide bay opening before them. Even so, there was no bridge across the strait in those days. The famous Golden Gate Bridge was not built until the 1930s. Yet the name stuck. For the forty-niners, sailing through the Golden Gate meant that their long journey was nearly over.
===
TOPIC: San Francisco, the instant city
===
IMG: san-francisco-1849-tents-wooden-buildings-hills | the fast-growing town of San Francisco in 1849 with tents, wooden shacks and new buildings spreading up sandy hills above a bay crowded with ships, people and wagons in muddy streets, soft afternoon light
HEAD: San Francisco grew from a small town of about a thousand people to a city of tens of thousands in just two years.
BODY: Before the gold rush, San Francisco was a small, quiet town. In early 1848, perhaps a thousand people lived there. Then the rush began. Ships arrived every day, bringing thousands of newcomers. By the end of 1849, the population had grown to around twenty-five thousand. People called it the instant city. There were not enough buildings. People lived in tents, wooden shacks and even in barrels. New buildings went up almost overnight. Some were made from wood shipped from Oregon or even from as far away as China. Others were prefabricated houses, shipped in pieces from the eastern United States and Europe. The streets were unpaved. In winter, they turned into deep mud. Even so, the city was full of energy. Shops, hotels, restaurants and gambling halls opened everywhere. Yet it was also chaotic. Fires broke out often. San Francisco burned down and was rebuilt several times in its first few years.
===
IMG: abandoned-ships-san-francisco-harbour-forest-masts | hundreds of abandoned wooden sailing ships crowded together in San Francisco Bay in 1849, a forest of bare masts, some ships turned into buildings on the shore, soft hazy light
HEAD: Hundreds of ships were abandoned in San Francisco Bay, because their crews had run off to the gold fields.
BODY: When ships arrived in San Francisco, their passengers hurried ashore. Very often, the sailors did too. They deserted their ships to go and look for gold. Captains could not find new crews. So many ships were simply left in the bay. By 1850, hundreds of empty ships lay at anchor. Their bare masts looked like a forest. Some rotted away. Others were put to new uses. Some were dragged ashore and turned into buildings. One became a hotel. Another became a warehouse. One ship was even used as the city's jail. As the city grew, the waterfront was filled in with sand and rubble. Some of the old ships were buried under the new land. Even so, they were not completely forgotten. Yet for more than a century, they lay hidden under the streets. In recent times, builders digging foundations in downtown San Francisco have uncovered the remains of several of these gold rush ships.
===
IMG: muddy-street-san-francisco-boards-planks-1850 | a very muddy street in San Francisco in 1850 with wooden planks and boards laid across it, men in boots and hats carefully walking, wooden shops on both sides, soft grey winter light
HEAD: The streets of early San Francisco were so muddy in winter that some people said a mule could sink out of sight.
BODY: The streets of San Francisco were not paved in 1849. In the dry summer, they were dusty. When the winter rains came, they turned into deep mud. Stories were told of horses and mules sinking up to their bellies. One famous sign, perhaps a joke, warned that a street was not passable, not even jackassable. People threw anything they could find into the mud to make a path. They used planks, branches, boxes and even sacks of flour and tobacco that were too cheap to sell. Some streets were covered with boards laid side by side. Walking across town could be an adventure. Men wore tall boots and still came home caked in mud. Even so, the city was growing so fast that there was no time to build proper roads. Yet over the next few years, the main streets were planked and later paved. The mud of 1849 became part of San Francisco's legend.
===
IMG: boudin-bakery-sourdough-bread-loaves-1849 | an indoor scene, enclosed by brick walls, a plain ceiling, a small bakery in San Francisco in 1849 with a baker in an apron pulling round crusty loaves of bread from a brick oven, warm glowing firelight
HEAD: A bakery that opened in San Francisco in 1849 still makes sourdough bread from the same starter today.
BODY: Bread was an important food for the gold seekers. In the mining camps, men often made sourdough bread. Sourdough is made with a starter, a mixture of flour and water that ferments naturally. Miners kept their starter in a small pot and carried it everywhere. Some even slept with it to keep it warm. Experienced miners were sometimes called sourdoughs. In 1849, a French baker named Isidore Boudin opened a bakery in San Francisco. He combined French baking methods with the sourdough of the miners. The bread had a crisp crust and a slightly sour taste. The foggy, cool climate of San Francisco seemed to suit it well. Even so, Boudin's bakery faced many challenges over the years. It survived fires and the great earthquake of 1906. Yet it kept going. The company says that it still uses a starter descended from the original one. Sourdough bread remains a famous symbol of San Francisco.
===
IMG: gambling-hall-1849-chandeliers-tables-music | an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, a large gambling hall in San Francisco in 1849 with chandeliers, mirrors, card tables and men in rough clothes and fine coats, a small band playing on a balcony, warm golden lamplight
HEAD: San Francisco's gambling halls were the grandest buildings in the young city.
BODY: In early San Francisco, some of the finest buildings were gambling halls. They stood around Portsmouth Square, the centre of the town. They had chandeliers, mirrors, paintings and carpets. Bands and musicians played to attract customers. Miners came down from the gold fields with bags of gold dust. Many gambled it away in a single night. Card games, dice and roulette were all popular. Gold dust was weighed on small scales at the tables. Even so, the halls were also social centres. Lonely men came for the music, the light and the company. Many simply came to watch and talk. Yet gambling took a heavy toll. Many miners lost everything they had worked for. Some of the most popular halls had names like the El Dorado and the Bella Union. As the city became more settled, gambling was gradually restricted. By the 1850s, churches, schools and theatres were beginning to change the character of San Francisco.
===
TOPIC: Life in a mining camp
===
IMG: mining-camp-tents-cabins-river-valley-pines | a small gold mining camp of canvas tents and rough log cabins beside a rushing river in a steep valley with tall pine trees in California in 1849, smoke rising from campfires, soft morning light
HEAD: Mining camps sprang up along the rivers almost overnight, with names like Rough and Ready and Hangtown.
BODY: The gold fields lay along the rivers and streams of the Sierra Nevada foothills. Wherever gold was found, a mining camp appeared. At first, camps were just groups of tents and brush shelters. As more people arrived, men built log cabins. A camp might grow to hundreds or even thousands of people in a few months. Then, if the gold ran out, it might be empty again just as quickly. The camps had colourful names. There were places called Rough and Ready, Hangtown, Fiddletown, You Bet and Whiskey Flat. Some names described the place. Others came from events or jokes. Even so, life in the camps was simple. There were few comforts and almost no women or children. Yet the camps were also lively. Each had its own store, saloon and boarding house. Some later grew into proper towns. Hangtown, for example, became the town of Placerville, which still exists today.
===
IMG: miners-cabin-interior-bunks-fireplace-1850 | an indoor scene, enclosed by rough log walls, a low wooden ceiling, the inside of a small miners' cabin in California in 1850 with wooden bunks, a stone fireplace, a table with tin plates, a gold pan and pick leaning in the corner, warm firelight
HEAD: Miners often lived together in small log cabins, sharing the work, the cooking and the gold.
BODY: Most miners did not work alone. They formed small groups called partnerships. Partners shared the hard work of mining. They also shared their cabin, their food and often their gold. A typical cabin was small and simple. It had walls of logs or rough boards. The roof was made of planks, canvas or bark. There was a stone fireplace for cooking and warmth. Bunks were built against the walls. A table and a few stools stood in the middle. Tools, guns and clothes hung on pegs. Even so, the cabins were often damp and draughty. In winter, rain poured through gaps in the roof. Yet many miners grew fond of their cabins. They became home. In the evenings, partners sat by the fire, smoking pipes, telling stories and making plans. They talked about their families, their hopes and what they would do when they struck it rich.
===
IMG: miners-cooking-beans-bacon-campfire-tin-plates | a few gold miners in flannel shirts and boots cooking beans and bacon in an iron pot over a campfire outside a log cabin in the evening, tin plates and coffee pot nearby, warm firelight
HEAD: Most miners lived on a simple diet of bread, beans, bacon and coffee.
BODY: Food in the mining camps was simple and often monotonous. Most miners cooked for themselves. Their usual meals were bread, beans, salt pork or bacon, and strong coffee. They made flapjacks, a kind of pancake, from flour and water. Fresh fruit and vegetables were rare and very expensive. As a result, many miners suffered from scurvy, especially in the early years. Some ate wild plants to stay healthy. One of these was a small green plant now called miner's lettuce. It grew in damp, shady places. Miners learned to eat it from Native Californians. It was rich in vitamin C. Even so, cooking was not every miner's favourite task. Many were young men who had never cooked before. Their first attempts were often a disaster. Yet over time, they learned. Some became quite proud of their cooking. A good cook was very welcome in any partnership.
===
IMG: sunday-camp-miners-washing-clothes-river | gold miners washing their shirts in a clear river on a sunny Sunday, clothes hanging on bushes to dry, others resting, reading letters or mending boots outside their tents, soft warm light
HEAD: Sunday was the miners' day of rest, used for washing clothes, writing letters and going to town.
BODY: Miners worked hard six days a week. Sunday was different. Most miners did not dig for gold on Sundays. Instead, they rested and did other jobs. They washed their clothes in the river. They mended their boots and shirts. They baked bread for the coming week. Many walked to the nearest town to buy supplies and collect mail. Towns were busiest on Sundays. Stores, saloons and auctions were crowded with miners. Some attended church services, held by travelling preachers in tents or under trees. Others wrote long letters home. Even so, Sunday was not always peaceful. Saloons and gambling tents did good business. Yet for many miners, it was the one day they could think of something other than gold. They read their Bibles, rested their aching bodies and remembered their families far away. The quiet of Sunday morning in a mining camp was something many later remembered fondly.
===
IMG: miners-dance-no-women-fiddle-cabin-evening | gold miners in flannel shirts dancing together to a fiddle in front of a log cabin on a summer evening, some wearing a patch of cloth on their sleeve, others clapping and laughing, warm firelight
HEAD: Because there were so few women in the camps, miners danced with each other at their evening gatherings.
BODY: Women were very rare in the early mining camps. In some camps, there were none at all. Yet the miners still liked to dance. So they danced with each other. At a miners' dance, some men took the role of women. To show who they were, they tied a patch of cloth or a handkerchief on their arm. Fiddlers played lively tunes. Men clapped, stamped and swung each other around. Visitors found these dances both funny and touching. The dances were a way to forget hard work and loneliness for a while. Even so, the miners missed the company of women and families. When a woman did arrive in a camp, it was big news. Men came from miles around just to see her. Some offered her gifts. Yet the dances showed the cheerfulness of the miners. Far from home, without their families, they still found ways to laugh, play and enjoy each other's company.
===
IMG: rough-and-ready-town-small-street-1850 | a tiny mining town in California in 1850 with a single dusty street of wooden buildings, a general store, a saloon and a hotel, pine-covered hills behind, warm afternoon light
HEAD: In 1850, the mining town of Rough and Ready briefly declared itself an independent nation.
BODY: One of the strangest stories of the gold rush comes from a camp called Rough and Ready. In 1850, its miners were angry. They disliked a new mining tax, and they felt that the government did nothing to help them. So, in April 1850, they held a meeting. They voted to leave the United States and form their own independent nation. They called it the Great Republic of Rough and Ready. They even chose a president. Even so, the republic did not last long. As the Fourth of July approached, the miners realised something. If they were no longer Americans, they could not celebrate Independence Day. That seemed wrong. So they voted to rejoin the United States. The republic had lasted less than three months. Yet the story is still told with a smile today. The town of Rough and Ready still exists, and it celebrates its brief independence every year.
===
TOPIC: How the gold was found
===
IMG: miner-panning-gold-river-pan-swirling | a gold miner in a wide-brimmed hat kneeling at the edge of a clear mountain river, swirling water in a shallow metal pan, small flakes of gold glinting in the bottom, soft golden morning light
HEAD: The simplest way to find gold was with a shallow metal pan.
BODY: In the early days of the rush, most gold was found near the surface. It lay in the gravel and sand of rivers and streams. The simplest tool for finding it was a pan. A gold pan was a wide, shallow metal dish. The miner scooped up some gravel and water. Then he swirled the pan gently in a circular motion. Gold is very heavy, much heavier than sand and stones. As the pan swirled, the lighter material washed over the edge. The heavy gold sank to the bottom. After many minutes of patient swirling, small flakes or grains of gold might appear. Sometimes, a lucky miner found a nugget. Even so, panning was slow, tiring work. Miners spent hours bent over in cold water. Their backs ached and their hands grew numb. Yet a skilled panner could recover a surprising amount of gold. The pan became the great symbol of the gold rush.
===
IMG: rocker-cradle-miners-working-together-stream | two gold miners working a wooden rocker box, also called a cradle, beside a stream, one pouring water and gravel into the top, the other rocking it back and forth, sunlight filtering through trees
HEAD: A wooden box called a rocker, or cradle, let miners wash much more gravel than a pan.
BODY: Panning was too slow to process large amounts of gravel. So miners used a simple machine called a rocker. It was also called a cradle, because it rocked back and forth like a baby's cradle. It was a wooden box with a sieve on top and small wooden bars, called riffles, along the bottom. One miner shovelled gravel into the sieve. Another poured water over it and rocked the box. The water washed the gravel through. Heavy gold sank and was caught behind the riffles. Lighter material washed out the end. At the end of the day, the miners collected the gold from the riffles. A rocker needed two or three men to work it. That is one reason miners formed partnerships. Even so, a rocker could process many times more gravel than a pan. Yet it still required hard physical labour. The rhythmic sound of rocking cradles filled the river valleys of California during the gold rush.
===
IMG: long-tom-sluice-wooden-channel-miners-shovelling | several miners shovelling gravel into a long wooden channel called a sluice, with water rushing through it, on the bank of a river in California in the early 1850s, pine trees and hills behind, bright daylight
HEAD: Long wooden channels called sluices allowed teams of miners to wash huge amounts of gravel.
BODY: As the easy surface gold disappeared, miners needed better methods. One was the long tom. It was a long wooden trough, about three or four metres long. Water flowed through it constantly. Miners shovelled gravel into the top end. The water washed it down the trough. Gold sank and was trapped by riffles along the bottom. An even larger version was the sluice. It was made of many wooden boxes joined together in a long line. Some sluices stretched for hundreds of metres. Teams of miners shovelled gravel into them all day. Water had to be brought from rivers and streams, often through long wooden channels called flumes. Even so, these methods needed more men, more water and more money. Yet they could recover gold from gravel that would never have been worth panning. Mining was slowly becoming an organised business, rather than a matter of luck and a single pan.
===
IMG: river-diverted-wing-dam-miners-dry-riverbed | miners working in a partly dry riverbed after the river has been turned aside by a wooden and stone dam, rocks and gravel exposed, the diverted water rushing past, warm afternoon light
HEAD: Some groups of miners turned whole rivers aside to dig for gold in the dry riverbeds.
BODY: Miners knew that the richest gold often lay at the bottom of rivers. It had settled there over thousands of years. To reach it, some groups did something bold. They built dams and channels to turn the river aside. This left part of the riverbed dry. Then they dug through the exposed gravel and rock. This kind of work was usually done in late summer and autumn, when rivers were low. Large companies of miners worked together. They built wooden dams, flumes and wing dams of logs and stones. Even so, the work was risky. If heavy rain came, the river could rise and sweep everything away. Months of work could be lost in a single night. Yet when it succeeded, it could be very rewarding. Some river-bed claims yielded great amounts of gold. The scale of the work showed how determined the miners were, and how much they would risk for the chance of a rich find.
===
IMG: assay-scales-gold-dust-weighing-store-counter | small brass scales weighing a little pile of gold dust on the wooden counter of a general store in a California mining town in 1850, a leather pouch beside it, warm lamplight
HEAD: In the mining camps, gold dust was used as money, measured with small scales on every shop counter.
BODY: In the gold fields, there were few coins. So miners used gold dust as money. Every store, saloon and boarding house had a small set of scales. When a miner bought something, the shopkeeper weighed out the right amount of gold dust from the miner's pouch. A pinch of gold dust, taken between thumb and finger, was a common way to pay for a drink. It was said that some barkeepers grew their fingernails long, or had large thumbs, to take bigger pinches. Gold was usually valued at about sixteen dollars an ounce. Miners carried their gold in leather pouches, in glass bottles or in the toes of old socks. Even so, using gold dust as money had problems. Scales could be dishonest. Some people mixed brass filings or sand with the gold. Yet the system worked well enough. Later, private companies and then the government began to mint gold coins in San Francisco.
===
TOPIC: Up the rivers to the diggings
===
IMG: riverboat-steamer-sacramento-river-1850 | a white wooden paddle steamboat with a tall smokestack carrying passengers up a wide calm river lined with tall reeds and oak trees in California in 1850, soft golden afternoon light
HEAD: From San Francisco, gold seekers travelled up the rivers by steamboat to Sacramento and Stockton.
BODY: San Francisco was only the first stop. The gold fields lay more than a hundred miles inland. To get there, most people travelled by water. Small sailing boats and, soon, paddle steamers carried passengers up the Sacramento and San Joaquin rivers. The trip took about a day. Steamboats were crowded with miners, their tools and their supplies. The fares were high at first, but competition soon lowered them. Some steamboats raced each other, which could be dangerous. Boilers sometimes exploded. Along the way, passengers saw wide marshes, flocks of birds and herds of elk. The boats arrived at two growing river towns. Sacramento served the northern mines. Stockton served the southern mines. Even so, the journey was not over. From these towns, gold seekers continued on foot, by mule or by wagon into the hills. Yet the river steamers were the first great link between the coast and the gold country.
===
IMG: sacramento-1849-tents-river-front-boats | the riverfront of Sacramento in 1849 with rows of canvas tents and wooden warehouses beside a broad river, sailing boats and steamboats tied up along the bank, people unloading goods, soft afternoon light
HEAD: Sacramento grew almost overnight beside Sutter's Fort, into a busy town of tents and warehouses.
BODY: Sacramento lay where the American River meets the Sacramento River, close to Sutter's Fort. In 1848, there was little there. Then the gold rush began. Sutter's son laid out a town on the riverbank. Lots were sold quickly. Within months, a busy town of tents, shacks and warehouses had grown up. Ships and steamboats unloaded goods at the riverfront. Merchants sold supplies to miners heading into the hills. Hotels, saloons and boarding houses opened. By late 1849, thousands of people lived there. Even so, the town had one great weakness. It was built on low, flat land beside two rivers. In January 1850, heavy rains caused a great flood. Much of the town was under water. People rowed boats through the streets. Yet Sacramento recovered. Later, the people raised the level of the streets by several metres to protect against floods. In 1854, Sacramento became the capital of California, which it still is today.
===
IMG: mule-train-mountain-trail-packs-supplies | a long line of pack mules loaded with boxes and sacks walking along a narrow mountain trail through pine forest in the California gold country, a driver in a wide hat leading them, soft morning light
HEAD: Long trains of pack mules carried food and tools up steep trails to the mining camps.
BODY: Many mining camps were in steep canyons and on high ridges. There were no roads for wagons. So supplies were carried by mules. A mule train might have dozens of animals, walking in a long line. Each mule carried a heavy load of flour, beans, bacon, tools, clothing or whisky. The loads were tied on with ropes and canvas. Skilled packers, often Mexican, led the trains. They knew how to balance the loads so the mules could walk safely on narrow paths. A bell mare, a horse wearing a bell, often led the line. The mules followed the sound. Even so, the trails were dangerous. Mules sometimes slipped and fell. Bad weather could stop the trains for days. Yet the mule trains were the lifeline of the mining camps. When a train arrived, the whole camp came out to meet it. It meant fresh supplies, news and letters from the outside world.
===
IMG: concord-stagecoach-dusty-road-oak-hills | a red and yellow wooden stagecoach pulled by six horses along a dusty road through golden hills dotted with oak trees in California in the 1850s, the driver holding the reins, soft late afternoon light
HEAD: By the 1850s, stagecoaches carried passengers, mail and gold along the dusty roads of the gold country.
BODY: As the gold country developed, roads were built between the towns. Stagecoach lines began to run on them. The most famous coaches were made in Concord, New Hampshire. They were strong, painted in bright colours and hung on leather straps instead of springs. This made them rock gently, like a cradle. Six horses usually pulled them. Up to nine passengers sat inside, and more rode on the roof. The driver sat on top at the front. He was called a whip or a jehu. Good drivers were famous for their skill. They could guide six horses down narrow mountain roads at speed. Even so, the journey was uncomfortable. Passengers were thrown about and covered in dust. Yet stagecoaches were the fastest way to travel overland. One of the best known California drivers was Charley Parkhurst. Only after Charley died did people learn that Charley had been born a woman.
===
IMG: river-ferry-crossing-wagon-raft-rope | a simple wooden ferry raft carrying a wagon and horses across a wide green river, pulled along a rope stretched from bank to bank, a ferryman working the rope, oak trees on the shore, soft afternoon light
HEAD: Enterprising settlers built ferries and toll bridges across the rivers and charged miners to cross.
BODY: The gold country was crossed by many rivers. In spring, they were deep, cold and fast. Crossing them was dangerous. So some people saw a business opportunity. They built ferries and charged travellers to cross. A typical ferry was a flat wooden raft. It was pulled across the river along a strong rope stretched from bank to bank. It could carry people, horses, mules and even wagons. Later, some ferry owners built wooden toll bridges. Fees could be very high, especially in the busy years. Some ferry owners became rich. They earned more from the miners than the miners earned from the gold. Even so, the ferries provided a real service. Without them, many people would have drowned trying to swim or wade across. Yet travellers often grumbled about the prices. Many place names in the gold country still remember these old crossings, in names that end in Ferry or Bridge.
