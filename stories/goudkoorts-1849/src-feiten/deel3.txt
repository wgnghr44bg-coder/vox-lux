TOPIC: Prices and shopkeepers
===
IMG: general-store-mining-town-shelves-goods-1850 | an indoor scene, enclosed by wooden walls, a plain ceiling, a crowded general store in a California mining town in 1850 with shelves of tins, barrels of flour, picks, shovels, boots and blankets, a shopkeeper behind the counter with scales, warm lamplight
HEAD: In the gold fields, prices were so high that a single egg could cost a dollar or more.
BODY: The gold rush brought huge numbers of people to California very quickly. There were not enough goods for all of them. Almost everything had to be brought in by ship or by mule train. As a result, prices rose to astonishing levels. In some camps, a single egg cost a dollar or more. A loaf of bread might cost as much. A pair of boots could cost many times its price in New York. Shovels, picks and pans were also very expensive. A miner might find a good amount of gold in a day and still spend most of it on food and supplies. Even so, prices changed quickly. When a new shipment arrived, prices could fall overnight. When supplies ran short, they rose again. Yet for many miners, the high prices were a shock. They had imagined that their gold would make them rich. Instead, much of it went straight into the pockets of the shopkeepers.
===
IMG: levi-strauss-dry-goods-store-san-francisco-1853 | an indoor scene, enclosed by brick walls, a plain ceiling, a dry goods store in San Francisco in the 1850s with bolts of canvas and denim cloth on shelves, a young merchant with a beard showing fabric to a miner, soft window light
HEAD: A young merchant named Levi Strauss arrived in San Francisco in 1853 and later made the first riveted blue jeans.
BODY: Levi Strauss was born in Bavaria, in Germany, in 1829. As a young man, he emigrated to New York, where his brothers ran a dry goods business. In 1853, he moved to San Francisco to open a branch of the family business. He sold cloth, clothing and other goods to shops in the mining towns. Miners needed strong clothes that would not tear. Years later, a tailor in Nevada named Jacob Davis had an idea. He put small copper rivets on the pockets of work trousers to make them stronger. Davis bought his cloth from Levi Strauss. In 1873, the two men received a patent for riveted work trousers. They were made of a sturdy blue cotton cloth called denim. These were the first blue jeans. Even so, they were made a little after the gold rush itself. Yet they grew from the needs of the mining world. Today, jeans are worn all over the world.
===
IMG: studebaker-wheelbarrow-workshop-placerville | an indoor scene, enclosed by wooden walls, a plain ceiling, a small workshop in Placerville in the 1850s with a young craftsman building sturdy wooden wheelbarrows, tools on the walls, sawdust on the floor, warm window light
HEAD: Several famous American businesses began by selling to the gold miners.
BODY: Some of the most successful people of the gold rush never found much gold. Instead, they sold goods or services to the miners. In Placerville, a young man named John Studebaker built wheelbarrows for miners. He saved his money and later joined his brothers in their wagon business in Indiana. The Studebaker company became one of the largest wagon makers in America. Much later, it turned to motor vehicles. In Sacramento, a merchant named Leland Stanford ran a store selling supplies. He later became governor of California, built a great railway and founded Stanford University. Another young man, Philip Armour, dug water ditches for miners. He saved his earnings and later built a huge meat business in Chicago. Even so, these were exceptional cases. Many shopkeepers also failed. Yet the stories show a lesson of the gold rush. The surest way to profit was often to supply the people searching for gold, rather than to search for it.
===
IMG: wells-fargo-office-gold-shipments-strongbox | an indoor scene, enclosed by wooden walls, a plain ceiling, a Wells Fargo express office in a California town in the 1850s with a clerk weighing gold, a green strongbox on the counter, a large safe in the corner, warm lamplight
HEAD: Wells Fargo, founded in 1852, carried gold, mail and money across California.
BODY: Miners needed a safe way to send their gold to banks and their money home to their families. They also needed a way to send and receive letters. In 1852, two businessmen, Henry Wells and William Fargo, founded a new company. It was called Wells, Fargo and Company. It opened offices in San Francisco and in mining towns across California. The company bought gold dust from miners and shipped it to San Francisco. It carried letters and packages. It also offered banking services. Its agents became trusted figures in many small towns. Stagecoaches carried the mail and the company's famous green wooden strongboxes. Even so, the work could be dangerous. Robbers sometimes held up the stagecoaches. Yet the company built a reputation for honesty and reliability. When robbers stole a shipment, it often repaid its customers. Wells Fargo grew into one of the largest banks in the United States.
===
IMG: boarding-house-long-table-miners-dinner | an indoor scene, enclosed by wooden walls, a plain ceiling, a crowded boarding house dining room in a mining town in 1850 with miners in rough clothes eating at a long wooden table, a woman serving food from large dishes, warm lamplight
HEAD: Boarding houses offered miners a bed and hot meals, for a high price.
BODY: Not every miner wanted to cook for himself or live in a tent. In the towns, boarding houses offered an alternative. A boarding house was a building where people rented a bed and received meals. The rooms were often crowded. Several men might share one room, sleeping in bunks or on the floor. Meals were served at a long table. All the boarders ate together. The food was simple but filling. There was meat, bread, potatoes, beans and coffee. For miners who had been living on their own cooking, a hot meal at a boarding house was a treat. Prices were high, often several dollars a day. Many boarding houses were run by women. Even so, the business was hard work. Cooking for dozens of hungry men every day took great effort. Yet it could be very profitable. Some women who ran boarding houses earned more than the miners who stayed with them.

TOPIC: People from every land
===
IMG: miners-many-nations-river-chinese-chilean-french | a group of gold miners of many backgrounds working along a California river in 1850, including Chinese miners in wide straw hats, Mexican and Chilean miners in ponchos and Americans in flannel shirts, soft morning light
HEAD: Gold seekers came to California from all over the world, including China, Chile, Mexico, France and Australia.
BODY: The gold rush drew people from every part of the globe. Americans came from every state. Thousands came from Mexico, especially from the mining state of Sonora. Chileans and Peruvians sailed north. Many brought valuable mining experience. From Europe came the French, Germans, Irish, Italians and British. Australians crossed the Pacific. Hawaiians came too. From China came tens of thousands of people, mainly from the southern province of Guangdong. In the mining camps, many languages could be heard. People cooked different foods and celebrated different holidays. Even so, life together was not always peaceful. Many American miners resented foreigners. They wanted the gold for themselves. Yet the mix of peoples changed California forever. It became one of the most diverse places in the world. San Francisco, in particular, grew into a truly international city in just a few years.
===
IMG: chinese-miners-wide-hats-working-claim-river | Chinese miners in wide straw hats and loose blue cotton clothing carefully working an old gold claim along a quiet river in California in the 1850s, wooden rockers and baskets, soft afternoon light
HEAD: Chinese immigrants called California Gam Saan, which means Gold Mountain.
BODY: News of the gold reached southern China in 1848 and 1849. At that time, the region was suffering from war, floods and poverty. Many young men decided to try their luck in California. They called it Gam Saan, which means Gold Mountain in Cantonese. By the early 1850s, thousands were arriving each year. Many worked in the gold fields. They often formed companies and worked claims that others had abandoned. They were patient and careful, and they often found gold that others had missed. Others opened shops, laundries and restaurants. San Francisco's Chinatown grew quickly. Even so, Chinese miners faced harsh discrimination. They were taxed heavily and sometimes driven from their claims. Yet they stayed and built communities. Later, many Chinese workers helped build the railway across the mountains. Today, San Francisco's Chinatown is one of the oldest and largest Chinese communities outside Asia.
===
IMG: foreign-miners-tax-collector-camp-1850 | an official in a dark coat and hat collecting money from a group of miners in front of a small tent at a mining camp in 1850, some miners looking unhappy, soft afternoon light
HEAD: In 1850, California passed a tax on foreign miners that drove thousands of them from the gold fields.
BODY: As the gold rush grew, competition for good claims became fierce. Many American miners believed that the gold should belong to Americans. In 1850, the new state of California passed a law called the Foreign Miners' Tax. It required miners who were not American citizens to pay twenty dollars a month. That was a very large sum. The tax was aimed mainly at Mexican, Chilean and other Latin American miners. Many could not or would not pay. Thousands left the gold fields. Some returned home. The tax was so unpopular, and so damaging to trade in the mining towns, that it was cancelled the following year. Even so, a new tax was introduced in 1852, aimed mainly at Chinese miners. Yet the story of these taxes is an important one. It shows how fear and greed could lead to unfair treatment. It is a reminder that the gold rush was not equally open to everyone who came.
===
IMG: french-miners-camp-flag-tricolour-1850 | a camp of French gold miners in California in 1850 with tents and a small French flag on a pole, men cooking and talking, a wine bottle on a table, wooded hills behind, soft evening light
HEAD: Thousands of French gold seekers sailed to California, some of them sent by a lottery in Paris.
BODY: France also caught gold fever. In 1849 and the early 1850s, thousands of French people sailed to California. Some were adventurers. Others had been left poor by a revolution and economic troubles at home. A famous lottery in Paris, called the Lottery of the Golden Ingots, used its profits to send several thousand people to California. The French government saw it partly as a way to send away unemployed and restless men. In California, the French settled in San Francisco and in the mining camps. Many found work as cooks, bakers, wine sellers and shopkeepers. French restaurants appeared in San Francisco. Even so, French miners often faced hostility, like other foreigners. Yet they left their mark. San Francisco gained a French hospital, French newspapers and French bakeries. The French community became an important part of the city's life. Some French families who came during the gold rush stayed for many generations.
===
IMG: african-american-miner-freedom-california-1850 | an African American gold miner in a wide hat and work clothes standing proudly beside his claim on a sunny California hillside in 1850, a pan and shovel at his feet, soft warm light
HEAD: Some African Americans found gold in California and used it to buy freedom for themselves or their families.
BODY: Among the gold seekers were about two thousand African Americans by the early 1850s. Some were free men from the northern states. Others had been brought to California as enslaved people by their owners. California joined the United States in 1850 as a free state, where slavery was not allowed. Yet the law was not always enforced. Some enslaved people worked in the mines and were allowed to keep part of their earnings. A few used the money to buy their freedom. Some bought the freedom of family members still in the South. Free Black miners also worked claims and ran businesses. In San Francisco, a small but active Black community formed. They founded churches, newspapers and organisations to fight for equal rights. Even so, they faced discrimination and unjust laws. Yet their stories are an important part of the gold rush. For some, California offered a hope of freedom that could not be found elsewhere.
===
TOPIC: Women in the gold fields
===
IMG: luzena-wilson-cooking-biscuits-camp-stove | a pioneer woman in a simple 1840s dress and apron baking biscuits on an iron stove outside a rough cabin in a mining camp, a bearded miner standing nearby holding a coin, soft morning light
HEAD: A pioneer woman named Luzena Wilson was offered five dollars for one of her biscuits.
BODY: Luzena Stanley Wilson travelled to California by wagon in 1849, with her husband and two small sons. When they arrived in Sacramento, they had very little money. One evening, she was cooking supper over a campfire. A hungry miner came by. He smelled her biscuits and offered her five dollars for one. That was a huge sum. She could hardly believe it. He gave her the money, and she gave him the biscuit. She realised that she could earn a living by cooking. She later opened a boarding house and hotel. She fed many miners and became quite prosperous. Even so, her life was not easy. She lost her business twice, once to flood and once to fire. Yet each time, she started again. Many years later, she told her story to her daughter, who wrote it down. Her memoir is one of the best accounts of a woman's life in the gold rush.
===
IMG: dame-shirley-writing-letters-cabin-rich-bar | an indoor scene, enclosed by log walls, a low wooden ceiling, an educated young woman in a neat 1850s dress writing a letter at a small table in a rough log cabin in a mining camp, a candle and a few books, warm soft light
HEAD: A woman known as Dame Shirley wrote famous letters describing life in a mining camp.
BODY: Louise Clappe was a well-educated woman from New England. In 1851, she moved with her husband, a doctor, to a remote mining camp called Rich Bar. It lay deep in the mountains on the Feather River. She was one of only a handful of women there. Over the next year, she wrote a series of long letters to her sister back east. She signed them with the name Dame Shirley. Her letters were witty, observant and full of detail. She described the cabins, the miners, the food and the landscape. She wrote about happy times and sad ones. She described fires, accidents, quarrels and celebrations. Her letters were later published in a San Francisco magazine. Even so, she never became rich. Her marriage ended, and she later worked as a teacher in San Francisco. Yet her letters are treasured today. Historians consider them one of the finest first-hand accounts of daily life in the gold rush camps.
===
IMG: woman-washing-laundry-business-mining-town | a woman in a long dress and apron washing shirts in large tubs beside a stream in a California mining town in the 1850s, lines of clean shirts hanging to dry in the sun, soft warm light
HEAD: Women who washed laundry for miners could earn more money than many of the miners themselves.
BODY: There were very few women in the early gold rush years. In 1850, women made up only a small fraction of California's population. In the mining areas, the share was even smaller. This meant that women's work was in great demand. Miners needed their clothes washed, their meals cooked and their socks mended. Most men had never done these tasks. Women who offered these services could charge high prices. Washing a dozen shirts could earn several dollars. Some women ran laundries, bakeries, boarding houses and stores. They often earned more than the miners they served. Even so, the work was exhausting. It meant long days of scrubbing, cooking and carrying water. Yet it gave women an unusual degree of independence. Some women earned their own money for the first time in their lives. A few became wealthy business owners. The gold rush gave them opportunities that were rare elsewhere.
===
IMG: family-arriving-mining-town-wagon-children | a family with a mother, father and two young children arriving in a small mining town in a wagon in the early 1850s, miners stopping to look and wave, wooden buildings and pine hills, soft golden afternoon light
HEAD: When women and children began arriving in the camps, the mining towns started to change.
BODY: In the first years of the gold rush, the mining camps were almost entirely male. Then, slowly, more families arrived. Some wives travelled out to join husbands who had decided to stay. Others came with their families from the beginning. When women and children arrived, the camps began to change. Families wanted proper houses, schools and churches. They wanted shops that sold more than picks and whisky. They wanted laws and order. Many camps grew into real towns with schools, churches, libraries and newspapers. Miners who had lived rough for years began to build cabins with gardens. Even so, the change was gradual. Some camps remained rough places for many years. Yet the arrival of families marked the end of the wildest days of the rush. Many towns founded in the gold rush still exist today. Their old churches and schoolhouses show how families turned temporary camps into lasting homes.

TOPIC: Letters home
===
IMG: miners-crowd-post-office-waiting-mail-line | a long line of gold miners in rough clothes and hats waiting outside a small wooden post office in a California town in 1850, some reading letters, others looking eagerly at the door, soft morning light
HEAD: Miners would stand in line for hours at the post office, hoping for a letter from home.
BODY: For miners far from home, letters were precious. They were the only link with their families. In the early days, mail took a very long time to reach California. Letters came by ship, either around Cape Horn or across Panama. They could take two months or more. When the mail ship arrived in San Francisco, the post office was overwhelmed. Long lines of men formed outside, stretching down the street. Some waited all night. Men who did not want to wait could buy a place near the front of the line from someone who had queued. In the mining camps, private express riders carried letters from the cities. Miners often paid a dollar or more for each letter. Even so, many letters never arrived. Some were lost at sea. Others went to the wrong camp. Yet when a letter did arrive, it was a moment of great joy. Men read their letters again and again until the paper wore thin.
===
IMG: miner-writing-letter-candle-cabin-night | an indoor scene, enclosed by log walls, a low wooden ceiling, a young gold miner with a beard writing a letter by candlelight at a rough wooden table in his cabin at night, a pan and boots in the corner, warm soft candlelight
HEAD: Many miners' letters survive, full of hope, homesickness and honest descriptions of their hard lives.
BODY: Miners wrote many letters home. Thousands of them survive today, kept by families and libraries. They describe life in the gold fields in vivid detail. Some letters are full of excitement. They describe a lucky find or a promising new claim. Others are honest about the hardships. Writers tell of cold, rain, sickness, high prices and disappointment. Many letters express deep homesickness. Miners asked about their children, their parents and their farms. They described dreams of home. Some apologised for not sending more money. Others promised they would return soon. Even so, many miners tried to sound cheerful, so their families would not worry. Yet behind their words, the loneliness is often clear. Some letters were written by men who later died in California. Their families treasured these last messages for the rest of their lives. Together, the letters form one of the richest records of the gold rush.
===
IMG: lettersheet-illustrated-gold-rush-drawing | an old illustrated letter sheet from the 1850s with a printed drawing of a mining camp at the top and handwritten lines below, lying on a wooden table beside an ink bottle and a quill, warm lamplight
HEAD: Miners often wrote home on special printed letter sheets with pictures of life in the gold fields.
BODY: In the 1850s, printers in San Francisco created a special kind of writing paper. These were called lettersheets. At the top of each sheet was a printed picture. It might show a mining camp, a view of San Francisco or a scene of miners at work. Some pictures were serious. Others were humorous, showing the troubles of miners in a funny way. Below the picture, there was space to write a letter. Miners bought these sheets to show their families what life in California looked like. In a time before photographs were common, these pictures were very valuable. Families back east could finally see the places their loved ones described. Even so, the pictures were sometimes idealised. They made life look neater and more cheerful than it was. Yet they are treasured by historians today. Hundreds of different designs were printed. They give a colourful picture of how the gold rush wanted to see itself.
===
IMG: daguerreotype-miner-portrait-pick-pan-studio | an old photograph-style portrait of a young gold miner in the 1850s posing in a small studio with a pick over his shoulder and a pan in his hand, a serious expression, soft daylight
HEAD: Many miners had their photographs taken to send home, often posing proudly with their picks and pans.
BODY: Photography was still quite new in the 1850s. The most common method was the daguerreotype, which made a single image on a silver-coated plate. Photographers set up studios in San Francisco and in larger mining towns. Many miners visited them. They wanted to send a picture of themselves home to their families. They often posed with their tools, such as picks, shovels and pans. Some held up nuggets or bags of gold. Others wore their best clothes. Some posed with their partners. Some travelling photographers took pictures of miners at work along the rivers. Even so, sitting for a photograph took patience. Subjects had to stay still for several seconds. Yet the results were remarkable. Hundreds of these images survive today. They show the real faces of the gold rush. Young men stare out from the past, tired, proud and hopeful, wanting their families to see them as they were.

TOPIC: Winter in the camps
===
IMG: mining-camp-winter-rain-mud-cabins-smoke | a small gold mining camp of log cabins in a steep valley on a grey rainy winter day, smoke rising from chimneys, a swollen muddy river below, misty pine trees, soft muted light
HEAD: In winter, heavy rain and snow could stop mining for weeks and cut camps off from supplies.
BODY: Winter in the gold country was very different from summer. Summers were hot and dry. Winters brought heavy rain in the foothills and deep snow in the higher mountains. Rivers rose and flooded. Many river claims could not be worked. The winter of 1849 to 1850 was especially wet. Trails turned into mud. Mule trains could not get through to some camps. Supplies ran low, and prices soared. Some miners went down to the cities to wait for spring. Others stayed in their cabins. They read, wrote letters, played cards and repaired their tools. Some worked dry diggings, away from the rivers, when the weather allowed. Even so, winter could be lonely and difficult. Illness spread in the damp, crowded cabins. Yet the rain also brought something good. When spring came, the rivers had washed down fresh gravel. New gold might be waiting in the streams.
===
IMG: miners-reading-newspapers-cabin-winter-evening | an indoor scene, enclosed by log walls, a low wooden ceiling, three miners sitting close to a stone fireplace in a cabin on a winter evening, one reading a newspaper aloud, another mending a boot, rain on the window, warm firelight
HEAD: On long winter evenings, miners read every newspaper and book they could find, sometimes aloud to each other.
BODY: Reading material was precious in the mining camps. Newspapers from San Francisco and the eastern states were eagerly passed from cabin to cabin. Some sold for a dollar or more. Men read them over and over, even the advertisements. Often, one miner read aloud while the others listened. Books were also shared. Bibles, novels, poems and old magazines travelled from camp to camp. Some miners kept small libraries in their cabins. In larger towns, reading rooms and lending libraries opened. Theatre companies and travelling lecturers also visited. Even so, many miners could not read well. Their friends read for them. Yet reading brought comfort on long, wet winter nights. It linked the miners to the wider world. A good story, read by the fire while the rain fell outside, helped many forget the cold, the mud and the loneliness of camp life for a while.
===
IMG: christmas-mining-camp-cabin-small-tree-candles | an indoor scene, enclosed by log walls, a low wooden ceiling, miners celebrating Christmas in a small cabin with a tiny pine tree decorated with candles and ribbons, a simple feast on the table, warm golden candlelight
HEAD: Miners celebrated Christmas in their cabins with whatever treats they could find.
BODY: Christmas was a special time even in the mining camps. Many miners felt their homesickness most strongly at Christmas. They thought of family dinners and church services far away. So they tried to make the day special. Some cut small pine trees and decorated them with candles or ribbons. Partners prepared a feast. They might cook a wild turkey, venison or a precious tin of oysters. Some made puddings or pies. Others bought special treats from the store, whatever the price. Some camps held dances or parties. Miners sang carols and Christmas songs. Even so, some Christmases were lonely. Snow or rain might keep men in their cabins. Yet many miners wrote warmly about their Christmas celebrations. In their letters home, they described simple feasts and kind companions. Far from family, they created small, warm holidays together in the rough cabins of the gold country.
===
TOPIC: Luck, music and the wild land
===
IMG: large-gold-nugget-hand-dark-cloth-lamp | a large rough gold nugget held in a weathered hand above a dark cloth, glowing softly in warm lamplight, an indoor scene, enclosed by wooden walls, a plain ceiling
HEAD: In 1854, miners at Carson Hill found a single mass of gold weighing about one hundred and ninety-five pounds.
BODY: Most miners found gold as small flakes and grains. Yet every miner dreamed of finding a large nugget. A few lucky people did. The largest mass of gold ever found in California came from Carson Hill, in Calaveras County. It was discovered in 1854. It weighed about one hundred and ninety-five pounds, or nearly ninety kilograms. It was worth a fortune at the time. Smaller nuggets of several pounds were found in many places. News of a big find spread quickly through the camps. Men rushed to stake claims nearby, hoping to find more. Even so, such finds were very rare. Most miners never found a nugget larger than a fingernail. Yet the stories of great nuggets kept the dream alive. They encouraged miners to keep digging through the long, hard days. Sadly, most of the great California nuggets were melted down for coins. Very few survive today. Their size is known only from old records.
===
IMG: miner-sitting-empty-pan-disappointed-river-dusk | a tired gold miner sitting on a rock beside a river at dusk with an empty pan in his lap, looking at the water, pine trees darkening behind him, soft fading light
HEAD: Most forty-niners earned little more than they would have earned at home, and many lost money.
BODY: The gold rush is often remembered as a time of great fortunes. Yet for most people, the reality was very different. In 1848 and early 1849, some miners did make large amounts of money. The gold was easy to find. But as more people arrived, the best claims were taken. The easy gold was soon gone. By 1850 and 1851, the average miner earned only a few dollars a day. That sounds like a lot compared with wages in the east. Yet prices in California were so high that little was left over. Many miners went into debt. Some fell ill and could not work. Others gave up and went home with nothing. Even so, not everyone regretted the adventure. Many said they had learned a great deal. Yet the gold rush made only a small number of people truly rich. Most of the gold found its way into the hands of merchants, bankers and the owners of large mining companies.
===
IMG: lola-montez-house-grass-valley-garden | a small white wooden house with a porch and a pretty garden of flowers in the gold rush town of Grass Valley in the 1850s, a tall pine tree beside it, soft warm afternoon light
HEAD: The famous dancer Lola Montez lived for a time in the mining town of Grass Valley.
BODY: The gold rush attracted entertainers of every kind. One of the most famous was Lola Montez. She was born in Ireland, but she presented herself as a Spanish dancer. She had become famous in Europe for her daring Spider Dance. She had even been the companion of the King of Bavaria. In 1853, she arrived in California. She performed in San Francisco and in the mining towns. Then she settled for a while in Grass Valley. She bought a small house with a garden. She kept pets, including a bear cub, and entertained visitors. The miners were fascinated by her. Even so, she was not only a performer. She encouraged a young girl in the town named Lotta Crabtree, who loved to sing and dance. Lotta later became one of the richest and most popular entertainers in America. A copy of Lola Montez's house now stands on the old spot in Grass Valley. It is used as a visitor centre for the town.
===
IMG: grizzly-bear-meadow-sierra-foothills-1850 | a large California grizzly bear walking slowly through a meadow of tall golden grass and wild flowers in the Sierra foothills, oak trees and pine forest behind, soft warm evening light
HEAD: California grizzly bears were common in the gold country, and one still appears on the state flag.
BODY: In 1849, California was home to many grizzly bears. They were large, powerful animals. Some weighed more than five hundred kilograms. They lived in the valleys, foothills and mountains. They ate grass, acorns, berries, salmon and sometimes cattle. Miners often met them. Many wrote about these encounters in their letters. Some miners hunted bears for meat and fur. Others captured them for shows. In some places, bears were made to fight bulls for entertainment, a cruel sport that was later banned. As more people arrived, the bears lost their homes and were hunted heavily. Even so, the grizzly became a symbol of California. A bear appears on the state flag, first raised in 1846. Yet the real California grizzly did not survive. The last known one was killed in the 1920s. Today, the bear lives on only on the flag, a quiet reminder of the wild land the forty-niners found.
===
IMG: salmon-run-clear-river-gold-country-autumn | silver salmon swimming upstream in a clear rocky river in the Sierra foothills in autumn, golden leaves on the trees along the banks, soft warm light
HEAD: Before mining muddied them, the rivers of the gold country were full of salmon.
BODY: The rivers that flowed down from the Sierra Nevada were once rich with fish. Every year, great runs of salmon swam up from the sea to lay their eggs in clear gravel beds. Native Californians had fished them for thousands of years. Early miners also caught salmon for food. Some wrote about rivers so full of fish that they could be caught by hand. Yet mining changed the rivers. Digging, damming and washing gravel filled the water with mud and sand. Later, hydraulic mining sent millions of tonnes of debris downstream. The gravel beds where salmon laid their eggs were buried. Fish numbers fell sharply. Even so, the rivers have slowly recovered in some places. Efforts have been made to restore salmon runs. Yet the old abundance has never fully returned. The story of the salmon shows how the search for gold affected not only people, but the whole living landscape of California.
===
IMG: sierra-nevada-meadow-wildflowers-mountains-dawn | a high mountain meadow in the Sierra Nevada filled with colourful wild flowers, granite peaks and pine forests around it, a clear stream winding through, soft pink dawn light
HEAD: Some forty-niners were so moved by the beauty of the Sierra Nevada that they wrote more about the scenery than about gold.
BODY: The Sierra Nevada is one of the most beautiful mountain ranges in America. It has granite peaks, deep valleys, clear lakes and huge trees. Many forty-niners had never seen anything like it. In their letters and diaries, they described the mountains with wonder. They wrote about giant pines, roaring waterfalls and meadows full of flowers. In 1851, a group of men chasing Native people entered a deep valley with tall granite walls. It was the Yosemite Valley. Some of them were struck by its beauty. A few years later, the first tourists came to see it. In 1852, a hunter came across a grove of giant sequoia trees, the largest living things on earth. Even so, the miners also harmed these landscapes. Forests were cut for timber and fuel. Yet the beauty of the Sierra helped inspire a new idea. In 1864, Yosemite was protected by law, one of the first wild places in the world to be set aside for everyone.

TOPIC: After the rush
===
IMG: native-californian-village-oak-trees-acorns | a quiet Native Californian village in the Sierra foothills with round bark houses, women grinding acorns in stone mortars under large oak trees, soft warm afternoon light
HEAD: The gold rush brought devastating losses to the Native peoples of California.
BODY: Before the gold rush, perhaps one hundred and fifty thousand Native people lived in California. They belonged to many different nations, with their own languages and traditions. They lived from fishing, hunting and gathering acorns, which they ground into flour. The gold rush changed their world almost overnight. Miners took over their rivers and hills. Mining muddied the streams and destroyed the salmon runs. Forests were cut down. Game was driven away. New diseases spread quickly. Violence against Native people was common, and the state government did little to stop it. Within about twenty years, the Native population had fallen to around thirty thousand. Even so, Native Californians survived. They kept their languages, songs and traditions alive, often in very hard conditions. Yet this loss is one of the darkest parts of the gold rush story. Today, many California tribes continue to rebuild their communities and share their history.

HEAD: Thanks to the gold rush, California became a state in 1850, only two years after the discovery.
BODY: The gold rush brought so many people to California that it quickly needed a proper government. In 1849, Californians held a convention and wrote a state constitution. It banned slavery. They then asked to join the United States as a state. Normally, a territory had to wait many years before becoming a state. Yet California was growing so fast that Congress agreed quickly. On the ninth of September 1850, California became the thirty-first state. The news took more than a month to reach California by ship. When it arrived in San Francisco in October, the city held a great celebration. Bands played, guns were fired and flags waved. Even so, the decision had been difficult in Washington. California's entry as a free state upset the balance between free and slave states. It was part of a larger compromise. Yet for Californians, statehood was a proud moment. Their new home was now a full part of the nation.

IMG: hydraulic-mining-water-cannon-hillside-1860s | a powerful jet of water from a large iron nozzle washing away a reddish hillside in the California gold country in the 1860s, a few workers nearby, muddy water flowing down, soft afternoon light
HEAD: Later, miners used powerful water cannons that washed away whole hillsides to reach the gold.
BODY: By the 1850s, most easy surface gold had been found. Mining became a big business. Companies used larger and more powerful methods. One of the most dramatic was hydraulic mining. It began in 1853. Water was brought down from the mountains through long ditches and pipes. At the end, it shot out through a large iron nozzle with tremendous force. The jet of water could wash away whole hillsides. The mud and gravel were then sent through sluices to catch the gold. Hydraulic mining recovered huge amounts of gold. Yet it also caused terrible damage. Millions of tonnes of mud washed down the rivers. Riverbeds filled up, and farms and towns downstream flooded. Farmers fought back in the courts. In 1884, a judge ruled that the mines could no longer dump their waste into the rivers. This was one of the first important environmental decisions in American history.

HEAD: Neither John Sutter nor James Marshall became rich from the gold that was found on Sutter's land.
BODY: The gold discovery brought ruin to John Sutter. His workers left to search for gold. Gold seekers swarmed over his land, killed his cattle and took his property. Courts later decided that many of his land claims were not valid. Sutter lost most of what he had owned. He eventually moved east to Pennsylvania. For years, he travelled to Washington, asking Congress to pay him for his losses. It never did. He died in 1880, in a hotel in Washington, still waiting. James Marshall, who found the first gold, did not do much better. He tried mining and other businesses, but most failed. For a while, California paid him a small pension. He died poor in 1885, near Coloma. Even so, he was not forgotten. In 1890, the state built the tall statue of him that still stands above the valley. Yet both men are reminders that the gold rush did not reward everyone fairly.

HEAD: The young Mark Twain heard a story in a mining camp that became his first famous tale.
BODY: Long after the first rush, the gold country still attracted writers and storytellers. In the winter of 1864 to 1865, a young writer named Samuel Clemens stayed in the mining area of Calaveras County. He wrote under the name Mark Twain. In a tavern, he heard a funny story from an old miner. It was about a man who bet on a jumping frog. The man's rival secretly filled the frog with lead shot, so it could not jump. Twain wrote the story down in his own words. It was published in 1865 as The Celebrated Jumping Frog of Calaveras County. It became a great success and made Twain famous across America. Even so, it was only the beginning of his career. He went on to write Tom Sawyer and Huckleberry Finn. Yet he never forgot the mining camps. Today, Calaveras County still holds a frog-jumping contest every year, in honour of his story.

HEAD: Today, the old mining towns of California's gold country are linked by a road named Highway 49, after the forty-niners.
BODY: Many of the towns that grew up during the gold rush still exist today. They lie in the foothills of the Sierra Nevada, in what is now called the Gold Country. They are linked by a winding road called Highway 49, named after the forty-niners. Towns like Sonora, Nevada City, Placerville and Columbia still have their old buildings. There are brick stores with heavy iron shutters, built to resist fire. There are wooden hotels with balconies and old churches. At Columbia, the whole town is preserved as a state park. Visitors can ride a stagecoach, watch a blacksmith at work and pan for gold. Even so, many camps disappeared completely. Only a name on a map or a few stones remain. Yet the Gold Country is a peaceful place now. Its rivers run clear again in many places. Oak trees and pines cover the hills. It is hard to imagine the noise and crowds of 1849.

IMG: california-state-seal-eureka-golden-hills-sunset | rolling golden hills in California at sunset with scattered oak trees, a quiet river winding through a valley below, soft warm orange and pink light, peaceful and still
HEAD: California's state motto is "Eureka", a Greek cry of discovery.
BODY: The gold rush shaped California forever. Its population grew enormously. San Francisco became a great city. Farms, railways and new industries followed. People from all over the world settled there. In memory of the gold rush, California chose a special motto. It is the Greek word Eureka, which announces that something has been found. According to legend, the ancient Greek scientist Archimedes shouted it when he made a famous discovery. For California, it refers to the discovery of gold. The state is also known as the Golden State. Its official state mineral is gold, and the poppy, its state flower, is golden orange. Even so, most forty-niners never found the fortune they dreamed of. Yet many stayed and built new lives. Their adventure helped create one of the most important places in the world. In the golden light of a California evening, the hills still seem to glow with that old promise.
