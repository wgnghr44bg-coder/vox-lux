TOPIC: Gold at Sutter's Mill
===
IMG: sutters-sawmill-american-river-coloma-1848 | a simple wooden sawmill beside a clear rushing river in a green valley with pine-covered hills in California in 1848, a water channel running from the mill, soft early morning light
HEAD: On the twenty-fourth of January 1848, a carpenter named James Marshall found gold at a sawmill in California.
BODY: In the winter of 1847, a carpenter named James Marshall was building a sawmill. It stood beside the American River, in a valley called Coloma, in the foothills of the Sierra Nevada mountains. The mill belonged to a Swiss businessman named John Sutter. On the morning of the twenty-fourth of January 1848, Marshall walked along the channel that carried water away from the mill wheel. In the water, something glittered. He bent down and picked up a few small flakes of yellow metal. He wondered if it might be gold. He tested it by hammering it. It flattened but did not break. That was a good sign. He showed it to the workers. They tested it further by boiling it in strong soap. It stayed bright. Even so, no one was certain at first. Yet that small handful of flakes would change California, the United States and the lives of hundreds of thousands of people.
===
IMG: sutters-fort-adobe-walls-sacramento-valley | a large square fort with thick white adobe walls and a central two-storey building in a wide green valley in California in the 1840s, oak trees and a river nearby, warm afternoon light
HEAD: John Sutter had built a small empire in the Sacramento Valley, centred on a fort of adobe walls.
BODY: John Sutter was born in Germany and grew up in Switzerland. In 1834, deep in debt, he left his family and sailed to America. A few years later, he arrived in California. At that time, California belonged to Mexico. Sutter received a large grant of land in the Sacramento Valley. He called his colony New Helvetia, meaning New Switzerland. He built a large fort with thick walls of adobe, a kind of sun-dried brick. It had workshops, stores and living quarters. Native Californian workers farmed his fields and tended his cattle. Travellers arriving from the east often stopped at the fort. Sutter welcomed them and sold them supplies. He dreamed of building a great agricultural empire. The sawmill at Coloma was part of that plan. Even so, his dream depended on keeping his workers on the land. Yet the discovery of gold would soon draw almost all of them away. Sutter's Fort still stands in Sacramento today.
===
IMG: marshall-sutter-testing-gold-flakes-office-1848 | an indoor scene, enclosed by adobe walls, a plain wooden ceiling, two men in 1840s clothes bending over a small pile of gold flakes on a table in a simple office, scales and a book open beside them, warm lamplight
HEAD: Marshall rode to Sutter's Fort in the rain, and the two men tested the gold in secret.
BODY: A few days after his discovery, Marshall rode to Sutter's Fort. It was raining. He arrived soaked and asked to speak with Sutter alone. Behind a locked door, he showed Sutter the little flakes. Sutter looked up the properties of gold in an encyclopedia. The two men weighed the metal and tested it with acid. Everything showed that it was real gold. Sutter was not overjoyed. He feared what would happen if the news spread. His workers would leave to look for gold. Strangers would come and take over his land. So he asked everyone at the mill to keep the discovery secret for six weeks. Even so, a secret like this could not be kept for long. Workers wrote letters. Teamsters carrying supplies saw the gold. Yet for a few quiet weeks, life at the mill went on almost as before. The men finished their work on the sawmill and looked for gold on their days off.
===
IMG: treaty-signing-room-mexico-1848-quiet-desk | an indoor scene, enclosed by thick walls, a plain ceiling, a quiet room in Mexico in 1848 with a wooden desk, a long treaty document, quill pens and an inkwell, tall windows letting in soft daylight
HEAD: Just nine days after gold was found, Mexico signed a treaty that handed California to the United States.
BODY: The timing of the gold discovery was remarkable. In 1848, the United States and Mexico had been at war for nearly two years. American troops had occupied California and much of northern Mexico. On the second of February 1848, the two countries signed a peace treaty. It was signed at a town called Guadalupe Hidalgo, near Mexico City. Under the treaty, Mexico gave up a huge area of land. It included California, Nevada, Utah and parts of several other future states. In return, the United States paid fifteen million dollars. Neither side knew that gold had been found in California only nine days earlier. News travelled slowly in those days. Even so, the treaty would have been signed anyway. Yet many people later wondered whether Mexico would have agreed so easily if it had known. The land it gave up was about to become one of the richest places on earth.
===
IMG: native-californian-workers-river-sacramento-valley | Native Californian men in simple clothing working beside a river in the Sacramento Valley in the 1840s, oak trees and golden grass hills behind, soft warm light
HEAD: Some of the first people to dig for gold were Native Californians who worked for Sutter.
BODY: Long before the gold rush, the valleys and hills of California were home to many Native peoples. They included the Nisenan, Maidu, Miwok and many others. They had lived there for thousands of years. They gathered acorns, fished for salmon and hunted deer. Many Native people worked on Sutter's lands and at his mill. When gold was discovered, some of them were among the first to collect it. In the early months, Native Californian workers gathered much of the gold found in the region. Some ranchers and traders hired Native workers to dig for them. Even so, the gold rush brought terrible suffering to Native Californians. Their lands were overrun. Their rivers were muddied and their food sources destroyed. Disease and violence followed. Yet their story is an important part of the gold rush. Their knowledge of the land and rivers helped others, even as their own world was being taken from them.
===
IMG: coloma-valley-today-reconstructed-mill-river | a reconstructed wooden sawmill beside the clear American River in a quiet green valley with oak and pine trees, a park path and a small museum building, soft golden evening light
HEAD: Today, the site of Marshall's discovery is a peaceful state park, with a copy of the old sawmill.
BODY: The little valley of Coloma, where it all began, is now a quiet place. It is part of the Marshall Gold Discovery State Historic Park. Visitors can walk along the American River and see the spot where Marshall found the first flakes. A full-sized copy of Sutter's sawmill has been built near the original site. On some days, it is run to show how it worked. There are old buildings from the gold rush years, a small museum and shady picnic areas. Visitors can even try panning for gold in the river. On a hill above the valley stands a tall statue of James Marshall. He is pointing down towards the place where he made his famous discovery. Even so, the town that grew up here during the rush has mostly gone. Yet the valley keeps the memory alive. The river still runs clear and cool, much as it did on that January morning in 1848.

TOPIC: The news spreads
===
IMG: sam-brannan-street-san-francisco-bottle-gold-1848 | a man in a dark coat and hat holding up a small glass bottle of gold dust in a muddy street of the small town of San Francisco in 1848, wooden buildings and a few people turning to look, soft morning light
HEAD: In May 1848, a merchant named Sam Brannan ran through the streets of San Francisco shouting "Gold!"
BODY: For several months, news of the gold spread slowly. Some people heard rumours but did not believe them. Then a businessman named Samuel Brannan took action. He owned a store near Sutter's Fort. He had seen the gold for himself. In May 1848, he travelled to San Francisco. According to a famous story, he walked through the streets holding up a bottle of gold dust. He shouted that there was gold from the American River. People crowded around him. Within days, the small town began to empty. Men left their jobs, their shops and their ships to go to the gold fields. Even so, Brannan had a clever plan. Before making his announcement, he had bought up picks, shovels and pans. He sold them to the gold seekers at very high prices. Yet his trick was not unusual. Throughout the gold rush, many of those who grew richest were not miners, but those who sold them supplies.
===
IMG: empty-san-francisco-1848-closed-shops-harbour | a nearly empty small town of wooden buildings on a hillside by a bay in 1848, shops closed, a few ships at anchor with no one on deck, quiet and still, soft afternoon light
HEAD: By the summer of 1848, San Francisco was almost deserted, as nearly every man had left for the gold fields.
BODY: The effect of the news was astonishing. In the summer of 1848, towns across California emptied. In San Francisco, shops closed. Newspapers stopped printing, because their workers had gone. Soldiers deserted their posts. Sailors left their ships in the harbour. Even the town's officials joined the rush. Farmers left their crops in the fields. Teachers left their schools. One newspaper published a final issue, explaining that the whole town had gone gold hunting. Even so, at this stage, the rush was still mostly local. People came from California, Oregon, Hawaii and Mexico. They had the advantage of being close. In 1848, a hard-working miner could still find a lot of gold quite easily. Some found more in a few days than they could earn in a year. Yet in the eastern United States and in Europe, most people still knew nothing. The real flood of gold seekers had not yet begun.
===
IMG: president-polk-message-congress-washington-1848 | an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, a grand government chamber in Washington in 1848 with men in dark coats listening to a speech being read, a small box of gold displayed on a table, soft window light
HEAD: In December 1848, President Polk confirmed the gold discovery in a message to Congress.
BODY: News of the gold reached the eastern United States slowly. At first, many people thought the stories were exaggerated. Then the army sent an officer east with a small box of gold from California. In December 1848, President James Polk spoke to Congress about California. He said that the reports of gold were true. He described the large amounts being found. The gold itself was displayed in Washington. Newspapers across the country printed the news. Suddenly, everyone was talking about California. Thousands of people decided to go. They sold their farms and businesses. They borrowed money and formed companies with friends and neighbours. Even so, many people warned against the rush. They said it was madness to travel so far on the hope of gold. Yet these warnings were ignored. By early 1849, a great wave of people was preparing to head west. They would become known as the forty-niners.
===
IMG: newspaper-office-crowd-reading-gold-news-1849 | a crowd of men in 1840s coats and top hats gathered outside a newspaper office in an eastern American city in 1849, reading posted notices about California, brick buildings, soft morning light
HEAD: Gold fever spread through America and across the world in 1849.
BODY: In early 1849, the excitement was everywhere. People called it gold fever. Newspapers printed letters from California describing nuggets and rich rivers. Shops sold guidebooks to the gold fields, many written by people who had never been there. Songs were written about the rush. One of the best known was Oh! Susanna, with new words about going to California. Groups of men formed mining companies. They pooled their money, bought supplies and promised to share whatever they found. News of the gold also spread overseas. It reached Britain, France, Germany, Chile, China and Australia. People in all these countries began to make plans. Even so, few of them understood how far away California was. Yet they were willing to risk everything. For many young men, the gold rush seemed like the chance of a lifetime. It promised adventure and the hope of escaping poverty, all at once.
===
IMG: young-man-farewell-family-farmhouse-1849 | a young man in 1840s travelling clothes with a bundle and a hat saying goodbye to his mother and younger siblings outside a wooden farmhouse in the eastern United States, early spring morning light
HEAD: Most forty-niners were young men who left their families behind, planning to return rich within a year or two.
BODY: The people who joined the gold rush were mostly men, and most of them were young. Many were in their twenties. They came from farms, small towns and cities. Some were married with children. Others were single. Most did not plan to stay in California. They hoped to find gold quickly and return home rich. Many expected to be back within a year or two. Families gathered to say goodbye. Wives and mothers sewed clothes and packed food for the journey. Some gave their sons Bibles and small keepsakes. Fathers gave advice and sometimes money. The goodbyes were often emotional. Everyone knew that the journey was long and dangerous. Even so, there was also excitement and hope. Yet many of those who left in 1849 would not come back for years. Some never came back at all. Others returned poorer than when they left, but full of stories they would tell for the rest of their lives.

TOPIC: The long road west
===
IMG: covered-wagons-prairie-trail-wide-sky-1849 | a long line of white canvas covered wagons pulled by oxen across a wide green prairie in 1849, people walking beside them, a vast open sky with soft clouds, warm morning light
HEAD: Tens of thousands of gold seekers crossed the continent in covered wagons pulled by oxen.
BODY: Many forty-niners travelled to California overland. They gathered in towns on the Missouri River, such as Independence and Saint Joseph. There, they bought wagons, oxen, mules and supplies. Then, in the spring, they set out across the plains. The journey to California was about two thousand miles. It usually took four to six months. Wagons were covered with canvas stretched over wooden hoops. They carried food, tools, clothes and cooking pots. Most people walked beside the wagons, because there was no room to ride. Oxen were slow but strong and steady. They could pull heavy loads for long distances. In 1849, tens of thousands of people took this route. Long lines of wagons stretched across the prairie. Even so, the journey was exhausting. Every day began before dawn and ended at dusk. Yet many travellers also wrote about the beauty of the land. The open sky and endless grass amazed them.
===
IMG: independence-rock-names-carved-granite-wagons | a large rounded granite rock rising from the dry plains of Wyoming with names carved and painted on its surface, covered wagons camped nearby, soft golden evening light
HEAD: Travellers carved their names on a great rock in Wyoming, known as the register of the desert.
BODY: Along the trail west, there were famous landmarks. Travellers watched for them with excitement. One of the most famous was Independence Rock, in what is now Wyoming. It is a huge, rounded mound of granite rising from the plains. Travellers hoped to reach it by the fourth of July, Independence Day. If they did, they were on schedule to cross the mountains before winter. Many people climbed the rock. They carved or painted their names on it. Thousands of names covered its surface. It became known as the register of the desert. Some of the names can still be read today. Other landmarks included Chimney Rock, a tall thin spire of stone in Nebraska, and Scotts Bluff. Even so, these landmarks were not only for sightseeing. They told travellers how far they had come. Yet they also gave a sense of wonder. For many people from flat farmland, these rocks were like nothing they had seen before.
===
IMG: wagon-camp-evening-circle-campfires-prairie | covered wagons arranged in a large circle on the open prairie at dusk, families cooking over small campfires inside the circle, oxen grazing nearby, a soft pink and orange evening sky
HEAD: Each evening, wagon trains often formed a circle to protect their animals and camp.
BODY: At the end of each long day, the wagon train stopped to camp. The wagons were often drawn into a large circle. This made a corral to keep the oxen and mules from wandering off at night. Inside the circle, families lit fires and cooked their evening meal. Common food was bacon, beans, bread and coffee. Wood was scarce on the plains. So people burned dried buffalo dung, which they called buffalo chips. It burned surprisingly well. After supper, people talked, mended clothes and repaired wagons. Some played fiddles or harmonicas and sang. Children played games before bed. Then most people slept in their wagons or in tents on the ground. Men took turns standing guard through the night. Even so, sleep could be short. The day began again before dawn. Yet for many travellers, these quiet evenings around the fire were the best part of the journey. They shared stories, food and hope under the stars.
===
IMG: cholera-trail-graves-small-wooden-markers-prairie | a few small wooden grave markers beside a dusty wagon trail on the wide prairie, wild flowers growing around them, a single wagon passing in the distance, soft grey morning light, quiet and peaceful
HEAD: In 1849, the disease cholera killed thousands of people on the trails west.
BODY: The overland journey was dangerous. The greatest danger was not wild animals or attacks. It was disease. In 1849, a terrible illness called cholera spread along the trails. It was caused by dirty water. It struck quickly and could kill a healthy person within a day. Travellers did not understand how it spread. They drank from the same streams and camped in the same places as those who had been ill. Thousands died. Many were buried in shallow graves beside the trail. Travellers later wrote of seeing graves every few miles. Accidents were also common. People fell under wagon wheels or drowned crossing rivers. Even so, most travellers survived the journey. Yet almost everyone saw death along the way. Some lost family members or close friends. They had to bury them quickly and move on. These sad moments stayed with many forty-niners for the rest of their lives.
===
IMG: emigrant-guidebook-open-table-map-1849 | a small worn guidebook for emigrants lying open on a wooden wagon seat beside a folded paper map, a compass and a tin cup, soft morning light on the prairie
HEAD: Travellers relied on cheap guidebooks that were often wrong about distances, water and grass.
BODY: Before setting off, many emigrants bought a guidebook. These small books described the route west. They listed rivers, springs, landmarks and good places to camp. They gave advice on what to pack and how to care for oxen. Some were written by experienced travellers. Others were written by people who had barely seen the trail, or not at all. One famous guide described a shortcut that turned out to be far longer and harder than promised. Emigrants who followed it suffered badly in the desert. Many guidebooks underestimated the distances. Others promised water and grass where there was none. Even so, travellers studied them carefully every evening by the fire. Yet they soon learned to trust their own eyes and the advice of those coming back east. Over time, the guides improved. Travellers also left notes for each other on rocks, bones and wooden boards beside the trail, sharing news about water, grass and the road ahead.
===
IMG: river-crossing-wagons-oxen-platte-river-shallow | covered wagons pulled by oxen slowly crossing a wide shallow river with sandy banks on the open plains in 1849, people wading beside the wagons, soft golden morning light
HEAD: The Platte River was so wide and shallow that travellers joked it was a mile wide and an inch deep.
BODY: For hundreds of miles, the trail west followed the Platte River across the plains of Nebraska. The river was a guide and a source of water for people and animals. It was a strange river. It was very wide, often more than a kilometre across. Yet in most places, it was quite shallow. Its water was brown with sand and mud. Travellers joked that it was a mile wide and an inch deep. Some said it was too thick to drink and too thin to plough. They let the water stand in buckets so the mud could settle before drinking. Crossing the river could be tricky. Its sandy bottom shifted, and wagons could get stuck. Even so, the river gave travellers grass for their animals and wood from the few trees along its banks. Yet after weeks beside it, many grew tired of its brown waters. They were glad when the trail finally turned towards the mountains.
===
IMG: wagons-crossing-sierra-nevada-pass-pine-forest | covered wagons struggling up a steep rocky mountain pass in the Sierra Nevada through tall pine forests, people pushing and oxen pulling, distant snowy peaks, clear autumn light
HEAD: The hardest part of the overland journey was crossing the Sierra Nevada mountains before the winter snow.
BODY: After crossing the deserts of Nevada, travellers faced one last great obstacle. It was the Sierra Nevada, a high mountain range on the eastern edge of California. The trails over the mountains were steep and rocky. Wagons had to be pulled up cliffs with ropes and chains. Sometimes they were taken apart and carried in pieces. Oxen were exhausted and starving by this point. Many travellers had run out of food. Timing was everything. If they did not cross before the snow came, they could be trapped. Everyone knew the story of the Donner Party. In the winter of 1846, a group of emigrants had been trapped by snow in these mountains. Nearly half of them died. Even so, most forty-niners crossed safely. Relief parties from California helped late travellers. Yet when they finally came down the western slopes, many wept with joy. They saw green valleys and rivers below. They had reached California.
===
IMG: seeing-the-elephant-traveller-resting-trail | a tired traveller in dusty 1840s clothes sitting on a rock beside a mountain trail, looking out over a wide valley, his hat in his hand, soft warm evening light
HEAD: The forty-niners had a saying for their hard experiences: they had "seen the elephant".
BODY: The gold seekers had a special phrase. They talked about seeing the elephant. In those days, travelling circuses brought elephants to American towns. For people who had never seen one, an elephant was a wonder. It was strange, huge and exciting. Seeing the elephant came to mean seeing something extraordinary, often at great cost. For the forty-niners, the phrase described the whole adventure. It meant the long journey, the hardships, the dangers and the disappointments. Someone who had seen the elephant had experienced the real thing, not just the dream. Some travellers turned back before reaching California. They said they had seen enough of the elephant. Even so, the phrase was often used with humour. Yet it captured something true. Many people set off with grand hopes and discovered how hard the world could be. They came home wiser, if not richer. The phrase stayed in American English for many years.
