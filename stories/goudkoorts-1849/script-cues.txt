In January 1848, in a quiet valley among the pine-covered hills of California, a carpenter bent down beside a sawmill and saw something glittering in the cold water. Within two years, hundreds of thousands of people were on their way to find gold.

[long-pause]

On the twenty-fourth of January 1848, a carpenter named James Marshall found gold at a sawmill in California. In the winter of 1847, a carpenter named James Marshall was building a sawmill. It stood beside the American River, in a valley called Coloma, in the foothills of the Sierra Nevada mountains. The mill belonged to a Swiss businessman named John Sutter. On the morning of the twenty-fourth of January 1848, Marshall walked along the channel that carried water away from the mill wheel. In the water, something glittered. He bent down and picked up a few small flakes of yellow metal. He wondered if it might be gold. He tested it by hammering it. It flattened but did not break. That was a good sign. He showed it to the workers. They tested it further by boiling it in strong soap. It stayed bright. Even so, no one was certain at first. Yet that small handful of flakes would change California, the United States and the lives of hundreds of thousands of people.

[long-pause]

{img:02-sutters-fort-adobe-walls-sacramento-valley}

John Sutter had built a small empire in the Sacramento Valley, centred on a fort of adobe walls. John Sutter was born in Germany and grew up in Switzerland. In 1834, deep in debt, he left his family and sailed to America. A few years later, he arrived in California. At that time, California belonged to Mexico. Sutter received a large grant of land in the Sacramento Valley. He called his colony New Helvetia, meaning New Switzerland. He built a large fort with thick walls of adobe, a kind of sun-dried brick. It had workshops, stores and living quarters. Native Californian workers farmed his fields and tended his cattle. Travellers arriving from the east often stopped at the fort. Sutter welcomed them and sold them supplies. He dreamed of building a great agricultural empire. The sawmill at Coloma was part of that plan. Even so, his dream depended on keeping his workers on the land. Yet the discovery of gold would soon draw almost all of them away. Sutter's Fort still stands in Sacramento today.

[long-pause]

If wandering through old rivers and mining camps helps the night along, a subscription keeps the next calm trail open.

[long-pause]

{img:03-marshall-sutter-testing-gold-flakes-office-1848}

Marshall rode to Sutter's Fort in the rain, and the two men tested the gold in secret. A few days after his discovery, Marshall rode to Sutter's Fort. It was raining. He arrived soaked and asked to speak with Sutter alone. Behind a locked door, he showed Sutter the little flakes. Sutter looked up the properties of gold in an encyclopedia. The two men weighed the metal and tested it with acid. Everything showed that it was real gold. Sutter was not overjoyed. He feared what would happen if the news spread. His workers would leave to look for gold. Strangers would come and take over his land. So he asked everyone at the mill to keep the discovery secret for six weeks. Even so, a secret like this could not be kept for long. Workers wrote letters. Teamsters carrying supplies saw the gold. Yet for a few quiet weeks, life at the mill went on almost as before. The men finished their work on the sawmill and looked for gold on their days off.

[long-pause]

{img:04-treaty-signing-room-mexico-1848-quiet-desk}

Just nine days after gold was found, Mexico signed a treaty that handed California to the United States. The timing of the gold discovery was remarkable. In 1848, the United States and Mexico had been at war for nearly two years. American troops had occupied California and much of northern Mexico. On the second of February 1848, the two countries signed a peace treaty. It was signed at a town called Guadalupe Hidalgo, near Mexico City. Under the treaty, Mexico gave up a huge area of land. It included California, Nevada, Utah and parts of several other future states. In return, the United States paid fifteen million dollars. Neither side knew that gold had been found in California only nine days earlier. News travelled slowly in those days. Even so, the treaty would have been signed anyway. Yet many people later wondered whether Mexico would have agreed so easily if it had known. The land it gave up was about to become one of the richest places on earth.

[long-pause]

{img:05-native-californian-workers-river-sacramento-valley}

Some of the first people to dig for gold were Native Californians who worked for Sutter. Long before the gold rush, the valleys and hills of California were home to many Native peoples. They included the Nisenan, Maidu, Miwok and many others. They had lived there for thousands of years. They gathered acorns, fished for salmon and hunted deer. Many Native people worked on Sutter's lands and at his mill. When gold was discovered, some of them were among the first to collect it. In the early months, Native Californian workers gathered much of the gold found in the region. Some ranchers and traders hired Native workers to dig for them. Even so, the gold rush brought terrible suffering to Native Californians. Their lands were overrun. Their rivers were muddied and their food sources destroyed. Disease and violence followed. Yet their story is an important part of the gold rush. Their knowledge of the land and rivers helped others, even as their own world was being taken from them.

[long-pause]

{img:06-coloma-valley-today-reconstructed-mill-river}

Today, the site of Marshall's discovery is a peaceful state park, with a copy of the old sawmill. The little valley of Coloma, where it all began, is now a quiet place. It is part of the Marshall Gold Discovery State Historic Park. Visitors can walk along the American River and see the spot where Marshall found the first flakes. A full-sized copy of Sutter's sawmill has been built near the original site. On some days, it is run to show how it worked. There are old buildings from the gold rush years, a small museum and shady picnic areas. Visitors can even try panning for gold in the river. On a hill above the valley stands a tall statue of James Marshall. He is pointing down towards the place where he made his famous discovery. Even so, the town that grew up here during the rush has mostly gone. Yet the valley keeps the memory alive. The river still runs clear and cool, much as it did on that January morning in 1848.

[long-pause]

{img:07-sam-brannan-street-san-francisco-bottle-gold-1848}

In May 1848, a merchant named Sam Brannan ran through the streets of San Francisco shouting "Gold!" For several months, news of the gold spread slowly. Some people heard rumours but did not believe them. Then a businessman named Samuel Brannan took action. He owned a store near Sutter's Fort. He had seen the gold for himself. In May 1848, he travelled to San Francisco. According to a famous story, he walked through the streets holding up a bottle of gold dust. He shouted that there was gold from the American River. People crowded around him. Within days, the small town began to empty. Men left their jobs, their shops and their ships to go to the gold fields. Even so, Brannan had a clever plan. Before making his announcement, he had bought up picks, shovels and pans. He sold them to the gold seekers at very high prices. Yet his trick was not unusual. Throughout the gold rush, many of those who grew richest were not miners, but those who sold them supplies.

[long-pause]

{img:08-empty-san-francisco-1848-closed-shops-harbour}

By the summer of 1848, San Francisco was almost deserted, as nearly every man had left for the gold fields. The effect of the news was astonishing. In the summer of 1848, towns across California emptied. In San Francisco, shops closed. Newspapers stopped printing, because their workers had gone. Soldiers deserted their posts. Sailors left their ships in the harbour. Even the town's officials joined the rush. Farmers left their crops in the fields. Teachers left their schools. One newspaper published a final issue, explaining that the whole town had gone gold hunting. Even so, at this stage, the rush was still mostly local. People came from California, Oregon, Hawaii and Mexico. They had the advantage of being close. In 1848, a hard-working miner could still find a lot of gold quite easily. Some found more in a few days than they could earn in a year. Yet in the eastern United States and in Europe, most people still knew nothing. The real flood of gold seekers had not yet begun.

[long-pause]

{img:09-president-polk-message-congress-washington-1848}

In December 1848, President Polk confirmed the gold discovery in a message to Congress. News of the gold reached the eastern United States slowly. At first, many people thought the stories were exaggerated. Then the army sent an officer east with a small box of gold from California. In December 1848, President James Polk spoke to Congress about California. He said that the reports of gold were true. He described the large amounts being found. The gold itself was displayed in Washington. Newspapers across the country printed the news. Suddenly, everyone was talking about California. Thousands of people decided to go. They sold their farms and businesses. They borrowed money and formed companies with friends and neighbours. Even so, many people warned against the rush. They said it was madness to travel so far on the hope of gold. Yet these warnings were ignored. By early 1849, a great wave of people was preparing to head west. They would become known as the forty-niners.

[long-pause]

{img:10-newspaper-office-crowd-reading-gold-news-1849}

Gold fever spread through America and across the world in 1849. In early 1849, the excitement was everywhere. People called it gold fever. Newspapers printed letters from California describing nuggets and rich rivers. Shops sold guidebooks to the gold fields, many written by people who had never been there. Songs were written about the rush. One of the best known was Oh! Susanna, with new words about going to California. Groups of men formed mining companies. They pooled their money, bought supplies and promised to share whatever they found. News of the gold also spread overseas. It reached Britain, France, Germany, Chile, China and Australia. People in all these countries began to make plans. Even so, few of them understood how far away California was. Yet they were willing to risk everything. For many young men, the gold rush seemed like the chance of a lifetime. It promised adventure and the hope of escaping poverty, all at once.

[long-pause]

{img:11-young-man-farewell-family-farmhouse-1849}

Most forty-niners were young men who left their families behind, planning to return rich within a year or two. The people who joined the gold rush were mostly men, and most of them were young. Many were in their twenties. They came from farms, small towns and cities. Some were married with children. Others were single. Most did not plan to stay in California. They hoped to find gold quickly and return home rich. Many expected to be back within a year or two. Families gathered to say goodbye. Wives and mothers sewed clothes and packed food for the journey. Some gave their sons Bibles and small keepsakes. Fathers gave advice and sometimes money. The goodbyes were often emotional. Everyone knew that the journey was long and dangerous. Even so, there was also excitement and hope. Yet many of those who left in 1849 would not come back for years. Some never came back at all. Others returned poorer than when they left, but full of stories they would tell for the rest of their lives.

[long-pause]

{img:12-covered-wagons-prairie-trail-wide-sky-1849}

Tens of thousands of gold seekers crossed the continent in covered wagons pulled by oxen. Many forty-niners travelled to California overland. They gathered in towns on the Missouri River, such as Independence and Saint Joseph. There, they bought wagons, oxen, mules and supplies. Then, in the spring, they set out across the plains. The journey to California was about two thousand miles. It usually took four to six months. Wagons were covered with canvas stretched over wooden hoops. They carried food, tools, clothes and cooking pots. Most people walked beside the wagons, because there was no room to ride. Oxen were slow but strong and steady. They could pull heavy loads for long distances. In 1849, tens of thousands of people took this route. Long lines of wagons stretched across the prairie. Even so, the journey was exhausting. Every day began before dawn and ended at dusk. Yet many travellers also wrote about the beauty of the land. The open sky and endless grass amazed them.

[long-pause]

{img:13-independence-rock-names-carved-granite-wagons}

Travellers carved their names on a great rock in Wyoming, known as the register of the desert. Along the trail west, there were famous landmarks. Travellers watched for them with excitement. One of the most famous was Independence Rock, in what is now Wyoming. It is a huge, rounded mound of granite rising from the plains. Travellers hoped to reach it by the fourth of July, Independence Day. If they did, they were on schedule to cross the mountains before winter. Many people climbed the rock. They carved or painted their names on it. Thousands of names covered its surface. It became known as the register of the desert. Some of the names can still be read today. Other landmarks included Chimney Rock, a tall thin spire of stone in Nebraska, and Scotts Bluff. Even so, these landmarks were not only for sightseeing. They told travellers how far they had come. Yet they also gave a sense of wonder. For many people from flat farmland, these rocks were like nothing they had seen before.

[long-pause]

{img:14-wagon-camp-evening-circle-campfires-prairie}

Each evening, wagon trains often formed a circle to protect their animals and camp. At the end of each long day, the wagon train stopped to camp. The wagons were often drawn into a large circle. This made a corral to keep the oxen and mules from wandering off at night. Inside the circle, families lit fires and cooked their evening meal. Common food was bacon, beans, bread and coffee. Wood was scarce on the plains. So people burned dried buffalo dung, which they called buffalo chips. It burned surprisingly well. After supper, people talked, mended clothes and repaired wagons. Some played fiddles or harmonicas and sang. Children played games before bed. Then most people slept in their wagons or in tents on the ground. Men took turns standing guard through the night. Even so, sleep could be short. The day began again before dawn. Yet for many travellers, these quiet evenings around the fire were the best part of the journey. They shared stories, food and hope under the stars.

[long-pause]

{img:15-cholera-trail-graves-small-wooden-markers-prairie}

In 1849, the disease cholera killed thousands of people on the trails west. The overland journey was dangerous. The greatest danger was not wild animals or attacks. It was disease. In 1849, a terrible illness called cholera spread along the trails. It was caused by dirty water. It struck quickly and could kill a healthy person within a day. Travellers did not understand how it spread. They drank from the same streams and camped in the same places as those who had been ill. Thousands died. Many were buried in shallow graves beside the trail. Travellers later wrote of seeing graves every few miles. Accidents were also common. People fell under wagon wheels or drowned crossing rivers. Even so, most travellers survived the journey. Yet almost everyone saw death along the way. Some lost family members or close friends. They had to bury them quickly and move on. These sad moments stayed with many forty-niners for the rest of their lives.

[long-pause]

{img:16-emigrant-guidebook-open-table-map-1849}

Travellers relied on cheap guidebooks that were often wrong about distances, water and grass. Before setting off, many emigrants bought a guidebook. These small books described the route west. They listed rivers, springs, landmarks and good places to camp. They gave advice on what to pack and how to care for oxen. Some were written by experienced travellers. Others were written by people who had barely seen the trail, or not at all. One famous guide described a shortcut that turned out to be far longer and harder than promised. Emigrants who followed it suffered badly in the desert. Many guidebooks underestimated the distances. Others promised water and grass where there was none. Even so, travellers studied them carefully every evening by the fire. Yet they soon learned to trust their own eyes and the advice of those coming back east. Over time, the guides improved. Travellers also left notes for each other on rocks, bones and wooden boards beside the trail, sharing news about water, grass and the road ahead.

[long-pause]

{img:17-river-crossing-wagons-oxen-platte-river-shallow}

The Platte River was so wide and shallow that travellers joked it was a mile wide and an inch deep. For hundreds of miles, the trail west followed the Platte River across the plains of Nebraska. The river was a guide and a source of water for people and animals. It was a strange river. It was very wide, often more than a kilometre across. Yet in most places, it was quite shallow. Its water was brown with sand and mud. Travellers joked that it was a mile wide and an inch deep. Some said it was too thick to drink and too thin to plough. They let the water stand in buckets so the mud could settle before drinking. Crossing the river could be tricky. Its sandy bottom shifted, and wagons could get stuck. Even so, the river gave travellers grass for their animals and wood from the few trees along its banks. Yet after weeks beside it, many grew tired of its brown waters. They were glad when the trail finally turned towards the mountains.

[long-pause]

{img:18-wagons-crossing-sierra-nevada-pass-pine-forest}

The hardest part of the overland journey was crossing the Sierra Nevada mountains before the winter snow. After crossing the deserts of Nevada, travellers faced one last great obstacle. It was the Sierra Nevada, a high mountain range on the eastern edge of California. The trails over the mountains were steep and rocky. Wagons had to be pulled up cliffs with ropes and chains. Sometimes they were taken apart and carried in pieces. Oxen were exhausted and starving by this point. Many travellers had run out of food. Timing was everything. If they did not cross before the snow came, they could be trapped. Everyone knew the story of the Donner Party. In the winter of 1846, a group of emigrants had been trapped by snow in these mountains. Nearly half of them died. Even so, most forty-niners crossed safely. Relief parties from California helped late travellers. Yet when they finally came down the western slopes, many wept with joy. They saw green valleys and rivers below. They had reached California.

[long-pause]

{img:19-seeing-the-elephant-traveller-resting-trail}

The forty-niners had a saying for their hard experiences: they had "seen the elephant". The gold seekers had a special phrase. They talked about seeing the elephant. In those days, travelling circuses brought elephants to American towns. For people who had never seen one, an elephant was a wonder. It was strange, huge and exciting. Seeing the elephant came to mean seeing something extraordinary, often at great cost. For the forty-niners, the phrase described the whole adventure. It meant the long journey, the hardships, the dangers and the disappointments. Someone who had seen the elephant had experienced the real thing, not just the dream. Some travellers turned back before reaching California. They said they had seen enough of the elephant. Even so, the phrase was often used with humour. Yet it captured something true. Many people set off with grand hopes and discovered how hard the world could be. They came home wiser, if not richer. The phrase stayed in American English for many years.

[long-pause]

{img:20-sailing-ship-cape-horn-grey-waves-1849}

Many gold seekers sailed around Cape Horn, a voyage of about eighteen thousand miles. Not everyone went overland. Many gold seekers from the eastern cities chose to travel by sea. The longest route went all the way around South America. Ships sailed south from New York or Boston, down past Brazil and Argentina. Then they rounded Cape Horn, at the stormy southern tip of the continent. From there, they sailed north along the coast of Chile and Peru, and on to San Francisco. The whole voyage was about eighteen thousand miles. It usually took five to eight months. Passengers were crowded together below deck. The food was poor, and fresh water was limited. Many suffered from seasickness. The waters around Cape Horn were famous for storms, huge waves and icy winds. Even so, the sea route had advantages. Passengers could carry heavy supplies and machinery. They did not have to walk or drive oxen. Yet the long months at sea tested everyone's patience. Most were overjoyed to see the hills of California at last.

[long-pause]

{img:21-ship-deck-passengers-1849-games-calm-sea}

On the long sea voyage, passengers passed the time with games, music, newspapers and lectures. Months at sea could be very boring. So passengers found ways to fill their time. They played cards, chess and dominoes. They sang songs and played instruments. Some ships had small libraries. Passengers wrote in diaries and composed letters to send home from the next port. On some ships, passengers even published their own handwritten newspapers. They were full of jokes, poems and news of life on board. Some passengers gave lectures on science, history or mining. Others held debates. On Sundays, there were church services on deck. Even so, tempers could grow short. People argued about food, space and the slow progress of the ship. Yet friendships also formed. Many passengers stayed together after they reached California. They joined forces to work in the mines. For some, the long voyage was a strange kind of school. They learned to live closely with strangers from very different backgrounds.

[long-pause]

{img:22-valparaiso-harbour-chile-colourful-houses-hills}

Ships bound for California often stopped at Valparaíso in Chile, where passengers enjoyed fresh fruit and solid ground. After rounding Cape Horn, most ships stopped at a port on the coast of Chile. The most popular was Valparaíso. Its colourful houses climbed up steep hills around a wide bay. After months at sea, passengers were delighted to go ashore. They walked through the markets and ate fresh fruit, bread and meat. They wrote letters and sent them home. They bought supplies for the rest of the voyage. Valparaíso became very busy during the gold rush years. Many Chileans also caught gold fever. They had experience of mining in their own country. Thousands of them sailed north to California. Chilean miners brought useful skills and methods to the gold fields. Even so, they later faced hostility from some American miners. Yet the link between Chile and California was strong. Ships carried people, flour and goods between the two coasts. For a few years, Valparaíso was an important stop on the road to the gold.

[long-pause]

{img:23-chagres-river-canoes-jungle-panama-1849}

A faster route to California went across the narrow jungle of Panama, by canoe and on mule-back. There was a quicker way to reach California by sea. Instead of sailing around South America, travellers could cross the narrow strip of land at Panama. They sailed from New York to the mouth of the Chagres River, on the Caribbean coast. There, they hired local boatmen to take them up the river in long dugout canoes. The journey took several days through thick jungle. The boatmen poled the canoes against the current. Travellers saw monkeys, parrots and strange plants. At night, they slept in small villages along the river. Then they continued on mules along rough mountain trails to Panama City, on the Pacific coast. There, they waited for a steamer to San Francisco. Even so, the route had its dangers. Tropical diseases like malaria and yellow fever were common. Yet if all went well, the whole journey could take just six to eight weeks. In 1855, a railway was built across Panama, making the crossing much easier.

[long-pause]

{img:24-panama-city-waiting-crowds-old-walls-steamer}

In Panama City, thousands of gold seekers waited for weeks for a place on a steamship to San Francisco. When the gold seekers reached Panama City, they often faced a long wait. There were not enough steamships to carry everyone to California. Thousands of people crowded into the old city. They stayed in hotels, boarding houses and tents. Prices rose quickly. Tickets for the steamers were sold for high prices. Some people waited for weeks or even months. Many fell ill in the heat. Others ran out of money. The old Spanish city, with its stone walls and churches, was suddenly full of Americans. Local merchants did good business. Even so, the waiting was frustrating. Every day, people watched the bay, hoping a ship would arrive. When one did, there was a rush to get on board. Ships were often dangerously overcrowded. Yet once aboard, the voyage north to San Francisco took only a few weeks. For those who had waited, the sight of the steamer's smoke on the horizon was a great relief.

[long-pause]

{img:25-steamship-golden-gate-entrance-1849-hills}

Ships entered San Francisco Bay through a narrow strait that had recently been named the Golden Gate. The entrance to San Francisco Bay is a narrow strait between two headlands. In 1846, an American officer named John Frémont gave it the name Chrysopylae, or Golden Gate. He chose the name because it reminded him of the Golden Horn, the harbour of Istanbul. He also expected it to become a gateway for rich trade. He could not have known how fitting the name would become. Just two years later, gold was discovered. Ships carrying thousands of gold seekers sailed through the Golden Gate. Often, fog rolled in through the strait in the morning. As it lifted, passengers saw the green hills and the wide bay opening before them. Even so, there was no bridge across the strait in those days. The famous Golden Gate Bridge was not built until the 1930s. Yet the name stuck. For the forty-niners, sailing through the Golden Gate meant that their long journey was nearly over.

[long-pause]

{img:26-san-francisco-1849-tents-wooden-buildings-hills}

San Francisco grew from a small town of about a thousand people to a city of tens of thousands in just two years. Before the gold rush, San Francisco was a small, quiet town. In early 1848, perhaps a thousand people lived there. Then the rush began. Ships arrived every day, bringing thousands of newcomers. By the end of 1849, the population had grown to around twenty-five thousand. People called it the instant city. There were not enough buildings. People lived in tents, wooden shacks and even in barrels. New buildings went up almost overnight. Some were made from wood shipped from Oregon or even from as far away as China. Others were prefabricated houses, shipped in pieces from the eastern United States and Europe. The streets were unpaved. In winter, they turned into deep mud. Even so, the city was full of energy. Shops, hotels, restaurants and gambling halls opened everywhere. Yet it was also chaotic. Fires broke out often. San Francisco burned down and was rebuilt several times in its first few years.

[long-pause]

{img:27-abandoned-ships-san-francisco-harbour-forest-masts}

Hundreds of ships were abandoned in San Francisco Bay, because their crews had run off to the gold fields. When ships arrived in San Francisco, their passengers hurried ashore. Very often, the sailors did too. They deserted their ships to go and look for gold. Captains could not find new crews. So many ships were simply left in the bay. By 1850, hundreds of empty ships lay at anchor. Their bare masts looked like a forest. Some rotted away. Others were put to new uses. Some were dragged ashore and turned into buildings. One became a hotel. Another became a warehouse. One ship was even used as the city's jail. As the city grew, the waterfront was filled in with sand and rubble. Some of the old ships were buried under the new land. Even so, they were not completely forgotten. Yet for more than a century, they lay hidden under the streets. In recent times, builders digging foundations in downtown San Francisco have uncovered the remains of several of these gold rush ships.

[long-pause]

{img:28-muddy-street-san-francisco-boards-planks-1850}

The streets of early San Francisco were so muddy in winter that some people said a mule could sink out of sight. The streets of San Francisco were not paved in 1849. In the dry summer, they were dusty. When the winter rains came, they turned into deep mud. Stories were told of horses and mules sinking up to their bellies. One famous sign, perhaps a joke, warned that a street was not passable, not even jackassable. People threw anything they could find into the mud to make a path. They used planks, branches, boxes and even sacks of flour and tobacco that were too cheap to sell. Some streets were covered with boards laid side by side. Walking across town could be an adventure. Men wore tall boots and still came home caked in mud. Even so, the city was growing so fast that there was no time to build proper roads. Yet over the next few years, the main streets were planked and later paved. The mud of 1849 became part of San Francisco's legend.

[long-pause]

{img:29-boudin-bakery-sourdough-bread-loaves-1849}

A bakery that opened in San Francisco in 1849 still makes sourdough bread from the same starter today. Bread was an important food for the gold seekers. In the mining camps, men often made sourdough bread. Sourdough is made with a starter, a mixture of flour and water that ferments naturally. Miners kept their starter in a small pot and carried it everywhere. Some even slept with it to keep it warm. Experienced miners were sometimes called sourdoughs. In 1849, a French baker named Isidore Boudin opened a bakery in San Francisco. He combined French baking methods with the sourdough of the miners. The bread had a crisp crust and a slightly sour taste. The foggy, cool climate of San Francisco seemed to suit it well. Even so, Boudin's bakery faced many challenges over the years. It survived fires and the great earthquake of 1906. Yet it kept going. The company says that it still uses a starter descended from the original one. Sourdough bread remains a famous symbol of San Francisco.

[long-pause]

{img:30-gambling-hall-1849-chandeliers-tables-music}

San Francisco's gambling halls were the grandest buildings in the young city. In early San Francisco, some of the finest buildings were gambling halls. They stood around Portsmouth Square, the centre of the town. They had chandeliers, mirrors, paintings and carpets. Bands and musicians played to attract customers. Miners came down from the gold fields with bags of gold dust. Many gambled it away in a single night. Card games, dice and roulette were all popular. Gold dust was weighed on small scales at the tables. Even so, the halls were also social centres. Lonely men came for the music, the light and the company. Many simply came to watch and talk. Yet gambling took a heavy toll. Many miners lost everything they had worked for. Some of the most popular halls had names like the El Dorado and the Bella Union. As the city became more settled, gambling was gradually restricted. By the 1850s, churches, schools and theatres were beginning to change the character of San Francisco.

[long-pause]

{img:31-mining-camp-tents-cabins-river-valley-pines}

Mining camps sprang up along the rivers almost overnight, with names like Rough and Ready and Hangtown. The gold fields lay along the rivers and streams of the Sierra Nevada foothills. Wherever gold was found, a mining camp appeared. At first, camps were just groups of tents and brush shelters. As more people arrived, men built log cabins. A camp might grow to hundreds or even thousands of people in a few months. Then, if the gold ran out, it might be empty again just as quickly. The camps had colourful names. There were places called Rough and Ready, Hangtown, Fiddletown, You Bet and Whiskey Flat. Some names described the place. Others came from events or jokes. Even so, life in the camps was simple. There were few comforts and almost no women or children. Yet the camps were also lively. Each had its own store, saloon and boarding house. Some later grew into proper towns. Hangtown, for example, became the town of Placerville, which still exists today.

[long-pause]

{img:32-miners-cabin-interior-bunks-fireplace-1850}

Miners often lived together in small log cabins, sharing the work, the cooking and the gold. Most miners did not work alone. They formed small groups called partnerships. Partners shared the hard work of mining. They also shared their cabin, their food and often their gold. A typical cabin was small and simple. It had walls of logs or rough boards. The roof was made of planks, canvas or bark. There was a stone fireplace for cooking and warmth. Bunks were built against the walls. A table and a few stools stood in the middle. Tools, guns and clothes hung on pegs. Even so, the cabins were often damp and draughty. In winter, rain poured through gaps in the roof. Yet many miners grew fond of their cabins. They became home. In the evenings, partners sat by the fire, smoking pipes, telling stories and making plans. They talked about their families, their hopes and what they would do when they struck it rich.

[long-pause]

{img:33-miners-cooking-beans-bacon-campfire-tin-plates}

Most miners lived on a simple diet of bread, beans, bacon and coffee. Food in the mining camps was simple and often monotonous. Most miners cooked for themselves. Their usual meals were bread, beans, salt pork or bacon, and strong coffee. They made flapjacks, a kind of pancake, from flour and water. Fresh fruit and vegetables were rare and very expensive. As a result, many miners suffered from scurvy, especially in the early years. Some ate wild plants to stay healthy. One of these was a small green plant now called miner's lettuce. It grew in damp, shady places. Miners learned to eat it from Native Californians. It was rich in vitamin C. Even so, cooking was not every miner's favourite task. Many were young men who had never cooked before. Their first attempts were often a disaster. Yet over time, they learned. Some became quite proud of their cooking. A good cook was very welcome in any partnership.

[long-pause]

{img:34-sunday-camp-miners-washing-clothes-river}

Sunday was the miners' day of rest, used for washing clothes, writing letters and going to town. Miners worked hard six days a week. Sunday was different. Most miners did not dig for gold on Sundays. Instead, they rested and did other jobs. They washed their clothes in the river. They mended their boots and shirts. They baked bread for the coming week. Many walked to the nearest town to buy supplies and collect mail. Towns were busiest on Sundays. Stores, saloons and auctions were crowded with miners. Some attended church services, held by travelling preachers in tents or under trees. Others wrote long letters home. Even so, Sunday was not always peaceful. Saloons and gambling tents did good business. Yet for many miners, it was the one day they could think of something other than gold. They read their Bibles, rested their aching bodies and remembered their families far away. The quiet of Sunday morning in a mining camp was something many later remembered fondly.

[long-pause]

{img:35-miners-dance-no-women-fiddle-cabin-evening}

Because there were so few women in the camps, miners danced with each other at their evening gatherings. Women were very rare in the early mining camps. In some camps, there were none at all. Yet the miners still liked to dance. So they danced with each other. At a miners' dance, some men took the role of women. To show who they were, they tied a patch of cloth or a handkerchief on their arm. Fiddlers played lively tunes. Men clapped, stamped and swung each other around. Visitors found these dances both funny and touching. The dances were a way to forget hard work and loneliness for a while. Even so, the miners missed the company of women and families. When a woman did arrive in a camp, it was big news. Men came from miles around just to see her. Some offered her gifts. Yet the dances showed the cheerfulness of the miners. Far from home, without their families, they still found ways to laugh, play and enjoy each other's company.

[long-pause]

{img:36-rough-and-ready-town-small-street-1850}

In 1850, the mining town of Rough and Ready briefly declared itself an independent nation. One of the strangest stories of the gold rush comes from a camp called Rough and Ready. In 1850, its miners were angry. They disliked a new mining tax, and they felt that the government did nothing to help them. So, in April 1850, they held a meeting. They voted to leave the United States and form their own independent nation. They called it the Great Republic of Rough and Ready. They even chose a president. Even so, the republic did not last long. As the Fourth of July approached, the miners realised something. If they were no longer Americans, they could not celebrate Independence Day. That seemed wrong. So they voted to rejoin the United States. The republic had lasted less than three months. Yet the story is still told with a smile today. The town of Rough and Ready still exists, and it celebrates its brief independence every year.

[long-pause]

{img:37-miner-panning-gold-river-pan-swirling}

The simplest way to find gold was with a shallow metal pan. In the early days of the rush, most gold was found near the surface. It lay in the gravel and sand of rivers and streams. The simplest tool for finding it was a pan. A gold pan was a wide, shallow metal dish. The miner scooped up some gravel and water. Then he swirled the pan gently in a circular motion. Gold is very heavy, much heavier than sand and stones. As the pan swirled, the lighter material washed over the edge. The heavy gold sank to the bottom. After many minutes of patient swirling, small flakes or grains of gold might appear. Sometimes, a lucky miner found a nugget. Even so, panning was slow, tiring work. Miners spent hours bent over in cold water. Their backs ached and their hands grew numb. Yet a skilled panner could recover a surprising amount of gold. The pan became the great symbol of the gold rush.

[long-pause]

{img:38-rocker-cradle-miners-working-together-stream}

A wooden box called a rocker, or cradle, let miners wash much more gravel than a pan. Panning was too slow to process large amounts of gravel. So miners used a simple machine called a rocker. It was also called a cradle, because it rocked back and forth like a baby's cradle. It was a wooden box with a sieve on top and small wooden bars, called riffles, along the bottom. One miner shovelled gravel into the sieve. Another poured water over it and rocked the box. The water washed the gravel through. Heavy gold sank and was caught behind the riffles. Lighter material washed out the end. At the end of the day, the miners collected the gold from the riffles. A rocker needed two or three men to work it. That is one reason miners formed partnerships. Even so, a rocker could process many times more gravel than a pan. Yet it still required hard physical labour. The rhythmic sound of rocking cradles filled the river valleys of California during the gold rush.

[long-pause]

{img:39-long-tom-sluice-wooden-channel-miners-shovelling}

Long wooden channels called sluices allowed teams of miners to wash huge amounts of gravel. As the easy surface gold disappeared, miners needed better methods. One was the long tom. It was a long wooden trough, about three or four metres long. Water flowed through it constantly. Miners shovelled gravel into the top end. The water washed it down the trough. Gold sank and was trapped by riffles along the bottom. An even larger version was the sluice. It was made of many wooden boxes joined together in a long line. Some sluices stretched for hundreds of metres. Teams of miners shovelled gravel into them all day. Water had to be brought from rivers and streams, often through long wooden channels called flumes. Even so, these methods needed more men, more water and more money. Yet they could recover gold from gravel that would never have been worth panning. Mining was slowly becoming an organised business, rather than a matter of luck and a single pan.

[long-pause]

{img:40-river-diverted-wing-dam-miners-dry-riverbed}

Some groups of miners turned whole rivers aside to dig for gold in the dry riverbeds. Miners knew that the richest gold often lay at the bottom of rivers. It had settled there over thousands of years. To reach it, some groups did something bold. They built dams and channels to turn the river aside. This left part of the riverbed dry. Then they dug through the exposed gravel and rock. This kind of work was usually done in late summer and autumn, when rivers were low. Large companies of miners worked together. They built wooden dams, flumes and wing dams of logs and stones. Even so, the work was risky. If heavy rain came, the river could rise and sweep everything away. Months of work could be lost in a single night. Yet when it succeeded, it could be very rewarding. Some river-bed claims yielded great amounts of gold. The scale of the work showed how determined the miners were, and how much they would risk for the chance of a rich find.

[long-pause]

{img:41-assay-scales-gold-dust-weighing-store-counter}

In the mining camps, gold dust was used as money, measured with small scales on every shop counter. In the gold fields, there were few coins. So miners used gold dust as money. Every store, saloon and boarding house had a small set of scales. When a miner bought something, the shopkeeper weighed out the right amount of gold dust from the miner's pouch. A pinch of gold dust, taken between thumb and finger, was a common way to pay for a drink. It was said that some barkeepers grew their fingernails long, or had large thumbs, to take bigger pinches. Gold was usually valued at about sixteen dollars an ounce. Miners carried their gold in leather pouches, in glass bottles or in the toes of old socks. Even so, using gold dust as money had problems. Scales could be dishonest. Some people mixed brass filings or sand with the gold. Yet the system worked well enough. Later, private companies and then the government began to mint gold coins in San Francisco.

[long-pause]

{img:42-riverboat-steamer-sacramento-river-1850}

From San Francisco, gold seekers travelled up the rivers by steamboat to Sacramento and Stockton. San Francisco was only the first stop. The gold fields lay more than a hundred miles inland. To get there, most people travelled by water. Small sailing boats and, soon, paddle steamers carried passengers up the Sacramento and San Joaquin rivers. The trip took about a day. Steamboats were crowded with miners, their tools and their supplies. The fares were high at first, but competition soon lowered them. Some steamboats raced each other, which could be dangerous. Boilers sometimes exploded. Along the way, passengers saw wide marshes, flocks of birds and herds of elk. The boats arrived at two growing river towns. Sacramento served the northern mines. Stockton served the southern mines. Even so, the journey was not over. From these towns, gold seekers continued on foot, by mule or by wagon into the hills. Yet the river steamers were the first great link between the coast and the gold country.

[long-pause]

{img:43-sacramento-1849-tents-river-front-boats}

Sacramento grew almost overnight beside Sutter's Fort, into a busy town of tents and warehouses. Sacramento lay where the American River meets the Sacramento River, close to Sutter's Fort. In 1848, there was little there. Then the gold rush began. Sutter's son laid out a town on the riverbank. Lots were sold quickly. Within months, a busy town of tents, shacks and warehouses had grown up. Ships and steamboats unloaded goods at the riverfront. Merchants sold supplies to miners heading into the hills. Hotels, saloons and boarding houses opened. By late 1849, thousands of people lived there. Even so, the town had one great weakness. It was built on low, flat land beside two rivers. In January 1850, heavy rains caused a great flood. Much of the town was under water. People rowed boats through the streets. Yet Sacramento recovered. Later, the people raised the level of the streets by several metres to protect against floods. In 1854, Sacramento became the capital of California, which it still is today.

[long-pause]

{img:44-mule-train-mountain-trail-packs-supplies}

Long trains of pack mules carried food and tools up steep trails to the mining camps. Many mining camps were in steep canyons and on high ridges. There were no roads for wagons. So supplies were carried by mules. A mule train might have dozens of animals, walking in a long line. Each mule carried a heavy load of flour, beans, bacon, tools, clothing or whisky. The loads were tied on with ropes and canvas. Skilled packers, often Mexican, led the trains. They knew how to balance the loads so the mules could walk safely on narrow paths. A bell mare, a horse wearing a bell, often led the line. The mules followed the sound. Even so, the trails were dangerous. Mules sometimes slipped and fell. Bad weather could stop the trains for days. Yet the mule trains were the lifeline of the mining camps. When a train arrived, the whole camp came out to meet it. It meant fresh supplies, news and letters from the outside world.

[long-pause]

{img:45-concord-stagecoach-dusty-road-oak-hills}

By the 1850s, stagecoaches carried passengers, mail and gold along the dusty roads of the gold country. As the gold country developed, roads were built between the towns. Stagecoach lines began to run on them. The most famous coaches were made in Concord, New Hampshire. They were strong, painted in bright colours and hung on leather straps instead of springs. This made them rock gently, like a cradle. Six horses usually pulled them. Up to nine passengers sat inside, and more rode on the roof. The driver sat on top at the front. He was called a whip or a jehu. Good drivers were famous for their skill. They could guide six horses down narrow mountain roads at speed. Even so, the journey was uncomfortable. Passengers were thrown about and covered in dust. Yet stagecoaches were the fastest way to travel overland. One of the best known California drivers was Charley Parkhurst. Only after Charley died did people learn that Charley had been born a woman.

[long-pause]

{img:46-river-ferry-crossing-wagon-raft-rope}

Enterprising settlers built ferries and toll bridges across the rivers and charged miners to cross. The gold country was crossed by many rivers. In spring, they were deep, cold and fast. Crossing them was dangerous. So some people saw a business opportunity. They built ferries and charged travellers to cross. A typical ferry was a flat wooden raft. It was pulled across the river along a strong rope stretched from bank to bank. It could carry people, horses, mules and even wagons. Later, some ferry owners built wooden toll bridges. Fees could be very high, especially in the busy years. Some ferry owners became rich. They earned more from the miners than the miners earned from the gold. Even so, the ferries provided a real service. Without them, many people would have drowned trying to swim or wade across. Yet travellers often grumbled about the prices. Many place names in the gold country still remember these old crossings, in names that end in Ferry or Bridge.

[long-pause]

{img:47-general-store-mining-town-shelves-goods-1850}

In the gold fields, prices were so high that a single egg could cost a dollar or more. The gold rush brought huge numbers of people to California very quickly. There were not enough goods for all of them. Almost everything had to be brought in by ship or by mule train. As a result, prices rose to astonishing levels. In some camps, a single egg cost a dollar or more. A loaf of bread might cost as much. A pair of boots could cost many times its price in New York. Shovels, picks and pans were also very expensive. A miner might find a good amount of gold in a day and still spend most of it on food and supplies. Even so, prices changed quickly. When a new shipment arrived, prices could fall overnight. When supplies ran short, they rose again. Yet for many miners, the high prices were a shock. They had imagined that their gold would make them rich. Instead, much of it went straight into the pockets of the shopkeepers.

[long-pause]

{img:48-levi-strauss-dry-goods-store-san-francisco-1853}

A young merchant named Levi Strauss arrived in San Francisco in 1853 and later made the first riveted blue jeans. Levi Strauss was born in Bavaria, in Germany, in 1829. As a young man, he emigrated to New York, where his brothers ran a dry goods business. In 1853, he moved to San Francisco to open a branch of the family business. He sold cloth, clothing and other goods to shops in the mining towns. Miners needed strong clothes that would not tear. Years later, a tailor in Nevada named Jacob Davis had an idea. He put small copper rivets on the pockets of work trousers to make them stronger. Davis bought his cloth from Levi Strauss. In 1873, the two men received a patent for riveted work trousers. They were made of a sturdy blue cotton cloth called denim. These were the first blue jeans. Even so, they were made a little after the gold rush itself. Yet they grew from the needs of the mining world. Today, jeans are worn all over the world.

[long-pause]

{img:49-studebaker-wheelbarrow-workshop-placerville}

Several famous American businesses began by selling to the gold miners. Some of the most successful people of the gold rush never found much gold. Instead, they sold goods or services to the miners. In Placerville, a young man named John Studebaker built wheelbarrows for miners. He saved his money and later joined his brothers in their wagon business in Indiana. The Studebaker company became one of the largest wagon makers in America. Much later, it turned to motor vehicles. In Sacramento, a merchant named Leland Stanford ran a store selling supplies. He later became governor of California, built a great railway and founded Stanford University. Another young man, Philip Armour, dug water ditches for miners. He saved his earnings and later built a huge meat business in Chicago. Even so, these were exceptional cases. Many shopkeepers also failed. Yet the stories show a lesson of the gold rush. The surest way to profit was often to supply the people searching for gold, rather than to search for it.

[long-pause]

{img:50-wells-fargo-office-gold-shipments-strongbox}

Wells Fargo, founded in 1852, carried gold, mail and money across California. Miners needed a safe way to send their gold to banks and their money home to their families. They also needed a way to send and receive letters. In 1852, two businessmen, Henry Wells and William Fargo, founded a new company. It was called Wells, Fargo and Company. It opened offices in San Francisco and in mining towns across California. The company bought gold dust from miners and shipped it to San Francisco. It carried letters and packages. It also offered banking services. Its agents became trusted figures in many small towns. Stagecoaches carried the mail and the company's famous green wooden strongboxes. Even so, the work could be dangerous. Robbers sometimes held up the stagecoaches. Yet the company built a reputation for honesty and reliability. When robbers stole a shipment, it often repaid its customers. Wells Fargo grew into one of the largest banks in the United States.

[long-pause]

{img:51-boarding-house-long-table-miners-dinner}

Boarding houses offered miners a bed and hot meals, for a high price. Not every miner wanted to cook for himself or live in a tent. In the towns, boarding houses offered an alternative. A boarding house was a building where people rented a bed and received meals. The rooms were often crowded. Several men might share one room, sleeping in bunks or on the floor. Meals were served at a long table. All the boarders ate together. The food was simple but filling. There was meat, bread, potatoes, beans and coffee. For miners who had been living on their own cooking, a hot meal at a boarding house was a treat. Prices were high, often several dollars a day. Many boarding houses were run by women. Even so, the business was hard work. Cooking for dozens of hungry men every day took great effort. Yet it could be very profitable. Some women who ran boarding houses earned more than the miners who stayed with them.

[long-pause]

{img:52-miners-many-nations-river-chinese-chilean-french}

Gold seekers came to California from all over the world, including China, Chile, Mexico, France and Australia. The gold rush drew people from every part of the globe. Americans came from every state. Thousands came from Mexico, especially from the mining state of Sonora. Chileans and Peruvians sailed north. Many brought valuable mining experience. From Europe came the French, Germans, Irish, Italians and British. Australians crossed the Pacific. Hawaiians came too. From China came tens of thousands of people, mainly from the southern province of Guangdong. In the mining camps, many languages could be heard. People cooked different foods and celebrated different holidays. Even so, life together was not always peaceful. Many American miners resented foreigners. They wanted the gold for themselves. Yet the mix of peoples changed California forever. It became one of the most diverse places in the world. San Francisco, in particular, grew into a truly international city in just a few years.

[long-pause]

{img:53-chinese-miners-wide-hats-working-claim-river}

Chinese immigrants called California Gam Saan, which means Gold Mountain. News of the gold reached southern China in 1848 and 1849. At that time, the region was suffering from war, floods and poverty. Many young men decided to try their luck in California. They called it Gam Saan, which means Gold Mountain in Cantonese. By the early 1850s, thousands were arriving each year. Many worked in the gold fields. They often formed companies and worked claims that others had abandoned. They were patient and careful, and they often found gold that others had missed. Others opened shops, laundries and restaurants. San Francisco's Chinatown grew quickly. Even so, Chinese miners faced harsh discrimination. They were taxed heavily and sometimes driven from their claims. Yet they stayed and built communities. Later, many Chinese workers helped build the railway across the mountains. Today, San Francisco's Chinatown is one of the oldest and largest Chinese communities outside Asia.

[long-pause]

{img:54-foreign-miners-tax-collector-camp-1850}

In 1850, California passed a tax on foreign miners that drove thousands of them from the gold fields. As the gold rush grew, competition for good claims became fierce. Many American miners believed that the gold should belong to Americans. In 1850, the new state of California passed a law called the Foreign Miners' Tax. It required miners who were not American citizens to pay twenty dollars a month. That was a very large sum. The tax was aimed mainly at Mexican, Chilean and other Latin American miners. Many could not or would not pay. Thousands left the gold fields. Some returned home. The tax was so unpopular, and so damaging to trade in the mining towns, that it was cancelled the following year. Even so, a new tax was introduced in 1852, aimed mainly at Chinese miners. Yet the story of these taxes is an important one. It shows how fear and greed could lead to unfair treatment. It is a reminder that the gold rush was not equally open to everyone who came.

[long-pause]

{img:55-french-miners-camp-flag-tricolour-1850}

Thousands of French gold seekers sailed to California, some of them sent by a lottery in Paris. France also caught gold fever. In 1849 and the early 1850s, thousands of French people sailed to California. Some were adventurers. Others had been left poor by a revolution and economic troubles at home. A famous lottery in Paris, called the Lottery of the Golden Ingots, used its profits to send several thousand people to California. The French government saw it partly as a way to send away unemployed and restless men. In California, the French settled in San Francisco and in the mining camps. Many found work as cooks, bakers, wine sellers and shopkeepers. French restaurants appeared in San Francisco. Even so, French miners often faced hostility, like other foreigners. Yet they left their mark. San Francisco gained a French hospital, French newspapers and French bakeries. The French community became an important part of the city's life. Some French families who came during the gold rush stayed for many generations.

[long-pause]

{img:56-african-american-miner-freedom-california-1850}

Some African Americans found gold in California and used it to buy freedom for themselves or their families. Among the gold seekers were about two thousand African Americans by the early 1850s. Some were free men from the northern states. Others had been brought to California as enslaved people by their owners. California joined the United States in 1850 as a free state, where slavery was not allowed. Yet the law was not always enforced. Some enslaved people worked in the mines and were allowed to keep part of their earnings. A few used the money to buy their freedom. Some bought the freedom of family members still in the South. Free Black miners also worked claims and ran businesses. In San Francisco, a small but active Black community formed. They founded churches, newspapers and organisations to fight for equal rights. Even so, they faced discrimination and unjust laws. Yet their stories are an important part of the gold rush. For some, California offered a hope of freedom that could not be found elsewhere.

[long-pause]

{img:57-luzena-wilson-cooking-biscuits-camp-stove}

A pioneer woman named Luzena Wilson was offered five dollars for one of her biscuits. Luzena Stanley Wilson travelled to California by wagon in 1849, with her husband and two small sons. When they arrived in Sacramento, they had very little money. One evening, she was cooking supper over a campfire. A hungry miner came by. He smelled her biscuits and offered her five dollars for one. That was a huge sum. She could hardly believe it. He gave her the money, and she gave him the biscuit. She realised that she could earn a living by cooking. She later opened a boarding house and hotel. She fed many miners and became quite prosperous. Even so, her life was not easy. She lost her business twice, once to flood and once to fire. Yet each time, she started again. Many years later, she told her story to her daughter, who wrote it down. Her memoir is one of the best accounts of a woman's life in the gold rush.

[long-pause]

{img:58-dame-shirley-writing-letters-cabin-rich-bar}

A woman known as Dame Shirley wrote famous letters describing life in a mining camp. Louise Clappe was a well-educated woman from New England. In 1851, she moved with her husband, a doctor, to a remote mining camp called Rich Bar. It lay deep in the mountains on the Feather River. She was one of only a handful of women there. Over the next year, she wrote a series of long letters to her sister back east. She signed them with the name Dame Shirley. Her letters were witty, observant and full of detail. She described the cabins, the miners, the food and the landscape. She wrote about happy times and sad ones. She described fires, accidents, quarrels and celebrations. Her letters were later published in a San Francisco magazine. Even so, she never became rich. Her marriage ended, and she later worked as a teacher in San Francisco. Yet her letters are treasured today. Historians consider them one of the finest first-hand accounts of daily life in the gold rush camps.

[long-pause]

{img:59-woman-washing-laundry-business-mining-town}

Women who washed laundry for miners could earn more money than many of the miners themselves. There were very few women in the early gold rush years. In 1850, women made up only a small fraction of California's population. In the mining areas, the share was even smaller. This meant that women's work was in great demand. Miners needed their clothes washed, their meals cooked and their socks mended. Most men had never done these tasks. Women who offered these services could charge high prices. Washing a dozen shirts could earn several dollars. Some women ran laundries, bakeries, boarding houses and stores. They often earned more than the miners they served. Even so, the work was exhausting. It meant long days of scrubbing, cooking and carrying water. Yet it gave women an unusual degree of independence. Some women earned their own money for the first time in their lives. A few became wealthy business owners. The gold rush gave them opportunities that were rare elsewhere.

[long-pause]

{img:60-family-arriving-mining-town-wagon-children}

When women and children began arriving in the camps, the mining towns started to change. In the first years of the gold rush, the mining camps were almost entirely male. Then, slowly, more families arrived. Some wives travelled out to join husbands who had decided to stay. Others came with their families from the beginning. When women and children arrived, the camps began to change. Families wanted proper houses, schools and churches. They wanted shops that sold more than picks and whisky. They wanted laws and order. Many camps grew into real towns with schools, churches, libraries and newspapers. Miners who had lived rough for years began to build cabins with gardens. Even so, the change was gradual. Some camps remained rough places for many years. Yet the arrival of families marked the end of the wildest days of the rush. Many towns founded in the gold rush still exist today. Their old churches and schoolhouses show how families turned temporary camps into lasting homes.

[long-pause]

{img:61-miners-crowd-post-office-waiting-mail-line}

Miners would stand in line for hours at the post office, hoping for a letter from home. For miners far from home, letters were precious. They were the only link with their families. In the early days, mail took a very long time to reach California. Letters came by ship, either around Cape Horn or across Panama. They could take two months or more. When the mail ship arrived in San Francisco, the post office was overwhelmed. Long lines of men formed outside, stretching down the street. Some waited all night. Men who did not want to wait could buy a place near the front of the line from someone who had queued. In the mining camps, private express riders carried letters from the cities. Miners often paid a dollar or more for each letter. Even so, many letters never arrived. Some were lost at sea. Others went to the wrong camp. Yet when a letter did arrive, it was a moment of great joy. Men read their letters again and again until the paper wore thin.

[long-pause]

{img:62-miner-writing-letter-candle-cabin-night}

Many miners' letters survive, full of hope, homesickness and honest descriptions of their hard lives. Miners wrote many letters home. Thousands of them survive today, kept by families and libraries. They describe life in the gold fields in vivid detail. Some letters are full of excitement. They describe a lucky find or a promising new claim. Others are honest about the hardships. Writers tell of cold, rain, sickness, high prices and disappointment. Many letters express deep homesickness. Miners asked about their children, their parents and their farms. They described dreams of home. Some apologised for not sending more money. Others promised they would return soon. Even so, many miners tried to sound cheerful, so their families would not worry. Yet behind their words, the loneliness is often clear. Some letters were written by men who later died in California. Their families treasured these last messages for the rest of their lives. Together, the letters form one of the richest records of the gold rush.

[long-pause]

{img:63-lettersheet-illustrated-gold-rush-drawing}

Miners often wrote home on special printed letter sheets with pictures of life in the gold fields. In the 1850s, printers in San Francisco created a special kind of writing paper. These were called lettersheets. At the top of each sheet was a printed picture. It might show a mining camp, a view of San Francisco or a scene of miners at work. Some pictures were serious. Others were humorous, showing the troubles of miners in a funny way. Below the picture, there was space to write a letter. Miners bought these sheets to show their families what life in California looked like. In a time before photographs were common, these pictures were very valuable. Families back east could finally see the places their loved ones described. Even so, the pictures were sometimes idealised. They made life look neater and more cheerful than it was. Yet they are treasured by historians today. Hundreds of different designs were printed. They give a colourful picture of how the gold rush wanted to see itself.

[long-pause]

{img:64-daguerreotype-miner-portrait-pick-pan-studio}

Many miners had their photographs taken to send home, often posing proudly with their picks and pans. Photography was still quite new in the 1850s. The most common method was the daguerreotype, which made a single image on a silver-coated plate. Photographers set up studios in San Francisco and in larger mining towns. Many miners visited them. They wanted to send a picture of themselves home to their families. They often posed with their tools, such as picks, shovels and pans. Some held up nuggets or bags of gold. Others wore their best clothes. Some posed with their partners. Some travelling photographers took pictures of miners at work along the rivers. Even so, sitting for a photograph took patience. Subjects had to stay still for several seconds. Yet the results were remarkable. Hundreds of these images survive today. They show the real faces of the gold rush. Young men stare out from the past, tired, proud and hopeful, wanting their families to see them as they were.

[long-pause]

{img:65-mining-camp-winter-rain-mud-cabins-smoke}

In winter, heavy rain and snow could stop mining for weeks and cut camps off from supplies. Winter in the gold country was very different from summer. Summers were hot and dry. Winters brought heavy rain in the foothills and deep snow in the higher mountains. Rivers rose and flooded. Many river claims could not be worked. The winter of 1849 to 1850 was especially wet. Trails turned into mud. Mule trains could not get through to some camps. Supplies ran low, and prices soared. Some miners went down to the cities to wait for spring. Others stayed in their cabins. They read, wrote letters, played cards and repaired their tools. Some worked dry diggings, away from the rivers, when the weather allowed. Even so, winter could be lonely and difficult. Illness spread in the damp, crowded cabins. Yet the rain also brought something good. When spring came, the rivers had washed down fresh gravel. New gold might be waiting in the streams.

[long-pause]

{img:66-miners-reading-newspapers-cabin-winter-evening}

On long winter evenings, miners read every newspaper and book they could find, sometimes aloud to each other. Reading material was precious in the mining camps. Newspapers from San Francisco and the eastern states were eagerly passed from cabin to cabin. Some sold for a dollar or more. Men read them over and over, even the advertisements. Often, one miner read aloud while the others listened. Books were also shared. Bibles, novels, poems and old magazines travelled from camp to camp. Some miners kept small libraries in their cabins. In larger towns, reading rooms and lending libraries opened. Theatre companies and travelling lecturers also visited. Even so, many miners could not read well. Their friends read for them. Yet reading brought comfort on long, wet winter nights. It linked the miners to the wider world. A good story, read by the fire while the rain fell outside, helped many forget the cold, the mud and the loneliness of camp life for a while.

[long-pause]

{img:67-christmas-mining-camp-cabin-small-tree-candles}

Miners celebrated Christmas in their cabins with whatever treats they could find. Christmas was a special time even in the mining camps. Many miners felt their homesickness most strongly at Christmas. They thought of family dinners and church services far away. So they tried to make the day special. Some cut small pine trees and decorated them with candles or ribbons. Partners prepared a feast. They might cook a wild turkey, venison or a precious tin of oysters. Some made puddings or pies. Others bought special treats from the store, whatever the price. Some camps held dances or parties. Miners sang carols and Christmas songs. Even so, some Christmases were lonely. Snow or rain might keep men in their cabins. Yet many miners wrote warmly about their Christmas celebrations. In their letters home, they described simple feasts and kind companions. Far from family, they created small, warm holidays together in the rough cabins of the gold country.

[long-pause]

{img:68-large-gold-nugget-hand-dark-cloth-lamp}

In 1854, miners at Carson Hill found a single mass of gold weighing about one hundred and ninety-five pounds. Most miners found gold as small flakes and grains. Yet every miner dreamed of finding a large nugget. A few lucky people did. The largest mass of gold ever found in California came from Carson Hill, in Calaveras County. It was discovered in 1854. It weighed about one hundred and ninety-five pounds, or nearly ninety kilograms. It was worth a fortune at the time. Smaller nuggets of several pounds were found in many places. News of a big find spread quickly through the camps. Men rushed to stake claims nearby, hoping to find more. Even so, such finds were very rare. Most miners never found a nugget larger than a fingernail. Yet the stories of great nuggets kept the dream alive. They encouraged miners to keep digging through the long, hard days. Sadly, most of the great California nuggets were melted down for coins. Very few survive today. Their size is known only from old records.

[long-pause]

{img:69-miner-sitting-empty-pan-disappointed-river-dusk}

Most forty-niners earned little more than they would have earned at home, and many lost money. The gold rush is often remembered as a time of great fortunes. Yet for most people, the reality was very different. In 1848 and early 1849, some miners did make large amounts of money. The gold was easy to find. But as more people arrived, the best claims were taken. The easy gold was soon gone. By 1850 and 1851, the average miner earned only a few dollars a day. That sounds like a lot compared with wages in the east. Yet prices in California were so high that little was left over. Many miners went into debt. Some fell ill and could not work. Others gave up and went home with nothing. Even so, not everyone regretted the adventure. Many said they had learned a great deal. Yet the gold rush made only a small number of people truly rich. Most of the gold found its way into the hands of merchants, bankers and the owners of large mining companies.

[long-pause]

{img:70-lola-montez-house-grass-valley-garden}

The famous dancer Lola Montez lived for a time in the mining town of Grass Valley. The gold rush attracted entertainers of every kind. One of the most famous was Lola Montez. She was born in Ireland, but she presented herself as a Spanish dancer. She had become famous in Europe for her daring Spider Dance. She had even been the companion of the King of Bavaria. In 1853, she arrived in California. She performed in San Francisco and in the mining towns. Then she settled for a while in Grass Valley. She bought a small house with a garden. She kept pets, including a bear cub, and entertained visitors. The miners were fascinated by her. Even so, she was not only a performer. She encouraged a young girl in the town named Lotta Crabtree, who loved to sing and dance. Lotta later became one of the richest and most popular entertainers in America. A copy of Lola Montez's house now stands on the old spot in Grass Valley. It is used as a visitor centre for the town.

[long-pause]

{img:71-grizzly-bear-meadow-sierra-foothills-1850}

California grizzly bears were common in the gold country, and one still appears on the state flag. In 1849, California was home to many grizzly bears. They were large, powerful animals. Some weighed more than five hundred kilograms. They lived in the valleys, foothills and mountains. They ate grass, acorns, berries, salmon and sometimes cattle. Miners often met them. Many wrote about these encounters in their letters. Some miners hunted bears for meat and fur. Others captured them for shows. In some places, bears were made to fight bulls for entertainment, a cruel sport that was later banned. As more people arrived, the bears lost their homes and were hunted heavily. Even so, the grizzly became a symbol of California. A bear appears on the state flag, first raised in 1846. Yet the real California grizzly did not survive. The last known one was killed in the 1920s. Today, the bear lives on only on the flag, a quiet reminder of the wild land the forty-niners found.

[long-pause]

{img:72-salmon-run-clear-river-gold-country-autumn}

Before mining muddied them, the rivers of the gold country were full of salmon. The rivers that flowed down from the Sierra Nevada were once rich with fish. Every year, great runs of salmon swam up from the sea to lay their eggs in clear gravel beds. Native Californians had fished them for thousands of years. Early miners also caught salmon for food. Some wrote about rivers so full of fish that they could be caught by hand. Yet mining changed the rivers. Digging, damming and washing gravel filled the water with mud and sand. Later, hydraulic mining sent millions of tonnes of debris downstream. The gravel beds where salmon laid their eggs were buried. Fish numbers fell sharply. Even so, the rivers have slowly recovered in some places. Efforts have been made to restore salmon runs. Yet the old abundance has never fully returned. The story of the salmon shows how the search for gold affected not only people, but the whole living landscape of California.

[long-pause]

{img:73-sierra-nevada-meadow-wildflowers-mountains-dawn}

Some forty-niners were so moved by the beauty of the Sierra Nevada that they wrote more about the scenery than about gold. The Sierra Nevada is one of the most beautiful mountain ranges in America. It has granite peaks, deep valleys, clear lakes and huge trees. Many forty-niners had never seen anything like it. In their letters and diaries, they described the mountains with wonder. They wrote about giant pines, roaring waterfalls and meadows full of flowers. In 1851, a group of men chasing Native people entered a deep valley with tall granite walls. It was the Yosemite Valley. Some of them were struck by its beauty. A few years later, the first tourists came to see it. In 1852, a hunter came across a grove of giant sequoia trees, the largest living things on earth. Even so, the miners also harmed these landscapes. Forests were cut for timber and fuel. Yet the beauty of the Sierra helped inspire a new idea. In 1864, Yosemite was protected by law, one of the first wild places in the world to be set aside for everyone.

[long-pause]

{img:74-native-californian-village-oak-trees-acorns}

The gold rush brought devastating losses to the Native peoples of California. Before the gold rush, perhaps one hundred and fifty thousand Native people lived in California. They belonged to many different nations, with their own languages and traditions. They lived from fishing, hunting and gathering acorns, which they ground into flour. The gold rush changed their world almost overnight. Miners took over their rivers and hills. Mining muddied the streams and destroyed the salmon runs. Forests were cut down. Game was driven away. New diseases spread quickly. Violence against Native people was common, and the state government did little to stop it. Within about twenty years, the Native population had fallen to around thirty thousand. Even so, Native Californians survived. They kept their languages, songs and traditions alive, often in very hard conditions. Yet this loss is one of the darkest parts of the gold rush story. Today, many California tribes continue to rebuild their communities and share their history.

[long-pause]

{img:75-statehood-celebration-san-francisco-1850-flags}

Thanks to the gold rush, California became a state in 1850, only two years after the discovery. The gold rush brought so many people to California that it quickly needed a proper government. In 1849, Californians held a convention and wrote a state constitution. It banned slavery. They then asked to join the United States as a state. Normally, a territory had to wait many years before becoming a state. Yet California was growing so fast that Congress agreed quickly. On the ninth of September 1850, California became the thirty-first state. The news took more than a month to reach California by ship. When it arrived in San Francisco in October, the city held a great celebration. Bands played, guns were fired and flags waved. Even so, the decision had been difficult in Washington. California's entry as a free state upset the balance between free and slave states. It was part of a larger compromise. Yet for Californians, statehood was a proud moment. Their new home was now a full part of the nation.

[long-pause]

{img:76-hydraulic-mining-water-cannon-hillside-1860s}

Later, miners used powerful water cannons that washed away whole hillsides to reach the gold. By the 1850s, most easy surface gold had been found. Mining became a big business. Companies used larger and more powerful methods. One of the most dramatic was hydraulic mining. It began in 1853. Water was brought down from the mountains through long ditches and pipes. At the end, it shot out through a large iron nozzle with tremendous force. The jet of water could wash away whole hillsides. The mud and gravel were then sent through sluices to catch the gold. Hydraulic mining recovered huge amounts of gold. Yet it also caused terrible damage. Millions of tonnes of mud washed down the rivers. Riverbeds filled up, and farms and towns downstream flooded. Farmers fought back in the courts. In 1884, a judge ruled that the mines could no longer dump their waste into the rivers. This was one of the first important environmental decisions in American history.

[long-pause]

{img:77-john-sutter-old-man-washington-petition-1870s}

Neither John Sutter nor James Marshall became rich from the gold that was found on Sutter's land. The gold discovery brought ruin to John Sutter. His workers left to search for gold. Gold seekers swarmed over his land, killed his cattle and took his property. Courts later decided that many of his land claims were not valid. Sutter lost most of what he had owned. He eventually moved east to Pennsylvania. For years, he travelled to Washington, asking Congress to pay him for his losses. It never did. He died in 1880, in a hotel in Washington, still waiting. James Marshall, who found the first gold, did not do much better. He tried mining and other businesses, but most failed. For a while, California paid him a small pension. He died poor in 1885, near Coloma. Even so, he was not forgotten. In 1890, the state built the tall statue of him that still stands above the valley. Yet both men are reminders that the gold rush did not reward everyone fairly.

[long-pause]

{img:78-mark-twain-young-writer-mining-town-hotel}

The young Mark Twain heard a story in a mining camp that became his first famous tale. Long after the first rush, the gold country still attracted writers and storytellers. In the winter of 1864 to 1865, a young writer named Samuel Clemens stayed in the mining area of Calaveras County. He wrote under the name Mark Twain. In a tavern, he heard a funny story from an old miner. It was about a man who bet on a jumping frog. The man's rival secretly filled the frog with lead shot, so it could not jump. Twain wrote the story down in his own words. It was published in 1865 as The Celebrated Jumping Frog of Calaveras County. It became a great success and made Twain famous across America. Even so, it was only the beginning of his career. He went on to write Tom Sawyer and Huckleberry Finn. Yet he never forgot the mining camps. Today, Calaveras County still holds a frog-jumping contest every year, in honour of his story.

[long-pause]

{img:79-gold-country-highway-49-old-town-main-street}

Today, the old mining towns of California's gold country are linked by a road named Highway 49, after the forty-niners. Many of the towns that grew up during the gold rush still exist today. They lie in the foothills of the Sierra Nevada, in what is now called the Gold Country. They are linked by a winding road called Highway 49, named after the forty-niners. Towns like Sonora, Nevada City, Placerville and Columbia still have their old buildings. There are brick stores with heavy iron shutters, built to resist fire. There are wooden hotels with balconies and old churches. At Columbia, the whole town is preserved as a state park. Visitors can ride a stagecoach, watch a blacksmith at work and pan for gold. Even so, many camps disappeared completely. Only a name on a map or a few stones remain. Yet the Gold Country is a peaceful place now. Its rivers run clear again in many places. Oak trees and pines cover the hills. It is hard to imagine the noise and crowds of 1849.

[long-pause]

{img:80-california-state-seal-eureka-golden-hills-sunset}

California's state motto is "Eureka", a Greek cry of discovery. The gold rush shaped California forever. Its population grew enormously. San Francisco became a great city. Farms, railways and new industries followed. People from all over the world settled there. In memory of the gold rush, California chose a special motto. It is the Greek word Eureka, which announces that something has been found. According to legend, the ancient Greek scientist Archimedes shouted it when he made a famous discovery. For California, it refers to the discovery of gold. The state is also known as the Golden State. Its official state mineral is gold, and the poppy, its state flower, is golden orange. Even so, most forty-niners never found the fortune they dreamed of. Yet many stayed and built new lives. Their adventure helped create one of the most important places in the world. In the golden light of a California evening, the hills still seem to glow with that old promise.

[long-pause]

So the great rush for gold slowly settles into history.

In the foothills, the last cabin fires burn low. A tin pan rests against a doorway, still wet from the river, and a pair of muddy boots stands beside it. The stream runs on in the dark, carrying its sand and its small hidden flakes down towards the valley, just as it has for thousands of years.
