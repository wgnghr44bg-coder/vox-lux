Tonight we travel back about one hundred and seventy-five years. We go to California, on the far western edge of America. The year is 1849. And all over the world, people are talking about one thing: gold.

[long-pause]

It began about a year earlier, on a cold January morning in 1848. In a quiet valley in the foothills of the Sierra Nevada mountains, a group of men were building a sawmill. It stood beside a fast, clear river, called the American River. The man in charge of building it was a carpenter called James Marshall. Every morning, he walked along the channel that carried water to the mill, to check it.

[pause]

On that morning, he saw something shining in the cold water. He bent down and picked it up. It was a little flake of yellow metal, about the size of a small pea. He picked up a few more. He bit one of them, and it was soft. He hammered one with a stone, and it went flat, but did not break. He thought it might be gold.

[pause]

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

{img:gold-flakes-in-hand-cold-river-water-pan-sunlight-close}
It was gold. The men at the mill tried to keep it a secret. But secrets like that do not stay secret for long. Within a few months, people from all over California were rushing to the rivers to look for gold. And by the end of 1848, the news had reached the rest of America, and the rest of the world. In 1849, hundreds of thousands of people set off for California, hoping to become rich.

[pause]

These people became known as the forty-niners, after the year 1849. They came from the eastern states of America, from Mexico and Chile, from China and Hawaii, from Europe and Australia. They came by land and by sea. Most of them were young men. Most of them had never seen a gold mine in their lives. And most of them did not become rich. But together, they changed California forever.

[pause]

{img:mining-camp-1849-tents-cabins-river-pine-hills-smoke-evening}
Tonight we will follow a father and his son from a small town in Massachusetts, on the east coast of America. We will sail with them on a long voyage, all the way around the bottom of South America. We will arrive with them in the noisy tent city of San Francisco. We will travel up the rivers to the gold fields, and live in a mining camp. We will learn how to wash gold from the sand of the rivers. And we will see what they found in the end, which was not quite what they expected.

[long-pause]

There will be some exciting moments in this story, in storms at sea and in the hard winter in the mountains. But mostly, it is a story about ordinary people, far from home, working hard and hoping. It is a story about friendship and letters, about patience and luck. And it is a story about a family that missed each other very much.

[pause]

{img:father-son-1849-standing-ship-deck-looking-west-sunset-hope}
So let us begin in a quiet town on the coast of Massachusetts, in the cold winter of 1848. Snow lies on the roofs and the fields. The harbour is grey and still. And in a little wooden house near the church, a family is sitting around the fire, reading a newspaper. The newspaper says there is gold in California. Gold in the rivers, gold in the hills, gold for anyone who goes to find it. And the father of the family puts down the newspaper, and looks at his son.

[pause]

The valley where the gold was found was owned by a man called John Sutter. He had come to California from Switzerland, and built a great farm and a fort near the river. He wanted the sawmill to cut wood for his buildings. When he heard about the gold, he was not happy. He knew that people would come and take over his land. And he was right.

[pause]

In 1849, most people in the eastern states had never been to California. They had only read about it in books and newspapers. They thought of it as a faraway land of sunshine and oranges, of mountains and the Pacific Ocean. Now they also thought of it as a land of gold. And thousands of them decided to see it for themselves.

[long-pause]

{img:newspaper-1848-headline-gold-california-men-reading-general-store}
How did the news of gold spread across the world, in a time without telephones, radio or the internet?

[pause]

At first, it spread slowly. California was very far from the rest of America. In 1848, it had only just become part of the United States, after a war with Mexico. There were no railways to the west, and no telegraph lines. Letters and news travelled by ship, around South America, or by riders across the deserts and mountains. It took months.

[long-pause]

In San Francisco, then just a small town of about eight hundred people, a shopkeeper called Sam Brannan heard about the gold. He was a clever businessman. He bought up all the picks and shovels and pans he could find. Then, one day in May 1848, he walked through the streets of San Francisco, holding up a little bottle of gold dust. Gold, he shouted. Gold from the American River! Within weeks, almost every man in San Francisco had left for the gold fields. And Sam Brannan sold all his shovels.

[pause]

{img:san-francisco-1848-small-town-empty-streets-men-leaving-for-gold}
Ships sailing from California carried the news to Hawaii, to Oregon, to Mexico and to Chile. People there began to set off for California too. Then, in December 1848, the president of the United States, James Polk, spoke about the gold in a speech to Congress. He said there was more gold in California than anyone could have imagined. After that, everyone believed it.

[long-pause]

All over the eastern states, people caught what they called gold fever. Newspapers printed stories of men who had found huge nuggets of gold in a single day. Shops sold guidebooks to California and special tools for finding gold. Young men formed companies, groups of friends who would travel together and share what they found. Farmers sold their farms, and shopkeepers sold their shops, to pay for the journey.

[pause]

{img:general-store-1849-selling-gold-pans-shovels-guidebooks-eager-men}
There were three main ways to get to California from the east. The first was overland, by wagon, across the plains and the mountains. It took about five months, and it could only be done in summer. The second was by ship, all the way around Cape Horn, at the southern tip of South America. That took five or six months too. The third was by ship to Panama, then across the narrow strip of land through the jungle, and then by ship again up the Pacific coast. That was fastest, but it was crowded and could be unhealthy.

[long-pause]

In the harbours of the east coast, ships of every kind were made ready for California. There were old whaling ships and cargo ships, fast clippers and slow barques. Any ship that could float was used. Thousands of men stood in line to buy tickets. The harbours of Boston and New York were crowded with men and their luggage. They carried picks and shovels, tents and blankets, and strange machines that they had been told would wash gold.

[pause]

{img:boston-harbour-1849-ships-crowds-men-luggage-tools-departing-winter}
Not everyone thought it was a good idea. Some newspapers warned that the journey was long and dangerous. They said that most men would not find much gold. They said that prices in California were very high, and that life there was hard. Some wives and mothers begged their husbands and sons to stay at home. But most of the men who caught gold fever did not listen. They dreamed of coming home rich, in a year or two. And they set off, full of hope.

[pause]

In the towns of New England, the talk was all of California. In the general stores and the churches, at the mills and the harbours, men talked about nothing else. Some went to meetings to form companies. Some sold everything they had to buy a ticket. Wives and mothers sewed shirts and knitted socks. And children played at being gold miners in the snow, with spoons and pie dishes.

[pause]

Some people made up funny songs and cartoons about the gold seekers. The cartoons showed men going to California with enormous shovels and silly machines. They showed them coming back with bags of gold, or with nothing at all. People laughed at them. But many of the people who laughed were secretly thinking about going too.

[long-pause]

{img:massachusetts-town-1848-wooden-house-snow-church-harbour-winter}
Let us meet the family from Massachusetts. They live in a small town on the coast, not far from Boston.

[pause]

The father is called Samuel Turner. He is about forty years old, a carpenter. He builds houses and barns, and makes doors and windows and furniture. He is a careful, steady man, good with his hands. He is not rich, but he earns enough to keep his family. He has a workshop behind the house, where he works in the winter.

[pause]

The mother is called Hannah. She is a strong, practical woman, with quick hands and a warm smile. She runs the house and the garden, and keeps chickens and a cow. She sews all the family's clothes. She also takes in sewing for neighbours, to earn a little extra. She can read and write well, and she loves to read the newspapers and books from the town library.

[long-pause]

They have two children. The son is called Eli, and he is fourteen. He is tall and thin, with sandy hair and freckles. He has helped his father in the workshop since he was small. He can use a saw and a plane, and he knows all the different kinds of wood. He is curious and adventurous. He has read every story about faraway places that he can find. And when he reads about the gold in California, his eyes light up.

[pause]

{img:boy-fourteen-1848-reading-newspaper-by-fire-excited-father-watching}
The daughter is called Ruth, and she is ten. She is quieter than her brother, with dark braids and serious eyes. She loves to draw, and she draws everything: the birds, the boats in the harbour, the cow in the barn. She is very close to her brother. When she hears that he might go to California, she does not say anything. But she goes up to her room and cries a little.

[long-pause]

The family is not poor, but life is not easy. Samuel has had less work this year. Some of the families in town cannot afford to build new houses. Prices are going up. Hannah worries about money. She counts the coins in the jar on the kitchen shelf every week. When Samuel reads about the gold in California, he thinks about all the things he could do with a little more money. He could build a bigger house. He could send the children to a good school. He could stop worrying.

[pause]

{img:kitchen-1848-new-england-family-table-oil-lamp-discussion-evening}
For weeks, the family talks about it in the evenings. Samuel wants to go, and to take Eli with him. Eli is old enough, he says, and strong, and he can help. Hannah is not sure. California is so far away, and the journey is long and hard. What if something happens to them? What if they find nothing? But in the end, she agrees. Two years, she says. Not a day more. And you must write to me every week.

[long-pause]

Samuel joins a company of men from the town, who are going to California together. There are twelve of them. They each pay a share of the costs. They buy tickets on a ship from Boston, sailing around Cape Horn. They buy tools and tents and food for the journey. They agree to share everything they find in California equally. The leader of the company is a doctor called Dr. Hale, a kind and sensible man.

[pause]

{img:group-men-1849-company-signing-agreement-table-tools-piled-town-hall}
The ship will leave Boston in February 1849. That gives the family a few more weeks together. They spend them quietly. Samuel finishes his last jobs in town. Hannah sews warm shirts and thick socks for her husband and son. Eli packs and unpacks his little bag again and again. And Ruth draws a picture of the family, all four of them, standing in front of the house. She gives it to Eli, to take with him to California.

[pause]

On Sundays, the family goes to the white wooden church in the middle of the town. After church, they walk along the harbour, looking at the boats. In summer, they have picnics on the beach and pick blueberries on the hills. In winter, they go skating on the frozen pond. It is a simple, quiet life. And in the evenings, Hannah reads aloud to the family from a book, by the light of the oil lamp.

[pause]

Grandfather Turner, Samuel's father, was a sailor who sailed all over the world. He died before Eli was born. But Samuel has his old sea chest in the attic. Inside, there is a compass, a spyglass and a few old charts. Eli loves to take them out and look at them. He decides to take the old compass with him to California, for luck.

[long-pause]

{img:boston-wharf-february-1849-ship-departing-families-waving-snow-cold}
On a cold morning in February 1849, the family travels to Boston. The ship is waiting at the wharf. It is called the Orion, a wooden sailing ship with three masts. It is not new, and not very big. But it is strong, and it has made long voyages before.

[pause]

The wharf is crowded with people. There are hundreds of men going to California, with their families come to see them off. There are piles of luggage and boxes of tools everywhere. Sailors are loading barrels and crates onto the ship. A band is playing cheerful music. And snow is falling softly on everything.

[long-pause]

Samuel and Eli carry their bags up the gangplank onto the ship. They find their bunks, in a crowded room below deck. Then they come back up to say goodbye. Hannah hugs Eli for a long time. She tells him to be good, and to listen to his father, and to keep warm. She gives him a little Bible, with her name written inside. Then she hugs Samuel. Two years, she says. Not a day more. He promises.

[pause]

{img:mother-hugging-son-ship-gangplank-little-sister-crying-1849}
Ruth stands holding her mother's hand. She does not cry, but her lip is shaking. Eli kneels down in front of her. He tells her that he will bring her back a gold nugget, as big as an egg. She laughs a little, through her tears. Then she gives him one more drawing. It is a picture of a ship, with two little figures standing on the deck. She has written underneath: Come home soon.

[long-pause]

The bell on the ship rings. All the passengers must go on board. The gangplank is pulled up. The sailors untie the ropes. Slowly, the ship moves away from the wharf. The band plays a last song. The people on the wharf wave their hats and handkerchiefs. On the ship, the men wave back. Eli finds his mother and sister in the crowd and waves until he cannot see them anymore.

[pause]

{img:ship-sailing-out-boston-harbour-winter-grey-sea-snow-receding-town}
The ship sails out of Boston harbour, past the islands and the lighthouse. The town gets smaller and smaller behind them. The wind is cold and the sea is grey. Many of the passengers stay on deck for a long time, watching the land disappear. Some of them are quiet. Some of them are laughing and joking, excited about the adventure. Samuel stands at the rail beside Eli, with his hand on his son's shoulder.

[long-pause]

At home, Hannah and Ruth take the train back to their town. The house seems very quiet and empty without Samuel and Eli. That evening, they eat supper alone at the kitchen table. Hannah lights the lamp and puts a map of the world on the table. She traces the route of the ship with her finger. Down the coast of America, across the equator, around Cape Horn, and up the other side to California. It is a very long way.

[pause]

{img:mother-daughter-kitchen-table-world-map-tracing-route-lamp-evening}
Ruth asks how long it will take. Five months, perhaps six, says Hannah. That is the whole spring and the whole summer. Ruth decides she will draw a picture every day, of what she thinks Eli is doing. She will keep them all in a box, and give them to him when he comes home. Hannah thinks that is a lovely idea. She says she will write a letter every week, even though the letters will take months to arrive. And that night, she writes the first one.

[pause]

On the ship, Samuel and Eli unpack their bags in their bunks. Their bunks are one above the other, in a corner of the steerage. Samuel takes the bottom one, and Eli the top one. Eli pins Ruth's drawing to the wooden wall beside his pillow. Then he takes out his grandfather's compass and looks at the needle. It points north, toward home. And then the ship turns, and the needle swings slowly around.

[pause]

In the weeks after they leave, Hannah keeps busy. She takes in more sewing, to earn a little extra money. She looks after the cow and the chickens, and the garden in the spring. Ruth helps her, and goes to school, and draws her pictures every evening. The neighbours are very kind. They come to visit, and bring cakes and news. But the house still feels empty without Samuel and Eli.

[long-pause]

{img:sailing-ship-1849-atlantic-full-sail-blue-sea-sunshine-passengers-deck}
Life on board the Orion is crowded and simple. There are about a hundred and thirty passengers, and a crew of about twenty sailors. The ship is only about forty metres long.

[pause]

The passengers sleep in a big room below deck, called the steerage. There are rows of narrow wooden bunks, two or three on top of each other. Each bunk is just big enough for one man to lie down. There is no privacy at all. The room is dark and stuffy, and it smells of tar and wet wool. When the sea is rough, everything moves and creaks, and the men roll around in their bunks.

[long-pause]

The food is simple and the same every day. There is salted beef or salted pork, hard ship's biscuits, beans and rice, and dried apples. There is coffee and tea, with a little molasses for sweetness. On Sundays, there is a plum pudding, made with flour and raisins. The men eat at long tables below deck, or sitting on the deck in good weather. They grumble about the food, but they eat it all.

[pause]

{img:passengers-eating-long-table-below-deck-tin-plates-lantern-swaying}
To pass the time, the passengers find many things to do. They read books and newspapers, again and again. They play cards and checkers and dominoes. They write letters and diaries. They sing songs and play instruments: fiddles and banjos, flutes and harmonicas. One of the most popular songs is about California, set to the tune of an old song called Oh, Susanna. Oh, California, they sing, that's the land for me.

[long-pause]

Some of the passengers hold classes, to teach each other useful things. Dr. Hale gives talks about staying healthy. A man who has read about mining explains how to wash gold. Another man teaches a little Spanish, because many people in California speak it. Eli goes to all the classes. He learns a few Spanish words. Hola means hello. Gracias means thank you. Oro means gold.

[pause]

{img:men-on-deck-singing-banjo-fiddle-evening-sun-sails-above}
After a few weeks, the ship sails into warm waters, near the equator. The weather becomes hot and sunny. The men take off their coats and sit in the shade of the sails. They see flying fish leaping out of the water, gliding over the waves on their wing-like fins. Sometimes one lands on the deck, and the cook grills it for supper. They see dolphins swimming beside the ship, playing in the bow wave.

[long-pause]

When the ship crosses the equator, there is a funny old ceremony. One of the sailors dresses up as King Neptune, the king of the sea, with a crown and a long beard made of rope. All the passengers who have never crossed the equator before must come before him. He pretends to shave them with a big wooden razor, and then they are dunked in a tub of seawater. Everyone laughs and cheers. Eli is dunked too, and comes up spluttering and grinning.

[pause]

{img:equator-crossing-ceremony-sailor-neptune-crown-passengers-laughing-tub}
In the evenings, Samuel and Eli often sit together on the deck, looking at the stars. In the south, there are new stars they have never seen before. The sailors show them the Southern Cross, four bright stars in the shape of a cross. Samuel tells Eli about his own father, who was a sailor too. Eli tells his father about his hopes for California. They talk for a long time, as the ship sails on through the warm night.

[pause]

Some of the passengers keep diaries of the voyage. They write about the weather, the food and the other passengers. They write about the fish and the birds they see. They write about their hopes for California. Many of these diaries have survived, and they are kept in libraries today. They tell us a lot about the long voyage around Cape Horn.

[pause]

Eli makes friends with the cabin boy, a boy called Jack, who is about his age. Jack has been at sea for two years already. He teaches Eli to tie sailors' knots and to climb a little way up the rigging. He tells him stories of whales and storms and faraway ports. In return, Eli helps Jack peel potatoes in the galley. The cook gives them both an extra piece of pudding on Sundays.

[long-pause]

{img:cape-horn-1849-stormy-sea-grey-waves-ship-small-sails-cold}
After about three months at sea, the Orion comes to the most dangerous part of the voyage. It must sail around Cape Horn, the southern tip of South America.

[pause]

Cape Horn is a rocky island at the very end of the continent. Beyond it, there is nothing but ocean, all the way to the ice of Antarctica. Here, the Atlantic Ocean meets the Pacific Ocean. The winds blow almost always from the west, very strong and very cold. Great waves roll all the way around the world, with nothing to stop them. Sailors say it is the hardest place in the world to sail.

[long-pause]

As the ship sails south, the weather gets colder and colder. It is winter in the south now, because the seasons are reversed. The passengers put on all their warm clothes again. There is ice on the ropes in the mornings. Snow falls on the deck. The days get shorter. The sky is grey, and the sea is grey, and the wind never stops.

[pause]

{img:passengers-huddled-below-deck-cold-blankets-lantern-ship-rolling}
For almost three weeks, the ship fights its way around the Horn. The wind blows against it, and the waves are huge. The sailors work day and night, climbing the icy rigging to change the sails. The ship rolls and pitches in the waves. Water crashes over the deck. The passengers stay below, wrapped in blankets, holding on to their bunks.

[long-pause]

One night, there is a great storm. The wind howls through the rigging. The waves are as high as houses. The ship climbs up each wave, slowly, and then slides down the other side. Things fall off the shelves and roll around the floor. Some of the men are frightened, and some are seasick. Eli lies in his bunk, holding on tight, listening to the creaking of the ship. His father lies in the bunk below him. Every now and then, Samuel reaches up and squeezes his son's hand.

[pause]

{img:sailors-climbing-icy-rigging-storm-night-lanterns-waves-crashing}
The captain stays on deck all night, giving orders. He is an old sailor who has been around the Horn many times. He knows what to do. The sailors take in almost all the sails, and the ship rides out the storm. By the morning, the wind has calmed a little. The passengers come up on deck, tired and pale. The sea is still rough, but the worst is over. The ship has come through safely.

[long-pause]

A few days later, the wind changes, and the ship turns north at last. They have rounded Cape Horn. Now they are sailing into the Pacific Ocean. Everyone cheers. The captain gives every man an extra ration of coffee and sugar, to celebrate. That evening, there is singing and fiddle music below deck, for the first time in weeks.

[pause]

{img:ship-turning-north-pacific-calm-dawn-albatross-flying-relief}
As they sail north, a great white bird follows the ship. It is an albatross, with enormous wings, longer than a man is tall. It glides beside the ship for hours, hardly moving its wings at all. The sailors say an albatross brings good luck. Eli watches it for a long time. He thinks it is the most beautiful bird he has ever seen. That night, he writes about it in his diary, and draws a little picture of it for Ruth.

[pause]

Even in the worst weather, the cook keeps the stove going in the galley. He makes hot coffee and soup for everyone. The passengers hold their mugs with both hands, to warm their fingers. Nothing has ever tasted so good. Samuel says that the cook is the bravest man on the ship. The cook laughs and says that he just likes to keep busy.

[pause]

Off the coast of Tierra del Fuego, the land at the bottom of South America, the passengers see little fires burning on the shore at night. The sailors say they are the fires of the people who live there. They live in one of the coldest and windiest places in the world. They paddle canoes in the icy water and fish and hunt seals. Eli wonders how anyone can live in such a cold place. And he admires them very much.

[long-pause]

{img:valparaiso-chile-1849-harbour-colourful-houses-hills-ships-sunny}
A few weeks after rounding Cape Horn, the Orion stops at a port in Chile, called Valparaíso. It is a chance to rest, and to buy fresh food and water.

[pause]

Valparaíso is a busy harbour town, built on steep hills above a wide bay. The houses are painted in bright colours: yellow and blue, pink and green. They climb up the hills, one above the other. There are churches with white towers, and markets full of fruit. The harbour is full of ships, many of them on their way to California too.

[long-pause]

The passengers go ashore for a few days. After months at sea, it is wonderful to walk on solid ground again. Their legs feel strange at first, as if the ground were moving. They walk through the streets, looking at everything. They eat fresh bread and fruit, and drink fresh water. Some of them sleep in an inn, in a real bed. Eli thinks it is the best bed he has ever slept in.

[pause]

{img:valparaiso-market-fruit-stalls-peaches-grapes-sailors-buying-1849}
At the market, there are fruits that Eli has never seen before. There are big juicy peaches and bunches of green grapes. There are figs and melons and oranges. There are little sweet cakes made with honey. Eli buys a bag of peaches and eats three of them, one after another, with juice running down his chin. His father laughs and eats two himself.

[long-pause]

In Valparaíso, everyone is talking about California too. Many Chileans are going there to look for gold. Some of them are experienced miners, from the silver and copper mines of Chile. They know much more about mining than the men from Massachusetts. Dr. Hale talks with some of them in the inn. They tell him about the best ways to find gold, and the best tools to use. He listens carefully and takes notes.

[pause]

{img:chilean-miners-explaining-gold-pan-americans-listening-inn-evening}
Samuel posts a letter home at Valparaíso. It will travel back on another ship, around Cape Horn and up to Boston. It will take months to arrive. In the letter, he tells Hannah that they are well, and that they have come safely around the Horn. He tells her about the storm, but not too much, so she will not worry. He tells her that Eli is growing taller every week. And he tells her that he misses her very much.

[long-pause]

After a few days, the ship sails again, north along the coast of South America. The weather becomes warmer and sunnier. The Pacific Ocean is calmer than the Atlantic, and the voyage is easier now. The passengers spend their days on deck, in the sunshine. They clean and sharpen their tools, ready for the gold fields. They talk about nothing but gold.

[pause]

{img:men-sharpening-picks-shovels-deck-sunshine-pacific-calm-1849}
Some days, they see whales, spouting water high into the air, far away across the sea. Some days, they see the coast of South America, with high mountains rising behind it, with snow on their tops. Those are the Andes, the longest mountain range in the world. Eli stands at the rail and looks at them for hours. He has never imagined that the world was so big and so beautiful. And he thinks of Ruth, and wishes she could see it too.

[pause]

In Valparaíso, there are ships from all over the world. There are British and French ships, American whalers and Chilean coastal boats. Sailors from many countries walk the streets. There are little taverns by the harbour, where they sing songs in many languages. Eli and Samuel walk along the harbour and look at all the ships. Samuel tells Eli the names of the different kinds, as his own father once told him.

[pause]

Before the ship leaves, the passengers buy fresh food to take on board. They buy barrels of apples and onions, sacks of potatoes, and live chickens in wooden cages. The cook is very pleased. For the next few weeks, the food on the ship is much better. There are fresh eggs for breakfast, and onions in the stew. Everyone feels healthier.

[long-pause]

{img:san-francisco-bay-1849-hundreds-abandoned-ships-masts-forest-tents-hills}
In August 1849, after almost six months at sea, the Orion sails through a narrow gap between two headlands. On the other side lies a great bay. And on the edge of the bay, on sandy hills, is San Francisco.

[pause]

The first thing the passengers see is the ships. There are hundreds of them, anchored in the bay. Their masts stand so close together that they look like a forest of bare trees. Many of them are empty. When they arrived, their crews ran off to the gold fields, and left the ships behind. Some of them will never sail again. Later, some will be pulled up onto the shore and used as shops and hotels and warehouses.

[long-pause]

San Francisco itself is a strange and exciting sight. A year ago, it was a quiet little town. Now it is full of people, perhaps twenty thousand of them, and more arriving every day. There are a few wooden buildings, and hundreds and hundreds of tents. There are tents of canvas and tents made of old sails. There are little huts made from packing crates and wood from old ships. The streets are just sand and mud.

[pause]

{img:san-francisco-1849-muddy-street-tents-wooden-shops-crowds-busy}
The streets are crowded with people from all over the world. There are Americans from every state, in red flannel shirts and big boots. There are Mexicans in wide hats and colourful blankets. There are Chileans and Peruvians, Hawaiians and Chinese, Frenchmen and Germans and Irish. Everyone is talking at once, in many languages. Everyone is in a hurry. And everyone is talking about gold.

[long-pause]

The prices in San Francisco are amazing. Everything is very expensive, because there is so little of everything and so many people. A loaf of bread costs as much as a man earns in a whole day back in Massachusetts. A pair of boots costs a small fortune. A room in a hotel costs more for one night than a family pays for a month at home. Samuel shakes his head. We had better find gold quickly, he says.

[pause]

{img:shop-1849-san-francisco-high-prices-shopkeeper-scales-gold-dust-customers}
In the shops, people pay with gold dust. The shopkeeper has a little set of scales on the counter. The customer pours a little gold dust from a leather bag onto the scale. The shopkeeper weighs it carefully. A pinch of gold dust, between the thumb and finger, is worth about a dollar. People joke that shopkeepers with big thumbs get rich quickly.

[long-pause]

Samuel notices something else. Carpenters are earning a lot of money in San Francisco. Everyone needs houses and shops and warehouses. There are not enough carpenters to build them all. A carpenter can earn sixteen dollars a day here. That is more than he earns in two weeks at home. Samuel thinks about it carefully. But the company has agreed to go to the gold fields together. So he decides to go with them, at least for now.

[pause]

{img:carpenters-building-wooden-houses-san-francisco-1849-hammering-busy}
The company spends a few days in San Francisco, buying supplies and getting ready. They camp in a tent on a sandy hill, with a view of the bay. In the evenings, Eli sits outside the tent and watches the city. Lamps glow in the tents and the windows. Music and laughter drift up from the gambling halls. Ships come and go in the bay. It is noisy and crowded and wild. And Eli thinks it is the most exciting place he has ever been.

[pause]

In San Francisco, there are also many new businesses. There are newspapers and theatres, bakeries and banks. A man from Boston has opened a restaurant serving oysters. A woman from France has opened a little shop selling hats. A Chinese merchant has opened a store selling tea and silk and rice. Everyone is trying to make a living. And most of them are working very hard.

[pause]

On their first evening in San Francisco, Samuel and Eli eat dinner in a little restaurant in a tent. It costs a lot of money, but they want to celebrate their arrival. They have beef stew with potatoes, fresh bread and butter, and a slice of apple pie. It is the best meal they have had for six months. Eli eats two slices of pie, and his father does not stop him.

[pause]

There are also many children in San Francisco now, though not many yet. Some came with their families from the east. Some are the children of Mexican and Californian families who lived here before. Eli sees a group of boys selling newspapers in the street, shouting the headlines. He sees a little girl selling flowers. He thinks of Ruth, and wishes she were here to see it all.

[long-pause]

{img:river-steamboat-1849-sacramento-river-passengers-crowded-deck-tules}
To reach the gold fields, the company must travel inland, up the rivers. They buy tickets on a small boat going up the Sacramento River.

[pause]

The boat is crowded with men, all going to the gold fields. They sit on their bags and boxes, on the deck and below. The boat travels slowly up the river, through wide flat country. On both sides, there are marshes full of tall reeds, called tules. Thousands of birds live there: ducks and geese, herons and cranes. In some places, there are great oak trees along the banks, with grassy meadows under them.

[long-pause]

After a day or two, they reach a new town called Sacramento. It is the gateway to the gold fields. A year ago, there was almost nothing here. Now there are tents and wooden buildings, shops and hotels, crowded together along the river. Boats unload goods on the riverbank. Wagons and mules carry them away into the hills. Everyone is busy, buying and selling.

[pause]

{img:sacramento-1849-riverbank-tents-wooden-stores-mule-trains-dusty}
From Sacramento, the company sets off on foot toward the hills. They buy two mules to carry their tools and supplies. Mules are strong and sure-footed, and they can carry heavy loads up steep paths. The men walk beside them. It is hot and dusty, because it is late summer, and there has been no rain for months. The grass is dry and golden. The road is crowded with men going up to the mines, and some men coming back.

[long-pause]

As they climb into the foothills, the land changes. There are rolling hills covered with oak trees and pine trees. There are deep valleys with rivers running through them. The air is fresher, and smells of pine. In the distance, they can see the high mountains of the Sierra Nevada, with a little snow still on their tops. It is beautiful country.

[pause]

{img:foothills-sierra-nevada-1849-oak-pine-trail-men-mules-walking-dusty}
Along the way, they meet men coming back from the mines. Some of them look happy, with heavy bags of gold dust. Some of them look tired and thin and disappointed. Some say there is gold everywhere. Some say all the good places are already taken. The company does not know whom to believe. But they keep walking.

[long-pause]

At night, they camp beside the road. They light a small fire and cook beans and bacon. They sleep on the ground, wrapped in their blankets. The stars are very bright up here, away from the cities. Coyotes howl far away in the hills. Eli lies awake for a while, listening. He thinks about home, and about the long journey they have made. And he thinks that tomorrow, perhaps, he will see gold in a river for the first time.

[pause]

{img:campfire-night-foothills-men-sleeping-blankets-mules-stars-coyote}
After four days of walking, they come to a river in a deep green valley. It is a branch of the American River, not far from where the gold was first found. There are many tents and cabins along its banks already. Men are working in the river, standing in the water, bent over their pans. Dr. Hale looks around and says: This is the place. And the company begins to unpack.

[pause]

On the river boat, Eli sits at the front and watches the water. Fish jump out of the river, catching flies. Great flocks of geese fly overhead, honking. On the banks, he sees deer drinking at the water's edge. They lift their heads and watch the boat go by, and then go back to drinking. Eli has never seen so much wild country.

[long-pause]

{img:mining-camp-1849-river-valley-tents-log-cabins-men-working-water}
The mining camp is in a narrow valley, beside a fast, clear river. Pine trees and oaks grow on the steep slopes on both sides. Along the banks of the river, there are tents and little cabins, scattered among the rocks.

[pause]

The camp has a name, like all the mining camps. The men call it Rough and Ready Bar. Other camps nearby have funny names too: Hangtown, Poker Flat, Fiddletown, Shirt Tail Canyon, You Bet, Rich Bar. Many of them were named by the miners, often as a joke. Some of the names are still on the map of California today.

[long-pause]

The company sets up its tents on a flat place above the river. Then they start building a cabin, because winter will come in a few months. Samuel is in charge of the building, because he is a carpenter. The men cut pine logs from the hillside with axes. They drag them down to the camp with the mules. They notch the ends so the logs fit together at the corners. And they build the walls, log by log.

[pause]

{img:men-building-log-cabin-pine-logs-axes-carpenter-father-son-sunny}
Eli helps his father with the building. He learns to notch the logs and to fit them tightly together. They fill the gaps between the logs with mud and moss, to keep out the wind. They make a roof of split wood, called shakes. They build a fireplace and chimney from river stones and mud. And they make bunks along the walls, and a table and benches from split logs. In two weeks, the cabin is finished. It is small and rough, but it is warm and dry.

[long-pause]

The camp is like a little town without women or children. Almost everyone is a young man, between eighteen and thirty. They come from all over America and all over the world. There is a store, run by a man from New York, in a big tent. He sells flour and bacon, beans and coffee, tools and boots, all at very high prices. There is a little saloon, in another tent, where men drink and play cards in the evenings.

[pause]

{img:mining-camp-store-tent-1849-shelves-flour-barrels-miners-buying-scales}
There are no laws and no police in the camps at first. So the miners make their own rules. They hold meetings and vote on them. They decide how big each man's claim can be. A claim is a piece of the riverbank where a miner has the right to dig for gold. In this camp, each claim is about ten paces long. A miner must work on his claim regularly, or someone else can take it.

[long-pause]

The company of twelve men stakes out several claims side by side, along the river. They mark the corners with wooden stakes and piles of stones. They will work them together, and share whatever gold they find. Dr. Hale writes everything down in a notebook. Samuel and Eli are proud. They have come all this way, and now they have their own piece of California.

[pause]

{img:wooden-claim-stakes-riverbank-stones-notebook-miners-proud-1849}
On the first evening in the new cabin, the company has a little celebration. They cook a big stew of beans and bacon, with some wild onions Eli found on the hillside. They drink coffee with sugar. One of the men plays the fiddle, and the others sing. Through the open door, they can hear the river rushing over the rocks below. Tomorrow, they will start looking for gold. And everyone goes to sleep dreaming of nuggets.

[pause]

The miners of the camp have a little ceremony for new arrivals. In the evening, they gather around a big fire. The newcomers must tell where they come from and why they have come. Then the old miners give them advice, and some of it is useful and some of it is just for fun. At the end, everyone sings a song together. And the newcomers feel welcome.

[pause]

The camp also has a blacksmith, a big man from Pennsylvania. He works under a roof of canvas, beside a little forge. He sharpens picks and shovels, mends tools and makes shoes for the mules. The sound of his hammer rings through the camp all day long. In the evenings, the miners often gather around his forge to keep warm and talk.

[long-pause]

{img:miner-panning-gold-river-bending-tin-pan-swirling-water-sunlight}
How do you find gold in a river? It is simpler than you might think, but it takes a lot of patience.

[pause]

Gold is very heavy, much heavier than sand and gravel. Over thousands of years, the rivers have carried little bits of gold down from the mountains. Because it is so heavy, the gold sinks down and settles in quiet places in the river. It collects in the gravel at the bottom, behind rocks and in cracks. Sometimes it is in flakes, sometimes in tiny grains like dust. And sometimes, very rarely, it is in bigger lumps, called nuggets.

[long-pause]

The simplest way to find gold is with a pan. A gold pan is a flat metal dish, a little like a frying pan without a handle. The miner fills it with gravel and sand from the river. Then he crouches at the edge of the water, and dips the pan into the river. He swirls it around and around, gently, tipping it a little. The water washes the light sand and gravel over the edge. The heavy gold sinks to the bottom of the pan.

[pause]

{img:close-pan-bottom-black-sand-gold-flakes-shining-water}
After many swirls, there is only a little dark sand left at the bottom of the pan. And if you are lucky, among the dark sand, there are little flecks of gold, shining yellow in the sunlight. The miner picks them out carefully with his fingers, or with a little piece of tin. He puts them in a little bottle or a leather bag. It takes about ten minutes to wash one pan. On a good day, a miner might wash fifty pans.

[long-pause]

Panning is very hard work. The miner must crouch at the edge of the river all day long, with his hands in the cold water. His back aches and his hands go numb. And most pans have only a few tiny flecks of gold in them, or none at all. So the miners soon start to use a better tool, called a rocker, or a cradle.

[pause]

{img:wooden-rocker-cradle-two-miners-rocking-pouring-water-gravel}
A rocker is a wooden box, about the size of a baby's cradle. It sits on two curved pieces of wood, so it can rock from side to side. At the top, there is a metal sheet with holes in it. One man shovels gravel onto the sheet. Another pours water over it with a bucket, and rocks the box back and forth. The water and the small stones wash through the holes and down the box. On the bottom of the box, there are little wooden bars, called riffles. The heavy gold gets caught behind them.

[long-pause]

The company works with two rockers and several pans. They work in teams. Some men dig the gravel from the riverbank with picks and shovels. Some carry it to the rockers in buckets. Some rock the rockers and pour the water. Eli carries the water, up and down from the river, all day long. At the end of each day, the men clean out the riffles very carefully. They wash the last sand in a pan, and pick out the gold.

[pause]

{img:miners-evening-weighing-gold-dust-small-scales-cabin-lamp-hopeful}
On the first day, they find only a little gold, a few pinches of dust. On the second day, a little more. By the end of the first week, they have about two ounces between them, worth about thirty dollars. It is not a fortune, shared between twelve men. But it is a start. Dr. Hale weighs it on a little pair of scales, and writes it down in his notebook. And Eli takes the tiniest flake, with his father's permission, and puts it in an envelope for Ruth.

[pause]

Gold is not found everywhere in the river. Experienced miners know where to look. They look on the inside of bends in the river, where the water slows down. They look behind big rocks, and in cracks in the bedrock, the solid rock at the bottom of the river. They look in old dried-up riverbeds, high on the hillsides. Learning where to look is the most important skill of all.

[pause]

Later, the miners build an even bigger tool, called a long tom. It is a long wooden trough, many metres long, with riffles at the bottom. A stream of water runs through it all the time. Several men shovel gravel into it at once. It can wash much more gravel than a rocker. The company builds one in the autumn, with Samuel in charge of the carpentry.

[long-pause]

{img:mining-camp-dawn-1849-smoke-cabin-chimneys-river-mist-pines}
Let us spend an ordinary day in the mining camp, in the early autumn of 1849.

[pause]

The day begins at dawn. The air is cold and fresh, and there is mist over the river. The men wake up in their bunks in the cabin. One of them lights the fire and puts on a big pot of coffee. The coffee is strong and black, with a little sugar. Breakfast is pancakes, called flapjacks, fried in bacon fat, with a little molasses poured over them. And of course, more coffee.

[long-pause]

After breakfast, the men go down to the river and start work. They work hard all morning, digging and carrying and rocking. The sun climbs over the hills and the day gets warm. The river sparkles. Along the banks, hundreds of other men are working too. The sound of picks and shovels and rocking cradles fills the valley. Now and then, someone shouts, when they find a good piece of gold.

[pause]

{img:hundreds-miners-working-river-banks-picks-shovels-rockers-sunny}
At midday, the men stop to eat. They sit on the rocks by the river and eat bread and cold bacon, and drink water from the river. Some of them take a short nap in the shade of a tree. Then they go back to work for the afternoon. They work until the sun goes down behind the hills.

[long-pause]

In the evening, the men clean out the rockers and weigh the day's gold. Then they wash in the river and go back to the cabin. Someone cooks supper: beans and bacon again, or a stew, with bread baked in a pot over the fire. Sometimes there is fresh meat, if someone has shot a deer or bought some beef from a passing trader. Fresh fruit and vegetables are very rare and very expensive.

[pause]

{img:miners-cooking-supper-iron-pot-fireplace-cabin-beans-bread-evening}
After supper, the men sit around the fire and talk. They talk about the gold, of course. They talk about home and their families. They mend their clothes and their boots. They write letters, or read old newspapers that have come up from San Francisco. Someone plays the fiddle or the banjo. Sometimes, men from other cabins come over to visit, and share news and stories.

[long-pause]

On Sundays, most of the miners do not work. It is a day of rest. They wash their clothes in the river and hang them on the bushes to dry. They clean the cabin. They walk into the nearest town to buy supplies and pick up letters. Some of them go to a church service, held in a tent or under a tree, by a travelling preacher. Others just sleep, or play cards, or go fishing in the river.

[pause]

{img:sunday-miners-washing-clothes-river-hanging-bushes-relaxing-sunny}
Eli likes Sundays best. He washes his shirt and his socks in the river. Then he climbs up the hillside behind the camp, to a big rock with a view of the whole valley. He sits there and writes in his diary, and draws pictures for Ruth. He draws the cabin and the river, the miners and the mules, the pine trees and the mountains. He draws a little squirrel that comes to steal his bread. And he thinks of home, far away on the other side of the continent.

[pause]

Some evenings, the miners organise little entertainments. There are wrestling matches and races. There are singing contests and dances, even though there are no women. Some of the men dance the women's parts, with a handkerchief tied around their arm. Everyone laughs a lot. It is a way to forget, for a while, the hard work and the loneliness.

[pause]

There is also a dog in the camp, a big brown dog that belongs to nobody and everybody. The miners call him Nugget. He goes from cabin to cabin, and everyone gives him scraps. He sleeps in a different cabin every night. He likes Eli best, because Eli always shares his bacon. On Sundays, Nugget follows Eli up the hillside to the big rock, and lies beside him while he draws.

[long-pause]

{img:mining-town-store-1849-price-board-eggs-flour-boots-miner-shaking-head}
In the gold fields, everything costs a great deal of money. That is one of the strangest things about the Gold Rush.

[pause]

Everything must be brought up from San Francisco, by boat and by mule, over rough roads. And there are so many people wanting to buy things. So the prices are very high. An egg costs a dollar, sometimes more. That is about what a worker earns in a whole day back in Massachusetts. A pound of flour costs a dollar. A pair of boots costs twenty dollars or more. A shovel costs ten dollars.

[long-pause]

So even when a miner finds gold, much of it goes straight to the store. A miner who finds ten dollars of gold in a day might spend five of them on food. The shopkeepers and the traders often become richer than the miners. Some people say that the surest way to get rich in the Gold Rush is not to dig for gold, but to sell things to the people who dig.

[pause]

{img:levi-strauss-style-merchant-1850s-canvas-trousers-shop-miners-trying}
Some people became very rich in this way. A young man from Germany called Levi Strauss came to San Francisco a few years later. He sold cloth and clothes to the miners. Later, he and a tailor made strong work trousers of blue denim, with little copper rivets at the corners of the pockets, so they would not tear. They are called jeans. People all over the world still wear them today.

[long-pause]

Other people made money by cooking and baking. A good cook could earn more than a miner. Some women made pies and sold them to the miners for a dollar each. Some men opened little restaurants in tents, serving beans and bacon and coffee. Some people washed clothes for the miners, and charged a lot for it. Some miners even sent their shirts all the way to Hawaii or China to be washed, because it was cheaper there!

[pause]

{img:woman-baking-pies-outdoor-oven-mining-camp-miners-queuing-coins}
Samuel notices all this carefully. He sees that the miners need cabins and tables, bunks and rockers. Many of them cannot build these things themselves. So in the evenings, after the day's work, he starts to make things for other miners. He makes a rocker for one man and a table for another. He builds a cabin for two brothers from Ohio. They pay him in gold dust. By the end of the autumn, he has earned more from his carpentry than from his share of the gold.

[long-pause]

The company finds a little gold every week, but not very much. Some weeks are good, and some weeks are poor. After paying for food and supplies, there is only a little left for each man. Some of the men in the company are disappointed. They thought they would be rich by now. Two of them decide to go home before the winter. Another goes off to try his luck in a different river.

[pause]

{img:company-men-around-table-cabin-dividing-small-gold-pile-lamp-serious}
Dr. Hale is calm about it. He tells the others that patience is the most important thing. Gold is not easy to find, he says. Those who keep working usually find something in the end. And those who keep their health are the luckiest of all. Samuel agrees. He has always believed in steady work. And he writes to Hannah that they are well, and working hard, and saving a little. He does not tell her they are rich. Because they are not.

[pause]

Some miners grow tired of beans and bacon, and plant little vegetable gardens beside their cabins. They grow potatoes, onions, lettuce and beans. The soil is rich, and things grow quickly in the warm sun. A few miners discover that they can earn more by selling vegetables than by digging gold. Some of them give up mining altogether and become farmers.

[pause]

Gold dust is used as money everywhere in the gold fields. Every store and saloon has a little pair of scales on the counter. Miners carry their gold dust in little leather bags or glass bottles. When they buy something, they pour out a little gold onto the scales. Some shopkeepers are honest, and some are not. The miners learn to watch the scales very carefully.

[long-pause]

{img:diverse-miners-1849-mexican-chilean-chinese-american-river-working-side-by-side}
In the gold fields, people from all over the world live and work side by side. It is one of the most mixed places on Earth at that time.

[pause]

There are miners from Mexico, many of them from the state of Sonora. They are very skilled, because there are many mines in Mexico. They brought with them a special wooden bowl for washing gold, called a batea. They taught many Americans how to find gold. There are miners from Chile and Peru, who know about mining too. There are men from Hawaii, who are excellent swimmers and can dive into the deep river pools to look for gold.

[long-pause]

From 1850 onward, more and more miners come from China. They come from the south of China, from the region around the city of Canton. They call California Gam Saan, which means Gold Mountain. They work in groups, very hard and very carefully. They often work old claims that others have given up on, and find gold that others have missed. They wear blue cotton jackets and wide straw hats. They cook rice and vegetables and dried fish, and drink tea.

[pause]

{img:chinese-miners-1850-blue-jackets-straw-hats-working-old-claim-river}
There are men from France and Germany, from Ireland and England, from Australia and many other places. Each group brings its own food and songs and customs. Some camps are mostly French, or mostly German, or mostly Chilean. In others, everyone is mixed together. On Sundays, you can hear many languages in the camp, and smell many different kinds of cooking.

[long-pause]

Not everyone gets along. Some of the American miners do not like the foreign miners. In 1850, the state of California makes a law that foreign miners must pay a special tax every month. It is very unfair, and many Mexican and Chilean miners are forced to leave. Later, the Chinese miners have to pay the tax too. It is a sad part of the story, and it is good to remember it.

[pause]

{img:native-californian-village-oak-trees-acorn-baskets-before-gold-rush-calm}
The saddest part of the story is what happened to the native people of California. Many different peoples had lived in California for thousands of years. Near the gold fields lived the Nisenan, the Maidu, the Miwok and others. They lived in villages beside the rivers. They gathered acorns from the oak trees and ground them into flour. They fished for salmon and made beautiful baskets. When the gold seekers came, their lives were turned upside down. Their lands were taken, and many of them suffered greatly. We remember them tonight, gently and with respect.

[long-pause]

In the camp at Rough and Ready Bar, Eli makes friends with a Mexican boy called Tomás, who is the same age as him. Tomás works with his father and his uncles on a claim further down the river. He shows Eli how to use the batea, swirling it in a special way. Eli teaches Tomás some English words. They meet on Sundays, and fish together in the river. They do not always understand each other's words. But they understand each other.

[pause]

{img:two-boys-american-mexican-fishing-river-rocks-sunday-afternoon-laughing}
Tomás's father plays the guitar in the evenings, sitting outside their tent. He plays old songs from Sonora, slow and beautiful. Sometimes Eli comes to listen, and Tomás's mother gives him a warm tortilla, made of corn flour and cooked on a flat iron pan. Eli thinks it is delicious. He writes to Ruth about his friend, and the music, and the tortillas. He says that when he comes home, he will teach her to say hola and gracias.

[pause]

In the evenings, the different groups sometimes meet around the fires. A Frenchman shares his wine. A German plays an accordion. A Chilean tells a story about the mountains of his country. A Hawaiian sings a soft song from his islands. They do not always speak the same language. But music and food and a warm fire are something everyone understands.

[pause]

There are also a few African Americans in the gold fields. Some came as free men, to look for gold like everyone else. Some came with people who held them unfairly. Some of them found enough gold to buy their freedom, or the freedom of their families. When California became a state, it was a free state, without slavery. For many, California meant a new chance in life.

[long-pause]

{img:woman-boarding-house-1849-mining-town-cooking-stove-miners-eating-table}
In the early days of the Gold Rush, there are very few women in the gold fields. But the women who do come play a very important part.

[pause]

In some camps, there are only one or two women among hundreds of men. When a woman comes into a camp, the men stop work to look at her. Some of them have not seen a woman for months. They miss their wives and mothers and sisters very much. They take off their hats and are very polite.

[long-pause]

Some women come with their husbands, and work beside them. Some come alone, to make a living. Many of them open boarding houses, where miners can sleep and eat. They cook and wash and keep house, and they charge good prices for it. Some of them earn more money than their husbands. Some of them become quite rich.

[pause]

{img:luzena-wilson-style-woman-1849-cooking-outdoor-table-miners-sacramento}
One of these women was called Luzena Wilson. She came to California in 1849, with her husband and two little sons. They crossed the plains in a wagon. When they arrived in Sacramento, a miner offered her five dollars for a biscuit she had baked. She could hardly believe it. Then he offered her ten dollars for a whole loaf of bread. That gave her an idea.

[long-pause]

Later, in a town called Nevada City, Luzena set up a table under a tree and started cooking meals for the miners. Twenty men came to eat on the first night. Soon, she had a proper hotel, with many guests. She worked very hard, from before dawn until late at night. She cooked and served and washed dishes. But she was proud of her work. And many years later, she wrote a little book about her life in the gold fields, which we can still read today.

[pause]

{img:mining-town-hotel-1850-wooden-two-storey-sign-woman-doorway-miners}
Other women worked as cooks and laundresses, seamstresses and shopkeepers. Some taught school, when the first children came to the camps. Some were nurses, looking after sick miners. Some wrote letters and stories about life in the gold fields, which were printed in newspapers in the east.

[long-pause]

One of the best of these writers was a woman called Louise Clappe. She lived in a little mining camp called Rich Bar, high in the mountains, with her husband, who was a doctor. She wrote long letters to her sister, describing everything she saw. She wrote about the miners and their cabins, the river and the mountains, the funny events and the sad ones. She signed them with a pen name: Dame Shirley. Her letters are still among the best descriptions of life in the gold camps.

[pause]

{img:woman-writing-letter-log-cabin-window-mountain-camp-candle-1851}
In Massachusetts, Hannah reads about these women in the newspapers. She reads about the boarding houses and the high prices. She reads that a woman can earn a good living in California, cooking and sewing. She thinks about it for a long time. She is a good cook and an excellent seamstress. And she misses her husband and son very much. She begins to wonder if perhaps, one day, she and Ruth might go to California too.

[pause]

When a woman with a baby arrives in a mining camp, it is a great event. Miners walk many kilometres to see the baby. They bring little presents: a gold nugget, a carved toy, a piece of cloth. Some of them hold the baby very carefully in their big rough hands, and their eyes fill with tears. They are thinking of their own children, far away.

[long-pause]

{img:miners-crowding-post-office-san-francisco-1849-long-line-letters}
For the forty-niners, letters from home are the most precious thing in the world. Gold is good. But a letter from home is better.

[pause]

In 1849, letters take a very long time to travel between the east coast and California. They go by ship, either around Cape Horn or across Panama. It takes at least six weeks, and often two or three months. Sometimes letters are lost on the way. So when the mail ship arrives in San Francisco, there is great excitement.

[long-pause]

In San Francisco, the post office is a small wooden building. When the mail arrives, the line of men waiting for letters stretches far down the street. Some men wait for hours, even all night. Some men pay others to stand in line for them. Some men sell their places in the line for a lot of money. When a man finally gets a letter, he often opens it right there, in the street. Some of them smile, and some of them cry.

[pause]

{img:miner-reading-letter-from-home-sitting-rock-river-tears-smile}
In the gold fields, the letters come up from San Francisco with special messengers, called expressmen. They ride from camp to camp on horseback, with saddlebags full of letters and newspapers. The miners pay them well for each letter they bring. When the expressman rides into camp, everyone stops work and comes running. Is there a letter for me, they ask? And for me?

[long-pause]

The first letter from Hannah reaches Samuel and Eli in November, three months after they arrived. It was written in March, just after they left. It is a long letter, written on both sides of thin paper, in Hannah's neat handwriting. She tells them about the snow, and the cow, and the neighbours. She tells them that she misses them. And folded inside, there is a drawing from Ruth. It shows a ship sailing on blue waves, with a big sun above it.

[pause]

{img:father-son-reading-letter-together-cabin-lamplight-drawing-unfolded}
Samuel and Eli read the letter together, by the light of the fire in the cabin. Then they read it again. And again. Eli looks at Ruth's drawing for a long time. Then he pins it to the log wall, above his bunk. The other men in the cabin come to look at it. Some of them have children at home too. They are quiet for a while, thinking of their own families.

[long-pause]

Eli and Samuel write back every week. They write on whatever paper they can find. They tell Hannah about the cabin and the river, the gold and the people. They tell her about Tomás and his family, and Dr. Hale and the company. Eli draws pictures for Ruth of the camp and the mules and the squirrels. Samuel writes that he is earning money as a carpenter, and that there might be a good future for carpenters in California.

[pause]

{img:boy-writing-letter-candle-cabin-table-drawing-squirrel-for-sister}
In one of his letters, Eli puts the tiny flake of gold he saved for Ruth. He folds it carefully inside a little piece of paper, and writes on it: For Ruth. Real California gold. Found by your brother. It will take three months to reach her. But when it does, Ruth will hold it up to the window and watch it shine. And she will keep it all her life.

[pause]

Some miners cannot read or write. So they ask others to write letters for them, and to read the letters they receive. Dr. Hale writes letters for several men in the camp. He sits at the table in the evenings, writing what they tell him. Some of the letters are very short. Some are long and full of love. Dr. Hale never tells anyone what is in them.

[pause]

Hannah keeps all the letters from Samuel and Eli in a wooden box on the kitchen shelf. In the evenings, she takes them out and reads them again. Ruth reads them too, and looks at Eli's drawings. She pins them to the wall of her bedroom. Soon, the whole wall is covered with pictures of California: rivers and cabins, mules and mountains. Ruth looks at them every night before she goes to sleep.

[long-pause]

{img:mining-camp-winter-rain-1849-muddy-river-swollen-cabins-smoke-grey}
In December, the winter comes to the gold fields. And the winter of 1849 is one of the wettest anyone can remember.

[pause]

In California, winter is not very cold in the foothills. But it is the rainy season. The rain begins in November and goes on for weeks. Some days, it pours down without stopping. The paths turn into rivers of mud. The rivers rise higher and higher, brown and fast. Higher up, in the mountains, it snows.

[long-pause]

Working in the river becomes impossible. The water is too high and too fast. It covers the claims and washes away the stakes. Some of the rockers and tools that men left by the river are washed away too. So most of the miners stop working, and stay in their cabins and tents. They wait for the rain to stop.

[pause]

{img:inside-log-cabin-winter-miners-around-fire-playing-cards-rain-roof}
In the company's cabin, it is warm and dry, thanks to Samuel's careful building. The fire burns all day long. The men sit around it, playing cards and checkers, mending their clothes, and telling stories. They read the few books they have, again and again. One of them has a copy of a novel by Charles Dickens, and he reads it aloud in the evenings. Everyone listens, even the men who usually do not like books.

[long-pause]

Not everyone is so lucky. Some miners live only in tents, and the rain comes through. They are cold and wet all the time. Some of them become ill. Dr. Hale goes from tent to tent, with his little bag of medicines. He helps where he can. Samuel and Eli help too. They build little shelters of wood for the men in tents. They bring them firewood and hot soup.

[pause]

{img:doctor-visiting-sick-miner-tent-rain-lantern-medicine-bag-kind}
Food becomes scarce in the winter, because the roads are too muddy for the mule trains to bring supplies. The prices in the store go up even higher. Flour, bacon, coffee and sugar run low. The miners share what they have. They hunt deer and rabbits on the hillsides when the rain stops. They catch fish in the river. They make do.

[long-pause]

In the middle of the winter, there is a great flood. The river rises higher than anyone has seen before. In January, the rain is so heavy that the river comes right up to the edge of the camp. Some tents and cabins near the water are swept away. Everyone climbs up the hillside, carrying what they can. The company's cabin stands on high ground, and it is safe. Samuel invites the men who have lost their shelters to stay with them.

[pause]

{img:crowded-cabin-night-men-sleeping-floor-fire-glow-flood-outside}
For a week, eighteen men sleep in a cabin built for twelve. They sleep on the floor, side by side, wrapped in their blankets. It is very crowded. But nobody complains. Outside, the rain drums on the roof, and the river roars in the dark. Inside, the fire crackles, and someone plays the fiddle softly. Eli lies on his bunk and listens to the music and the rain. He thinks that he has never been so far from home. But somehow, he does not feel alone.

[pause]

When the rain stops for a day, the sun comes out, and the whole valley sparkles. The miners come out of their cabins and tents, blinking in the light. They hang their wet blankets and clothes on the bushes to dry. They go hunting and fishing. They look at the river and wonder when they can start work again. And then the clouds come back, and the rain begins once more.

[pause]

Christmas comes in the middle of the rainy winter. The men of the company want to celebrate, even far from home. They cut a little pine tree and put it in the corner of the cabin. They decorate it with bits of tin and paper. Dr. Hale reads the Christmas story from the Bible. They have a special dinner, with roast venison and a pudding made of flour and dried apples. And they sing Christmas carols, thinking of their families.

[long-pause]

{img:spring-1850-mining-camp-sunshine-river-clear-wildflowers-hills-green}
In March, the rain finally stops, and spring comes to the foothills. It is beautiful. The hills turn bright green, and wildflowers bloom everywhere: orange poppies and blue lupins, yellow buttercups and purple brodiaea. The river goes down, and becomes clear and sparkling again. Birds sing in the oak trees. And the miners go back to work.

[pause]

The flood has changed the river. It has moved rocks and gravel, and opened up new places. Some miners find that their claims have been washed away. Others find that the flood has brought new gravel, with new gold in it. Everyone is digging again, full of hope.

[long-pause]

One morning in April, Eli is working at the river, filling buckets of gravel for the rocker. He is digging near a big rock, where the flood has left a deep crack. He pushes his shovel down into the gravel at the bottom of the crack. It hits something hard. He thinks it is a stone. He reaches down into the cold water with his hand to pull it out.

[pause]

{img:boy-hand-holding-gold-nugget-river-water-dripping-sunlight-amazed}
It is not a stone. It is heavy, much too heavy for its size. It is rough and knobbly, about the size of a walnut. He rubs it on his shirt, and it shines. Yellow. Bright yellow. Eli stares at it. His heart is pounding. He opens his mouth, but nothing comes out. Then he shouts, as loud as he can. Father! Father!

[long-pause]

Samuel comes running from the rocker, and the other men come too. They crowd around Eli. He holds out his hand. In his palm lies a gold nugget, glinting in the sunshine. Dr. Hale takes it and weighs it in his hand. Then he takes out his little scales and weighs it properly. It weighs nearly three ounces. That is worth about fifty dollars, more than the whole company has found in a month.

[pause]

{img:miners-crowding-around-boy-nugget-scales-cheering-river-bank}
The men cheer and slap Eli on the back. Samuel hugs his son tightly. Everyone wants to hold the nugget and look at it. Miners from other claims come to see it. It is the biggest nugget found at Rough and Ready Bar for weeks. That evening, the company has a celebration in the cabin. They cook a special dinner, with fresh beef and canned peaches from the store. And they drink a toast to Eli.

[long-pause]

According to the company's agreement, the nugget belongs to all of them. They will share its value equally. But the other men have a meeting. And they decide something kind. They tell Eli that he may keep a small piece of it for himself. They say he has worked harder than anyone, carrying water all through the year. Dr. Hale cuts off a tiny corner of the nugget with a knife. He gives it to Eli.

[pause]

{img:boy-holding-small-gold-piece-cabin-firelight-thinking-of-home}
That night, Eli sits by the fire and looks at his little piece of gold. It is smaller than a pea, but it shines in the firelight. He thinks about the promise he made to Ruth, at the wharf in Boston. A nugget as big as an egg, he had said. This one is much smaller. But it is real. And he found it himself, in a California river. He wraps it carefully in a piece of cloth. He will keep it for her, until he sees her again.

[pause]

The news of the nugget spreads quickly through the camp, and the next camp, and the next. By the end of the week, people are saying that a boy at Rough and Ready Bar has found a nugget as big as a fist. By the end of the month, they say it was as big as a hen's egg. Eli laughs when he hears this. That is how stories grow in the gold fields, says Dr. Hale.

[long-pause]

{img:sacramento-1850-growing-town-wooden-buildings-river-boats-busy-construction}
By the summer of 1850, Samuel has made a decision. He talks it over with Eli, and with Dr. Hale, and then he tells the company.

[pause]

He has seen that the miners in the company are earning only a little from the gold. After paying for food and supplies, there is only a small amount left each month. But as a carpenter, he can earn much more. The towns of California are growing very fast. Everyone needs houses, shops and warehouses. And there are not enough carpenters to build them.

[long-pause]

So Samuel and Eli leave the company, with their share of the gold, and go down the river to Sacramento. The other men understand. They shake hands and wish them luck. Dr. Hale gives Eli a little book about plants and animals, as a goodbye present. Tomás comes to say goodbye too. The two boys promise to write to each other. Eli gives Tomás his pocket knife. Tomás gives Eli a little carved wooden horse.

[pause]

{img:boy-saying-goodbye-mexican-friend-gifts-knife-wooden-horse-camp}
In Sacramento, Samuel finds work at once. There is so much building to do. He builds houses and shops, hotels and stables. He builds the wooden pavements, raised above the muddy streets. He hires other carpenters to help him. Eli works beside him every day, learning more and more. Soon, Samuel has his own little building business, with his name painted on a sign.

[long-pause]

Sacramento is growing very fast. Every week, new buildings go up. There are banks and hotels, churches and theatres, shops of every kind. Steamboats come up the river from San Francisco every day, bringing people and goods. The streets are busy and noisy and full of life. But the town is also built of wood, and close to the river. In the years ahead, it will face both fire and flood. And each time, the people will rebuild it.

[pause]

{img:carpenter-father-son-building-wooden-house-frame-sacramento-sunny}
Samuel and Eli build a little house of their own, on a quiet street near the edge of the town. It has two rooms and a kitchen, and a porch at the front. There is a little garden at the back, where Eli plants some vegetables. Samuel builds it very carefully, with the best wood he can find. He wants it to be just right. Because he has written to Hannah, and asked her to come.

[long-pause]

In his letter, Samuel tells Hannah that they will not become rich from gold. But they can have a good life in California. There is plenty of work for a carpenter, and good pay. The weather is warm and sunny most of the year. The land is rich and beautiful. He has saved enough money for her and Ruth to come, by ship and across Panama. He asks her to sell the house in Massachusetts, and to come.

[pause]

{img:small-wooden-house-porch-garden-sacramento-1850-father-son-painting-fence}
Every evening, after work, Samuel and Eli work a little more on the house. They paint the walls and the fence. They build a table and chairs, and beds for everyone. Eli builds a little shelf for Ruth's drawings. They plant a rose bush beside the porch, because Hannah loves roses. And every evening, they look down the road toward the river, and imagine the day when the boat will come.

[pause]

Eli goes to a little school in Sacramento in the mornings, for a few months. It is in a wooden building, with a teacher from New York. There are children from many different families: American, Mexican, German, Irish. Eli is older than most of them. But he likes learning again. In the afternoons, he works with his father. And in the evenings, he reads the little book about plants and animals from Dr. Hale.

[pause]

In the evenings, the people of Sacramento walk along the river and visit each other. There are band concerts on summer evenings and church socials on Sundays. Samuel and Eli make friends with their neighbours, a family from Missouri who run a bakery. The baker's wife invites them for supper every Sunday. She says two men living alone need a proper meal at least once a week.

[long-pause]

{img:panama-crossing-1850-jungle-river-dugout-canoe-travellers-palm-trees}
In Massachusetts, Hannah reads Samuel's letter many times. She talks it over with her family and friends. Some of them think she is very brave. Some of them think she is very foolish. But she has made up her mind. She sells the house, the cow and the chickens. She packs their best things in two big trunks. And in the autumn of 1850, she and Ruth set off for California.

[long-pause]

They go by the fastest way, across Panama. First, they sail by steamship from New York to the coast of Panama. That takes about ten days. Then they must cross the narrow strip of land between the Atlantic and the Pacific, through the jungle. It is about eighty kilometres. In 1850, there is no railway yet. So they travel up a river in long wooden canoes, paddled by local boatmen. Then they ride on mules over the hills, along muddy jungle paths.

[pause]

{img:mother-daughter-riding-mules-jungle-path-panama-parrots-green}
For Ruth, the jungle is the most amazing place she has ever seen. There are enormous trees, covered with vines and flowers. There are parrots and toucans, with bright coloured feathers. There are monkeys swinging in the branches above, chattering and calling. There are butterflies as big as her hand, blue and shining. It is very hot and very wet. But Ruth draws everything she sees, in her little book.

[long-pause]

At last they reach Panama City, on the Pacific coast. There, they wait for a steamship to San Francisco. The town is crowded with travellers, all waiting for ships. After two weeks, they get places on a steamship. The voyage up the coast takes about three weeks. And one bright morning in December, they sail through the Golden Gate into San Francisco Bay.

[pause]

{img:steamship-arriving-san-francisco-1850-golden-gate-passengers-rail}
Samuel and Eli are waiting on the wharf. They have come down from Sacramento to meet the ship. They have been waiting since dawn. When the ship ties up, they search every face among the passengers coming down the gangplank. And then they see them: Hannah in her grey travelling cloak, and Ruth beside her, a little taller than before, looking around with big eyes.

[long-pause]

Ruth sees Eli first. She drops her bag and runs down the gangplank and across the wharf. Eli catches her and swings her around. Hannah follows, and Samuel takes her in his arms. For a long time, nobody says anything at all. They just hold each other, on the busy wharf, with people pushing past them on every side. It has been almost two years.

[pause]

{img:family-reunion-wharf-san-francisco-hugging-boy-swinging-sister-1850}
Then Eli reaches into his pocket. He takes out a little cloth bundle and gives it to Ruth. She unwraps it slowly. Inside is the little piece of gold from the nugget, shining in the winter sun. It is not as big as an egg, Eli says, a little embarrassed. Ruth looks at it, and then at her brother. It is better than an egg, she says. Because you found it. And she puts her arms around his neck and hugs him again.

[pause]

On the river boat to Sacramento, Ruth stands at the front and watches everything. She sees the marshes and the birds and the great oak trees. She sees the hills in the distance, brown and gold. She asks Eli a hundred questions, and he answers every one. Hannah sits with Samuel on a bench, holding his hand. They do not talk much. They just smile at each other.

[pause]

That evening, Hannah walks slowly through the little house that Samuel and Eli have built. She looks at the painted walls and the new table and chairs. She looks at the shelf for Ruth's drawings and the rose bush by the porch. She does not say anything for a long time. Then she turns to Samuel and smiles. It is perfect, she says. And Samuel feels that all the long journey was worth it.

[long-pause]

{img:san-francisco-1850s-growing-city-brick-buildings-harbour-ships-hills}
The Gold Rush changed California very quickly, and very much.

[pause]

In 1848, there were only a few thousand people in California, apart from the native peoples. By 1852, there were more than two hundred thousand. In September 1850, California became a state of the United States, the thirty-first state. San Francisco grew from a little town into a great city. Within a few years, it had brick buildings, banks, theatres, newspapers and schools.

[long-pause]

After a few years, the easy gold in the rivers was mostly gone. The days when a man could find gold with a pan were over. Now, mining was done by big companies, with machines and many workers. They dug deep tunnels into the hills, and built great water channels. Later, they used powerful water hoses to wash away whole hillsides, looking for gold. This did a great deal of harm to the rivers and the land. The mud from the mining filled the rivers far downstream.

[pause]

{img:california-farmland-1860s-wheat-fields-orchards-oak-trees-golden}
Many of the forty-niners went home, some with a little gold and many with none. But many others stayed in California. They became farmers and shopkeepers, carpenters and teachers. They planted wheat and orchards in the rich valleys. They found that the real wealth of California was its land and its sunshine. California became one of the richest farming lands in the world.

[long-pause]

For the native peoples of California, the years after the Gold Rush were very hard. Many of them lost their lands, their homes and their families. Their numbers fell greatly. It is one of the saddest parts of the history of California. But they did not disappear. Today, there are many native Californian peoples, keeping their languages, their basket weaving and their traditions alive. We remember their story with respect.

[pause]

{img:transcontinental-railroad-1869-locomotive-golden-spike-celebration}
Many of the Chinese who came to the Gold Rush stayed too. They opened shops and restaurants, laundries and farms. Later, thousands of Chinese workers helped to build the great railway across the mountains, connecting California to the rest of America. It was finished in 1869. After that, the journey from the east coast to California took only about a week, instead of five months. San Francisco's Chinatown, which began in the Gold Rush, is still one of the most famous in the world.

[long-pause]

What about the family from Massachusetts? We can only imagine. Perhaps Samuel's building business grew and grew, and he built many of the houses of Sacramento. Perhaps Hannah opened a little boarding house, famous for its pies. Perhaps Ruth became a teacher, or an artist, drawing the hills and rivers of California. And perhaps Eli became a builder like his father, and later, an architect.

[pause]

{img:old-man-eli-porch-grandchildren-wooden-horse-gold-piece-sunset-california}
And perhaps, when Eli was an old man, he sat on the porch of his house with his grandchildren. On a shelf in the house, there was a little carved wooden horse, given to him by a Mexican boy on the American River. And in a little box, there was a tiny piece of gold, which his sister had kept all her life. He told the children about the ship and the storm, the river and the cabin, the flood and the nugget. And he told them that the best thing he found in California was not gold at all. It was a home.

[pause]

Many of the forty-niners wrote memoirs when they were old, telling the story of their adventures. Some of these books are funny, some are sad, and some are full of tall tales. They tell of the long voyages and the hard work, the friendships and the disappointments. Reading them today, we can hear the voices of the men and women of the Gold Rush, telling their stories.

[pause]

The Gold Rush also changed the rest of the world. Gold from California made many countries richer. Ships sailed to San Francisco from all over the world. New trade routes across the Pacific were opened. And news of the Gold Rush inspired people in other countries to look for gold too. A few years later, there were gold rushes in Australia, in New Zealand and in Canada.

[long-pause]

{img:coloma-state-park-today-replica-sutters-mill-american-river-green-today}
Today, you can still visit the place where gold was first found in California. It is called Coloma, on the American River.

[pause]

It is a quiet and peaceful place now, a state park. There is a copy of the old sawmill, built just like the original, beside the river. There is a little museum, with tools and pictures from the Gold Rush. There are old buildings from the 1850s: a little store, a schoolhouse, a church. And there is a statue of James Marshall, the man who found the first gold, on a hill above the valley.

[long-pause]

The river still flows through the valley, clear and fast, over the rocks. In summer, people swim in it and float down it in rafts and kayaks. And some people still look for gold, with pans, just like the forty-niners. At Coloma, visitors can try it for themselves. Children crouch at the edge of a little water trough with a pan, swirling and swirling. And sometimes, they find a tiny flake of gold at the bottom. Their faces light up, just like the faces of the miners long ago.

[pause]

{img:children-panning-gold-trough-park-today-pans-smiling-summer-today}
There are other old Gold Rush towns too, in the foothills of the Sierra Nevada. Some of them have been carefully kept, just as they were. In a town called Columbia, the main street looks almost exactly as it did in the 1850s. There are wooden sidewalks and old brick shops. There is a stagecoach pulled by horses, and a blacksmith working at his forge. People in old-fashioned clothes work in the shops and tell visitors about the past.

[long-pause]

The road that runs through the old Gold Rush towns is called Highway 49, after the forty-niners. It winds through the foothills, past oak trees and pine forests, rivers and old mining towns. Along the way, there are old cabins and stone buildings, little cemeteries and old mines. In spring, the hills are green and covered with orange poppies. The poppy is now the state flower of California.

[pause]

{img:highway-49-foothills-spring-poppies-oak-trees-old-town-today}
In Sacramento, there is a part of the old town that has been rebuilt, beside the river. There are wooden buildings with raised sidewalks, and old shops and saloons. There is a big railway museum, with the old steam locomotives that crossed the mountains. And there is an old riverboat, tied up at the wharf, like the ones that carried the forty-niners up the river.

[long-pause]

California is still called the Golden State. That name comes from the Gold Rush. But today, people say it is golden for other reasons too. Golden sunshine, golden hills in summer, golden beaches and golden poppies. And San Francisco has a famous bridge across the narrow entrance to its bay. It is called the Golden Gate Bridge, after the old name of the strait that the ships of 1849 sailed through.

[pause]

{img:golden-gate-bridge-sunset-fog-san-francisco-bay-today}
In the evenings, when the sun sets over the Pacific Ocean, the Golden Gate Bridge glows red and gold. The fog rolls in from the sea, slowly, around the towers of the bridge. Ships sail in and out of the bay, as they have done for almost two hundred years. And somewhere, perhaps, a family is sitting on a porch in the warm evening, telling stories about the great-great-grandparents who came to California, long ago, to look for gold.

[pause]

In the foothills, there are also little museums in old stone buildings, full of things from the Gold Rush. There are pans and rockers, picks and shovels. There are old letters and diaries, photographs and maps. There are little bottles of gold dust and real gold nuggets, behind glass. And there are the stories of the people who came here, from all over the world, long ago.

[pause]

In the old Gold Rush towns, there are also little festivals every year. People dress up in clothes from the 1850s. There are parades and fiddle music and dancing. There are gold panning contests, where people race to find the most gold in a pan. Children ride in old stagecoaches. And in the evenings, people sit together on the wooden sidewalks and tell stories of the old days.

[long-pause]

{img:mining-camp-evening-1849-sunset-river-cabin-smoke-pines-golden-light}
So we leave the gold fields of California tonight, in the autumn of 1849, as the evening falls.

[pause]

The sun is going down behind the hills, and the valley is filling with golden light. The river glows red and gold, rushing softly over the rocks. The miners are putting down their picks and shovels, and walking slowly up the bank to their cabins and tents. Smoke rises from the chimneys. And the smell of coffee and frying bacon drifts through the camp.

[long-pause]

In the company's cabin, the fire is burning in the stone fireplace. Dr. Hale is sitting at the table with his notebook, writing down the day's gold, a few pinches of dust. One of the men is frying flapjacks in a pan. Another is mending his boots by the fire. The fiddle player is tuning his fiddle. Outside, the mules are munching hay, and an owl is calling in the pines.

[pause]

{img:log-cabin-interior-evening-miners-fire-fiddle-notebook-lamp-cozy}
Samuel is sitting on his bunk, writing a letter to Hannah by the light of a candle. He writes slowly and carefully. He tells her about the cabin and the river, and the little gold they have found. He tells her about the carpentry he has been doing in the evenings. He tells her that he misses her and Ruth every single day. And he tells her that Eli is well, and growing taller, and working hard.

[long-pause]

Eli is sitting outside the cabin door, on a log, with his drawing book on his knees. He is drawing the river in the sunset, for Ruth. He draws the cabins and the pine trees, the mules and the hills. He draws the first stars coming out above the valley. And at the bottom, he writes: Our camp, Rough and Ready Bar, California. Your brother, Eli.

[pause]

{img:boy-sitting-log-drawing-sunset-river-valley-stars-coming-out}
Down the river, he can hear the guitar of Tomás's father, playing a slow old song from Sonora. The music drifts softly up the valley, mixing with the sound of the river. Eli stops drawing and listens. Then the fiddle in his own cabin starts to play too, a different song. And for a little while, the two songs drift together over the dark river, in the warm evening air.

[long-pause]

Far away, on the other side of the continent, the same stars are shining over a little wooden house in Massachusetts. A mother is sitting by the kitchen fire, reading a letter for the third time. A girl is asleep upstairs, with a box of drawings beside her bed. And the sea is whispering softly in the harbour, as it always does.

[pause]

The stars come out, more and more, over the California hills. The fire burns low in the cabin. One by one, the miners go to sleep in their bunks. Eli comes inside and climbs into his bunk, under Ruth's drawing of the ship. He closes his eyes. And the river goes on singing over the rocks, through the long, quiet night.
