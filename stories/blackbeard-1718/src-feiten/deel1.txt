TOPIC: A day aboard a pirate ship
===
IMG: pirate-ship-dawn-calm-sea-men-waking-deck-hammocks | pirate ship dawn calm sea men waking deck hammocks
HEAD: A day aboard a pirate ship began at dawn, much as on any other sailing ship.
BODY: Daily life on a pirate ship followed many of the same routines as on other ships of the period. The crew was divided into watches, which took turns working the ship day and night. At dawn, men who had slept on deck in the warm air rolled up their blankets. Others climbed out of hammocks below decks. The decks were washed, the sails adjusted and the ship's position checked. Breakfast was simple, often biscuit and whatever food was available. Historians reconstruct these routines from sailors' accounts and the testimonies of captured men who had spent time aboard pirate ships. Some of these witnesses described daily life in detail during trials. Even so, pirate ships were often less orderly than navy ships. Crews were large, and with more men than needed for sailing, there was often plenty of free time. The work of a sailing ship still had to be done, however, whatever the crew's way of life.
===
IMG: lookout-masthead-telescope-distant-sail-horizon-sunny | lookout masthead telescope distant sail horizon sunny
HEAD: On most days, pirate ships saw no prey, and crews passed the time as sailors always did.
BODY: A lookout stood at the masthead, scanning the horizon for sails. When a ship was sighted, the pirates might give chase. But on many days, the sea was empty. Crews then filled the hours with ordinary activities. They talked, told stories and sang. They played cards and dice, though some pirate articles forbade gambling for money on board to prevent quarrels. They repaired clothing and equipment. Historians note that boredom was a common feature of life at sea for all sailors in this period. Weeks could pass with little change. Witnesses at trials sometimes described long periods of waiting and drifting. Even so, the lookout's call could change everything in moments. A distant sail meant possible loot, but also possible danger. It might be a warship or a well-armed merchant. The captain and crew then had to decide quickly whether to pursue the ship or avoid it.
===
IMG: pirates-fiddle-dancing-deck-afternoon-laughing | pirates fiddle dancing deck afternoon laughing
HEAD: Pirates fished, rested, mended clothes and enjoyed music during quiet days at sea.
BODY: When there was no ship to chase, pirates fished over the side, catching fish such as dolphinfish and bonito, and sometimes sharks. They rested in the shade of the sails. Music was popular. Some pirate articles mentioned musicians, who were allowed to rest on Sundays. Fiddles, drums and trumpets were played, and men sang and danced. Some pirates could read and took books from captured ships. Historians have found that Blackbeard's crew took books and papers during several captures. Pirates mostly wore ordinary sailors' clothes, such as loose shirts, short jackets, wide trousers and caps or hats. Captured fine clothes were sometimes worn ashore or on special occasions. Even so, there were no colourful uniforms. Practical clothing suited the hot climate and the work. The image of pirates in elaborate costumes comes largely from later illustrations, theatre and films, rather than from the descriptions of people who actually met them.
===
IMG: tropical-night-stars-milky-way-ship-sailing-glowing-wake | tropical night stars milky way ship sailing glowing wake
HEAD: At night, the watch kept the ship sailing while the rest of the crew slept.
BODY: As darkness fell, lights below decks were put out early, often around eight o'clock, to reduce the risk of fire. Fire was one of the greatest dangers on a wooden ship filled with gunpowder, tar and rope. Some pirate articles specifically required candles and lamps to be extinguished at a fixed hour. The men off duty slept in hammocks or on deck. The helmsman steered, and the lookout watched for other ships, rocks or changes in weather. In warm tropical waters, the sea sometimes glowed at night with bioluminescence, as tiny organisms lit up in the ship's wake. Sailors described this light in many accounts of the period. Historians note that night sailing required great care, especially near coasts and reefs. Even so, in open water, nights could be calm and quiet. The ship moved steadily under the stars, and the sound of wind and water accompanied the sleeping crew.
===
IMG: ship-galley-hearth-iron-pot-cook-hardtack-barrels | ship galley hearth iron pot cook hardtack barrels
HEAD: At sea, sailors lived mainly on food that kept well, such as hard biscuit and salted meat.
BODY: Keeping food fresh on long voyages in a hot climate was very difficult. The main supplies on most ships were ship's biscuit, also called hardtack, and salted beef and pork stored in barrels of brine. Dried peas, oatmeal, cheese and butter were also carried. Ships had a small galley with a brick hearth, where a cook prepared meals in large iron pots. Biscuit often became infested with weevils after long storage. Water stored in casks could turn stale, so sailors drank beer, wine or rum when possible. Historians know about shipboard diets from navy records, merchant accounts and archaeological finds from wrecks. Excavations of the Queen Anne's Revenge have recovered animal bones from preserved meat. Even so, pirates had one advantage over ordinary sailors. They could take fresh food and drink from the ships they captured, and they often spent time ashore on islands where fresh food was available.
===
IMG: pirates-roasting-pig-beach-fire-palm-trees-evening | pirates roasting pig beach fire palm trees evening
HEAD: On Caribbean islands, pirates hunted wild pigs and goats and gathered fruit and shellfish.
BODY: Many Caribbean islands had populations of wild pigs and goats, descended from animals released by earlier European sailors. Pirates hunted them and roasted the meat over fires on the beach. They gathered coconuts, oranges, limes and other fruit. They caught fish and collected shellfish, lobsters and crabs. The tradition of roasting meat on a wooden frame came from Indigenous Caribbean peoples and was adopted by the buccaneers of the seventeenth century. The word barbecue comes from the Taíno word barbacoa. Historians note that the earlier buccaneers took their name from a word for a smoking grill. Even so, such feasts depended on time ashore, which was not always possible. Pirates had to be careful when landing on islands, as colonial authorities or hostile ships might appear. Fresh food was valuable for health. Citrus fruit helped prevent scurvy, though the cause of the disease was not yet understood at the time.
===
IMG: green-sea-turtles-moonlit-beach-sand-waves | green sea turtles moonlit beach sand waves
HEAD: Sea turtles were an important source of fresh meat for sailors in the Caribbean.
BODY: Green sea turtles came ashore on Caribbean beaches at night to lay their eggs. Sailors caught them easily on the sand. Turtles could be kept alive on board for weeks, providing fresh meat during voyages. Turtle meat and turtle soup were common foods for sailors in the region. Historians find many references to turtle hunting in ship logs and travellers' accounts. The Cayman Islands were especially known for their turtle beaches. A popular dish among sailors was salmagundi, a mixture of chopped meat, fish, eggs, vegetables and pickled foods, seasoned with oil, vinegar and spices. It is often described as a favourite pirate meal, though the dish was also eaten in England. Even so, heavy hunting greatly reduced turtle populations over the centuries. Today, sea turtles are protected throughout the Caribbean. Conservation groups monitor nesting beaches, and many of the islands where pirates once hunted turtles now help protect them.
===
IMG: limes-oranges-basket-ship-deck-tropical-island-near | limes oranges basket ship deck tropical island near
HEAD: Pirates drank rum, punch, wine and sometimes chocolate taken from captured ships.
BODY: Rum was produced on the sugar islands of the Caribbean and was cheap and widely available. Sailors often mixed it with water, sugar, lime juice and spices to make punch. Wine and brandy were taken from captured European ships. Cocoa beans from Spanish cargoes could be ground and mixed with hot water, sugar and spices to make chocolate, which was a popular drink in this period. Tea and coffee were also becoming fashionable in Europe. Historians note that drinking was a major part of pirate life ashore. Some reports describe long celebrations after successful captures. Even so, drunkenness at sea could be dangerous. Some pirate articles limited drinking on board, and captains knew that a drunken crew might lose a fight or wreck the ship. Records of trials sometimes mention pirates who were captured because they were drunk when navy ships attacked. Moderation was not common, but it was sometimes enforced.

TOPIC: The Caribbean in 1718
===
IMG: caribbean-turquoise-sea-sailing-ship-palm-island-1718 | Caribbean turquoise sea sailing ship palm island in 1718
HEAD: In the early eighteenth century, the Caribbean Sea was one of the busiest trading regions in the world.
BODY: Around the year 1718, the Caribbean was a warm sea of islands, reefs and harbours between North and South America. European powers had divided it among themselves. Spain held Cuba, Puerto Rico and much of the mainland. Britain held Jamaica, Barbados and many smaller islands. France, the Netherlands and Denmark also had colonies there. Ships sailed constantly between these islands, the American colonies, Europe and Africa. Historians call the years from about 1715 to 1726 the golden age of piracy, because so many pirates were active in this short period. The most famous of them was Edward Thatch, known as Blackbeard. Records of trials, ship logs and letters from colonial governors describe his activities. Even so, many popular ideas about pirates come from later books and films rather than from these records. The real story is quieter and more human. It is the story of sailors, traders, islanders and settlers living around a crowded and beautiful sea.
===
IMG: sea-turtle-coral-reef-clear-water-sunlight-fish | sea turtle coral reef clear water sunlight fish
HEAD: The Caribbean climate was warm all year, with a dry season and a wet season.
BODY: The weather in the Caribbean is warm throughout the year. From about December to May, a dry season brings blue skies and steady trade winds blowing from the east. From about June to November, a wet season brings heavy rain and thunderstorms. This is also the hurricane season. Sailors of the eighteenth century knew these patterns well and planned their voyages around them. The seas were rich in life. Coral reefs surrounded many islands, and the shallow water was full of fish, lobsters and sea turtles. Turtles were an important source of fresh meat for sailors. Historians know about the weather and the sea from ship logs, which recorded wind, weather and position every day. Many of these logs survive in British archives. Even so, the region could be dangerous. Reefs and sandbars wrecked many ships. Disease was also common, especially yellow fever and malaria, which killed many newcomers from Europe.
===
IMG: sugar-plantation-hills-mill-windmill-distance-hazy | sugar plantation hills mill windmill distance hazy
HEAD: Ships carried silver, sugar, tobacco and many other goods between the Americas, Europe and Africa.
BODY: The wealth of the Caribbean and the Americas moved by sea. Spanish fleets carried silver from the mines of Mexico and Peru across the Atlantic. Ships from the English and French islands carried sugar, molasses and rum. The American colonies exported tobacco, rice, timber and furs. In the other direction came cloth, tools, guns and manufactured goods from Europe. Sugar plantations depended on the labour of enslaved Africans. Hundreds of thousands of people were carried across the Atlantic in terrible conditions in this period. Historians use shipping records, customs documents and plantation accounts to measure the size of this trade. Hundreds of ships sailed between the islands every year. Even so, most were small merchant ships with small crews, often poorly armed. This made them easy targets. The great wealth moving across the sea, combined with weak protection, created the conditions in which piracy could grow so quickly in these years.
===
IMG: pirate-sloop-black-flag-sunset-silhouette-calm-sea | pirate sloop black flag sunset silhouette calm sea
HEAD: The pirates of these few years shaped how people have imagined pirates ever since.
BODY: When people today think of pirates, they often imagine black flags, buried treasure, parrots and wooden legs. Most of these images come from stories about the pirates of the early eighteenth century. In the years after 1715, a few thousand pirates operated in the Caribbean, along the American coast, off West Africa and in the Indian Ocean. Their names were reported in newspapers in London and Boston. Their trials were published, and their stories were collected in books. Historians such as Marcus Rediker and David Cordingly have studied the records of these men in detail. They found that real pirate life was very different from the legends. Most pirates were ordinary sailors. Many were in their twenties. Few became rich, and most were active for only a year or two. Even so, their story has lasted for three centuries. It reflects a time of war, trade and change across the Atlantic world.

TOPIC: Why men became pirates
===
IMG: navy-sailors-paid-off-dockside-1713-crowds-ships | navy sailors paid off dockside in 1713 crowds ships
HEAD: The end of a long European war in 1713 left thousands of sailors without work.
BODY: From 1701 to 1714, the major European powers fought the War of the Spanish Succession. In America, the conflict was called Queen Anne's War, after the queen of England. During the war, Britain and other countries employed large numbers of sailors in their navies and on privateers. Privateers were privately owned ships licensed by a government to attack enemy shipping. When peace treaties were signed at Utrecht in 1713, many of these sailors were dismissed. The Royal Navy alone reduced its numbers from about fifty thousand men to around thirteen thousand. Thousands of experienced seamen suddenly had no work. Historians see this as one of the main causes of the rise of piracy after 1715. Many former privateers simply continued attacking ships, now without a licence. Even so, not every unemployed sailor became a pirate. Most found work on merchant ships, often for low wages and under harsh discipline.
===
IMG: merchant-ship-deck-sailors-hard-work-stern-captain | merchant ship deck sailors hard work stern captain
HEAD: In July 1715, a hurricane wrecked a Spanish treasure fleet off the coast of Florida.
BODY: Life on merchant ships could be very hard. Wages were low, food was poor, and captains could punish sailors severely. These conditions encouraged some men to turn to piracy. Then, in July 1715, a hurricane struck a Spanish treasure fleet of eleven ships sailing from Havana to Spain. The ships were wrecked on the coast of Florida, and their cargo of silver and gold scattered across the shallow sea. Spanish salvage teams worked to recover the treasure. Soon, however, sailors from Jamaica, the Bahamas and elsewhere arrived to take what they could. Historians regard the wreck of the 1715 fleet as a turning point. Many of the men who raided the wrecks later became pirates. Divers still find coins from these wrecks today along the coast of Florida, which is sometimes called the Treasure Coast. Even so, only a few people became rich from the treasure, and many lost what they found quickly.
===
IMG: sloops-anchored-bahamas-harbour-palm-shore-sunlight | sloops anchored Bahamas harbour palm shore sunlight
HEAD: Pirates gathered at Nassau, on the island of New Providence in the Bahamas.
BODY: The Bahamas lie to the southeast of Florida. In 1715, the islands were a neglected British colony with no effective government. Nassau, the main town on the island of New Providence, had been attacked several times during the war and was largely in ruins. Its harbour was sheltered and shallow, ideal for the small, fast sloops that pirates preferred. Larger warships could not easily enter. The town was also close to the busy shipping routes between the Caribbean and Europe. For these reasons, pirates began to use Nassau as their base. By 1716, hundreds of pirates lived there. Historians note that the British colonies were growing quickly, increasing the amount of trade. Yet the Royal Navy had only a few ships in American waters to protect it. Even so, Nassau was never a planned settlement. It grew because it was convenient, and pirates came and went as they pleased.
===
IMG: diverse-sailors-young-men-dock-caribbean-port-afternoon | diverse sailors young men dock Caribbean port afternoon
HEAD: Most pirates were young, experienced sailors from many different backgrounds.
BODY: Historians have studied trial records and lists of captured pirates to learn who they were. Most were in their twenties, and almost all had worked at sea before. Many came from England, Wales, Scotland and Ireland. Others came from the American colonies, the Caribbean, the Netherlands and France. A significant number were of African descent. Some were free sailors, while others had escaped slavery. Pirates were skilled at handling ships, reading the weather and navigating among the islands. They knew the risks of their choice. Piracy was punished by hanging, and many pirates were executed in public. Even so, some sailors preferred a short life of freedom to a long life of hardship on merchant ships. The historian Marcus Rediker has described pirate crews as communities that rejected the harsh discipline of the ordinary maritime world. Yet pirates also used violence and threats against the people they robbed.
===
TOPIC: Nassau and the harbour towns
===
IMG: nassau-1716-harbour-shacks-palms-ships-ruined-fort | Nassau 1716 harbour shacks palms ships ruined fort
HEAD: By 1717, Nassau had become the main pirate base in the Caribbean.
BODY: Nassau in 1717 was a small and untidy town. It stood on the northern shore of New Providence, overlooking a harbour protected by a long, low island. An old fort, damaged in earlier attacks, stood near the water. A few hundred settlers lived there alongside the pirates. The Bahamas consist of hundreds of low islands and cays with white sand beaches and clear lagoons. The waters around them are shallow and full of reefs, which made them difficult for unfamiliar ships to navigate. Pirates who knew the channels could escape easily. Historians know about Nassau in this period partly from reports sent by colonial officials to London. These letters complained about the pirates and asked for help. Even so, life in Nassau was not entirely lawless. Pirates and local traders depended on each other. The traders sold supplies, and the pirates provided cheap goods taken from captured ships.
===
IMG: pirate-camp-beach-sailcloth-tents-cooking-fires | pirate camp beach sailcloth tents cooking fires
HEAD: Some writers have called Nassau in this period a republic of pirates.
BODY: Nassau had no governor and no regular government between 1715 and 1718. Pirate captains and crews made decisions among themselves. For this reason, some historians and writers, such as Colin Woodard, have described the town as a republic of pirates. The phrase is partly symbolic, since there was no formal constitution or system of government. Many pirates lived in tents made from old sails or in simple huts of wood and palm leaves along the beach. Taverns and stalls sold drink and food. Traders from other colonies came to buy stolen goods at low prices. Historians note that some merchants in respectable ports, such as Charleston and Boston, also traded with pirates in secret. Even so, Nassau was crowded, dirty and often unhealthy. Fevers spread easily in the heat. For many pirates, it was simply a place to rest, repair their ships and spend their money before sailing again.
===
IMG: beach-tavern-palm-roof-traders-barrels-crates-lively | beach tavern palm roof traders barrels crates lively
HEAD: Goods taken from captured ships were traded and spent in Nassau.
BODY: Pirates brought many kinds of goods to Nassau. Captured cargoes included cloth, sugar, wine, rum, cocoa, indigo, tea and tools. Since most of these goods were difficult to sell openly, pirates traded them cheaply to merchants willing to take the risk. Ships that had been captured and stripped were sometimes abandoned in the harbour. Pirates spent much of their time ashore drinking, gambling, eating and singing. Historians have found descriptions of these activities in the testimonies of captured sailors and in reports from officials. Some accounts may have been exaggerated to make pirates seem more dangerous. Even so, the picture is consistent. Life ashore was relaxed and disorderly. Money and goods were spent quickly. Many pirates did not plan for the future, since they knew their careers might be short. When supplies ran low or news of a rich ship arrived, crews prepared their sloops and returned to sea.
===
IMG: pirate-captains-meeting-tavern-table-maps-rum-candle | pirate captains meeting tavern table maps rum candle
HEAD: Edward Thatch, later known as Blackbeard, sailed with the pirate captain Benjamin Hornigold.
BODY: Benjamin Hornigold was one of the leading pirate captains at Nassau. He had been active since about 1713, attacking mainly Spanish and French ships. He was known for refusing to attack British ships, which caused disagreements with some of his crew. In 1716 and 1717, a younger man named Edward Thatch sailed with him. Thatch learned the skills of command and soon received his own sloop. His name appears in documents spelled in different ways, including Thatch, Thache, Teach and Tach. Historians note that spelling was not standardised in the period, so variations are common. Records from 1717 show Thatch and Hornigold operating together off the American coast. Even so, they eventually separated. Hornigold later accepted a royal pardon and even helped hunt down other pirates. Thatch continued his career and became far more famous than his former captain. He became known by a nickname that described his appearance: Blackbeard.
===
IMG: port-royal-jamaica-harbour-1718-ships-warehouses-quay | port Royal Jamaica harbour in 1718 ships warehouses quay
HEAD: Large Caribbean ports such as Port Royal and Kingston in Jamaica were busy centres of trade.
BODY: Pirates did not live only in Nassau. They also visited other ports where goods could be sold and supplies bought. Jamaica was the most important British colony in the Caribbean. Its main harbour had been a centre for privateers in the seventeenth century, when the town of Port Royal was known as one of the wealthiest and most lively ports in the Americas. Ships from Britain, Africa, the American colonies and other islands arrived there constantly. The waterfront was lined with warehouses, taverns and shops. Historians know about Port Royal from records, letters and extensive underwater archaeology. Even so, by the time of Blackbeard, Jamaican authorities were hostile to pirates. Pirates captured there could be tried and hanged. Many pirates therefore avoided the main ports or visited them in disguise. They preferred smaller harbours and quiet islands where officials were weak or willing to look the other way.
===
IMG: wharf-porters-barrels-mule-carts-merchants-hot-sun | wharf porters barrels mule carts merchants hot sun
HEAD: Caribbean port towns were crowded with ships, merchants, sailors and workers from many lands.
BODY: A busy Caribbean port of the early eighteenth century received ships from many places. Vessels arrived from London, Bristol and Liverpool, from Boston, New York and Philadelphia, and from West Africa, Spain and France. Porters, many of them enslaved, carried barrels and crates between ships and warehouses. Mule carts moved goods through the streets. Merchants inspected cargoes and negotiated prices. Behind the waterfront, streets were lined with houses of brick and wood, often with shutters and balconies to keep out the sun. Taverns served rum punch to sailors. Historians use port records, inventories and travellers' accounts to reconstruct these towns. Many visitors commented on the heat, the noise and the mixture of languages. Even so, port towns could be unhealthy. Yellow fever and other diseases spread quickly, and many newcomers died within months of arrival. Port life combined great wealth with real danger, especially for the poor and the enslaved.
===
IMG: calm-harbour-kingston-jamaica-blue-mountains-morning | calm harbour Kingston Jamaica blue mountains morning
HEAD: In 1692, a great earthquake destroyed much of Port Royal, and Kingston grew up across the harbour.
BODY: On the seventh of June 1692, a powerful earthquake struck Jamaica. Port Royal was built on a sandy spit at the entrance to the harbour. When the earthquake hit, much of the loose ground turned liquid and slid into the sea. Large parts of the town sank, and thousands of people died. Survivors moved across the harbour and founded a new town, Kingston, which later became the capital of Jamaica. Historians and archaeologists have studied the ruins of Port Royal under the water. Excavations in the twentieth century recovered buildings, tools, pottery and even a pocket watch that stopped at the time of the earthquake, according to tradition. Even so, Port Royal was partly rebuilt and remained a naval base. By Blackbeard's time, Kingston was growing into the main commercial port of Jamaica. The Blue Mountains rose behind it, and its harbour was one of the largest natural harbours in the Caribbean.

TOPIC: Edward Thatch
===
IMG: tall-man-black-beard-1717-caribbean-dock-portrait-calm | tall man black beard in 1717 Caribbean dock portrait calm
HEAD: Little is known for certain about Edward Thatch's life before he became a pirate.
BODY: Much of what people believe about Blackbeard comes from one book. It was published in London in 1724, six years after his death, and was called A General History of the Robberies and Murders of the most notorious Pyrates. The author used the name Captain Charles Johnson, which was probably a pen name. Scholars still debate who really wrote it. The book drew on trial records, newspapers and interviews, but it also included dramatic stories that cannot be confirmed. Historians therefore treat it with caution. According to Johnson, Thatch was born in Bristol, England. More recent research, especially by the historian Baylus Brooks, suggests that Thatch may have come from a respectable family in Jamaica, the son of a ship's captain. Even so, the evidence is incomplete. What is certain is that Thatch was an experienced sailor who could read and write. He appears in records as a pirate only from about 1716.
===
IMG: bristol-harbour-1700s-tall-ships-quayside-houses | Bristol harbour in the 1700s tall ships quayside houses
HEAD: Edward Thatch was probably born around 1680, making him older than most pirates.
BODY: If the usual estimate of about 1680 is correct, Thatch was in his late thirties in 1718. Most pirates were younger, often in their twenties. Johnson's book says that Thatch served on privateers from Jamaica during Queen Anne's War and showed courage and skill. This is plausible, because many pirates had previously served as privateers, but no direct record of his privateering has been found. Bristol, the city named by Johnson as his birthplace, was one of the busiest ports in England. Its quays were crowded with ships trading with Africa, the Caribbean and the American colonies. Historians note that sailors from Bristol were common throughout the Atlantic world. Even so, the link between Thatch and Bristol remains uncertain. Whatever his origins, he had the experience and authority to command men. By 1717, he was the captain of his own ship and was beginning to build a reputation along the American coast.
===
IMG: blackbeard-beard-braided-ribbons-tricorn-hat-ship-deck | Blackbeard beard braided ribbons tricorn hat ship deck
HEAD: People who met Blackbeard described him as tall and thin, with a very long black beard.
BODY: Some of the best evidence about Blackbeard comes from the captains of ships he captured. In December 1717, Captain Henry Bostock was captured near the island of Saint Christopher. He later described Thatch as a tall, spare man with a very black beard, which he wore very long. Other witnesses said that Blackbeard treated his prisoners reasonably and often released them with their ships. Historians note that there is no reliable record of Blackbeard killing anyone before his final battle in 1718. He seems to have relied mainly on fear and reputation rather than violence. Most ships surrendered without a fight when they saw his flag and his large crew. Even so, captured sailors lost their cargoes and sometimes their ships. Some were forced to join the pirate crew. The image of Blackbeard as a monster comes mainly from later writers, while the records show a cautious and calculating leader.
===
TOPIC: The Queen Anne's Revenge
===
IMG: queen-annes-revenge-ship-full-sail-forty-guns-blue-sea | Queen Anne's revenge ship full sail forty guns blue sea
HEAD: In November 1717, Blackbeard captured a large French ship and made it his flagship.
BODY: On the twenty-eighth of November 1717, near the island of Martinique, Blackbeard and his men captured a French ship called La Concorde. It was a slave ship, carrying several hundred enslaved Africans from West Africa. Blackbeard took the ship to the island of Bequia in the Grenadines. There, he gave the French crew a smaller vessel so they could return home. Many of the Africans were left on the island, though some joined or were taken by the pirates. Historians know about this capture from the testimony of the French officers, which survives in French archives. Blackbeard fitted the ship with around forty cannons and renamed it the Queen Anne's Revenge. It became one of the most powerful pirate ships in the Americas. Even so, the large ship had disadvantages. It needed a big crew and deep water. In the end, its size would contribute to its loss on the coast of North Carolina.
===
IMG: portrait-queen-anne-old-painting-candlelight-frame | portrait Queen Anne old painting candlelight frame
HEAD: The name Queen Anne's Revenge may have reflected support for the old royal family of Britain.
BODY: Queen Anne ruled Great Britain until her death in 1714. She was succeeded by George the First, a German prince from Hanover. Some people in Britain did not accept the new king. They supported the exiled Stuart family, the descendants of Anne's half-brother, and were known as Jacobites. Historians have suggested that the name Queen Anne's Revenge may express Jacobite sympathies. Some pirates were known to drink toasts to the Stuart claimant. Even so, this interpretation is uncertain. The name might also have been simply defiant, a reference to the war fought in Anne's name. On board the ship were around three hundred men at times. This was far more than a merchant ship of the same size would carry, which might have needed only twenty or so. Pirates needed large crews to sail captured ships, fire many guns and overwhelm their victims quickly. Large crews also meant that loot had to be divided among many people.
===
IMG: small-pirate-fleet-four-ships-sailing-together-sunset | small pirate fleet four ships sailing together sunset
HEAD: During the winter of 1717 and 1718, Blackbeard sailed through the Caribbean with a small fleet.
BODY: By early 1718, Blackbeard commanded several vessels, including the Queen Anne's Revenge and a number of sloops. Together, they formed one of the strongest pirate forces in the Americas. During the winter, the fleet sailed among the islands of the eastern Caribbean. Records show captures near Saint Vincent, Saint Lucia, Nevis, Antigua and the Virgin Islands, and later near Puerto Rico and Hispaniola. Blackbeard took food, supplies, weapons and sometimes new crew members from the ships he captured. Historians piece together these movements from the reports of captured captains and colonial governors, who wrote to London to describe the threat. Even so, the exact route is not always clear, because reports were sometimes confused or delayed. In the spring of 1718, the fleet sailed north toward the coast of the American colonies. This route avoided the coming hurricane season and brought the pirates close to busy trade routes.
===
IMG: navigator-backstaff-measuring-sun-deck-noon-tropics | navigator backstaff measuring sun deck noon tropics
HEAD: Navigators in 1718 found their position using the sun, simple instruments and careful calculation.
BODY: In the early eighteenth century, sailors had no engines, radios or satellites. They depended on wind and on their skill in navigation. To find latitude, their position north or south, navigators measured the height of the sun at noon or of the Pole Star at night. They used instruments such as the backstaff, also called the Davis quadrant, which allowed them to measure the sun's angle without looking directly at it. Using tables, they could then calculate their latitude quite accurately. Longitude, the position east or west, was much harder to determine. Navigators estimated it by a method called dead reckoning, using speed, direction and time. Historians know about these methods from navigation manuals and ship logs of the period. Even so, errors were common, especially on long voyages. Many ships were wrecked because sailors believed they were farther from land than they really were. Experience and knowledge of local waters were therefore extremely valuable.
===
IMG: sailors-throwing-log-line-knots-sandglass-stern | sailors throwing log line knots sandglass stern
HEAD: Sailors measured their speed with a log line and a sandglass.
BODY: To estimate speed, sailors used a device called a log line. A piece of wood, the log, was thrown into the water from the stern. It was attached to a long line with knots tied at regular intervals. As the ship moved forward, the line ran out. A sailor counted how many knots passed through his hands while a small sandglass emptied, usually in about thirty seconds. The number of knots showed the ship's speed. This is the origin of the word knot as a unit of speed at sea. The results were recorded in the logbook, together with direction and time. Historians use these logbooks to reconstruct voyages. Even so, dead reckoning was often inaccurate. Currents and wind could push a ship off course without anyone noticing. The longitude problem was solved only later in the century, when the English clockmaker John Harrison built accurate marine timekeepers in the 1750s and 1760s.

HEAD: In shallow waters, sailors measured depth with a lead line and relied on charts and local knowledge.
BODY: Close to land, the greatest danger was running aground. Sailors measured the depth of the water with a lead line, a weighted rope marked at regular intervals. A sailor at the bow threw the lead forward and called out the depth. The bottom of the lead was often filled with tallow, which picked up sand or shells, showing the type of seabed. Charts of coasts and islands also helped, though many were inaccurate. Pirates valued good charts highly and took them from captured ships. The steady trade winds of the Caribbean blew mostly from the east. This made sailing west easy and sailing east difficult. Historians note that pirates often followed seasonal patterns, using the winds and avoiding storms. Even so, much depended on the experience of pilots who knew particular waters. In the Bahamas and along the Carolina coast, local knowledge of channels and sandbars could mean the difference between escape and disaster.

HEAD: During the hurricane season, many pirates left the Caribbean and sailed north to the American coast.
BODY: Hurricanes were the greatest natural danger for ships in the Caribbean. They usually came between July and October. Experienced sailors watched for warning signs, such as a long swell from an unusual direction, strange skies and a falling barometer, though barometers were rare on ships at the time. When a storm threatened, ships tried to reach a sheltered harbour and anchored securely. Historians find many records of ships lost in hurricanes, including the Spanish treasure fleet of 1715. For this reason, pirates often changed their hunting grounds with the seasons. In summer, many sailed north along the coast of the American colonies, from the Carolinas to New England. There, they could find merchant ships and avoid the worst storms. Even so, the northern coasts had their own dangers. Sandbars, shoals and sudden gales made navigation difficult. In the spring of 1718, Blackbeard's fleet followed this pattern and headed toward the Carolinas.
