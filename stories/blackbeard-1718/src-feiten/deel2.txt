TOPIC: The crew
===
IMG: pirate-crew-gathered-deck-listening-quartermaster-reading | pirate crew gathered deck listening quartermaster reading
HEAD: Pirate crews were often governed by written articles that the crew agreed to together.
BODY: Life on a pirate ship was organised differently from life on a merchant or navy ship. On those ships, the captain held almost total authority, and sailors had no say in decisions. Many pirate crews instead agreed on a set of rules, called articles. Each man signed or made his mark on them when he joined. The articles covered the division of loot, punishments, compensation for injuries and rules about behaviour on board. Captains were usually elected by the crew and could be removed by a vote. Historians know about pirate articles because several sets were recorded in Johnson's book and in trial documents. Those of Bartholomew Roberts are among the best known. Blackbeard's own articles have not survived. Even so, historians believe his crew followed similar customs. The system gave ordinary sailors more power than they had on other ships, though discipline could still be harsh.
===
IMG: quartermaster-sharing-coins-crew-table-lantern-careful | quartermaster sharing coins crew table lantern careful
HEAD: On pirate ships, loot was divided by the quartermaster according to fixed shares.
BODY: When pirates captured a ship, everything of value was collected and kept together until it could be divided. The quartermaster, an officer elected by the crew, was responsible for this. He also settled disputes and handled discipline in many crews. According to surviving articles, the captain usually received two shares, officers such as the quartermaster, boatswain and gunner received one and a half or one and a quarter shares, and every ordinary pirate received one share. Some articles also promised payments to men who were injured, such as a set sum for the loss of an arm or a leg. Historians note that this system was much more equal than on merchant ships, where owners and captains took most of the profits. Even so, the actual amounts were often small. Cargoes of cloth, sugar or tools were hard to sell for good prices. Few pirates ever gained the large fortunes of popular legend.
===
IMG: pirate-sailors-many-backgrounds-working-rigging-together | pirate sailors many backgrounds working rigging together
HEAD: Pirate crews included men of many nationalities, and many crews included men of African descent.
BODY: Records of captured pirate crews show great variety. Men from Britain, Ireland and the American colonies were the largest groups, but there were also French, Dutch, Scandinavian and Portuguese sailors. Many crews included men of African descent. Some were free sailors. Others had escaped slavery or had been taken from captured slave ships. On some ships, Black pirates were full members of the crew, with a vote and a share of the loot. Historians estimate that in some crews, perhaps a quarter or more of the men were of African descent. In Blackbeard's final crew, several Black men were captured and tried alongside the others. Even so, treatment varied greatly between ships. On some, Africans were treated as captives or sold. The historian Kenneth Kinkor and others have studied these records. They show that pirate ships could offer opportunities, but also that the wider world of slavery reached even into pirate crews.

TOPIC: The face of Blackbeard
===
IMG: blackbeard-deck-dramatic-black-beard-pistols-sash-calm-sea | Blackbeard deck dramatic black beard pistols sash calm sea
HEAD: Johnson's book described Blackbeard as wearing ribbons in his beard and burning fuses under his hat.
BODY: The most famous description of Blackbeard comes from Johnson's book of 1724. According to Johnson, Blackbeard's beard was deep black and very long, covering his face up to his eyes. He twisted it into small tails tied with ribbons. In battle, Johnson wrote, he carried several pistols in holsters across his chest and stuck lit slow matches, burning fuses used to fire cannons, under his hat, so that smoke surrounded his face. This image has shaped every later picture of Blackbeard. Historians note that some details may be true, while others may be exaggerated. The account was written after his death, partly to entertain readers. Even so, it fits what is known of Blackbeard's methods. He seems to have understood that a frightening appearance could make ships surrender without a fight. A reputation for terror saved time, ammunition and lives, including the lives of his own crew.
===
IMG: ship-hatch-smoke-drifting-out-sailors-coughing-laughing | ship hatch smoke drifting out sailors coughing laughing
HEAD: Many stories about Blackbeard, such as his fourteen wives, cannot be confirmed.
BODY: Johnson's book included several colourful stories about Blackbeard. It claimed that he had fourteen wives, with one in every port. It also told how he once shut himself and some of his men below decks with burning pots of sulphur, to see who could endure the smoke the longest. Historians have found no evidence for these stories. They may be inventions or exaggerations meant to make Blackbeard seem wild and devilish. There is evidence that Blackbeard married a young woman in North Carolina in 1718, according to later reports, though even this is uncertain. Johnson also reported that before his final battle, Blackbeard was asked whether his wife knew where his money was hidden. He supposedly answered that only he and the devil knew. Even so, such stories reveal more about how people imagined pirates than about the real man. They helped turn Blackbeard into a legend within a few years of his death.
===
IMG: empty-beach-palm-trees-moonlight-legend-of-treasure | empty beach palm trees moonlight legend of Treasure
HEAD: Historians believe Blackbeard never buried treasure, and no treasure of his has ever been found.
BODY: Ever since the eighteenth century, people have searched for Blackbeard's treasure on islands and beaches from the Caribbean to the Carolinas. Nothing has ever been found. Historians think there probably was never any buried treasure. Pirates usually spent their money quickly on food, drink and pleasure. Most loot consisted of goods rather than gold and silver. Burying it would have made little sense. The idea of buried pirate treasure became popular mainly through later fiction. The famous pirate flag associated with Blackbeard, showing a horned skeleton holding a spear beside a red heart, also has no confirmed link to him. It appears to have been attributed to him in the twentieth century. Even so, pirate flags were real. Many pirates flew black flags, sometimes with skulls, skeletons or hourglasses. A black flag meant that the crew would show mercy if the ship surrendered. A red flag warned that no mercy would be given.
===
IMG: merchant-captain-handing-over-ship-papers-pirate-politely | merchant captain handing over ship papers pirate politely
HEAD: Blackbeard relied on reputation and intimidation more than on violence.
BODY: Witnesses who met Blackbeard at sea rarely described brutal violence. Most ships he attacked surrendered quickly. Their captains handed over their papers, cargo and valuables, and were usually released afterwards. Historians such as Angus Konstam have argued that Blackbeard deliberately built a frightening image to make his work easier. A large crew, a powerful ship and a fearsome appearance encouraged ships to surrender without resistance. Fighting was dangerous for pirates too, since injuries could be fatal without proper medical care. Even so, intimidation was itself a form of violence. Captured sailors feared for their lives. Some ships were burned, and some men were forced to join the pirates. Blackbeard's reputation spread through newspapers and letters along the American coast. Merchants and governors feared him greatly. In this sense, he was an early example of the power of a public image. More than three centuries later, his name is still the best known of all pirates.

TOPIC: Stede Bonnet and the ship's surgeon
===
IMG: gentleman-planter-barbados-1717-wig-fine-coat-garden | gentleman planter Barbados in 1717 wig fine coat garden
HEAD: Stede Bonnet, a wealthy planter from Barbados, became a pirate in 1717 and sailed with Blackbeard.
BODY: Stede Bonnet was born in Barbados around 1688. He inherited a large sugar plantation and lived as a wealthy gentleman, with a wife and children. He was educated and owned a library. He had no experience as a sailor. In 1717, for reasons that remain unclear, he decided to become a pirate. Johnson suggested that he was unhappy in his marriage, but historians are cautious about this explanation. Some have suggested that he suffered from a mental illness. Bonnet's lack of experience soon caused problems. His crew had little respect for his leadership. In late 1717, he met Blackbeard, who took control of Bonnet's ship and kept Bonnet aboard the Queen Anne's Revenge as a sort of guest. Historians know about Bonnet from his trial records, which are unusually detailed. Even so, many aspects of his motives remain mysterious. He is often called the gentleman pirate because of his unusual background.
===
IMG: sloop-being-fitted-barbados-harbour-cannons-loaded | sloop being fitted Barbados harbour cannons loaded
HEAD: Stede Bonnet bought his own ship and paid his crew wages, unlike most pirates.
BODY: Most pirates captured their ships. Stede Bonnet bought his. He purchased a sloop, armed it with about ten guns and hired a crew of around seventy men. He named the ship the Revenge. Unusually, he paid his crew regular wages rather than sharing loot according to pirate custom. One night in 1717, he left Barbados without telling his family and began attacking ships along the American coast. Historians note that this behaviour was very unusual. Bonnet seems to have been attracted by the idea of piracy rather than driven by poverty. He captured several ships off Virginia, New York and the Carolinas. Even so, his lack of sailing skill made him dependent on his crew. When he joined Blackbeard, the more experienced pirate quickly took command. Bonnet's crew seems to have accepted the change willingly, since Blackbeard was a far more capable leader. Bonnet remained aboard, reportedly reading books and walking the deck.
===
IMG: charleston-1718-town-harbour-wooden-houses-church-spire | Charleston in 1718 town harbour wooden houses church spire
HEAD: Stede Bonnet was captured in 1718 and hanged in Charleston in December of that year.
BODY: After leaving Blackbeard, Stede Bonnet briefly accepted a royal pardon but soon returned to piracy under a false name. In September 1718, he was cornered in the Cape Fear River in North Carolina by an expedition sent from South Carolina under Colonel William Rhett. After a long battle, Bonnet and his crew surrendered. He was taken to Charleston for trial. Before the trial, he escaped, reportedly disguised as a woman, but he was recaptured. Most of his crew were hanged in November. Bonnet wrote an emotional letter to the governor asking for mercy, but he was executed on the tenth of December 1718. Historians use the detailed trial records to study his life. Even so, his story remains puzzling. He was a wealthy man who gave up comfort for a dangerous life he was poorly prepared for. His case attracted great attention at the time and was widely reported in colonial newspapers.
===
IMG: white-point-garden-oaks-harbour-benches-quiet-afternoon | LATER: White Point Garden in Charleston today, old live oak trees, benches and a view over the harbour, quiet afternoon
HEAD: Stede Bonnet's story has been retold many times, including in a recent television comedy.
BODY: Bonnet was hanged near the waterfront of Charleston, probably close to the area now known as White Point Garden. Today, this is a quiet public park with large oak trees, benches and views over the harbour. A small marker recalls the pirates who were executed nearby in 1718. Historians note that Charleston played a major role in the fight against pirates. Its merchants suffered greatly from pirate attacks, and its authorities organised expeditions against them. Bonnet's story has fascinated writers ever since. In 2022, it inspired a television comedy, which imagined him as a kind and gentle man who became friends with Blackbeard. Historians enjoyed the attention but pointed out that the show was fiction. Even so, the real story contained some of the same elements: an unlikely pirate, a famous partnership and a sad ending. Bonnet's life shows that the world of piracy attracted people from very different backgrounds.
===
IMG: ships-surgeon-1718-wooden-medicine-chest-bottles-jars | ships surgeon in 1718 wooden medicine chest bottles jars
HEAD: Sailors in the tropics suffered from many diseases, and medicine was precious on pirate ships.
BODY: Staying healthy at sea in the early eighteenth century was very difficult, especially in tropical waters. Sailors suffered from scurvy on long voyages, caused by a lack of vitamin C, though this was not understood at the time. Fevers such as yellow fever and malaria were common in the Caribbean. Dysentery spread easily in crowded ships with poor sanitation. Injuries from accidents and fighting were frequent. Sexually transmitted diseases, especially syphilis, were also widespread among sailors. Historians know about shipboard health from medical manuals, ship records and the writings of naval surgeons. Most ships carried a medicine chest, and larger ships had a surgeon. Even so, medical knowledge was limited. Many treatments were ineffective or harmful. Yet some, such as cleaning wounds and setting bones, did help. For pirates, who could not visit doctors or apothecaries in towns, a good supply of medicine was particularly valuable, as the events at Charleston in 1718 would show.
===
IMG: surgeon-bandaging-sailors-arm-cabin-lantern-kindly | surgeon bandaging sailors arm cabin lantern kindly
HEAD: Pirates often forced ship's surgeons from captured vessels to join their crews.
BODY: Surgeons were among the most valued members of any crew. Pirates frequently captured surgeons from merchant ships and forced them to serve on board. Trial records show that several surgeons later claimed in court that they had been taken against their will, which sometimes saved them from hanging. Pirates generally treated surgeons well and paid them a good share. A surgeon's work included treating wounds, setting broken bones, amputating damaged limbs and treating fevers. Historians note that a surgeon's skills could greatly improve a crew's chances of survival. Even so, operations were performed without anaesthetic and in difficult conditions. Many patients died from infection. The surgeon's chest contained herbs, roots, powders, pills and ointments. One important medicine was Peruvian bark, from the cinchona tree of South America. It contained quinine, which helped treat malaria, although its active ingredient was not identified until the nineteenth century. Mercury compounds were used to treat syphilis.
===
IMG: apothecary-jars-labels-latin-shelves-mortar-pestle | apothecary jars labels latin shelves mortar pestle
HEAD: Archaeologists have found medical instruments and traces of medicines on the wreck of the Queen Anne's Revenge.
BODY: Surgeons' tools in the early eighteenth century included knives, saws, probes, forceps and syringes. A brass bowl was used for bleeding patients, a common treatment believed to restore the balance of the body. Medicines were kept in glass bottles, pottery jars and wooden boxes, often labelled in Latin. Apothecaries in towns prepared them using mortars and pestles. Archaeologists excavating the wreck of the Queen Anne's Revenge have found medical equipment, including a brass syringe of the type used to treat syphilis with mercury. Traces of mercury were also detected. Historians link these finds to the importance of medicine for Blackbeard's crew. Pirates could not buy medicines openly in towns without risking arrest. They had to take them from captured ships or obtain them in other ways. Even so, medicine chests were not always found on captured ships. This shortage may explain one of Blackbeard's most famous actions, the blockade of Charleston in May 1718.

TOPIC: The blockade of Charleston
===
IMG: pirate-sloop-chasing-merchant-ship-full-sail-sunny-sea | pirate sloop chasing merchant ship full sail sunny sea
HEAD: Most pirate captures involved a chase and a threat rather than a fierce battle.
BODY: Popular films often show pirate attacks as dramatic battles, with cannon fire and sword fights. Historical records suggest that most captures were much quieter. Pirates first had to find a suitable ship. They often waited near busy routes, headlands or harbour entrances. When a sail was spotted, the pirates approached, sometimes flying a friendly flag to avoid alarming their target. Their sloops were fast and could usually catch heavy merchant ships. Historians have studied many accounts from captured captains. Most described being overtaken and threatened rather than attacked with heavy fire. Even so, some ships did resist, and fights could be deadly. Pirates preferred to avoid fighting because it risked damage to the ship they wanted to capture and injury to their own crew. Speed, surprise and intimidation were their most important weapons. Large crews made a strong impression as soon as the pirates came close.
===
IMG: black-flag-rising-mast-merchant-crew-watching-surprised | black flag rising mast merchant crew watching surprised
HEAD: When pirates came close, they raised their flag and fired a warning shot to demand surrender.
BODY: Once close enough, pirates raised their black flag to reveal who they were. They often fired a single warning shot across the bow of the target ship. Then the crew crowded onto the deck, shouting, waving weapons and making as much noise as possible with drums and trumpets. The purpose was to frighten the merchant crew into surrendering quickly. Merchant ships usually had small crews, often fewer than twenty men, and few guns. Sailors were not paid to fight, and the cargo usually did not belong to them. Historians note that most merchant crews chose to surrender rather than risk their lives. Records show that pirates generally treated crews that surrendered quickly better than those that resisted. Even so, the moment of surrender was frightening. Merchant sailors knew stories of pirate cruelty and could not be sure how they would be treated. The black flag was meant to make that fear work in the pirates' favour.
===
IMG: merchant-sailors-lowering-sails-calm-sea-giving-up | merchant sailors lowering sails calm sea giving up
HEAD: After a ship surrendered, pirates boarded it and searched it for valuables and supplies.
BODY: A surrendering ship lowered its sails and its flag. The pirates then rowed across in boats and climbed aboard. They gathered the crew on deck and questioned the captain about the cargo and any money on board. They searched the ship thoroughly, from the captain's cabin to the hold. They took money, gold and silver if any was found, and also cloth, food, drink, tools, weapons, sails, rope, medicine and navigational instruments. Historians note that ordinary supplies were often more important to pirates than treasure. A pirate ship needed constant repair and resupply. Captured captains later described these searches in testimony, listing what had been taken. Even so, behaviour varied. Some pirates were orderly and polite, while others were rough and threatening. Captains were sometimes pressured to reveal hidden valuables. Pirates also looked for skilled sailors, such as carpenters, navigators and surgeons, whom they might force to join their crew.
===
IMG: pirates-talking-captured-sailors-deck-friendly-invitation | pirates talking captured sailors deck friendly invitation
HEAD: Pirates often invited captured sailors to join them, and some accepted willingly.
BODY: After taking what they wanted, pirates usually released the captured ship with its captain and remaining crew. Sometimes they kept a good ship for their own use, or burned it to prevent it from raising the alarm. Pirates also invited captured sailors to join them. Some sailors accepted, attracted by the share system and the freedom of pirate life. Others were forced to join, especially if they had useful skills. Historians note that forced men often asked for written certificates stating that they had been taken against their will, in case they were later captured and tried. Courts sometimes accepted such evidence. Even so, it could be difficult to prove. For most people who met Blackbeard at sea, the experience was frightening and costly but not deadly. They lost cargo and sometimes their ship, but kept their lives. Their accounts of these meetings are among the most valuable sources historians have about pirate life.
===
IMG: charleston-harbour-1718-ships-sandbars-low-town-spires | Charleston harbour in 1718 ships sandbars low town spires
HEAD: In May 1718, Blackbeard blockaded the harbour of Charleston, South Carolina.
BODY: In May 1718, Blackbeard sailed north from the Caribbean with a fleet of four ships and around four hundred men. He anchored at the entrance to the harbour of Charleston, then called Charles Town. It was one of the wealthiest towns in the American colonies, exporting rice and deerskins. For about a week, Blackbeard stopped every ship entering or leaving the harbour. He captured several vessels and took their passengers and crews as prisoners. Among them was Samuel Wragg, a member of the colony's governing council. Historians know about the blockade from letters written by the governor of South Carolina, Robert Johnson, and from later testimony. Even so, the exact number of ships captured is uncertain. The town had no warships to defend it, and its fortifications were weak. The blockade was one of the boldest actions by any pirate of the period and caused great alarm throughout the colonies.
===
IMG: pirate-ships-anchored-harbour-mouth-captured-vessels-near | pirate ships anchored harbour mouth captured vessels near
HEAD: During the blockade, Blackbeard demanded a chest of medicines from the governor of South Carolina.
BODY: Charleston was in a state of panic. People feared that the pirates might attack the town itself. Blackbeard sent a small group of his men ashore, together with one of the prisoners, with a demand for the governor. He did not ask for gold or silver. Instead, he asked for a chest of medicines. If the medicines were not delivered, the message said, the prisoners would be harmed. Historians have debated the reason for this unusual demand. Many believe that some of Blackbeard's crew were seriously ill, possibly with syphilis or tropical fevers. The traces of mercury found on the wreck of the Queen Anne's Revenge support this idea. Even so, there is no direct record explaining the demand. The governor's council considered the situation carefully. With no means of defence, they decided to agree. The delivery took several days, partly because the pirates sent ashore were delayed in town.
===
IMG: pirate-boat-rowing-to-town-quay-messengers-crowd | pirate boat rowing to town quay messengers crowd
HEAD: The pirates' messengers walked openly through Charleston while the town gathered the medicines.
BODY: The pirates sent ashore, including one named Richards according to some accounts, reportedly walked through the streets of Charleston while the medicine chest was being prepared. Their presence angered the townspeople, but no one could stop them. The pirates' return was delayed, and Blackbeard began to fear that his men had been captured. According to later testimony, he threatened to attack the town and harm the prisoners. Historians note that this is one of the most tense moments recorded in the story of the blockade. In the end, the messengers returned with the medicine chest, said to be worth several hundred pounds. Blackbeard released the prisoners, though he kept their valuables and some of their clothes. Even so, no one was killed. The episode shows how pirates could use the threat of violence to obtain what they needed. It also shows how vulnerable colonial towns were without naval protection.
===
IMG: prisoners-rowing-ashore-relieved-harbour-evening-light | prisoners rowing ashore relieved harbour evening light
HEAD: The blockade of Charleston lasted about a week and ended without loss of life.
BODY: After receiving the medicine chest, Blackbeard released his prisoners and sailed north along the coast. The whole blockade lasted about a week. Although no one was harmed, the people of Charleston never forgot it. Governor Robert Johnson wrote angry letters to London asking for naval protection against pirates. The event increased pressure on British authorities to act. Historians see the blockade as a high point of Blackbeard's career. It showed his boldness and his ability to plan. Even so, it also attracted the attention of powerful enemies. Within a few months, South Carolina sent its own expedition against pirates, which captured Stede Bonnet. Today, Charleston preserves much of its historic town centre. Visitors can walk along the waterfront and through streets lined with eighteenth century houses. Plaques and museums tell the story of the pirates who once blocked the harbour, and of the town that survived their visit.
