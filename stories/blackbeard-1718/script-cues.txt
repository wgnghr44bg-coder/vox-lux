In 1718, the warm waters of the Caribbean and the coast of the American colonies were crowded with ships carrying sugar, silver, rum and tobacco. For a few short years, they were also the hunting ground of pirates. This story follows the most famous of them, Edward Thatch, known as Blackbeard: the harbour of Nassau, the great ship called the Queen Anne's Revenge, daily life and food aboard a pirate crew, the blockade of Charleston, and the quiet sounds of North Carolina. It ends on the island of Ocracoke today, where his name still lingers.

In the early eighteenth century, the Caribbean Sea was one of the busiest trading regions in the world. Around the year 1718, the Caribbean was a warm sea of islands, reefs and harbours between North and South America. European powers had divided it among themselves. Spain held Cuba, Puerto Rico and much of the mainland. Britain held Jamaica, Barbados and many smaller islands. France, the Netherlands and Denmark also had colonies there. Ships sailed constantly between these islands, the American colonies, Europe and Africa. Historians call the years from about 1715 to 1726 the golden age of piracy, because so many pirates were active in this short period. The most famous of them was Edward Thatch, known as Blackbeard. Records of trials, ship logs and letters from colonial governors describe his activities. Even so, many popular ideas about pirates come from later books and films rather than from these records. The real story is quieter and more human. It is the story of sailors, traders, islanders and settlers living around a crowded and beautiful sea.

[long-pause]

{img:02-sea-turtle-coral-reef-clear-water-sunlight-fish}

The Caribbean climate was warm all year, with a dry season and a wet season. The weather in the Caribbean is warm throughout the year. From about December to May, a dry season brings blue skies and steady trade winds blowing from the east. From about June to November, a wet season brings heavy rain and thunderstorms. This is also the hurricane season. Sailors of the eighteenth century knew these patterns well and planned their voyages around them. The seas were rich in life. Coral reefs surrounded many islands, and the shallow water was full of fish, lobsters and sea turtles. Turtles were an important source of fresh meat for sailors. Historians know about the weather and the sea from ship logs, which recorded wind, weather and position every day. Many of these logs survive in British archives. Even so, the region could be dangerous. Reefs and sandbars wrecked many ships. Disease was also common, especially yellow fever and malaria, which killed many newcomers from Europe.

[long-pause]

{img:03-sugar-plantation-hills-mill-windmill-distance-hazy}

Ships carried silver, sugar, tobacco and many other goods between the Americas, Europe and Africa. The wealth of the Caribbean and the Americas moved by sea. Spanish fleets carried silver from the mines of Mexico and Peru across the Atlantic. Ships from the English and French islands carried sugar, molasses and rum. The American colonies exported tobacco, rice, timber and furs. In the other direction came cloth, tools, guns and manufactured goods from Europe. Sugar plantations depended on the labour of enslaved Africans. Hundreds of thousands of people were carried across the Atlantic in terrible conditions in this period. Historians use shipping records, customs documents and plantation accounts to measure the size of this trade. Hundreds of ships sailed between the islands every year. Even so, most were small merchant ships with small crews, often poorly armed. This made them easy targets. The great wealth moving across the sea, combined with weak protection, created the conditions in which piracy could grow so quickly in these years.

[long-pause]

{img:04-pirate-sloop-black-flag-sunset-silhouette-calm-sea}

The pirates of these few years shaped how people have imagined pirates ever since. When people today think of pirates, they often imagine black flags, buried treasure, parrots and wooden legs. Most of these images come from stories about the pirates of the early eighteenth century. In the years after 1715, a few thousand pirates operated in the Caribbean, along the American coast, off West Africa and in the Indian Ocean. Their names were reported in newspapers in London and Boston. Their trials were published, and their stories were collected in books. Historians such as Marcus Rediker and David Cordingly have studied the records of these men in detail. They found that real pirate life was very different from the legends. Most pirates were ordinary sailors. Many were in their twenties. Few became rich, and most were active for only a year or two. Even so, their story has lasted for three centuries. It reflects a time of war, trade and change across the Atlantic world.

[long-pause]

{img:05-navy-sailors-paid-off-dockside-1713-crowds-ships}

The end of a long European war in 1713 left thousands of sailors without work. From 1701 to 1714, the major European powers fought the War of the Spanish Succession. In America, the conflict was called Queen Anne's War, after the queen of England. During the war, Britain and other countries employed large numbers of sailors in their navies and on privateers. Privateers were privately owned ships licensed by a government to attack enemy shipping. When peace treaties were signed at Utrecht in 1713, many of these sailors were dismissed. The Royal Navy alone reduced its numbers from about fifty thousand men to around thirteen thousand. Thousands of experienced seamen suddenly had no work. Historians see this as one of the main causes of the rise of piracy after 1715. Many former privateers simply continued attacking ships, now without a licence. Even so, not every unemployed sailor became a pirate. Most found work on merchant ships, often for low wages and under harsh discipline.

[long-pause]

{img:06-merchant-ship-deck-sailors-hard-work-stern-captain}

In July 1715, a hurricane wrecked a Spanish treasure fleet off the coast of Florida. Life on merchant ships could be very hard. Wages were low, food was poor, and captains could punish sailors severely. These conditions encouraged some men to turn to piracy. Then, in July 1715, a hurricane struck a Spanish treasure fleet of eleven ships sailing from Havana to Spain. The ships were wrecked on the coast of Florida, and their cargo of silver and gold scattered across the shallow sea. Spanish salvage teams worked to recover the treasure. Soon, however, sailors from Jamaica, the Bahamas and elsewhere arrived to take what they could. Historians regard the wreck of the 1715 fleet as a turning point. Many of the men who raided the wrecks later became pirates. Divers still find coins from these wrecks today along the coast of Florida, which is sometimes called the Treasure Coast. Even so, only a few people became rich from the treasure, and many lost what they found quickly.

[long-pause]

{img:07-sloops-anchored-bahamas-harbour-palm-shore-sunlight}

Pirates gathered at Nassau, on the island of New Providence in the Bahamas. The Bahamas lie to the southeast of Florida. In 1715, the islands were a neglected British colony with no effective government. Nassau, the main town on the island of New Providence, had been attacked several times during the war and was largely in ruins. Its harbour was sheltered and shallow, ideal for the small, fast sloops that pirates preferred. Larger warships could not easily enter. The town was also close to the busy shipping routes between the Caribbean and Europe. For these reasons, pirates began to use Nassau as their base. By 1716, hundreds of pirates lived there. Historians note that the British colonies were growing quickly, increasing the amount of trade. Yet the Royal Navy had only a few ships in American waters to protect it. Even so, Nassau was never a planned settlement. It grew because it was convenient, and pirates came and went as they pleased.

[long-pause]

{img:08-diverse-sailors-young-men-dock-caribbean-port-afternoon}

Most pirates were young, experienced sailors from many different backgrounds. Historians have studied trial records and lists of captured pirates to learn who they were. Most were in their twenties, and almost all had worked at sea before. Many came from England, Wales, Scotland and Ireland. Others came from the American colonies, the Caribbean, the Netherlands and France. A significant number were of African descent. Some were free sailors, while others had escaped slavery. Pirates were skilled at handling ships, reading the weather and navigating among the islands. They knew the risks of their choice. Piracy was punished by hanging, and many pirates were executed in public. Even so, some sailors preferred a short life of freedom to a long life of hardship on merchant ships. The historian Marcus Rediker has described pirate crews as communities that rejected the harsh discipline of the ordinary maritime world. Yet pirates also used violence and threats against the people they robbed.

[long-pause]

{img:09-nassau-1716-harbour-shacks-palms-ships-ruined-fort}

By 1717, Nassau had become the main pirate base in the Caribbean. Nassau in 1717 was a small and untidy town. It stood on the northern shore of New Providence, overlooking a harbour protected by a long, low island. An old fort, damaged in earlier attacks, stood near the water. A few hundred settlers lived there alongside the pirates. The Bahamas consist of hundreds of low islands and cays with white sand beaches and clear lagoons. The waters around them are shallow and full of reefs, which made them difficult for unfamiliar ships to navigate. Pirates who knew the channels could escape easily. Historians know about Nassau in this period partly from reports sent by colonial officials to London. These letters complained about the pirates and asked for help. Even so, life in Nassau was not entirely lawless. Pirates and local traders depended on each other. The traders sold supplies, and the pirates provided cheap goods taken from captured ships.

[long-pause]

{img:10-pirate-camp-beach-sailcloth-tents-cooking-fires}

Some writers have called Nassau in this period a republic of pirates. Nassau had no governor and no regular government between 1715 and 1718. Pirate captains and crews made decisions among themselves. For this reason, some historians and writers, such as Colin Woodard, have described the town as a republic of pirates. The phrase is partly symbolic, since there was no formal constitution or system of government. Many pirates lived in tents made from old sails or in simple huts of wood and palm leaves along the beach. Taverns and stalls sold drink and food. Traders from other colonies came to buy stolen goods at low prices. Historians note that some merchants in respectable ports, such as Charleston and Boston, also traded with pirates in secret. Even so, Nassau was crowded, dirty and often unhealthy. Fevers spread easily in the heat. For many pirates, it was simply a place to rest, repair their ships and spend their money before sailing again.

[long-pause]

{img:11-beach-tavern-palm-roof-traders-barrels-crates-lively}

Goods taken from captured ships were traded and spent in Nassau. Pirates brought many kinds of goods to Nassau. Captured cargoes included cloth, sugar, wine, rum, cocoa, indigo, tea and tools. Since most of these goods were difficult to sell openly, pirates traded them cheaply to merchants willing to take the risk. Ships that had been captured and stripped were sometimes abandoned in the harbour. Pirates spent much of their time ashore drinking, gambling, eating and singing. Historians have found descriptions of these activities in the testimonies of captured sailors and in reports from officials. Some accounts may have been exaggerated to make pirates seem more dangerous. Even so, the picture is consistent. Life ashore was relaxed and disorderly. Money and goods were spent quickly. Many pirates did not plan for the future, since they knew their careers might be short. When supplies ran low or news of a rich ship arrived, crews prepared their sloops and returned to sea.

[long-pause]

{img:12-pirate-captains-meeting-tavern-table-maps-rum-candle}

Edward Thatch, later known as Blackbeard, sailed with the pirate captain Benjamin Hornigold. Benjamin Hornigold was one of the leading pirate captains at Nassau. He had been active since about 1713, attacking mainly Spanish and French ships. He was known for refusing to attack British ships, which caused disagreements with some of his crew. In 1716 and 1717, a younger man named Edward Thatch sailed with him. Thatch learned the skills of command and soon received his own sloop. His name appears in documents spelled in different ways, including Thatch, Thache, Teach and Tach. Historians note that spelling was not standardised in the period, so variations are common. Records from 1717 show Thatch and Hornigold operating together off the American coast. Even so, they eventually separated. Hornigold later accepted a royal pardon and even helped hunt down other pirates. Thatch continued his career and became far more famous than his former captain. He became known by a nickname that described his appearance: Blackbeard.

[long-pause]

{img:13-port-royal-jamaica-harbour-1718-ships-warehouses-quay}

Large Caribbean ports such as Port Royal and Kingston in Jamaica were busy centres of trade. Pirates did not live only in Nassau. They also visited other ports where goods could be sold and supplies bought. Jamaica was the most important British colony in the Caribbean. Its main harbour had been a centre for privateers in the seventeenth century, when the town of Port Royal was known as one of the wealthiest and most lively ports in the Americas. Ships from Britain, Africa, the American colonies and other islands arrived there constantly. The waterfront was lined with warehouses, taverns and shops. Historians know about Port Royal from records, letters and extensive underwater archaeology. Even so, by the time of Blackbeard, Jamaican authorities were hostile to pirates. Pirates captured there could be tried and hanged. Many pirates therefore avoided the main ports or visited them in disguise. They preferred smaller harbours and quiet islands where officials were weak or willing to look the other way.

[long-pause]

{img:14-wharf-porters-barrels-mule-carts-merchants-hot-sun}

Caribbean port towns were crowded with ships, merchants, sailors and workers from many lands. A busy Caribbean port of the early eighteenth century received ships from many places. Vessels arrived from London, Bristol and Liverpool, from Boston, New York and Philadelphia, and from West Africa, Spain and France. Porters, many of them enslaved, carried barrels and crates between ships and warehouses. Mule carts moved goods through the streets. Merchants inspected cargoes and negotiated prices. Behind the waterfront, streets were lined with houses of brick and wood, often with shutters and balconies to keep out the sun. Taverns served rum punch to sailors. Historians use port records, inventories and travellers' accounts to reconstruct these towns. Many visitors commented on the heat, the noise and the mixture of languages. Even so, port towns could be unhealthy. Yellow fever and other diseases spread quickly, and many newcomers died within months of arrival. Port life combined great wealth with real danger, especially for the poor and the enslaved.

[long-pause]

{img:15-calm-harbour-kingston-jamaica-blue-mountains-morning}

In 1692, a great earthquake destroyed much of Port Royal, and Kingston grew up across the harbour. On the seventh of June 1692, a powerful earthquake struck Jamaica. Port Royal was built on a sandy spit at the entrance to the harbour. When the earthquake hit, much of the loose ground turned liquid and slid into the sea. Large parts of the town sank, and thousands of people died. Survivors moved across the harbour and founded a new town, Kingston, which later became the capital of Jamaica. Historians and archaeologists have studied the ruins of Port Royal under the water. Excavations in the twentieth century recovered buildings, tools, pottery and even a pocket watch that stopped at the time of the earthquake, according to tradition. Even so, Port Royal was partly rebuilt and remained a naval base. By Blackbeard's time, Kingston was growing into the main commercial port of Jamaica. The Blue Mountains rose behind it, and its harbour was one of the largest natural harbours in the Caribbean.

[long-pause]

{img:16-tall-man-black-beard-1717-caribbean-dock-portrait-calm}

Little is known for certain about Edward Thatch's life before he became a pirate. Much of what people believe about Blackbeard comes from one book. It was published in London in 1724, six years after his death, and was called A General History of the Robberies and Murders of the most notorious Pyrates. The author used the name Captain Charles Johnson, which was probably a pen name. Scholars still debate who really wrote it. The book drew on trial records, newspapers and interviews, but it also included dramatic stories that cannot be confirmed. Historians therefore treat it with caution. According to Johnson, Thatch was born in Bristol, England. More recent research, especially by the historian Baylus Brooks, suggests that Thatch may have come from a respectable family in Jamaica, the son of a ship's captain. Even so, the evidence is incomplete. What is certain is that Thatch was an experienced sailor who could read and write. He appears in records as a pirate only from about 1716.

[long-pause]

{img:17-bristol-harbour-1700s-tall-ships-quayside-houses}

Edward Thatch was probably born around 1680, making him older than most pirates. If the usual estimate of about 1680 is correct, Thatch was in his late thirties in 1718. Most pirates were younger, often in their twenties. Johnson's book says that Thatch served on privateers from Jamaica during Queen Anne's War and showed courage and skill. This is plausible, because many pirates had previously served as privateers, but no direct record of his privateering has been found. Bristol, the city named by Johnson as his birthplace, was one of the busiest ports in England. Its quays were crowded with ships trading with Africa, the Caribbean and the American colonies. Historians note that sailors from Bristol were common throughout the Atlantic world. Even so, the link between Thatch and Bristol remains uncertain. Whatever his origins, he had the experience and authority to command men. By 1717, he was the captain of his own ship and was beginning to build a reputation along the American coast.

[long-pause]

{img:18-blackbeard-beard-braided-ribbons-tricorn-hat-ship-deck}

People who met Blackbeard described him as tall and thin, with a very long black beard. Some of the best evidence about Blackbeard comes from the captains of ships he captured. In December 1717, Captain Henry Bostock was captured near the island of Saint Christopher. He later described Thatch as a tall, spare man with a very black beard, which he wore very long. Other witnesses said that Blackbeard treated his prisoners reasonably and often released them with their ships. Historians note that there is no reliable record of Blackbeard killing anyone before his final battle in 1718. He seems to have relied mainly on fear and reputation rather than violence. Most ships surrendered without a fight when they saw his flag and his large crew. Even so, captured sailors lost their cargoes and sometimes their ships. Some were forced to join the pirate crew. The image of Blackbeard as a monster comes mainly from later writers, while the records show a cautious and calculating leader.

[long-pause]

{img:19-queen-annes-revenge-ship-full-sail-forty-guns-blue-sea}

In November 1717, Blackbeard captured a large French ship and made it his flagship. On the twenty-eighth of November 1717, near the island of Martinique, Blackbeard and his men captured a French ship called La Concorde. It was a slave ship, carrying several hundred enslaved Africans from West Africa. Blackbeard took the ship to the island of Bequia in the Grenadines. There, he gave the French crew a smaller vessel so they could return home. Many of the Africans were left on the island, though some joined or were taken by the pirates. Historians know about this capture from the testimony of the French officers, which survives in French archives. Blackbeard fitted the ship with around forty cannons and renamed it the Queen Anne's Revenge. It became one of the most powerful pirate ships in the Americas. Even so, the large ship had disadvantages. It needed a big crew and deep water. In the end, its size would contribute to its loss on the coast of North Carolina.

[long-pause]

{img:20-portrait-queen-anne-old-painting-candlelight-frame}

The name Queen Anne's Revenge may have reflected support for the old royal family of Britain. Queen Anne ruled Great Britain until her death in 1714. She was succeeded by George the First, a German prince from Hanover. Some people in Britain did not accept the new king. They supported the exiled Stuart family, the descendants of Anne's half-brother, and were known as Jacobites. Historians have suggested that the name Queen Anne's Revenge may express Jacobite sympathies. Some pirates were known to drink toasts to the Stuart claimant. Even so, this interpretation is uncertain. The name might also have been simply defiant, a reference to the war fought in Anne's name. On board the ship were around three hundred men at times. This was far more than a merchant ship of the same size would carry, which might have needed only twenty or so. Pirates needed large crews to sail captured ships, fire many guns and overwhelm their victims quickly. Large crews also meant that loot had to be divided among many people.

[long-pause]

{img:21-small-pirate-fleet-four-ships-sailing-together-sunset}

During the winter of 1717 and 1718, Blackbeard sailed through the Caribbean with a small fleet. By early 1718, Blackbeard commanded several vessels, including the Queen Anne's Revenge and a number of sloops. Together, they formed one of the strongest pirate forces in the Americas. During the winter, the fleet sailed among the islands of the eastern Caribbean. Records show captures near Saint Vincent, Saint Lucia, Nevis, Antigua and the Virgin Islands, and later near Puerto Rico and Hispaniola. Blackbeard took food, supplies, weapons and sometimes new crew members from the ships he captured. Historians piece together these movements from the reports of captured captains and colonial governors, who wrote to London to describe the threat. Even so, the exact route is not always clear, because reports were sometimes confused or delayed. In the spring of 1718, the fleet sailed north toward the coast of the American colonies. This route avoided the coming hurricane season and brought the pirates close to busy trade routes.

[long-pause]

{img:22-navigator-backstaff-measuring-sun-deck-noon-tropics}

Navigators in 1718 found their position using the sun, simple instruments and careful calculation. In the early eighteenth century, sailors had no engines, radios or satellites. They depended on wind and on their skill in navigation. To find latitude, their position north or south, navigators measured the height of the sun at noon or of the Pole Star at night. They used instruments such as the backstaff, also called the Davis quadrant, which allowed them to measure the sun's angle without looking directly at it. Using tables, they could then calculate their latitude quite accurately. Longitude, the position east or west, was much harder to determine. Navigators estimated it by a method called dead reckoning, using speed, direction and time. Historians know about these methods from navigation manuals and ship logs of the period. Even so, errors were common, especially on long voyages. Many ships were wrecked because sailors believed they were farther from land than they really were. Experience and knowledge of local waters were therefore extremely valuable.

[long-pause]

{img:23-sailors-throwing-log-line-knots-sandglass-stern}

Sailors measured their speed with a log line and a sandglass. To estimate speed, sailors used a device called a log line. A piece of wood, the log, was thrown into the water from the stern. It was attached to a long line with knots tied at regular intervals. As the ship moved forward, the line ran out. A sailor counted how many knots passed through his hands while a small sandglass emptied, usually in about thirty seconds. The number of knots showed the ship's speed. This is the origin of the word knot as a unit of speed at sea. The results were recorded in the logbook, together with direction and time. Historians use these logbooks to reconstruct voyages. Even so, dead reckoning was often inaccurate. Currents and wind could push a ship off course without anyone noticing. The longitude problem was solved only later in the century, when the English clockmaker John Harrison built accurate marine timekeepers in the 1750s and 1760s.

[long-pause]

{img:24-sailor-sounding-lead-line-bow-turquoise-shallow-water}

In shallow waters, sailors measured depth with a lead line and relied on charts and local knowledge. Close to land, the greatest danger was running aground. Sailors measured the depth of the water with a lead line, a weighted rope marked at regular intervals. A sailor at the bow threw the lead forward and called out the depth. The bottom of the lead was often filled with tallow, which picked up sand or shells, showing the type of seabed. Charts of coasts and islands also helped, though many were inaccurate. Pirates valued good charts highly and took them from captured ships. The steady trade winds of the Caribbean blew mostly from the east. This made sailing west easy and sailing east difficult. Historians note that pirates often followed seasonal patterns, using the winds and avoiding storms. Even so, much depended on the experience of pilots who knew particular waters. In the Bahamas and along the Carolina coast, local knowledge of channels and sandbars could mean the difference between escape and disaster.

[long-pause]

{img:25-calm-bay-sheltered-anchorage-palms-before-storm-season}

During the hurricane season, many pirates left the Caribbean and sailed north to the American coast. Hurricanes were the greatest natural danger for ships in the Caribbean. They usually came between July and October. Experienced sailors watched for warning signs, such as a long swell from an unusual direction, strange skies and a falling barometer, though barometers were rare on ships at the time. When a storm threatened, ships tried to reach a sheltered harbour and anchored securely. Historians find many records of ships lost in hurricanes, including the Spanish treasure fleet of 1715. For this reason, pirates often changed their hunting grounds with the seasons. In summer, many sailed north along the coast of the American colonies, from the Carolinas to New England. There, they could find merchant ships and avoid the worst storms. Even so, the northern coasts had their own dangers. Sandbars, shoals and sudden gales made navigation difficult. In the spring of 1718, Blackbeard's fleet followed this pattern and headed toward the Carolinas.

[long-pause]

{img:26-pirate-crew-gathered-deck-listening-quartermaster-reading}

Pirate crews were often governed by written articles that the crew agreed to together. Life on a pirate ship was organised differently from life on a merchant or navy ship. On those ships, the captain held almost total authority, and sailors had no say in decisions. Many pirate crews instead agreed on a set of rules, called articles. Each man signed or made his mark on them when he joined. The articles covered the division of loot, punishments, compensation for injuries and rules about behaviour on board. Captains were usually elected by the crew and could be removed by a vote. Historians know about pirate articles because several sets were recorded in Johnson's book and in trial documents. Those of Bartholomew Roberts are among the best known. Blackbeard's own articles have not survived. Even so, historians believe his crew followed similar customs. The system gave ordinary sailors more power than they had on other ships, though discipline could still be harsh.

[long-pause]

{img:27-quartermaster-sharing-coins-crew-table-lantern-careful}

On pirate ships, loot was divided by the quartermaster according to fixed shares. When pirates captured a ship, everything of value was collected and kept together until it could be divided. The quartermaster, an officer elected by the crew, was responsible for this. He also settled disputes and handled discipline in many crews. According to surviving articles, the captain usually received two shares, officers such as the quartermaster, boatswain and gunner received one and a half or one and a quarter shares, and every ordinary pirate received one share. Some articles also promised payments to men who were injured, such as a set sum for the loss of an arm or a leg. Historians note that this system was much more equal than on merchant ships, where owners and captains took most of the profits. Even so, the actual amounts were often small. Cargoes of cloth, sugar or tools were hard to sell for good prices. Few pirates ever gained the large fortunes of popular legend.

[long-pause]

{img:28-pirate-sailors-many-backgrounds-working-rigging-together}

Pirate crews included men of many nationalities, and many crews included men of African descent. Records of captured pirate crews show great variety. Men from Britain, Ireland and the American colonies were the largest groups, but there were also French, Dutch, Scandinavian and Portuguese sailors. Many crews included men of African descent. Some were free sailors. Others had escaped slavery or had been taken from captured slave ships. On some ships, Black pirates were full members of the crew, with a vote and a share of the loot. Historians estimate that in some crews, perhaps a quarter or more of the men were of African descent. In Blackbeard's final crew, several Black men were captured and tried alongside the others. Even so, treatment varied greatly between ships. On some, Africans were treated as captives or sold. The historian Kenneth Kinkor and others have studied these records. They show that pirate ships could offer opportunities, but also that the wider world of slavery reached even into pirate crews.

[long-pause]

{img:29-pirate-ship-dawn-calm-sea-men-waking-deck-hammocks}

A day aboard a pirate ship began at dawn, much as on any other sailing ship. Daily life on a pirate ship followed many of the same routines as on other ships of the period. The crew was divided into watches, which took turns working the ship day and night. At dawn, men who had slept on deck in the warm air rolled up their blankets. Others climbed out of hammocks below decks. The decks were washed, the sails adjusted and the ship's position checked. Breakfast was simple, often biscuit and whatever food was available. Historians reconstruct these routines from sailors' accounts and the testimonies of captured men who had spent time aboard pirate ships. Some of these witnesses described daily life in detail during trials. Even so, pirate ships were often less orderly than navy ships. Crews were large, and with more men than needed for sailing, there was often plenty of free time. The work of a sailing ship still had to be done, however, whatever the crew's way of life.

[long-pause]

{img:30-lookout-masthead-telescope-distant-sail-horizon-sunny}

On most days, pirate ships saw no prey, and crews passed the time as sailors always did. A lookout stood at the masthead, scanning the horizon for sails. When a ship was sighted, the pirates might give chase. But on many days, the sea was empty. Crews then filled the hours with ordinary activities. They talked, told stories and sang. They played cards and dice, though some pirate articles forbade gambling for money on board to prevent quarrels. They repaired clothing and equipment. Historians note that boredom was a common feature of life at sea for all sailors in this period. Weeks could pass with little change. Witnesses at trials sometimes described long periods of waiting and drifting. Even so, the lookout's call could change everything in moments. A distant sail meant possible loot, but also possible danger. It might be a warship or a well-armed merchant. The captain and crew then had to decide quickly whether to pursue the ship or avoid it.

[long-pause]

{img:31-pirates-fiddle-dancing-deck-afternoon-laughing}

Pirates fished, rested, mended clothes and enjoyed music during quiet days at sea. When there was no ship to chase, pirates fished over the side, catching fish such as dolphinfish and bonito, and sometimes sharks. They rested in the shade of the sails. Music was popular. Some pirate articles mentioned musicians, who were allowed to rest on Sundays. Fiddles, drums and trumpets were played, and men sang and danced. Some pirates could read and took books from captured ships. Historians have found that Blackbeard's crew took books and papers during several captures. Pirates mostly wore ordinary sailors' clothes, such as loose shirts, short jackets, wide trousers and caps or hats. Captured fine clothes were sometimes worn ashore or on special occasions. Even so, there were no colourful uniforms. Practical clothing suited the hot climate and the work. The image of pirates in elaborate costumes comes largely from later illustrations, theatre and films, rather than from the descriptions of people who actually met them.

[long-pause]

{img:32-tropical-night-stars-milky-way-ship-sailing-glowing-wake}

At night, the watch kept the ship sailing while the rest of the crew slept. As darkness fell, lights below decks were put out early, often around eight o'clock, to reduce the risk of fire. Fire was one of the greatest dangers on a wooden ship filled with gunpowder, tar and rope. Some pirate articles specifically required candles and lamps to be extinguished at a fixed hour. The men off duty slept in hammocks or on deck. The helmsman steered, and the lookout watched for other ships, rocks or changes in weather. In warm tropical waters, the sea sometimes glowed at night with bioluminescence, as tiny organisms lit up in the ship's wake. Sailors described this light in many accounts of the period. Historians note that night sailing required great care, especially near coasts and reefs. Even so, in open water, nights could be calm and quiet. The ship moved steadily under the stars, and the sound of wind and water accompanied the sleeping crew.

[long-pause]

{img:33-ship-galley-hearth-iron-pot-cook-hardtack-barrels}

At sea, sailors lived mainly on food that kept well, such as hard biscuit and salted meat. Keeping food fresh on long voyages in a hot climate was very difficult. The main supplies on most ships were ship's biscuit, also called hardtack, and salted beef and pork stored in barrels of brine. Dried peas, oatmeal, cheese and butter were also carried. Ships had a small galley with a brick hearth, where a cook prepared meals in large iron pots. Biscuit often became infested with weevils after long storage. Water stored in casks could turn stale, so sailors drank beer, wine or rum when possible. Historians know about shipboard diets from navy records, merchant accounts and archaeological finds from wrecks. Excavations of the Queen Anne's Revenge have recovered animal bones from preserved meat. Even so, pirates had one advantage over ordinary sailors. They could take fresh food and drink from the ships they captured, and they often spent time ashore on islands where fresh food was available.

[long-pause]

{img:34-pirates-roasting-pig-beach-fire-palm-trees-evening}

On Caribbean islands, pirates hunted wild pigs and goats and gathered fruit and shellfish. Many Caribbean islands had populations of wild pigs and goats, descended from animals released by earlier European sailors. Pirates hunted them and roasted the meat over fires on the beach. They gathered coconuts, oranges, limes and other fruit. They caught fish and collected shellfish, lobsters and crabs. The tradition of roasting meat on a wooden frame came from Indigenous Caribbean peoples and was adopted by the buccaneers of the seventeenth century. The word barbecue comes from the Taíno word barbacoa. Historians note that the earlier buccaneers took their name from a word for a smoking grill. Even so, such feasts depended on time ashore, which was not always possible. Pirates had to be careful when landing on islands, as colonial authorities or hostile ships might appear. Fresh food was valuable for health. Citrus fruit helped prevent scurvy, though the cause of the disease was not yet understood at the time.

[long-pause]

{img:35-green-sea-turtles-moonlit-beach-sand-waves}

Sea turtles were an important source of fresh meat for sailors in the Caribbean. Green sea turtles came ashore on Caribbean beaches at night to lay their eggs. Sailors caught them easily on the sand. Turtles could be kept alive on board for weeks, providing fresh meat during voyages. Turtle meat and turtle soup were common foods for sailors in the region. Historians find many references to turtle hunting in ship logs and travellers' accounts. The Cayman Islands were especially known for their turtle beaches. A popular dish among sailors was salmagundi, a mixture of chopped meat, fish, eggs, vegetables and pickled foods, seasoned with oil, vinegar and spices. It is often described as a favourite pirate meal, though the dish was also eaten in England. Even so, heavy hunting greatly reduced turtle populations over the centuries. Today, sea turtles are protected throughout the Caribbean. Conservation groups monitor nesting beaches, and many of the islands where pirates once hunted turtles now help protect them.

[long-pause]

{img:36-limes-oranges-basket-ship-deck-tropical-island-near}

Pirates drank rum, punch, wine and sometimes chocolate taken from captured ships. Rum was produced on the sugar islands of the Caribbean and was cheap and widely available. Sailors often mixed it with water, sugar, lime juice and spices to make punch. Wine and brandy were taken from captured European ships. Cocoa beans from Spanish cargoes could be ground and mixed with hot water, sugar and spices to make chocolate, which was a popular drink in this period. Tea and coffee were also becoming fashionable in Europe. Historians note that drinking was a major part of pirate life ashore. Some reports describe long celebrations after successful captures. Even so, drunkenness at sea could be dangerous. Some pirate articles limited drinking on board, and captains knew that a drunken crew might lose a fight or wreck the ship. Records of trials sometimes mention pirates who were captured because they were drunk when navy ships attacked. Moderation was not common, but it was sometimes enforced.

[long-pause]

{img:37-blackbeard-deck-dramatic-black-beard-pistols-sash-calm-sea}

Johnson's book described Blackbeard as wearing ribbons in his beard and burning fuses under his hat. The most famous description of Blackbeard comes from Johnson's book of 1724. According to Johnson, Blackbeard's beard was deep black and very long, covering his face up to his eyes. He twisted it into small tails tied with ribbons. In battle, Johnson wrote, he carried several pistols in holsters across his chest and stuck lit slow matches, burning fuses used to fire cannons, under his hat, so that smoke surrounded his face. This image has shaped every later picture of Blackbeard. Historians note that some details may be true, while others may be exaggerated. The account was written after his death, partly to entertain readers. Even so, it fits what is known of Blackbeard's methods. He seems to have understood that a frightening appearance could make ships surrender without a fight. A reputation for terror saved time, ammunition and lives, including the lives of his own crew.

[long-pause]

{img:38-ship-hatch-smoke-drifting-out-sailors-coughing-laughing}

Many stories about Blackbeard, such as his fourteen wives, cannot be confirmed. Johnson's book included several colourful stories about Blackbeard. It claimed that he had fourteen wives, with one in every port. It also told how he once shut himself and some of his men below decks with burning pots of sulphur, to see who could endure the smoke the longest. Historians have found no evidence for these stories. They may be inventions or exaggerations meant to make Blackbeard seem wild and devilish. There is evidence that Blackbeard married a young woman in North Carolina in 1718, according to later reports, though even this is uncertain. Johnson also reported that before his final battle, Blackbeard was asked whether his wife knew where his money was hidden. He supposedly answered that only he and the devil knew. Even so, such stories reveal more about how people imagined pirates than about the real man. They helped turn Blackbeard into a legend within a few years of his death.

[long-pause]

{img:39-empty-beach-palm-trees-moonlight-legend-of-treasure}

Historians believe Blackbeard never buried treasure, and no treasure of his has ever been found. Ever since the eighteenth century, people have searched for Blackbeard's treasure on islands and beaches from the Caribbean to the Carolinas. Nothing has ever been found. Historians think there probably was never any buried treasure. Pirates usually spent their money quickly on food, drink and pleasure. Most loot consisted of goods rather than gold and silver. Burying it would have made little sense. The idea of buried pirate treasure became popular mainly through later fiction. The famous pirate flag associated with Blackbeard, showing a horned skeleton holding a spear beside a red heart, also has no confirmed link to him. It appears to have been attributed to him in the twentieth century. Even so, pirate flags were real. Many pirates flew black flags, sometimes with skulls, skeletons or hourglasses. A black flag meant that the crew would show mercy if the ship surrendered. A red flag warned that no mercy would be given.

[long-pause]

{img:40-merchant-captain-handing-over-ship-papers-pirate-politely}

Blackbeard relied on reputation and intimidation more than on violence. Witnesses who met Blackbeard at sea rarely described brutal violence. Most ships he attacked surrendered quickly. Their captains handed over their papers, cargo and valuables, and were usually released afterwards. Historians such as Angus Konstam have argued that Blackbeard deliberately built a frightening image to make his work easier. A large crew, a powerful ship and a fearsome appearance encouraged ships to surrender without resistance. Fighting was dangerous for pirates too, since injuries could be fatal without proper medical care. Even so, intimidation was itself a form of violence. Captured sailors feared for their lives. Some ships were burned, and some men were forced to join the pirates. Blackbeard's reputation spread through newspapers and letters along the American coast. Merchants and governors feared him greatly. In this sense, he was an early example of the power of a public image. More than three centuries later, his name is still the best known of all pirates.

[long-pause]

{img:41-gentleman-planter-barbados-1717-wig-fine-coat-garden}

Stede Bonnet, a wealthy planter from Barbados, became a pirate in 1717 and sailed with Blackbeard. Stede Bonnet was born in Barbados around 1688. He inherited a large sugar plantation and lived as a wealthy gentleman, with a wife and children. He was educated and owned a library. He had no experience as a sailor. In 1717, for reasons that remain unclear, he decided to become a pirate. Johnson suggested that he was unhappy in his marriage, but historians are cautious about this explanation. Some have suggested that he suffered from a mental illness. Bonnet's lack of experience soon caused problems. His crew had little respect for his leadership. In late 1717, he met Blackbeard, who took control of Bonnet's ship and kept Bonnet aboard the Queen Anne's Revenge as a sort of guest. Historians know about Bonnet from his trial records, which are unusually detailed. Even so, many aspects of his motives remain mysterious. He is often called the gentleman pirate because of his unusual background.

[long-pause]

{img:42-sloop-being-fitted-barbados-harbour-cannons-loaded}

Stede Bonnet bought his own ship and paid his crew wages, unlike most pirates. Most pirates captured their ships. Stede Bonnet bought his. He purchased a sloop, armed it with about ten guns and hired a crew of around seventy men. He named the ship the Revenge. Unusually, he paid his crew regular wages rather than sharing loot according to pirate custom. One night in 1717, he left Barbados without telling his family and began attacking ships along the American coast. Historians note that this behaviour was very unusual. Bonnet seems to have been attracted by the idea of piracy rather than driven by poverty. He captured several ships off Virginia, New York and the Carolinas. Even so, his lack of sailing skill made him dependent on his crew. When he joined Blackbeard, the more experienced pirate quickly took command. Bonnet's crew seems to have accepted the change willingly, since Blackbeard was a far more capable leader. Bonnet remained aboard, reportedly reading books and walking the deck.

[long-pause]

{img:43-charleston-1718-town-harbour-wooden-houses-church-spire}

Stede Bonnet was captured in 1718 and hanged in Charleston in December of that year. After leaving Blackbeard, Stede Bonnet briefly accepted a royal pardon but soon returned to piracy under a false name. In September 1718, he was cornered in the Cape Fear River in North Carolina by an expedition sent from South Carolina under Colonel William Rhett. After a long battle, Bonnet and his crew surrendered. He was taken to Charleston for trial. Before the trial, he escaped, reportedly disguised as a woman, but he was recaptured. Most of his crew were hanged in November. Bonnet wrote an emotional letter to the governor asking for mercy, but he was executed on the tenth of December 1718. Historians use the detailed trial records to study his life. Even so, his story remains puzzling. He was a wealthy man who gave up comfort for a dangerous life he was poorly prepared for. His case attracted great attention at the time and was widely reported in colonial newspapers.

[long-pause]

{img:44-white-point-garden-oaks-harbour-benches-quiet-afternoon}

Stede Bonnet's story has been retold many times, including in a recent television comedy. Bonnet was hanged near the waterfront of Charleston, probably close to the area now known as White Point Garden. Today, this is a quiet public park with large oak trees, benches and views over the harbour. A small marker recalls the pirates who were executed nearby in 1718. Historians note that Charleston played a major role in the fight against pirates. Its merchants suffered greatly from pirate attacks, and its authorities organised expeditions against them. Bonnet's story has fascinated writers ever since. In 2022, it inspired a television comedy, which imagined him as a kind and gentle man who became friends with Blackbeard. Historians enjoyed the attention but pointed out that the show was fiction. Even so, the real story contained some of the same elements: an unlikely pirate, a famous partnership and a sad ending. Bonnet's life shows that the world of piracy attracted people from very different backgrounds.

[long-pause]

{img:45-ships-surgeon-1718-wooden-medicine-chest-bottles-jars}

Sailors in the tropics suffered from many diseases, and medicine was precious on pirate ships. Staying healthy at sea in the early eighteenth century was very difficult, especially in tropical waters. Sailors suffered from scurvy on long voyages, caused by a lack of vitamin C, though this was not understood at the time. Fevers such as yellow fever and malaria were common in the Caribbean. Dysentery spread easily in crowded ships with poor sanitation. Injuries from accidents and fighting were frequent. Sexually transmitted diseases, especially syphilis, were also widespread among sailors. Historians know about shipboard health from medical manuals, ship records and the writings of naval surgeons. Most ships carried a medicine chest, and larger ships had a surgeon. Even so, medical knowledge was limited. Many treatments were ineffective or harmful. Yet some, such as cleaning wounds and setting bones, did help. For pirates, who could not visit doctors or apothecaries in towns, a good supply of medicine was particularly valuable, as the events at Charleston in 1718 would show.

[long-pause]

{img:46-surgeon-bandaging-sailors-arm-cabin-lantern-kindly}

Pirates often forced ship's surgeons from captured vessels to join their crews. Surgeons were among the most valued members of any crew. Pirates frequently captured surgeons from merchant ships and forced them to serve on board. Trial records show that several surgeons later claimed in court that they had been taken against their will, which sometimes saved them from hanging. Pirates generally treated surgeons well and paid them a good share. A surgeon's work included treating wounds, setting broken bones, amputating damaged limbs and treating fevers. Historians note that a surgeon's skills could greatly improve a crew's chances of survival. Even so, operations were performed without anaesthetic and in difficult conditions. Many patients died from infection. The surgeon's chest contained herbs, roots, powders, pills and ointments. One important medicine was Peruvian bark, from the cinchona tree of South America. It contained quinine, which helped treat malaria, although its active ingredient was not identified until the nineteenth century. Mercury compounds were used to treat syphilis.

[long-pause]

{img:47-apothecary-jars-labels-latin-shelves-mortar-pestle}

Archaeologists have found medical instruments and traces of medicines on the wreck of the Queen Anne's Revenge. Surgeons' tools in the early eighteenth century included knives, saws, probes, forceps and syringes. A brass bowl was used for bleeding patients, a common treatment believed to restore the balance of the body. Medicines were kept in glass bottles, pottery jars and wooden boxes, often labelled in Latin. Apothecaries in towns prepared them using mortars and pestles. Archaeologists excavating the wreck of the Queen Anne's Revenge have found medical equipment, including a brass syringe of the type used to treat syphilis with mercury. Traces of mercury were also detected. Historians link these finds to the importance of medicine for Blackbeard's crew. Pirates could not buy medicines openly in towns without risking arrest. They had to take them from captured ships or obtain them in other ways. Even so, medicine chests were not always found on captured ships. This shortage may explain one of Blackbeard's most famous actions, the blockade of Charleston in May 1718.

[long-pause]

{img:48-pirate-sloop-chasing-merchant-ship-full-sail-sunny-sea}

Most pirate captures involved a chase and a threat rather than a fierce battle. Popular films often show pirate attacks as dramatic battles, with cannon fire and sword fights. Historical records suggest that most captures were much quieter. Pirates first had to find a suitable ship. They often waited near busy routes, headlands or harbour entrances. When a sail was spotted, the pirates approached, sometimes flying a friendly flag to avoid alarming their target. Their sloops were fast and could usually catch heavy merchant ships. Historians have studied many accounts from captured captains. Most described being overtaken and threatened rather than attacked with heavy fire. Even so, some ships did resist, and fights could be deadly. Pirates preferred to avoid fighting because it risked damage to the ship they wanted to capture and injury to their own crew. Speed, surprise and intimidation were their most important weapons. Large crews made a strong impression as soon as the pirates came close.

[long-pause]

{img:49-black-flag-rising-mast-merchant-crew-watching-surprised}

When pirates came close, they raised their flag and fired a warning shot to demand surrender. Once close enough, pirates raised their black flag to reveal who they were. They often fired a single warning shot across the bow of the target ship. Then the crew crowded onto the deck, shouting, waving weapons and making as much noise as possible with drums and trumpets. The purpose was to frighten the merchant crew into surrendering quickly. Merchant ships usually had small crews, often fewer than twenty men, and few guns. Sailors were not paid to fight, and the cargo usually did not belong to them. Historians note that most merchant crews chose to surrender rather than risk their lives. Records show that pirates generally treated crews that surrendered quickly better than those that resisted. Even so, the moment of surrender was frightening. Merchant sailors knew stories of pirate cruelty and could not be sure how they would be treated. The black flag was meant to make that fear work in the pirates' favour.

[long-pause]

{img:50-merchant-sailors-lowering-sails-calm-sea-giving-up}

After a ship surrendered, pirates boarded it and searched it for valuables and supplies. A surrendering ship lowered its sails and its flag. The pirates then rowed across in boats and climbed aboard. They gathered the crew on deck and questioned the captain about the cargo and any money on board. They searched the ship thoroughly, from the captain's cabin to the hold. They took money, gold and silver if any was found, and also cloth, food, drink, tools, weapons, sails, rope, medicine and navigational instruments. Historians note that ordinary supplies were often more important to pirates than treasure. A pirate ship needed constant repair and resupply. Captured captains later described these searches in testimony, listing what had been taken. Even so, behaviour varied. Some pirates were orderly and polite, while others were rough and threatening. Captains were sometimes pressured to reveal hidden valuables. Pirates also looked for skilled sailors, such as carpenters, navigators and surgeons, whom they might force to join their crew.

[long-pause]

{img:51-pirates-talking-captured-sailors-deck-friendly-invitation}

Pirates often invited captured sailors to join them, and some accepted willingly. After taking what they wanted, pirates usually released the captured ship with its captain and remaining crew. Sometimes they kept a good ship for their own use, or burned it to prevent it from raising the alarm. Pirates also invited captured sailors to join them. Some sailors accepted, attracted by the share system and the freedom of pirate life. Others were forced to join, especially if they had useful skills. Historians note that forced men often asked for written certificates stating that they had been taken against their will, in case they were later captured and tried. Courts sometimes accepted such evidence. Even so, it could be difficult to prove. For most people who met Blackbeard at sea, the experience was frightening and costly but not deadly. They lost cargo and sometimes their ship, but kept their lives. Their accounts of these meetings are among the most valuable sources historians have about pirate life.

[long-pause]

{img:52-charleston-harbour-1718-ships-sandbars-low-town-spires}

In May 1718, Blackbeard blockaded the harbour of Charleston, South Carolina. In May 1718, Blackbeard sailed north from the Caribbean with a fleet of four ships and around four hundred men. He anchored at the entrance to the harbour of Charleston, then called Charles Town. It was one of the wealthiest towns in the American colonies, exporting rice and deerskins. For about a week, Blackbeard stopped every ship entering or leaving the harbour. He captured several vessels and took their passengers and crews as prisoners. Among them was Samuel Wragg, a member of the colony's governing council. Historians know about the blockade from letters written by the governor of South Carolina, Robert Johnson, and from later testimony. Even so, the exact number of ships captured is uncertain. The town had no warships to defend it, and its fortifications were weak. The blockade was one of the boldest actions by any pirate of the period and caused great alarm throughout the colonies.

[long-pause]

{img:53-pirate-ships-anchored-harbour-mouth-captured-vessels-near}

During the blockade, Blackbeard demanded a chest of medicines from the governor of South Carolina. Charleston was in a state of panic. People feared that the pirates might attack the town itself. Blackbeard sent a small group of his men ashore, together with one of the prisoners, with a demand for the governor. He did not ask for gold or silver. Instead, he asked for a chest of medicines. If the medicines were not delivered, the message said, the prisoners would be harmed. Historians have debated the reason for this unusual demand. Many believe that some of Blackbeard's crew were seriously ill, possibly with syphilis or tropical fevers. The traces of mercury found on the wreck of the Queen Anne's Revenge support this idea. Even so, there is no direct record explaining the demand. The governor's council considered the situation carefully. With no means of defence, they decided to agree. The delivery took several days, partly because the pirates sent ashore were delayed in town.

[long-pause]

{img:54-pirate-boat-rowing-to-town-quay-messengers-crowd}

The pirates' messengers walked openly through Charleston while the town gathered the medicines. The pirates sent ashore, including one named Richards according to some accounts, reportedly walked through the streets of Charleston while the medicine chest was being prepared. Their presence angered the townspeople, but no one could stop them. The pirates' return was delayed, and Blackbeard began to fear that his men had been captured. According to later testimony, he threatened to attack the town and harm the prisoners. Historians note that this is one of the most tense moments recorded in the story of the blockade. In the end, the messengers returned with the medicine chest, said to be worth several hundred pounds. Blackbeard released the prisoners, though he kept their valuables and some of their clothes. Even so, no one was killed. The episode shows how pirates could use the threat of violence to obtain what they needed. It also shows how vulnerable colonial towns were without naval protection.

[long-pause]

{img:55-prisoners-rowing-ashore-relieved-harbour-evening-light}

The blockade of Charleston lasted about a week and ended without loss of life. After receiving the medicine chest, Blackbeard released his prisoners and sailed north along the coast. The whole blockade lasted about a week. Although no one was harmed, the people of Charleston never forgot it. Governor Robert Johnson wrote angry letters to London asking for naval protection against pirates. The event increased pressure on British authorities to act. Historians see the blockade as a high point of Blackbeard's career. It showed his boldness and his ability to plan. Even so, it also attracted the attention of powerful enemies. Within a few months, South Carolina sent its own expedition against pirates, which captured Stede Bonnet. Today, Charleston preserves much of its historic town centre. Visitors can walk along the waterfront and through streets lined with eighteenth century houses. Plaques and museums tell the story of the pirates who once blocked the harbour, and of the town that survived their visit.

[long-pause]

{img:56-north-carolina-coast-sandy-inlet-dunes-ships-june-1718}

After Charleston, Blackbeard sailed north to the coast of North Carolina. The coast of North Carolina is very different from the Caribbean. It is lined by the Outer Banks, a chain of long, narrow sandy islands stretching for about two hundred miles. Behind them lie wide, shallow lagoons called sounds, such as Pamlico Sound and Albemarle Sound. Narrow channels, called inlets, connect the sounds with the ocean. These inlets shift constantly as storms move the sand. In 1718, the coast was sparsely settled. Historians note that the shallow waters and shifting channels made the area difficult for large ships, but suitable for small sloops that knew the way. This made the coast attractive to pirates. Few officials lived there, and the colony's government was weak. In early June 1718, Blackbeard's fleet approached an inlet called Topsail Inlet, today known as Beaufort Inlet. Even so, the shallow entrance was risky for a large ship like the Queen Anne's Revenge. What happened next changed the course of Blackbeard's career.

[long-pause]

{img:57-large-ship-aground-sandbar-inlet-tilted-calm-sea}

In June 1718, the Queen Anne's Revenge ran aground at Topsail Inlet, now Beaufort Inlet, and was lost. As the Queen Anne's Revenge entered Topsail Inlet, she struck a sandbar and stuck fast. One of the smaller ships, the sloop Adventure, commanded by Israel Hands, tried to help pull her free. The Adventure also ran aground and was wrecked. Historians have long debated whether this was an accident. Some members of the crew later claimed that Blackbeard ran the ships aground deliberately. According to this view, his fleet had become too large, with too many men expecting a share of the loot. By losing the big ship, he could reduce the crew and keep more for himself and his closest companions. Testimony from a crew member named David Herriot supports this interpretation. Even so, it is impossible to know for certain. The inlet was difficult, and accidents were common. Whatever the cause, the loss of the Queen Anne's Revenge marked the end of Blackbeard's great fleet.

[long-pause]

{img:58-sailors-unloading-boats-sandy-shore-inlet-busy}

After the wreck, Blackbeard left many of his crew behind and sailed away with the most valuable goods. After the ships ran aground, Blackbeard acted quickly. He sent Stede Bonnet to the town of Bath to ask the governor of North Carolina for a royal pardon. While Bonnet was away, Blackbeard transferred the most valuable goods from the wrecks to a smaller sloop. He then sailed off with a small group of trusted men. Many others were left stranded on a sandy island, according to later testimony. When Bonnet returned, he rescued some of the stranded men. Historians use the accounts of crew members, given during later trials, to reconstruct these events. These accounts suggest that Blackbeard deliberately broke up his crew. Even so, the testimonies were given by men trying to save their own lives, so they may not be fully reliable. The episode shows that pirate loyalty had limits. Blackbeard, like other captains, made decisions that served his own interests, even at the expense of his crew.

[long-pause]

{img:59-bath-north-carolina-1718-small-village-creek-wooden-houses}

In June 1718, Blackbeard accepted a royal pardon from the governor of North Carolina at Bath. In September 1717, King George the First issued a proclamation offering a pardon to any pirate who surrendered to a colonial governor before a set date in 1718. Pirates who accepted it would be forgiven for past crimes if they gave up piracy. Many pirates accepted, including Benjamin Hornigold. In June 1718, Blackbeard sailed to Bath, the oldest town in North Carolina, founded in 1705. There, he received a pardon from Governor Charles Eden. Bath was a small settlement on a creek off the Pamlico River, with a few dozen houses, a courthouse and a church under construction. Historians have debated the relationship between Blackbeard and Governor Eden. Some suspected that Eden and his secretary, Tobias Knight, cooperated with Blackbeard in exchange for goods. Even so, the evidence is unclear. A later investigation cleared Eden of wrongdoing, though suspicions about Knight remained. For a time, Blackbeard lived in or near Bath as a pardoned man.

[long-pause]

{img:60-creek-side-house-garden-oaks-summer-evening-calm}

Local stories about Blackbeard's house and treasure at Bath cannot be confirmed. People in Bath still tell stories about Blackbeard. Some say he lived in a house on Plum Point, a point of land near the mouth of Bath Creek. Others say tunnels connected his house to the governor's. There are also stories of hidden treasure. Historians have found no evidence to support these tales. They appear to have developed over generations of local storytelling. Even so, Blackbeard clearly spent time in the area during the summer of 1718. He did not remain peaceful for long. Within a few months, he returned to sea. Near Bermuda, he captured two French ships loaded with sugar and cocoa. He brought one of them back to North Carolina and claimed that he had found it abandoned at sea. The local court accepted this story and allowed him to keep the cargo. Historians see this as further evidence that some officials in the colony were willing to overlook his activities.

[long-pause]

{img:61-ocracoke-island-sound-side-live-oaks-sloop-anchored-calm}

Blackbeard used Ocracoke Island, on the Outer Banks, as his base in the autumn of 1718. Ocracoke is an island near the southern end of the Outer Banks. Next to it lies Ocracoke Inlet, one of the most important channels between the ocean and Pamlico Sound in the eighteenth century. Ships bound for the towns of North Carolina passed through it. From Ocracoke, Blackbeard could watch traffic in and out of the sound. He anchored his sloop in a deep channel on the sound side of the island, near the southern tip. This place is still known as Teach's Hole. In October 1718, another famous pirate, Charles Vane, visited Blackbeard at Ocracoke. Vane had refused the pardon and escaped from Nassau. According to reports, the two crews held a large gathering on the island, with drinking and celebration. Historians know of this meeting from later testimony and from alarmed reports by officials. Even so, details of the gathering are uncertain. It raised fears that Ocracoke might become a new pirate base like Nassau.

[long-pause]

{img:62-governor-spotswood-williamsburg-palace-office-maps-candles}

Governor Alexander Spotswood of Virginia secretly planned an expedition to capture Blackbeard. Alexander Spotswood was the lieutenant governor of Virginia, the neighbouring colony to the north. He was an energetic and ambitious administrator. Spotswood was alarmed by reports of pirates gathering at Ocracoke. He did not trust the government of North Carolina to deal with them, suspecting that some officials there protected Blackbeard. Legally, Spotswood had no authority to send an expedition into another colony. Even so, he decided to act. In November 1718, he secretly organised a mission to find and capture Blackbeard. He also issued a proclamation offering rewards for the capture or death of pirates. Historians know about his plans from his own letters to London, which survive in British archives. Spotswood defended his actions as necessary to protect trade. Yet his decision was controversial. It later caused disputes with the government of North Carolina. Spotswood's residence in Williamsburg, the Governor's Palace, has been reconstructed and is now part of Colonial Williamsburg.

[long-pause]

{img:63-colonial-farmhouse-north-carolina-1718-tobacco-field-pines}

In 1718, North Carolina was a poor and thinly settled colony of small farms. North Carolina was one of the least developed English colonies in America. Its population in 1718 was perhaps around twenty thousand settlers, together with enslaved Africans and Native American communities. Most colonists lived on small farms along rivers and sounds. They grew tobacco, corn and vegetables, raised pigs and cattle, and produced tar, pitch and turpentine from pine forests. Roads were few, and travel was mostly by boat. Historians note that the shallow coast and lack of good harbours made trade difficult. Much of the colony's produce passed through Virginia. Even so, land was available, and many settlers valued their independence. There were few towns, schools or churches. Courts and government were weak. Historians describe North Carolina at this time as a place where people were used to managing their own affairs and were often suspicious of authority. This environment helped make the colony a convenient place for pirates.

[long-pause]

{img:64-log-house-porch-brick-chimney-children-chickens-yard}

Ordinary settlers in North Carolina lived simple lives in log or timber houses. A typical settler family in North Carolina in 1718 lived in a small house built of logs or timber, often with a single room and a loft. A chimney of brick or clay stood at one end. Families raised chickens and pigs in the yard and grew vegetables in a garden. Life was hard and often lonely. Doctors, schools and churches were few and far apart. Historians know about daily life in the colony from court records, wills, inventories and the reports of missionaries. Some colonists traded with passing ships and did not ask too many questions about where goods came from. Pirates could sell cloth, sugar and tools cheaply. This made them welcome to some settlers, who had little access to imported goods. Even so, others feared and disliked them. Settlers knew that pirates could be dangerous. Some had suffered from raids and robberies along the coast. Attitudes toward Blackbeard in the colony were therefore mixed.

[long-pause]

{img:65-small-colonial-wharf-trading-sloop-barrels-settlers}

North Carolina had recently suffered the Tuscarora War, which left much of the colony poor and unsettled. From 1711 to 1715, the colony of North Carolina was involved in a bitter conflict with the Tuscarora people. The Tuscarora were a powerful Native nation whose lands settlers had steadily occupied. In September 1711, Tuscarora warriors attacked settlements along the rivers, killing many colonists. The colony requested help from South Carolina and Virginia. Expeditions supported by Native allies defeated the Tuscarora in the following years. Many Tuscarora were killed or enslaved, and many others moved north to join the Haudenosaunee, or Iroquois Confederacy, in New York. Historians note that the war left North Carolina weakened, with damaged farms and a struggling economy. Even so, by 1718 settlement was expanding again. The colony's weakness helped explain why it had little power to stop pirates. Trade with pirates offered some colonists goods they could not otherwise afford, which made enforcement even more difficult for the authorities.

[long-pause]

{img:66-outer-banks-fisherman-small-boat-nets-sound-sunrise}

People living on the Outer Banks fished, raised livestock and watched ships passing along the coast. In the early eighteenth century, only a few people lived on the Outer Banks. They fished in the sounds, raised cattle and horses on the islands and salvaged goods from shipwrecks. They knew the channels and sandbars well. Some acted as pilots, guiding ships through the dangerous inlets. Historians note that wrecks were frequent along this coast, which later became known as the Graveyard of the Atlantic. Islanders saw many ships pass. In the summer and autumn of 1718, among them was a sloop commanded by a captain with a long black beard. Local traditions about Blackbeard developed among these communities and were passed down through generations. Even so, few written records survive from islanders of this period. Most of what historians know comes from colonial officials, court records and later accounts. The Outer Banks remained a remote and quiet place for many generations, shaped by the sea and by isolation.

[long-pause]

{img:67-navy-warships-entering-nassau-harbour-july-1718-flags}

In July 1718, Woodes Rogers arrived in Nassau as the new governor of the Bahamas. British authorities decided to restore control over the Bahamas. They appointed Woodes Rogers as governor. Rogers was a former privateer captain from Bristol. Between 1708 and 1711, he had led a famous voyage around the world, during which his crew rescued Alexander Selkirk, a castaway whose story later inspired the novel Robinson Crusoe. In July 1718, Rogers arrived at Nassau with warships, soldiers and settlers. He announced the king's pardon to the pirates. Many accepted it. One pirate captain, Charles Vane, refused. He set a captured ship on fire and sent it toward the warships, then escaped through another channel. Historians know about these events from Rogers' own reports and other accounts. Even so, the pardon did not end piracy immediately. Some pardoned men soon returned to their old ways. Rogers faced a difficult task, governing a lawless town with few resources and many former pirates.

[long-pause]

{img:68-sloop-escaping-harbour-dawn-warships-behind-calm-water}

Woodes Rogers gradually restored order in Nassau and made it a working colony again. After his arrival, Woodes Rogers repaired the fort, organised a militia and encouraged settlers to clear land and plant crops. The work was difficult. Many of the newly arrived settlers fell ill with fevers, and some died. Supplies were short, and help from Britain was limited. Some pardoned pirates returned to piracy. In December 1718, Rogers held a trial of pirates who had broken the pardon, and eight were hanged in Nassau. Historians see this as a turning point in ending the pirate republic. Benjamin Hornigold, Blackbeard's former captain, helped Rogers by hunting down pirates. Even so, Rogers struggled financially and was later imprisoned for debt in England. He returned to the Bahamas as governor in 1729 and died there in 1732. The motto of the Bahamas for many years, Expulsis Piratis, Restituta Commercia, meaning pirates expelled, commerce restored, reflects his achievement.

[long-pause]

{img:69-colonial-fort-nassau-repair-workers-stone-sunshine}

By the autumn of 1718, the golden age of piracy was beginning to decline. The pirates had lost their main base at Nassau. The royal pardon had persuaded many to give up piracy. The Royal Navy was sending more warships to American waters. Colonies were organising their own expeditions against pirates. Historians note that between 1716 and 1726, hundreds of pirates were hanged in the Atlantic world. Several famous pirates met their end around this time. Samuel Bellamy, known as Black Sam, had drowned in April 1717 when his ship, the Whydah, was wrecked off Cape Cod in a storm. The wreck of the Whydah was discovered in 1984 and is the first confirmed pirate shipwreck ever found. Charles Vane was later captured and hanged in Jamaica in 1721. John Rackham, known as Calico Jack, was captured in 1720 with the famous women pirates Anne Bonny and Mary Read. Even so, piracy did not disappear at once. It continued in decreasing numbers until the late 1720s.

[long-pause]

{img:70-two-small-sloops-sailing-calm-sound-november-grey-dawn}

In November 1718, two small sloops sailed from Virginia toward Ocracoke under Lieutenant Robert Maynard. Governor Spotswood hired two small sloops, named the Jane and the Ranger, for his expedition. Large navy warships could not enter the shallow waters of the sounds, so smaller vessels were needed. The sloops carried no cannons. On board were about sixty sailors from two Royal Navy ships, HMS Pearl and HMS Lyme, which were stationed in Virginia. They were commanded by Lieutenant Robert Maynard of HMS Pearl. At the same time, Spotswood sent a force overland toward Bath. Historians know about the expedition from Maynard's own report and Spotswood's letters. The sloops reached Ocracoke on the evening of the twenty-first of November 1718. They found Blackbeard's sloop, the Adventure, anchored near the southern end of the island. Blackbeard had only about twenty men with him at the time. Even so, he was experienced and well armed. Maynard decided to wait until morning before approaching through the shallow channels.

[long-pause]

{img:71-evening-sound-anchored-sloops-lanterns-quiet-island-trees}

On the twenty-second of November 1718, Blackbeard was killed in a short battle at Ocracoke. Maynard did not attack at night because the channels were narrow and full of sandbars that he did not know. The two sides waited through the night, a short distance apart. At dawn on the twenty-second of November, Maynard's sloops moved toward Blackbeard's ship. Both of Maynard's sloops ran aground on sandbars as they approached. Blackbeard's crew fired their cannons, causing many casualties. Maynard then ordered most of his men to hide below deck. When Blackbeard's men boarded Maynard's sloop, the hidden sailors rushed up to fight. In a fierce but short struggle, Blackbeard was killed. According to Maynard's report, he had received many wounds. Several other pirates died, and the rest surrendered. Historians rely mainly on Maynard's report and on later accounts, which differ in some details. Even so, the main events are clear. The most famous pirate of his age died not in a great sea battle, but in a brief fight in a quiet sound.

[long-pause]

{img:72-ocracoke-sound-calm-morning-after-gulls-low-light}

Blackbeard's surviving crew were tried in Williamsburg, Virginia, in March 1719. After the battle, Maynard sailed to Bath and then back to Virginia with his prisoners. The captured pirates, together with others arrested in North Carolina, were taken to Williamsburg, the capital of Virginia. Their trial took place in March 1719. Most were found guilty and hanged. One man, Samuel Odell, was acquitted. He had been a trader who happened to be aboard Blackbeard's sloop the night before the battle. Israel Hands, Blackbeard's former sailing master, had not been present at Ocracoke. He gave evidence against others, received a pardon and, according to Johnson, later lived as a beggar in London. Historians use the surviving trial records and Spotswood's letters to understand these events. Even so, the full records of the trial have not survived. The case also led to a long dispute between Virginia and North Carolina, because Spotswood had acted in another colony without permission.

[long-pause]

{img:73-quiet-colonial-church-graveyard-oaks-bath-autumn}

Blackbeard was a pirate for only about two years, yet he became the most famous pirate in history. Blackbeard's career as a pirate lasted roughly from 1716 to 1718. In that short time, he captured dozens of ships, blockaded Charleston and commanded one of the most powerful pirate fleets ever seen in the Americas. Historians note that his fame owed much to his deliberate use of reputation and appearance, and to Johnson's book, which made him the central character of pirate history. In Bath, the Church of Saint Thomas, begun in 1734, is the oldest existing church in North Carolina. It stands among old oaks, close to the waters Blackbeard once sailed. The town remains small and quiet, with historic houses and gardens. Even so, Blackbeard's story was quickly turned into legend. Within a few years of his death, he had become a symbol of pirate life. His real life, as recorded in documents, is less dramatic than the legend, but in many ways more interesting and more human.

[long-pause]

{img:74-old-book-general-history-pyrates-1724-engraving-pages}

The golden age of piracy ended in the 1720s, after navies and colonies acted against pirates. After Blackbeard's death, piracy continued for several more years, but on a smaller scale. Navies of Britain, France and Spain sent more warships to American waters. Colonies passed stricter laws and held more trials. Bartholomew Roberts, perhaps the most successful pirate of the period, captured hundreds of ships before he was killed in battle off West Africa in 1722. After his death, more than fifty of his crew were hanged. Historians generally date the end of the golden age of piracy to about 1726. In 1724, Johnson's book was published in London. It became very popular and was reprinted and translated many times. It shaped how people imagined pirates for centuries. Even so, historians note that Johnson mixed fact with fiction. Some chapters, such as those about certain pirates, may be largely invented. Yet the book remains an important source, because it preserved information from trials and newspapers that has otherwise been lost.

[long-pause]

{img:75-one-legged-sailor-parrot-shoulder-ship-galley-friendly}

Robert Louis Stevenson's novel Treasure Island, published in 1883, created many of the most familiar pirate images. Many popular ideas about pirates come from a single novel. Treasure Island was written by the Scottish author Robert Louis Stevenson and published as a book in 1883. It tells the story of a boy, Jim Hawkins, who joins a voyage to find buried treasure. The book introduced or popularised treasure maps marked with an X, buried treasure on islands, a parrot that cries pieces of eight, and the one-legged pirate Long John Silver. It also included the song about fifteen men on the dead man's chest. Stevenson used real history in places. He mentioned Blackbeard and named one of his characters Israel Hands, after Blackbeard's real sailing master. Historians note that the novel drew on Johnson's book. Even so, most of the story was invented. Treasure Island became one of the most famous adventure stories in the world. It has inspired countless films, plays and books, and continues to shape how pirates are imagined today.

[long-pause]

{img:76-theatre-stage-pirate-ship-painted-scenery-children-audience}

Walking the plank and buried treasure were very rare or unknown among real pirates. Pirates appear in many plays, films and books, often with sword fights, treasure and dramatic punishments. Walking the plank is one of the best-known pirate images. Historians have found almost no evidence that pirates of Blackbeard's time made prisoners walk the plank. A few later reports exist, mainly from the nineteenth century, but the practice was very rare. Buried treasure is also mostly a myth. One famous exception is the privateer William Kidd, who buried some valuables on Gardiners Island, near New York, in 1699, before he was arrested. The treasure was recovered by the authorities soon afterwards. Historians note that Kidd's story helped create the legend of buried pirate gold. Even so, most pirates had no reason to bury anything. Their loot was usually goods, not coins, and they spent what they gained quickly. Popular entertainment has made pirates more colourful and dramatic than the records suggest.

[long-pause]

{img:77-pirate-ship-sunset-calm-sea-crew-silhouettes-gentle}

Most real pirates did not become rich and did not live long. Historians who study trial records and shipping documents have found that the lives of most pirates were short and difficult. Many died of disease, drowned in wrecks or were killed in fights. Hundreds were hanged. Few managed to retire with their gains. Most of what they captured was spent quickly. Historians such as Marcus Rediker estimate that between about 1716 and 1726, several thousand men took part in piracy in the Atlantic. Some accepted pardons and returned to ordinary lives as sailors or settlers. Their later lives are mostly unknown. Even so, for a few years, pirates challenged the power of empires and the trade on which those empires depended. Their stories were told in newspapers, trials and books, and later in novels and films. Unlike Blackbeard, most of them were soon forgotten. Their names survive only in a few documents, quietly preserved in archives on both sides of the Atlantic.

[long-pause]

{img:78-research-boat-beaufort-inlet-divers-calm-sea-1996}

The wreck of the Queen Anne's Revenge was discovered in Beaufort Inlet in 1996. For almost three hundred years, nobody knew exactly where the Queen Anne's Revenge lay. People knew that she had run aground at Topsail Inlet, now called Beaufort Inlet, in North Carolina. But the inlet had changed many times as storms moved the sand. In November 1996, a team from a private research company, Intersal, found a wreck in about twenty feet of water near the inlet. They found cannons, anchors and other objects. Over the following years, archaeologists from the state of North Carolina studied the site. In 2011, after careful research, the state officially confirmed that the wreck was the Queen Anne's Revenge. Historians and archaeologists consider it one of the most important pirate shipwrecks ever found. Even so, the identification depended on careful comparison of finds with historical records. Among the clues were the large number of cannons, French objects and a bell dated 1709, all consistent with Blackbeard's ship.

[long-pause]

{img:79-conservation-lab-cannon-concretion-tank-water}

Objects from the wreck are slowly cleaned and conserved in a laboratory in North Carolina. When objects lie on the seabed for centuries, they become covered with a hard crust of sand, shells and rust, called concretion. Large lumps of concretion often contain smaller objects hidden inside. Archaeologists recover these lumps and bring them to the conservation laboratory of the Queen Anne's Revenge project in Greenville, North Carolina. There, specialists carefully remove the crust, using X-rays to see what lies inside. Iron objects such as cannons must be soaked in special solutions for years to remove salt, which would otherwise destroy them. Historians note that this work is slow and expensive. More than thirty cannons have been recovered so far, together with anchors and hundreds of thousands of smaller objects. Even so, the work continues, and parts of the wreck still remain on the seabed. Each object adds a small detail to the picture of life aboard the ship, from weapons to cooking utensils and personal belongings.

[long-pause]

{img:80-museum-display-ship-bell-anchor-cannon-soft-light}

Finds from the Queen Anne's Revenge include gold dust, pewter plates, medical instruments and navigation tools. The wreck has produced a remarkable variety of objects. Archaeologists have found small amounts of gold dust and a few gold coins, possibly from West Africa. They have found pewter plates and cups, glass bottles, wine glasses and pottery. Medical items include the brass syringe and other instruments. Navigation tools include parts of instruments, sounding leads and weights. There are cannonballs, lead shot, pieces of weapons and the ship's bell. Many of these finds are displayed at the North Carolina Maritime Museum in Beaufort, close to where the ship was lost. Historians value these objects because they show the real material world of pirates, beyond the legends. Some items, such as French tableware, reflect the ship's earlier life as La Concorde. Even so, there is no great treasure. The finds tell the story of a working ship with a large crew. Visitors can see the bell, cannons and many smaller objects in quiet museum galleries.

[long-pause]

{img:81-ocracoke-village-harbour-lighthouse-white-boats-evening}

Today, Ocracoke is a quiet island village reached by ferry, with a lighthouse built in 1823. Ocracoke lies near the southern end of the Outer Banks of North Carolina. Most of the island is protected as part of the Cape Hatteras National Seashore. The island can be reached only by ferry or small aircraft. Ferries cross from Hatteras Island to the north and from the mainland across Pamlico Sound. The longer crossings take more than two hours. Pelicans often fly low beside the ferries, and dolphins can sometimes be seen. The small village surrounds a harbour called Silver Lake, lined with boats and houses. The white Ocracoke Lighthouse, built in 1823, is one of the oldest operating lighthouses in the United States. Historians note that the island remained isolated for most of its history, and its residents developed a distinctive local dialect. Even so, Ocracoke is now a popular destination for visitors. In the evenings, the harbour is calm, and the light from the lighthouse shines over the sound.

[long-pause]

{img:82-empty-wide-beach-dunes-sea-oats-atlantic-sunset}

The wild ponies of Ocracoke have lived on the island for centuries and are cared for by the National Park Service. Ocracoke is known for its wild ponies, small and sturdy horses that have lived on the island for centuries. Their origins are uncertain. According to tradition, they descend from Spanish horses brought ashore from shipwrecks or early expeditions in the sixteenth or seventeenth centuries. Genetic studies suggest Spanish ancestry. Historians note that horses roamed freely on the Outer Banks for generations, grazing on marsh grass. In the twentieth century, as roads and traffic increased, the ponies were moved into a large fenced pasture for their safety. Since 1959, they have been cared for by the National Park Service. Visitors can see them from a viewing platform. Beyond the village, long beaches stretch along the Atlantic, backed by dunes covered with sea oats. Even so, the island remains vulnerable to storms and rising seas. Hurricanes have flooded the village several times in recent decades, and residents work hard to protect their community.

[long-pause]

{img:83-sound-side-marsh-grass-still-water-channel-dusk}

Teach's Hole, where Blackbeard anchored, is still a channel near the southern tip of Ocracoke. On the sound side of Ocracoke, near the southern tip of the island, lies a deeper channel in the shallow water. It is called Teach's Hole, after Blackbeard, whose name was sometimes spelled Teach. According to tradition, this is where he anchored before his final battle in 1718. The area is surrounded by marsh grass and quiet water. Historians note that the channel has changed shape over three centuries, but the name has survived. In the village, a small shop and exhibition tell the story of Blackbeard and the pirates. Every year in autumn, Ocracoke holds a pirate festival with costumes, music and storytelling. Local legends say that on dark nights, a light can sometimes be seen moving over the water at Teach's Hole. Even so, such stories belong to folklore rather than history. They show how deeply the memory of Blackbeard has become part of the island's identity, more than three hundred years after his death.

[long-pause]

{img:84-ferry-crossing-calm-sound-pelicans-golden-hour}

Blackbeard's story is preserved today in archives, museums and the landscapes where he sailed. More than three hundred years after his death, Blackbeard remains the best-known pirate in history. His story can be followed through many places: Nassau in the Bahamas, Charleston in South Carolina, Bath and Ocracoke in North Carolina, and Williamsburg in Virginia. Letters, trial records and ship logs in archives in London, Paris and the American states record his actions. The wreck of the Queen Anne's Revenge has added physical evidence, from cannons to cups and coins. Historians continue to study his life, and new research still changes how he is understood. Even so, much remains uncertain, including his origins and many details of his career. The legend and the history continue to exist side by side. In the evening, ferries cross the wide, calm sounds of the Outer Banks. Pelicans fly low over the water, and the low islands lie quietly under a golden sky, as they did in the autumn of 1718.

[long-pause]

That was the world of Blackbeard: the pirate town of Nassau, the long days at sea, the turtles and the salmagundi, the medicine chest at Charleston, and a short fight in the grey dawn at Ocracoke. Most of it became legend, and a little of it lies today in a laboratory and a quiet museum.
