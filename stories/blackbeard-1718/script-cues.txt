{img:caribbean-turquoise-sea-sailing-ship-palm-island-1718}
Tonight we are going about three hundred years back, to the warm blue waters of the Caribbean Sea, in the spring of the year 1718.

[long-pause]

A great ship is sailing slowly between the green islands, with white sails, and forty cannons on her decks. She is called the Queen Anne's Revenge. And her captain is a tall, dark man with a long black beard, whom everyone along the coasts of America knows by one name: Blackbeard.

[pause]

If you like to drift away to quiet stories from long ago, you are welcome to subscribe to the channel, and to come back for the next one.

[long-pause]

Let us imagine the Caribbean, as the sailors of 1718 knew it.

[pause]

It is a great warm sea, between North America and South America, scattered with islands: large ones, like Cuba, and Hispaniola, and Jamaica, and Puerto Rico, and small ones, hundreds of them, stretching in a long curve, like a necklace, from Florida down to the coast of Venezuela. The water is clear and blue and green, and so warm that you could swim in it all year round.

[long-pause]

The islands are green and mountainous, covered with forests, and fringed with beaches of white sand, and palm trees, and coral reefs, where the water turns a pale, shining turquoise.

[pause]

There are flying fish, which leap out of the waves and glide over the water, and dolphins, and turtles, and great shoals of bright fish over the reefs. Pelicans dive into the sea. Frigate birds hang in the sky, with their long, thin wings. In the forests, there are parrots, green and red and blue, and hummingbirds, and little lizards, sunning themselves on the rocks.

[pause]

{img:sea-turtle-coral-reef-clear-water-sunlight-fish}
The weather is warm, all year round. There is a dry season, in the winter and spring, with blue skies and steady, cooling winds, the trade winds, blowing from the east. And there is a wet season, in the summer and autumn, with heavy rains, and thunderstorms, and sometimes, terrible storms, which the sailors call hurricanes, from a word of the Taíno people, who lived on the islands long ago.

[long-pause]

In 1718, the Caribbean belongs to the great powers of Europe.

[pause]

Spain owns the largest islands, Cuba and Hispaniola and Puerto Rico, and the coasts of Mexico and Central and South America around the sea, which the English sailors call the Spanish Main. England owns Jamaica, and Barbados, and many of the smaller islands. France owns Martinique and Guadeloupe, and the western part of Hispaniola. The Dutch have Curaçao, and the Danes have Saint Thomas.

[pause]

And to the north, along the east coast of North America, there are the English colonies: Virginia, and the Carolinas, and New England, and Pennsylvania, and New York, and the others, growing fast, with their busy ports and their farms and their plantations.

[long-pause]

The Caribbean is one of the richest places in the world. But the wealth comes from great suffering.

[pause]

On the islands, there are great plantations, where sugar cane is grown, and made into sugar, and molasses, and rum, which are sent back to Europe, and make the planters very rich. The work on the plantations is done by hundreds of thousands of enslaved Africans, brought across the Atlantic in slave ships, in terrible conditions, and forced to work in the fields, under the hot sun, without pay, for all their lives.

[pause]

{img:sugar-plantation-hills-mill-windmill-distance-hazy}
From the Spanish colonies, there is silver, from the great mines of Mexico and Peru, and gold, and emeralds, and cocoa, and dyes, and tobacco, carried home to Spain by great treasure fleets. And from the English colonies, there is tobacco, and rice, and timber, and furs.

[long-pause]

And all this wealth is carried across the sea, in ships.

[pause]

Hundreds of ships, every year, sail between the islands, and the colonies, and Europe, and Africa: big merchant ships, and small sloops, and fishing boats, and slave ships, and treasure ships, loaded with sugar and rum and silver and cloth and tools and wine and food.

[pause]

And where there are rich ships, sailing across lonely seas, far from help, there are pirates.

[long-pause]

For a few years, from about 1715 to about 1725, there are more pirates in the Caribbean and along the coasts of America than ever before, or since. Historians call it the golden age of piracy.

[pause]

It is the age of the most famous pirates in history: Blackbeard, and Black Sam Bellamy, and Calico Jack Rackham, and Anne Bonny and Mary Read, and Bartholomew Roberts, and Charles Vane, and Stede Bonnet.

[pause]

{img:pirate-sloop-black-flag-sunset-silhouette-calm-sea}
Their stories will be told, and retold, and changed, and made more exciting, in books and plays and films, for three hundred years. So that today, when we think of pirates, with their black flags, and their treasure, and their parrots, and their wooden legs, we are thinking, mostly, of the pirates of these few years.

[pause]

Tonight, we will try to find out what life was really like, aboard a pirate ship, with Blackbeard, in the year 1718.

[long-pause]

{img:navy-sailors-paid-off-dockside-1713-crowds-ships}
Why are there suddenly so many pirates, in these years?

[pause]

For a long time, the great powers of Europe have been at war with each other. From 1701 to 1714, there was a long war, called the War of the Spanish Succession, or in America, Queen Anne's War, after the queen of England, between England and the Dutch on one side, and France and Spain on the other.

[long-pause]

During the war, the navies needed many thousands of sailors. And the governments also gave special licences, called letters of marque, to private captains, allowing them to attack and capture the ships of the enemy, and keep most of the loot for themselves. These captains, and their ships, were called privateers.

[pause]

Privateering was a kind of legal piracy. Thousands of sailors worked on privateers, during the war, and became very good at chasing and capturing ships. And it was perfectly respectable. Many privateers became rich, and famous, and were treated as heroes at home.

[pause]

But in 1713 and 1714, the war ended. The navies sent most of their sailors home. And the privateers were not allowed to attack anyone any more.

[long-pause]

Suddenly, there were thousands of sailors, in the ports of England, and America, and the Caribbean, with no work, and no money.

[pause]

Some of them went back to work on merchant ships. But life on a merchant ship was very hard. The pay was low, and often late, or not paid at all. The food was bad. The captains could be cruel, and beat and flog the men for the smallest mistakes. And a sailor had no rights, and no way to complain.

[pause]

{img:merchant-ship-deck-sailors-hard-work-stern-captain}
So some of these sailors thought: why should we work so hard, for so little, for cruel captains? Why not take a ship, and sail for ourselves?

[long-pause]

And then, in July 1715, something happened that changed everything.

[pause]

A great Spanish treasure fleet, eleven ships, loaded with silver and gold and jewels, from Mexico and South America, was sailing home from Havana, in Cuba, to Spain, along the coast of Florida. And a hurricane struck the fleet, and drove the ships onto the reefs and the beaches of the east coast of Florida, and wrecked them.

[pause]

Hundreds of sailors were lost. And millions of silver coins were scattered across the sea floor, in the shallow water, near the beach.

[long-pause]

The Spanish set up a camp on the beach, and began to bring up the silver, with divers. But when the news reached Jamaica and the other islands, hundreds of English sailors and adventurers rushed to Florida, in small boats, to get their share.

[pause]

One of them, a Jamaican captain named Henry Jennings, attacked the Spanish camp, and carried off a great load of silver. And then, instead of going back to Jamaica, where he might have been arrested, he and his men went to a little island in the Bahamas, called New Providence, where there was a ruined English town, and a good harbour, and no government at all.

[pause]

{img:sloops-anchored-bahamas-harbour-palm-shore-sunlight}
The town was called Nassau. And soon, it became the home of the pirates.

[long-pause]

There is one more reason for the golden age of piracy.

[pause]

The English colonies in America and the Caribbean are growing fast. There are more ships than ever, and more trade, and more rich cargoes. But the Royal Navy has only a few ships in American waters, to protect them all. And the colonies are far from England, and their governors are often weak, or poor, or, sometimes, happy to buy cheap goods from the pirates, and ask no questions.

[pause]

So for a few years, the pirates can do almost as they please.

[long-pause]

Most pirates are ordinary sailors.

[pause]

They are young men, most of them in their twenties. They come from England, and Scotland, and Wales, and Ireland, and from the American colonies, and the Caribbean islands. Some are Dutch, or French, or Swedish. And many are African, or of African descent, men who have escaped from slavery, or been freed, or been sailors all their lives.

[pause]

{img:diverse-sailors-young-men-dock-caribbean-port-afternoon}
They are tough, and skilled, and used to danger and hardship. They know the sea, and the winds, and the islands. And they have decided, for one reason or another, to live outside the law, and to take what they want, from the rich ships that sail past.

[pause]

They know that, if they are caught, they will be hanged. But they say, as one pirate captain, Bartholomew Roberts, is supposed to have said: a merry life and a short one shall be my motto.

[long-pause]

{img:nassau-1716-harbour-shacks-palms-ships-ruined-fort}
Let us go to Nassau, in the year 1717.

[pause]

Nassau is a little town, on the north side of the island of New Providence, in the Bahamas. The Bahamas are a great scattering of low, flat islands, with white sand and green palms and shining blue lagoons, about two hundred miles off the coast of Florida.

[long-pause]

Nassau has a perfect harbour for pirates.

[pause]

It is sheltered by a long, low island in front of it, now called Paradise Island, which protects it from storms. The water is too shallow for the big warships of the Navy to come in, but deep enough for the small, fast sloops of the pirates. And there are two entrances, so that the pirates can always escape by one, if an enemy comes in by the other.

[pause]

From Nassau, the pirates can reach all the great shipping routes: the Florida Strait, where the Spanish treasure ships pass, and the routes between the Caribbean and the American colonies, and the routes to Europe.

[long-pause]

The town was once an English settlement. But in the wars, the French and the Spanish attacked it, and burned it, and the governor and most of the settlers left. Now, it is a ruin, with a broken-down fort, and a few hundred settlers, poor fishermen and farmers and wood-cutters, living in huts.

[pause]

And then the pirates come. By 1717, there are perhaps a thousand or more pirates living in Nassau, or coming and going, more than anywhere else in the world.

[pause]

{img:pirate-camp-beach-sailcloth-tents-cooking-fires}
Some people call it the Republic of Pirates. There is no governor, and no law, except what the pirates decide among themselves.

[long-pause]

The town is a busy, noisy, untidy place.

[pause]

The pirates live in tents made of old sails, and in huts made of driftwood and palm leaves, along the beach. There are taverns, and gambling houses, and the shacks of traders, who come from the other islands, and from the American colonies, to buy the pirates' stolen goods, cheaply, and sell them food, and rum, and powder, and shot, and clothes.

[pause]

{img:beach-tavern-palm-roof-traders-barrels-crates-lively}
There are piles of stolen goods on the beach: bales of cloth, and barrels of sugar and wine, and chests of tea, and cocoa, and indigo. And in the harbour, there are dozens of ships, captured ships, and pirate sloops, and the wrecks of ships that have been stripped and abandoned.

[long-pause]

The pirates spend their time ashore drinking, and gambling, and eating, and dancing, and singing, and quarrelling, and sleeping in the shade. They roast turtles and wild pigs on the beach. They fish, and swim, and lie in the sun.

[pause]

When their money is gone, they go back to sea, to find more.

[pause]

It is also a place where pirates can repair their ships. On the sandy beaches, they careen their ships: they haul them over onto their sides, at low tide, and scrape the barnacles and the seaweed off the bottom, and repair the planks, and cover them with tar and tallow, to protect them from the shipworms, which eat the wood. A clean ship is a fast ship. And a pirate ship must be fast.

[long-pause]

The leaders of the pirates in Nassau are a few experienced captains.

[pause]

There is Benjamin Hornigold, an older man, who was a privateer in the war, and still thinks of himself as an Englishman, and will only attack the ships of the French and the Spanish, not the English. There is Henry Jennings, who brought the silver from Florida. There is Charles Vane, fierce and quarrelsome. There is Sam Bellamy, called Black Sam, young and handsome and generous, who calls himself a Robin Hood of the sea.

[pause]

{img:pirate-captains-meeting-tavern-table-maps-rum-candle}
And with Hornigold, there is a tall young man, from England, who has been sailing with him for a year or two, and learning, and who is about to become the most famous pirate of all.

[pause]

His name is Edward Thatch. Or Teach. Or Thache. The spelling is different in every document. History will remember him as Blackbeard.

[long-pause]

{img:port-royal-jamaica-harbour-1718-ships-warehouses-quay}
The pirates do not only live in Nassau. They come and go from many ports, all around the Caribbean and the American coast, where they can sell their stolen goods, and buy food and supplies, and spend their money.

[pause]

Let us visit one of the great ports of the time, as an ordinary sailor might, coming ashore after a long voyage.

[long-pause]

The port is busy and crowded, and noisy, and hot.

[pause]

Along the waterfront, there are long wooden wharves, where ships are tied up, loading and unloading. Porters carry barrels and bales and crates on their backs, up and down the gangplanks, shouting. Carts pulled by mules and oxen rumble along the cobbled streets. Merchants stand at the doors of their warehouses, checking the cargoes, writing in their books.

[pause]

{img:wharf-porters-barrels-mule-carts-merchants-hot-sun}
There are ships from everywhere: from London and Bristol and Liverpool, from Boston and New York and Philadelphia, from Africa, from Spain and France and Holland, and little sloops from the other islands, loaded with sugar and molasses and rum and timber and cattle.

[long-pause]

Behind the waterfront, the streets of the town are lined with houses of brick and wood, two or three storeys high, with balconies, and shutters, painted in bright colours, to keep out the sun.

[pause]

There are taverns everywhere, full of sailors, drinking rum punch, and singing, and gambling, and quarrelling. There are shops selling everything: cloth, and hats, and shoes, and knives, and tobacco, and pipes, and food. There are markets, where the people of the islands sell fruit and vegetables and fish and chickens, in baskets, under the shade of awnings.

[pause]

There are churches, and a fort, with cannons pointing out to sea, and soldiers in red coats, sweating in the heat. And there is the governor's house, the finest house in the town, with a garden.

[long-pause]

Port Royal, in Jamaica, was once the most famous pirate town of all.

[pause]

Fifty years before Blackbeard, in the 1660s and 1670s, it was the home of the buccaneers, the earlier pirates and privateers, who attacked the Spanish towns of the Caribbean, led by the famous Henry Morgan. People called Port Royal the wickedest city in the world.

[pause]

{img:calm-harbour-kingston-jamaica-blue-mountains-morning}
But in 1692, a great earthquake struck Port Royal, and much of the town slid into the sea, and was lost. Most of the survivors moved across the harbour, to a new town, Kingston, which is now the capital of Jamaica. The ruins of old Port Royal are still under the water of the harbour, and divers can still see them today.

[long-pause]

In Blackbeard's time, the pirates must be more careful in the big ports, because there are governors, and soldiers, and courts.

[pause]

But there are always merchants who are willing to buy cheap goods without asking too many questions, and traders who will sail out to meet the pirates at quiet islands, and buy their loot, and sell them what they need, in return. Many fortunes in the colonies were made, quietly, in this way.

[pause]

{img:trading-sloop-meeting-ship-quiet-cove-barrels-exchanged}
And so the stolen sugar, and cloth, and silver, found its way back into the ordinary trade of the world, and into the shops and homes of people who never knew where it came from.

[long-pause]

{img:tall-man-black-beard-1717-caribbean-dock-portrait-calm}
Who was Edward Thatch?

[pause]

The truth is that we know very little about him, before he became a pirate. Most of what people believe about him comes from a book, published in London in 1724, six years after his death, called A General History of the Robberies and Murders of the most notorious Pyrates, by a Captain Charles Johnson.

[long-pause]

Nobody knows who Captain Charles Johnson really was. Some scholars think he was a journalist, or a printer, or a writer named Daniel Defoe, who wrote Robinson Crusoe. The book is very exciting, and full of wonderful stories about the pirates. Some of them are true, and come from the records of trials, and from newspapers, and from people who knew the pirates. And some of them, it seems, were made up, or improved, to make the book sell better.

[pause]

The book became a bestseller, and it has been the main source for almost every pirate story ever since.

[long-pause]

According to Johnson, Edward Thatch was born in Bristol, the great port in the west of England, from which many ships sailed to the Caribbean and America.

[pause]

Some historians today think that his family may have been well off, and educated. He could read and write, which was unusual for a sailor. Recent research suggests that his family may have moved to Jamaica, when he was young, and owned land, and even enslaved people there, and that his father may have been a sea captain. But we do not know for certain.

[pause]

{img:bristol-harbour-1700s-tall-ships-quayside-houses}
He was probably born around the year 1680. So in 1718, he would have been in his late thirties, older than most pirates.

[long-pause]

Johnson says that Thatch sailed on privateers from Jamaica, during Queen Anne's War, and was a brave and skilful sailor. But he was never given his own command.

[pause]

After the war, like so many other sailors, he found his way to Nassau. And in about 1716, he joined the crew of Benjamin Hornigold, the old privateer captain. Hornigold soon saw that Thatch was clever, and bold, and a good leader, and gave him command of a small sloop of his own, with a crew of seventy men.

[pause]

In 1717, Thatch and Hornigold sailed together, capturing ships off the coast of Cuba, and Virginia, and the Carolinas, and taking wine and flour and cloth and money.

[long-pause]

Then, in the autumn of 1717, the crew of Hornigold's ship held a vote.

[pause]

Hornigold still would not attack English ships. The crew wanted to attack any ship they could. So they voted him out as captain. Hornigold left, with a few loyal men, and sailed back to Nassau.

[pause]

And Edward Thatch went on, as captain of his own ship. From now on, he would attack ships of any nation.

[long-pause]

What was he like?

[pause]

Johnson describes him as tall, and strong, with a huge black beard, which covered his whole face, almost up to his eyes, and which he let grow very long, and twisted into little tails, tied with ribbons, and turned up over his ears. That is why he was called Blackbeard.

[pause]

{img:blackbeard-beard-braided-ribbons-tricorn-hat-ship-deck}
But the people who actually met him, and wrote about him at the time, tell us something a little different. Captains of ships that he captured describe him as a tall, spare man, with a very black beard, which he wore very long. They say that he was polite, and calm, and sometimes even kind to his prisoners. There is no record, before his very last day, of him ever harming anyone he captured.

[long-pause]

This was perhaps the secret of his success.

[pause]

Blackbeard understood that most ships would surrender without a fight, if they were frightened enough. A fight was dangerous for the pirates too. Pirates might be wounded, or lose their lives, and the ship and the cargo they wanted might be damaged. So the best pirate was not the most cruel pirate. It was the most frightening one, who made other ships give up without firing a shot.

[pause]

And so Blackbeard made himself look as frightening as possible. And he let stories about him spread, all along the coasts, so that every sailor in America knew his name, and feared it. And when they saw his flag, they gave up at once.

[long-pause]

In November 1717, near the island of Martinique, in the eastern Caribbean, Blackbeard and his men see a large ship, sailing from Africa.

[pause]

It is a French ship, called La Concorde, a big, strong ship, of about two hundred tons, built for war, and later used to carry enslaved Africans across the Atlantic, to the plantations of Martinique. It has been at sea for months, and many of the crew are ill. When the pirates come close, and fire two shots, the French captain surrenders at once.

[pause]

{img:large-french-ship-at-sea-tropical-sky-pirates-approaching}
And that ship will become Blackbeard's flagship, the Queen Anne's Revenge.

[long-pause]

{img:queen-annes-revenge-ship-full-sail-forty-guns-blue-sea}
Blackbeard takes the French ship to a little island called Bequia, in the Grenadines, south of Saint Vincent, and anchors there.

[pause]

He lets the French crew go, on shore, with one of his own smaller ships, so that they can sail home. And there were also hundreds of Africans on board, captured in their homelands and carried across the sea, in chains. Most of them are left on the island, with the French. Some of them, it seems, join the pirates.

[long-pause]

We do not know exactly what happened to these men and women. History, as so often, did not write down their names. But it reminds us that, in the Caribbean of 1718, the line between freedom and slavery ran right through every ship and every island.

[pause]

Then Blackbeard turns La Concorde into a pirate ship.

[pause]

He has her refitted, and adds more cannons, until she carries forty guns, making her one of the most powerful ships in American waters. She is about a hundred feet long, with three masts, and square sails. He gives her a new name: the Queen Anne's Revenge.

[long-pause]

Why that name?

[pause]

Queen Anne was the queen of England during the war, when many of the pirates had been privateers, fighting for England. She passed away in 1714, and the new king, George the First, came from Germany. Many people in England and in the colonies did not like him, and wished that the old royal family, the Stuarts, would come back.

[pause]

{img:portrait-queen-anne-old-painting-candlelight-frame}
So the name, Queen Anne's Revenge, may be a little message: that Blackbeard and his men are loyal to the old Queen, and her family, and not to the new German king. Many pirates felt this way.

[long-pause]

On board the Queen Anne's Revenge, there are about three hundred men.

[pause]

That is far more than a merchant ship of the same size would carry, perhaps fifteen or twenty men. But a pirate ship needs many men: to fire all the cannons at once, and to board other ships, and to sail the captured ships away, and to frighten the crews of other ships into surrendering.

[pause]

The ship is very crowded. The men sleep wherever they can find a place: in hammocks, below decks, packed close together, or on the open deck, under the stars, in the warm nights. There is no privacy at all.

[long-pause]

The Queen Anne's Revenge is not alone.

[pause]

With her, there are usually two or three smaller ships, sloops, which are faster, and can sail in shallow water, where the big ship cannot go. Blackbeard adds captured ships to his little fleet, and lets others go. By the spring of 1718, he has four ships, and about four hundred men.

[pause]

{img:small-pirate-fleet-four-ships-sailing-together-sunset}
It is the most powerful pirate fleet in the Americas.

[long-pause]

During the winter of 1717 and 1718, Blackbeard sails through the Caribbean with his fleet, from island to island.

[pause]

He captures ships off Saint Vincent, and Saint Lucia, and Nevis, and Antigua, and the Virgin Islands, and Puerto Rico, and Hispaniola. He takes their cargoes: sugar, and rum, and cloth, and silver, and gold, and tools, and food, and weapons, and powder. Sometimes, he takes the ships too, and sometimes their sailors, especially carpenters and surgeons and navigators, whose skills the pirates need.

[pause]

He burns a few ships, to frighten the others. And the governors of the islands send frightened letters to London, asking for help, and warships, to deal with the pirates.

[long-pause]

Captain Henry Bostock, of a sloop called the Margaret, whose ship was captured by Blackbeard near the island of Anguilla in December 1717, later gives a description to the governor.

[pause]

He says that the pirate captain was a tall, spare man, with a very black beard, which he wore very long. He says that Blackbeard's men took his cargo of cattle and hogs, and his books, and his instruments, and some of his men, but did not harm him, and let him go after eight hours. He says the pirates asked him much about the news, and whether there was a pardon for pirates.

[pause]

{img:merchant-captain-telling-governor-colonial-office-1718}
Because, at that very moment, a pardon is coming, from England.

[long-pause]

{img:navigator-backstaff-measuring-sun-deck-noon-tropics}
How do sailors find their way across the sea, in the year 1718?

[pause]

There is no radio, of course, and no satellites, and no engines. A ship goes where the wind takes it, and the sailors must know, every day, where they are, and which way to go, using only the sun, and the stars, and a few simple instruments, and their own skill and experience.

[long-pause]

First, there is the compass: a magnetised needle, floating in a little bowl, in a wooden box called the binnacle, beside the wheel, lit at night by a small lamp. The needle always points north, and so the helmsman can steer a steady course.

[pause]

Then there is latitude: how far north or south of the equator the ship is. This, the sailors can find quite easily, by measuring the height of the sun above the horizon, at noon, when it is at its highest, or of the Pole Star, at night.

[pause]

They use an instrument called a backstaff, or a quadrant. Standing with their backs to the sun, they line up its shadow with the horizon, and read the angle on a scale. Then, from tables in a book, they can work out the latitude.

[long-pause]

But longitude, how far east or west the ship is, is much harder.

[pause]

In 1718, there is no good way to measure it at sea. The sailors must guess, by dead reckoning: by knowing which way they have been sailing, and how fast, and for how long. To measure the speed, they throw a log, a piece of wood, tied to a long line, with knots tied in it, at regular intervals, over the stern of the ship, and count how many knots run out in half a minute, measured by a little sand glass. That is why we still measure the speed of ships in knots.

[pause]

{img:sailors-throwing-log-line-knots-sandglass-stern}
Dead reckoning is often wrong, after a long voyage. Many ships are wrecked because they think they are further from land than they really are. It will be another fifty years before an English clockmaker, John Harrison, invents a clock good enough to be taken to sea, to measure longitude properly.

[long-pause]

So the best sailors rely on their experience, and their knowledge of the sea.

[pause]

They know the colour of the water, which changes from deep blue to green, as the water becomes shallower, near land. They watch for birds, which fly out from the land in the morning, and back in the evening. They watch the clouds, which often gather over islands. They throw a lead weight on a line, to measure the depth of the water, with a little tallow in the bottom, which brings up sand or mud or shells from the bottom, and tells them where they are.

[pause]

{img:sailor-sounding-lead-line-bow-turquoise-shallow-water}
And they have charts: maps of the coasts and the islands, drawn by hand, or printed, and often wrong. Pirates value good charts very highly, and take them from every ship they capture.

[long-pause]

And there is the wind.

[pause]

In the Caribbean, the trade winds blow steadily from the east, for most of the year. So it is easy to sail west, and hard to sail east, against the wind. Ships coming from Europe sail south first, to the Canary Islands, and then west, with the trade winds, across the Atlantic, to the Caribbean. And to go home, they sail north, along the coast of America, with the Gulf Stream, a great warm current in the sea, which carries them north, before they turn east, across the Atlantic, with the westerly winds.

[pause]

The pirates know these routes very well. They know where the ships will pass, and when, and they wait for them there.

[long-pause]

And they fear the hurricanes.

[pause]

In the late summer and the autumn, from about August to October, great storms come out of the Atlantic, and sweep across the Caribbean, and up the coast of America. The sailors watch the sky, and the sea, for the signs: a long, slow swell, coming from far away, and high, thin clouds, and a strange, coppery colour in the sky, and a falling barometer, in the ships that have one.

[pause]

{img:calm-bay-sheltered-anchorage-palms-before-storm-season}
When they see the signs, they run for shelter, into a deep, sheltered harbour, among the hills, and anchor their ship with every anchor they have, and wait. That is why, in the hurricane season, many pirates leave the Caribbean, and sail north, to the coasts of the American colonies.

[long-pause]

{img:pirate-crew-gathered-deck-listening-quartermaster-reading}
Life on a pirate ship is very different from life on a merchant ship, or a navy ship, in the year 1718.

[pause]

On a merchant ship, or a navy ship, the captain is the master. His word is law. He can punish the sailors as he likes. The sailors have no say in anything. The owners of the ship take most of the profits, and the sailors are paid a small wage.

[long-pause]

On a pirate ship, the men rule themselves.

[pause]

They choose their own captain, by vote. And if they do not like him, they can vote him out, as Hornigold's men did. The captain has absolute command only during a chase, or a fight, when someone must make quick decisions. The rest of the time, he is just one of the crew, and important decisions, about where to sail, and which ships to attack, are made by everyone together, in a council, by vote.

[pause]

The captain does not even have his own cabin, all to himself, on many pirate ships. Anyone can come into it, and eat his food, and drink his wine.

[long-pause]

The most important officer, after the captain, is the quartermaster.

[pause]

He is also elected by the crew. He looks after the men's interests. He keeps order, and settles quarrels, and decides on punishments, if anyone breaks the rules. He shares out the food and the drink, fairly. And above all, he shares out the loot.

[pause]

{img:quartermaster-sharing-coins-crew-table-lantern-careful}
When a ship is captured, everything of value is brought on board, and kept together, until it can be shared. Then the quartermaster divides it, according to the rules. The captain gets two shares. The quartermaster and other officers, a share and a half. And every ordinary pirate gets one share. Compared with the merchant ships, where the owners and the captain got almost everything, this is remarkably fair.

[long-pause]

Every pirate crew has its own articles: a set of rules, agreed by everyone, and written down, which every new member of the crew must swear to obey, often with his hand on a Bible, or on an axe.

[pause]

We do not have Blackbeard's articles. But we have the articles of other pirate crews of the time, such as those of Bartholomew Roberts. They say things like this.

[pause]

Every man has a vote in affairs of moment, and an equal right to fresh food and strong drink. No man may gamble for money on board. The lights and candles must be put out at eight o'clock at night, and anyone who wants to drink after that must do so on the open deck. Every man must keep his pistols and his cutlass clean and ready. No woman or boy is allowed on board. The musicians may rest on the Sabbath day.

[long-pause]

There is even a kind of insurance. If a pirate is injured, in a fight, and loses an arm or a leg, he is given a sum of money from the common fund: perhaps eight hundred pieces of eight, which is a great deal of money. A smaller injury, a smaller sum.

[pause]

This was hundreds of years before any government or employer gave such help to injured workers.

[pause]

So, in some ways, a pirate ship was one of the freest and fairest places in the world, at that time, for an ordinary working man. Of course, the pirates were also thieves, who took by force what belonged to other people. But among themselves, they were equals.

[long-pause]

The crew of the Queen Anne's Revenge is a mixture of men from many places.

[pause]

There are Englishmen, and Scots, and Irish, and Welsh, and men from the American colonies, and Frenchmen, and Dutchmen. And there are many Africans, and men of African descent. When Blackbeard's crew was finally captured, about a third of the men were Black.

[pause]

{img:pirate-sailors-many-backgrounds-working-rigging-together}
Some of them were free sailors. Some had escaped from slavery, and found on a pirate ship a kind of freedom they could not find anywhere else. On some pirate ships, Black men were full members of the crew, with a vote, and a share, like everyone else. On others, sadly, they were treated as servants, or even sold. It was different from ship to ship, and we do not always know.

[long-pause]

Some men did not choose to be pirates.

[pause]

When the pirates captured a ship, they sometimes forced skilled men to join them: carpenters, to repair the ships, and surgeons, to look after the sick and wounded, and navigators, and coopers, who made barrels, and musicians, to play for them.

[pause]

These forced men often asked their captain to give them a certificate, to say that they had been forced, so that, if the pirates were ever caught, they could prove they were innocent. Sometimes it worked, and they were set free. Sometimes it did not.

[long-pause]

And there is the captain himself.

[pause]

Blackbeard was chosen by his men, and kept his command because they trusted him. He was clever, and experienced, and lucky, and he brought them rich prizes, and kept them safe. As long as he did that, they followed him.

[pause]

{img:blackbeard-standing-quarterdeck-wind-hair-thoughtful}
But he knew that, if he made a mistake, or if they got tired of him, they could vote him out, at any time.

[long-pause]

{img:pirate-ship-dawn-calm-sea-men-waking-deck-hammocks}
Let us spend an ordinary day on board the Queen Anne's Revenge, in the spring of 1718, sailing northward through the Caribbean, toward the coast of America.

[pause]

The day begins at dawn. The sun rises out of the sea, red and gold, and the warm wind fills the sails. The men who have been sleeping on deck wake up, and stretch, and roll up their blankets. Below decks, the others climb out of their hammocks.

[long-pause]

As on any ship, the work must be done.

[pause]

The men are divided into watches, and take turns at the work: steering, and setting the sails, and keeping a lookout from the masthead, and pumping out the water that leaks into the hull, and mending the sails and the ropes, and cleaning the cannons, and scrubbing the decks.

[pause]

But on a pirate ship, there is less work for each man than on a merchant ship, because there are so many men. And so there is more free time.

[long-pause]

The lookout, high up at the top of the mainmast, watches the horizon all day long, for sails.

[pause]

When he sees a sail, he calls out: sail ho! And everyone comes to look. Is it a rich merchant ship? Or a warship of the Navy, which might attack them? The captain and the officers look through their telescopes. If it is a merchant ship, the chase begins.

[pause]

{img:lookout-masthead-telescope-distant-sail-horizon-sunny}
But most days, there is no ship to chase. The sea is empty. And the men pass the day as best they can.

[long-pause]

They talk, and tell stories, about their adventures, and the ships they have taken, and the places they have been, and the girls they have known.

[pause]

They play cards, and dice, although on some ships this is forbidden, because it leads to quarrels. They play music: there are fiddlers on board, and pipers, and drummers, and trumpeters, many of them taken from captured ships. They sing songs, and dance on the deck, in the sun.

[pause]

{img:pirates-fiddle-dancing-deck-afternoon-laughing}
They fish, over the side of the ship, and catch dolphins, and bonitos, and sharks. They sleep in the shade of the sails. They mend their clothes. Some of them read, or have someone read to them, from books taken from captured ships. Blackbeard himself likes books, and takes them from the ships he captures.

[long-pause]

What do pirates wear?

[pause]

Mostly, the same as other sailors: loose linen shirts, and short jackets, and wide, baggy trousers, cut off at the knee, or below, so that they do not get in the way when climbing the rigging, and a knitted cap, or a scarf tied around the head, or a broad-brimmed hat, against the sun. Many go barefoot, on board.

[pause]

But when they capture a rich ship, the pirates often put on the fine clothes they find: silk coats, and velvet jackets, and lace shirts, and gold buttons, and feathered hats, and silk stockings, and they strut about the deck, looking very fine, and very strange, all mixed together.

[long-pause]

In the evening, as the sun goes down, the men gather on the deck, and eat their supper, and drink their rum, and talk, and sing.

[pause]

The sun sets quickly, in the tropics, in a blaze of red and orange and purple, and then the stars come out, thousands of them, brighter than anywhere on land. The sea glows with little lights, phosphorescence, where the ship's bow cuts through the water.

[pause]

{img:tropical-night-stars-milky-way-ship-sailing-glowing-wake}
At eight o'clock, the lights are put out, below decks. And the men lie down to sleep, on the deck, or in their hammocks, and the ship sails on through the warm night, with the helmsman at the wheel, and the lookout at the masthead, and the stars wheeling slowly overhead.

[long-pause]

{img:ship-galley-hearth-iron-pot-cook-hardtack-barrels}
What do pirates eat?

[pause]

At sea, in the warm Caribbean, it is very hard to keep food fresh. There are no refrigerators, of course. So the main food of every ship, pirate or not, is food that keeps for a long time: salt beef and salt pork, packed in barrels of brine, and dried peas and beans, and rice, and ship's biscuit.

[long-pause]

Ship's biscuit, which the sailors call hardtack, is a hard, dry, flat bread, made of flour and water and a little salt, baked several times, until it is as hard as a stone. It lasts for months, or even years. The sailors break it with a hammer, or soak it in their soup or their coffee, to soften it.

[pause]

But in the warm, damp air of the tropics, the biscuit soon gets weevils, little beetles, which burrow into it. The sailors tap the biscuit on the table, to knock them out, before they eat it. Or they eat it in the dark, so that they cannot see them.

[pause]

The cook prepares the food in the galley, the little kitchen, with a brick hearth and a great iron pot. Often, the ship's cook is an older sailor, who has lost a leg, or an arm, in a fight, and can no longer climb the rigging. This is where the idea of the pirate cook with a wooden leg comes from, like Long John Silver in Treasure Island.

[long-pause]

But pirates eat better than most sailors.

[pause]

They have plenty of men, and plenty of time, and they can stop at the islands whenever they like, to hunt and fish and gather fruit. And they take food from the ships they capture: flour, and wine, and cheese, and butter, and hams, and live animals, chickens and pigs and goats and cattle.

[pause]

{img:pirates-roasting-pig-beach-fire-palm-trees-evening}
On the islands, they hunt wild pigs and goats, which run free in the forests, and roast them over fires on the beach. They gather coconuts, and oranges, and limes, and bananas, and mangoes. They fish, and gather shellfish, and lobsters, and crabs.

[long-pause]

And above all, they eat turtles.

[pause]

The great green sea turtles come up onto the sandy beaches of the islands, at night, to lay their eggs. The pirates turn them over onto their backs, so that they cannot escape, and carry them back to the ship. A turtle can live for weeks on board, without food or water, and so the pirates always have fresh meat. They make turtle soup, and turtle stew, and roast turtle. Many sailors of the time said that it was the best food in the world.

[pause]

{img:green-sea-turtles-moonlit-beach-sand-waves}
There is a famous pirate dish, called salmagundi. It is a great mixture of everything they have: pieces of meat, and fish, and turtle, and eggs, and cabbage, and onions, and grapes, and olives, all chopped up together, and mixed with oil and vinegar and pepper and garlic. A sort of great salad. It is said to have been the favourite food of Bartholomew Roberts.

[long-pause]

And what do pirates drink?

[pause]

Water, of course, which they carry in great wooden barrels, and fill up at the springs and streams on the islands. But water goes bad quickly, in the tropics, in the barrels, and becomes green and slimy. So the sailors prefer to drink beer, or wine, while it lasts, and above all, rum.

[pause]

Rum is made from molasses, the dark, sticky syrup left over when sugar is made from sugar cane, on the plantations of the islands. It is cheap, and strong, and there is plenty of it in the Caribbean. The pirates drink it neat, or in punch, mixed with water, and sugar, and lime juice, and nutmeg. They call one of these drinks bumbo: rum, water, sugar and nutmeg.

[long-pause]

The lime juice in the punch is very important, although the sailors do not know why.

[pause]

On long voyages, sailors who eat no fresh fruit or vegetables fall ill with scurvy, a terrible illness, which makes them weak and tired, and makes their gums bleed, and their old wounds open again. We now know that it is caused by a lack of vitamin C, which is found in fresh fruit and vegetables, and especially in lemons and limes. In the Caribbean, with plenty of limes, the pirates are lucky.

[pause]

{img:limes-oranges-basket-ship-deck-tropical-island-near}
And they drink chocolate, too, made from cocoa beans taken from the Spanish ships, mixed with hot water and sugar and spices. And sometimes tea, and coffee, which were new and fashionable drinks in Europe in those days.

[long-pause]

In the evenings, after supper, when there is plenty of food and drink, the pirates hold a great feast on deck, with music, and dancing, and singing, and toasts.

[pause]

One of their favourite toasts, it is said, is: Damnation to King George! Because they do not like the new German king. And another is: a merry life, and a short one.

[long-pause]

{img:blackbeard-deck-dramatic-black-beard-pistols-sash-calm-sea}
Let us look more closely at Blackbeard, as Captain Johnson describes him in his famous book.

[pause]

He was tall, Johnson says, and strong. And his beard was the most remarkable thing about him. It was jet black, and very long, and covered his whole face, up to his eyes, like a frightful meteor. He twisted it into little tails, with ribbons, and turned them up about his ears.

[long-pause]

When he went into battle, Johnson says, he wore a sling over his shoulders, with three pairs of pistols hanging from it, in holsters, like a bandolier. And a cutlass, a short, curved sword, at his belt.

[pause]

And under his hat, on each side of his face, he stuck lighted slow matches: long cords of hemp, soaked in saltpetre, which burned very slowly, giving off smoke, and which were used to light the cannons. So that his face was wreathed in smoke, and his eyes looked out, fierce and wild, through the smoke, so that, as Johnson says, imagination cannot form an idea of a fury, from hell, to look more frightful.

[pause]

Was this true? Some historians think it was, and that Blackbeard used this costume, very cleverly, to frighten the crews of the ships he attacked into surrendering without a fight. Others think that Johnson made it up, or improved it, to sell his book. But it is the picture of Blackbeard that everyone remembers.

[long-pause]

Johnson also tells many stories about Blackbeard's wild and strange behaviour.

[pause]

He says that, one day, at sea, Blackbeard said to his men: come, let us make a hell of our own, and see how long we can bear it. And he took some of the men down into the hold of the ship, and closed the hatches, and filled the hold with smoke, from pots of burning brimstone, to see who could stay there the longest. The others came out, choking and gasping, but Blackbeard stayed the longest of all, and came out, laughing.

[pause]

{img:ship-hatch-smoke-drifting-out-sailors-coughing-laughing}
Johnson also says that Blackbeard was married fourteen times, with a wife in every port. There is no evidence of this.

[long-pause]

And Johnson tells a story about the end of Blackbeard's life.

[pause]

He says that, the night before his last battle, one of his men asked him whether his wife knew where he had hidden his money, in case anything should happen to him. And Blackbeard answered that nobody but himself and the devil knew where it was, and the longest liver should take all.

[pause]

{img:empty-beach-palm-trees-moonlight-legend-of-treasure}
Ever since, people have searched for Blackbeard's buried treasure, on the islands and the beaches of the Carolinas and the Caribbean. Nobody has ever found it. Most historians think that there never was any. Pirates spent their money as fast as they got it, on food and drink and fun. They very rarely buried it.

[long-pause]

And what about the flag?

[pause]

Every pirate ship flies a black flag, when it attacks, to tell its victims that it is a pirate, and that if they surrender, they will be spared, but if they fight, they will get no mercy. The flags often have pictures on them, in white or red: skulls, and crossed bones, and skeletons, and hourglasses, and swords, and hearts.

[pause]

The skull and crossbones is called the Jolly Roger. Nobody is quite sure where the name comes from.

[pause]

There is a famous flag, which is often said to be Blackbeard's: a black flag, with a white skeleton, holding an hourglass in one hand, and a spear in the other, pointing at a red heart. But in fact, this design first appears in a book, more than a hundred years after Blackbeard's death. We do not really know what his flag looked like. Some people who met him said that he flew a black flag, with a death's head on it.

[long-pause]

Whatever he really looked like, Blackbeard was very good at being frightening.

[pause]

In the whole of his career as a pirate, which lasted only about two years, there is no record of him killing anyone, before his very last battle. His name, and his beard, and his reputation, were enough.

[pause]

{img:merchant-captain-handing-over-ship-papers-pirate-politely}
He was, in a way, one of the first people in history to understand the power of an image, of a story, of a name. And three hundred years later, he is still the most famous pirate who ever lived.

[long-pause]

{img:gentleman-planter-barbados-1717-wig-fine-coat-garden}
One of the strangest pirates of all sails with Blackbeard, in the spring of 1718. His name is Stede Bonnet.

[pause]

Stede Bonnet is not a sailor at all. He is a rich gentleman, from the island of Barbados, the owner of a large sugar plantation, with a fine house, and a wife, and children. He is educated, and owns a library of books. He is about thirty years old. He is a major in the island's militia. He has everything that a man of his time could want.

[long-pause]

And then, one day, in 1717, he decides to become a pirate.

[pause]

Nobody knows why. Captain Johnson says that it was because his wife was unkind to him, and that he was suffering from a disorder of the mind. Some historians think that he was unhappy, and in debt, and bored. Whatever the reason, he does something that no other pirate ever did. He buys his own pirate ship.

[pause]

{img:sloop-being-fitted-barbados-harbour-cannons-loaded}
Most pirates steal their ships. Bonnet buys a sloop, and fits it out with ten guns, and hires a crew of seventy men, and pays them wages, as if it were an ordinary ship. He names it the Revenge. And one night, he sails away from Barbados, without telling his family, and becomes a pirate.

[long-pause]

He sails up the coast of America, and captures a few ships, off Virginia, and the Carolinas, and New York. But he does not know how to sail a ship, or how to command men. His crew does not respect him. They call him the gentleman pirate.

[pause]

In the autumn of 1717, he arrives in Nassau, and meets Blackbeard. And Blackbeard, seeing that Bonnet is not a seaman, persuades him to come aboard his own ship, as his guest, and to let one of Blackbeard's men command the Revenge.

[pause]

Johnson says that Bonnet spent the next months on Blackbeard's ship, walking up and down the deck in his morning gown, reading his books, and feeling rather unhappy, and wishing he could go home. He told his friends that he was ashamed of what he had done, and would be glad to leave the life, if only he could live in some country where he was not known.

[long-pause]

Bonnet sails with Blackbeard through the winter and the spring of 1718. And in the end, Blackbeard leaves him behind, at Beaufort, in North Carolina, as we shall see.

[pause]

Then Bonnet takes back his sloop, and goes on as a pirate for a few more months, under a false name, Captain Thomas. But in the autumn of 1718, he is caught by a ship from Charleston, at the mouth of the Cape Fear River, in North Carolina, after a long fight on the sandbanks.

[pause]

{img:charleston-1718-town-harbour-wooden-houses-church-spire}
He is taken to Charleston, and put on trial. He escapes, once, disguised as a woman, but is caught again. And in December 1718, he is hanged in Charleston, after writing a long and sorrowful letter to the governor, asking for mercy. It is a sad story, of a man who wanted adventure, and did not know what he was doing.

[long-pause]

Bonnet's sloop, the Revenge, was later used by the Royal Navy. And the place where he and his men were hanged, at the edge of the marsh, near Charleston harbour, was called White Point. Today, it is a quiet, beautiful park, with old oak trees, and benches, and a bandstand, where people walk their dogs, and look out over the harbour.

[pause]

There is a small stone there, which remembers the pirates.

[pause]

{img:white-point-garden-oaks-harbour-benches-quiet-afternoon}
Stede Bonnet's strange story has been told many times since. In recent years, it was even made into a television comedy, which imagined him as a gentle, kind man, who becomes a pirate, and becomes friends with Blackbeard.

[long-pause]

{img:ships-surgeon-1718-wooden-medicine-chest-bottles-jars}
Why did Blackbeard want a chest of medicines, at Charleston?

[pause]

To understand, we must think about how hard it was, in the year 1718, to stay healthy on a ship at sea, especially in the tropics.

[long-pause]

The sailors of that time suffered from many illnesses.

[pause]

There was scurvy, on long voyages. There were fevers: yellow fever, and malaria, which they called the ague, carried by the mosquitoes of the tropics, although nobody knew that then. There was dysentery, the bloody flux, from dirty water and bad food. There were coughs and colds and infections. And there were injuries: falls from the rigging, and wounds, and burns, and broken bones.

[pause]

On a crowded pirate ship, with three hundred men living close together, illness could spread very fast.

[long-pause]

Every ship that could, carried a surgeon.

[pause]

Ships' surgeons were not doctors, as we know them. They had usually learned their work as apprentices, to other surgeons, or to barbers, who in those days also pulled teeth, and let blood. They knew how to set broken bones, and bandage wounds, and pull teeth, and give medicines.

[pause]

{img:surgeon-bandaging-sailors-arm-cabin-lantern-kindly}
Pirates often forced surgeons to join them, from the ships they captured, because they needed them so badly. And they paid them well, and treated them with respect.

[long-pause]

The surgeon's medicine chest was full of strange things.

[pause]

There were herbs, and roots, and powders, and pills, and ointments, in little bottles and jars and boxes. There was Peruvian bark, from the cinchona tree, from South America, which helped with fevers, and which we now know contains quinine, which fights malaria. There was laudanum, a medicine made from opium, which eased pain. There were medicines to make people sick, or to empty their stomachs, which doctors of that time used for almost every illness. There was mercury, which was used, wrongly, against many diseases.

[pause]

{img:apothecary-jars-labels-latin-shelves-mortar-pestle}
And there were the surgeon's tools: knives, and saws, and probes, and syringes, and forceps, and a little brass bowl, for bleeding patients, which was thought to cure many illnesses, although it usually did more harm than good.

[long-pause]

Medicines were expensive, and very hard for pirates to get. They could not walk into an apothecary's shop in a town, and buy them, without being arrested. So when they captured a ship, they always took the medicine chest, if there was one.

[pause]

That is perhaps why Blackbeard asked for medicines at Charleston, instead of gold. Some historians think that many of his men were ill, perhaps with fevers, or other illnesses, and needed help.

[pause]

When the archaeologists explored the wreck of the Queen Anne's Revenge, they found some of the surgeon's things: a brass syringe, and a little pewter bowl, and a mortar and pestle, for grinding medicines, and the remains of small glass bottles, and little weights for weighing medicines. Perhaps some of them came from the chest from Charleston.

[long-pause]

Even in good health, a sailor's life was short, and hard.

[pause]

Most sailors of the time did not live to be old. Life on a merchant ship, or a navy ship, was perhaps even more dangerous than life on a pirate ship. Sailors were lost to illness, and accidents, and storms, and shipwrecks, every year, in great numbers.

[pause]

{img:old-sailor-mending-net-harbour-quay-sunshine-calm}
So when a pirate said, a merry life and a short one, he was, in a way, only saying what every sailor of his time already knew.

[long-pause]

{img:pirate-sloop-chasing-merchant-ship-full-sail-sunny-sea}
How does a pirate ship capture another ship?

[pause]

It is not, usually, the great battle that we imagine, from the films, with cannons firing, and men swinging across on ropes, and fighting with swords on the decks. Most of the time, it is much quieter than that.

[long-pause]

First, the pirates must find a ship.

[pause]

They wait near the places where ships often pass: the narrow straits between the islands, and the entrances to harbours, and the headlands along the coast, where ships must sail close to the land. They often hide behind an island, or in a quiet bay, and wait for a sail to appear.

[pause]

When they see one, they chase it. Pirate ships are usually fast, because they are kept clean, and because they carry many men, who can set the sails quickly, and row, if there is no wind. A heavy merchant ship, full of cargo, with a small crew, is slow.

[long-pause]

Often, the pirates use a trick, as they come closer. They fly a friendly flag, an English flag, or a French flag, or a Spanish flag, whichever the other ship is likely to trust. And they hide most of their men below decks, so that they look like an ordinary merchant ship, with a small crew.

[pause]

Only when they are very close do they suddenly raise the black flag.

[pause]

{img:black-flag-rising-mast-merchant-crew-watching-surprised}
Then they fire a cannon, often just a single shot, across the bow of the other ship, as a warning, and call to it to surrender. And all the pirates come up on deck, hundreds of them, shouting, and waving their weapons, and banging on drums, and blowing trumpets, to make as much noise as possible, and look as frightening as possible.

[long-pause]

Most merchant ships surrender at once.

[pause]

The merchant sailors know that if they surrender, the pirates will usually let them go, after taking what they want. But if they fight, and lose, the pirates may be very cruel. And the merchant sailors are not paid to fight. It is not their ship, or their cargo. Why should they risk their lives for the owners?

[pause]

{img:merchant-sailors-lowering-sails-calm-sea-giving-up}
So they lower their sails, and their flag, and wait.

[long-pause]

Then the pirates row over to the ship, in their boats, and climb on board.

[pause]

They gather the captain and the crew on deck. They search the ship, from top to bottom, for anything of value: money, and gold, and silver, and jewels, and cloth, and food, and drink, and weapons, and powder, and medicines, and tools, and sails, and ropes, and anchors, and navigation instruments, and charts, and books. Whatever they can use, or sell.

[pause]

They ask the captain and the crew questions: where they have come from, where they are going, what news there is, and what ships they have seen. They ask the crew how the captain has treated them. If the crew says that the captain was cruel, the pirates sometimes punish him. If the crew says that he was a good captain, they treat him well.

[long-pause]

Then, often, they invite the sailors of the captured ship to join them, as pirates.

[pause]

Many of them do. A pirate's life, with its freedom, and its fair shares, and its plentiful food and drink, seems much better to them than the hard life of a merchant sailor. Most new pirates were recruited in this way.

[pause]

{img:pirates-talking-captured-sailors-deck-friendly-invitation}
And then the pirates let the ship go, usually, with its captain and the rest of its crew, after taking what they want. Sometimes they keep the ship, if it is a good one, or burn it, or sink it. But usually they let it sail away.

[long-pause]

And so, for most of the people who met Blackbeard at sea, the meeting was frightening, and costly, but not dangerous. They lost their cargo, and some of their men, and perhaps their ship. But they came home, and told the story.

[pause]

And with every story, Blackbeard's name became more famous, and more feared.

[long-pause]

{img:charleston-harbour-1718-ships-sandbars-low-town-spires}
In May 1718, Blackbeard does the boldest thing of his whole career.

[pause]

He sails north from the Caribbean, with his fleet of four ships and about four hundred men, past Florida, to the coast of South Carolina. And he anchors his ships across the entrance to the harbour of Charleston, one of the richest towns in the American colonies.

[long-pause]

Charleston, or Charles Town, as it was called then, is a busy port, of about three thousand people, on a point of land between two rivers, with a fine harbour, protected by long sandbars, with only a narrow channel for ships to come in and out.

[pause]

It is the capital of South Carolina, and grows rich from the trade in rice, and deerskins, and naval stores, tar and pitch and turpentine, and from the trade in enslaved people. Its streets are lined with brick and wooden houses, and churches, and warehouses, along the waterfront.

[pause]

And for about a week, in May 1718, Blackbeard holds the whole town to ransom.

[long-pause]

Every ship that tries to come in or go out of the harbour is stopped and captured by the pirates. In a few days, they capture eight or nine ships, and take their cargoes, and their crews, and their passengers.

[pause]

Among the passengers, on a ship bound for London, there are some important people from Charleston, including a member of the governor's council, named Samuel Wragg, and his little son, who is four years old.

[pause]

{img:pirate-ships-anchored-harbour-mouth-captured-vessels-near}
The town is in a panic. There are no warships in the harbour to fight the pirates. People are afraid that the pirates will attack the town itself.

[long-pause]

Then Blackbeard sends a message to the governor of South Carolina, Robert Johnson.

[pause]

He sends some of his men, and one of the prisoners, ashore in a boat, with a demand. He does not want gold, or silver. He wants a chest of medicines.

[pause]

{img:pirate-boat-rowing-to-town-quay-messengers-crowd}
We do not know exactly why. Perhaps many of his men are ill, with fevers, or other illnesses, and need medicine. Medicine was very hard to get, for pirates, and very valuable. If the governor does not send the medicine, the message says, the prisoners will suffer, and the pirates will burn the captured ships.

[long-pause]

The governor and his council meet, and decide that they have no choice. They gather the medicines, from the apothecaries of the town, worth about three or four hundred pounds, and put them in a chest.

[pause]

But the pirates who went ashore with the message are slow to come back. They are wandering around the streets of Charleston, enjoying themselves, and drinking in the taverns, while the people of the town watch them, angry and afraid, and can do nothing.

[pause]

Meanwhile, on the ships, the prisoners wait, frightened. When the pirates do not come back on time, Blackbeard moves his ships closer to the town, and the prisoners are terrified. But at last, the messengers return, with the medicine chest. And Blackbeard keeps his word.

[long-pause]

He lets the prisoners go, unharmed, and the ships too.

[pause]

But he takes everything of value from them first. Samuel Wragg, the councillor, has to give up about six thousand dollars in gold and silver. And some of the prisoners, it is said, are sent ashore without most of their fine clothes.

[pause]

{img:prisoners-rowing-ashore-relieved-harbour-evening-light}
Then Blackbeard and his fleet sail away, northward, along the coast. The whole blockade lasted about a week. Nobody was harmed. But the people of Charleston never forgot it, and the governor wrote angry letters to London, asking for help against the pirates.

[long-pause]

Today, if you visit Charleston, you can walk along the waterfront, by the old harbour, past the beautiful old houses, painted in soft colours, pink and yellow and blue and green, and the old church spires.

[pause]

And you can look out over the water, to the mouth of the harbour, between the islands, where the Queen Anne's Revenge and her fleet lay at anchor, three hundred years ago, waiting for a chest of medicine.

[long-pause]

{img:north-carolina-coast-sandy-inlet-dunes-ships-june-1718}
After Charleston, Blackbeard sails north, to the coast of North Carolina.

[pause]

The coast of North Carolina is very different from the Caribbean. It is a long, low line of sandy islands, called the Outer Banks, stretching for two hundred miles along the coast, like a wall of sand, between the open Atlantic Ocean and the great calm sounds of water behind them.

[long-pause]

The islands are narrow, only a few hundred metres wide in some places, with long, wide beaches of pale sand on the ocean side, and dunes, covered with sea oats and grass, and behind them, little woods of twisted live oaks and pines and myrtle, and salt marshes, on the side of the sounds.

[pause]

Between the islands, there are narrow channels, called inlets, where the tide rushes in and out, and where the sand is always moving, and shifting, building new shoals and sandbars. It is a very dangerous coast for ships. Hundreds of ships have been wrecked here. Sailors call it the graveyard of the Atlantic.

[pause]

But for pirates, it is a good place to hide. The sounds behind the islands are shallow, and calm, and sheltered, and the big warships of the Navy cannot follow the pirates' small sloops into them.

[long-pause]

At the beginning of June, 1718, Blackbeard's fleet comes to an inlet called Topsail Inlet, which we now call Beaufort Inlet, near the little town of Beaufort.

[pause]

The Queen Anne's Revenge tries to go in through the inlet, into the sheltered water behind. And she runs aground, on a sandbar, and sticks fast.

[pause]

{img:large-ship-aground-sandbar-inlet-tilted-calm-sea}
One of the smaller ships, a sloop called the Adventure, commanded by one of Blackbeard's men, Israel Hands, tries to help her, to pull her off. And the Adventure runs aground too, and is wrecked.

[long-pause]

Was it an accident? Some of the men thought, then and later, that Blackbeard did it on purpose.

[pause]

His fleet had become too big. Four hundred men, all expecting a share of the loot, were too many. Blackbeard wanted to make a new start, with a smaller crew, and a smaller ship, and to keep more of the treasure for himself and a few trusted men. And, at the same time, he wanted to take the King's pardon, and perhaps settle down for a while, ashore.

[pause]

{img:sailors-unloading-boats-sandy-shore-inlet-busy}
Whatever the truth, after the wreck, Blackbeard acts very quickly.

[long-pause]

He sends Stede Bonnet, the gentleman pirate, to the town of Bath, inland, to ask the governor of North Carolina for a pardon. And while Bonnet is away, Blackbeard takes all the most valuable things from the wrecked ships, and loads them onto the one remaining sloop, and sails away, with about forty of his most trusted men.

[pause]

He leaves the rest of the crew, perhaps two hundred men, behind on the beach, and on the wrecked ships, with no way to leave. And he leaves about twenty-five men marooned on a small, bare island of sand, with no food or water.

[pause]

When Bonnet comes back from Bath, with his pardon, he finds that Blackbeard has gone, and taken everything. He rescues the men from the sandbank, and takes back his own sloop, the Revenge. And he goes off, in a rage, to look for Blackbeard. But he never finds him.

[long-pause]

The Queen Anne's Revenge stays where she lies, on the sandbar, in the inlet, slowly breaking up in the waves, and sinking into the sand.

[pause]

For almost three hundred years, nobody knows exactly where she is. Until 1996.

[pause]

{img:shallow-water-over-sandbar-inlet-waves-quiet-morning}
But Blackbeard has sailed away, with his forty men, and his treasure, through the calm sounds, toward the little town of Bath, and a new life.

[long-pause]

{img:bath-north-carolina-1718-small-village-creek-wooden-houses}
In June 1718, Blackbeard sails into the little town of Bath, in North Carolina, and asks for the King's pardon.

[pause]

The pardon was announced by King George the First, in September 1717. It said that any pirate who surrendered to a governor, before September 1718, would be forgiven for all his crimes, and could go free, as long as he gave up piracy forever.

[long-pause]

Bath is the oldest town in North Carolina, but it is very small: a few dozen wooden houses, and a church, and some warehouses, and wharves, on a quiet creek, off a wide river, the Pamlico, surrounded by forests and fields and marshes.

[pause]

The governor of North Carolina, Charles Eden, lives near the town. North Carolina is a poor colony, with few people, and little money, and no warships. Governor Eden receives Blackbeard politely, and gives him and his men the King's pardon.

[pause]

And for a few months, Blackbeard lives in Bath, as an ordinary, respectable man.

[long-pause]

Johnson says that Blackbeard married a young woman, of sixteen, the daughter of a planter near Bath, and that the governor himself performed the wedding. Some local stories give her name as Mary Ormond. We do not know if it is true.

[pause]

The people of Bath, it seems, are friendly to Blackbeard. He has money, and he spends it. He brings goods to sell cheaply. Some of them, perhaps, are happy to buy them, without asking where they came from.

[pause]

{img:creek-side-house-garden-oaks-summer-evening-calm}
There are stories, still told in Bath today, of Blackbeard's house, on a point of land at the mouth of the creek, now called Plum Point, and of the tunnel from it, and of the treasure buried there. But there is no evidence for any of them.

[long-pause]

But Blackbeard does not stay ashore for long.

[pause]

In the summer, he goes back to sea, in his sloop, the Adventure, and captures two French ships, off Bermuda, loaded with sugar and cocoa. He brings one of them back to North Carolina, and tells the governor that he found it drifting, abandoned, at sea. The governor's court agrees that it is a wreck, and that Blackbeard can keep the cargo. Some of the sugar, it is said, goes to the governor's secretary, Tobias Knight.

[pause]

Many people in North Carolina suspect that the governor and his secretary are in league with the pirate. Nobody can prove it.

[long-pause]

Blackbeard spends much of his time on the island of Ocracoke, on the Outer Banks.

[pause]

Ocracoke is a long, low island of sand, at the southern end of the Outer Banks, with a wide beach on the ocean side, and woods of live oak and cedar and yaupon on the side of the sound, and a little natural harbour. Just south of it is Ocracoke Inlet, the main channel through the Outer Banks, through which ships pass to and from the towns of North Carolina.

[pause]

{img:ocracoke-island-sound-side-live-oaks-sloop-anchored-calm}
From Ocracoke, Blackbeard can watch every ship that comes and goes. He anchors his sloop in a deep channel on the inside of the island, near the southern tip, which is still called Teach's Hole.

[long-pause]

In October 1718, there is a great gathering of pirates at Ocracoke.

[pause]

Charles Vane, the fierce pirate from Nassau, who has refused the pardon, comes to Ocracoke with his ship, and meets Blackbeard there. And for several days, the two pirate crews camp together on the beach, and feast, and drink, and dance, and sing, around great bonfires, in what Johnson calls a great party.

[pause]

It is perhaps the largest gathering of pirates ever seen on the coast of America. The people of the colonies hear about it, and are alarmed. They fear that the pirates are planning to make Ocracoke a new pirate base, a new Nassau, on the coast of America.

[long-pause]

One man, especially, is alarmed. He is Alexander Spotswood, the governor of the neighbouring colony of Virginia.

[pause]

Spotswood is a proud, energetic, rather difficult man, a former soldier, who has fought in the wars in Europe. He hates pirates. He does not trust the governor of North Carolina. And he decides that, if North Carolina will do nothing about Blackbeard, Virginia will.

[pause]

{img:governor-spotswood-williamsburg-palace-office-maps-candles}
He has no right to send an expedition into another colony. But he does not care. In November 1718, he secretly plans an expedition, to go to Ocracoke, and capture Blackbeard.

[long-pause]

{img:colonial-farmhouse-north-carolina-1718-tobacco-field-pines}
What was life like for ordinary people in North Carolina, in the year 1718?

[pause]

North Carolina is one of the poorest and quietest of the English colonies in America. There are perhaps twenty thousand settlers, scattered thinly across the low, flat, green country, along the rivers and the sounds, on small farms, among the forests and the swamps.

[long-pause]

There are very few towns. Bath is one of only two or three. Most people live on their farms, miles from their nearest neighbour, and travel by boat, along the rivers and the sounds, because there are hardly any roads.

[pause]

They grow corn, which they call Indian corn, learned from the Native people, and tobacco, to sell, and wheat, and peas and beans, and sweet potatoes. They keep cattle and pigs, which run half-wild in the woods, and are rounded up once a year. They catch fish, and oysters, and crabs, in the sounds and the rivers. They hunt deer, and wild turkeys, and ducks and geese, which come in great flocks in the winter.

[pause]

They make tar and pitch and turpentine, from the great pine forests, for the ships of the Navy, and sell them. And they cut timber, for the ships, and for barrels.

[long-pause]

The houses are simple, built of logs, or of wooden boards, with a big brick chimney at one end, and a porch, for the hot summers. Inside, there are one or two rooms, and a loft above, where the children sleep. The furniture is simple and homemade: a table, and benches, and a bed, and a chest for clothes.

[pause]

The family eats corn bread, and corn mush, and pork, and beans, and fish, and greens from the garden, and peaches from their orchard, and drinks cider, and milk, and rum, when they can get it.

[pause]

{img:log-house-porch-brick-chimney-children-chickens-yard}
Life is hard, and lonely. There are few doctors, few schools, few churches, and few ministers. But the land is rich, and the climate is mild, and the people are independent, and do not like being told what to do by governors and officials.

[long-pause]

Many of the people of North Carolina are not unfriendly to pirates.

[pause]

Pirates bring goods that are hard to get in a poor colony: cloth, and sugar, and rum, and tools, and they sell them cheaply. They spend their money freely. And many of the settlers are poor people themselves, who do not love the rich merchants and the officials of the government. So they often ask no questions.

[pause]

{img:small-colonial-wharf-trading-sloop-barrels-settlers}
This was one of the reasons why Blackbeard felt safe in North Carolina, and why Governor Spotswood of Virginia did not trust the people there to deal with him.

[long-pause]

The colony has also been through a terrible time, a few years before.

[pause]

From 1711 to 1715, there was a long and cruel war between the settlers and the Tuscarora people, the most powerful of the Native nations of the region, whose lands the settlers had been taking. Many people, settlers and Tuscarora, lost their lives, and many farms were burned. In the end, the Tuscarora were defeated, and most of them left their homeland, and went north, to New York, to join their relatives, the Iroquois.

[pause]

So in 1718, the colony is quiet again, but poor, and still recovering.

[long-pause]

Along the Outer Banks, a few people live on the sandy islands themselves: fishermen, and herders, who let their cattle and sheep and horses graze on the islands, where they cannot wander away, and some pilots, who guide ships through the dangerous inlets.

[pause]

They live a simple, lonely life, between the ocean and the sound, in little wooden houses, among the live oaks, sheltered from the wind. They fish, and gather shellfish, and hunt the ducks and geese in the marshes. And when a ship is wrecked on the beaches, as happens often, they gather what washes ashore, and sometimes, rescue the sailors.

[pause]

{img:outer-banks-fisherman-small-boat-nets-sound-sunrise}
They see many strange ships pass, along their coast. And in the summer and autumn of 1718, among them, a sloop with a captain with a long black beard.

[long-pause]

{img:navy-warships-entering-nassau-harbour-july-1718-flags}
Meanwhile, in Nassau, everything has changed.

[pause]

In July 1718, a new governor arrives in the Bahamas, sent by the King, with three warships, and soldiers, and settlers. His name is Woodes Rogers.

[long-pause]

Woodes Rogers is a famous man. He is a former privateer captain, from Bristol, the same town as Blackbeard. From 1708 to 1711, he sailed all the way around the world, capturing Spanish ships, and came home rich and famous.

[pause]

On that voyage, he rescued a Scottish sailor, named Alexander Selkirk, who had been living alone on a desert island, off the coast of Chile, for more than four years, dressed in goatskins. Selkirk's story, told in Rogers's book about the voyage, gave Daniel Defoe the idea for his famous novel, Robinson Crusoe.

[pause]

Now Woodes Rogers has come to Nassau, to get rid of the pirates, and to make the Bahamas a respectable English colony again.

[long-pause]

When he arrives, most of the pirates in Nassau accept the King's pardon. Hundreds of them line up to sign. Even Benjamin Hornigold, Blackbeard's old teacher, takes the pardon, and becomes a pirate hunter, working for the governor.

[pause]

But Charles Vane refuses. On the night that Rogers arrives, Vane sets fire to a captured ship, and sends it, burning, toward the warships, and escapes, in his sloop, out of the other entrance of the harbour, firing his guns, and flying the black flag.

[pause]

{img:sloop-escaping-harbour-dawn-warships-behind-calm-water}
Woodes Rogers sets to work. He repairs the fort, and builds new defences, and sets the people to clearing the land, and planting crops. It is very hard. Many of the new settlers fall ill, with fevers. Many of the pardoned pirates go back to piracy. But slowly, Nassau becomes a quieter place.

[long-pause]

The motto of the Bahamas, which Woodes Rogers chose, was Expulsis Piratis, Restituta Commercia: the pirates expelled, commerce restored. It was the motto of the Bahamas for more than two hundred and fifty years.

[pause]

And in Nassau today, in front of a grand hotel, there is a statue of Woodes Rogers.

[pause]

{img:colonial-fort-nassau-repair-workers-stone-sunshine}
So, by the autumn of 1718, the golden age of piracy is beginning to end. The pirates' home is gone. The King's pardon has persuaded many of them to give up. The Navy is sending more warships. And the colonies are beginning to fight back.

[long-pause]

Other famous pirates of the time meet their ends in these years too.

[pause]

Black Sam Bellamy, the young, generous pirate who called himself a Robin Hood, captured a great slave ship called the Whydah, full of gold and silver, in 1717. A few weeks later, in a terrible storm, the Whydah was wrecked off Cape Cod, in Massachusetts, and almost all on board were lost, including Bellamy, who was only twenty-eight.

[pause]

In 1984, the wreck of the Whydah was found, with thousands of coins, and the ship's bell, with the name Whydah on it. It is the only pirate ship ever found and identified for certain, apart from Blackbeard's Queen Anne's Revenge.

[long-pause]

And in 1720, near Jamaica, a pirate named Calico Jack Rackham, so called because he wore bright clothes of calico cotton, was captured with his crew.

[pause]

And in his crew, there were two women: Anne Bonny and Mary Read. Both of them dressed as men, and worked, and fought, as pirates, alongside the men. When they were captured and tried, they told the court that they were expecting babies. And so they were not hanged. Mary Read passed away in prison, of a fever. Nobody knows for certain what happened to Anne Bonny. Some say that her father, a rich planter, took her home, and that she married, and lived to be an old woman.

[pause]

{img:two-women-sailors-ship-deck-1720-wind-jackets}
Their story became one of the most famous of all the pirate stories, and has been told in many books and films.

[long-pause]

{img:two-small-sloops-sailing-calm-sound-november-grey-dawn}
In November 1718, Governor Spotswood of Virginia sends two small sloops, the Jane and the Ranger, south along the coast, to Ocracoke.

[pause]

They are not warships. They are small, private sloops, hired by the governor, because the Navy's warships are too big to go into the shallow waters of the sounds. They carry no cannons. But on board, there are about sixty sailors from two Navy warships, which are anchored in Virginia.

[long-pause]

They are led by a young Navy officer, Lieutenant Robert Maynard, of the warship Pearl. He is about thirty-five, an experienced and steady officer.

[pause]

At the same time, Spotswood sends soldiers overland, to the town of Bath, to look for Blackbeard there, and to seize any goods he has hidden.

[pause]

Spotswood also announces a reward: one hundred pounds for capturing Blackbeard, and smaller sums for each of his men. This is a great deal of money, more than most sailors would earn in many years.

[long-pause]

On the evening of the twenty-first of November, 1718, Maynard's two sloops arrive at Ocracoke Inlet, and see Blackbeard's sloop, the Adventure, at anchor, in the channel inside the island, at Teach's Hole.

[pause]

Blackbeard has only about twenty men with him. The rest are in Bath, or elsewhere. He does not know that anyone is coming. Johnson says that he spent the night drinking, and talking, with his men, and with the master of a trading sloop that was anchored nearby.

[pause]

{img:evening-sound-anchored-sloops-lanterns-quiet-island-trees}
Maynard does not attack in the dark, because the channels are narrow and shallow, and full of sandbars, and he does not know them. So the two sides wait, through the night, a little way apart, on the quiet water, among the islands, each knowing that the other is there.

[long-pause]

At dawn, on the twenty-second of November, Maynard's sloops raise their anchors, and move toward Blackbeard's sloop.

[pause]

What happened next, we will tell gently, and from a distance, as if we were standing far away, on the beach of Ocracoke, in the early morning light, watching the three little ships on the water.

[pause]

There is a short, fierce fight, on the water of the sound, and then on the deck of Maynard's sloop, the Jane, as the two crews meet. It lasts only a few minutes.

[pause]

And when it is over, the morning is quiet again. And Blackbeard has fallen, on the deck of the Jane, in the cold grey light of a November morning, at the age of about thirty-eight.

[long-pause]

Maynard writes afterward, in a letter, that Blackbeard was a brave man, and fought hard to the end. Several of Maynard's men, and about half of Blackbeard's, have also been lost. The rest of Blackbeard's men surrender.

[pause]

The story of the fight has been told, and retold, and made more and more dramatic, over the centuries. Johnson says that Blackbeard received many wounds before he fell. But we do not need to know all that.

[pause]

{img:ocracoke-sound-calm-morning-after-gulls-low-light}
It is enough to know that the most famous pirate in history ended his days, not in a great sea battle, but in a short, confused fight, in a quiet sound, behind a sandy island, on the coast of North Carolina.

[long-pause]

Maynard sails back to Virginia, with his prisoners, and with proof that Blackbeard is gone.

[pause]

The prisoners are taken to Williamsburg, the capital of Virginia, and put on trial in March 1719. Most of them are hanged. But one, Samuel Odell, who was a trader who had only happened to be on board that night, is found not guilty. And another, Israel Hands, Blackbeard's sailing master, who was in Bath at the time of the fight, is pardoned, in return for giving evidence against others. Johnson says that he ended his days as a beggar in London.

[pause]

More than a hundred and sixty years later, the writer Robert Louis Stevenson would borrow his name, Israel Hands, for one of the pirates in his famous novel, Treasure Island.

[long-pause]

Governor Eden of North Carolina, and his secretary, Tobias Knight, are accused of helping Blackbeard. Some letters, and sugar from the French ships, are found in Knight's barn. But they are never punished. Knight passes away the next year, and Eden a few years later.

[pause]

And Spotswood, the governor of Virginia, who sent the expedition, is criticized by many people, for sending his men into another colony without permission. And there are long quarrels, for years, about the reward money.

[pause]

{img:quiet-colonial-church-graveyard-oaks-bath-autumn}
But Blackbeard's story is over. He was a pirate for only about two years. And yet, three hundred years later, he is still the most famous pirate who ever lived.

[long-pause]

{img:old-book-general-history-pyrates-1724-engraving-pages}
After Blackbeard, the golden age of piracy lasts only a few more years.

[pause]

The navies of Britain and France and Spain send more warships to the Caribbean and the American coast. The colonies pass new laws against pirates, and hang hundreds of them. The great pirates are caught, one by one. Bartholomew Roberts, the most successful pirate of all, who captured more than four hundred ships, falls in battle with a British warship, off the coast of Africa, in 1722.

[long-pause]

By about 1726, the golden age is over. There are still pirates, in other places and other times, as there always have been. But never again so many, so famous, in the Caribbean and the coasts of America.

[pause]

And then, the pirates become a legend.

[pause]

It begins with Captain Johnson's book, in 1724, A General History of the Pyrates, which is a bestseller, and is printed again and again, in English, and Dutch, and French, and German, for many years. It gives the world the stories of Blackbeard, and Bonnet, and Bonny and Read, and Roberts, and many others, full of adventure and wonder and wickedness.

[long-pause]

Then, in 1883, the Scottish writer Robert Louis Stevenson publishes a book for boys, called Treasure Island.

[pause]

It is the story of a boy, Jim Hawkins, who finds a pirate's treasure map, and sails to a tropical island, to look for the buried treasure, with a crew that includes a cheerful, cunning pirate cook, with one leg and a parrot on his shoulder, called Long John Silver.

[pause]

{img:one-legged-sailor-parrot-shoulder-ship-galley-friendly}
Treasure Island becomes one of the most famous books in the world. And almost everything that we think we know about pirates comes from it: treasure maps, with X marks the spot. Buried treasure on desert islands. Parrots that say pieces of eight. Wooden legs. Pirate songs: fifteen men on the dead man's chest, yo ho ho, and a bottle of rum. Stevenson made them up, or borrowed them from Johnson, and made them famous.

[long-pause]

In Treasure Island, Long John Silver's old captain, the most fearsome pirate of all, is called Captain Flint. And one of the characters says of him: Blackbeard was a child to Flint.

[pause]

Then, in 1904, the Scottish writer J. M. Barrie writes a play, for children, called Peter Pan, about a boy who never grows up, and lives in Neverland, and fights against the pirates, led by Captain Hook, a strange, elegant, wicked pirate with a hook for a hand. Barrie says that Hook was the only man that Barbecue was afraid of. And Barbecue was the nickname of Long John Silver.

[pause]

{img:theatre-stage-pirate-ship-painted-scenery-children-audience}
And in the twentieth and twenty-first centuries, there are hundreds of films about pirates, with sword fights, and treasure, and swinging from ropes, and walking the plank.

[long-pause]

Did pirates really make people walk the plank?

[pause]

Almost certainly not, or only very rarely. There is hardly any record of it, in the time of Blackbeard. And did they really bury treasure? Almost never. Captain William Kidd buried some, on Gardiners Island, near New York, in 1699, and it was dug up soon after. But most pirates spent their money as soon as they got it.

[pause]

Did they have parrots? Some sailors did, because parrots were bought and sold in the Caribbean, and could be sold for good money in Europe. Did they wear eye patches, and have wooden legs? Some, perhaps, because many sailors of the time were injured. But not many more than other sailors.

[long-pause]

So the pirates of the films are very different from the real pirates of 1718.

[pause]

The real pirates were young sailors, tired of hard work and cruel captains and low pay, who chose a dangerous life outside the law, for a few years. They stole, and frightened people, and sometimes did terrible things. They lived in a strange world of their own, with their own rules, of freedom and equality, in a time when ordinary people had very little of either.

[pause]

{img:pirate-ship-sunset-calm-sea-crew-silhouettes-gentle}
Most of them did not grow rich. Most of them did not live long. And most of them, unlike Blackbeard, were soon forgotten.

[long-pause]

{img:research-boat-beaufort-inlet-divers-calm-sea-1996}
For almost three hundred years, nobody knew exactly where the Queen Anne's Revenge lay.

[pause]

People knew that she had run aground at Topsail Inlet, now Beaufort Inlet, in North Carolina. But the inlet had changed, many times, over the centuries, as the sand moved, and the channel shifted. The wreck was buried somewhere under the sand and the water.

[long-pause]

In November 1996, a team from a private research company, called Intersal, was searching the inlet with a magnetometer, an instrument that can find iron under the sand, looking for a different wreck, a Spanish ship.

[pause]

And they found a pile of cannons, and anchors, lying on the sea floor, about seven metres down, about a mile off the beach.

[pause]

At first, nobody was sure what the wreck was. But over the following years, the archaeologists of the state of North Carolina studied it very carefully, and brought up thousands of objects. And by 2011, they were sure. It was the Queen Anne's Revenge.

[long-pause]

The wreck is in a difficult place.

[pause]

The water in the inlet is murky, and the currents are strong, and the divers can only work in the summer, on calm days, when the visibility is good. The wreck itself is mostly broken up, and scattered, and buried in the sand. The wooden hull has mostly been eaten away by shipworms, over the centuries. But some of the timbers, buried deep in the sand, have survived.

[pause]

{img:conservation-lab-cannon-concretion-tank-water}
The heavy objects, made of iron and bronze and lead and stone, are still there: the cannons, and the anchors, and the cannonballs, and the ship's bell. Over the years, they have become covered with a thick, hard crust, called concretion, of sand and shells and rust, cemented together, like rock. Inside these great lumps, the archaeologists find small things, hidden, sometimes perfectly preserved.

[long-pause]

They have brought up more than four hundred thousand objects. And slowly, carefully, they are cleaning them, and studying them, in a laboratory at East Carolina University, in Greenville, North Carolina.

[pause]

They have found more than thirty cannons, of many different sizes, from England, and France, and Sweden. They have found the great anchors, several metres long. They have found the ship's bell, made of bronze, with the date 1709 on it.

[pause]

{img:museum-display-ship-bell-anchor-cannon-soft-light}
They have found gold dust, and a few gold coins, and pieces of gold, perhaps from Africa. They have found pewter plates, and cups, and glass bottles, and wine glasses, and a brass syringe and other medical instruments, perhaps those of the ship's surgeon. They have found navigation instruments, and weights, and pieces of a clock.

[long-pause]

They have found the buckles of shoes, and buttons, and a sword hilt, and pieces of pistols, and musket balls, and lead shot. They have found the bones of animals: pigs, and cows, and turtles, and fish. They have found seeds, and peppercorns, and a piece of rope.

[pause]

And, most surprising of all, they have found paper.

[pause]

In 2018, inside a lump of concretion taken from the chamber of one of the cannons, the scientists found sixteen tiny fragments of paper, each no bigger than a coin, with printed words on them. Somebody had perhaps used the pages of a book as wadding, to pack the gunpowder into the cannon.

[pause]

And after a long search, they discovered which book it was. It was a book about a voyage around the world: A Voyage to the South Sea, and Round the World, by Captain Edward Cooke, published in 1712. Cooke had sailed with Woodes Rogers, on the very voyage on which they rescued Alexander Selkirk from his desert island.

[long-pause]

So Blackbeard, or one of his men, had a copy of the book about the voyage of Woodes Rogers, the man who drove the pirates out of Nassau.

[pause]

The objects from the wreck are now shown in museums, in North Carolina. The largest collection is at the North Carolina Maritime Museum, in the town of Beaufort, just a few miles from the inlet where the ship went aground, three hundred years ago.

[pause]

{img:maritime-museum-beaufort-waterfront-building-boats}
There, you can see the cannons, and the anchor, and the bell, and the gold dust, and the little pieces of paper, and many other things, and imagine the life on board the most famous pirate ship in history.

[long-pause]

{img:ocracoke-village-harbour-lighthouse-white-boats-evening}
Today, the island of Ocracoke is a quiet, peaceful place, at the southern end of the Outer Banks of North Carolina.

[pause]

You can only reach it by ferry, across the wide, calm sounds, or across Hatteras Inlet, from the island to the north. The ferry ride takes an hour or more, across the shining water, with pelicans flying low over the waves beside the boat, and dolphins, sometimes, swimming alongside.

[long-pause]

The village of Ocracoke is small, with about a thousand people living there all year round. It is built around a round harbour, called Silver Lake, full of fishing boats and sailing boats. There are little wooden houses, with porches, under the shade of live oaks and cedars, and narrow lanes of sand and shell, and little shops, and cafés, and an old white lighthouse, built in 1823, the oldest still working in North Carolina.

[pause]

For a long time, the people of Ocracoke lived by fishing, and piloting ships through the dangerous inlet, and rescuing sailors from shipwrecks. Some of the old families of the island still speak with a special accent, called the Hoi Toider brogue, from the way they say high tide, which sounds a little like the speech of old England.

[long-pause]

Most of the island is part of a national seashore, protected, with no houses, or roads, except one road, running along the island.

[pause]

There are miles and miles of empty beaches, of pale sand, with the waves of the Atlantic rolling in. There are dunes, covered with sea oats, waving in the wind. There are salt marshes, on the sound side, full of herons, and egrets, and little crabs.

[pause]

{img:empty-wide-beach-dunes-sea-oats-atlantic-sunset}
And there are the wild ponies of Ocracoke, small, sturdy horses, which have lived on the island for centuries, perhaps since the time of the first Spanish or English explorers, whose ships were wrecked on the coast. Today, they live in a large pasture, looked after by the National Park Service.

[long-pause]

And at the southern end of the island, on the sound side, there is Teach's Hole.

[pause]

It is a deep channel, in the shallow water, close to the shore, where Blackbeard anchored his sloop, in the autumn of 1718, and where he spent his last night. There is nothing to see there now, except the water, and the marsh grass, and the sky. The sandbars and channels have moved many times, in three hundred years. But the place still has his name.

[pause]

{img:sound-side-marsh-grass-still-water-channel-dusk}
In the village, there is a little shop called Teach's Hole, with a small exhibition about Blackbeard and the pirates. And every year, in the autumn, there is a pirate festival, with people dressed as pirates, and music, and storytelling, and children with eye patches and paper hats.

[long-pause]

The people of Ocracoke tell stories about Blackbeard.

[pause]

They say that, on dark nights, a strange light can sometimes be seen over the water at Teach's Hole, which they call Teach's light. They say that his treasure is still buried somewhere on the island. They say that he still walks the beach, at night, looking for something he has lost.

[pause]

Of course, these are only stories. But they are part of the island, and of the long story of the man with the black beard, who sailed these waters, three hundred years ago, and became a legend.

[long-pause]

And in the little town of Bath, on its quiet creek, the old houses still stand, among the trees, and the old church, Saint Thomas, built in 1734, the oldest church in North Carolina. And people still point out the place where Blackbeard's house is said to have been, at Plum Point, at the mouth of the creek, looking out over the wide, calm river.

[pause]

So the story of the Queen Anne's Revenge comes to an end tonight, on a sandy island where the ferries cross the sound, and the wild ponies graze, and the old lighthouse turns its light over the quiet water.
