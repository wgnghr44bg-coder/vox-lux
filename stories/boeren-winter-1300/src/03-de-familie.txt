In this house lives a family that we might call the family of John the Ploughman.

We do not know their real names. The people of villages like Wharram Percy left very few records of their lives: a name here and there in the account books of the manor, a few words in the records of the manor court. But we know a great deal about how they lived, from those records, and from the things they left behind, which archaeologists have found in the ground, and from the pictures that monks and artists painted in the margins of their books.

--

John is about thirty-five years old.

He is short and strong and weathered, with a brown face and big rough hands, and a short beard. He has worked on the land since he was a small boy, and he knows everything about it: how to plough a straight furrow, how to sow seed evenly, how to read the weather in the sky, how to care for an ox when it is sick.

He holds a little more land than most of his neighbours: about thirty acres, in strips scattered across the open fields, which his father held before him, and his grandfather before that. He and his neighbours share their oxen and their ploughs, and work together in the fields.

--

His wife is called Agnes.

She is a few years younger than John, quick and capable and cheerful, with a broad, kind face, and her hair always covered by a linen kerchief. She runs the household, and that is a great deal of work. She cooks and bakes and brews the ale. She milks the cow, and makes the butter and the cheese. She looks after the hens and the geese and the pig, and the garden behind the house. She spins and weaves and sews, and makes all the family's clothes.

{img:peasant-woman-kerchief-churning-butter-doorway}
And at harvest time, and at other busy times of the year, she works in the fields beside her husband, with a sickle in her hand.

--

They have four children.

The eldest is a boy of fourteen, called Will, who already works beside his father in the fields, and who will one day take over the land. Then there is a girl of eleven, called Alice, who helps her mother in the house, and looks after the younger children, and is learning to spin. Then there is a boy of seven, called Tom, who looks after the geese, and scares the birds away from the newly sown seed, and runs errands. And there is a baby, a little girl, who is not yet two, and who toddles about the house, and gets into everything.

{img:peasant-children-geese-yard-autumn}
There were other children too, who did not live to grow up. In those days, many children did not. The family remembers them, and prays for them, at the church, on Sundays.

--

John's mother lives with them too.

She is very old, perhaps sixty, which is old for those days. Her husband, John's father, died some years ago, and when he died, John took over the land, and promised to look after his mother for the rest of her life. That was the custom.

{img:old-grandmother-spinning-fireside-stool}
She has her own place by the fire, on a stool, near the warmest corner. She cannot do heavy work any more, but she spins wool, and minds the baby, and tells the children stories, and gives advice to Agnes, whether Agnes wants it or not. She knows the old remedies for coughs and colds and fevers, and the old songs, and the names of all the saints and their feast days.

--

Life in the family is hard work for everyone, even the youngest.

The children learn to work as soon as they can walk. They fetch water from the stream and the well, gather firewood, feed the hens, collect the eggs, mind the baby, and help in the garden and the fields. There is no school in Wharram Percy. The children learn everything they need to know from their parents and their grandparents, by watching and helping.

Only a very few children, the sons of the richer families, or boys who show a special talent, are taught to read and write a little by the priest, in Latin, so that one day they might become priests themselves.

--

But it is not all work.

There is time for play, too, especially for the children. They play games of chase and hide-and-seek around the houses and the yards. They play with balls made of rags or a pig's bladder. They play with little toys that their fathers carve for them out of wood: tops and whistles and little animals. They climb the trees by the stream, and fish in the millpond, and slide on the ice in winter.

{img:medieval-children-playing-ball-village-lane}
And in the evenings, the whole family sits together by the fire, and talks, and laughs, and sings, and tells stories. They are poor, by the standards of our time. But they have each other, and their home, and their village, and their neighbours. And on a winter's evening, by the fire, that is a great deal.
