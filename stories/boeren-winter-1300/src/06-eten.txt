In the winter, the most important food for the family is pottage.

Pottage is a thick soup, or stew, cooked in a great iron or clay pot, hanging from a chain over the fire, or standing on three little legs in the embers. Agnes keeps it simmering all day long, and adds whatever she has to it: dried peas and beans, oats or barley, onions and leeks and cabbages from the garden, herbs, and a little bacon or salt pork, for flavour.

--

Every day, a little more is added to the pot, and a little is eaten, so that the pottage goes on and on, all through the winter, changing a little from day to day, but never quite running out.

Many centuries later, English children would sing a little rhyme about it: Pease porridge hot, pease porridge cold, pease porridge in the pot, nine days old.

The family eats the pottage from wooden bowls, with wooden spoons, sitting around the table by the fire. It is warm and thick and filling, and on a cold winter's day, after hours of work in the frost, there is nothing better.

--

With the pottage, there is bread.

The bread of the peasants is not white, like the bread of the lord. It is dark and heavy, made of a mixture of rye and barley, or wheat and rye together, which is called maslin, and sometimes, in a bad year, of peas and beans ground into flour.

{img:dark-rye-loaves-baking-clay-oven}
Agnes makes the dough in a wooden trough, and the family's loaves are baked in the village oven, which belongs to the lord, and the baker takes a small part of the dough as payment. Or, in some houses, she bakes flat loaves on a hot stone in the embers of the hearth. A loaf of good bread, with a piece of cheese and an onion, is a fine meal.

--

And there is ale.

Everyone drinks ale, morning, noon and night, the men and the women and the children. Water from the stream and the well is not always safe to drink, and ale is safer, and it is also food: thick and nourishing, made from barley.

{img:woman-brewing-ale-wooden-tub-steam-barley}
Agnes brews it herself, every week or so. She soaks the barley in water until it sprouts, then dries it by the fire, to make malt. She grinds the malt, and soaks it in hot water in a big wooden tub, and drains off the sweet liquid, and adds yeast, and leaves it to work for a few days, until it is ready. The ale is weak and cloudy and a little sweet, and it does not keep long, so it must be brewed often.

When a woman in the village has brewed more ale than her family needs, she hangs a branch of green leaves over her door, as a sign, and her neighbours come and buy a jug of it.

--

The family also has the milk and the butter and the cheese from their cow and their sheep, when they are giving milk, which is mostly in the spring and summer.

Agnes makes the butter by churning the cream in a tall wooden churn, and salts it, so that it will keep. She makes the cheese by warming the milk, adding rennet, and pressing the curds in a wooden mould, and the cheeses are wrapped in cloth and stored on a shelf, and eaten through the winter.

{img:hard-cheeses-shelf-cloth-wrapped-dim-light}
In the winter, the cow gives very little milk, so the cheese and the butter from the summer are very precious.

--

There are eggs, too, from the hens, though they lay very few in the dark winter months.

There is fish, sometimes, from the millpond, or from the stream: eels and trout and roach. On Fridays, and in Lent, and on the many other fast days of the Church, nobody is allowed to eat meat, and so the family eats fish, if they can get it, or salted herring, bought from a pedlar who comes from the coast.

And there are rabbits and hares and birds in the fields and the woods. But they belong to the lord, and the villagers are not allowed to hunt them. Sometimes, on a dark night, a hungry man might set a little snare in the hedge, and hope that the reeve does not find out.

--

Meat is rare for the peasant family. They eat it mostly in the winter, from the pig that was slaughtered at Martinmas, and at the great feasts, like Christmas and Easter.

The food of the peasants is plain and simple. But it is, mostly, healthy, with plenty of grains and peas and beans and vegetables. Archaeologists who have studied the people of Wharram Percy have found that they worked very hard all their lives, and that in good years, most of them had enough to eat.

{img:family-supper-candle-rushlight-bread-ale-jug}
At the end of the day, as the family sits by the fire with their bowls of pottage, and their bread, and their cups of ale, they say a short grace, and thank God for the food. And they mean it.
