TOPIC: Caring for each other, and the snow
===
IMG: herbs-drying-rafters-wise-woman-pestle-mortar | An old medieval wise woman grinding herbs with a pestle and mortar under bunches of drying herbs hanging from the rafters
HEAD: Most peasants treated illness at home with herbal remedies and traditional knowledge.
BODY: Professional physicians were rare and expensive in medieval England, and few villagers could consult one. Most illnesses were treated at home. Women in particular knew how to prepare remedies from plants grown in gardens or gathered from hedgerows and fields. Common herbs included sage, mint, thyme, rue, betony and comfrey. Many households hung bunches of herbs from the rafters to dry. Some villages had a woman with special knowledge of healing, who helped neighbours with illness and childbirth. Historians find recipes for remedies in medieval manuscripts, sometimes called leechbooks. Some remedies had real benefits, such as honey for wounds, which has antibacterial properties. Others combined plants with prayers and charms. Even so, medical knowledge was limited, and infections were often dangerous. Monasteries also cared for the sick, and some ran hospitals. Studies of skeletons from Wharram Percy reveal evidence of illness, injury and healed fractures. These remains show that people often survived serious conditions with care.
===
IMG: honey-pot-herbs-wooden-cup-steam-fireside | A clay honey pot, dried herbs and a steaming wooden cup by a medieval fireside, close view
HEAD: Honey was the main sweetener in medieval England, and it was also used in medicine.
BODY: Before sugar became widely available, honey was the most common sweetener. Many households kept bees in hives made of straw, called skeps. Honey was used in cooking and to make mead, a fermented drink. Beeswax was valuable for candles, especially those used in churches. Honey also had an important place in medicine. It was mixed with herbs to make syrups for coughs and sore throats, and it was applied to wounds. Medieval medical texts recommend honey for many conditions. Modern research has shown that honey can help prevent infection in wounds, giving some support to these practices. Historians note that colds, fevers and chest infections were common in winter. Damp, smoky houses and close living conditions made illness spread easily. Warm drinks of ale or water with honey and herbs were common home remedies. Even so, serious illness was frightening. Families relied on care, rest and prayer, as well as on practical remedies.
===
IMG: neighbour-bringing-broth-bowl-cottage-door-winter | A medieval peasant woman bringing a bowl of broth to a neighbour's cottage door in winter, kind and caring
HEAD: Neighbours in medieval villages often helped each other in times of illness and hardship.
BODY: Village communities depended on mutual support. When a family faced illness, injury or the death of a member, neighbours often helped with food, work and care. Historians find evidence of such help in wills, court records and religious writings. The Church taught that caring for the sick and the poor were works of mercy. Parish guilds and fraternities, which became more common in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, helped members in need and paid for funerals. In some places, neighbours helped plough or harvest the land of a sick tenant. Even so, villages were not always harmonious. Court rolls also record quarrels, thefts and disputes over land and boundaries. Historians emphasise that cooperation and conflict existed side by side. Yet in the hardest weeks of winter, practical help mattered greatly. A bowl of broth, a share of fuel or help with animals could make a real difference to a struggling household.
===
IMG: boy-chasing-geese-frosty-yard-sunshine-recovered | A happy medieval peasant boy chasing geese around a frosty cottage yard in winter sunshine
HEAD: Studies of skeletons from Wharram Percy have revealed much about the health of medieval villagers.
BODY: Excavations at the churchyard of Wharram Percy uncovered the remains of hundreds of people buried over several centuries. Scientists have studied these skeletons to learn about diet, disease and life expectancy. The results show that many children died young, but those who survived childhood often lived into their forties, fifties or longer. Many adults show signs of hard physical work, such as worn joints and back problems. Some bones show evidence of childhood illness or periods of poor nutrition. Chemical analysis of bones has given information about diet, showing that most protein came from plants and dairy products, with less from meat and fish. Historians and archaeologists caution that skeletons do not record every illness. Many diseases leave no trace on bone. Even so, the Wharram Percy studies are among the most detailed of any English medieval village. They show both the hardships of rural life and the resilience of the people who lived it.
===
IMG: snow-covered-medieval-village-valley-morning | A medieval village in a valley covered in snow on a still morning, thatched roofs white, smoke rising straight up, a church by a frozen stream
HEAD: Heavy snow and severe frosts sometimes struck medieval England, as chronicles record.
BODY: Medieval chroniclers, often monks, recorded unusual weather in their annals. They noted severe winters, floods, storms and droughts, because these events affected harvests, prices and daily life. Some winters brought heavy snow and long frosts. Chronicles describe rivers freezing, including the Thames in London in certain years. Historians use these accounts, together with records of prices and harvests, to reconstruct the climate of the period. Scientists also study tree rings, ice cores and other natural evidence. Even so, medieval records are incomplete and sometimes exaggerated. A chronicler might describe only the most dramatic events. In a village like Wharram Percy, heavy snow made travel difficult and cut off contact with markets. People stayed close to home, feeding animals and keeping warm. Paths were cleared to the byre, the well and the church. When the sky cleared after snowfall, the valley could look very still and bright under the low winter sun.
===
IMG: shepherd-carrying-lamb-snow-dogs-hills | A medieval shepherd carrying a sheep through deep snow on a hillside with his dogs
HEAD: Lambing on many English farms took place in late winter and early spring.
BODY: Ewes on medieval farms usually gave birth between February and April, depending on the region and the breed. In upland areas, lambing often happened later, when the weather was milder. Shepherds watched their flocks closely at this time, day and night. Newborn lambs were vulnerable to cold, snow and predators such as foxes. Shepherds sometimes carried weak lambs to shelter or brought them into their huts or houses to warm them by the fire. Manor accounts record lamb numbers and losses, and payments made to shepherds during lambing. Writers on farming, such as the author of the thirteenth century text known as the Seneschaucy, described the duties of a good shepherd. Historians note that a successful lambing season was vital, both for the wool trade and for the household's future. Even so, losses were common. Bad weather could kill many lambs, and diseases spread quickly in a weakened flock.
===
IMG: mother-drying-snowy-children-by-fire-warm | A medieval mother drying her red-cheeked snowy children by the fire and giving them hot pottage
HEAD: Children in medieval villages enjoyed snow and ice, and bone skates have been found by archaeologists.
BODY: Snow and ice brought hardship, but they also offered opportunities for play. Children threw snowballs, built snow figures and slid on frozen ponds. A famous description of London written in the twelfth century by William Fitzstephen tells how young people tied animal bones to their feet and skated across the frozen marshes, pushing themselves with iron-tipped poles. Archaeologists have found many bone skates, made from the leg bones of horses and cattle, in London, York and other places. Historians use this evidence to show that winter games were common. In villages, children played on frozen ponds and streams. After playing outdoors, they returned home cold and wet. Clothes were dried by the fire, and warm food was provided. Even so, ice could be dangerous. Coroners' records describe accidents in which children fell through ice. Parents warned children about these risks, as they still do today.
===
IMG: thaw-dripping-thatch-melting-snow-fields-mist | Snow melting from a thatched roof and fields in a medieval village during a thaw, mist and dripping water
HEAD: When winter ended, melting snow and rain often caused floods and muddy conditions.
BODY: The end of winter brought a thaw. Snow melted from roofs and fields, and streams swelled with water. In low-lying areas, floods were common, and fields could become waterlogged. Roads and lanes turned to deep mud, making travel difficult. Historians find records of flooding in chronicles and manorial documents, sometimes noting damage to mills, bridges and crops. On chalk hills, water drained more quickly, but valleys could still flood. Farmers inspected their fields and fences, repaired damage and prepared for spring work. Even so, the thaw was generally welcomed. It meant that the coldest part of the year was ending. The days were longer, and the land was coming back to life. Birds sang more often, and buds appeared on trees and hedges. In medieval calendars, the months of February and March are often illustrated with scenes of pruning, digging and sowing, which show this return to outdoor work.

TOPIC: Candlemas and the coming of spring
===
IMG: candlemas-procession-candles-church-february | Medieval villagers in a candlelit procession inside and around a small stone church on Candlemas in February
HEAD: Candlemas, on the second of February, was celebrated with processions of candles in church.
BODY: Candlemas celebrated the presentation of Christ in the Temple and the purification of the Virgin Mary. In medieval England, it was an important feast. Candles were blessed in church, and people carried them in procession. Many took the blessed candles home, believing that they offered protection against storms, illness and evil. Historians find descriptions of Candlemas in liturgical books and in church accounts, which record purchases of wax. The feast also had links to the farming year. In folk tradition, the weather on Candlemas was said to predict the rest of winter. A popular saying claimed that if Candlemas was fair and bright, winter would have another flight, but if it brought clouds and rain, winter was gone and would not come again. Such sayings are recorded mainly in later centuries. Even so, they reflect long traditions. Candlemas marked the midpoint between winter and spring, and it brought light and ceremony into the dark season.
===
IMG: farmer-looking-at-sky-february-grey-fields | A medieval farmer standing at his cottage door looking at a grey February sky over bare fields
HEAD: Medieval farmers watched the weather closely, because their survival depended on it.
BODY: Farmers in the Middle Ages had no weather forecasts. They relied on observation and traditional knowledge. They watched clouds, wind direction, the behaviour of animals and the appearance of plants. Many sayings about the weather were passed down from generation to generation. Some, such as red sky at night being a sign of good weather, have some scientific basis. Others were less reliable. Historians note that weather lore was important because a bad season could mean a poor harvest and hunger. Calendars and almanacs, which became more common in later centuries, recorded such sayings. Even so, the medieval period also had more learned approaches. Some scholars attempted to predict the weather using astronomy and astrology. For ordinary villagers, however, experience was the main guide. Late winter was a time of watching and waiting. Farmers judged when the ground would be dry enough to plough and when it was safe to sow spring crops.
===
IMG: salted-herring-barrel-pedlar-cart-village | A medieval pedlar selling salted herring from a barrel on a small cart in a village lane in Lent
HEAD: During Lent, the forty days before Easter, meat was forbidden, and salted herring was a common food.
BODY: Lent began on Ash Wednesday and lasted until Easter. During this period, the Church forbade eating meat, and in many cases also eggs and dairy products. The day before Lent began, Shrove Tuesday, was a time for using up these foods. Making pancakes on this day became a well-known tradition, though it is better documented in later centuries. During Lent, people ate fish, peas, beans and bread. Salted or smoked herring was the most common fish, because it was cheap and kept well. It was caught in large quantities in the North Sea, and towns such as Great Yarmouth were famous for the herring trade. Pedlars brought barrels of herring to villages and markets. Historians find many purchases of herring in household and manorial accounts. Even so, Lent came at a time when food stores were already low. Many people found it a hard season, both physically and spiritually.
===
IMG: shepherd-hut-lantern-night-lambs-ewes-fold | A medieval shepherd's small hut with a lantern at night in a lambing fold, ewes and newborn lambs, calm
HEAD: During lambing, shepherds often stayed with their flocks at night in simple huts.
BODY: At lambing time, shepherds needed to be close to the flock at all hours. Many stayed overnight in small huts in the fields or near the fold. These shelters were simple, sometimes moveable, and gave protection from wind and rain. Inside, a shepherd might keep a lantern, a few tools and supplies for caring for ewes and lambs. The fold itself was made from hurdles of woven hazel, which could be moved and rebuilt as needed. Manor accounts record payments for hurdles, lanterns and other equipment, as well as extra food or ale for shepherds during lambing. The Seneschaucy describes the shepherd's duty to stay with the flock at night. Historians note that shepherds were skilled workers, trusted with valuable animals. Even so, the work was tiring and cold. The sound of ewes and lambs in the fold, and the light of a lantern on the hillside, were common sights in the countryside in early spring.
===
IMG: spring-ploughing-oxen-team-green-hills-larks | Medieval peasants ploughing with oxen on green spring hills, blackthorn blossom in the hedges, larks above
HEAD: In spring, fields were ploughed again and sown with barley, oats, peas and beans.
BODY: When the ground dried in March and April, farmers began spring ploughing. The heavy plough, pulled by oxen, turned the soil for crops such as barley, oats, peas and beans. These spring crops grew during the summer and were harvested in late summer. Barley was used mainly for brewing ale and for bread, oats for porridge and animal feed, and peas and beans for pottage. After ploughing, the ground was harrowed to break up clods and cover the seed. Children and young people scared birds away from the newly sown fields. Manuscripts such as the Luttrell Psalter show ploughing, sowing, harrowing and bird-scaring scenes. Historians use manor accounts to study the amounts of seed sown and the yields obtained. Medieval yields were low by modern standards, often around four times the amount of seed. Even so, the open-field system produced enough food to support a growing population in good years.
===
IMG: woman-planting-garden-spring-sunshine-children-helping | A medieval peasant woman planting a cottage garden in spring sunshine with her children helping
HEAD: Peasant gardens provided vegetables, herbs and fruit for the household.
BODY: Behind each house lay the croft, where families grew vegetables and herbs. In spring, women and children planted onions, leeks, garlic, cabbages, peas and beans, as well as herbs for cooking and medicine. Some crofts also had fruit trees, such as apples and pears. Gardens were important because they belonged to the household itself. Their produce did not need to be shared with the lord, although it might be subject to tithes. Historians find evidence of garden crops in manorial records, in archaeological remains of seeds and pollen, and in medieval gardening texts. One of the earliest English texts on gardening, written in the fifteenth century, lists many plants grown at the time. Archaeologists at Wharram Percy studied the crofts behind the houses. Even so, details of individual gardens are hard to recover. Gardens provided fresh food, flavour and variety. After a long winter of stored food, the first fresh greens were especially welcome.
===
IMG: may-day-young-people-hawthorn-blossom-dawn-singing | Young medieval villagers carrying armfuls of hawthorn blossom and flowers back to the village at dawn on May Day, singing
HEAD: Easter and May Day were joyful festivals that marked the end of winter and the coming of summer.
BODY: Easter was the most important feast of the Christian year. After the fasting of Lent, people celebrated with church services, feasting and the return of meat, eggs and dairy foods. Eggs were sometimes given as gifts. Soon afterwards came May Day, on the first of May, which celebrated the arrival of summer. Young people went out early in the morning to gather branches of hawthorn and other greenery, a custom known as bringing in the May. Houses and churches were decorated with flowers. In many places, people danced and sang. Historians find descriptions of May Day customs in medieval literature and in later records. Maypoles are documented in England from the fourteenth century onwards. Even so, the Church sometimes disapproved of these celebrations. Some customs may have had roots in older traditions. For rural communities, these festivals marked a turning point. The long, difficult winter was over, and the warmer months had arrived.
===
IMG: grandmother-bench-doorway-sunshine-swallows-spring | An old medieval grandmother spinning on a bench by her cottage door in spring sunshine, swallows building nests under the thatch
HEAD: Spring brought longer days, warmer weather and the return of migrating birds such as swallows.
BODY: By April and May, the countryside was changing quickly. Days grew longer, and the sun was warmer. Grass began to grow again, giving animals fresh grazing. Cows gave more milk, and hens laid more eggs. Swallows, cuckoos and other birds returned from their winter homes in the south. Medieval people did not know where swallows went in winter. Some writers believed they hibernated in mud or at the bottom of ponds. Historians find these ideas in medieval and later natural history texts. Even so, people knew that the return of the swallow marked the arrival of spring. Older people often sat outside their doors in the sunshine, after months spent close to the fire. Work in the fields and gardens increased. For peasant families, surviving another winter was an achievement. It depended on careful preparation, hard work, community support and good fortune. Spring brought the hope of a good harvest in the year ahead.

TOPIC: The lost village, and Wharram Percy today
===
IMG: medieval-village-summer-harvest-golden-1320s | Medieval villagers harvesting golden wheat with sickles on open fields above their village in summer
HEAD: From 1315 to 1317, England suffered a severe famine caused by heavy rains and failed harvests.
BODY: The early fourteenth century brought great hardship. In 1315, heavy rain fell through the spring and summer across northern Europe. Crops rotted in the fields, and harvests failed. The same happened in 1316, and conditions remained difficult in 1317. Food prices rose sharply, and many people died of hunger and disease. Historians call this the Great Famine. It affected England, France, the Low Countries, Germany and Scandinavia. Chroniclers described people eating anything they could find. Manor records show high prices, sales of livestock and increased deaths among tenants. Soon afterwards, a cattle disease killed large numbers of oxen and cows, making recovery even harder. Historians estimate that a significant proportion of the population died in some areas. Even so, many villages, including Wharram Percy, survived and continued. The famine weakened many communities. It was followed by the even greater disaster of the Black Death in 1348 and 1349.
===
IMG: free-peasant-family-better-house-1400s | A 15th century English peasant family outside a larger timber-framed house with a garden, prosperous and content
HEAD: After the Black Death, surviving peasants often gained better wages, more land and greater freedom.
BODY: The Black Death reached England in 1348 and killed perhaps between a third and a half of the population. In the decades that followed, the number of workers was much smaller, and land was more plentiful. Surviving peasants could demand higher wages and better terms. Lords tried to control wages through laws, such as the Statute of Labourers of 1351, but these were difficult to enforce. Discontent contributed to the Peasants' Revolt of 1381. Over time, villeinage declined, and many peasants became free tenants paying rent. Historians note that living standards for many ordinary people improved in the fifteenth century. Diets included more meat and ale, and houses were sometimes larger and better built. Archaeology at Wharram Percy shows changes in houses during this period. Even so, the changes were gradual and varied from place to place. They transformed rural society over several generations and helped bring an end to the medieval manorial system.
===
IMG: grassy-bumps-hollows-evening-light-sheep | LATER: Grassy bumps and hollows of a lost medieval village on a slope in low evening light, sheep grazing, a ruined church below
HEAD: By around 1500, Wharram Percy had been abandoned, and its land was used as sheep pasture.
BODY: During the fifteenth century, the population of Wharram Percy declined. Families left, and houses fell into ruin. Landowners found that keeping large flocks of sheep was more profitable than growing grain with many tenants. In the late fifteenth century, the remaining villagers were evicted, and the land was turned into pasture. Historians have found documents recording the conversion, including the number of houses destroyed. Wharram Percy was one of many deserted medieval villages in England. Historians estimate that several thousand villages were abandoned between the fourteenth and sixteenth centuries. Some were deserted after the Black Death, others because of enclosure for sheep. Even so, the church at Wharram Percy continued to serve nearby communities for several centuries. It remained in use until the twentieth century. Meanwhile, the house sites were covered by grass. Their outlines remained as low banks and hollows in the fields, grazed by sheep for hundreds of years.
===
IMG: archaeological-finds-tray-bone-comb-pottery-spindle | LATER: A tray of archaeological finds: medieval pottery sherds, a bone comb, a spindle whorl, iron keys and a bone skate, soft light
HEAD: From 1950, Wharram Percy was excavated by archaeologists for about forty years.
BODY: In 1948, the historian Maurice Beresford visited Wharram Percy while researching deserted villages. Two years later, he began excavations there, soon joined by the archaeologist John Hurst. Their project continued every summer until 1990, making it one of the longest-running archaeological excavations in Britain. Many volunteers took part, including students and members of the public. The excavations uncovered houses, the church, the manor houses, the mill and the churchyard. Archaeologists found pottery, tools, coins, buckles, combs, spindle whorls and animal bones. They also studied the remains of hundreds of people buried in the churchyard. The project changed the way historians understood medieval villages. It showed that houses were rebuilt frequently and that village layouts changed over time. Even so, the results took many years to publish fully, in a series of detailed reports. Today, Wharram Percy remains one of the most studied medieval villages in Europe.
===
IMG: wharram-percy-valley-today-church-ruin-pond | LATER: A quiet green valley in the Yorkshire Wolds with a roofless ruined medieval church beside a millpond and a stream, sheep, wide sky
HEAD: Today, Wharram Percy is cared for by English Heritage and is open to visitors.
BODY: The site of Wharram Percy now belongs to the care of English Heritage. Visitors can walk along a path down into the valley from a nearby car park. The roofless ruins of the church of Saint Martin still stand beside the stream. The tower is partly preserved, and the outlines of earlier phases can be seen in the walls. The church was last used for services in the mid twentieth century, and parts of it later collapsed. Near the church is the restored millpond, surrounded by reeds and trees. On the slopes above, the outlines of houses and crofts can be seen as grassy banks and hollows. Information boards explain what life was like in the medieval village. Historians and archaeologists continue to study the site and its records. Even so, it is now a quiet and peaceful place. Sheep still graze across the fields where the villagers once lived and worked.
===
IMG: old-mill-pond-reeds-ducks-willows-evening | LATER: An old mill pond with reeds, ducks and willows in a quiet valley at evening, peaceful
HEAD: The millpond at Wharram Percy was originally created by damming the stream to power the village mill.
BODY: In the Middle Ages, a dam was built across the stream at Wharram Percy to create a pond. The water from this pond drove a watermill, where villagers' grain was ground into flour. Archaeologists discovered that the dam and the pond had a long history, with earlier phases going back before the Norman Conquest. Mills were important sources of income for lords, who required tenants to grind their grain there and pay a share. Historians note that disputes over mill rights were common in medieval England. Some peasants secretly used hand mills at home to avoid the fee, and lords sometimes confiscated them. Today, the pond has been restored. It is surrounded by reeds and willows and is home to ducks and other wildlife. Even so, it serves as a reminder of the busy village life that once surrounded it. The pond is one of the best-preserved features of the site.

IMG: visitors-walking-grassy-outlines-medieval-houses | LATER: A few walkers on the grassy outlines of medieval houses on a hillside in the Yorkshire Wolds, sheep grazing
HEAD: Visitors to Wharram Percy can walk among the outlines of medieval houses and imagine village life.
BODY: The grassy banks at Wharram Percy mark the positions of house walls, yards and property boundaries. From above, the layout of the village can be seen clearly, with rows of house plots along the hillside. Visitors can follow the line of the main street and see where houses once stood. The excavations showed that many houses were rebuilt several times on slightly different sites. Historians use the site to explain how medieval villages were organised. School groups and history enthusiasts often visit. Aerial photographs and modern survey techniques have added further detail to the picture. Even so, much of what once existed is invisible. Wooden walls, thatched roofs and furniture have long since rotted away. Yet the landscape still preserves the shape of the village. Standing in the quiet valley, visitors gain a sense of the scale of the community. A hundred and fifty people once lived, worked and celebrated here.
===
IMG: illuminated-manuscript-margin-ploughing-scene | LATER: An open medieval illuminated manuscript with a colourful margin painting of peasants ploughing with oxen, soft library light
HEAD: The Luttrell Psalter, made around 1320 to 1340, contains some of the most famous images of medieval rural life.
BODY: The Luttrell Psalter is an illuminated manuscript made for Sir Geoffrey Luttrell, a wealthy landowner in Lincolnshire. It contains the Psalms and other religious texts. Its margins are decorated with many lively images of everyday life. These include ploughing, sowing, harrowing, harvesting and threshing. Other scenes show women spinning and feeding chickens, a cook preparing food and people playing games. There are also many fantastical creatures and humorous scenes. Historians value the manuscript as a rich visual source for rural life in fourteenth century England. Even so, they note that the images were created for a wealthy patron, and they may present an idealised view of peasant work. Some scenes may also be symbolic or humorous rather than realistic. The Luttrell Psalter is now kept in the British Library in London. Its images appear in many books about medieval history and help bring the lives of ordinary people into view.
===
IMG: winter-dusk-valley-church-ruin-frost-sheep-stars | LATER: Winter dusk in a quiet valley with a ruined medieval church, frost on the grass, sheep sheltering and the first stars
HEAD: The story of Wharram Percy shows how ordinary families lived and survived through medieval winters.
BODY: Around 1300, Wharram Percy was a small but lively village. Its people lived in houses of wood, clay and thatch, sharing them with their animals. They grew grain in open fields, kept sheep, cattle and pigs, and depended on the harvest. In winter, they relied on stored food, careful use of fuel, warm wool clothing and the support of their neighbours. The Church calendar gave shape to the season, with Advent, Christmas, Candlemas and Lent. Historians and archaeologists have reconstructed this way of life through documents, excavations and studies of skeletons and objects. Even so, much about these people remains unknown. Most left no written words of their own. Yet the evidence shows their skill, endurance and resourcefulness. Today, the village is silent. The church stands roofless in the valley, and sheep graze over the old house sites. On frosty evenings, the stars shine over the quiet hills of the Yorkshire Wolds.
