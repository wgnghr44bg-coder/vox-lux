TOPIC: Food for the long winter
===
IMG: pottage-cauldron-hearth-wooden-spoon-steam | A large iron pot of thick pottage bubbling over a medieval hearth fire, a wooden spoon, steam rising, warm light
HEAD: The main daily food of medieval peasants was pottage, a thick stew cooked in a pot over the fire.
BODY: Pottage was the basic meal of most rural households in medieval England. It was made in a large iron or clay pot that hung over the hearth or stood among the embers. The ingredients changed with the season and with what was available. In winter, pottage usually contained dried peas or beans, oats or barley, onions, leeks, cabbage and herbs. A little salted pork or bacon could be added for flavour when the household could afford it. The pot often simmered for hours, and fresh ingredients were added from day to day. Historians use recipes, household accounts and archaeological evidence to reconstruct this diet. Analysis of residues in excavated cooking pots shows traces of cereals, vegetables and animal fats. Studies of skeletons from Wharram Percy show a diet based mainly on grain. Even so, pottage was filling and nourishing. In the cold months, a warm bowl of it was the centre of the family's meals.
===
IMG: dark-rye-loaves-baking-clay-oven | Dark round rye loaves being taken out of a clay village oven by a medieval baker, warm glow
HEAD: Peasant bread was usually made from rye, barley or a mixture of grains, rather than pure wheat.
BODY: Bread was the other basic food of medieval peasants. Fine white bread made from wheat was mostly eaten by wealthier people. Ordinary villagers ate darker and heavier loaves made from rye, barley or maslin, a mixture of wheat and rye grown together. In some areas, oats were also used. Grain had to be ground into flour at the lord's mill, and the miller kept a share as payment, called multure. On many manors, tenants were also required to bake at the lord's oven and pay a fee. These rights are recorded in manor documents. Some households baked at home anyway, in small ovens or on the hearth, and were sometimes fined for it in the manor court. Historians note that bread consumption varied with harvests and prices. In poor years, more barley, oats and even beans went into the dough. Even so, bread and ale together supplied a large share of a peasant's daily energy.
===
IMG: woman-brewing-ale-wooden-tub-steam-barley | A medieval peasant woman brewing ale in a large wooden tub with steam rising, sacks of barley malt beside her, cottage interior
HEAD: Ale, brewed from malted barley, was the everyday drink of medieval peasant families.
BODY: Ale was drunk daily by adults and children in medieval England. It was weaker than most modern beer and was brewed from malted barley, water and sometimes oats. Hops were not commonly used in England until the fifteenth century, so ale did not keep well and had to be brewed often. Brewing was usually women's work. The grain was soaked, allowed to sprout, dried and then mashed in hot water, before the liquid was fermented. Many women brewed for sale as well as for their own households. When a fresh batch was ready, a pole with a bush of leaves, called an ale-stake, was hung outside the house as a sign. Officials known as ale-tasters checked quality and price. Manor court rolls record many fines for brewing, which historians interpret as a kind of licence fee. Water was also drunk, but ale was considered nourishing. It provided a significant part of the daily diet.
===
IMG: hard-cheeses-shelf-cloth-wrapped-dim-light | Hard cheeses wrapped in cloth on a wooden shelf in a dim medieval cottage, with clay jugs and wooden bowls
HEAD: Cheese, butter and eggs were important sources of protein in the peasant diet.
BODY: Dairy products were an essential part of rural food in medieval England. In summer, when cows, ewes and goats gave plenty of milk, women made butter and cheese. Hard cheeses could be stored for many months and were eaten through the winter. Soft cheeses and butter were often salted to help them keep. Eggs came from hens kept in the yard, though hens laid few eggs in the darkest months. Historians sometimes call these foods white meats, a term used in the period for dairy products. Meat was eaten less often by poorer peasants. The Church also set many fast days when meat was forbidden. These included Fridays, often Wednesdays and Saturdays, and the whole of Lent. On such days, people ate fish, usually salted or dried herring, and dishes of peas or beans. Manor and household accounts show regular purchases of herring. Even so, diets varied greatly with wealth, region and season.
===
IMG: family-supper-candle-rushlight-bread-ale-jug | A medieval peasant family at supper with wooden bowls of pottage, bread and a jug of ale by the light of a rushlight and the fire
HEAD: Peasant families ate their main meal around midday and a lighter supper in the evening.
BODY: Medieval rural households usually ate two main meals each day. Dinner was taken around the middle of the day and was the largest meal. Supper was eaten in the evening and was usually simpler, often bread, cheese, the remains of the pottage and ale. In winter, supper took place after dark, by the light of the fire or a rushlight. Food was eaten from wooden bowls with spoons and knives. Bread could also serve as a plate. Prayers were often said before eating. Archaeologists have found wooden bowls, spoons, pottery jugs and iron knives at village sites. Writers of the period describe peasant meals as plain but filling. Historians estimate that a working adult needed a great deal of energy, especially for ploughing and threshing. Even so, food supplies were often tight in late winter and early spring. A careful household divided its stores so that they would last until new food became available.

TOPIC: Wood, fire and warm clothes
===
IMG: hearth-fire-glowing-embers-winter-night-cottage | A hearth fire glowing in the middle of a dark medieval cottage on a winter night, embers and soft flames, rafters in shadow
HEAD: Fuel for the hearth came from wood, peat or dried dung, depending on the local landscape.
BODY: Keeping the fire burning through the winter required a large supply of fuel. In wooded areas, peasants used fallen branches, dead wood and coppiced timber. In other regions, they burned peat cut from moorland, furze from heaths or dried cattle dung. The Yorkshire Wolds had relatively little woodland, so fuel was valuable there. Rights to collect wood were regulated by the lord and by local custom. The phrase by hook or by crook may come from medieval rules allowing peasants to take dead branches that could be pulled down with a billhook or a shepherd's crook. Historians are not certain of this explanation, but it is widely repeated. Manor court rolls record fines for cutting wood without permission. Fuel was gathered and stacked during the autumn. Even so, households often had to economise on fuel in long winters. The hearth was carefully tended, and the fire was rarely allowed to go out entirely.
===
IMG: wet-boots-drying-by-fire-steam-cottage | Wet leather boots and a woollen cloak drying by a medieval hearth fire, steam rising, cosy
HEAD: Winter weather made outdoor work wet and cold, and clothes and shoes were dried by the fire.
BODY: In winter, peasants still had to work outside, feeding animals, fetching water, mending fences and carrying fuel. Rain, mud and snow soaked clothing and footwear. On returning home, wet shoes and garments were placed near the hearth to dry. Clothing was mostly made of wool, which stays warm even when damp, and this helped people cope with the weather. Historians who study medieval climate note that around 1300, England was beginning to move from a relatively warm period into a cooler and wetter one. Some historians call the following centuries the Little Ice Age, though its timing and severity are debated. Records of harvests, prices and weather from monasteries and manors provide evidence of these changes. The wet years from 1315 to 1317 were especially severe. Even so, daily life in winter followed familiar routines. Practical knowledge of how to stay warm and dry was passed from one generation to the next.
===
IMG: rushlight-holder-flickering-dark-cottage | A small iron rushlight holder with a burning rush giving a small flickering flame in a dark medieval cottage
HEAD: Peasant homes were lit by the fire and by rushlights, made by dipping rushes in animal fat.
BODY: Artificial light was scarce and valuable in medieval homes. Wax candles were expensive and mainly used in churches and wealthy households. Tallow candles, made from animal fat, were cheaper but still a cost. Most peasants relied on the light of the fire and on rushlights. To make a rushlight, the outer skin of a dried rush was peeled away, leaving the white pith, which was then dipped in melted fat. The result was a thin taper that burned for a short time with a small flame. It was held in a simple iron clip. Writers in later centuries described the making of rushlights in detail, and historians believe the method was much older. Archaeologists have found iron holders at village sites. Even so, light remained limited. In the depth of winter, the dark evenings were long, and families often went to bed early to save fuel and fat.
===
IMG: family-huddled-blankets-sheepskins-embers-glow | A medieval family huddled under blankets and sheepskins close to the glowing embers on a very cold night, peaceful
HEAD: In the coldest weather, families stayed close to the fire and wrapped themselves in wool and sheepskins.
BODY: When frost and wind came, the peasant house was cold away from the hearth. Draughts entered through doors, gaps in the walls and the thatch. Families stayed near the fire, wrapped in woollen cloaks, blankets and sheepskins. Children often slept together with adults for warmth. Historians studying household inventories find blankets, coverlets and sheepskins listed among the most important possessions. The livestock in the byre also added warmth. Even so, cold was a real danger, especially for the very young, the old and the sick. Studies of skeletons from Wharram Percy show signs of hardship, such as interrupted growth in childhood. Yet these households had developed many ways of coping with winter. Thick walls of clay and thatch insulated the house well. Stored food, careful use of fuel and warm clothing helped them through the darkest weeks. Families relied on one another and on their neighbours when conditions became difficult.
===
IMG: shepherd-sheep-chalk-downs-woollen-cloak | A medieval shepherd in a woollen hooded cloak with a crook and a dog watching his flock on rolling chalk downs, cold grey sky
HEAD: Wool from sheep was the main material for clothing in medieval England, and it was also the country's most valuable export.
BODY: Sheep were kept in large numbers across England, especially on chalk downs and hills such as the Yorkshire Wolds. Their wool was used for most clothing and bedding. English wool was also highly valued abroad, especially in Flanders and Italy, where it was woven into fine cloth. Around 1300, wool exports were a major source of income for landowners, monasteries and the king. Customs records of the period show tens of thousands of sacks of wool leaving English ports each year. Peasants kept sheep for wool, milk and manure, as well as meat. The flock grazed on the open hills and on fallow fields. A village shepherd often cared for many households' animals together. Historians note that sheep farming later became even more important. In the fifteenth century, many villages, including Wharram Percy, were turned into large sheep pastures. Even so, in 1300 sheep were part of a mixed farming system with grain.
===
IMG: upright-loom-clay-weights-woman-weaving-light | A medieval woman weaving on an upright wooden loom with clay weights hanging from the threads, light from a doorway
HEAD: Spinning and weaving were essential household skills, carried out by women through the winter.
BODY: Turning wool into cloth took many steps. Fleeces were washed and combed or carded to untangle the fibres. Then the wool was spun into thread, traditionally with a hand spindle and distaff. The spinning wheel was introduced to England around this period, but the spindle remained common. Women spun whenever they had a spare moment, even while walking or tending animals. Thread was woven into cloth on a loom. Archaeologists at many sites have found spindle whorls, the small weights used on spindles, and loom weights of clay. By 1300, much weaving was done by professional weavers in towns, often on horizontal looms. Cloth could be dyed with plants such as woad for blue, weld for yellow and madder for red. It was also fulled, or thickened by beating in water, often at a fulling mill. Linen for shirts and underclothes was made from flax. Winter evenings were a common time for spinning and making clothes.
===
IMG: peasant-man-hood-cloak-snowy-lane | A medieval peasant man in a woollen hood with a long tail and a thick cloak walking along a snowy village lane
HEAD: Peasant men usually wore a woollen tunic, hose and a hood, and women wore long woollen gowns.
BODY: Clothing for ordinary people in England around 1300 was simple and practical. Men typically wore a linen shirt and braies, a kind of undergarment, beneath a woollen tunic that reached the knees. Their legs were covered by hose, and a hood with a short cape protected the head and shoulders. In bad weather, a heavy cloak was added. Women wore a linen shift under a long woollen gown, with a cloak and a head covering, often a kerchief or wimple. Colours were usually natural shades of wool or simple dyes, such as brown, grey, blue and russet. Historians study clothing through manuscript illustrations, such as those in the Luttrell Psalter, and through surviving fragments of textiles. Clothing was valuable and was worn for many years. Garments were mended, patched and passed down. Even so, they were well suited to the climate. Layers of wool provided warmth and some protection from rain.
===
IMG: leather-shoes-wooden-pattens-doorway-mud | Simple medieval leather shoes and wooden pattens by a cottage doorway with mud outside, close view
HEAD: Peasant footwear included simple leather shoes, and wooden pattens were sometimes worn over them in mud.
BODY: Medieval shoes were usually made of leather, stitched together and turned inside out, a method known as turnshoe construction. Soles were thin, so shoes wore out quickly and needed frequent repair. Archaeologists have found many leather shoes preserved in waterlogged sites, especially in towns such as York and London. To protect shoes from wet ground, some people wore pattens, wooden platforms strapped over the shoes. They raised the foot above mud and water. Historians note that pattens became more common in later centuries, especially in towns. In the countryside, people also wrapped their feet in cloth or straw inside their shoes for extra warmth. Some children and poor labourers went barefoot in summer. Even so, in winter suitable footwear was essential. Muddy lanes, frozen fields and snowy paths made walking difficult. Shoemakers and cobblers in market towns supplied new shoes, while villagers often carried out simple repairs at home.

TOPIC: The animals in winter
===
IMG: oxen-byre-hay-breath-steaming-lantern-dawn | Two red and brown oxen with curved horns in a byre at dawn, breath steaming, lantern light, hay in a rack
HEAD: Oxen were the most valuable animals on a medieval farm, and they were carefully fed and housed in winter.
BODY: In much of England around 1300, oxen were the main animals used for ploughing. They were strong, patient and cheaper to feed than horses, and their meat and hides could be used when they grew old. Horses were becoming more common for harrowing and carrying, but oxen remained central to the plough. Because a team was so important, peasants took great care of their oxen in winter. The animals were kept in the byre or in barns and fed on hay, straw and sometimes peas and oats. Writers of agricultural treatises of the period, such as Walter of Henley, gave advice on feeding and caring for oxen. Manor accounts record the costs of fodder and the number of animals kept. Historians note that a shortage of hay at the end of winter could weaken oxen before spring ploughing. Even so, healthy animals were essential. Good care in winter prepared them for the work of spring.
===
IMG: woman-milking-cow-byre-wooden-pail-warm | A medieval peasant woman milking a small brown cow into a wooden pail in a warm byre, children watching
HEAD: Fodder was often scarce in late winter, and farmers had to make their hay last until spring grass appeared.
BODY: Hay was cut and dried in summer and stored for the winter. Meadows near streams produced the best hay, and they were highly valued. Manor records show that meadow land was often worth more than arable land. In winter, cattle, oxen and sheep depended on stored hay, straw and other fodder. Farmers needed to calculate carefully how much they had. If winter lasted longer than expected, supplies could run out before grass began to grow again in spring. Animals then became thin and weak, and some might die. Historians call the period of late winter and early spring the hungry gap, both for animals and for people. Cows gave little milk during these months. Women still milked those that were in milk, in the warmth of the byre. Even so, farmers had several strategies. They reduced their herds at Martinmas, saved fodder and sometimes cut branches of ivy or holly as emergency feed.
===
IMG: village-shepherd-dog-flock-snowy-hills-dusk | A medieval shepherd and his dog driving a flock of sheep down snowy hills toward a village fold at dusk
HEAD: The village shepherd looked after the sheep through the winter, often with the help of dogs.
BODY: Sheep were hardier than cattle and could graze outdoors for much of the winter. On chalk hills like the Yorkshire Wolds, flocks grazed on open downland and on fallow fields. A shepherd, often employed by the village or the manor, cared for them. Dogs helped to guard and drive the flock. In bad weather, sheep were brought into folds, enclosures made of hurdles, or sheltered by hedges and banks. Manor accounts record payments to shepherds, purchases of hurdles and tar for treating sheep, and losses from disease. Sheep scab and liver fluke were serious problems, especially in wet years. Historians note that shepherds often had special privileges, such as keeping some of their own sheep with the lord's flock. Writers of the period described the shepherd's work in detail. Even so, it was a hard and lonely job. Shepherds spent long hours outdoors in cold and snow, especially during lambing in late winter.
===
IMG: pig-sty-wattle-yard-frost-boy-feeding-scraps | A small boy feeding scraps to a pig in a wattle sty in a frosty medieval cottage yard
HEAD: Pigs were kept by almost every peasant household, because they ate scraps and provided much of the family's meat.
BODY: Pigs were especially useful animals for peasant families. They ate kitchen waste, spoiled grain and almost anything else. In autumn, they fattened on acorns and beechmast in the woods. Most pigs were slaughtered at Martinmas, but breeding sows and a few young pigs were kept through the winter. They lived in small sties of wattle or wood in the yard, and children often helped to feed them. Hens and geese also lived in the yard, providing eggs, meat and feathers. Archaeologists find many pig bones at medieval village sites. Studies of animal bones from Wharram Percy show that cattle, sheep and pigs were all eaten. Historians use these bones to understand the size of animals and the age at which they were killed. Medieval pigs were smaller and hairier than modern breeds, closer in appearance to wild boar. Even so, they were an important resource. A household with a pig had valuable insurance against hunger.
===
IMG: sleeping-oxen-straw-byre-rushlight-quiet | Oxen lying down asleep in straw in a medieval byre lit by a single rushlight, hens on the rafters, very peaceful
HEAD: Caring for animals in winter included cleaning stalls and spreading manure on the fields.
BODY: Animals kept indoors in winter needed daily care. Their stalls had to be cleaned, and fresh straw laid down. The dirty straw and manure were piled in a heap, called a midden, in the yard. In spring, this manure was spread on the fields to improve the soil. Historians note that manure was very important in medieval farming, because there were no artificial fertilisers. Land that received more manure produced better crops. Lords sometimes required tenants to fold their sheep on the lord's land, so that it received the benefit of their dung. Manor records and agricultural treatises discuss these practices. Archaeologists find traces of middens and drains at excavated houses. At night, the byre was quiet. The animals lay in the straw, warm and fed. Families checked on them before sleep, by the light of a rushlight or lantern. Healthy animals in spring were the result of careful work through the winter.

TOPIC: Christmas and the new year
===
IMG: advent-village-church-frosty-morning-bell | A small medieval stone church on a frosty December morning, the bell ringing, villagers in cloaks walking toward it
HEAD: Advent, the four weeks before Christmas, was a time of fasting and preparation in the medieval Church.
BODY: The Christian year shaped the rhythm of life in medieval villages. Advent began four weeks before Christmas. It was a season of preparation and, in principle, of fasting, although rules were less strict than in Lent. Church services included special prayers and readings, and the clergy wore purple vestments. The church bell rang for services, and villagers attended Mass on Sundays and feast days. Historians use church calendars, sermons and guides for priests to understand how these seasons were observed in parishes. One such guide, written for parish priests in the fourteenth century, explains the meaning of each feast in simple terms. Even so, practices varied from place to place. In many villages, Advent was also a time to prepare food, clean houses and gather fuel for the coming festivities. For people living through a cold and dark season, the church year gave a sense of order. It also promised celebrations to come.
===
IMG: yule-log-dragged-into-cottage-hearth-family | A medieval peasant family dragging a great yule log into their cottage toward the hearth on Christmas Eve, holly and ivy on the walls
HEAD: Christmas celebrations in medieval England lasted for twelve days, from Christmas Day to Twelfth Night.
BODY: Christmas was one of the most important festivals of the medieval year. On Christmas Eve, many households brought in greenery, such as holly and ivy, to decorate their homes. A large log was sometimes brought in to burn through the season. The custom of the Yule log is better documented in later centuries, and historians are not certain how widespread it was around 1300. On Christmas Day, villagers attended Mass, sometimes beginning at midnight. The twelve days that followed were a time of rest from most work. Manor records show that labour services to the lord were often suspended during this period. Feasting, games and visits filled the days. Historians use household accounts, poems and chronicles to reconstruct these customs. Even so, much depended on local tradition and on the household's resources. For peasants, the Christmas season provided a rare break in the hardest part of the year.
===
IMG: manor-hall-christmas-feast-villagers-long-tables-fire | Medieval villagers feasting at long tables in a manor hall at Christmas, a great fire, roast meat, bread and ale, holly decorations
HEAD: On some manors, the lord provided a Christmas meal for his tenants, though they often contributed food themselves.
BODY: Several manor records describe Christmas customs between lords and tenants. On some estates, tenants brought gifts to the lord, such as hens or loaves of bread. In return, the lord provided a meal at the manor house. The meal could include bread, ale, meat and cheese. Some records specify exactly what each tenant should bring and what they would receive, including how much firewood. Historians have studied these arrangements as examples of reciprocal obligations on the manor. A famous example comes from an estate in Somerset, where tenants were entitled to a Christmas meal and a fire. Even so, such customs were not universal. On manors where the lord rarely lived, like Wharram Percy, celebrations may have been organised by the steward or by the villagers themselves. Feasts were noisy and crowded. For a short time, the usual routine of hard work was set aside for food, warmth and company.
===
IMG: villagers-ring-dance-carol-snow-lanterns | Medieval villagers dancing in a ring holding hands and singing a carol in the snow by lantern light
HEAD: Carols in the Middle Ages were originally ring dances accompanied by singing.
BODY: In medieval England, the word carol originally referred to a dance in which people held hands in a ring and sang. One person sang the verses, and the others joined in the refrain. Carols were performed at many festivals, not only at Christmas. Over time, the word came to mean a song for the Christmas season. Many medieval carols survive in manuscripts from the fifteenth century, with lyrics in English and Latin. Church authorities sometimes disapproved of dancing, especially in churchyards, and preachers complained about it. Historians use these complaints as evidence that such dances were popular. Mummers, groups of people in masks and costumes, also went from house to house at Christmas, performing simple plays and receiving food or drink. Even so, details of these performances around 1300 are scarce. Most evidence comes from later records. The Christmas season was a time of music, dancing and community celebration.
===
IMG: plough-monday-decorated-plough-young-men-village | Young medieval men dragging a plough decorated with ribbons through a village lane in January, laughing, villagers at their doors
HEAD: Twelfth Night ended the Christmas season, and Plough Monday marked the return to work in the fields.
BODY: The twelve days of Christmas ended with Twelfth Night on the fifth of January, followed by the feast of Epiphany. Twelfth Night was often celebrated with a special cake. In later centuries, a bean was hidden inside, and whoever found it became king of the feast for the evening. Wassailing, drinking to health and good fortune, was also associated with this time, including blessing apple trees in some regions. Historians find many of these customs documented mainly in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, though their roots may be older. The first Monday after Epiphany was known as Plough Monday. It marked the traditional return to agricultural work. In some areas, young men decorated a plough and dragged it around the village, asking for money or food. The earliest records of Plough Monday customs come from the fifteenth century. Even so, the general pattern of a winter break followed by a return to work was ancient.
===
IMG: two-men-threshing-flails-barn-floor-dust-light | Two medieval peasants threshing grain with wooden flails on a barn floor between open doors, dust rising in beams of light
HEAD: Threshing grain with flails was one of the main winter tasks on medieval farms.
BODY: Grain was harvested in late summer and stored as sheaves in barns. During the winter, it was threshed to separate the seeds from the straw. This was done with a flail, a tool made of two wooden sticks joined by a leather strap. Workers swung the flail and beat the sheaves laid on the barn floor. The work was hard and repetitive. Threshing often took place throughout the winter, as grain was needed. After threshing, the grain was winnowed. It was tossed in the air or fanned, so that the wind blew away the lighter chaff. Manor accounts record payments for threshing and winnowing, often calculated per quarter of grain. Historians use these records to estimate the amount of labour required. Illustrations in manuscripts also show threshers at work. Even so, threshing was usually done in a barn with open doors to provide light and a draught. The straw was kept for animal bedding, thatch and fodder.
===
IMG: washing-clothes-wooden-tub-hearth-steam-winter | A medieval peasant woman washing clothes in a wooden tub by the hearth in winter, steam rising, clothes drying by the fire
HEAD: Household tasks such as washing clothes and fetching water were more difficult in winter.
BODY: Daily chores continued throughout the cold months. Water had to be fetched from a stream, spring or well, often by women and children. In freezing weather, ice sometimes had to be broken first. Clothes and linen were washed in wooden tubs, using water heated on the fire. Lye, made from wood ash and water, was used as a cleaning agent. Washing was hard work, so it was done less often in winter. Historians note that medieval people valued cleanliness, washing their hands and faces daily, even if full bathing was rare. Advice books of the period stress washing before meals. Linen undergarments were changed and washed more often than woollen outer clothes. Archaeologists find wooden buckets, tubs and bowls preserved in waterlogged sites. Even so, many tasks depended on daylight, which was short in winter. Families organised their work to make the best use of the few hours of light.
===
IMG: child-fetching-water-icy-stream-wooden-bucket | A medieval child breaking ice on a stream with a stick to fill a wooden bucket, frosty willows, cold morning
HEAD: Children in peasant families began helping with household and farm work from an early age.
BODY: Children in medieval villages took on responsibilities while still young. Small children fetched water, collected firewood, fed hens and minded younger siblings. Older children helped with herding, scaring birds from crops and other farm tasks. By their early teens, many worked almost like adults. Coroners' records, which describe accidental deaths, provide some of the best evidence for children's lives. Historians such as Barbara Hanawalt have used them to show the tasks children performed and the places they went. These records mention children falling into streams or wells while fetching water, for example. Even so, children also played. Archaeologists have found toys and games at medieval sites. Writers of the period describe children making toys and playing in the fields. Parents taught practical skills by example. Boys usually learned farming tasks, and girls learned household work, though there was some overlap. Childhood was short by modern standards, but it was not without joy.
===
IMG: snowdrops-churchyard-january-grey-light | Snowdrops flowering in a medieval churchyard under bare trees in grey January light, an old stone church behind
HEAD: January and February were often the coldest and most difficult months of the medieval year.
BODY: After the celebrations of Christmas and the new year, winter continued. January and February were typically the coldest months in England. Days were still short, food stores were shrinking, and fuel supplies were running low. Records from monasteries and manors note severe frosts in some years, including freezing rivers. Historians use such records to reconstruct past weather. Even so, the turning of the year brought some signs of change. The days grew slowly longer. In sheltered places, early flowers such as snowdrops began to appear, although historians believe that snowdrops may have been introduced to Britain later than the medieval period. Hazel catkins and the first green shoots showed that spring was not far away. For farmers, these signs were watched carefully. Calendars of the period listed the tasks of each month, and the coming of spring brought new work. The darkest part of the year, however, was gradually coming to an end.

TOPIC: Winter evenings and a girl's day
===
IMG: family-gathered-fire-winter-evening-stories-1300 | A medieval peasant family gathered around the fire on a winter evening listening to stories, faces glowing, rushlights, peaceful
HEAD: Storytelling, singing and games were common ways to pass long winter evenings.
BODY: Winter evenings were long, and families spent many hours by the fire. In addition to spinning, mending and carving, people told stories. These included tales of saints, local legends, ghost stories and humorous tales. Many such stories were passed down orally and were written down only much later. Preachers used short stories, called exempla, in their sermons, and some of these were also told at home. Ballads and songs were sung. Historians know that tales about Robin Hood were popular by the fourteenth century, since a famous poem of the 1370s mentions them. Riddles and proverbs were also part of everyday entertainment. Even so, much of this oral culture is lost. Only fragments survive in written sources. Yet it was clearly important to people. Stories connected them to their history and their faith. Storytelling also taught moral lessons and passed the time pleasantly during the darkest months of the year.
===
IMG: saint-cuthbert-otters-island-shore-legend-painting | A gentle painting-like scene of a medieval monk on a rocky island shore at dawn with otters warming his feet, seabirds, calm sea
HEAD: Saint Cuthbert was a much-loved saint in northern England, and many stories were told about him.
BODY: Cuthbert was a monk and bishop who lived in the seventh century in Northumbria. He spent time as a hermit on the island of Inner Farne, off the coast of Northumberland. After his death, he became one of the most popular saints in the north. His shrine at Durham Cathedral attracted many pilgrims. One famous story, recorded by the scholar Bede, tells that Cuthbert spent nights praying in the cold sea. When he came out, two otters dried his feet with their fur and warmed them with their breath. This story was often told and depicted in art. Historians study Bede's writings as important sources for early English history. Even so, they distinguish between historical facts and later legends. In medieval villages, stories of saints were told for instruction and comfort. They gave examples of faith, kindness and endurance. For people living through a northern winter, such stories had special meaning.

IMG: men-playing-nine-mens-morris-board-firelight | Two medieval peasant men playing nine men's morris on a scratched wooden board by firelight, children watching
HEAD: Nine men's morris was a popular board game in medieval England.
BODY: Nine men's morris is a strategy game for two players. Each player has nine pieces, which are placed on a board of three squares, one inside the other, connected by lines. Players try to form rows of three pieces, which allows them to remove an opponent's piece. The game was widely played in medieval England. Boards have been found scratched into stones in churches, castles and monasteries, sometimes on benches or window ledges. Outdoor versions were also cut into turf. Historians believe that the game is very old, and versions of it were known in the Roman world. Other games were also popular, such as dice, draughts and, among the wealthy, chess. The Church sometimes disapproved of gambling with dice, and laws tried to restrict it. Even so, games remained a common pastime. On winter evenings, simple boards and pieces of stone, wood or bone provided entertainment for adults and children alike.
===
IMG: girl-waking-before-dawn-cottage-embers-cold | An eleven-year-old medieval peasant girl waking before dawn in a cottage, her mother blowing the embers into flame
HEAD: In winter, peasant days usually began before dawn with the rekindling of the fire.
BODY: Medieval rural life followed the rhythm of daylight. In winter, people woke before dawn to make the most of the short day. The first task was usually to uncover the embers of the fire, which had been covered with ashes overnight, and add fuel. Women and girls often did this. Water was fetched, animals fed and bread or porridge prepared. Historians use descriptions in literature, sermons and household books to understand daily routines. A well-known poem of the period, describing a poor family's hardships, mentions women rising at night to rock cradles, card and spin. Even so, routines varied with the season and the household. In the coldest weeks, outdoor work was reduced, and more time was spent on indoor tasks. Church bells marked the hours for prayer and helped people keep track of time. The pattern of each day was shaped by light, weather and the needs of family and animals.
===
IMG: girl-spinning-wool-fireside-little-sister-playing | A medieval peasant girl spinning wool by the fire beside her grandmother while her little sister plays on the floor
HEAD: Girls learned spinning and other household skills from their mothers and grandmothers.
BODY: Spinning was so closely associated with women that the word spinster originally meant a woman who spun. Girls learned to spin at an early age, often by about seven or eight. They practised with a spindle and distaff, gradually producing thinner and more even thread. Medieval illustrations often show women spinning while caring for children or watching animals. Historians note that the demand for thread was very high, since weaving required large quantities. Some estimates suggest that several spinners were needed to keep one weaver supplied. Girls also learned to cook, brew, make butter and cheese, and care for poultry. These skills prepared them for running a household. Even so, girls also helped in the fields and with livestock when needed. Younger children played nearby while older girls worked. Archaeologists find spindle whorls at almost every medieval village site, showing how common spinning was. The skill passed from one generation of women to the next.
===
IMG: village-children-playing-chase-haystacks-winter-sun | Medieval village children playing chase around haystacks in winter sunshine, laughing
HEAD: Medieval children played games such as tag, hide and seek and ball games.
BODY: Evidence from art, literature and archaeology shows that medieval children enjoyed many games. They played chasing games, hide and seek and ball games. They used hoops, spinning tops, whistles and dolls. Some toys were made at home from wood, cloth or bone. Others, made of metal, were sold at markets. Archaeologists have found small metal toys, such as figures and miniature pots, in London and other towns. Writers describe children building small houses, making mud pies and playing at being adults. In winter, they slid on ice and played in the snow. Bone skates, made from the leg bones of horses or cattle, have been found at medieval sites. Historians note that children's play was valued, even though they also had to work. Some medieval writers argued that play was natural and good for children's growth. Even so, play was fitted around chores and daylight. On bright winter days, children spent time outdoors whenever they could.
===
IMG: mother-daughter-kneading-bread-trough-firelight | A medieval mother and daughter kneading bread dough in a wooden trough by firelight
HEAD: Baking bread was a regular household task, and dough was kneaded in a wooden trough.
BODY: Bread was made from flour, water, salt and a leavening agent. Leaven came from a piece of the previous batch of dough, or from the yeast left over from brewing ale. The dough was mixed and kneaded in a wooden trough, then left to rise. It was baked in an oven, which could be the lord's oven or a small household oven, or on a flat stone near the fire. Historians note that the frequency of baking depended on fuel supplies and the size of the household. Larger households might bake once a week. Bread was a central part of every meal. Wills and inventories often list troughs and other baking equipment. Manuscripts show women kneading dough and placing loaves in ovens. Even so, many people bought bread from bakers in towns, where strict laws controlled weight and price. In the countryside, home baking remained common. The smell of baking bread was a familiar part of peasant life.
===
IMG: girl-holding-blue-ribbon-firelight-grandmother | A medieval peasant girl holding a small blue ribbon by firelight, her grandmother smiling beside her
HEAD: Small decorations, such as ribbons or brooches, were valued possessions for peasant families.
BODY: Although peasants owned few possessions, they valued small items of decoration. Ribbons, simple brooches, belt buckles and beads were bought at markets or made at home. Archaeologists find many such items at village sites, including pins, buckles and small jewellery of copper alloy or pewter. Some were decorated with simple patterns. Religious items, such as small badges from pilgrimage sites, were also popular. These were worn on clothing as a sign of devotion. Historians use such finds to understand the tastes and wealth of ordinary people. Even small items show that peasants cared about appearance and personal expression. Colours were important, too. Blue dye from woad was common and relatively affordable. Even so, the Church and sometimes the law tried to limit displays of luxury by lower classes. Small decorations were treasured and passed down in families. They brought colour and individuality to plain clothing and to the dark winter months.
