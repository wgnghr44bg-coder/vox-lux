TOPIC: Food for the long winter
BRIDGE: The cart is unloaded, and the family is home again. So what would you have eaten here, all winter long?
===
IMG: pottage-cauldron-hearth-wooden-spoon-steam | A large iron pot of thick pottage bubbling over a medieval hearth fire, a wooden spoon, steam rising, warm light
HEAD: In the winter, the most important food for the family is pottage.
BODY: Pottage is a thick soup, or stew, cooked in a great iron or clay pot, hanging from a chain over the fire, or standing on three little legs in the embers. You'd eat it every single day, and you wouldn't mind. Agnes keeps it simmering all day long. And adds whatever she has to it: dried peas and beans, oats or barley, onions and leeks and cabbages from the garden, herbs. And a little bacon or salt pork, for flavour. Every day, a little more is added to the pot. And a little is eaten, so that the pottage goes on and on, all through the winter, changing a little from day to day, but never quite running out. Many centuries later, English children would sing a little rhyme about it: Pease porridge hot, pease porridge cold, pease porridge in the pot, nine days old. The family eats the pottage from wooden bowls, with wooden spoons, sitting around the table by the fire. It's warm and thick and filling. And on a cold winter's day, after hours of work in the frost, there's nothing better. With the pottage, there's bread. The bread of the peasants isn't white, like the bread of the lord. It's dark and heavy, made of a mixture of rye and barley, or wheat and rye together, which is called maslin. You'd find a little more in your bowl on feast days. And sometimes, in a bad year, of peas and beans ground into flour.
===
IMG: dark-rye-loaves-baking-clay-oven | Dark round rye loaves being taken out of a clay village oven by a medieval baker, warm glow
HEAD: Agnes makes the dough in a wooden trough, and the family's loaves are baked in the village oven, which belongs to the lord, and the baker takes a small part of the dough as payment.
BODY: Or, in some houses, she bakes flat loaves on a hot stone in the embers of the hearth. You'd hear her kneading the dough with a soft, steady rhythm. A loaf of good bread, with a piece of cheese and an onion, is a fine meal. And there's ale. Everyone drinks ale, morning, noon and night, the men and the women and the children. Water from the stream and the well isn't always safe to drink. And ale is safer, and it's also food: thick and nourishing, made from barley.
===
IMG: woman-brewing-ale-wooden-tub-steam-barley | A medieval peasant woman brewing ale in a large wooden tub with steam rising, sacks of barley malt beside her, cottage interior
HEAD: Agnes brews it herself, every week or so.
BODY: She soaks the barley in water until it sprouts, then dries it by the fire, to make malt. You'd smell the sweet, warm scent of malt drifting through the house. She grinds the malt, and soaks it in hot water in a big wooden tub, and drains off the sweet liquid. And adds yeast, and leaves it to work for a few days, until it's ready. The ale is weak and cloudy and a little sweet, and it doesn't keep long, so it must be brewed often. When a woman in the village has brewed more ale than her family needs, she hangs a branch of green leaves over her door, as a sign. And her neighbours come and buy a jug of it. The family also has the milk and the butter and the cheese from their cow and their sheep, when they are giving milk, which is mostly in the spring and summer. Agnes makes the butter by churning the cream in a tall wooden churn, and salts it, so that it will keep. She makes the cheese by warming the milk, adding rennet, and pressing the curds in a wooden mould. And the cheeses are wrapped in cloth and stored on a shelf, and eaten through the winter.
===
IMG: hard-cheeses-shelf-cloth-wrapped-dim-light | Hard cheeses wrapped in cloth on a wooden shelf in a dim medieval cottage, with clay jugs and wooden bowls
HEAD: In the winter, the cow gives very little milk, so the cheese and the butter from the summer are very precious.
BODY: There are eggs, too, from the hens, though they lay very few in the dark winter months. You'd learn to make every drop last. There's fish, sometimes, from the millpond, or from the stream: eels and trout and roach. On Fridays, and in Lent, and on the many other fast days of the Church, nobody is allowed to eat meat. And so the family eats fish, if they can get it, or salted herring, bought from a pedlar who comes from the coast. And there are rabbits and hares and birds in the fields and the woods. But they belong to the lord, and the villagers aren't allowed to hunt them. Sometimes, on a dark night, a hungry man might set a little snare in the hedge. And hope that the reeve doesn't find out. Meat is rare for the peasant family. They eat it mostly in the winter, from the pig that was slaughtered at Martinmas. And at the great feasts, like Christmas and Easter. The food of the peasants is plain and simple. But it's, mostly, healthy, with plenty of grains and peas and beans and vegetables. Archaeologists who have studied the people of Wharram Percy have found that they worked very hard all their lives. Could you go a whole winter without fresh milk? And that in good years, most of them had enough to eat.
===
IMG: family-supper-candle-rushlight-bread-ale-jug | A medieval peasant family at supper with wooden bowls of pottage, bread and a jug of ale by the light of a rushlight and the fire
HEAD: At the end of the day, as the family sits by the fire with their bowls of pottage, and their bread, and their cups of ale, they say a short grace, and thank God for the food.
BODY: And they mean it. You'd feel your eyes growing heavy as you ate.
===
TOPIC: Wood, fire and warm clothes
BRIDGE: The pottage is bubbling, and the bread is on the board. But first, you'll need to keep warm.
===
IMG: hearth-fire-glowing-embers-winter-night-cottage | A hearth fire glowing in the middle of a dark medieval cottage on a winter night, embers and soft flames, rafters in shadow
HEAD: In the winter, the fire is the heart of the house.
BODY: It burns on the hearth in the middle of the floor, day and night, from the autumn until the spring. You'd never let it go out, not even at night. It's never allowed to go out. If it did, it would be a great trouble to light it again, with a flint and a piece of steel, striking sparks onto a little bundle of dry tinder. And blowing it gently into a flame. So it's kept burning, always. At night, before the family goes to sleep, Agnes covers the glowing embers of the fire with ashes, to keep them alive, but quiet and safe, until the morning. She uses a special cover for this, a round dome of pottery, with holes in it, which is placed over the embers, so that no sparks can fly out and catch the straw on the floor, or the thatch of the roof. In French, it's called a couvre-feu, which means cover the fire. And from that, we have our word curfew. In the morning, she lifts off the cover, and blows on the embers. And adds a few small sticks, and the fire springs back to life, and the house is warm again. The fire is used for everything. It heats the house. It cooks the food. It heats the water for washing and brewing. It dries the wet clothes and boots of the family when they come in from the fields. It smokes the bacon hanging from the rafters. And it gives light, in the long, dark winter evenings.
===
IMG: wet-boots-drying-by-fire-steam-cottage | Wet leather boots and a woollen cloak drying by a medieval hearth fire, steam rising, cosy
HEAD: The family sits around it, close together, with their backs to the cold, and their faces and hands warmed by the glow.
BODY: The old grandmother sits closest of all, on her stool, with her shawl around her shoulders. Finding enough fuel for the fire is one of the most important tasks of the year. The villagers have certain rights to collect wood in the lord's wood. They are allowed to take the fallen branches and the dead wood, which they can pull down with a hook, or knock down with a crook. This is where our saying by hook or by crook comes from, people say. They also cut wood from the coppices: places in the wood where hazel and willow and ash trees are cut down to the ground every few years. And grow up again from the stumps in many thin, straight poles. The poles are used for fences and wattle and tool handles. And the smaller branches are tied in bundles, called faggots, for the fire. In some villages, where there isn't much wood, the people burn other things too. They dig up blocks of turf from the moors, or peat from the bogs, and dry them in stacks. And burn them on the fire, where they glow red, and give off a sweet, earthy smoke. They burn gorse and bracken and heather from the hills. And dried cow dung, and the straw and the stubble from the fields. Nothing that will burn is wasted. Light is precious in the winter. The days are very short in the north of England in December, with the sun rising late and setting early. And the evenings are long and dark. Candles are expensive. Good wax candles are only for the church and the lord's hall. Can you feel the warmth on your face? The peasants use something much simpler.
===
IMG: rushlight-holder-flickering-dark-cottage | A small iron rushlight holder with a burning rush giving a small flickering flame in a dark medieval cottage
HEAD: In the summer and autumn, the children gather tall green rushes from the edge of the stream.
BODY: Agnes peels away most of the green skin, leaving the white pith inside, and dries them. You'd see them coming back from the stream with armfuls of green rushes. And then dips them in the melted fat from the kitchen, and lets them harden. These are called rushlights. Clipped into a little iron holder, a rushlight burns with a small, steady, flickering flame for perhaps half an hour. On winter evenings, by the light of the fire and a rushlight or two, the family carries on with the little jobs that can be done indoors. John mends his tools, and makes new handles for them, and repairs the harness for the oxen. He carves spoons and bowls, and little toys for the children. Will helps him, and learns. Agnes and Alice and the grandmother spin and sew and mend. The little boy, Tom, sits by the fire, playing with a wooden horse, and watching the flames, until his eyes grow heavy. On the coldest nights, the family moves as close to the fire as they can. They pile more blankets and sheepskins on their beds. They wear their hoods and their woollen stockings to bed. The baby sleeps between her parents, wrapped up warm. The dogs crawl in among the blankets. You'd learn to dip them in fat and make your own little lights. And the warmth of the oxen and the cow, at the other end of the house, drifts through the darkness.
===
IMG: family-huddled-blankets-sheepskins-embers-glow | A medieval family huddled under blankets and sheepskins close to the glowing embers on a very cold night, peaceful
HEAD: Outside, the wind howls over the hills, and the frost settles on the thatch, and the stars glitter in the black sky.
BODY: But inside, under the thick roof of straw, by the glowing embers, the family sleeps warm, and safe, until the morning. You'd pull your blanket a little closer and listen.
===
IMG: shepherd-sheep-chalk-downs-woollen-cloak | A medieval shepherd in a woollen hooded cloak with a crook and a dog watching his flock on rolling chalk downs, cold grey sky
HEAD: To keep warm in winter, the people of Wharram Percy depend, above all, on wool.
BODY: The village keeps hundreds of sheep, on the rough pastures of the hills. You'd wear almost everything you owned on the coldest days. In the summer, they are sheared, and their thick fleeces are washed and dried. Some of the wool is sold, to merchants who come from the towns. And who send it to the great cloth-making cities of Flanders and Italy. But much of it's kept, and turned into clothes for the family. Turning a fleece into a warm winter coat takes a great deal of work. And it's almost all done by the women and the girls. First, the wool must be combed or carded, to clean it and straighten the fibres. Then it must be spun into yarn. Agnes and Alice and the grandmother spin all through the year, whenever their hands are free: in the evenings by the fire, sitting in the doorway in the summer, even walking along the lanes, with a distaff tucked under one arm, holding the cloud of wool. And a spindle turning beside them. It takes many hours of spinning to make enough yarn for a single garment. Then the yarn is woven into cloth, on a loom. In some houses in the village, there's a big upright wooden loom, standing against the wall, with the threads hanging down, weighted with little rings of clay. And the weaver walks back and forth in front of it, passing the thread through from side to side. You'd be grateful for every sheep in the valley. In other villages, there's a weaver who weaves cloth for everyone.
===
IMG: upright-loom-clay-weights-woman-weaving-light | A medieval woman weaving on an upright wooden loom with clay weights hanging from the threads, light from a doorway
HEAD: The cloth is thick and rough, in the natural colours of the sheep, white and grey and brown and black, or dyed with plants: brown from walnut husks, yellow from weld, blue from woad, red from madder root.
BODY: Then it's fulled, beaten and soaked in water, to make it thicker and warmer, and keep out the rain. You'd find it itchy at first, but you'd be very glad of it in January. From the cloth, the family makes their clothes. The men wear a long woollen tunic, reaching to the knees, with a belt around the waist. And woollen hose, like long stockings, on their legs. Over the tunic, in winter, they wear a thick cloak, or a coat lined with sheepskin. On their heads, they wear a hood, which covers the head and the neck and the shoulders, with a long tail hanging down the back, which can be wrapped around the neck like a scarf.
===
IMG: peasant-man-hood-cloak-snowy-lane | A medieval peasant man in a woollen hood with a long tail and a thick cloak walking along a snowy village lane
HEAD: The women wear a long woollen dress, reaching to the ankles, over a linen shift, with a woollen cloak or shawl, and a linen kerchief over their hair, and a hood in cold weather.
BODY: The children are dressed like little copies of their parents. You'd feel the scratchy wool against your arms. Underneath, next to the skin, people wear linen, made from flax. The family grows a little flax in their garden. In the summer, it has pretty blue flowers. When it's ripe, they pull it up. And soak it in the stream to soften it, and beat it to separate the fibres. And comb it, and spin it into thread, and weave it into linen, which is cool and soft and strong. On their hands, in winter, people wear mittens of wool or leather. On their feet, they wear leather shoes, or ankle boots, made by the village shoemaker, or by the family themselves. They are simple and soft, stitched together from a few pieces of leather, without a heel. And they don't keep out the water very well. You'd be glad of every layer when the wind blew. In the mud of winter, people often wear wooden pattens underneath them, like little platforms, to lift them out of the mud.
===
IMG: leather-shoes-wooden-pattens-doorway-mud | Simple medieval leather shoes and wooden pattens by a cottage doorway with mud outside, close view
HEAD: In the evening, everyone's shoes are put by the fire to dry, and the house smells of warm wet leather.
BODY: Clothes are very precious, because they take so long to make. You'd line your shoes up by the fire with everyone else's. A family might have only one or two sets of clothes each: one for working, and one for Sundays and feast days. Clothes are mended again and again, and patched, and handed down from parents to children, and from older children to younger ones. When they are finally worn out, the cloth is cut up and used for rags. And for patching other clothes, and for padding, and nothing is wasted. In a will of the time, a peasant might leave his best tunic to his son. And his cloak to his brother, and his wife's best dress to his daughter, as treasured possessions. In the winter, when the work outside is less, Agnes and Alice spend many hours sewing and mending. They sit by the fire, with their needles of bone or iron, and their thread of linen or wool. And sew new tunics for the boys, who are growing fast. And mend the holes in John's hose, and patch the elbows of the cloaks. The grandmother spins, and hums an old song. The baby sleeps in her wooden cradle, rocked now and then by someone's foot.

TOPIC: The animals in winter
BRIDGE: Your woollen hood is pulled up, and your shoes are drying by the fire. Now let's go and see the animals at the other end of the house.
===
IMG: oxen-byre-hay-breath-steaming-lantern-dawn | Two red and brown oxen with curved horns in a byre at dawn, breath steaming, lantern light, hay in a rack
HEAD: In the winter, the animals need as much care as the people.
BODY: The most precious of all are the oxen. You'd be up before dawn to feed them. Without them, there can be no ploughing in the spring, and without ploughing, there can be no harvest. John has two oxen of his own, big, slow, gentle beasts, red and brown, with great curved horns and soft dark eyes, called Brok and Lyard. He shares them with his neighbours, who have oxen of their own, to make up a full plough team. In the winter, the oxen live in the byre at the end of the house, on a thick bed of straw. Every morning, before dawn, John goes down to them with a lantern, and feeds them hay from the loft. And a little straw, and sometimes some oats or peas, if they are working. He fetches water for them from the stream, in a wooden bucket, and breaks the ice on it if it's frozen. He cleans out their stalls, and spreads fresh straw. And puts the dung on the heap in the yard, where it will rot down into manure for the fields in the spring. He talks to them as he works, quietly, and rubs their noses, and checks their feet. They know his voice, and they turn their great heads to watch him. The cow lives in the byre too. She is a small, brown, sturdy cow, who gives milk in the spring and summer, and less in the winter. In the autumn, she was taken to the bull, and she will have a calf in the spring. Agnes looks after her, and milks her, and talks to her, and gives her the best of the hay.
===
IMG: woman-milking-cow-byre-wooden-pail-warm | A medieval peasant woman milking a small brown cow into a wooden pail in a warm byre, children watching
HEAD: The children love the cow, and sit with her in the evenings, and lean against her warm side, and listen to her chewing.
BODY: The sheep stay outside most of the winter. You'd sit beside her in the straw, feeling her warm side rise and fall. They are hardy animals, with thick woolly fleeces, and they can survive the cold and the rain. The village shepherd takes them out every day onto the hills, to graze on the short grass and the stubble of the fields. And brings them back in the evening to the village fold, a big enclosure of wattle hurdles, where they are safe from foxes.
===
IMG: village-shepherd-dog-flock-snowy-hills-dusk | A medieval shepherd and his dog driving a flock of sheep down snowy hills toward a village fold at dusk
HEAD: The shepherd is one of the most important people in the village.
BODY: He knows every sheep, and its mother, and its lambs. You'd see him out on the cold hills, with his crook and his dog. He lives with his flock almost all the time. And in the worst weather, in the lambing season, he sleeps out with them on the hills, in a little hut on wheels, or a shelter of hurdles and straw. In heavy snow, the sheep are in danger. They can be buried in the drifts, and lost. When the snow comes, the shepherd and the men of the village go out onto the hills to find them. And dig them out, and drive them down into the valley, where there's shelter. The shepherd's dogs help to find them, sniffing at the snow until they find a buried sheep, and barking. The pigs that were kept back from the slaughter at Martinmas spend the winter in a little sty in the yard, made of wattle and thatch, or in the byre. They eat whatever scraps there are: the peelings from the vegetables, the husks from the grain, the whey from the cheese, the leftovers from the pottage. You'd always know where he was by the barking of his dog. In the spring, the sow will have piglets, and the circle of the year will begin again.
===
IMG: pig-sty-wattle-yard-frost-boy-feeding-scraps | A small boy feeding scraps to a pig in a wattle sty in a frosty medieval cottage yard
HEAD: The hens live in the house too, in the winter, perching on the rafters, or in a little coop by the door.
BODY: They lay very few eggs in the dark months, but the family keeps them, for the spring. You'd hear them clucking sleepily up in the rafters. The geese wander about the village, and the children chase them home in the evening. One of the hardest things in the winter is making the hay last. The hay was cut and dried in the summer, in the meadows by the stream. And brought in and stored in the loft and in stacks in the yard. It must last until the grass begins to grow again, in April or May. If the winter is long, and the spring is late, the hay may run out. Then the animals go hungry, and grow thin and weak, and the oxen may be too weak to plough. So John and Agnes watch the hay carefully, all through the winter. And measure out a little every day, and hope that the spring will come early. On a cold winter night, before he goes to bed, John always goes down to the byre with a rushlight, to check on the animals one last time. The oxen are lying down in the straw, chewing slowly, with their eyes half closed. The cow is standing, swinging her tail. The hens are asleep on the rafters, with their heads tucked under their wings. Everything is quiet and warm and peaceful, and smells of hay and animals.
===
IMG: sleeping-oxen-straw-byre-rushlight-quiet | Oxen lying down asleep in straw in a medieval byre lit by a single rushlight, hens on the rafters, very peaceful
HEAD: John stands there for a moment, in the soft light, listening to them breathe.
BODY: I've always found that a very peaceful moment. Stand beside him, and you'll hear it too. Then he goes back to the fire, and covers the embers, and lies down beside his family, and sleeps.

TOPIC: Christmas and the new year
BRIDGE: The cow is chewing quietly in the dark. And in the darkest weeks of all, you'd see something wonderful coming.
===
IMG: advent-village-church-frosty-morning-bell | A small medieval stone church on a frosty December morning, the bell ringing, villagers in cloaks walking toward it
HEAD: In the darkest weeks of the winter, there comes the greatest feast of the year: Christmas.
BODY: It's preceded by Advent, the four weeks before Christmas, which is a time of waiting and preparing, and of fasting. You'd feel the whole village wake up with excitement. During Advent, nobody eats meat, and the church is quiet and plain, and the priest wears purple. But in the last days before Christmas, the village bustles with preparations. The women clean their houses, and sweep out the old rushes from the floors. And lay down fresh ones, mixed with sweet-smelling herbs. They bake bread and cakes, and brew extra ale, strong and dark, for the feast. The children go out into the woods and the hedges to gather greenery: holly with its red berries. And ivy, and bay, and branches of yew and fir, to decorate the houses and the church. In the church, the priest and the villagers decorate the walls and the screen with green branches. And set out candles on the altar, more candles than at any other time of the year. On Christmas Eve, the men drag a great log into the house, from the wood: the Yule log. It's the biggest log they can find, often the trunk of a whole tree. And it's pushed into the fire, end first. And lit from a piece of last year's log, which has been kept carefully all year. It's meant to burn, slowly, through all the twelve days of Christmas.
===
IMG: yule-log-dragged-into-cottage-hearth-family | A medieval peasant family dragging a great yule log into their cottage toward the hearth on Christmas Eve, holly and ivy on the walls
HEAD: As it burns, the family gathers around it, and drinks a cup of ale, and makes a wish for the coming year.
BODY: At midnight, the whole village walks down to the church, in the darkness, with lanterns and torches, for the first Mass of Christmas. You'd make a wish too, staring into the flames. The church is full, and glowing with candles, and decorated with holly and ivy. The bell rings out across the frosty valley. The priest, in his white and gold vestments, says the Mass. And the villagers kneel on the cold stone floor, in their best clothes. And listen to the story of the birth of Jesus, in a stable, among the animals, with the shepherds coming down from the hills. The villagers understand that story very well. They are shepherds and farmers themselves. They know about stables, and animals, and cold nights on the hills. On Christmas Day, there's a great feast. In many villages, the lord of the manor gives a Christmas dinner for the villagers, in his hall. Each family brings its own bread and its own bowl and cup. And a bundle of firewood for the fire, and sits at long tables in the great hall. And eats as much as they can: roast meat and bread, pies and puddings, and ale, as much as they want. In return, the villagers give the lord a gift of hens, or eggs, at Christmas.
===
IMG: manor-hall-christmas-feast-villagers-long-tables-fire | Medieval villagers feasting at long tables in a manor hall at Christmas, a great fire, roast meat, bread and ale, holly decorations
HEAD: If the lord isn't there, the families feast in their own houses, with a joint of pork or a goose, roasted over the fire, and fresh bread, and cheese, and strong ale, and the neighbours visit each other, and share what they have.
BODY: Christmas lasts for twelve days, from Christmas Day until Twelfth Night, on the fifth of January. You'd smell roasting pork from every chimney. It's a time of rest, and feasting, and games. Nobody works in the fields, except to feed the animals. The villagers visit each other, and eat and drink, and play games. And sing carols, which in those days were songs that people danced to in a ring, holding hands.
===
IMG: villagers-ring-dance-carol-snow-lanterns | Medieval villagers dancing in a ring holding hands and singing a carol in the snow by lantern light
HEAD: There are mummers, too: groups of young men from the village, who dress up in strange costumes, with masks and paper hats and blackened faces, and go from house to house in the evenings, acting out little plays and silly dances, with mock fights and a great deal of noise and laughter.
BODY: Afterwards, they are given ale and cakes, and they go on to the next house. You'd laugh at their strange masks and silly voices. On Twelfth Night, there's one last feast. A great cake is baked, with a bean hidden inside it. Whoever finds the bean in their slice is the King of the Bean. And rules over the feast for the evening, giving silly orders that everyone must obey. There's wassailing, too: the villagers go out to the orchards, in the darkness, with lanterns and cups of hot spiced ale. And sing to the apple trees, and pour ale on their roots, to wish them a good harvest in the coming year. Waes hael, they call to each other, which means be well, in the old English language. And they answer: drinc hael, drink well. And then, the next morning, Christmas is over. The Yule log has burned down to ashes, and a little piece of it's kept, to light next year's log. The greenery is taken down from the walls, and burned. The last of the Christmas ale is drunk.
===
IMG: plough-monday-decorated-plough-young-men-village | Young medieval men dragging a plough decorated with ribbons through a village lane in January, laughing, villagers at their doors
HEAD: The first Monday after Twelfth Night is called Plough Monday.
BODY: On that day, the young men of the village decorate a plough with ribbons. You'd see the young men dragging a plough through the village, singing. And drag it around the village, from house to house, singing and dancing and shouting. And asking for money or food or ale. The money is used to pay for candles for the church, to bless the ploughs for the year. If someone refuses to give, it's said, the young men plough up the ground in front of his door, as a joke. After Plough Monday, the real work of the new year begins. In January and February, there isn't much that can be done in the fields. The ground is often frozen hard, or covered in snow, or so wet that the ploughs can't go into it. But there's still plenty of work to do, indoors and out. The men mend the hedges and the fences around the closes and the gardens, weaving new hazel stakes into the wattle. And cutting back the old growth with a billhook. They dig out the ditches, so that the water can drain away from the fields. They mend the walls of the houses and the barns, and patch the thatch where the wind has torn it. The most important winter work of all is threshing. All through the winter, a little at a time, the sheaves of wheat and barley and oats that were stored in the barn after the harvest are threshed, to separate the grain from the straw.
===
IMG: two-men-threshing-flails-barn-floor-dust-light | Two medieval peasants threshing grain with wooden flails on a barn floor between open doors, dust rising in beams of light
HEAD: The threshing is done on the floor of the barn, between the two great doors, which are opened on either side so that the wind blows through.
BODY: The sheaves are laid out on the floor. You'd hear the steady thud of the flails from across the valley. And the men beat them with flails: two sticks, joined together with a leather strap, one long, which is the handle. And one short, which swings around and strikes the grain. Thump, thump, thump, all day long, in a steady rhythm, while the dust rises in the light from the doors. Then the grain must be winnowed, to blow away the chaff, the light, dry husks. On a windy day, the threshed grain is tossed up into the air with a wide, flat basket, called a winnowing fan, in the doorway of the barn. And the wind blows the chaff away, and the heavy grain falls back down. Or the grain is poured slowly from a height, and the wind does the same. The clean grain is put into sacks, and stored, ready to be taken to the mill, to be ground into flour. The straw is used for thatching, and for bedding for the animals, and for stuffing mattresses. The chaff is fed to the animals, or put in the pillows. The women, too, have their winter work. There's the spinning and the weaving and the sewing, which never stops. There's the brewing and the baking and the cooking. There are the animals to feed and milk and care for. There are the children to look after. There's the washing, which in the winter is hard and cold work, done in a wooden tub with water heated over the fire. You'd see the chaff drifting out of the doors like snow. And the clothes hung to dry inside, by the hearth, steaming.

HEAD: On fine winter days, the women gather in a neighbour's house, to spin and sew together, and talk, and share the news of the village.

They help with the threshing, gathering up the straw. You'd learn all the village news in a single afternoon. They mind the baby.

TOPIC: Winter evenings and a girl's day
BRIDGE: The feasting is over, and the long weeks of January stretch ahead. How would you have filled those dark evenings?
===
IMG: family-gathered-fire-winter-evening-stories-1300 | A medieval peasant family gathered around the fire on a winter evening listening to stories, faces glowing, rushlights, peaceful
HEAD: The winter evenings are long, and the family spends them together, by the fire.
BODY: As soon as it's dark, which in December and January is around four o'clock in the afternoon, the work outside must stop. You'd sit close to the fire, with the whole family around you. The animals are fed and settled for the night. The door is closed and barred. The shutters are fastened. And the family gathers around the hearth, in the warm, flickering light of the fire. There's supper first: a bowl of pottage, a piece of bread, a cup of ale. Then, while the grownups go on with their quiet work, spinning and mending and carving, the talk begins. They talk about the day, and the animals, and the neighbours, and the news. They talk about the past, and the old days, and people who have died. They talk about the future, and the spring, and the harvest. And then, very often, someone tells a story. The grandmother is the best storyteller in the family. She knows a great many stories, which she heard from her own mother and grandmother, long ago, by the same fire. She tells stories about the saints. And the miracles they did: Saint Martin, who cut his cloak in half to give to a beggar in the snow; Saint Nicholas, who secretly gave gold to three poor girls; Saint Cuthbert, who lived on a little island in the sea, among the seabirds and the seals. And was kind to the otters, who came out of the sea to warm his feet.
===

HEAD: She tells stories about giants and dragons and fairies, and the little folk who live in the hollow hills, and steal babies, and sour the milk, and must be left a bowl of cream at night to keep them happy.
BODY: She tells stories about bold outlaws who lived in the greenwood, and outwitted the sheriffs and the rich abbots. You'd listen with wide eyes, half believing every word. She tells stories about the old kings, King Arthur and his knights, and King Alfred, who burned the cakes. The children listen, wide-eyed, sitting on the floor, wrapped in blankets, with their chins on their knees. And the firelight on their faces. Tom, the little boy, likes the stories about dragons best. Alice likes the stories about the fairies. Will, who is fourteen and thinks he is too old for stories, pretends not to listen, while he mends a basket, but he listens anyway. Sometimes, there are riddles. What has a mouth but never speaks, a bed but never sleeps? A river. What goes on four legs in the morning, two legs at noon, and three legs in the evening? A man, who crawls as a baby, walks as a man, and leans on a stick when he is old. The children try to guess, and shout out answers, and laugh. And sometimes, there's singing. The family sings old songs, ballads and carols, songs about love and work and the seasons. Sometimes a neighbour comes in with a pipe or a little drum, and plays, and everyone sings together. There are games, too, played by the fire. There are board games, scratched on a board of wood, or on a flat stone: games like merels, which we call nine men's morris, played with little pebbles or wooden pegs; and draughts; and dice. The men play, and gamble a little, for beans or nuts. The children play knucklebones, tossing little bones in the air and catching them on the back of the hand.

HEAD: On very special evenings, at Christmas, there might be a game of blind man's buff, with everyone laughing and stumbling around the dark house, bumping into the furniture and the animals.
BODY: Then, at last, the evening grows late. The fire burns low. The rushlights flicker out. The children's eyes grow heavy. The baby is already asleep in her cradle. The grandmother yawns. The family says its prayers together, kneeling by the fire: the Our Father, and the Hail Mary. And the Creed, which they know by heart, in Latin, though they don't understand all the words. They ask God and the saints to keep them safe through the night. And to bless their animals, and their fields, and their village. Then they lie down on their beds of straw, close together by the warm embers. And pull their blankets and sheepskins over them. Agnes covers the fire, and the house grows dark, except for a faint red glow from the embers under their cover. The oxen sigh and shift in the byre. The dog turns around three times and settles at Tom's feet. Did you ever play that game as a child? The wind sighs in the thatch.

HEAD: Let's spend one winter's day with Alice, the eleven-year-old daughter of John and Agnes.
BODY: She wakes before dawn, in the dark, in the warm nest of blankets and sheepskins by the hearth, with her little sister curled up against her back. You'll like her, I think. For a moment, she lies still, listening. She can hear her grandmother snoring softly, and the oxen shifting in the byre, and the wind in the thatch. It's very cold outside the blankets. She doesn't want to get up. But her mother is already up, lifting the cover from the embers. And blowing on them, and adding sticks, and the fire is crackling into life. So Alice gets up too, quickly, and pulls on her woollen dress over her shift. And her stockings and her shoes, and wraps her shawl around her shoulders, and goes to help. Her first job is to fetch the water. She takes the two wooden buckets, and goes out into the cold, grey dawn, and down the path to the stream. The grass is white with frost, and her breath steams in the air. At the stream, there's a thin layer of ice at the edges, and she breaks it with a stick. And fills the buckets, and carries them back up the hill, slowly, one in each hand, trying not to spill them. Her hands are red and stinging with cold. Back at the house, the pottage is warming on the fire. And the family eats breakfast together: a bowl of pottage, a piece of bread, a cup of ale. Then her father and Will go out to the barn to thresh, and her mother goes to milk the cow. And Alice's next job is to mind her little sister, and feed the hens. And sweep the floor, and then to sit by the fire and spin.
