Around the year 1300, a small village called Wharram Percy lay in a quiet valley in the Yorkshire Wolds, in the north of England. About a hundred and fifty people lived there, in low houses of timber, clay and thatch. This story follows an ordinary medieval winter in such a village: the food that was stored in autumn, the fires and the warm wool, the animals in the byre, the long evenings, the Christmas feasts, and the slow return of spring. It ends with the village as it is today, a peaceful field of grassy outlines.

Around the year 1300, the village of Wharram Percy lay in a quiet valley in the Yorkshire Wolds. Wharram Percy was a small farming village in the north of England, in a fold of the Yorkshire Wolds, a region of rolling chalk hills. It stood about twenty miles from the city of York. Its houses were low and thatched, set on a slope above a small stream. Around 1300, perhaps a hundred and fifty people lived there, in around thirty households. The surrounding hills were covered with large open fields, divided into long narrow strips farmed by different families. Beyond the fields lay rough pasture for sheep, and a small wood. A stone church, dedicated to Saint Martin, stood near the stream. Historians know a great deal about the village because archaeologists excavated it for more than forty years. It was later abandoned, and its remains lay undisturbed under grass. Even so, in 1300 it was an ordinary, busy community. For its people, the valley was the centre of their world.

[long-pause]

{img:02-small-stone-church-square-tower-stream-willows}

Wharram Percy had a stone church, a priest's house, a mill and a manor house belonging to its lord. The main buildings of the village reflected the structure of medieval rural life. Beside the stream stood the church of Saint Martin, the only large stone building in the village. Near it was the house of the priest. A watermill, driven by a dammed pond, ground the villagers' grain into flour. On higher ground stood the manor house, used by the lord or his officials. The manor was held by members of the Percy family, a powerful noble family of northern England, which gave the village part of its name. The lords rarely lived there and relied on officials to manage the estate. Archaeologists have uncovered the foundations of two manor houses, the mill pond and the church at Wharram Percy. Even so, the lord's presence was felt mainly through rules, rents and labour services. For most villagers, the church and the mill were the buildings they used most often in daily life.

[long-pause]

{img:03-peasant-family-walking-home-fields-dusk-tools}

Most villagers in medieval England rarely travelled far beyond their own district. For many peasants around 1300, the world was quite small. People knew every field, path and stream in their own valley. A trip to the nearest market town was an occasional event, and a journey to a large city such as York might happen rarely, if at all. Manor records and court rolls show that many families stayed in the same village for generations. Historians note, however, that people were not entirely fixed in place. Some moved to nearby villages through marriage, and others went to towns in search of work. Pilgrims, traders and soldiers also travelled. Even so, daily life was shaped by the local landscape and the seasons. Winter was the most difficult time of year. The days were short, the fields bare and the weather often wet, cold and windy. Paths turned to mud. Families relied on what they had stored during the autumn, and on the help of their neighbours.

[long-pause]

{img:04-medieval-longhouse-exterior-thatch-wattle-daub}

Many peasant families lived in longhouses built of timber, earth and thatch. A typical peasant house in northern England around 1300 was long and narrow. It might be about forty or fifty feet long and fifteen feet wide. Each house stood in its own enclosed yard, called a toft, with a garden or small field, called a croft, behind it. The frame was often made of crucks, pairs of large curved timbers that met at the top like the ribs of an upturned boat. Walls were made of woven hazel panels, known as wattle, covered with a mixture of clay, dung and straw, called daub. At Wharram Percy, some houses also had chalk foundations. Roofs were thickly thatched with straw or reeds. Archaeologists found that houses at Wharram Percy were rebuilt many times, often on slightly different sites. This was normal for buildings of wood and earth. Even so, such houses could be warm and practical, and many lasted for a generation or more before being replaced.

[long-pause]

{img:05-longhouse-interior-dim-firelight-smoke-rafters}

A longhouse was usually divided into living space for the family and a byre for the animals. Inside a longhouse, one end was often used as a byre, where livestock were kept in winter. Oxen, a cow and perhaps a few sheep or pigs were housed in stalls, with straw on the floor and a drain running under the wall. The other end was the family's living area. The two parts were separated by a cross passage between opposite doors, or by a low partition. Archaeologists identify byres by features such as drains and the remains of stalls. Keeping animals inside protected them from cold, thieves and wild animals. It also allowed families to watch them closely. Livestock was very valuable, especially oxen, which were needed for ploughing. Historians debate how common longhouses were in different regions, as house types varied across England. Even so, the arrangement shows how closely people and animals lived together in the medieval countryside. The shared roof reflected the importance of livestock to every household.

[long-pause]

{img:06-oxen-cow-byre-straw-stalls-lantern}

Animals kept inside the house helped to warm it during the winter. The animals in the byre gave off a considerable amount of heat. On cold nights, their warmth helped to keep the whole building more comfortable. The family could hear them moving, breathing and chewing in the dark. In the living area, the hearth stood in the middle of the floor. It was usually a flat bed of stones or clay, and the fire burned on it day and night through the winter. There was often no chimney. Smoke rose into the roof space and escaped through the thatch or a small opening. Archaeologists find hearths as patches of burnt clay and stone in excavated houses. The arrangement had disadvantages, including smoke, smells and the risk of fire. Yet it also had practical benefits. Historians who study rural life note that peasants made careful use of every available source of warmth. In winter, animals and fire together made the house a sheltered place.

[long-pause]

{img:07-central-hearth-fire-cooking-pot-family-around}

Smoke from the open hearth blackened the roof, but it also helped preserve food and thatch. With no chimney, smoke filled the upper part of the house and blackened the rafters and the underside of the thatch. It made the air smoky and could make eyes water. Yet it also kept insects away and helped dry and preserve meat and cheese hung from the rafters. It may have helped protect the thatch from rot as well. The floor was made of beaten earth or chalk and was often covered with rushes or straw, which were swept out and renewed regularly. Furniture was simple. Typical items included a trestle table that could be taken down, benches, three-legged stools and a wooden chest for clothes and valuables. Pots, bowls and cooking tools stood near the hearth. Archaeologists at Wharram Percy found pieces of pottery, metal tools and other household objects. Wills and inventories from the period list similar goods. The houses were plain by modern standards, but they were organised for warmth and work.

[long-pause]

{img:08-family-sleeping-straw-mattresses-near-embers}

Peasant families usually slept close to the hearth, on straw mattresses with woollen blankets. At night, family members slept near the fire, often together, for warmth. Beds were usually mattresses filled with straw or chaff, laid on the floor or on simple wooden frames. Blankets of wool and sheepskins provided extra warmth. Wealthier peasants might own a proper bed frame and better bedding, which appear in inventories as valued possessions. The fire was banked with ashes at night to keep it smouldering until morning. Historians point out that privacy was limited, since the whole family shared one main room. Even so, sleeping together made sense in a cold climate. Medieval illustrations sometimes show people sleeping in shared beds. Accounts from the period also mention the danger of house fires at night. The longhouse was simple, but it offered shelter from wind and rain. On winter nights, with the embers glowing and the animals resting nearby, it provided the warmth and security that rural families needed to survive the season.

[long-pause]

{img:09-peasant-family-portrait-hearth-evening-1300}

Most medieval peasants left few written records, but historians can reconstruct their lives from many sources. Ordinary villagers around 1300 rarely appear in written documents as individuals. They are mentioned mainly in manor accounts, rentals and the records of the manor court. These record names, land holdings, rents, fines and disputes. Historians combine such documents with other evidence to understand daily life. Archaeology reveals houses, tools, pottery and food remains. Studies of skeletons from churchyards show diet, health and age at death. Illustrations in manuscripts, such as psalters and books of hours, show ploughing, harvesting and household work. Wharram Percy is especially important, because excavations there uncovered both houses and hundreds of burials. Even so, much remains uncertain. Historians must be careful not to describe one village as if it represented all of England. Conditions varied by region, wealth and status. Yet together, these sources give a remarkably detailed picture of how ordinary rural families lived, worked and faced the winter.

[long-pause]

{img:10-peasant-woman-kerchief-churning-butter-doorway}

Peasant women managed the household and also worked in the fields, especially at harvest. In medieval peasant households, women carried out a wide range of work. They cooked, brewed ale, made butter and cheese, kept poultry and tended the garden. They spun wool, wove cloth and made and mended clothing. They cared for children and for elderly or sick family members. At busy times of the farming year, especially harvest, women also worked in the fields, using sickles to cut grain and binding sheaves. Manor records show women paying rents, holding land as widows and being fined in court. Ale-brewing was often women's work and could provide extra income. Historians such as Judith Bennett have studied these records in detail. Even so, women's work is often less visible in written sources than men's. Illustrations from the period, including the Luttrell Psalter, show women milking, spinning and feeding hens. Their labour was essential to the survival of every peasant household.

[long-pause]

{img:11-peasant-children-geese-yard-autumn}

Child mortality was high in medieval villages, and many children did not survive to adulthood. In medieval England, a large proportion of children died before reaching adulthood. Illness, infection and poor nutrition were common causes. Excavations at the churchyard of Wharram Percy uncovered the remains of many infants and children. Studies of these skeletons give historians evidence of their health and growth. Families often had several children, and many experienced the loss of at least one. Parish practices, such as prayers and remembrance in church, were part of how families coped with grief. Older relatives frequently lived with their children's families. When a tenant died, the land usually passed to a son, who was expected to support his widowed mother. Manor court records sometimes describe such arrangements in detail. Historians stress that medieval parents loved and cared for their children, despite the high risks. Toys found by archaeologists, and descriptions of childhood games, show that children also played and enjoyed themselves when they could.

[long-pause]

{img:12-old-grandmother-spinning-fireside-stool}

Elderly villagers often lived with their families and contributed by spinning and caring for children. Older people in medieval villages remained part of the household for as long as they could. When they could no longer do heavy farm work, they often took on lighter tasks. Spinning wool was one of the most common. Older women minded small children, helped with cooking and passed on practical knowledge. They knew remedies for common illnesses, songs, prayers and the dates of saints' days. Manor court records include agreements in which a son or new tenant promised to provide food, shelter and space by the fire for a retired parent. These arrangements are sometimes called maintenance agreements. Historians use them to understand care for the elderly before modern pensions. Children learned to work alongside the adults from an early age. They fetched water, gathered firewood, fed animals and looked after younger siblings. Even so, the presence of grandparents gave households continuity. Knowledge passed from one generation to the next mainly by example and conversation.

[long-pause]

{img:13-medieval-children-playing-ball-village-lane}

Evenings by the fire were an important time for family conversation, songs and stories. When the day's work ended, especially in winter, families gathered around the hearth. They ate their evening meal and continued with indoor tasks such as spinning, mending and carving. Conversation, singing and storytelling filled the long evenings. Many traditional songs and tales were passed on orally in this way. Children played games in the lanes and yards during daylight. Archaeologists have found toys such as small balls, whistles and spinning tops at medieval sites. Writers and preachers of the time also mention children's games. Historians emphasise that peasants were poor by modern standards. They owned few possessions and depended on good harvests. Yet they also had strong ties to family, neighbours and their village community. Shared work and shared celebrations created a sense of belonging. In winter, the evening gathering by the fire was one of the main comforts of rural life, giving warmth, company and a sense of security.

[long-pause]

{img:14-open-fields-strips-ploughmen-oxen-autumn}

Many villagers around 1300 were villeins, unfree tenants with duties to their lord. In medieval England, much of the rural population was legally unfree. These tenants were known as villeins or serfs. They were not slaves. They had their own houses and land and could keep much of what they produced. However, they were bound to the manor. They needed the lord's permission to leave, and they owed him labour and payments. A villein typically worked a set number of days each week on the lord's land, known as the demesne. Additional labour was required at busy times, such as harvest. Villeins also paid fees on certain occasions, such as when a daughter married or when land was inherited. Manor records list these obligations in detail. Historians note that the burden varied greatly between manors. Free tenants also lived in many villages and owed rent rather than labour. Over the following two centuries, villeinage gradually declined in England, especially after the Black Death.

[long-pause]

{img:15-reeve-tally-sticks-counting-sheep-barn}

Village officials called reeves often kept accounts using notched wooden tally sticks. The day-to-day running of a manor was often managed by a reeve, usually a villager chosen from among the tenants. The reeve supervised work on the lord's land, collected payments and kept track of produce. Many reeves could not read or write. They recorded quantities using tally sticks, pieces of wood marked with notches. A tally could be split lengthwise, so that each party kept half as a record. Once a year, the lord's steward or auditor came to examine the accounts. A clerk wrote them down on long parchment rolls. Many such manorial accounts survive in English archives and are a major source for historians. Courts were also held regularly on the manor. All tenants were expected to attend. These courts dealt with inheritance, disputes, failures to perform labour services and other local matters. Their records, called court rolls, provide many details about village life.

[long-pause]

{img:16-manor-court-hall-villagers-benches-steward}

Penalties in the manor court were mostly small fines of a few pence. Manor courts dealt with many everyday problems. Typical cases included animals straying into other people's crops, failing to repair fences, brewing ale that did not meet standards, or not attending the lord's work. Most penalties were small fines, often a few pence. The courts also recorded agreements, land transfers and inheritance. Historians use court rolls to study how villages governed themselves. The courts helped keep the peace and enforced local customs. Open fields were farmed according to shared rules. Usually, fields were organised in a rotation. One was sown with winter grain such as wheat or rye, another with spring crops such as oats, barley, peas or beans, and one was left fallow to recover, while animals grazed on it and manured the soil. Decisions about when to plough, sow and harvest were often made together. Even so, disputes were common, and the court rolls record many of them.

[long-pause]

{img:17-villagers-ploughing-together-oxen-team-hill}

Ploughs in medieval England were often pulled by teams of six or eight oxen, shared between neighbours. The heavy plough used in much of medieval England required a large team of oxen. Few peasant families owned enough animals for a full team. Neighbours therefore pooled their oxen and ploughed each other's strips in turn. Manor records and agreements describe such arrangements. Villages also employed common officials. A village shepherd looked after the sheep of many households together. A swineherd took pigs into the woods to feed on acorns and beechmast in autumn. At harvest, families helped one another as well as performing labour for the lord. The parish priest was an important figure in village life. He farmed his own land, called the glebe, and received tithes, a tenth of the produce of the parish. Historians note that cooperation was essential in the open-field system. Even so, it depended on trust and was supported by the manor court. The village functioned as a community as well as a collection of households.

[long-pause]

{img:18-villagers-standing-church-nave-candles-sunday}

Many medieval parish churches were decorated with wall paintings showing saints and Bible stories. Parish churches in medieval England were often brightly painted inside. Walls displayed images of saints, scenes from the life of Christ and depictions of the Last Judgement. These paintings helped teach religious stories to people who could not read. Many survive today, though often in faded form, because they were covered with whitewash after the Reformation and later uncovered. Services were in Latin, but sermons and some teaching were in English. On Sundays and feast days, villagers attended Mass. Afterwards, they often gathered in the churchyard to talk and share news. The church was the centre of community life, not only of worship. Church ales, festivals and processions brought people together. At Wharram Percy, archaeologists studied the church of Saint Martin in great detail. They found evidence of several stages of building and rebuilding over the centuries. The church reflected both the faith of the villagers and their changing fortunes.

[long-pause]

{img:19-autumn-ploughing-winter-wheat-oxen-gulls}

Preparations for winter began at Michaelmas, at the end of September. In medieval England, Michaelmas, the feast of Saint Michael on the twenty-ninth of September, marked the end of the harvest season. Rents were often due at this time, and the agricultural year began again. After Michaelmas, fields were ploughed and sown with winter crops such as wheat or rye. The heavy plough, with an iron share, was pulled slowly along the long strips by teams of oxen. A ploughman guided it, while a helper, often a boy, walked beside the animals. Birds followed the plough to feed on worms in the freshly turned soil. Manuscript illustrations, including the Luttrell Psalter, show such scenes. Historians use farm accounts from manors to understand the timing of these tasks. Even so, the exact schedule depended on weather and soil. A wet autumn could delay ploughing and sowing. Getting the winter grain into the ground in good time was essential for the next year's harvest.

[long-pause]

{img:20-swineherd-pigs-oak-wood-acorns-autumn-leaves}

Villagers paid the lord a small fee, called pannage, for each pig that fed in his woods in autumn. In autumn, pigs were driven into woodland to feed on fallen acorns and beechmast. This practice was known as pannage. Lords usually charged a small fee for each pig allowed into the woods. Manorial records frequently list these payments. Pigs fattened quickly on this rich food and were an important source of meat for the winter. At the same time, households gathered other food. They picked apples and pears and stored them carefully in lofts or barns. They collected blackberries, sloes, hazelnuts and crab apples from hedgerows. Crab apples could be made into verjuice, a sour juice used in cooking. Historians of diet note that wild foods supplemented the produce of fields and gardens. Even so, gathering rights were often regulated by the lord or by village custom. The autumn months were therefore busy with harvesting, storing and preparing. Everything gathered at this time helped families get through the long winter ahead.

[long-pause]

{img:21-children-picking-hazelnuts-hedgerow-baskets}

Martinmas, on the eleventh of November, was the traditional time to slaughter livestock for the winter. Gardens supplied onions, leeks, cabbages and herbs, which were harvested and stored in autumn. Peas and beans were dried and kept in sacks for use in winter pottage. Herbs were hung from the rafters to dry. In November came Martinmas, the feast of Saint Martin. It was traditionally the time for slaughtering animals, because there was not enough fodder to keep all livestock through the winter. Farmers kept oxen, breeding animals and some cows and sheep. Others, especially pigs, were killed and their meat preserved. Pork was salted or smoked into bacon and hams. Nearly every part of the animal was used, including fat for cooking and lighting. Historians find references to Martinmas slaughter in many medieval calendars and agricultural writings. The custom shaped the rhythm of the year. Even so, practices varied with local conditions. A good autumn could allow a family to keep more animals through the winter.

[long-pause]

{img:22-sides-of-bacon-hanging-smoke-rafters-glow}

By late autumn, a well-prepared household stored bacon, salted pork, grain, pulses and cheese for the winter. After slaughter and harvest, peasant households tried to lay in enough supplies to last until spring. Sides of bacon and hams were hung in the smoke from the rafters. Barrels of salted pork stood in a corner. Sacks of dried peas and beans, baskets of onions and apples, and cheeses wrapped in cloth were kept in the house. Sheaves of wheat, barley and oats were stored in barns and threshed gradually through the winter. Slaughter was often accompanied by a feast of fresh meat, shared with neighbours, since fresh meat was a rare treat. Historians point out that the amount of food stored varied greatly between households. Wealthier peasants had more land and livestock. Poorer families, especially landless labourers, had much less security. Even so, careful storage was essential for everyone. A family's survival through the winter depended largely on the work of the previous months.

[long-pause]

{img:23-peasants-walking-to-market-town-cart-autumn-road}

Peasants travelled to nearby market towns to buy goods they could not produce themselves. No village was entirely self-sufficient. Households needed salt, especially for preserving meat. They also needed iron goods such as plough shares, knives, nails and pots, as well as cloth, shoes and other items. These were bought at markets in nearby towns. For Wharram Percy, the market town of Malton lay about eight miles away. Many market towns received charters from the king in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, and the number of markets grew rapidly. Historians estimate that by 1300, most villagers in England lived within a day's journey of a market. Journeys were made on foot or with carts pulled by oxen or horses. Villagers often left before dawn and returned before dark. Even so, markets were not only places of trade. They were also sources of news and entertainment. A trip to market was a significant event in the rural calendar.

[long-pause]

{img:24-medieval-market-place-stalls-awnings-crowds-castle}

Medieval markets offered the goods of smiths, coopers, potters and salt merchants. A market town around 1300 was busy on market days. Blacksmiths worked at their forges, making and repairing tools. Coopers sold barrels, and potters displayed jugs, bowls and cooking pots. Salt merchants brought salt from coastal salt pans. Villagers sold their own produce, including grain, eggs, cheese, wool and poultry. With the money they earned, they bought essential goods, such as salt, iron plough parts, pots, leather shoes and needles. Small luxuries, such as spices like pepper and ginger, were sometimes bought for special occasions. Historians use the records of markets, tolls and town courts to understand trade at this level. Archaeologists also find pottery from regional workshops at village sites, showing that goods travelled across the countryside. Even so, many peasants handled relatively little money. Much exchange still took place through barter or in kind. The market linked rural households to a wider economy.

[long-pause]

{img:25-saltman-sacks-of-salt-market-stall-buyer}

Markets also offered small luxuries, entertainment and news from the wider world. Alongside essential goods, markets sold small items such as ribbons, buckles and simple ornaments. These were affordable treats for families on special occasions. Entertainment was common as well. Jugglers, acrobats and travelling musicians performed. Pedlars sold religious items, some of which were of doubtful authenticity. Friars sometimes preached to crowds in public places. Historians find evidence of these activities in town records, sermons and literary works. Writers such as Geoffrey Chaucer, later in the fourteenth century, described pardoners selling dubious relics. Markets were also places where people exchanged news and gossip. Information about prices, harvests, wars and royal decisions spread through these gatherings. Even so, much of what was heard was rumour. For villagers who rarely travelled, a market day provided rare contact with people from other places. It offered a break from routine and a chance to see a wider world.

[long-pause]

{img:26-minstrel-juggler-crowd-market-medieval-afternoon}

Alehouses and pie shops in market towns served food and drink to visiting country people. Market towns had alehouses, cookshops and stalls selling pies and other prepared food. Country people visiting on market day could buy a hot meal, a rare treat for many of them. These places were also centres for conversation and news. Visitors heard about the king, his wars in Scotland, the price of wool in Flanders and events in other parts of the country. Around 1300, Edward the First was fighting campaigns in Scotland, which affected taxes and supplies across northern England. Historians use town records and royal accounts to trace such connections. Some market towns had castles, priories and stone houses, which impressed visitors from small villages. Malton, for example, had a priory and a castle. Even so, most visitors spent only a few hours in town. They needed to return home before dark, especially in winter. The journey back with a loaded cart was often slow and tiring along muddy roads.

[long-pause]

{img:27-family-cart-coming-over-hill-village-smoke-dusk}

Returning from market, villagers brought back supplies essential for the winter months. Goods bought at market were vital for the coming season. Salt allowed meat to be preserved after the autumn slaughter. A new plough share or iron tools helped prepare for spring work. Pots, shoes and cloth replaced worn items. These purchases represented a significant part of a household's limited cash. Historians studying household accounts of wealthier peasants find that spending on salt, iron and clothing was a regular part of their budget. Records of poorer families are much rarer. Archaeological finds at villages, such as iron tools and fragments of pottery from regional kilns, show the results of such trade. Even so, much of what households used was made or repaired at home. A trip to market was therefore planned carefully, often once or twice in the autumn. Returning before dark with essential supplies helped give the household confidence that it could face the winter. The village itself remained the centre of daily life.

[long-pause]

{img:28-pottage-cauldron-hearth-wooden-spoon-steam}

The main daily food of medieval peasants was pottage, a thick stew cooked in a pot over the fire. Pottage was the basic meal of most rural households in medieval England. It was made in a large iron or clay pot that hung over the hearth or stood among the embers. The ingredients changed with the season and with what was available. In winter, pottage usually contained dried peas or beans, oats or barley, onions, leeks, cabbage and herbs. A little salted pork or bacon could be added for flavour when the household could afford it. The pot often simmered for hours, and fresh ingredients were added from day to day. Historians use recipes, household accounts and archaeological evidence to reconstruct this diet. Analysis of residues in excavated cooking pots shows traces of cereals, vegetables and animal fats. Studies of skeletons from Wharram Percy show a diet based mainly on grain. Even so, pottage was filling and nourishing. In the cold months, a warm bowl of it was the centre of the family's meals.

[long-pause]

{img:29-dark-rye-loaves-baking-clay-oven}

Peasant bread was usually made from rye, barley or a mixture of grains, rather than pure wheat. Bread was the other basic food of medieval peasants. Fine white bread made from wheat was mostly eaten by wealthier people. Ordinary villagers ate darker and heavier loaves made from rye, barley or maslin, a mixture of wheat and rye grown together. In some areas, oats were also used. Grain had to be ground into flour at the lord's mill, and the miller kept a share as payment, called multure. On many manors, tenants were also required to bake at the lord's oven and pay a fee. These rights are recorded in manor documents. Some households baked at home anyway, in small ovens or on the hearth, and were sometimes fined for it in the manor court. Historians note that bread consumption varied with harvests and prices. In poor years, more barley, oats and even beans went into the dough. Even so, bread and ale together supplied a large share of a peasant's daily energy.

[long-pause]

{img:30-woman-brewing-ale-wooden-tub-steam-barley}

Ale, brewed from malted barley, was the everyday drink of medieval peasant families. Ale was drunk daily by adults and children in medieval England. It was weaker than most modern beer and was brewed from malted barley, water and sometimes oats. Hops were not commonly used in England until the fifteenth century, so ale did not keep well and had to be brewed often. Brewing was usually women's work. The grain was soaked, allowed to sprout, dried and then mashed in hot water, before the liquid was fermented. Many women brewed for sale as well as for their own households. When a fresh batch was ready, a pole with a bush of leaves, called an ale-stake, was hung outside the house as a sign. Officials known as ale-tasters checked quality and price. Manor court rolls record many fines for brewing, which historians interpret as a kind of licence fee. Water was also drunk, but ale was considered nourishing. It provided a significant part of the daily diet.

[long-pause]

{img:31-hard-cheeses-shelf-cloth-wrapped-dim-light}

Cheese, butter and eggs were important sources of protein in the peasant diet. Dairy products were an essential part of rural food in medieval England. In summer, when cows, ewes and goats gave plenty of milk, women made butter and cheese. Hard cheeses could be stored for many months and were eaten through the winter. Soft cheeses and butter were often salted to help them keep. Eggs came from hens kept in the yard, though hens laid few eggs in the darkest months. Historians sometimes call these foods white meats, a term used in the period for dairy products. Meat was eaten less often by poorer peasants. The Church also set many fast days when meat was forbidden. These included Fridays, often Wednesdays and Saturdays, and the whole of Lent. On such days, people ate fish, usually salted or dried herring, and dishes of peas or beans. Manor and household accounts show regular purchases of herring. Even so, diets varied greatly with wealth, region and season.

[long-pause]

{img:32-family-supper-candle-rushlight-bread-ale-jug}

Peasant families ate their main meal around midday and a lighter supper in the evening. Medieval rural households usually ate two main meals each day. Dinner was taken around the middle of the day and was the largest meal. Supper was eaten in the evening and was usually simpler, often bread, cheese, the remains of the pottage and ale. In winter, supper took place after dark, by the light of the fire or a rushlight. Food was eaten from wooden bowls with spoons and knives. Bread could also serve as a plate. Prayers were often said before eating. Archaeologists have found wooden bowls, spoons, pottery jugs and iron knives at village sites. Writers of the period describe peasant meals as plain but filling. Historians estimate that a working adult needed a great deal of energy, especially for ploughing and threshing. Even so, food supplies were often tight in late winter and early spring. A careful household divided its stores so that they would last until new food became available.

[long-pause]

{img:33-hearth-fire-glowing-embers-winter-night-cottage}

Fuel for the hearth came from wood, peat or dried dung, depending on the local landscape. Keeping the fire burning through the winter required a large supply of fuel. In wooded areas, peasants used fallen branches, dead wood and coppiced timber. In other regions, they burned peat cut from moorland, furze from heaths or dried cattle dung. The Yorkshire Wolds had relatively little woodland, so fuel was valuable there. Rights to collect wood were regulated by the lord and by local custom. The phrase by hook or by crook may come from medieval rules allowing peasants to take dead branches that could be pulled down with a billhook or a shepherd's crook. Historians are not certain of this explanation, but it is widely repeated. Manor court rolls record fines for cutting wood without permission. Fuel was gathered and stacked during the autumn. Even so, households often had to economise on fuel in long winters. The hearth was carefully tended, and the fire was rarely allowed to go out entirely.

[long-pause]

{img:34-wet-boots-drying-by-fire-steam-cottage}

Winter weather made outdoor work wet and cold, and clothes and shoes were dried by the fire. In winter, peasants still had to work outside, feeding animals, fetching water, mending fences and carrying fuel. Rain, mud and snow soaked clothing and footwear. On returning home, wet shoes and garments were placed near the hearth to dry. Clothing was mostly made of wool, which stays warm even when damp, and this helped people cope with the weather. Historians who study medieval climate note that around 1300, England was beginning to move from a relatively warm period into a cooler and wetter one. Some historians call the following centuries the Little Ice Age, though its timing and severity are debated. Records of harvests, prices and weather from monasteries and manors provide evidence of these changes. The wet years from 1315 to 1317 were especially severe. Even so, daily life in winter followed familiar routines. Practical knowledge of how to stay warm and dry was passed from one generation to the next.

[long-pause]

{img:35-rushlight-holder-flickering-dark-cottage}

Peasant homes were lit by the fire and by rushlights, made by dipping rushes in animal fat. Artificial light was scarce and valuable in medieval homes. Wax candles were expensive and mainly used in churches and wealthy households. Tallow candles, made from animal fat, were cheaper but still a cost. Most peasants relied on the light of the fire and on rushlights. To make a rushlight, the outer skin of a dried rush was peeled away, leaving the white pith, which was then dipped in melted fat. The result was a thin taper that burned for a short time with a small flame. It was held in a simple iron clip. Writers in later centuries described the making of rushlights in detail, and historians believe the method was much older. Archaeologists have found iron holders at village sites. Even so, light remained limited. In the depth of winter, the dark evenings were long, and families often went to bed early to save fuel and fat.

[long-pause]

{img:36-family-huddled-blankets-sheepskins-embers-glow}

In the coldest weather, families stayed close to the fire and wrapped themselves in wool and sheepskins. When frost and wind came, the peasant house was cold away from the hearth. Draughts entered through doors, gaps in the walls and the thatch. Families stayed near the fire, wrapped in woollen cloaks, blankets and sheepskins. Children often slept together with adults for warmth. Historians studying household inventories find blankets, coverlets and sheepskins listed among the most important possessions. The livestock in the byre also added warmth. Even so, cold was a real danger, especially for the very young, the old and the sick. Studies of skeletons from Wharram Percy show signs of hardship, such as interrupted growth in childhood. Yet these households had developed many ways of coping with winter. Thick walls of clay and thatch insulated the house well. Stored food, careful use of fuel and warm clothing helped them through the darkest weeks. Families relied on one another and on their neighbours when conditions became difficult.

[long-pause]

{img:37-shepherd-sheep-chalk-downs-woollen-cloak}

Wool from sheep was the main material for clothing in medieval England, and it was also the country's most valuable export. Sheep were kept in large numbers across England, especially on chalk downs and hills such as the Yorkshire Wolds. Their wool was used for most clothing and bedding. English wool was also highly valued abroad, especially in Flanders and Italy, where it was woven into fine cloth. Around 1300, wool exports were a major source of income for landowners, monasteries and the king. Customs records of the period show tens of thousands of sacks of wool leaving English ports each year. Peasants kept sheep for wool, milk and manure, as well as meat. The flock grazed on the open hills and on fallow fields. A village shepherd often cared for many households' animals together. Historians note that sheep farming later became even more important. In the fifteenth century, many villages, including Wharram Percy, were turned into large sheep pastures. Even so, in 1300 sheep were part of a mixed farming system with grain.

[long-pause]

{img:38-upright-loom-clay-weights-woman-weaving-light}

Spinning and weaving were essential household skills, carried out by women through the winter. Turning wool into cloth took many steps. Fleeces were washed and combed or carded to untangle the fibres. Then the wool was spun into thread, traditionally with a hand spindle and distaff. The spinning wheel was introduced to England around this period, but the spindle remained common. Women spun whenever they had a spare moment, even while walking or tending animals. Thread was woven into cloth on a loom. Archaeologists at many sites have found spindle whorls, the small weights used on spindles, and loom weights of clay. By 1300, much weaving was done by professional weavers in towns, often on horizontal looms. Cloth could be dyed with plants such as woad for blue, weld for yellow and madder for red. It was also fulled, or thickened by beating in water, often at a fulling mill. Linen for shirts and underclothes was made from flax. Winter evenings were a common time for spinning and making clothes.

[long-pause]

{img:39-peasant-man-hood-cloak-snowy-lane}

Peasant men usually wore a woollen tunic, hose and a hood, and women wore long woollen gowns. Clothing for ordinary people in England around 1300 was simple and practical. Men typically wore a linen shirt and braies, a kind of undergarment, beneath a woollen tunic that reached the knees. Their legs were covered by hose, and a hood with a short cape protected the head and shoulders. In bad weather, a heavy cloak was added. Women wore a linen shift under a long woollen gown, with a cloak and a head covering, often a kerchief or wimple. Colours were usually natural shades of wool or simple dyes, such as brown, grey, blue and russet. Historians study clothing through manuscript illustrations, such as those in the Luttrell Psalter, and through surviving fragments of textiles. Clothing was valuable and was worn for many years. Garments were mended, patched and passed down. Even so, they were well suited to the climate. Layers of wool provided warmth and some protection from rain.

[long-pause]

{img:40-leather-shoes-wooden-pattens-doorway-mud}

Peasant footwear included simple leather shoes, and wooden pattens were sometimes worn over them in mud. Medieval shoes were usually made of leather, stitched together and turned inside out, a method known as turnshoe construction. Soles were thin, so shoes wore out quickly and needed frequent repair. Archaeologists have found many leather shoes preserved in waterlogged sites, especially in towns such as York and London. To protect shoes from wet ground, some people wore pattens, wooden platforms strapped over the shoes. They raised the foot above mud and water. Historians note that pattens became more common in later centuries, especially in towns. In the countryside, people also wrapped their feet in cloth or straw inside their shoes for extra warmth. Some children and poor labourers went barefoot in summer. Even so, in winter suitable footwear was essential. Muddy lanes, frozen fields and snowy paths made walking difficult. Shoemakers and cobblers in market towns supplied new shoes, while villagers often carried out simple repairs at home.

[long-pause]

{img:41-oxen-byre-hay-breath-steaming-lantern-dawn}

Oxen were the most valuable animals on a medieval farm, and they were carefully fed and housed in winter. In much of England around 1300, oxen were the main animals used for ploughing. They were strong, patient and cheaper to feed than horses, and their meat and hides could be used when they grew old. Horses were becoming more common for harrowing and carrying, but oxen remained central to the plough. Because a team was so important, peasants took great care of their oxen in winter. The animals were kept in the byre or in barns and fed on hay, straw and sometimes peas and oats. Writers of agricultural treatises of the period, such as Walter of Henley, gave advice on feeding and caring for oxen. Manor accounts record the costs of fodder and the number of animals kept. Historians note that a shortage of hay at the end of winter could weaken oxen before spring ploughing. Even so, healthy animals were essential. Good care in winter prepared them for the work of spring.

[long-pause]

{img:42-woman-milking-cow-byre-wooden-pail-warm}

Fodder was often scarce in late winter, and farmers had to make their hay last until spring grass appeared. Hay was cut and dried in summer and stored for the winter. Meadows near streams produced the best hay, and they were highly valued. Manor records show that meadow land was often worth more than arable land. In winter, cattle, oxen and sheep depended on stored hay, straw and other fodder. Farmers needed to calculate carefully how much they had. If winter lasted longer than expected, supplies could run out before grass began to grow again in spring. Animals then became thin and weak, and some might die. Historians call the period of late winter and early spring the hungry gap, both for animals and for people. Cows gave little milk during these months. Women still milked those that were in milk, in the warmth of the byre. Even so, farmers had several strategies. They reduced their herds at Martinmas, saved fodder and sometimes cut branches of ivy or holly as emergency feed.

[long-pause]

{img:43-village-shepherd-dog-flock-snowy-hills-dusk}

The village shepherd looked after the sheep through the winter, often with the help of dogs. Sheep were hardier than cattle and could graze outdoors for much of the winter. On chalk hills like the Yorkshire Wolds, flocks grazed on open downland and on fallow fields. A shepherd, often employed by the village or the manor, cared for them. Dogs helped to guard and drive the flock. In bad weather, sheep were brought into folds, enclosures made of hurdles, or sheltered by hedges and banks. Manor accounts record payments to shepherds, purchases of hurdles and tar for treating sheep, and losses from disease. Sheep scab and liver fluke were serious problems, especially in wet years. Historians note that shepherds often had special privileges, such as keeping some of their own sheep with the lord's flock. Writers of the period described the shepherd's work in detail. Even so, it was a hard and lonely job. Shepherds spent long hours outdoors in cold and snow, especially during lambing in late winter.

[long-pause]

{img:44-pig-sty-wattle-yard-frost-boy-feeding-scraps}

Pigs were kept by almost every peasant household, because they ate scraps and provided much of the family's meat. Pigs were especially useful animals for peasant families. They ate kitchen waste, spoiled grain and almost anything else. In autumn, they fattened on acorns and beechmast in the woods. Most pigs were slaughtered at Martinmas, but breeding sows and a few young pigs were kept through the winter. They lived in small sties of wattle or wood in the yard, and children often helped to feed them. Hens and geese also lived in the yard, providing eggs, meat and feathers. Archaeologists find many pig bones at medieval village sites. Studies of animal bones from Wharram Percy show that cattle, sheep and pigs were all eaten. Historians use these bones to understand the size of animals and the age at which they were killed. Medieval pigs were smaller and hairier than modern breeds, closer in appearance to wild boar. Even so, they were an important resource. A household with a pig had valuable insurance against hunger.

[long-pause]

{img:45-sleeping-oxen-straw-byre-rushlight-quiet}

Caring for animals in winter included cleaning stalls and spreading manure on the fields. Animals kept indoors in winter needed daily care. Their stalls had to be cleaned, and fresh straw laid down. The dirty straw and manure were piled in a heap, called a midden, in the yard. In spring, this manure was spread on the fields to improve the soil. Historians note that manure was very important in medieval farming, because there were no artificial fertilisers. Land that received more manure produced better crops. Lords sometimes required tenants to fold their sheep on the lord's land, so that it received the benefit of their dung. Manor records and agricultural treatises discuss these practices. Archaeologists find traces of middens and drains at excavated houses. At night, the byre was quiet. The animals lay in the straw, warm and fed. Families checked on them before sleep, by the light of a rushlight or lantern. Healthy animals in spring were the result of careful work through the winter.

[long-pause]

{img:46-advent-village-church-frosty-morning-bell}

Advent, the four weeks before Christmas, was a time of fasting and preparation in the medieval Church. The Christian year shaped the rhythm of life in medieval villages. Advent began four weeks before Christmas. It was a season of preparation and, in principle, of fasting, although rules were less strict than in Lent. Church services included special prayers and readings, and the clergy wore purple vestments. The church bell rang for services, and villagers attended Mass on Sundays and feast days. Historians use church calendars, sermons and guides for priests to understand how these seasons were observed in parishes. One such guide, written for parish priests in the fourteenth century, explains the meaning of each feast in simple terms. Even so, practices varied from place to place. In many villages, Advent was also a time to prepare food, clean houses and gather fuel for the coming festivities. For people living through a cold and dark season, the church year gave a sense of order. It also promised celebrations to come.

[long-pause]

{img:47-yule-log-dragged-into-cottage-hearth-family}

Christmas celebrations in medieval England lasted for twelve days, from Christmas Day to Twelfth Night. Christmas was one of the most important festivals of the medieval year. On Christmas Eve, many households brought in greenery, such as holly and ivy, to decorate their homes. A large log was sometimes brought in to burn through the season. The custom of the Yule log is better documented in later centuries, and historians are not certain how widespread it was around 1300. On Christmas Day, villagers attended Mass, sometimes beginning at midnight. The twelve days that followed were a time of rest from most work. Manor records show that labour services to the lord were often suspended during this period. Feasting, games and visits filled the days. Historians use household accounts, poems and chronicles to reconstruct these customs. Even so, much depended on local tradition and on the household's resources. For peasants, the Christmas season provided a rare break in the hardest part of the year.

[long-pause]

{img:48-manor-hall-christmas-feast-villagers-long-tables-fire}

On some manors, the lord provided a Christmas meal for his tenants, though they often contributed food themselves. Several manor records describe Christmas customs between lords and tenants. On some estates, tenants brought gifts to the lord, such as hens or loaves of bread. In return, the lord provided a meal at the manor house. The meal could include bread, ale, meat and cheese. Some records specify exactly what each tenant should bring and what they would receive, including how much firewood. Historians have studied these arrangements as examples of reciprocal obligations on the manor. A famous example comes from an estate in Somerset, where tenants were entitled to a Christmas meal and a fire. Even so, such customs were not universal. On manors where the lord rarely lived, like Wharram Percy, celebrations may have been organised by the steward or by the villagers themselves. Feasts were noisy and crowded. For a short time, the usual routine of hard work was set aside for food, warmth and company.

[long-pause]

{img:49-villagers-ring-dance-carol-snow-lanterns}

Carols in the Middle Ages were originally ring dances accompanied by singing. In medieval England, the word carol originally referred to a dance in which people held hands in a ring and sang. One person sang the verses, and the others joined in the refrain. Carols were performed at many festivals, not only at Christmas. Over time, the word came to mean a song for the Christmas season. Many medieval carols survive in manuscripts from the fifteenth century, with lyrics in English and Latin. Church authorities sometimes disapproved of dancing, especially in churchyards, and preachers complained about it. Historians use these complaints as evidence that such dances were popular. Mummers, groups of people in masks and costumes, also went from house to house at Christmas, performing simple plays and receiving food or drink. Even so, details of these performances around 1300 are scarce. Most evidence comes from later records. The Christmas season was a time of music, dancing and community celebration.

[long-pause]

{img:50-plough-monday-decorated-plough-young-men-village}

Twelfth Night ended the Christmas season, and Plough Monday marked the return to work in the fields. The twelve days of Christmas ended with Twelfth Night on the fifth of January, followed by the feast of Epiphany. Twelfth Night was often celebrated with a special cake. In later centuries, a bean was hidden inside, and whoever found it became king of the feast for the evening. Wassailing, drinking to health and good fortune, was also associated with this time, including blessing apple trees in some regions. Historians find many of these customs documented mainly in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, though their roots may be older. The first Monday after Epiphany was known as Plough Monday. It marked the traditional return to agricultural work. In some areas, young men decorated a plough and dragged it around the village, asking for money or food. The earliest records of Plough Monday customs come from the fifteenth century. Even so, the general pattern of a winter break followed by a return to work was ancient.

[long-pause]

{img:51-two-men-threshing-flails-barn-floor-dust-light}

Threshing grain with flails was one of the main winter tasks on medieval farms. Grain was harvested in late summer and stored as sheaves in barns. During the winter, it was threshed to separate the seeds from the straw. This was done with a flail, a tool made of two wooden sticks joined by a leather strap. Workers swung the flail and beat the sheaves laid on the barn floor. The work was hard and repetitive. Threshing often took place throughout the winter, as grain was needed. After threshing, the grain was winnowed. It was tossed in the air or fanned, so that the wind blew away the lighter chaff. Manor accounts record payments for threshing and winnowing, often calculated per quarter of grain. Historians use these records to estimate the amount of labour required. Illustrations in manuscripts also show threshers at work. Even so, threshing was usually done in a barn with open doors to provide light and a draught. The straw was kept for animal bedding, thatch and fodder.

[long-pause]

{img:52-washing-clothes-wooden-tub-hearth-steam-winter}

Household tasks such as washing clothes and fetching water were more difficult in winter. Daily chores continued throughout the cold months. Water had to be fetched from a stream, spring or well, often by women and children. In freezing weather, ice sometimes had to be broken first. Clothes and linen were washed in wooden tubs, using water heated on the fire. Lye, made from wood ash and water, was used as a cleaning agent. Washing was hard work, so it was done less often in winter. Historians note that medieval people valued cleanliness, washing their hands and faces daily, even if full bathing was rare. Advice books of the period stress washing before meals. Linen undergarments were changed and washed more often than woollen outer clothes. Archaeologists find wooden buckets, tubs and bowls preserved in waterlogged sites. Even so, many tasks depended on daylight, which was short in winter. Families organised their work to make the best use of the few hours of light.

[long-pause]

{img:53-child-fetching-water-icy-stream-wooden-bucket}

Children in peasant families began helping with household and farm work from an early age. Children in medieval villages took on responsibilities while still young. Small children fetched water, collected firewood, fed hens and minded younger siblings. Older children helped with herding, scaring birds from crops and other farm tasks. By their early teens, many worked almost like adults. Coroners' records, which describe accidental deaths, provide some of the best evidence for children's lives. Historians such as Barbara Hanawalt have used them to show the tasks children performed and the places they went. These records mention children falling into streams or wells while fetching water, for example. Even so, children also played. Archaeologists have found toys and games at medieval sites. Writers of the period describe children making toys and playing in the fields. Parents taught practical skills by example. Boys usually learned farming tasks, and girls learned household work, though there was some overlap. Childhood was short by modern standards, but it was not without joy.

[long-pause]

{img:54-snowdrops-churchyard-january-grey-light}

January and February were often the coldest and most difficult months of the medieval year. After the celebrations of Christmas and the new year, winter continued. January and February were typically the coldest months in England. Days were still short, food stores were shrinking, and fuel supplies were running low. Records from monasteries and manors note severe frosts in some years, including freezing rivers. Historians use such records to reconstruct past weather. Even so, the turning of the year brought some signs of change. The days grew slowly longer. In sheltered places, early flowers such as snowdrops began to appear, although historians believe that snowdrops may have been introduced to Britain later than the medieval period. Hazel catkins and the first green shoots showed that spring was not far away. For farmers, these signs were watched carefully. Calendars of the period listed the tasks of each month, and the coming of spring brought new work. The darkest part of the year, however, was gradually coming to an end.

[long-pause]

{img:55-family-gathered-fire-winter-evening-stories-1300}

Storytelling, singing and games were common ways to pass long winter evenings. Winter evenings were long, and families spent many hours by the fire. In addition to spinning, mending and carving, people told stories. These included tales of saints, local legends, ghost stories and humorous tales. Many such stories were passed down orally and were written down only much later. Preachers used short stories, called exempla, in their sermons, and some of these were also told at home. Ballads and songs were sung. Historians know that tales about Robin Hood were popular by the fourteenth century, since a famous poem of the 1370s mentions them. Riddles and proverbs were also part of everyday entertainment. Even so, much of this oral culture is lost. Only fragments survive in written sources. Yet it was clearly important to people. Stories connected them to their history and their faith. Storytelling also taught moral lessons and passed the time pleasantly during the darkest months of the year.

[long-pause]

{img:56-saint-cuthbert-otters-island-shore-legend-painting}

Saint Cuthbert was a much-loved saint in northern England, and many stories were told about him. Cuthbert was a monk and bishop who lived in the seventh century in Northumbria. He spent time as a hermit on the island of Inner Farne, off the coast of Northumberland. After his death, he became one of the most popular saints in the north. His shrine at Durham Cathedral attracted many pilgrims. One famous story, recorded by the scholar Bede, tells that Cuthbert spent nights praying in the cold sea. When he came out, two otters dried his feet with their fur and warmed them with their breath. This story was often told and depicted in art. Historians study Bede's writings as important sources for early English history. Even so, they distinguish between historical facts and later legends. In medieval villages, stories of saints were told for instruction and comfort. They gave examples of faith, kindness and endurance. For people living through a northern winter, such stories had special meaning.

[long-pause]

{img:57-men-playing-nine-mens-morris-board-firelight}

Nine men's morris was a popular board game in medieval England. Nine men's morris is a strategy game for two players. Each player has nine pieces, which are placed on a board of three squares, one inside the other, connected by lines. Players try to form rows of three pieces, which allows them to remove an opponent's piece. The game was widely played in medieval England. Boards have been found scratched into stones in churches, castles and monasteries, sometimes on benches or window ledges. Outdoor versions were also cut into turf. Historians believe that the game is very old, and versions of it were known in the Roman world. Other games were also popular, such as dice, draughts and, among the wealthy, chess. The Church sometimes disapproved of gambling with dice, and laws tried to restrict it. Even so, games remained a common pastime. On winter evenings, simple boards and pieces of stone, wood or bone provided entertainment for adults and children alike.

[long-pause]

{img:58-girl-waking-before-dawn-cottage-embers-cold}

In winter, peasant days usually began before dawn with the rekindling of the fire. Medieval rural life followed the rhythm of daylight. In winter, people woke before dawn to make the most of the short day. The first task was usually to uncover the embers of the fire, which had been covered with ashes overnight, and add fuel. Women and girls often did this. Water was fetched, animals fed and bread or porridge prepared. Historians use descriptions in literature, sermons and household books to understand daily routines. A well-known poem of the period, describing a poor family's hardships, mentions women rising at night to rock cradles, card and spin. Even so, routines varied with the season and the household. In the coldest weeks, outdoor work was reduced, and more time was spent on indoor tasks. Church bells marked the hours for prayer and helped people keep track of time. The pattern of each day was shaped by light, weather and the needs of family and animals.

[long-pause]

{img:59-girl-spinning-wool-fireside-little-sister-playing}

Girls learned spinning and other household skills from their mothers and grandmothers. Spinning was so closely associated with women that the word spinster originally meant a woman who spun. Girls learned to spin at an early age, often by about seven or eight. They practised with a spindle and distaff, gradually producing thinner and more even thread. Medieval illustrations often show women spinning while caring for children or watching animals. Historians note that the demand for thread was very high, since weaving required large quantities. Some estimates suggest that several spinners were needed to keep one weaver supplied. Girls also learned to cook, brew, make butter and cheese, and care for poultry. These skills prepared them for running a household. Even so, girls also helped in the fields and with livestock when needed. Younger children played nearby while older girls worked. Archaeologists find spindle whorls at almost every medieval village site, showing how common spinning was. The skill passed from one generation of women to the next.

[long-pause]

{img:60-village-children-playing-chase-haystacks-winter-sun}

Medieval children played games such as tag, hide and seek and ball games. Evidence from art, literature and archaeology shows that medieval children enjoyed many games. They played chasing games, hide and seek and ball games. They used hoops, spinning tops, whistles and dolls. Some toys were made at home from wood, cloth or bone. Others, made of metal, were sold at markets. Archaeologists have found small metal toys, such as figures and miniature pots, in London and other towns. Writers describe children building small houses, making mud pies and playing at being adults. In winter, they slid on ice and played in the snow. Bone skates, made from the leg bones of horses or cattle, have been found at medieval sites. Historians note that children's play was valued, even though they also had to work. Some medieval writers argued that play was natural and good for children's growth. Even so, play was fitted around chores and daylight. On bright winter days, children spent time outdoors whenever they could.

[long-pause]

{img:61-mother-daughter-kneading-bread-trough-firelight}

Baking bread was a regular household task, and dough was kneaded in a wooden trough. Bread was made from flour, water, salt and a leavening agent. Leaven came from a piece of the previous batch of dough, or from the yeast left over from brewing ale. The dough was mixed and kneaded in a wooden trough, then left to rise. It was baked in an oven, which could be the lord's oven or a small household oven, or on a flat stone near the fire. Historians note that the frequency of baking depended on fuel supplies and the size of the household. Larger households might bake once a week. Bread was a central part of every meal. Wills and inventories often list troughs and other baking equipment. Manuscripts show women kneading dough and placing loaves in ovens. Even so, many people bought bread from bakers in towns, where strict laws controlled weight and price. In the countryside, home baking remained common. The smell of baking bread was a familiar part of peasant life.

[long-pause]

{img:62-girl-holding-blue-ribbon-firelight-grandmother}

Small decorations, such as ribbons or brooches, were valued possessions for peasant families. Although peasants owned few possessions, they valued small items of decoration. Ribbons, simple brooches, belt buckles and beads were bought at markets or made at home. Archaeologists find many such items at village sites, including pins, buckles and small jewellery of copper alloy or pewter. Some were decorated with simple patterns. Religious items, such as small badges from pilgrimage sites, were also popular. These were worn on clothing as a sign of devotion. Historians use such finds to understand the tastes and wealth of ordinary people. Even small items show that peasants cared about appearance and personal expression. Colours were important, too. Blue dye from woad was common and relatively affordable. Even so, the Church and sometimes the law tried to limit displays of luxury by lower classes. Small decorations were treasured and passed down in families. They brought colour and individuality to plain clothing and to the dark winter months.

[long-pause]

{img:63-herbs-drying-rafters-wise-woman-pestle-mortar}

Most peasants treated illness at home with herbal remedies and traditional knowledge. Professional physicians were rare and expensive in medieval England, and few villagers could consult one. Most illnesses were treated at home. Women in particular knew how to prepare remedies from plants grown in gardens or gathered from hedgerows and fields. Common herbs included sage, mint, thyme, rue, betony and comfrey. Many households hung bunches of herbs from the rafters to dry. Some villages had a woman with special knowledge of healing, who helped neighbours with illness and childbirth. Historians find recipes for remedies in medieval manuscripts, sometimes called leechbooks. Some remedies had real benefits, such as honey for wounds, which has antibacterial properties. Others combined plants with prayers and charms. Even so, medical knowledge was limited, and infections were often dangerous. Monasteries also cared for the sick, and some ran hospitals. Studies of skeletons from Wharram Percy reveal evidence of illness, injury and healed fractures. These remains show that people often survived serious conditions with care.

[long-pause]

{img:64-honey-pot-herbs-wooden-cup-steam-fireside}

Honey was the main sweetener in medieval England, and it was also used in medicine. Before sugar became widely available, honey was the most common sweetener. Many households kept bees in hives made of straw, called skeps. Honey was used in cooking and to make mead, a fermented drink. Beeswax was valuable for candles, especially those used in churches. Honey also had an important place in medicine. It was mixed with herbs to make syrups for coughs and sore throats, and it was applied to wounds. Medieval medical texts recommend honey for many conditions. Modern research has shown that honey can help prevent infection in wounds, giving some support to these practices. Historians note that colds, fevers and chest infections were common in winter. Damp, smoky houses and close living conditions made illness spread easily. Warm drinks of ale or water with honey and herbs were common home remedies. Even so, serious illness was frightening. Families relied on care, rest and prayer, as well as on practical remedies.

[long-pause]

{img:65-neighbour-bringing-broth-bowl-cottage-door-winter}

Neighbours in medieval villages often helped each other in times of illness and hardship. Village communities depended on mutual support. When a family faced illness, injury or the death of a member, neighbours often helped with food, work and care. Historians find evidence of such help in wills, court records and religious writings. The Church taught that caring for the sick and the poor were works of mercy. Parish guilds and fraternities, which became more common in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, helped members in need and paid for funerals. In some places, neighbours helped plough or harvest the land of a sick tenant. Even so, villages were not always harmonious. Court rolls also record quarrels, thefts and disputes over land and boundaries. Historians emphasise that cooperation and conflict existed side by side. Yet in the hardest weeks of winter, practical help mattered greatly. A bowl of broth, a share of fuel or help with animals could make a real difference to a struggling household.

[long-pause]

{img:66-boy-chasing-geese-frosty-yard-sunshine-recovered}

Studies of skeletons from Wharram Percy have revealed much about the health of medieval villagers. Excavations at the churchyard of Wharram Percy uncovered the remains of hundreds of people buried over several centuries. Scientists have studied these skeletons to learn about diet, disease and life expectancy. The results show that many children died young, but those who survived childhood often lived into their forties, fifties or longer. Many adults show signs of hard physical work, such as worn joints and back problems. Some bones show evidence of childhood illness or periods of poor nutrition. Chemical analysis of bones has given information about diet, showing that most protein came from plants and dairy products, with less from meat and fish. Historians and archaeologists caution that skeletons do not record every illness. Many diseases leave no trace on bone. Even so, the Wharram Percy studies are among the most detailed of any English medieval village. They show both the hardships of rural life and the resilience of the people who lived it.

[long-pause]

{img:67-snow-covered-medieval-village-valley-morning}

Heavy snow and severe frosts sometimes struck medieval England, as chronicles record. Medieval chroniclers, often monks, recorded unusual weather in their annals. They noted severe winters, floods, storms and droughts, because these events affected harvests, prices and daily life. Some winters brought heavy snow and long frosts. Chronicles describe rivers freezing, including the Thames in London in certain years. Historians use these accounts, together with records of prices and harvests, to reconstruct the climate of the period. Scientists also study tree rings, ice cores and other natural evidence. Even so, medieval records are incomplete and sometimes exaggerated. A chronicler might describe only the most dramatic events. In a village like Wharram Percy, heavy snow made travel difficult and cut off contact with markets. People stayed close to home, feeding animals and keeping warm. Paths were cleared to the byre, the well and the church. When the sky cleared after snowfall, the valley could look very still and bright under the low winter sun.

[long-pause]

{img:68-shepherd-carrying-lamb-snow-dogs-hills}

Lambing on many English farms took place in late winter and early spring. Ewes on medieval farms usually gave birth between February and April, depending on the region and the breed. In upland areas, lambing often happened later, when the weather was milder. Shepherds watched their flocks closely at this time, day and night. Newborn lambs were vulnerable to cold, snow and predators such as foxes. Shepherds sometimes carried weak lambs to shelter or brought them into their huts or houses to warm them by the fire. Manor accounts record lamb numbers and losses, and payments made to shepherds during lambing. Writers on farming, such as the author of the thirteenth century text known as the Seneschaucy, described the duties of a good shepherd. Historians note that a successful lambing season was vital, both for the wool trade and for the household's future. Even so, losses were common. Bad weather could kill many lambs, and diseases spread quickly in a weakened flock.

[long-pause]

{img:69-mother-drying-snowy-children-by-fire-warm}

Children in medieval villages enjoyed snow and ice, and bone skates have been found by archaeologists. Snow and ice brought hardship, but they also offered opportunities for play. Children threw snowballs, built snow figures and slid on frozen ponds. A famous description of London written in the twelfth century by William Fitzstephen tells how young people tied animal bones to their feet and skated across the frozen marshes, pushing themselves with iron-tipped poles. Archaeologists have found many bone skates, made from the leg bones of horses and cattle, in London, York and other places. Historians use this evidence to show that winter games were common. In villages, children played on frozen ponds and streams. After playing outdoors, they returned home cold and wet. Clothes were dried by the fire, and warm food was provided. Even so, ice could be dangerous. Coroners' records describe accidents in which children fell through ice. Parents warned children about these risks, as they still do today.

[long-pause]

{img:70-thaw-dripping-thatch-melting-snow-fields-mist}

When winter ended, melting snow and rain often caused floods and muddy conditions. The end of winter brought a thaw. Snow melted from roofs and fields, and streams swelled with water. In low-lying areas, floods were common, and fields could become waterlogged. Roads and lanes turned to deep mud, making travel difficult. Historians find records of flooding in chronicles and manorial documents, sometimes noting damage to mills, bridges and crops. On chalk hills, water drained more quickly, but valleys could still flood. Farmers inspected their fields and fences, repaired damage and prepared for spring work. Even so, the thaw was generally welcomed. It meant that the coldest part of the year was ending. The days were longer, and the land was coming back to life. Birds sang more often, and buds appeared on trees and hedges. In medieval calendars, the months of February and March are often illustrated with scenes of pruning, digging and sowing, which show this return to outdoor work.

[long-pause]

{img:71-candlemas-procession-candles-church-february}

Candlemas, on the second of February, was celebrated with processions of candles in church. Candlemas celebrated the presentation of Christ in the Temple and the purification of the Virgin Mary. In medieval England, it was an important feast. Candles were blessed in church, and people carried them in procession. Many took the blessed candles home, believing that they offered protection against storms, illness and evil. Historians find descriptions of Candlemas in liturgical books and in church accounts, which record purchases of wax. The feast also had links to the farming year. In folk tradition, the weather on Candlemas was said to predict the rest of winter. A popular saying claimed that if Candlemas was fair and bright, winter would have another flight, but if it brought clouds and rain, winter was gone and would not come again. Such sayings are recorded mainly in later centuries. Even so, they reflect long traditions. Candlemas marked the midpoint between winter and spring, and it brought light and ceremony into the dark season.

[long-pause]

{img:72-farmer-looking-at-sky-february-grey-fields}

Medieval farmers watched the weather closely, because their survival depended on it. Farmers in the Middle Ages had no weather forecasts. They relied on observation and traditional knowledge. They watched clouds, wind direction, the behaviour of animals and the appearance of plants. Many sayings about the weather were passed down from generation to generation. Some, such as red sky at night being a sign of good weather, have some scientific basis. Others were less reliable. Historians note that weather lore was important because a bad season could mean a poor harvest and hunger. Calendars and almanacs, which became more common in later centuries, recorded such sayings. Even so, the medieval period also had more learned approaches. Some scholars attempted to predict the weather using astronomy and astrology. For ordinary villagers, however, experience was the main guide. Late winter was a time of watching and waiting. Farmers judged when the ground would be dry enough to plough and when it was safe to sow spring crops.

[long-pause]

{img:73-salted-herring-barrel-pedlar-cart-village}

During Lent, the forty days before Easter, meat was forbidden, and salted herring was a common food. Lent began on Ash Wednesday and lasted until Easter. During this period, the Church forbade eating meat, and in many cases also eggs and dairy products. The day before Lent began, Shrove Tuesday, was a time for using up these foods. Making pancakes on this day became a well-known tradition, though it is better documented in later centuries. During Lent, people ate fish, peas, beans and bread. Salted or smoked herring was the most common fish, because it was cheap and kept well. It was caught in large quantities in the North Sea, and towns such as Great Yarmouth were famous for the herring trade. Pedlars brought barrels of herring to villages and markets. Historians find many purchases of herring in household and manorial accounts. Even so, Lent came at a time when food stores were already low. Many people found it a hard season, both physically and spiritually.

[long-pause]

{img:74-shepherd-hut-lantern-night-lambs-ewes-fold}

During lambing, shepherds often stayed with their flocks at night in simple huts. At lambing time, shepherds needed to be close to the flock at all hours. Many stayed overnight in small huts in the fields or near the fold. These shelters were simple, sometimes moveable, and gave protection from wind and rain. Inside, a shepherd might keep a lantern, a few tools and supplies for caring for ewes and lambs. The fold itself was made from hurdles of woven hazel, which could be moved and rebuilt as needed. Manor accounts record payments for hurdles, lanterns and other equipment, as well as extra food or ale for shepherds during lambing. The Seneschaucy describes the shepherd's duty to stay with the flock at night. Historians note that shepherds were skilled workers, trusted with valuable animals. Even so, the work was tiring and cold. The sound of ewes and lambs in the fold, and the light of a lantern on the hillside, were common sights in the countryside in early spring.

[long-pause]

{img:75-spring-ploughing-oxen-team-green-hills-larks}

In spring, fields were ploughed again and sown with barley, oats, peas and beans. When the ground dried in March and April, farmers began spring ploughing. The heavy plough, pulled by oxen, turned the soil for crops such as barley, oats, peas and beans. These spring crops grew during the summer and were harvested in late summer. Barley was used mainly for brewing ale and for bread, oats for porridge and animal feed, and peas and beans for pottage. After ploughing, the ground was harrowed to break up clods and cover the seed. Children and young people scared birds away from the newly sown fields. Manuscripts such as the Luttrell Psalter show ploughing, sowing, harrowing and bird-scaring scenes. Historians use manor accounts to study the amounts of seed sown and the yields obtained. Medieval yields were low by modern standards, often around four times the amount of seed. Even so, the open-field system produced enough food to support a growing population in good years.

[long-pause]

{img:76-woman-planting-garden-spring-sunshine-children-helping}

Peasant gardens provided vegetables, herbs and fruit for the household. Behind each house lay the croft, where families grew vegetables and herbs. In spring, women and children planted onions, leeks, garlic, cabbages, peas and beans, as well as herbs for cooking and medicine. Some crofts also had fruit trees, such as apples and pears. Gardens were important because they belonged to the household itself. Their produce did not need to be shared with the lord, although it might be subject to tithes. Historians find evidence of garden crops in manorial records, in archaeological remains of seeds and pollen, and in medieval gardening texts. One of the earliest English texts on gardening, written in the fifteenth century, lists many plants grown at the time. Archaeologists at Wharram Percy studied the crofts behind the houses. Even so, details of individual gardens are hard to recover. Gardens provided fresh food, flavour and variety. After a long winter of stored food, the first fresh greens were especially welcome.

[long-pause]

{img:77-may-day-young-people-hawthorn-blossom-dawn-singing}

Easter and May Day were joyful festivals that marked the end of winter and the coming of summer. Easter was the most important feast of the Christian year. After the fasting of Lent, people celebrated with church services, feasting and the return of meat, eggs and dairy foods. Eggs were sometimes given as gifts. Soon afterwards came May Day, on the first of May, which celebrated the arrival of summer. Young people went out early in the morning to gather branches of hawthorn and other greenery, a custom known as bringing in the May. Houses and churches were decorated with flowers. In many places, people danced and sang. Historians find descriptions of May Day customs in medieval literature and in later records. Maypoles are documented in England from the fourteenth century onwards. Even so, the Church sometimes disapproved of these celebrations. Some customs may have had roots in older traditions. For rural communities, these festivals marked a turning point. The long, difficult winter was over, and the warmer months had arrived.

[long-pause]

{img:78-grandmother-bench-doorway-sunshine-swallows-spring}

Spring brought longer days, warmer weather and the return of migrating birds such as swallows. By April and May, the countryside was changing quickly. Days grew longer, and the sun was warmer. Grass began to grow again, giving animals fresh grazing. Cows gave more milk, and hens laid more eggs. Swallows, cuckoos and other birds returned from their winter homes in the south. Medieval people did not know where swallows went in winter. Some writers believed they hibernated in mud or at the bottom of ponds. Historians find these ideas in medieval and later natural history texts. Even so, people knew that the return of the swallow marked the arrival of spring. Older people often sat outside their doors in the sunshine, after months spent close to the fire. Work in the fields and gardens increased. For peasant families, surviving another winter was an achievement. It depended on careful preparation, hard work, community support and good fortune. Spring brought the hope of a good harvest in the year ahead.

[long-pause]

{img:79-medieval-village-summer-harvest-golden-1320s}

From 1315 to 1317, England suffered a severe famine caused by heavy rains and failed harvests. The early fourteenth century brought great hardship. In 1315, heavy rain fell through the spring and summer across northern Europe. Crops rotted in the fields, and harvests failed. The same happened in 1316, and conditions remained difficult in 1317. Food prices rose sharply, and many people died of hunger and disease. Historians call this the Great Famine. It affected England, France, the Low Countries, Germany and Scandinavia. Chroniclers described people eating anything they could find. Manor records show high prices, sales of livestock and increased deaths among tenants. Soon afterwards, a cattle disease killed large numbers of oxen and cows, making recovery even harder. Historians estimate that a significant proportion of the population died in some areas. Even so, many villages, including Wharram Percy, survived and continued. The famine weakened many communities. It was followed by the even greater disaster of the Black Death in 1348 and 1349.

[long-pause]

{img:80-free-peasant-family-better-house-1400s}

After the Black Death, surviving peasants often gained better wages, more land and greater freedom. The Black Death reached England in 1348 and killed perhaps between a third and a half of the population. In the decades that followed, the number of workers was much smaller, and land was more plentiful. Surviving peasants could demand higher wages and better terms. Lords tried to control wages through laws, such as the Statute of Labourers of 1351, but these were difficult to enforce. Discontent contributed to the Peasants' Revolt of 1381. Over time, villeinage declined, and many peasants became free tenants paying rent. Historians note that living standards for many ordinary people improved in the fifteenth century. Diets included more meat and ale, and houses were sometimes larger and better built. Archaeology at Wharram Percy shows changes in houses during this period. Even so, the changes were gradual and varied from place to place. They transformed rural society over several generations and helped bring an end to the medieval manorial system.

[long-pause]

{img:81-grassy-bumps-hollows-evening-light-sheep}

By around 1500, Wharram Percy had been abandoned, and its land was used as sheep pasture. During the fifteenth century, the population of Wharram Percy declined. Families left, and houses fell into ruin. Landowners found that keeping large flocks of sheep was more profitable than growing grain with many tenants. In the late fifteenth century, the remaining villagers were evicted, and the land was turned into pasture. Historians have found documents recording the conversion, including the number of houses destroyed. Wharram Percy was one of many deserted medieval villages in England. Historians estimate that several thousand villages were abandoned between the fourteenth and sixteenth centuries. Some were deserted after the Black Death, others because of enclosure for sheep. Even so, the church at Wharram Percy continued to serve nearby communities for several centuries. It remained in use until the twentieth century. Meanwhile, the house sites were covered by grass. Their outlines remained as low banks and hollows in the fields, grazed by sheep for hundreds of years.

[long-pause]

{img:82-archaeological-finds-tray-bone-comb-pottery-spindle}

From 1950, Wharram Percy was excavated by archaeologists for about forty years. In 1948, the historian Maurice Beresford visited Wharram Percy while researching deserted villages. Two years later, he began excavations there, soon joined by the archaeologist John Hurst. Their project continued every summer until 1990, making it one of the longest-running archaeological excavations in Britain. Many volunteers took part, including students and members of the public. The excavations uncovered houses, the church, the manor houses, the mill and the churchyard. Archaeologists found pottery, tools, coins, buckles, combs, spindle whorls and animal bones. They also studied the remains of hundreds of people buried in the churchyard. The project changed the way historians understood medieval villages. It showed that houses were rebuilt frequently and that village layouts changed over time. Even so, the results took many years to publish fully, in a series of detailed reports. Today, Wharram Percy remains one of the most studied medieval villages in Europe.

[long-pause]

{img:83-wharram-percy-valley-today-church-ruin-pond}

Today, Wharram Percy is cared for by English Heritage and is open to visitors. The site of Wharram Percy now belongs to the care of English Heritage. Visitors can walk along a path down into the valley from a nearby car park. The roofless ruins of the church of Saint Martin still stand beside the stream. The tower is partly preserved, and the outlines of earlier phases can be seen in the walls. The church was last used for services in the mid twentieth century, and parts of it later collapsed. Near the church is the restored millpond, surrounded by reeds and trees. On the slopes above, the outlines of houses and crofts can be seen as grassy banks and hollows. Information boards explain what life was like in the medieval village. Historians and archaeologists continue to study the site and its records. Even so, it is now a quiet and peaceful place. Sheep still graze across the fields where the villagers once lived and worked.

[long-pause]

{img:84-old-mill-pond-reeds-ducks-willows-evening}

The millpond at Wharram Percy was originally created by damming the stream to power the village mill. In the Middle Ages, a dam was built across the stream at Wharram Percy to create a pond. The water from this pond drove a watermill, where villagers' grain was ground into flour. Archaeologists discovered that the dam and the pond had a long history, with earlier phases going back before the Norman Conquest. Mills were important sources of income for lords, who required tenants to grind their grain there and pay a share. Historians note that disputes over mill rights were common in medieval England. Some peasants secretly used hand mills at home to avoid the fee, and lords sometimes confiscated them. Today, the pond has been restored. It is surrounded by reeds and willows and is home to ducks and other wildlife. Even so, it serves as a reminder of the busy village life that once surrounded it. The pond is one of the best-preserved features of the site.

[long-pause]

{img:85-visitors-walking-grassy-outlines-medieval-houses}

Visitors to Wharram Percy can walk among the outlines of medieval houses and imagine village life. The grassy banks at Wharram Percy mark the positions of house walls, yards and property boundaries. From above, the layout of the village can be seen clearly, with rows of house plots along the hillside. Visitors can follow the line of the main street and see where houses once stood. The excavations showed that many houses were rebuilt several times on slightly different sites. Historians use the site to explain how medieval villages were organised. School groups and history enthusiasts often visit. Aerial photographs and modern survey techniques have added further detail to the picture. Even so, much of what once existed is invisible. Wooden walls, thatched roofs and furniture have long since rotted away. Yet the landscape still preserves the shape of the village. Standing in the quiet valley, visitors gain a sense of the scale of the community. A hundred and fifty people once lived, worked and celebrated here.

[long-pause]

{img:86-illuminated-manuscript-margin-ploughing-scene}

The Luttrell Psalter, made around 1320 to 1340, contains some of the most famous images of medieval rural life. The Luttrell Psalter is an illuminated manuscript made for Sir Geoffrey Luttrell, a wealthy landowner in Lincolnshire. It contains the Psalms and other religious texts. Its margins are decorated with many lively images of everyday life. These include ploughing, sowing, harrowing, harvesting and threshing. Other scenes show women spinning and feeding chickens, a cook preparing food and people playing games. There are also many fantastical creatures and humorous scenes. Historians value the manuscript as a rich visual source for rural life in fourteenth century England. Even so, they note that the images were created for a wealthy patron, and they may present an idealised view of peasant work. Some scenes may also be symbolic or humorous rather than realistic. The Luttrell Psalter is now kept in the British Library in London. Its images appear in many books about medieval history and help bring the lives of ordinary people into view.

[long-pause]

{img:87-winter-dusk-valley-church-ruin-frost-sheep-stars}

The story of Wharram Percy shows how ordinary families lived and survived through medieval winters. Around 1300, Wharram Percy was a small but lively village. Its people lived in houses of wood, clay and thatch, sharing them with their animals. They grew grain in open fields, kept sheep, cattle and pigs, and depended on the harvest. In winter, they relied on stored food, careful use of fuel, warm wool clothing and the support of their neighbours. The Church calendar gave shape to the season, with Advent, Christmas, Candlemas and Lent. Historians and archaeologists have reconstructed this way of life through documents, excavations and studies of skeletons and objects. Even so, much about these people remains unknown. Most left no written words of their own. Yet the evidence shows their skill, endurance and resourcefulness. Today, the village is silent. The church stands roofless in the valley, and sheep graze over the old house sites. On frosty evenings, the stars shine over the quiet hills of the Yorkshire Wolds.

[long-pause]

That was the winter of a medieval village: the stored grain and salted pork, the smoky hearth and the rushlight, the shepherd in the snow, the carols at Christmas, and the first lambs of spring. It was also the story of a village that was later emptied, and found again by patient diggers.
