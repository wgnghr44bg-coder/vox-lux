Tonight we are going more than seven hundred years back, to a small farming village in a quiet green valley in the north of England, as the long winter of the year 1300 begins.

[long-pause]

It is a cold evening in late November.

[pause]

The sun has already gone down behind the hills, and the sky is turning from pale gold to grey. Along the bottom of the valley, a little stream runs between willows and alders. Above it, on a gentle slope, there is a cluster of low houses with steep roofs of thatch, and thin blue threads of smoke rising from them into the still, cold air.

[pause]

If you enjoy slow and gentle stories like this, you are welcome to subscribe to the channel. Now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

The village is called Wharram Percy.

[pause]

It lies in a fold of the Yorkshire Wolds, a country of rolling chalk hills, in the north of England, about twenty miles from the old city of York. It is not an important place. Nobody outside the valley has ever heard of it. But about a hundred and fifty people live here, in about thirty houses, and for them, it is the whole world.

[pause]

Around the village, on the slopes and the tops of the hills, there are great open fields, without hedges or fences, divided into hundreds of long, narrow strips, each one belonging to a different family. Beyond the fields, there is rough pasture, where the sheep graze, and a little wood, and then the wide, empty hills, rolling away under the sky.

[long-pause]

At the bottom of the valley, beside the stream, there is a small stone church, with a square tower.

[pause]

It is the oldest building in the village, and the only one built of stone. Its bell rings for Mass on Sundays and holy days, and for births and weddings and burials, and on ordinary days, it rings out the hours of prayer across the valley, so that the people in the fields know what time it is.

[pause]

{img:small-stone-church-square-tower-stream-willows}
Beside the church, there is the priest's house, and a little way off, a mill, where the stream has been dammed to make a pond, and a great wooden wheel turns slowly in the water, grinding the villagers' grain into flour.

[long-pause]

On the hill above the village, there is the manor house, where the lord of the manor lives when he is here, which is not very often.

[pause]

The lord owns the village, and the land, and most of the people in it, in a way. He is a member of a great family called the Percys, who own many manors and villages in the north of England. He lives most of the time in another, grander house, far away, and his business here is looked after by his steward and his reeve.

[pause]

The manor house is a large stone hall, with a great fireplace, a kitchen, a chapel, and barns and stables around a courtyard. When the lord is away, it is mostly empty and quiet.

[long-pause]

The people of Wharram Percy are farmers, almost all of them.

[pause]

They grow wheat and barley and oats and peas and beans in the open fields. They keep sheep on the hills, for wool and meat and milk, and a few cows, and pigs, and geese and hens. They work hard, from before dawn until after dark, in all weathers, all year round, and almost everything they eat and wear and use, they grow or make themselves.

[pause]

{img:peasant-family-walking-home-fields-dusk-tools}
Most of them have never travelled further than York, and many of them have never been even that far. They know every field and every path and every tree and every stone in their valley, and every person in their village, and all their parents and grandparents before them.

[long-pause]

Winter is the hardest time of the year for them.

[pause]

The days are short and cold and dark. The fields are bare. Nothing grows. The wind blows across the open hills, and the rain and the sleet and the snow come down, and the paths turn to mud, and the stream floods, and then freezes. The animals must be fed, and kept alive, until the spring. The family must be fed, and kept warm, until the spring.

[pause]

Everything depends on how well they have prepared, in the autumn, for the long months ahead.

[long-pause]

This year, the people of Wharram Percy have prepared well.

[pause]

The harvest was good. The barns are full of sheaves of wheat and barley, waiting to be threshed. The lofts are full of hay. The pigs have been fattened on acorns in the wood. There is firewood stacked against the walls of the houses, and salted meat hanging from the rafters, and barrels of ale in the corners.

[pause]

{img:village-at-night-firelight-doors-stars-cold}
And so, on this cold November evening, as the darkness falls over the valley, the families of Wharram Percy go into their houses, and close their doors against the cold, and gather round their fires, ready for the winter.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-longhouse-exterior-thatch-wattle-daub}
Let us go into one of the houses in Wharram Percy, and see how a family lives there through the winter.

[pause]

It is a long, low building, about forty or fifty feet long, and about fifteen feet wide, standing in its own little yard, called a toft, with a fence of woven hazel around it, and a garden behind.

[long-pause]

The house is built mostly of wood and earth and straw.

[pause]

Its frame is made of great curved timbers, called crucks, cut from a single oak tree split down the middle, standing in pairs along the length of the house, leaning together at the top, like the ribs of an upturned boat. Between them, the walls are made of woven hazel and willow branches, called wattle, plastered over with a thick mixture of clay and chalk and straw and cow dung, called daub, which dries hard, and keeps out the wind.

[pause]

The lower part of the walls is built of chalk stones, from the hills around, to keep the wood away from the damp ground. The roof is steep, and thickly thatched with straw, a foot or more deep, which keeps the house warm in winter and cool in summer, and sheds the rain.

[long-pause]

There is only one door, in the middle of the long side of the house, and a few small windows, with no glass, only wooden shutters, which are closed at night and in bad weather.

[pause]

When you step inside, out of the cold, the first thing you notice is the smell: wood smoke and animals and hay and cooking, all mixed together. The second thing is the warmth. And the third is the dimness, because there is little light, except from the fire, and from the open door.

[pause]

{img:longhouse-interior-dim-firelight-smoke-rafters}
The house is divided into two parts.

[long-pause]

At one end, down a little slope, there is the byre, where the animals live in winter.

[pause]

The oxen are kept there, the great patient beasts that pull the plough, and the cow, and perhaps a few sheep or a pig, in stalls made of wattle, with straw on the floor, and a drain running out under the wall. The animals are very precious, and the family keeps them close, where they are safe and warm, and where the family can keep an eye on them.

[pause]

{img:oxen-cow-byre-straw-stalls-lantern}
And the animals, in turn, help to keep the house warm. Their bodies give off heat, and their breath, and on a cold winter night, the family can hear them breathing and shifting and chewing in the dark, at the other end of the house, which is a comforting sound.

[long-pause]

At the other end of the house, separated from the byre by a low wattle partition, or simply by the cross passage from the door, is the part where the family lives.

[pause]

In the middle of the floor, there is the hearth: a low, flat bed of stones, or a circle of clay, where the fire burns, day and night, all winter long. There is no chimney. The smoke rises up into the dark space under the roof, and finds its way out slowly through the thatch, or through a little hole at the top.

[pause]

{img:central-hearth-fire-cooking-pot-family-around}
The smoke blackens the rafters and the underside of the thatch, and makes the house smell of wood smoke, and makes the people's eyes water sometimes. But it also keeps away the insects, and dries the meat and the cheese hanging from the rafters, and keeps the thatch free of rot.

[long-pause]

The floor is made of beaten earth and chalk, covered with rushes or straw, which are swept out and replaced every few weeks.

[pause]

Around the hearth, there are a few pieces of simple furniture: a wooden table on trestles, which can be taken down and leaned against the wall; a bench or two; a few stools, made of a slice of tree trunk with three legs; a chest, where the family keeps its best clothes and its few precious things. On shelves and hooks around the walls, there are wooden bowls and spoons, clay pots, a few iron tools, baskets, and bunches of dried herbs.

[pause]

Hanging from the rafters, above the fire, there are sides of bacon, smoked and salted, and strings of onions, and bunches of herbs, and a cheese or two, wrapped in cloth.

[long-pause]

At the far end of the living part of the house, there might be a little inner room, separated by a wattle wall or a hanging blanket, where the parents sleep, and where the family keeps its stores of grain and ale.

[pause]

But most of the family sleeps around the fire, on mattresses stuffed with straw or chaff, laid on the floor, or on low wooden platforms, covered with woollen blankets and sheepskins. In the coldest weather, the whole family sleeps close together, near the warm embers of the hearth, with the dogs curled up at their feet.

[pause]

{img:family-sleeping-straw-mattresses-near-embers}
It is a simple house. But it is warm, and dry, and safe. And on a winter's night, with the fire glowing, and the wind blowing outside, and the animals breathing softly at the other end, it is a very cosy place to be.

[long-pause]

{img:peasant-family-portrait-hearth-evening-1300}
In this house lives a family that we might call the family of John the Ploughman.

[pause]

We do not know their real names. The people of villages like Wharram Percy left very few records of their lives: a name here and there in the account books of the manor, a few words in the records of the manor court. But we know a great deal about how they lived, from those records, and from the things they left behind, which archaeologists have found in the ground, and from the pictures that monks and artists painted in the margins of their books.

[long-pause]

John is about thirty-five years old.

[pause]

He is short and strong and weathered, with a brown face and big rough hands, and a short beard. He has worked on the land since he was a small boy, and he knows everything about it: how to plough a straight furrow, how to sow seed evenly, how to read the weather in the sky, how to care for an ox when it is sick.

[pause]

He holds a little more land than most of his neighbours: about thirty acres, in strips scattered across the open fields, which his father held before him, and his grandfather before that. He and his neighbours share their oxen and their ploughs, and work together in the fields.

[long-pause]

His wife is called Agnes.

[pause]

She is a few years younger than John, quick and capable and cheerful, with a broad, kind face, and her hair always covered by a linen kerchief. She runs the household, and that is a great deal of work. She cooks and bakes and brews the ale. She milks the cow, and makes the butter and the cheese. She looks after the hens and the geese and the pig, and the garden behind the house. She spins and weaves and sews, and makes all the family's clothes.

[pause]

{img:peasant-woman-kerchief-churning-butter-doorway}
And at harvest time, and at other busy times of the year, she works in the fields beside her husband, with a sickle in her hand.

[long-pause]

They have four children.

[pause]

The eldest is a boy of fourteen, called Will, who already works beside his father in the fields, and who will one day take over the land. Then there is a girl of eleven, called Alice, who helps her mother in the house, and looks after the younger children, and is learning to spin. Then there is a boy of seven, called Tom, who looks after the geese, and scares the birds away from the newly sown seed, and runs errands. And there is a baby, a little girl, who is not yet two, and who toddles about the house, and gets into everything.

[pause]

{img:peasant-children-geese-yard-autumn}
There were other children too, who did not live to grow up. In those days, many children did not. The family remembers them, and prays for them, at the church, on Sundays.

[long-pause]

John's mother lives with them too.

[pause]

She is very old, perhaps sixty, which is old for those days. Her husband, John's father, died some years ago, and when he died, John took over the land, and promised to look after his mother for the rest of her life. That was the custom.

[pause]

{img:old-grandmother-spinning-fireside-stool}
She has her own place by the fire, on a stool, near the warmest corner. She cannot do heavy work any more, but she spins wool, and minds the baby, and tells the children stories, and gives advice to Agnes, whether Agnes wants it or not. She knows the old remedies for coughs and colds and fevers, and the old songs, and the names of all the saints and their feast days.

[long-pause]

Life in the family is hard work for everyone, even the youngest.

[pause]

The children learn to work as soon as they can walk. They fetch water from the stream and the well, gather firewood, feed the hens, collect the eggs, mind the baby, and help in the garden and the fields. There is no school in Wharram Percy. The children learn everything they need to know from their parents and their grandparents, by watching and helping.

[pause]

Only a very few children, the sons of the richer families, or boys who show a special talent, are taught to read and write a little by the priest, in Latin, so that one day they might become priests themselves.

[long-pause]

But it is not all work.

[pause]

There is time for play, too, especially for the children. They play games of chase and hide-and-seek around the houses and the yards. They play with balls made of rags or a pig's bladder. They play with little toys that their fathers carve for them out of wood: tops and whistles and little animals. They climb the trees by the stream, and fish in the millpond, and slide on the ice in winter.

[pause]

{img:medieval-children-playing-ball-village-lane}
And in the evenings, the whole family sits together by the fire, and talks, and laughs, and sings, and tells stories. They are poor, by the standards of our time. But they have each other, and their home, and their village, and their neighbours. And on a winter's evening, by the fire, that is a great deal.

[long-pause]

{img:open-fields-strips-ploughmen-oxen-autumn}
The village of Wharram Percy belongs to its lord, and the people of the village have duties to him.

[pause]

Most of the families, like John's, are what were called villeins, or serfs. They are not slaves. They have their own houses and their own land, and they can keep most of what they grow. But they are not free, either. They are bound to the manor. They cannot leave the village without the lord's permission. And in return for their land, they must work for the lord, on his land, a certain number of days every week.

[long-pause]

The lord's land, which is called the demesne, is scattered among the strips of the open fields, just like the villagers' own land.

[pause]

So, two or three days a week, John and his son must go and plough and sow and harvest the lord's strips, before they can work on their own. At busy times of the year, like the harvest, they must work extra days, which are called boon days, when the whole village works together for the lord. On those days, the lord gives them a meal, of bread and cheese and ale, and sometimes meat, and everyone eats together in the fields.

[pause]

The villagers also pay rents to the lord, in money, or in kind: a few hens at Christmas, a basket of eggs at Easter. They must grind their grain at the lord's mill, and pay the miller a share of the flour. And when they marry, or when someone dies, they must pay a fee to the lord.

[long-pause]

The lord's business in the village is managed by a reeve.

[pause]

The reeve is one of the villagers, chosen by the others, every year, to organize the work on the lord's land, and to keep the accounts. He is a busy and important man, who must be fair to his neighbours, and honest with the lord, which is not always easy.

[pause]

{img:reeve-tally-sticks-counting-sheep-barn}
He keeps track of everything with notched wooden sticks, called tallies, and with his memory, because he cannot write. Once a year, the lord's steward comes, with a clerk who can write, and they go through the accounts together, and write them down on a long roll of parchment.

[long-pause]

Every few weeks, there is a meeting of the manor court, in the hall of the manor house.

[pause]

All the men of the village must come. The steward presides. And they deal with all the business of the village: who has inherited which land, who has not done his work for the lord, whose pig has got into whose garden, whose ale was too weak, who has been quarrelling with his neighbour, who has let his hedge fall down, who has taken wood from the lord's wood without permission.

[pause]

{img:manor-court-hall-villagers-benches-steward}
The punishments are mostly small fines, of a few pence. The court is a way of keeping the peace, and settling disputes, and making sure that everyone follows the customs of the village, which have been handed down for generations.

[long-pause]

The village works together, in many ways.

[pause]

The open fields are farmed according to rules that everyone agrees on. Every year, one of the great fields is left fallow, to rest, while the animals graze on it and manure it. The other fields are sown, one with wheat or rye in the autumn, and the other with barley or oats or peas and beans in the spring. Everyone must plough and sow and harvest at the same time, in the same field.

[pause]

{img:villagers-ploughing-together-oxen-team-hill}
The ploughs are heavy, and need a team of six or eight oxen to pull them, and no family has that many. So neighbours share their oxen, and plough each other's strips, in turn. The village shepherd looks after everyone's sheep together. The village swineherd takes everyone's pigs to the wood to feed on acorns. In the harvest, everyone helps everyone else.

[long-pause]

The most important person in the village, after the lord, is the priest.

[pause]

He lives in the house beside the church, and he has his own land, called the glebe, which he farms like the other villagers. He also receives a tenth of everything that the villagers grow, which is called the tithe: a tenth of the grain, a tenth of the lambs, a tenth of the eggs, a tenth of the wool.

[pause]

The priest says Mass every Sunday and on the feast days, and baptizes the babies, and marries the young couples, and visits the sick, and buries the dead. He blesses the fields in the spring, and the harvest in the autumn. He is a villager's son himself, perhaps, and knows everyone, and everyone knows him.

[long-pause]

On Sundays, the whole village goes to church.

[pause]

The families walk down the hill to the little stone church, in their best clothes, and stand together in the nave, because there are no seats, while the priest says the Mass in Latin, at the altar, behind a carved wooden screen. The villagers do not understand the Latin, but they know when to kneel, and when to stand, and when to cross themselves, and the bell rings at the most holy moment.

[pause]

{img:villagers-standing-church-nave-candles-sunday}
The walls of the church are painted with bright pictures of the saints and the stories of the Bible, so that the people, who cannot read, can learn the stories by looking at the pictures. And after Mass, the families stand around in the churchyard, in the cold, talking and gossiping, and arranging their week, before they walk back up the hill to their houses, and their Sunday dinner.

[long-pause]

{img:autumn-ploughing-winter-wheat-oxen-gulls}
For the people of Wharram Percy, the preparations for winter begin long before the first frost.

[pause]

They begin at Michaelmas, the feast of Saint Michael, at the end of September, when the harvest is in, and the rents are paid, and the farming year begins again.

[long-pause]

After Michaelmas, the men go out into the fields to plough for the winter wheat.

[pause]

The great wooden plough, with its iron blade, is pulled slowly up and down the long strips by a team of oxen, walking in pairs, with a boy walking beside them, calling to them by name and tapping them with a long stick, and the ploughman behind, holding the handles, guiding the blade through the heavy earth. Gulls and rooks follow the plough, picking at the worms in the fresh brown furrows.

[pause]

Then the sower walks across the ploughed ground, with a basket of seed hanging from his neck, scattering the grain in wide, even sweeps, left and right, as he walks. And the children follow behind with slings and stones and clappers, scaring away the crows, who want to eat the seed.

[pause]

The wheat will lie in the earth all winter, and sprout in the spring.

[long-pause]

In October, the pigs are taken to the wood.

[pause]

It is called pannage. The village swineherd drives all the pigs of the village into the lord's wood, where the oak trees and the beech trees are dropping their nuts, and the pigs root about in the fallen leaves, eating the acorns and the beech nuts, and growing fat. In the evening, the swineherd blows his horn, and the pigs come trotting back, and he drives them home to the village.

[pause]

{img:swineherd-pigs-oak-wood-acorns-autumn-leaves}
The villagers must pay the lord a small fee for every pig that feeds in his wood. But it is worth it. A fat pig is a great treasure for the winter.

[long-pause]

At the same time, the family is gathering in food from the garden and the hedgerows.

[pause]

Agnes and the children pick the last of the apples and pears from the little trees behind the house, and lay them out carefully on straw in the loft, so that they will keep through the winter. They gather blackberries and sloes and hazelnuts from the hedges, and crab apples, which Agnes makes into a sharp, sour juice, called verjuice, for cooking.

[pause]

{img:children-picking-hazelnuts-hedgerow-baskets}
They dig up the onions and the leeks and the turnips from the garden, and store them in the cool corner of the house. They pick the peas and the beans and dry them in the sun, and store them in sacks, for the pottage in winter. They pick herbs, and hang them in bunches from the rafters to dry.

[long-pause]

In November comes the most important day of the autumn: Martinmas, the feast of Saint Martin, on the eleventh of November.

[pause]

This is the time when the animals are slaughtered.

[pause]

There is not enough hay and grass to feed all the animals through the long winter. So the farmers keep the oxen, which they need for ploughing, and the cows, and the sheep, and a few pigs for breeding, and the rest are slaughtered, and their meat is salted and smoked, to keep through the winter.

[long-pause]

The slaughter is a busy and important time, and the whole family helps, and the neighbours too.

[pause]

Nothing is wasted. The meat is cut up, and rubbed with salt, and packed into barrels, or hung up in the smoke of the hearth, to be smoked into bacon and ham. The fat is melted down into lard, for cooking and for making candles. The blood is made into black puddings. The intestines are cleaned and stuffed with meat and oatmeal and herbs, to make sausages. The skin is tanned into leather. The bones are boiled for broth.

[pause]

{img:sides-of-bacon-hanging-smoke-rafters-glow}
In the evening, there is a feast, with fresh meat, roasted over the fire, which is a rare treat, and the neighbours come to share it, and there is ale, and singing, and laughter.

[long-pause]

By the end of November, the house is full of food for the winter.

[pause]

There are sides of bacon and hams hanging in the smoke from the rafters. There are barrels of salted pork in the corner. There are sacks of dried peas and beans, and baskets of onions and apples. There are cheeses wrapped in cloth on the shelf. There is a sack of oatmeal, and in the barn, the sheaves of wheat and barley, waiting to be threshed through the winter, a little at a time, for bread and ale.

[pause]

John and Agnes look around at it all, in the firelight, and feel content. If the winter is not too long, and nothing goes wrong, there will be enough.

[long-pause]

And there is firewood too.

[pause]

All through the autumn, John and Will have been cutting wood in the lord's wood, where the villagers are allowed to take fallen branches, and the dead wood, and to cut the hazel and the willow that grow in the coppices. They have brought it home on their backs and on a little cart, and stacked it high against the wall of the house, under the eaves of the thatch, where it will stay dry.

[pause]

{img:woodpile-against-cottage-wall-frosty-evening}
There is a saying in the village: wood warms you twice, once when you cut it, and once when you burn it.

[long-pause]

{img:peasants-walking-to-market-town-cart-autumn-road}
Before the winter comes, there is one more important journey to make: to the market.

[pause]

There are some things that the family cannot grow or make for themselves. They need salt, a great deal of it, for salting the meat at Martinmas. They need iron: a new blade for the plough, nails, a knife, a new pot. They need a few things for the house, and perhaps a little cloth, or a pair of shoes for the growing boys.

[long-pause]

So, one morning in late October, John and Agnes set off for the market town of Malton, about eight miles away.

[pause]

They leave before dawn, in the cold and the dark, with Will, and a neighbour's cart, pulled by an old horse, loaded with things to sell: a few sacks of oats, a basket of eggs, some cheeses, a bundle of wool that Agnes has spun, two fat geese, tied by their legs, protesting loudly. The younger children stay at home with the grandmother.

[pause]

The road winds over the chalk hills, through the open fields of other villages, past churches and farms and flocks of sheep. As the sun comes up, other people join them on the road, from other villages, all going to the market: farmers with carts and packhorses, women with baskets on their heads, a man driving a few pigs, a shepherd with a flock of sheep.

[long-pause]

Malton is a busy little town, on a river, with a castle and a priory, and a wide market place in the middle.

[pause]

On market day, the market place is crowded and noisy and full of colour. There are stalls of every kind, under awnings of canvas: stalls selling bread and pies and cakes, cheese and butter and eggs, fish from the coast, meat and poultry, onions and apples and nuts. There are stalls selling cloth, in every colour, and ribbons and buttons and needles, shoes and belts and gloves, pots and pans and knives.

[pause]

{img:medieval-market-place-stalls-awnings-crowds-castle}
There is a blacksmith's forge, ringing with hammers, and a cooper selling barrels, and a potter with rows of jugs and bowls, and a saltman, with great sacks of salt from the salt pans on the coast.

[long-pause]

John and Agnes sell their oats and their eggs and their cheeses, and the wool, and the geese, to the traders and the townspeople.

[pause]

Then, with the pennies they have earned, they buy what they need: a great sack of salt, for the winter, which is heavy and expensive; a new iron blade for the plough, from the blacksmith; a little iron pot, to replace the one that cracked; a pair of leather shoes for Will; a few needles; a little bag of spices, pepper and ginger, as a treat for Christmas.

[pause]

{img:saltman-sacks-of-salt-market-stall-buyer}
Agnes stops at a stall selling ribbons, and looks at them for a long time, and then buys a little piece of blue ribbon for Alice, to wear at Christmas. John pretends not to notice.

[long-pause]

There is entertainment at the market, too.

[pause]

There are jugglers and acrobats, and a man with a dancing bear, and a minstrel singing songs with a little harp. There is a pedlar selling holy relics, which he says are pieces of the true cross and hairs from the beard of Saint Peter, which nobody quite believes. There is a preacher, a friar in a brown robe, standing on a cart, preaching to a crowd about the end of the world and the need to repent.

[pause]

{img:minstrel-juggler-crowd-market-medieval-afternoon}
And there are alehouses and pie shops around the market place, where the country people go to eat and drink and talk, and hear the news from the wider world: about the king, and his wars in Scotland, and the price of wool in Flanders, and the weather in the south.

[long-pause]

At midday, John and Agnes and Will sit on a wall at the edge of the market, and eat a meat pie each, bought from a stall, hot and greasy and delicious, a rare treat. Will has never been to the town before, and he stares at everything with wide eyes: the castle, the priory, the stone houses, the crowds, the strange people from faraway places.

[pause]

Then, in the early afternoon, they load up the cart with their purchases, and set off for home, before it gets dark.

[long-pause]

The journey home is slow and tiring.

[pause]

The cart is heavy, and the old horse is slow, and the road is muddy, and the days are short in October. The sun sets as they come over the last hill, and the valley of Wharram Percy appears below them, in the dusk, with the smoke rising from the houses, and the little church tower by the stream.

[pause]

{img:family-cart-coming-over-hill-village-smoke-dusk}
The children and the grandmother are waiting at the door, with a lantern, and the fire is burning, and there is pottage in the pot. The children crowd around to see what has been bought, and Alice is given her blue ribbon, and is so happy that she cannot speak.

[pause]

That night, the family sleeps well, tired from the long day, with the sack of salt in the corner, and the new plough blade by the door, ready for the winter.

[long-pause]

{img:pottage-cauldron-hearth-wooden-spoon-steam}
In the winter, the most important food for the family is pottage.

[pause]

Pottage is a thick soup, or stew, cooked in a great iron or clay pot, hanging from a chain over the fire, or standing on three little legs in the embers. Agnes keeps it simmering all day long, and adds whatever she has to it: dried peas and beans, oats or barley, onions and leeks and cabbages from the garden, herbs, and a little bacon or salt pork, for flavour.

[long-pause]

Every day, a little more is added to the pot, and a little is eaten, so that the pottage goes on and on, all through the winter, changing a little from day to day, but never quite running out.

[pause]

Many centuries later, English children would sing a little rhyme about it: Pease porridge hot, pease porridge cold, pease porridge in the pot, nine days old.

[pause]

The family eats the pottage from wooden bowls, with wooden spoons, sitting around the table by the fire. It is warm and thick and filling, and on a cold winter's day, after hours of work in the frost, there is nothing better.

[long-pause]

With the pottage, there is bread.

[pause]

The bread of the peasants is not white, like the bread of the lord. It is dark and heavy, made of a mixture of rye and barley, or wheat and rye together, which is called maslin, and sometimes, in a bad year, of peas and beans ground into flour.

[pause]

{img:dark-rye-loaves-baking-clay-oven}
Agnes makes the dough in a wooden trough, and the family's loaves are baked in the village oven, which belongs to the lord, and the baker takes a small part of the dough as payment. Or, in some houses, she bakes flat loaves on a hot stone in the embers of the hearth. A loaf of good bread, with a piece of cheese and an onion, is a fine meal.

[long-pause]

And there is ale.

[pause]

Everyone drinks ale, morning, noon and night, the men and the women and the children. Water from the stream and the well is not always safe to drink, and ale is safer, and it is also food: thick and nourishing, made from barley.

[pause]

{img:woman-brewing-ale-wooden-tub-steam-barley}
Agnes brews it herself, every week or so. She soaks the barley in water until it sprouts, then dries it by the fire, to make malt. She grinds the malt, and soaks it in hot water in a big wooden tub, and drains off the sweet liquid, and adds yeast, and leaves it to work for a few days, until it is ready. The ale is weak and cloudy and a little sweet, and it does not keep long, so it must be brewed often.

[pause]

When a woman in the village has brewed more ale than her family needs, she hangs a branch of green leaves over her door, as a sign, and her neighbours come and buy a jug of it.

[long-pause]

The family also has the milk and the butter and the cheese from their cow and their sheep, when they are giving milk, which is mostly in the spring and summer.

[pause]

Agnes makes the butter by churning the cream in a tall wooden churn, and salts it, so that it will keep. She makes the cheese by warming the milk, adding rennet, and pressing the curds in a wooden mould, and the cheeses are wrapped in cloth and stored on a shelf, and eaten through the winter.

[pause]

{img:hard-cheeses-shelf-cloth-wrapped-dim-light}
In the winter, the cow gives very little milk, so the cheese and the butter from the summer are very precious.

[long-pause]

There are eggs, too, from the hens, though they lay very few in the dark winter months.

[pause]

There is fish, sometimes, from the millpond, or from the stream: eels and trout and roach. On Fridays, and in Lent, and on the many other fast days of the Church, nobody is allowed to eat meat, and so the family eats fish, if they can get it, or salted herring, bought from a pedlar who comes from the coast.

[pause]

And there are rabbits and hares and birds in the fields and the woods. But they belong to the lord, and the villagers are not allowed to hunt them. Sometimes, on a dark night, a hungry man might set a little snare in the hedge, and hope that the reeve does not find out.

[long-pause]

Meat is rare for the peasant family. They eat it mostly in the winter, from the pig that was slaughtered at Martinmas, and at the great feasts, like Christmas and Easter.

[pause]

The food of the peasants is plain and simple. But it is, mostly, healthy, with plenty of grains and peas and beans and vegetables. Archaeologists who have studied the people of Wharram Percy have found that they worked very hard all their lives, and that in good years, most of them had enough to eat.

[pause]

{img:family-supper-candle-rushlight-bread-ale-jug}
At the end of the day, as the family sits by the fire with their bowls of pottage, and their bread, and their cups of ale, they say a short grace, and thank God for the food. And they mean it.

[long-pause]

{img:hearth-fire-glowing-embers-winter-night-cottage}
In the winter, the fire is the heart of the house.

[pause]

It burns on the hearth in the middle of the floor, day and night, from the autumn until the spring. It is never allowed to go out. If it did, it would be a great trouble to light it again, with a flint and a piece of steel, striking sparks onto a little bundle of dry tinder, and blowing it gently into a flame. So it is kept burning, always.

[long-pause]

At night, before the family goes to sleep, Agnes covers the glowing embers of the fire with ashes, to keep them alive, but quiet and safe, until the morning.

[pause]

She uses a special cover for this, a round dome of pottery, with holes in it, which is placed over the embers, so that no sparks can fly out and catch the straw on the floor, or the thatch of the roof. In French, it is called a couvre-feu, which means cover the fire. And from that, we have our word curfew.

[pause]

In the morning, she lifts off the cover, and blows on the embers, and adds a few small sticks, and the fire springs back to life, and the house is warm again.

[long-pause]

The fire is used for everything.

[pause]

It heats the house. It cooks the food. It heats the water for washing and brewing. It dries the wet clothes and boots of the family when they come in from the fields. It smokes the bacon hanging from the rafters. And it gives light, in the long, dark winter evenings.

[pause]

{img:wet-boots-drying-by-fire-steam-cottage}
The family sits around it, close together, with their backs to the cold, and their faces and hands warmed by the glow. The old grandmother sits closest of all, on her stool, with her shawl around her shoulders.

[long-pause]

Finding enough fuel for the fire is one of the most important tasks of the year.

[pause]

The villagers have certain rights to collect wood in the lord's wood. They are allowed to take the fallen branches and the dead wood, which they can pull down with a hook, or knock down with a crook. This is where our saying by hook or by crook comes from, people say.

[pause]

They also cut wood from the coppices: places in the wood where hazel and willow and ash trees are cut down to the ground every few years, and grow up again from the stumps in many thin, straight poles. The poles are used for fences and wattle and tool handles, and the smaller branches are tied in bundles, called faggots, for the fire.

[long-pause]

In some villages, where there is not much wood, the people burn other things too.

[pause]

They dig up blocks of turf from the moors, or peat from the bogs, and dry them in stacks, and burn them on the fire, where they glow red, and give off a sweet, earthy smoke. They burn gorse and bracken and heather from the hills, and dried cow dung, and the straw and the stubble from the fields.

[pause]

Nothing that will burn is wasted.

[long-pause]

Light is precious in the winter.

[pause]

The days are very short in the north of England in December, with the sun rising late and setting early, and the evenings are long and dark. Candles are expensive. Good wax candles are only for the church and the lord's hall. The peasants use something much simpler.

[pause]

{img:rushlight-holder-flickering-dark-cottage}
In the summer and autumn, the children gather tall green rushes from the edge of the stream. Agnes peels away most of the green skin, leaving the white pith inside, and dries them, and then dips them in the melted fat from the kitchen, and lets them harden. These are called rushlights. Clipped into a little iron holder, a rushlight burns with a small, steady, flickering flame for perhaps half an hour.

[long-pause]

On winter evenings, by the light of the fire and a rushlight or two, the family carries on with the little jobs that can be done indoors.

[pause]

John mends his tools, and makes new handles for them, and repairs the harness for the oxen. He carves spoons and bowls, and little toys for the children. Will helps him, and learns. Agnes and Alice and the grandmother spin and sew and mend.

[pause]

The little boy, Tom, sits by the fire, playing with a wooden horse, and watching the flames, until his eyes grow heavy.

[long-pause]

On the coldest nights, the family moves as close to the fire as they can.

[pause]

They pile more blankets and sheepskins on their beds. They wear their hoods and their woollen stockings to bed. The baby sleeps between her parents, wrapped up warm. The dogs crawl in among the blankets. And the warmth of the oxen and the cow, at the other end of the house, drifts through the darkness.

[pause]

{img:family-huddled-blankets-sheepskins-embers-glow}
Outside, the wind howls over the hills, and the frost settles on the thatch, and the stars glitter in the black sky. But inside, under the thick roof of straw, by the glowing embers, the family sleeps warm, and safe, until the morning.

[long-pause]

{img:shepherd-sheep-chalk-downs-woollen-cloak}
To keep warm in winter, the people of Wharram Percy depend, above all, on wool.

[pause]

The village keeps hundreds of sheep, on the rough pastures of the hills. In the summer, they are sheared, and their thick fleeces are washed and dried. Some of the wool is sold, to merchants who come from the towns, and who send it to the great cloth-making cities of Flanders and Italy. But much of it is kept, and turned into clothes for the family.

[long-pause]

Turning a fleece into a warm winter coat takes a great deal of work, and it is almost all done by the women and the girls.

[pause]

First, the wool must be combed or carded, to clean it and straighten the fibres. Then it must be spun into yarn. Agnes and Alice and the grandmother spin all through the year, whenever their hands are free: in the evenings by the fire, sitting in the doorway in the summer, even walking along the lanes, with a distaff tucked under one arm, holding the cloud of wool, and a spindle turning beside them.

[pause]

It takes many hours of spinning to make enough yarn for a single garment.

[long-pause]

Then the yarn is woven into cloth, on a loom.

[pause]

In some houses in the village, there is a big upright wooden loom, standing against the wall, with the threads hanging down, weighted with little rings of clay, and the weaver walks back and forth in front of it, passing the thread through from side to side. In other villages, there is a weaver who weaves cloth for everyone.

[pause]

{img:upright-loom-clay-weights-woman-weaving-light}
The cloth is thick and rough, in the natural colours of the sheep, white and grey and brown and black, or dyed with plants: brown from walnut husks, yellow from weld, blue from woad, red from madder root. Then it is fulled, beaten and soaked in water, to make it thicker and warmer, and keep out the rain.

[long-pause]

From the cloth, the family makes their clothes.

[pause]

The men wear a long woollen tunic, reaching to the knees, with a belt around the waist, and woollen hose, like long stockings, on their legs. Over the tunic, in winter, they wear a thick cloak, or a coat lined with sheepskin. On their heads, they wear a hood, which covers the head and the neck and the shoulders, with a long tail hanging down the back, which can be wrapped around the neck like a scarf.

[pause]

{img:peasant-man-hood-cloak-snowy-lane}
The women wear a long woollen dress, reaching to the ankles, over a linen shift, with a woollen cloak or shawl, and a linen kerchief over their hair, and a hood in cold weather. The children are dressed like little copies of their parents.

[long-pause]

Underneath, next to the skin, people wear linen, made from flax.

[pause]

The family grows a little flax in their garden. In the summer, it has pretty blue flowers. When it is ripe, they pull it up, and soak it in the stream to soften it, and beat it to separate the fibres, and comb it, and spin it into thread, and weave it into linen, which is cool and soft and strong.

[long-pause]

On their hands, in winter, people wear mittens of wool or leather.

[pause]

On their feet, they wear leather shoes, or ankle boots, made by the village shoemaker, or by the family themselves. They are simple and soft, stitched together from a few pieces of leather, without a heel, and they do not keep out the water very well. In the mud of winter, people often wear wooden pattens underneath them, like little platforms, to lift them out of the mud.

[pause]

{img:leather-shoes-wooden-pattens-doorway-mud}
In the evening, everyone's shoes are put by the fire to dry, and the house smells of warm wet leather.

[long-pause]

Clothes are very precious, because they take so long to make.

[pause]

A family might have only one or two sets of clothes each: one for working, and one for Sundays and feast days. Clothes are mended again and again, and patched, and handed down from parents to children, and from older children to younger ones. When they are finally worn out, the cloth is cut up and used for rags, and for patching other clothes, and for padding, and nothing is wasted.

[pause]

In a will of the time, a peasant might leave his best tunic to his son, and his cloak to his brother, and his wife's best dress to his daughter, as treasured possessions.

[long-pause]

In the winter, when the work outside is less, Agnes and Alice spend many hours sewing and mending.

[pause]

They sit by the fire, with their needles of bone or iron, and their thread of linen or wool, and sew new tunics for the boys, who are growing fast, and mend the holes in John's hose, and patch the elbows of the cloaks. The grandmother spins, and hums an old song. The baby sleeps in her wooden cradle, rocked now and then by someone's foot.

[pause]

{img:cradle-baby-sleeping-firelight-spinning-wheel-quiet}
And the wind blows outside, and the rain beats on the shutters, and the work goes on, stitch by stitch, through the long winter evenings.

[long-pause]

{img:oxen-byre-hay-breath-steaming-lantern-dawn}
In the winter, the animals need as much care as the people.

[pause]

The most precious of all are the oxen. Without them, there can be no ploughing in the spring, and without ploughing, there can be no harvest. John has two oxen of his own, big, slow, gentle beasts, red and brown, with great curved horns and soft dark eyes, called Brok and Lyard. He shares them with his neighbours, who have oxen of their own, to make up a full plough team.

[long-pause]

In the winter, the oxen live in the byre at the end of the house, on a thick bed of straw.

[pause]

Every morning, before dawn, John goes down to them with a lantern, and feeds them hay from the loft, and a little straw, and sometimes some oats or peas, if they are working. He fetches water for them from the stream, in a wooden bucket, and breaks the ice on it if it is frozen. He cleans out their stalls, and spreads fresh straw, and puts the dung on the heap in the yard, where it will rot down into manure for the fields in the spring.

[pause]

He talks to them as he works, quietly, and rubs their noses, and checks their feet. They know his voice, and they turn their great heads to watch him.

[long-pause]

The cow lives in the byre too.

[pause]

She is a small, brown, sturdy cow, who gives milk in the spring and summer, and less in the winter. In the autumn, she was taken to the bull, and she will have a calf in the spring. Agnes looks after her, and milks her, and talks to her, and gives her the best of the hay.

[pause]

{img:woman-milking-cow-byre-wooden-pail-warm}
The children love the cow, and sit with her in the evenings, and lean against her warm side, and listen to her chewing.

[long-pause]

The sheep stay outside most of the winter.

[pause]

They are hardy animals, with thick woolly fleeces, and they can survive the cold and the rain. The village shepherd takes them out every day onto the hills, to graze on the short grass and the stubble of the fields, and brings them back in the evening to the village fold, a big enclosure of wattle hurdles, where they are safe from foxes.

[pause]

{img:village-shepherd-dog-flock-snowy-hills-dusk}
The shepherd is one of the most important people in the village. He knows every sheep, and its mother, and its lambs. He lives with his flock almost all the time, and in the worst weather, in the lambing season, he sleeps out with them on the hills, in a little hut on wheels, or a shelter of hurdles and straw.

[long-pause]

In heavy snow, the sheep are in danger.

[pause]

They can be buried in the drifts, and lost. When the snow comes, the shepherd and the men of the village go out onto the hills to find them, and dig them out, and drive them down into the valley, where there is shelter. The shepherd's dogs help to find them, sniffing at the snow until they find a buried sheep, and barking.

[long-pause]

The pigs that were kept back from the slaughter at Martinmas spend the winter in a little sty in the yard, made of wattle and thatch, or in the byre.

[pause]

They eat whatever scraps there are: the peelings from the vegetables, the husks from the grain, the whey from the cheese, the leftovers from the pottage. In the spring, the sow will have piglets, and the circle of the year will begin again.

[pause]

{img:pig-sty-wattle-yard-frost-boy-feeding-scraps}
The hens live in the house too, in the winter, perching on the rafters, or in a little coop by the door. They lay very few eggs in the dark months, but the family keeps them, for the spring. The geese wander about the village, and the children chase them home in the evening.

[long-pause]

One of the hardest things in the winter is making the hay last.

[pause]

The hay was cut and dried in the summer, in the meadows by the stream, and brought in and stored in the loft and in stacks in the yard. It must last until the grass begins to grow again, in April or May. If the winter is long, and the spring is late, the hay may run out. Then the animals go hungry, and grow thin and weak, and the oxen may be too weak to plough.

[pause]

So John and Agnes watch the hay carefully, all through the winter, and measure out a little every day, and hope that the spring will come early.

[long-pause]

On a cold winter night, before he goes to bed, John always goes down to the byre with a rushlight, to check on the animals one last time.

[pause]

The oxen are lying down in the straw, chewing slowly, with their eyes half closed. The cow is standing, swinging her tail. The hens are asleep on the rafters, with their heads tucked under their wings. Everything is quiet and warm and peaceful, and smells of hay and animals.

[pause]

{img:sleeping-oxen-straw-byre-rushlight-quiet}
John stands there for a moment, in the soft light, listening to them breathe. Then he goes back to the fire, and covers the embers, and lies down beside his family, and sleeps.

[long-pause]

{img:advent-village-church-frosty-morning-bell}
In the darkest weeks of the winter, there comes the greatest feast of the year: Christmas.

[pause]

It is preceded by Advent, the four weeks before Christmas, which is a time of waiting and preparing, and of fasting. During Advent, nobody eats meat, and the church is quiet and plain, and the priest wears purple.

[long-pause]

But in the last days before Christmas, the village bustles with preparations.

[pause]

The women clean their houses, and sweep out the old rushes from the floors, and lay down fresh ones, mixed with sweet-smelling herbs. They bake bread and cakes, and brew extra ale, strong and dark, for the feast. The children go out into the woods and the hedges to gather greenery: holly with its red berries, and ivy, and bay, and branches of yew and fir, to decorate the houses and the church.

[pause]

In the church, the priest and the villagers decorate the walls and the screen with green branches, and set out candles on the altar, more candles than at any other time of the year.

[long-pause]

On Christmas Eve, the men drag a great log into the house, from the wood: the Yule log.

[pause]

It is the biggest log they can find, often the trunk of a whole tree, and it is pushed into the fire, end first, and lit from a piece of last year's log, which has been kept carefully all year. It is meant to burn, slowly, through all the twelve days of Christmas.

[pause]

{img:yule-log-dragged-into-cottage-hearth-family}
As it burns, the family gathers around it, and drinks a cup of ale, and makes a wish for the coming year.

[long-pause]

At midnight, the whole village walks down to the church, in the darkness, with lanterns and torches, for the first Mass of Christmas.

[pause]

The church is full, and glowing with candles, and decorated with holly and ivy. The bell rings out across the frosty valley. The priest, in his white and gold vestments, says the Mass, and the villagers kneel on the cold stone floor, in their best clothes, and listen to the story of the birth of Jesus, in a stable, among the animals, with the shepherds coming down from the hills.

[pause]

The villagers understand that story very well. They are shepherds and farmers themselves. They know about stables, and animals, and cold nights on the hills.

[long-pause]

On Christmas Day, there is a great feast.

[pause]

In many villages, the lord of the manor gives a Christmas dinner for the villagers, in his hall. Each family brings its own bread and its own bowl and cup, and a bundle of firewood for the fire, and sits at long tables in the great hall, and eats as much as they can: roast meat and bread, pies and puddings, and ale, as much as they want. In return, the villagers give the lord a gift of hens, or eggs, at Christmas.

[pause]

{img:manor-hall-christmas-feast-villagers-long-tables-fire}
If the lord is not there, the families feast in their own houses, with a joint of pork or a goose, roasted over the fire, and fresh bread, and cheese, and strong ale, and the neighbours visit each other, and share what they have.

[long-pause]

Christmas lasts for twelve days, from Christmas Day until Twelfth Night, on the fifth of January.

[pause]

It is a time of rest, and feasting, and games. Nobody works in the fields, except to feed the animals. The villagers visit each other, and eat and drink, and play games, and sing carols, which in those days were songs that people danced to in a ring, holding hands.

[pause]

{img:villagers-ring-dance-carol-snow-lanterns}
There are mummers, too: groups of young men from the village, who dress up in strange costumes, with masks and paper hats and blackened faces, and go from house to house in the evenings, acting out little plays and silly dances, with mock fights and a great deal of noise and laughter. Afterwards, they are given ale and cakes, and they go on to the next house.

[long-pause]

On Twelfth Night, there is one last feast.

[pause]

A great cake is baked, with a bean hidden inside it. Whoever finds the bean in their slice is the King of the Bean, and rules over the feast for the evening, giving silly orders that everyone must obey. There is wassailing, too: the villagers go out to the orchards, in the darkness, with lanterns and cups of hot spiced ale, and sing to the apple trees, and pour ale on their roots, to wish them a good harvest in the coming year.

[pause]

Waes hael, they call to each other, which means be well, in the old English language. And they answer: drinc hael, drink well.

[long-pause]

And then, the next morning, Christmas is over.

[pause]

The Yule log has burned down to ashes, and a little piece of it is kept, to light next year's log. The greenery is taken down from the walls, and burned. The last of the Christmas ale is drunk.

[pause]

{img:frosty-dawn-after-twelfth-night-quiet-village}
And the villagers look out of their doors, at the cold, grey January morning, and the frost on the fields, and the long winter still ahead of them, and pick up their tools, and go back to work.

[long-pause]

{img:plough-monday-decorated-plough-young-men-village}
The first Monday after Twelfth Night is called Plough Monday.

[pause]

On that day, the young men of the village decorate a plough with ribbons, and drag it around the village, from house to house, singing and dancing and shouting, and asking for money or food or ale. The money is used to pay for candles for the church, to bless the ploughs for the year. If someone refuses to give, it is said, the young men plough up the ground in front of his door, as a joke.

[long-pause]

After Plough Monday, the real work of the new year begins.

[pause]

In January and February, there is not much that can be done in the fields. The ground is often frozen hard, or covered in snow, or so wet that the ploughs cannot go into it. But there is still plenty of work to do, indoors and out.

[pause]

The men mend the hedges and the fences around the closes and the gardens, weaving new hazel stakes into the wattle, and cutting back the old growth with a billhook. They dig out the ditches, so that the water can drain away from the fields. They mend the walls of the houses and the barns, and patch the thatch where the wind has torn it.

[long-pause]

The most important winter work of all is threshing.

[pause]

All through the winter, a little at a time, the sheaves of wheat and barley and oats that were stored in the barn after the harvest are threshed, to separate the grain from the straw.

[pause]

{img:two-men-threshing-flails-barn-floor-dust-light}
The threshing is done on the floor of the barn, between the two great doors, which are opened on either side so that the wind blows through. The sheaves are laid out on the floor, and the men beat them with flails: two sticks, joined together with a leather strap, one long, which is the handle, and one short, which swings around and strikes the grain. Thump, thump, thump, all day long, in a steady rhythm, while the dust rises in the light from the doors.

[long-pause]

Then the grain must be winnowed, to blow away the chaff, the light, dry husks.

[pause]

On a windy day, the threshed grain is tossed up into the air with a wide, flat basket, called a winnowing fan, in the doorway of the barn, and the wind blows the chaff away, and the heavy grain falls back down. Or the grain is poured slowly from a height, and the wind does the same.

[pause]

The clean grain is put into sacks, and stored, ready to be taken to the mill, to be ground into flour. The straw is used for thatching, and for bedding for the animals, and for stuffing mattresses. The chaff is fed to the animals, or put in the pillows.

[long-pause]

The women, too, have their winter work.

[pause]

There is the spinning and the weaving and the sewing, which never stops. There is the brewing and the baking and the cooking. There are the animals to feed and milk and care for. There are the children to look after. There is the washing, which in the winter is hard and cold work, done in a wooden tub with water heated over the fire, and the clothes hung to dry inside, by the hearth, steaming.

[pause]

{img:washing-clothes-wooden-tub-hearth-steam-winter}
On fine winter days, the women gather in a neighbour's house, to spin and sew together, and talk, and share the news of the village. These are happy times, full of gossip and laughter, and the children play together on the floor.

[long-pause]

The children have their winter work too.

[pause]

They fetch water from the stream, breaking the ice with a stick. They gather firewood, and sticks for kindling, in the wood. They feed the hens and the geese, and collect the few eggs. They help with the threshing, gathering up the straw. They mind the baby.

[pause]

{img:child-fetching-water-icy-stream-wooden-bucket}
And they go out into the frosty fields with their slings and their clappers, to scare away the flocks of pigeons and rooks and crows, which come down in their hundreds to eat the young green shoots of the winter wheat.

[long-pause]

In the evenings, the men of the village sometimes meet at the house of one of the neighbours, or at the house where the ale is being sold, to drink a cup of ale by the fire, and talk.

[pause]

They talk about the weather, and the animals, and the crops, and the price of wool, and the lord's demands, and the news from York or from further away, brought by a pedlar or a traveller. They make plans for the spring ploughing, and argue about whose turn it is to use the oxen.

[pause]

And then they go home, through the dark and the frost, with a lantern swinging, to their wives and their children and their beds.

[long-pause]

January is a long, quiet month.

[pause]

The days are short and grey. The weather is cold and wet and windy. But each day, the light lasts a little longer. By the end of the month, the evenings are noticeably lighter. The first snowdrops appear in the churchyard, small and white, under the trees.

[pause]

{img:snowdrops-churchyard-january-grey-light}
And the villagers, seeing them, feel a little lift in their hearts. The winter is not over yet. But it will not last forever.

[long-pause]

{img:family-gathered-fire-winter-evening-stories-1300}
The winter evenings are long, and the family spends them together, by the fire.

[pause]

As soon as it is dark, which in December and January is around four o'clock in the afternoon, the work outside must stop. The animals are fed and settled for the night. The door is closed and barred. The shutters are fastened. And the family gathers around the hearth, in the warm, flickering light of the fire.

[long-pause]

There is supper first: a bowl of pottage, a piece of bread, a cup of ale.

[pause]

Then, while the grownups go on with their quiet work, spinning and mending and carving, the talk begins. They talk about the day, and the animals, and the neighbours, and the news. They talk about the past, and the old days, and people who have died. They talk about the future, and the spring, and the harvest.

[pause]

And then, very often, someone tells a story.

[long-pause]

The grandmother is the best storyteller in the family.

[pause]

She knows a great many stories, which she heard from her own mother and grandmother, long ago, by the same fire. She tells stories about the saints, and the miracles they did: Saint Martin, who cut his cloak in half to give to a beggar in the snow; Saint Nicholas, who secretly gave gold to three poor girls; Saint Cuthbert, who lived on a little island in the sea, among the seabirds and the seals, and was kind to the otters, who came out of the sea to warm his feet.

[pause]

{img:saint-cuthbert-otters-island-shore-legend-painting}
She tells stories about giants and dragons and fairies, and the little folk who live in the hollow hills, and steal babies, and sour the milk, and must be left a bowl of cream at night to keep them happy. She tells stories about bold outlaws who lived in the greenwood, and outwitted the sheriffs and the rich abbots. She tells stories about the old kings, King Arthur and his knights, and King Alfred, who burned the cakes.

[long-pause]

The children listen, wide-eyed, sitting on the floor, wrapped in blankets, with their chins on their knees, and the firelight on their faces.

[pause]

Tom, the little boy, likes the stories about dragons best. Alice likes the stories about the fairies. Will, who is fourteen and thinks he is too old for stories, pretends not to listen, while he mends a basket, but he listens anyway.

[long-pause]

Sometimes, there are riddles.

[pause]

What has a mouth but never speaks, a bed but never sleeps? A river. What goes on four legs in the morning, two legs at noon, and three legs in the evening? A man, who crawls as a baby, walks as a man, and leans on a stick when he is old. The children try to guess, and shout out answers, and laugh.

[pause]

And sometimes, there is singing. The family sings old songs, ballads and carols, songs about love and work and the seasons. Sometimes a neighbour comes in with a pipe or a little drum, and plays, and everyone sings together.

[long-pause]

There are games, too, played by the fire.

[pause]

There are board games, scratched on a board of wood, or on a flat stone: games like merels, which we call nine men's morris, played with little pebbles or wooden pegs; and draughts; and dice. The men play, and gamble a little, for beans or nuts. The children play knucklebones, tossing little bones in the air and catching them on the back of the hand.

[pause]

{img:men-playing-nine-mens-morris-board-firelight}
On very special evenings, at Christmas, there might be a game of blind man's buff, with everyone laughing and stumbling around the dark house, bumping into the furniture and the animals.

[long-pause]

Then, at last, the evening grows late.

[pause]

The fire burns low. The rushlights flicker out. The children's eyes grow heavy. The baby is already asleep in her cradle. The grandmother yawns.

[pause]

The family says its prayers together, kneeling by the fire: the Our Father, and the Hail Mary, and the Creed, which they know by heart, in Latin, though they do not understand all the words. They ask God and the saints to keep them safe through the night, and to bless their animals, and their fields, and their village.

[long-pause]

Then they lie down on their beds of straw, close together by the warm embers, and pull their blankets and sheepskins over them.

[pause]

Agnes covers the fire, and the house grows dark, except for a faint red glow from the embers under their cover. The oxen sigh and shift in the byre. The dog turns around three times and settles at Tom's feet. The wind sighs in the thatch.

[pause]

{img:dark-cottage-faint-ember-glow-family-sleeping}
And one by one, the family falls asleep, in the warm darkness, while outside, over the valley and the frozen fields and the silent church, the stars wheel slowly across the winter sky.

[long-pause]

{img:girl-waking-before-dawn-cottage-embers-cold}
Let us spend one winter's day with Alice, the eleven-year-old daughter of John and Agnes.

[pause]

She wakes before dawn, in the dark, in the warm nest of blankets and sheepskins by the hearth, with her little sister curled up against her back. For a moment, she lies still, listening. She can hear her grandmother snoring softly, and the oxen shifting in the byre, and the wind in the thatch. It is very cold outside the blankets. She does not want to get up.

[long-pause]

But her mother is already up, lifting the cover from the embers, and blowing on them, and adding sticks, and the fire is crackling into life. So Alice gets up too, quickly, and pulls on her woollen dress over her shift, and her stockings and her shoes, and wraps her shawl around her shoulders, and goes to help.

[pause]

Her first job is to fetch the water.

[pause]

She takes the two wooden buckets, and goes out into the cold, grey dawn, and down the path to the stream. The grass is white with frost, and her breath steams in the air. At the stream, there is a thin layer of ice at the edges, and she breaks it with a stick, and fills the buckets, and carries them back up the hill, slowly, one in each hand, trying not to spill them. Her hands are red and stinging with cold.

[long-pause]

Back at the house, the pottage is warming on the fire, and the family eats breakfast together: a bowl of pottage, a piece of bread, a cup of ale.

[pause]

Then her father and Will go out to the barn to thresh, and her mother goes to milk the cow, and Alice's next job is to mind her little sister, and feed the hens, and sweep the floor, and then to sit by the fire and spin.

[pause]

{img:girl-spinning-wool-fireside-little-sister-playing}
Alice has been spinning since she was six. She is quite good at it now. She sits on a stool by the fire, with the distaff of wool tucked under her arm, and the spindle twirling down toward the floor, and draws out the thread, long and even. Her grandmother sits beside her, spinning too, and watches her, and sometimes reaches over to correct her.

[pause]

While they spin, the grandmother tells her stories, and teaches her the old songs, and the names of the herbs, and the prayers.

[long-pause]

In the middle of the day, the sun comes out, and Alice is allowed to go outside for a while.

[pause]

She takes her little sister by the hand, and they go out into the village, where the other children are playing. They play a game of chase, around the houses and the haystacks, and then they go down to the millpond, where the ice is thick, and slide on it, shrieking, until her mother calls her back.

[pause]

{img:village-children-playing-chase-haystacks-winter-sun}
On the way back, she stops to talk to her friend Margery, who lives in the next house, and who is the same age. They whisper together, and giggle, about the miller's son, who is fourteen, and who smiled at Alice in church last Sunday.

[long-pause]

In the afternoon, Alice helps her mother with the cooking and the baking.

[pause]

She kneads the dough for the bread, in the wooden trough, pushing and folding it with her hands, until it is smooth. She chops the onions and the leeks for the pottage, with a little knife, and her eyes water. She learns how much salt to put in, and how much water, and how to tell when the bread is ready.

[pause]

{img:mother-daughter-kneading-bread-trough-firelight}
Her mother tells her that one day, she will have a house of her own, and a family of her own, and will need to know all these things. Alice tries to imagine it, and cannot, quite.

[long-pause]

As the light fades, in the late afternoon, she goes out once more, to collect the eggs, and shut up the hens, and bring in more firewood from the stack by the wall.

[pause]

The sky is turning pink and gold over the hills. The rooks are flying home to their trees, in long black lines, cawing. The smoke is rising from all the houses in the village, straight up into the still, cold air. The church bell is ringing for the evening prayer. She stops for a moment, with her arms full of wood, and looks at it all, and feels, without knowing quite why, very happy.

[pause]

Then she goes back inside, and closes the door, and the warmth of the fire wraps around her.

[long-pause]

In the evening, after supper, the family sits by the fire.

[pause]

Alice sits by her grandmother, with her head on her lap, and listens to the stories, and watches the flames. She takes out her blue ribbon, from the market, which she keeps in a little wooden box, and looks at it, and puts it away again. She is saving it for Christmas.

[pause]

{img:girl-holding-blue-ribbon-firelight-grandmother}
Then they say their prayers, and lie down to sleep. And Alice curls up in her blankets, with her little sister warm against her back, and listens to the wind, and the oxen, and the crackle of the fire, and falls asleep, before she even knows it.

[long-pause]

{img:herbs-drying-rafters-wise-woman-pestle-mortar}
Winter is a hard time for health.

[pause]

The cold and the damp, the smoke in the houses, the short days without sunshine, and the end of the fresh food from the garden, all make people more likely to fall ill. Coughs and colds and fevers go round the village, from house to house, all through the winter months.

[long-pause]

There are no doctors in Wharram Percy.

[pause]

The nearest physicians are in the towns, in York or Beverley, and they are very expensive, and only the rich can afford them. So the villagers look after themselves, and each other, with the old remedies that have been handed down, from mother to daughter, for hundreds of years.

[pause]

Almost every woman in the village knows something about healing. They grow herbs in their gardens, and gather others from the fields and the hedges and the woods, and dry them, and hang them from the rafters, and use them to make medicines.

[long-pause]

For a cough, there is a drink made of honey and horehound, or hyssop, warmed by the fire. For a fever, there is willow bark, boiled in water, which tastes bitter, but brings the fever down. For a headache, there is lavender, or rosemary. For a cold, there is a hot drink of ale with spices and honey, and a warm bed by the fire.

[pause]

{img:honey-pot-herbs-wooden-cup-steam-fireside}
For cuts and wounds, there are poultices of plantain leaves and comfrey, and clean linen bandages, and honey, which helps wounds to heal. For an upset stomach, there is mint, or camomile. For sleeplessness, there is a little valerian, or a cup of warm milk with honey.

[pause]

Many of these remedies really do help, and some of them are still used today.

[long-pause]

In every village, there is usually one woman who knows more than the others: an older woman, perhaps a widow, who is especially wise in the ways of herbs and healing.

[pause]

The villagers call her when someone is very ill, or when a baby is being born. She comes with her basket of herbs and linen, and sits with the sick person, and makes her medicines, and gives advice, and comforts the family.

[pause]

She also helps the women of the village when they give birth, which is one of the most dangerous moments in a woman's life in those days. She is a midwife, and she has helped to bring almost every child in the village into the world.

[long-pause]

The villagers also believe in the power of prayer, and of the saints.

[pause]

When someone is ill, the family prays for them, and lights a candle in the church, and asks the priest to come and bless them. They pray to the saint who is believed to help with that particular illness: Saint Blaise for sore throats, whose feast day falls in February, and Saint Apollonia for toothache.

[pause]

Some of them carry little charms, or wear a sprig of a special plant, or say a special prayer, to keep illness away. Some go on pilgrimage, in the spring, to a holy well or a shrine, to ask for healing.

[long-pause]

When someone in the village is ill, the neighbours help.

[pause]

They bring food and firewood to the family. They take turns to sit with the sick person, through the night, so that the family can rest. They look after the animals, if the man of the house is too ill to do it. They mind the children. The women bring broth and bread and remedies.

[pause]

{img:neighbour-bringing-broth-bowl-cottage-door-winter}
This is how villages have always survived, through hard times: by helping each other.

[long-pause]

This winter, at John's house, it is little Tom who falls ill, in January.

[pause]

He has a cough, and a fever, and he is hot and tired and miserable. Agnes puts him to bed by the fire, wrapped in blankets, and gives him warm honey and horehound to drink, and willow bark for the fever. The grandmother sits beside him, all through the night, telling him stories in a low voice, and singing the old songs, and stroking his hair.

[pause]

The priest comes, and blesses him, and says a prayer. The neighbours bring him an apple, and a little honey cake, and a wooden toy.

[long-pause]

After a few days, the fever breaks.

[pause]

Tom wakes up one morning, and sits up in his blankets, and says that he is hungry. Agnes cries with relief, and gives him a bowl of warm pottage, and a piece of bread with butter, which he eats every crumb of. A few days later, he is out in the yard again, chasing the geese, and getting under everyone's feet.

[pause]

{img:boy-chasing-geese-frosty-yard-sunshine-recovered}
That Sunday, at church, Agnes lights a candle in thanks, in front of the painted picture of the Virgin Mary on the wall, and says a long prayer. And then she goes home, and makes a special pottage, with a little extra bacon in it, for the whole family, to celebrate.

[long-pause]

{img:snow-covered-medieval-village-valley-morning}
In late January, the snow comes.

[pause]

It begins in the evening, with a few soft flakes drifting down in the dusk. By the morning, the whole valley is white. The thatched roofs of the houses are thick with snow, and the fences and hedges are buried in drifts, and the open fields on the hills are smooth and white and empty, under a pale grey sky.

[long-pause]

The village is very quiet.

[pause]

Snow muffles every sound. There is no wind. The smoke rises straight up from the roofs into the still air. The only sounds are a dog barking somewhere, and the soft thump of snow falling from a branch, and the crunch of footsteps, as John goes out to the byre in the early morning, with a lantern, to see to the animals.

[pause]

He clears the snow from the door of the byre with a wooden shovel, and goes in, and the oxen and the cow turn their heads to look at him, warm and steaming in the straw.

[long-pause]

Up on the hills, the shepherd and the men of the village are out early, looking for the sheep.

[pause]

The flock was in the fold, near the village, when the snow began, so most of the sheep are safe. But a few had wandered off, and the men go out, with the dogs, across the white fields, to find them. They find most of them sheltering in the lee of a hedge, half buried, but alive, and dig them out, and drive them home.

[pause]

{img:shepherd-carrying-lamb-snow-dogs-hills}
In the afternoon, the men come home, cold and wet and tired, and sit by their fires to dry out, with their wet clothes steaming.

[long-pause]

For the children, the snow is wonderful.

[pause]

As soon as their work is done, they rush outside, and throw snowballs at each other, and build snowmen, and make long slides on the frozen paths, running and sliding along them, shrieking and falling over and laughing.

[pause]

On the frozen millpond, they skate. Their skates are made from the shin bones of horses or cattle, flattened on one side, and tied to their shoes with leather thongs. They do not lift their feet, but push themselves along the ice with long sticks with iron points, like ski poles, gliding fast across the frozen pond. A clerk in London, more than a hundred years earlier, wrote about the boys of London skating like this, on the frozen marshes outside the city walls, so fast that they seemed to fly like birds.

[pause]

The older boys race each other across the ice, and the younger children slide about on their bottoms, and everyone falls over, and gets up, and falls over again.

[long-pause]

The grown-ups, too, enjoy the snow, a little.

[pause]

The young men have snowball fights with each other. The old men stand at their doors, and remember the great snows of their childhood, which were always, they say, much deeper than this. The women stand at the doors and watch the children, and call them in, again and again, until they come in at last, red-cheeked and soaking wet, and are dried by the fire, and given a bowl of hot pottage.

[pause]

{img:mother-drying-snowy-children-by-fire-warm}
In the evening, by the fire, the family can hear the deep silence of the snow outside, and it is very peaceful.

[long-pause]

The snow lasts for a week.

[pause]

During that week, very little work can be done outside. The men thresh in the barns, and mend things in the houses. The women spin and weave and sew. The paths between the houses are trodden into narrow, slippery tracks. The stream freezes over, and the women must break the ice to get water, or melt snow in a pot by the fire.

[pause]

The birds come close to the houses, looking for food: robins and wrens and blackbirds and sparrows. The children throw them crumbs. A robin comes right into the house one morning, through the open door, and hops about on the floor, looking for crumbs, and the children watch it, holding their breath, until it flies out again.

[long-pause]

Then, one morning, the wind changes.

[pause]

It blows warm and wet from the west. The snow begins to melt. Water drips from the thatch, and runs in little streams down the paths, and the fields turn from white to grey and brown again. The stream rises, and runs fast and brown and noisy under the willows.

[pause]

{img:thaw-dripping-thatch-melting-snow-fields-mist}
The villagers come out of their houses, and look at the sky, and at the fields, and begin, once more, their winter work. And the children are a little sad, because the snow has gone.

[long-pause]

{img:candlemas-procession-candles-church-february}
On the second of February comes the feast of Candlemas.

[pause]

It is the feast of the Purification of the Virgin Mary, and of the presentation of the baby Jesus in the Temple. And it is the day when all the candles that will be used in the church for the coming year are blessed.

[long-pause]

On Candlemas morning, the villagers come to church, each carrying a candle.

[pause]

The priest blesses the candles with holy water and incense, and then everyone lights them, and walks in procession around the church, and out into the churchyard, and back again, singing, with the little flames flickering in the cold, grey February air.

[pause]

After the Mass, the villagers take their blessed candles home, and keep them carefully, to light in times of trouble: during a thunderstorm, or when someone is ill, or when a baby is being born, or when someone is dying.

[long-pause]

There is an old saying about Candlemas, which the farmers all know.

[pause]

If Candlemas Day be fair and bright, winter will have another flight. But if Candlemas Day be clouds and rain, winter is gone and will not come again.

[pause]

{img:farmer-looking-at-sky-february-grey-fields}
So on Candlemas morning, the farmers look up at the sky, to see what the weather is doing, and try to guess how long the winter will last.

[long-pause]

Candlemas is the middle of the winter, halfway between the shortest day and the spring.

[pause]

By now, the days are noticeably longer. The sun rises a little earlier, and sets a little later. On fine days, it has a little warmth in it, at midday. The birds are beginning to sing in the mornings: the robin, the thrush, the blackbird. In the hedges, the hazel catkins are hanging down, yellow and dusty with pollen, and the first pussy willows are showing silver on the willows by the stream.

[pause]

But the hardest part of the winter is still to come.

[long-pause]

After Candlemas, the stores of food begin to run low.

[pause]

The bacon is almost gone. The apples in the loft are wrinkled and few. The cheese is nearly finished. The sacks of peas and beans are getting lighter. The hay is running short. And nothing new is growing yet in the garden or the fields.

[pause]

The farmers call this time the hungry gap: the weeks at the end of the winter and the beginning of the spring, when the old food is running out, and the new food has not yet come.

[pause]

In a good year, like this one, the family has enough, if they are careful. But they must be careful. Agnes measures out the oatmeal and the peas very exactly, each day, and makes the pottage a little thinner, and the bread a little smaller.

[long-pause]

At about this time, too, comes Lent.

[pause]

Lent is the forty days before Easter, a time of fasting and prayer. During Lent, nobody is allowed to eat meat, or eggs, or cheese, or butter, or milk. The villagers eat bread and pottage, made with peas and beans and vegetables and a little oil, and fish, if they can get it, and salted herring.

[pause]

{img:salted-herring-barrel-pedlar-cart-village}
On the day before Lent begins, called Shrove Tuesday, the families eat up all their eggs and butter and milk, which would otherwise go to waste during Lent, by making pancakes, fried on a griddle over the fire. The children love it. It is a little feast before the long fast.

[long-pause]

The next day is Ash Wednesday.

[pause]

The villagers go to church, and the priest marks a cross of ash on each person's forehead, and says, in Latin: Remember, man, that you are dust, and to dust you shall return. Then Lent begins, and the church is quiet and plain again, and the statues and the pictures are covered with cloths, until Easter.

[long-pause]

But even in Lent, there are signs of hope.

[pause]

In the byre, the cow is getting heavy with her calf. In the sheepfold, the ewes are getting ready to lamb. And in the fields, under the brown earth, the winter wheat is already green, in long thin rows, waiting for the warmth of the spring.

[pause]

And one morning, in the middle of February, the shepherd comes down from the hills with a newborn lamb in his arms, the first of the year, wet and wobbly and bleating. He shows it to the children, who crowd around to see it, and stroke its soft wet wool.

[long-pause]

Lambing time has begun.

[pause]

For the next few weeks, the shepherd hardly sleeps. He lives up on the hill with his flock, in his little hut, with his dog, and a fire, and a lantern, checking on the ewes all night, helping them when they need it, and carrying the weakest lambs into his hut to warm them by the fire.

[pause]

{img:shepherd-hut-lantern-night-lambs-ewes-fold}
Sometimes, a lamb whose mother has died is brought down to the village, and the children feed it with milk from a horn, and it follows them around the yard, and sleeps by the fire, like a little dog.

[pause]

The year is turning. The spring is coming.

[long-pause]

{img:spring-ploughing-oxen-team-green-hills-larks}
In March, the spring comes to the valley.

[pause]

The days grow longer and warmer. The rain is softer. The wind blows from the south and the west, and smells of earth and growing things. The snowdrops in the churchyard are followed by primroses and violets and daffodils, and the blackthorn in the hedges covers itself with white blossom, like a late snowfall.

[long-pause]

The ground dries out, and the ploughing begins again.

[pause]

John and his neighbours yoke their oxen together, in a long team, and plough the fields for the spring crops: barley and oats and peas and beans. Brok and Lyard, thinner after the winter, but strong, lean into their yokes, and pull the heavy plough up and down the long strips, while the larks sing high above, and the rooks follow behind in the fresh brown furrows.

[pause]

Then comes the sowing, and the harrowing, and the children go back out into the fields with their slings and their clappers, to keep the birds away from the seed.

[long-pause]

In the garden behind the house, Agnes digs and plants.

[pause]

She sows peas and beans, and leeks and onions, and cabbages and turnips, and herbs: parsley and sage and thyme. She weeds the strawberry plants, and prunes the gooseberry bushes. The first young green leaves of the nettles appear by the fence, and she picks them, and makes them into a green pottage, the first fresh food of the year, which everyone eats eagerly, after the long winter of dried peas and salt bacon.

[pause]

{img:woman-planting-garden-spring-sunshine-children-helping}
The hens begin to lay again, more and more eggs every day. The cow has her calf, a little brown bull calf, and gives milk again, and there is butter and cheese once more.

[long-pause]

On Easter Sunday, the church is full of flowers and light.

[pause]

The cloths are taken off the statues and the pictures. The bells ring out across the valley, after being silent for three days. The priest, in his white and gold vestments, says the Mass, and the villagers receive Holy Communion, for the one time in the year when most of them do.

[pause]

After the long fast of Lent, there is a great feast. The villagers eat meat and eggs and cheese and butter again, and drink ale. The children are given eggs, coloured with onion skins and herbs, which they roll down the hills, to see whose goes furthest without breaking.

[long-pause]

On the first of May, there is May Day.

[pause]

Before dawn, the young people of the village go out into the woods and the fields, to gather green branches and flowers, which they call bringing in the May. They come back at sunrise, singing, carrying armfuls of hawthorn blossom and bluebells and cowslips, and decorate the doors and the windows of the houses.

[pause]

{img:may-day-young-people-hawthorn-blossom-dawn-singing}
In many villages, a tall pole is set up on the green, decorated with flowers and ribbons, and the young men and women dance around it, and choose a May Queen, the prettiest girl in the village, and crown her with flowers.

[long-pause]

By the end of May, the grass is long and green in the meadows by the stream.

[pause]

The animals are let out of the byres to graze, at last, after the long winter. The oxen walk out slowly, blinking in the sunshine, and lower their heads to the fresh grass, and eat, and eat. The cow and her calf follow them. The sheep, with their lambs at their sides, spread out across the green hills.

[pause]

The byre is cleaned out, and the dung is spread on the fields. The house is swept, and the old rushes thrown out, and fresh ones laid down. The shutters are opened, to let in the light and the warm air.

[long-pause]

The family of John the Ploughman has come through the winter.

[pause]

They are all still there: John and Agnes, Will and Alice and Tom, the baby, who is walking now, and the old grandmother, who sits outside in the sun now, on a bench by the door, spinning, and watching the swallows, which have just come back from far away, and are building their nests under the eaves of the thatch.

[pause]

{img:grandmother-bench-doorway-sunshine-swallows-spring}
The stores are empty. The hay is gone. The family is a little thinner than in the autumn. But they are well, and the animals are well, and the crops are growing in the fields.

[pause]

And in a few months, it will be harvest time again, and then autumn, and then, once more, the long winter.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-village-summer-harvest-golden-1320s}
The people of Wharram Percy went on living in their valley, year after year, winter after winter, for another two hundred years.

[pause]

The children of John and Agnes grew up, and married, and had children of their own, and took over the land, and ploughed the fields, and sowed and harvested, and kept the sheep, and spent their winters by the fire, as their parents and grandparents had done before them.

[long-pause]

There were good years and bad years.

[pause]

In the years around 1315, there was a terrible time, all over northern Europe. It rained and rained, summer after summer, and the crops rotted in the fields, and the animals sickened, and there was a great famine. Many people in England went hungry, for several years.

[pause]

Then, in 1348 and 1349, came the Black Death, the great plague, which swept across England, and reached even the quiet valleys of the Yorkshire Wolds. In many villages, a third or a half of the people were lost.

[long-pause]

After the Black Death, life in the villages changed.

[pause]

There were fewer people, and more land, and the people who survived could ask for better wages, and better terms from their lords. Over the next hundred years, the old duties of the villeins slowly faded away. The peasants of England became, slowly, free.

[pause]

{img:free-peasant-family-better-house-1400s}
In Wharram Percy, the houses were rebuilt, many times, as they always had been, every generation or so, because houses of wood and earth and straw do not last forever. Each time, they were rebuilt a little differently, a little bigger, a little better.

[long-pause]

But in the end, something else happened to Wharram Percy, and to many villages like it.

[pause]

In the 1400s, the price of wool rose, and the price of grain fell. It became more profitable for the lords to keep sheep on their land than to let it to farmers to grow crops. One shepherd and his dog could look after a great flock of sheep, on land that had once fed a whole village.

[pause]

So, little by little, the lords turned the open fields into sheep pasture. The families were moved away, or left of their own accord, to other villages, and to the towns, where there was work.

[long-pause]

By about the year 1500, the last families of Wharram Percy had gone.

[pause]

The houses were left empty, and slowly fell down. The thatch rotted. The walls of wattle and daub crumbled back into the earth. The chalk foundations were covered over with grass. The great open fields, with their long strips, became a sheep pasture, rolling green and empty over the hills.

[pause]

Only the little stone church survived, at the bottom of the valley, by the stream. It was still used, for many years, by the people of the nearby farms and hamlets, who came to it on Sundays across the fields. And the mill pond stayed, and the stream, and the willows.

[long-pause]

For more than four hundred years, the village lay forgotten, under the grass.

[pause]

Shepherds grazed their sheep over it. Farmers ploughed nearby. Travellers passed by, and saw only some strange bumps and hollows in the grass, and did not know what they were.

[pause]

{img:grassy-bumps-hollows-evening-light-sheep}
Then, in the twentieth century, people began to take an interest in the lost villages of England, the deserted medieval villages, of which there are thousands. And one of them was Wharram Percy.

[long-pause]

In 1948, a young historian named Maurice Beresford came to Wharram Percy, and began to study it.

[pause]

He realized that the bumps and hollows in the grass were the remains of the houses and the yards and the lanes of the old village. He and an archaeologist named John Hurst began to dig there, carefully, every summer, with teams of volunteers: students, schoolteachers, retired people, families with children. They camped in the old farm buildings nearby, and dug, and sifted the earth, and found the remains of the village, piece by piece.

[pause]

They dug there every summer for forty years, until 1990. It was one of the longest and most famous excavations in the history of archaeology.

[long-pause]

They found the foundations of the houses, and the hearths, and the byres, and the yards.

[pause]

They found the things that the villagers had used and lost and thrown away: pieces of pottery, bone combs, iron knives and keys and buckles, a few coins, spindle whorls for spinning, clay weights from a loom, a little bone skate, gaming pieces, the bones of the animals they had eaten, and the seeds of the plants they had grown.

[pause]

{img:archaeological-finds-tray-bone-comb-pottery-spindle}
And they learned, from all these little things, how the people of Wharram Percy had lived, through all those long winters, hundreds of years ago: what they ate, what they wore, how they built their houses, how they worked and played, how they kept warm, and how they cared for each other.

[pause]

Much of what we have told tonight, we know because of them.

[long-pause]

{img:wharram-percy-valley-today-church-ruin-pond}
Wharram Percy is still there today, in its quiet valley in the Yorkshire Wolds.

[pause]

It is a peaceful, lonely place. To reach it, you must leave your car at the top of the hill, and walk down a footpath, through the fields, and down into the valley, about half a mile. There are no houses here now, no roads, no shops. Only the green valley, and the stream, and the sheep, and the sky.

[long-pause]

At the bottom of the valley, beside the stream, the old church still stands.

[pause]

It is a ruin now. The roof has gone, and half of the tower has fallen. But the walls are still there, of grey and white stone, and the arches, and the windows, and the doorways, through which the villagers walked to Mass every Sunday, for hundreds of years. Inside, the grass grows where the floor once was, and the sky is the ceiling.

[pause]

In the churchyard around it, there are a few old gravestones, leaning in the long grass, and the trees grow tall and green.

[long-pause]

Beside the church, the old mill pond is still there, a quiet, dark pool, with reeds and water lilies, where ducks and moorhens swim. The stream still runs out of it, under the willows, as it did when the mill wheel turned there, seven hundred years ago.

[pause]

{img:old-mill-pond-reeds-ducks-willows-evening}
And up on the slope above the church, where the village once stood, there are the bumps and hollows in the grass: the outlines of the houses and the yards and the lanes, laid out in the grass like a map, if you know how to look at them.

[long-pause]

Little signs show where each house stood.

[pause]

You can walk along the line of the old village street, on the grass, and stand where the doors of the houses were, and where the hearths were, and the byres. You can look down the valley, and imagine the smoke rising from thirty chimneys, and the children running between the houses, and the geese on the green, and the women at their doors, and the oxen coming home from the fields in the evening.

[pause]

{img:visitors-walking-grassy-outlines-medieval-houses}
The sheep graze on the grass over the old houses, as they have done for five hundred years, and look up at the visitors with mild curiosity, and go on eating.

[long-pause]

In the British Library in London, there is a beautiful old book called the Luttrell Psalter.

[pause]

It was made about twenty or thirty years after the winter of our story, for a rich knight named Geoffrey Luttrell, who lived in Lincolnshire, not very far from Wharram Percy. It is a book of psalms, in Latin. But in its margins, the artists painted hundreds of little pictures of the ordinary life of the English countryside, in bright colours.

[pause]

{img:illuminated-manuscript-margin-ploughing-scene}
There are men ploughing with oxen, and sowing seed, and harrowing, and reaping, and threshing. There are women milking cows, and spinning, and feeding hens, and carrying sheaves. There is a cook stirring a pot, and a family eating at a table. There are children playing, and musicians, and a man scaring away crows.

[pause]

When people today want to know what life was like for the peasants of medieval England, these little pictures are among the first things they look at.

[long-pause]

In the villages of England today, there are still many reminders of those times.

[pause]

The old fields can still be seen in many places, with their long strips, which show up as ridges and furrows in the grass, when the sun is low, in the evening or in winter. The old village churches still stand, in thousands of villages, and the bells still ring. And some of the old customs survive: Plough Monday, May Day, wassailing the apple trees, the Christmas mummers.

[pause]

And many English words and sayings come from those days: curfew, by hook or by crook, and many more.

[long-pause]

On a winter's evening at Wharram Percy, the valley is very quiet.

[pause]

The sun goes down early, behind the chalk hills, and the sky turns pink and gold, and then grey. The frost begins to settle on the grass. The sheep gather together in the shelter of the hedge. An owl calls from the trees by the church. The stream runs on, quietly, under the willows, past the old mill pond.

[pause]

{img:winter-dusk-valley-church-ruin-frost-sheep-stars}
And where the houses once stood, and the fires once burned, there is only the grass, and the frost, and the stars coming out, one by one, over the silent valley.

[pause]

But if you stand there, in the twilight, and listen, it is not hard to imagine the voices of the villagers, and the laughter of the children, and the lowing of the oxen in the byre, and the crackle of the fire on the hearth, on a winter's night, seven hundred years ago.

[long-pause]

So we leave the little valley for tonight, under the winter stars, with its quiet church and its silent fields, and the memory of the families who lived there, and kept each other warm, through all the long winters.
