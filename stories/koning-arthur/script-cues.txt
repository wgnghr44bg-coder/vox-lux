Tonight we travel back about fifteen hundred years, to a time of mist and legend. We go to the west of Britain, around the year 500. It is a land of green hills, deep forests, rocky coasts and quiet lakes. The Romans have gone. Their towns are crumbling. King Arthur is one of the most famous figures in the world. Everyone has heard of him. The king who pulled a sword from a stone. Who ruled from his castle at Camelot. Who sat with his knights at the Round Table. Who had a wise wizard called Merlin, and a magic sword called Excalibur. And who, at the end, was carried away to a magical island called Avalon, where he sleeps, until Britain needs him again.

[pause]

But was there ever a real King Arthur? Nobody knows for sure. Historians have argued about it for hundreds of years. There is very little written evidence from the time when he is supposed to have lived. And the stories about him were written down much later, and changed and grew with every telling. Like a little stream that becomes a great river, the legend of Arthur grew over the centuries.

Tonight, we will follow both the history and the legend. First, we will visit the real Britain of around the year 500. We will see how people lived after the Romans left. We will visit a hill fort with a great wooden hall, and a rocky castle by the sea, where ships came from far away. We will listen to the bards, the poets who sang about heroes, by the fire.

We will meet the knights, Gawain and Lancelot and Percival, and follow them on their quest for the Holy Grail. And at the end, we will travel to Avalon, the island of apples, hidden in the mist.

There are a few battles in the stories of Arthur. We will see them only from a great distance, like a storm far away over the hills. Because most of all, the legend of Arthur is about other things. About friendship and loyalty. About kindness and courage. About a dream of a good kingdom, where the strong protect the weak. So let us begin, in the mist, on a hill in the west of Britain, around the year 500.

[long-pause]

{img:ruined-roman-town-britain-410-grass-growing-forum-columns-empty}
For almost four hundred years, Britain was part of the Roman Empire. The Romans built towns with stone houses and bath houses, straight roads, great walls and country villas with mosaic floors. Many Britons lived in a Roman way. They spoke Latin, wore Roman clothes and worshipped Roman gods, and later, the Christian God.

[long-pause]

But around the year 410, the Roman soldiers left Britain. The empire was in trouble everywhere, and the emperor needed his soldiers to defend Italy and Gaul. He told the Britons that they would have to look after themselves from now on. And so, suddenly, Britain was on its own.

[pause]

What happened next is one of the most mysterious times in the history of Britain. Historians sometimes call it the Dark Ages. Not because it was dark, but because we know so little about it. Very few people wrote anything down. And most of what was written has been lost. So we must piece together the story from a few old books, from archaeology, and from guesses.

[pause]

{img:roman-villa-abandoned-britain-mosaic-floor-overgrown-weeds-sheep}
Slowly, the Roman way of life faded. The towns emptied, as trade broke down and there was no more Roman money. The great villas were abandoned, and their mosaic floors were covered by grass. The bath houses fell into ruin. Without Roman engineers, nobody repaired the roads and the aqueducts. People went back to living in the countryside, in small villages and farms, growing their own food.

[pause]

Britain broke up into many small kingdoms. Each was ruled by a local king or chieftain, with his own band of warriors. They fought each other sometimes, and sometimes made alliances. They lived in wooden halls, often on the tops of old hill forts, which had been built long before the Romans came. And from these halls, they ruled their lands and their people.

[long-pause]

{img:saxon-boats-arriving-east-coast-britain-families-settlers-sea}
At the same time, new people began to arrive from across the sea. They were the Angles, the Saxons and the Jutes, from the lands that are now Germany and Denmark. At first, some came as soldiers, hired by British kings to help defend them. Then more and more came, with their families, looking for land to farm. They settled in the east of Britain, and slowly spread westward.

[pause]

The Britons and the newcomers sometimes fought, and sometimes lived side by side. Over two hundred years, the east and the south of Britain became English, speaking the language of the Anglo-Saxons. But in the west, in Wales, Cornwall and the north, the Britons kept their own language and their own kingdoms. Their language was a Celtic language, the ancestor of modern Welsh.

[pause]

{img:western-britain-green-hills-wales-celtic-church-stone-cross-mist}
It is in this western world, the world of the Britons, that the legend of Arthur was born. A world of small kingdoms and great halls, of Christian monks and old pagan stories, of poets and warriors. The Britons remembered a time, they said, when a great leader had united them, and had held back the newcomers for a while. They told stories about him by the fire. And they gave him a name. Arthur.

[long-pause]

Was Arthur a real man? Perhaps he was a British war leader, who won some battles around the year 500. Perhaps he was a mixture of several leaders, whose stories were combined into one. Perhaps he was a hero of old stories, who was made into a historical figure later on. We do not know. But we do know that the people of western Britain loved his stories. And that those stories would one day travel all over the world.

[long-pause]

{img:cadbury-castle-hill-fort-somerset-ramparts-timber-hall-gate-500ad}
In the county of Somerset, in the southwest of England, there is a big, flat-topped hill, called Cadbury Castle. It is surrounded by four rings of grassy banks and ditches, one inside the other, like the rings of a tree. These banks were built more than two thousand years ago, by the people of the Iron Age, long before the Romans came. And around the year 500, someone important came back to live on top of this hill.

[long-pause]

In the 1960s, archaeologists dug at Cadbury. And they found something exciting. On the top of the hill, they found the remains of a great timber hall, about twenty metres long. They found that the old banks had been repaired, and a new wall of stone and timber built on top, around the whole hill. And they found a gateway with a wooden tower. All of it from around the year 500.

[pause]

This was not the home of an ordinary farmer. It was the home of a great lord, or a king, with many people working for him. It was the biggest fortified place in Britain at that time, as far as we know. So who lived there? Nobody knows. But for hundreds of years, local people had called this hill Camelot. And a writer in the fifteen hundreds said that the people of the area believed that King Arthur had lived there.

[pause]

{img:great-timber-hall-interior-hearth-long-benches-shields-wall-500ad}
Let us imagine the hill fort as it was around the year 500. On top of the hill, there is the great hall. It is a long, rectangular building, made of thick wooden posts and planks, with a high roof of straw or reeds. Inside, there is one big room, with a fire burning in the middle. Along the walls, there are benches, covered with furs and woollen blankets. At one end, there is a raised seat, for the lord.

[pause]

Around the hall, there are other buildings. Smaller houses, workshops, storehouses and stables. There is a blacksmith's forge, where swords, spearheads and tools are made. There are weavers and leather workers. There are pens for cattle, sheep and pigs. And there are hundreds of people. The lord and his family. His warriors and their families. Servants, craftsmen and farmers.

[long-pause]

{img:hill-fort-family-daily-life-woman-weaving-children-playing-geese-500ad}
In one of the small houses near the hall, there lives a family. The father, Cadoc, is a blacksmith. He makes swords and spearheads for the lord's warriors, and tools for the farmers. The mother, Elen, is a weaver. She weaves the fine woollen cloth that the lord and his family wear, in bright colours. They have a daughter, called Nest, who is ten years old. And a little son, called Dewi, who is five.

[pause]

Nest helps her mother with the weaving, and fetches water from the spring below the hill. Dewi follows his father around the forge, watching the sparks fly. In the evenings, the family sits around their little fire, and eats bread, cheese and stew. And sometimes, when there is a feast in the great hall, they are allowed to go and listen to the bard, who sings the old stories.

[pause]

{img:view-from-hill-fort-somerset-levels-glastonbury-tor-distance-misty-morning}
From the top of the hill, the view is wonderful. To the north and west, there are the flat, wet lands of Somerset, with rivers, marshes and meadows. Far away, on a clear day, you can see a strange, steep, cone-shaped hill, rising out of the flat land like an island. It is Glastonbury Tor. In winter, when the marshes flood, it really does become an island, surrounded by water and mist. Later, people will say that this was the island of Avalon.

[long-pause]

Nest loves to sit on the bank of the hill fort in the evening, looking at the Tor in the distance. Her mother says that the Tor is a magical place, where the old fairy folk live, inside the hill. Her father says that there are monks living there now, Christian monks, who have built a little church at its foot. Nest wonders who is right. Perhaps both of them are, she thinks. The world is full of mysteries.

[long-pause]

{img:tintagel-headland-cornwall-cliffs-sea-ruins-buildings-500ad-sunlight}
On the wild north coast of Cornwall, in the far southwest of Britain, there is a rocky headland, sticking out into the sea. It is called Tintagel. On three sides, there are steep cliffs, with the waves of the Atlantic Ocean crashing at their feet. On the fourth side, there is only a narrow neck of rock, connecting it to the land. It is one of the most beautiful and dramatic places in Britain.

[long-pause]

Around the year 500, Tintagel was an important place. Archaeologists have found the remains of many buildings on the headland, made of stone, with slate roofs. Perhaps a hundred buildings or more. It may have been the summer home of the kings of Cornwall, or a great trading post. And among the ruins, the archaeologists found something astonishing.

[pause]

They found pieces of pottery from the Mediterranean. Big clay jars, called amphorae, which had carried wine and olive oil. Fine red dishes and bowls, from North Africa. Glass cups and bottles. These things came from the eastern Mediterranean, from places like Greece, Turkey and Egypt, thousands of kilometres away. More of this pottery has been found at Tintagel than at any other place in Britain from that time.

[pause]

{img:merchant-ship-arriving-tintagel-cove-amphorae-unloading-cliffs-sea}
So, around the year 500, ships came all the way from the Mediterranean, across the Bay of Biscay, to this little rocky headland in Cornwall. They brought wine, olive oil, fine pottery and glass, which only the richest people could afford. And they took back tin, a precious metal found in Cornwall, which was needed to make bronze. The lords of Tintagel were rich, and they had connections with the far side of the world.

[pause]

We can imagine the ships arriving in the little cove below the cliffs. The sailors, with dark hair and tanned faces, speaking Greek or Latin. The jars of wine being carried up the steep path. The lord of Tintagel and his family, drinking wine from the Mediterranean, from glass cups, looking out over the grey Atlantic. It is a surprising picture, at the edge of the world.

[long-pause]

{img:merlins-cave-tintagel-beach-sea-cave-waves-low-tide-mysterious}
Later, the legends made Tintagel the place where Arthur was born. The story says that his father was a king called Uther Pendragon, and his mother was a lady called Igraine, who lived at Tintagel. Through the magic of Merlin, Uther came to the castle in disguise. And there, Arthur was born. Merlin took the baby away, to be raised in secret, far from the court.

[pause]

Below the headland, there is a cave, which goes right through the rock, from one side to the other. The sea flows into it at high tide. People call it Merlin's Cave. The legend says that Merlin lived there, or that he found the baby Arthur there, washed up on the beach by a great wave. When the tide is low, you can walk into the cave. It is dark and echoing, and the sound of the waves fills it like music.

[pause]

{img:tintagel-sunset-headland-golden-light-sea-gulls-peaceful-evening}
In the thirteenth century, long after the time of Arthur, a rich earl called Richard built a castle at Tintagel. He built it there because of the legend, not because it was a good place for a castle. He wanted to live in the place where King Arthur was born. The ruins of his castle are still there today, on the headland, along with the much older ruins from around the year 500.

[long-pause]

On a summer evening, when the sun sets over the sea, Tintagel is magical. The cliffs glow golden. The sea turns pink and silver. The gulls cry overhead. And it is easy to imagine a great king being born here, in a castle on the cliffs, surrounded by the sea and the stories. Whether it is true or not, Tintagel feels like a place where legends begin.

[pause]

Nest, in her hill fort, has never seen Tintagel. But her father has. Once, as a young man, he travelled there with the lord, to buy wine and fine pottery from the ships. He tells her about the high cliffs, the roaring sea, and the strange sailors from the south, with their dark eyes and their soft, singing language. Nest listens, and dreams of the sea, which she has never seen.

[long-pause]

{img:celtic-bard-harp-singing-great-hall-firelight-listeners-500ad}
In the halls of the British kings, around the year 500, there was one person who was almost as important as the king himself. The bard. A bard was a poet and a singer, who knew hundreds of old poems by heart. He sang about the great deeds of the king and his warriors. He sang about the past, about the old heroes and the old gods. And he kept the memory of the people alive.

[long-pause]

To become a bard, a young man had to study for many years, with an older bard. He had to learn all the old poems, the history of the families, the names of the kings and the heroes, and the rules of poetry, which were very complicated. He had to learn to play the harp, or the lyre. And he had to learn to make new poems, on the spot, for every occasion. A good bard was greatly honoured, and well rewarded.

[pause]

In the hill fort at Cadbury, the bard is an old man called Taliesin. Well, perhaps not the famous Taliesin, the greatest bard of the Britons, who lived a little later. But our bard is named after a great bard of the past. He has long white hair and a white beard, and very bright eyes. He wears a blue cloak, and carries a small harp, made of willow wood, with strings of horsehair.

[pause]

{img:old-bard-with-harp-children-sitting-at-feet-listening-hall-evening}
On feast nights, the whole hill fort gathers in the great hall. The lord sits on his seat at the end. His warriors and their families sit along the benches. The fire blazes in the middle. There is roast meat, bread, and mead, the sweet drink made from honey. And when everyone has eaten, the bard stands up, plucks a few notes on his harp, and the hall becomes quiet.

[pause]

He sings about the old heroes. About the kings of long ago, who lived before the Romans came. About the great battles, the brave warriors, the generous lords who gave gold rings to their followers. He sings about love and loss, about the beauty of the land, and about the sadness of things that pass away. His voice is old, but strong. And everyone listens, in silence.

[long-pause]

{img:manuscript-old-welsh-poem-page-candlelight-monk-copying-later}
One of the oldest poems of the Britons is called Y Gododdin. It was made, perhaps, around the year 600, by a bard called Aneirin. It is about a group of brave warriors from the north of Britain, near where Edinburgh is today. In one of its lines, the poet praises a warrior, saying that he was brave, but he was no Arthur. It is one of the very first times that the name of Arthur appears anywhere.

[pause]

What does this tell us? It tells us that, already around the year 600, or a little later, the Britons knew the name of Arthur. And that he was a famous hero, the greatest example of bravery. So famous that the poet could compare other warriors to him, and everyone would understand. It is a small line. But it is like a tiny light, shining through the darkness of the past.

[pause]

{img:nest-girl-listening-by-fire-bard-story-wide-eyes-dark-age-hall}
In our hill fort, little Nest loves the bard more than anyone. She sits as close to the fire as she can, with her knees pulled up, and listens to every word. She learns the stories by heart. And sometimes, the old bard notices her, and smiles at her. One evening, after the feast, he asks her which story she likes best. She says the stories about the great leader who protected the Britons. The bard nods. Ah, he says. Arthur.

[long-pause]

He tells her that Arthur was a great war leader, long ago, in her grandfather's time, or a little before. He gathered the kings of the Britons together, and led them in many battles, and won a great victory, which gave the land peace for many years. Nest asks if Arthur was a king. The bard thinks about it. Perhaps, he says. Or perhaps he was something more. A leader of kings. Then he smiles, and says that it is late, and that she should go to bed.

[long-pause]

{img:monk-gildas-writing-in-cell-stone-small-window-quill-angry-sermon}
Around the year 540, a monk called Gildas wrote a book. It was a long, angry sermon, scolding the kings of the Britons for their sins. He thought they were wicked and lazy, and that God was punishing the Britons for it. But in his book, Gildas also told a little bit of history. And it is one of the very few pieces of writing that survive from the time of Arthur.

[long-pause]

Gildas wrote about how the Romans left, and how the Saxons came. He wrote about a leader of the Britons, called Ambrosius Aurelianus, who was of Roman family, and who rallied the Britons to defend their land. And then he wrote about a great battle, at a place called Mount Badon. He said that the Britons won a great victory there. And that after it, there was peace for many years, more than forty years, until his own time.

[pause]

Gildas did not say who led the Britons at Badon. He did not mention Arthur at all. Some people think that is strange, if Arthur was the great hero of the battle. Others think that Gildas simply did not like to praise any leader, because he was too busy scolding them. We do not know. And we do not even know where Mount Badon was. Perhaps near the town of Bath. Perhaps somewhere else.

[pause]

{img:distant-hills-storm-clouds-far-away-battle-memory-landscape-calm-valley}
We will not visit the battle itself. It is enough to know that, around the year 500, something happened that the Britons remembered for a very long time. A victory, after which there was peace. The fields could be farmed. The halls could feast. Children could grow up without fear. For a generation, the land was quiet.

[pause]

Later writers, much later, said that it was Arthur who won the battle of Badon. And so Badon became the greatest of Arthur's victories in the legend. Whether it was really him, we cannot know. But the memory of the peace that came after it was real. And perhaps that is why the Britons loved Arthur so much. Not because he fought, but because he brought peace.

[long-pause]

{img:peaceful-countryside-britain-farms-fields-children-playing-sunshine-500ad}
In our hill fort, life during those years of peace is calm. The farmers plough their fields and bring in their harvests. The blacksmith makes more ploughshares than swords. The weavers weave. The children play. Ships come to Tintagel with wine and oil. Monks build little churches in the hills and valleys, and on the islands off the coast. It is a quiet, good time.

[pause]

Many of these monks are very important. They keep learning alive, in a time when there are no more Roman schools. They copy books by hand. They teach children to read and write in Latin. They travel by sea between Wales, Cornwall, Ireland and Brittany, founding little monasteries everywhere. Some of them become saints, and many places in Wales and Cornwall are still named after them.

[pause]

{img:celtic-monk-small-boat-crossing-sea-to-island-monastery-dawn}
Elen, Nest's mother, often weaves cloth for the monks. She takes it down the hill to the little monastery by the river, where the monks live in small huts around a wooden church. Nest goes with her. The monks are kind. They give her apples from their orchard. And one old monk shows her a book, a real book, written by hand, with beautiful letters. Nest cannot read it. But she touches the page very gently, with one finger.

[long-pause]

The old monk tells her that, in the books, the stories can live forever. Even when the bards are gone, and the halls have fallen, the words written in the books will remain. Nest thinks about that on the way home. She thinks about the old bard, and his stories of Arthur. And she hopes that one day, someone will write them down, so that they will never be forgotten. And many years later, someone did.

[pause]

In the evenings of those peaceful years, the people of the hill fort sit outside their houses, in the warm summer air, and watch the sun go down over the marshes. The children play in the grass. The old people tell stories. And nobody is afraid. It is, perhaps, the kind of time that people would later remember as a golden age. And perhaps that is where the dream of Camelot began.

[long-pause]

{img:welsh-monk-writing-history-britons-829-manuscript-scriptorium-candle}
After Gildas, for about three hundred years, nobody seems to have written about Arthur. Perhaps they did, and the books were lost. But the bards kept singing about him. And then, around the year 830, a monk in Wales wrote a book called the History of the Britons. In it, Arthur appears for the first time as a real person in a history book.

[long-pause]

The monk's name was probably Nennius. He wrote that he had made a heap of all he could find, from old books, old poems and old stories. And in this heap, there is a short passage about Arthur. It says that Arthur fought together with the kings of the Britons, and that he was their leader in battle. Then it lists twelve battles that Arthur won, with strange old names. And the twelfth battle was at Mount Badon.

[pause]

Nennius also wrote down some marvels, wonders of Britain. One of them is about Arthur's dog, called Cabal. Nennius says that there is a heap of stones in Wales, and on top of it, there is a stone with the footprint of a dog. It was made by Cabal, the dog of Arthur the soldier, when they were hunting a great boar. And if you take the stone away, the next day it will be back on the heap.

[pause]

{img:large-hunting-dog-legendary-cabal-paw-print-stone-cairn-welsh-hills}
About a hundred years later, another book was written in Wales, called the Annals of Wales. It is a list of years, with short notes about what happened in each year. In the year that we would call 516, it says, the battle of Badon, in which Arthur carried the cross of our Lord Jesus Christ for three days and nights on his shoulders, and the Britons were the victors. And in the year 537, it says, the battle of Camlann, in which Arthur and Medraut fell.

[pause]

Medraut is the name that later became Mordred, the traitor in the legends. But in this early note, it does not say that they were enemies. Only that they both fell, at Camlann. Where was Camlann? Nobody knows. It is another mystery. But in the legend, it became the place of Arthur's last battle, the end of his kingdom.

[long-pause]

{img:welsh-storyteller-fireside-medieval-farmhouse-family-listening-evening}
In Wales, the people also told wonderful fairy tales about Arthur. One of the oldest is called Culhwch and Olwen. It was written down around the year 1100, but it is much older. In it, Arthur is a great king, with a court full of amazing warriors. One can drink a whole sea. One can hear an ant fifty miles away. One is so tall that he can see over the tops of the trees. And one, called Cai, can stay under water for nine days and nine nights.

[pause]

In this story, a young man called Culhwch must win the hand of a giant's daughter, the beautiful Olwen. Wherever Olwen walks, four white flowers grow in her footprints. Her father gives Culhwch a list of impossible tasks. And Arthur and his warriors help him to do them all. They hunt a magical boar across Wales and Cornwall, they find a lost prisoner with the help of the oldest animals in the world, and they win the princess. It is a funny, magical, wonderful story.

[pause]

{img:olwen-walking-meadow-white-flowers-growing-in-footsteps-dawn}
In the old Welsh stories, Arthur is not quite the king we know from later. He is a wild, magical hero, leading a band of strange warriors on fantastic adventures. He has a sword, a spear, a shield and a knife, each with its own name. His sword is called Caledfwlch. Later, that name became Excalibur. His ship is called Prydwen. And his wife is called Gwenhwyfar. Later, that name became Guinevere.

[long-pause]

So, by the year 1100, the stories of Arthur were well known in Wales, Cornwall and Brittany. They were told by the fire, in Welsh and Cornish and Breton. They were full of magic, giants, monsters, quests and marvels. But outside these lands, few people had heard of Arthur. That was about to change, because of one man, and one remarkable book.

[long-pause]

{img:geoffrey-of-monmouth-scholar-writing-oxford-1136-desk-books-candle}
In the year 1136, a scholar called Geoffrey of Monmouth was living in Oxford. He was a clever man, from the borders of Wales, who loved old stories. And he wrote a book that would change everything. It was called the History of the Kings of Britain. It told the story of all the kings of Britain, from the very beginning, more than two thousand years before, until the coming of the Anglo-Saxons.

[long-pause]

Geoffrey said that he had translated his book from a very old book in the British language, which a friend had given him. Nobody has ever found that old book. Most historians think that Geoffrey made up a lot of his story himself, using bits of old history, old legends and his own great imagination. But it did not matter. His book was a huge success. It was copied again and again, all over Europe. More than two hundred copies still exist today.

[pause]

And the best part of the book, the part that everyone loved, was the story of Arthur. Geoffrey turned Arthur from a Welsh war leader into a great and glorious king. He wrote the story that most people still know today, in its first form. Let us hear it, as Geoffrey told it, in a gentle way.

[pause]

{img:king-uther-pendragon-dragon-banner-castle-legend-medieval-style}
There was once a king of Britain called Uther Pendragon. Pendragon means chief dragon, and Uther saw a comet in the sky, shaped like a dragon, and took the dragon as his sign. With the help of Merlin's magic, Uther married the beautiful Igraine of Tintagel, and they had a son, called Arthur. When Uther passed away, Arthur became king, though he was only fifteen years old. He was generous, brave and kind, and the people loved him.

[pause]

Arthur gathered the best knights from all over the world at his court. He married Guinevere, the most beautiful lady in Britain. He brought peace and order to the land. And his court became so famous for its manners, its splendour and its knights, that people in faraway countries tried to copy it. In Geoffrey's book, Arthur even crosses the sea and becomes a great emperor, ruling over many lands.

[long-pause]

{img:arthur-crowned-court-feast-caerleon-banners-knights-ladies-legend}
Geoffrey wrote that Arthur held his court at the city of Caerleon, in Wales, where the Romans once had their legion's fortress. He described a great feast there, at Whitsun, when Arthur was crowned again before all his lords. There were kings and dukes from all over the world. There were processions, music, games and tournaments. The ladies would only love a knight who had proved himself three times. And everyone was happy.

[pause]

But the end of the story is sad. While Arthur is away, fighting across the sea, his nephew Mordred takes the crown, and Guinevere too. Arthur comes home, and there is a great battle at Camlann. Mordred falls. Arthur is badly wounded. And he is carried away to the island of Avalon, so that his wounds may be healed. And there, Geoffrey says, the story leaves him.

[pause]

{img:readers-across-medieval-europe-copying-geoffrey-book-monks-nobles}
Geoffrey's book spread like wildfire. Monks copied it. Nobles read it. Poets turned it into verse, in French, English and many other languages. Within a few years, everyone in Europe had heard of King Arthur. Kings of England began to call themselves the heirs of Arthur. And people began to visit the places in the story, looking for traces of the great king.

[long-pause]

Some people at the time did not believe Geoffrey. A historian called William of Newburgh said that Geoffrey had made it all up, out of love for lies. But most readers did not care. The story was too good. And so, thanks to a scholar in Oxford with a great imagination, the little Welsh hero became the most famous king in the world.

[long-pause]

{img:merlin-old-wizard-long-beard-robe-staff-forest-clearing-mist-legend}
In the legend of Arthur, there is one figure who is almost as famous as the king himself. Merlin, the wise magician. He is the one who helps Uther, who brings Arthur into the world, who guides the young king, and who sees the future. In the stories, he is an old man with a long white beard, a long robe and a staff. But he did not always look like that.

[long-pause]

Merlin, like Arthur, has his roots in old Welsh stories. In Wales, there were tales of a wild poet and prophet called Myrddin. The stories said that he went mad after a terrible battle, and fled into the great forest of Celyddon, in the north. There, he lived alone among the trees and the animals, speaking to a pig and an apple tree, and seeing visions of the future. He made poems about what was to come.

[pause]

Geoffrey of Monmouth took this Myrddin, and changed his name to Merlinus, in Latin. And he made him part of the story of Arthur. In Geoffrey's book, Merlin appears first as a boy, with no human father, who has strange powers. A king is trying to build a tower, but every night, the walls fall down. Nobody knows why. The boy Merlin tells the king that there is a pool under the foundations. And in the pool, there are two dragons, one red and one white, who fight every night.

[pause]

{img:two-dragons-red-white-pool-under-tower-legend-merlin-boy-watching}
The king's men dig, and they find the pool, and the two dragons, just as Merlin said. The red dragon stands for the Britons, and the white dragon for the Saxons. Merlin explains this to the king, and makes many prophecies about the future of Britain. Today, the red dragon is still the symbol of Wales, on its flag. It is one of the oldest flags in the world.

[pause]

In Geoffrey's book, Merlin also builds Stonehenge. The story says that Merlin brought the great stones by magic, from a mountain in Ireland, to make a monument for the Britons who had fallen in war. Of course, Stonehenge is much, much older than the time of Arthur. It was built about three thousand years before. But it is interesting that some of its smaller stones really did come from far away, from the mountains of Wales. So the legend had a tiny piece of truth in it.

[long-pause]

{img:merlin-advising-young-king-arthur-throne-room-candlelight-legend}
In later stories, Merlin becomes the teacher and adviser of the young Arthur. He arranges for the baby Arthur to be raised in secret, by a good knight called Sir Ector, in the countryside. He tells the young king what to do, and warns him about dangers. He knows everything, because he can see the past and the future. But he cannot change what is fated to happen. That is the sadness of Merlin.

[pause]

In some stories, Merlin can change his shape. He appears as an old man, a young boy, a shepherd, a deer. He walks in the forest and talks with the animals. He knows the secrets of the stars and the herbs. And in one later story, by an English writer, Merlin lives his life backwards. He is born old, and grows younger every day. So he remembers the future, but not the past.

[pause]

{img:merlin-and-nimue-forest-hawthorn-tree-enchanted-sleep-gentle-legend}
How did Merlin's story end? There are many versions. In one of the most famous, Merlin falls in love with a young enchantress, called Nimue, or Vivien. He teaches her all his magic. And she uses it to enchant him, gently, and puts him to sleep in a hawthorn tree, or a crystal cave, or a tower of air, in the forest. And there he sleeps still, the story says, and will sleep until the end of the world.

[long-pause]

Nest, in our hill fort, does not know about Merlin. He came into the story later. But she knows about the wise men and women of her own time. The old women who know the herbs. The monks who know the stars. And the bards, who know the past and seem to see the future. Perhaps Merlin is a little of all of them. A memory of all the wise people of the old days, rolled into one, and given a long white beard.

[pause]

In the Welsh poems, the old prophet Myrddin speaks to an apple tree in the forest, which hides him from his enemies. He calls it sweet apple tree, and tells it his sorrows and his visions. It is a strange and lovely picture. A wild old man, alone in the forest, talking to a tree. And perhaps it is the first seed of the island of apples, Avalon, which comes at the very end of the story.

[long-pause]

{img:sword-in-stone-anvil-churchyard-snow-christmas-morning-legend-quiet}
One of the most famous parts of the legend of Arthur is the sword in the stone. It was not in Geoffrey's book. It was added later, about the year 1200, by a French poet called Robert de Boron. And it is such a good story, that it has been told and retold ever since.

[long-pause]

The story goes like this. King Uther Pendragon has passed away. He has no son, as far as anyone knows. Because Merlin took the baby Arthur away, to be raised in secret. So the great lords of Britain quarrel about who should be the next king. Each one wants the crown for himself. The kingdom is in danger of falling apart.

[pause]

So Merlin goes to the archbishop, and asks him to call all the lords to London, at Christmas, to pray for a sign from God. On Christmas morning, as the lords come out of the great church, they see something strange in the churchyard. A big square stone, with an anvil of iron on top of it. And stuck in the anvil, there is a beautiful sword. On the stone, there are words written in gold. Whoever pulls this sword from this stone is the rightful king of all Britain.

[pause]

{img:lords-trying-to-pull-sword-from-stone-crowd-watching-medieval-legend}
One by one, the great lords try to pull the sword out. They pull and they pull, with all their strength. But the sword does not move, not even a little. Big strong knights, proud dukes, rich earls, all try. None of them can do it. So the archbishop says that they must wait, and that God will show them the true king in his own time. And he sets ten knights to guard the stone.

[pause]

At New Year, there is a great tournament in London. Among the knights who come is the good Sir Ector, with his son, Sir Kay, who has just been made a knight. And with them comes a young squire, a boy of about fifteen, who is Sir Ector's foster son. His name is Arthur. Nobody knows who his real parents are, not even Arthur himself.

[long-pause]

{img:young-arthur-squire-pulling-sword-easily-from-stone-quiet-churchyard}
On the way to the tournament, Sir Kay realises that he has forgotten his sword at home. He asks Arthur to go back and fetch it. Arthur rides back, but the house is locked, and everyone has gone to the tournament. Arthur does not know what to do. Then he remembers that he passed a sword in a stone, in a churchyard. He thinks that Kay can borrow that one. So he rides to the churchyard. The guards have gone to the tournament too. And Arthur takes hold of the sword, and pulls it out of the stone, quite easily, as if it were stuck in butter.

[pause]

He brings the sword to Kay. Kay recognises it at once. He goes to his father and says, look, I have the sword from the stone, so I must be king. But Sir Ector knows his son. He takes them both back to the churchyard. He tells Kay to put the sword back, and pull it out again. Kay cannot. Then he tells Arthur to try. And Arthur pulls it out again, easily.

[pause]

{img:old-knight-kneeling-before-surprised-boy-holding-sword-churchyard}
Sir Ector kneels down before Arthur. And so does Kay. Arthur is very confused. Why are you kneeling to me, father, he asks. And Sir Ector tells him the truth. That he is not his real father. That Merlin brought him as a baby. And that he must be the son of a king. Arthur is so upset that he starts to cry. He says that he does not want to lose his father. Sir Ector is very moved.

[long-pause]

Of course, the great lords are not happy that a boy has pulled out the sword. They make him put it back, and try again, at Candlemas, at Easter, and at Pentecost. Each time, the lords try and fail. And each time, Arthur pulls it out easily. In the end, the ordinary people of London shout out that they want Arthur as their king, and nobody else. And so the boy squire is crowned king of Britain. And the first thing he does is make Sir Kay his steward, so that he will always be close to his foster family.

[long-pause]

{img:lady-of-the-lake-arm-rising-from-water-holding-sword-misty-dawn}
Arthur has a sword from the stone. But in many of the stories, that is not his most famous sword. His most famous sword is called Excalibur. And it came to him in a very different way. From the hand of a mysterious lady, rising out of a lake.

[long-pause]

The story says that one day, the young king Arthur breaks his sword. He is sad and worried. A king without a sword is a king in danger. So Merlin takes him to a lake, deep in a forest, surrounded by hills. The water is very still, and very clear. A soft mist lies on its surface. And in the middle of the lake, Arthur sees something wonderful. An arm, dressed in white silk, rising out of the water. And in the hand, there is a beautiful sword, shining in the light.

[pause]

Then a lady comes walking across the water toward them. She is the Lady of the Lake. Merlin tells Arthur that she lives in a beautiful palace, under the water, deep in the lake. Arthur speaks to her politely, and asks her if he may have the sword. She says yes, if he will give her a gift, whenever she asks for it. Arthur agrees.

[pause]

{img:arthur-in-small-boat-rowing-to-sword-misty-lake-merlin-on-shore}
So Arthur and Merlin step into a little boat, which is waiting at the edge of the lake. They row out to the middle. Arthur takes the sword from the hand. And the hand and the arm sink slowly back into the water, without a sound. Arthur looks at the sword. It is the most beautiful sword he has ever seen. Its blade shines like a mirror. Its handle is decorated with gold and precious stones.

[pause]

Merlin asks him which he likes better, the sword or its sheath, the scabbard. Arthur says the sword, of course. Merlin tells him that he is wrong. The scabbard is ten times more precious. Because as long as Arthur wears it, he will never lose a drop of blood, however badly he is wounded. Arthur must keep it safe, always. It is a little lesson from Merlin. Protection is more precious than power.

[long-pause]

{img:excalibur-sword-glowing-scabbard-jewels-close-mystical-light}
The name Excalibur comes from the old Welsh name of Arthur's sword, Caledfwlch, which may mean hard lightning, or hard cleft. Geoffrey of Monmouth turned it into Caliburnus. And the French poets turned that into Escalibor. Which became Excalibur. Every storyteller changed it a little, like a pebble polished by many hands.

[pause]

Lakes were very special places for the ancient Britons. Long before the time of Arthur, people threw precious things into lakes and rivers, as gifts to the gods. Swords, shields, spears, cauldrons and jewellery. Archaeologists have found many of them, at the bottom of lakes and rivers all over Britain. So perhaps the story of the sword from the lake remembers that very old custom, of giving swords to the water, and receiving gifts back.

[pause]

{img:old-celtic-swords-found-in-lake-mud-museum-glass-case-bronze-iron}
There are several lakes in Britain that claim to be the lake of the Lady. In Cornwall, there is Dozmary Pool, a small, lonely lake on the wild moors, which people say is bottomless. In Wales, there are several lakes in the mountains of Snowdonia, deep and dark among the rocky peaks. Each has its own story. And on misty mornings, it is easy to imagine an arm in white silk, rising out of the still water.

[long-pause]

The Lady of the Lake appears in many stories. In some, she is a kind fairy, who helps Arthur and his knights. In others, she raises the baby Lancelot in her palace under the lake, which is why he is called Lancelot of the Lake. And at the very end of the story, she will come back once more, to take Excalibur home. But that is for later. For now, Arthur rides home with his shining sword, and Merlin rides beside him, smiling quietly to himself.

[long-pause]

{img:camelot-castle-towers-river-meadows-summer-legend-banners-flowers}
In the later stories, Arthur rules his kingdom from a wonderful castle called Camelot. It does not appear in the early Welsh stories, or in Geoffrey's book. It was first mentioned by a French poet, around the year 1180. And from then on, Camelot became the most famous castle in all the stories of the world.

[long-pause]

Where was Camelot? Nobody knows, because it was probably never a real place. It was a dream of a perfect castle, in a perfect kingdom. But people have always tried to find it. Some said it was at Winchester, the old capital of England. Some said it was at Caerleon, in Wales, where the Roman fortress was. Some said it was at Cadbury, the hill fort in Somerset. And some said it was in the north, or in Cornwall. Each place has its own story.

[pause]

In the stories, Camelot is a beautiful castle, with tall white towers, shining roofs and colourful banners flying in the wind. It stands on a hill, above a river, surrounded by green meadows, orchards and forests. Below the castle, there is a town, with houses, a market, and a great church. The roads leading to Camelot are always busy with knights, ladies, merchants and travellers, all coming to see the famous court of King Arthur.

[pause]

{img:castle-great-hall-tapestries-feast-musicians-ladies-knights-legend}
Inside the castle, there is a great hall, hung with beautiful tapestries in many colours. On feast days, the hall is full of people. Knights in fine clothes, ladies in silk gowns, musicians, jugglers and storytellers. There are long tables covered with white cloths and silver dishes, and the food is wonderful. Roast meats, pies, fruits and sweets, and wine in golden cups. And at every great feast, Arthur makes a rule. Nobody may eat until a marvel has been seen, or an adventure has begun.

[pause]

And there is always an adventure. A strange knight rides into the hall, with a challenge. A lady arrives, asking for help. A messenger brings news of a giant or a dragon or an enchanted castle. And one of the knights stands up, and promises to go. The next morning, he rides out of Camelot, and the adventure begins. Some stories say that a knight who went out on an adventure must come back and tell it, so that the clerks could write it down.

[long-pause]

{img:queen-guinevere-garden-castle-ladies-flowers-gentle-summer-legend}
The queen of Camelot is Guinevere. She is the most beautiful lady in the kingdom, and also kind, wise and generous. She has her own ladies, who sew, sing, play games and read stories together in the castle gardens. The knights of Camelot honour her, and some of them carry her colours, a ribbon or a sleeve, when they ride in tournaments. In the stories, the queen is the centre of the courtly life of Camelot.

[pause]

Camelot is a place of good manners. The knights must be polite to everyone, especially to ladies. They must help anyone who asks for help. They must be honest and keep their promises. They must protect the weak, the poor and the widows. They must never be proud or cruel. This way of behaving was called chivalry. In the real Middle Ages, knights often did not behave this way. But the stories of Camelot showed how they should behave.

[pause]

{img:tournament-meadow-camelot-pavilions-banners-crowds-sunny-gentle}
Outside the castle, in the meadows, there are tournaments. Colourful tents are put up, with flags and banners. The knights ride against each other, with blunt lances, in friendly contests, to show their skill. The ladies and the people watch from wooden stands. There is music and cheering. And the winner receives a prize, often from the hands of the queen herself. It is like a great festival.

[long-pause]

Camelot is a golden dream. A kingdom where the king is good, the knights are brave and kind, and everyone lives in peace and plenty. Many people in the Middle Ages, who lived in hard and dangerous times, loved to dream about it. And perhaps that is why the stories of Camelot are still loved today. Because everyone, everywhere, sometimes dreams of a place like that.

[pause]

In the evening, when the feast is over, the castle grows quiet. The knights go to their rooms. The musicians put away their harps. The candles are blown out one by one. Only the watchmen walk on the walls, looking out over the dark meadows. And the river flows softly below the castle, under the moon, toward the sea.

[long-pause]

{img:round-table-great-hall-knights-seated-equal-candles-legend-calm}
In the great hall of Camelot, there is a famous table. It is round. And around it sit the knights of King Arthur. It is called the Round Table. And it has become a symbol, all over the world, of people meeting together as equals.

[long-pause]

The Round Table first appears in a poem by a writer from the island of Jersey, called Wace, in the year 1155. He retold Geoffrey of Monmouth's story in French verse. And he added something new. He wrote that Arthur's knights often quarrelled about who should sit in the most honourable place at the table, near the king. Each knight thought he was the best. So Arthur had a round table made. At a round table, nobody sits at the head, and nobody sits at the foot. Everyone is equal.

[pause]

It was a simple idea. But it was a powerful one. In the Middle Ages, where you sat at the table showed how important you were. The most important people sat at the high table, at the top of the hall. The less important people sat further and further away. So a table where everyone was equal was something very special. It showed that Arthur valued all his knights, and that they were all brothers.

[pause]

{img:knights-swearing-oath-round-table-hands-raised-pentecost-light}
In later stories, the Round Table became even more magical. Some said it was made by Merlin. Some said it could seat a hundred and fifty knights. Each knight had his own seat, with his name written on it in gold letters, which appeared by magic when the right knight sat down. And there was one empty seat, called the Siege Perilous, the dangerous seat. Only the best and purest knight in the world could sit in it. Anyone else who tried would come to harm.

[pause]

Every year, at the feast of Pentecost, the knights of the Round Table gathered at Camelot and renewed their oath. They promised to be good knights. Never to be cruel. To show mercy to those who asked for it. To help ladies and maidens, and widows and orphans. To be honest, and to keep their word. Never to fight for a wrong cause, even for money or love. It was a beautiful promise.

[long-pause]

{img:winchester-round-table-hanging-wall-great-hall-painted-tudor-rose}
And here is a wonderful thing. In the city of Winchester, in the south of England, there is a real round table. It hangs on the wall of the great hall of Winchester Castle. It is huge, about five and a half metres across, and made of oak. It is painted in green and white, with the names of twenty-four knights around the edge, and a picture of King Arthur sitting at the top, with a crown and a sword.

[pause]

For a long time, people believed that this was the real Round Table of King Arthur. But scientists have studied the wood, and found that it was made around the year 1290, for King Edward the First, who loved the legend of Arthur and held tournaments with feasts at round tables. The painting was added later, around the year 1520, for King Henry the Eighth. And the face of King Arthur in the painting looks very much like young King Henry himself.

[pause]

{img:medieval-round-table-feast-tournament-edward-i-knights-banquet}
In the real Middle Ages, kings and nobles sometimes held feasts and tournaments called Round Tables, in honour of Arthur. Knights dressed up as the knights of the stories. There were jousts, feasts and dances. It was a kind of game, a way of living inside the legend for a few days. Edward the First loved these games. So did many other kings and lords, in England, France and Germany.

[long-pause]

The idea of the Round Table is still alive today. When people meet to discuss something as equals, we call it a round table. A round table discussion, a round table meeting. Every time we use these words, we are remembering Arthur's table, where nobody was more important than anyone else. And that, perhaps, is one of the greatest gifts that the legend of Arthur has given to the world.

[long-pause]

{img:knights-of-round-table-portraits-gawain-lancelot-percival-kay-legend}
The Round Table was famous for its knights. There were many of them, and each had his own stories. Let us meet a few of the most famous ones, gently, as if we were sitting in the great hall of Camelot, watching them come in to the feast.

[long-pause]

First, there is Sir Kay. He is Arthur's foster brother, the son of Sir Ector, who raised Arthur as a boy. He is the steward of Camelot, in charge of the household and the kitchens. In the old Welsh stories, he is a great hero, with magical powers. But in the later stories, he is often a bit grumpy and sharp-tongued. He makes fun of the young knights. But he is loyal to Arthur, always.

[pause]

Then there is Sir Gawain. He is Arthur's nephew, the son of Arthur's sister. He is one of the oldest knights in the stories. He is brave, strong and very courteous, especially to ladies. In some stories, his strength grows during the morning, and is greatest at noon, like the sun, and then fades in the afternoon. He has a famous horse called Gringolet. Many stories were written about Gawain, and he was one of the most popular knights of all.

[pause]

{img:sir-gawain-riding-horse-gringolet-green-forest-pentangle-shield}
Then there is Sir Lancelot of the Lake. He is the greatest knight of all, in the later stories. He was raised by the Lady of the Lake, in her palace under the water, which is how he got his name. He is the bravest and the strongest. But he is also gentle and sad, because he loves Queen Guinevere, though he knows he should not. That love causes great sorrow in the end. But the stories always say that Lancelot was the truest friend Arthur ever had.

[pause]

Then there is Sir Percival. He grew up in a forest, far from other people, with his mother, who wanted to keep him away from knights and danger. He knew nothing about the world. One day, he saw some knights riding through the forest, in their shining armour. He thought they were angels. And he decided to become a knight himself. He came to Camelot as a simple country boy, and everyone laughed at him. But he became one of the best knights of all.

[long-pause]

{img:young-percival-forest-boy-seeing-knights-first-time-amazed-sunbeams}
Then there is Sir Gareth. He came to Camelot as a young man, without telling anyone his name. He asked Arthur to let him work in the kitchens for a year. Sir Kay made fun of him, and gave him a nickname, Beaumains, which means beautiful hands, because his hands were so fine. After a year, Gareth went on an adventure, and showed that he was a great knight. And then it was revealed that he was actually Gawain's younger brother, a prince.

[pause]

There is Sir Bedivere, who was always loyal to Arthur, and who will be with him at the very end. There is Sir Tristan, a sad young knight from Cornwall, who played the harp beautifully and loved a lady called Isolde. There is Sir Galahad, the son of Lancelot, who is so pure and good that he is the only one who can sit in the dangerous seat. And there are many, many others.

[pause]

{img:knights-and-ladies-courtly-scene-garden-harp-tristan-playing-gentle}
Each knight has his own character. Some are brave and wild. Some are gentle and wise. Some are funny. Some are sad. Some make mistakes, and learn from them. That is one of the reasons why the stories of the Round Table are so loved. They are not about perfect heroes. They are about people, with their strengths and their weaknesses, trying to live up to a beautiful promise.

[long-pause]

In the Middle Ages, real knights read or listened to these stories, and tried to be like the knights of the Round Table. Children played at being Lancelot or Gawain. Ladies embroidered their stories on cushions and tapestries. And in castles all over Europe, people sat by the fire in the evenings, listening to a storyteller, and imagining themselves at the Round Table, in the great hall of Camelot.

[pause]

And of all the knights, the one Arthur trusts most is perhaps Sir Bedivere. He is not the strongest or the most famous. He has few great adventures of his own. But he is always there, quietly, at the king's side. In the oldest Welsh stories, he is one of Arthur's first companions. And at the very end of the legend, it is Bedivere who stays with the king, when everyone else has gone.

[long-pause]

{img:green-knight-entering-camelot-hall-christmas-green-horse-holly-branch}
One of the most beautiful stories about the knights of the Round Table was written in England, around the year 1390, by a poet whose name we do not know. It is called Sir Gawain and the Green Knight. It is a winter story, full of snow, magic and good manners. Let us tell it gently.

[long-pause]

It is Christmas at Camelot. The hall is full of light and music. Arthur and his knights are feasting, and the king is waiting, as always, for a marvel before he eats. And then, the doors burst open. A huge knight rides into the hall on a great horse. And both the knight and the horse are completely green. Green skin, green hair, green beard, green clothes. In one hand, he holds a branch of holly. In the other, a great green axe.

[pause]

The Green Knight laughs, and says he has come to play a Christmas game. Any knight may strike him once with the axe. And then, in a year and a day, the knight must come and find him, and receive a blow in return. The hall goes silent. Nobody moves. Then young Sir Gawain stands up, and says that he will play the game, so that his uncle the king does not have to.

[pause]

{img:snowy-forest-winter-knight-riding-alone-cold-mountains-gawain-journey}
Gawain strikes the blow. But the Green Knight, by magic, is not harmed at all. He simply laughs, picks up his axe, and reminds Gawain of his promise. In a year and a day, at the Green Chapel. Then he rides out of the hall, and disappears into the winter night. Everyone is amazed. And Gawain knows that he must keep his word, whatever happens.

[pause]

The year passes. Spring, summer and autumn go by, and the poet describes them beautifully. The leaves come out and fall. And in the winter, Gawain sets out alone, on his horse Gringolet, to find the Green Chapel. He rides through the wild hills and forests of the north, in the snow and the freezing rain. He sleeps outside, in his armour, among the frozen rocks. He has no idea where the Green Chapel is.

[long-pause]

{img:warm-castle-christmas-eve-lord-welcoming-knight-fire-feast-snow-outside}
On Christmas Eve, he finds a beautiful castle in the forest. The lord of the castle welcomes him warmly, and invites him to stay for Christmas. He says that the Green Chapel is very near, and that Gawain can rest until New Year. The lord is a big, cheerful man with a red beard. His wife is beautiful and kind. And there is an old lady too, who sits quietly by the fire.

[pause]

The lord proposes a game. Every day, he will go hunting in the forest. And Gawain will stay in the castle and rest. In the evening, they will exchange whatever they have won during the day. The lord gives Gawain what he has caught in the hunt. And Gawain gives the lord whatever he has received in the castle. Gawain agrees. It seems a simple, friendly game.

[pause]

{img:lady-offering-green-silk-girdle-to-knight-castle-chamber-candlelight}
For three days, the game goes on. The lord goes hunting, and the lady of the castle visits Gawain, and is very friendly to him. Gawain is polite, but careful. On the third day, she offers him a gift. A belt of green silk, which she says will protect anyone who wears it from all harm. Gawain thinks of the Green Knight's axe. And he takes the belt. And that evening, when the lord gives him his gifts, Gawain does not give him the belt. He keeps it, secretly.

[long-pause]

On New Year's morning, Gawain rides to the Green Chapel, wearing the green belt. The Green Knight is waiting. Gawain kneels. The axe falls three times. The first two times, the Green Knight stops. The third time, he barely touches Gawain's neck, leaving only a tiny scratch. Then he laughs, and reveals that he is the lord of the castle. The game was a test. Gawain was almost perfect. He only failed a little, by keeping the belt, because he wanted to live. And for that, the knight forgives him. Gawain rides home, ashamed but wiser. And at Camelot, all the knights decide to wear a green belt too, as a sign of friendship, and of the lesson he learned.

[long-pause]

{img:holy-grail-vision-glowing-cup-light-great-hall-knights-awe-legend}
The greatest adventure of the knights of the Round Table is the quest for the Holy Grail. It is the most mysterious part of the whole legend. And it is the adventure that changes Camelot forever.

[long-pause]

The Grail first appeared in a story by the French poet Chrétien de Troyes, around the year 1190. He wrote about Percival, the young knight from the forest. One evening, Percival comes to a strange castle, ruled by a king who is wounded and cannot walk. The king spends his days fishing in a boat on the river, so he is called the Fisher King. That night, during the meal, Percival sees a strange procession. A young man carrying a bleeding lance. And a maiden carrying a shining dish, made of gold and jewels, which fills the whole hall with light. It is the Grail.

[pause]

Percival is curious. But he has been taught not to ask too many questions. So he says nothing. In the morning, the castle is empty, and then it vanishes. Later, Percival learns that if he had only asked one question, whom does the Grail serve, the wounded king would have been healed, and the land around him, which was dry and barren, would have become green again. Chrétien never finished his story. He passed away, and left the mystery unsolved.

[pause]

{img:fisher-king-boat-river-mist-wounded-old-king-fishing-quiet-castle}
Other writers continued the story. And over time, the Grail became something holy. Writers said it was the cup that Jesus used at the Last Supper, with his disciples. They said that a man called Joseph of Arimathea brought it to Britain, long ago. And they said that it was hidden in a secret castle, and that only the purest knight in the world could find it.

[pause]

In the later stories, the Grail appears at Camelot, at the feast of Pentecost. The doors and windows close by themselves. A great light fills the hall, brighter than the sun. And the Grail floats through the hall, covered with a white cloth, so that nobody can see it. Every knight is filled with joy, and finds in front of him the food he loves most. Then the Grail disappears. And all the knights stand up and swear to search for it, for a year and a day.

[long-pause]

{img:knights-riding-out-from-camelot-different-directions-forest-dawn-quest}
So the knights ride out from Camelot, each alone, in different directions, into the great forests. Arthur is very sad. He knows that many of them will never come back to the Round Table. The quest is not like other adventures. It is not about strength or bravery. It is about goodness, honesty and purity. The knights must look inside themselves, as well as in the forests.

[pause]

Many knights fail. They are good men, but not good enough for the Grail. Gawain fails, because he cares more for adventures than for prayer. Lancelot comes very close. He sees the Grail, in a vision. But he cannot reach it, because of his love for the queen. He is sad, but he accepts it. Only three knights succeed. Galahad, the purest knight of all. Percival, the boy from the forest, who has learned to ask the right questions. And Bors, a humble and good knight.

[pause]

{img:three-knights-kneeling-before-light-chapel-grail-galahad-percival-bors}
The three knights find the Grail in a holy city by the sea, far away. Galahad looks into the Grail, and sees heavenly things that cannot be told. He is so happy that he asks to leave this world. And a hand comes down from heaven, and takes the Grail up into the sky, and Galahad's soul with it, gently. Percival becomes a hermit, and lives a quiet life of prayer. And Bors, alone, rides home to Camelot, to tell Arthur everything.

[long-pause]

What does the Grail mean? People have wondered about this for eight hundred years. Some say it is about the search for God. Some say it is about looking for the best part of yourself. Some say it is about healing, of people and of the land. And some say that the important thing is not to find the Grail at all, but to keep searching, and to ask the right questions. Perhaps that is the real lesson of Percival. Always ask. Whom does it serve?

[long-pause]

{img:sir-bedivere-throwing-excalibur-into-lake-arm-catching-mist-evening}
All stories must come to an end, even the greatest. And the story of King Arthur has one of the most famous endings in the world. It is sad, but also strangely beautiful and full of hope. We will tell it gently, from a distance, like a story told by the fire, on a winter night.

[long-pause]

After the quest for the Grail, the Round Table is never the same again. Many knights have not come back. Old friendships are broken. Quarrels grow among the knights who remain. And in the end, there is a great battle at Camlann, between Arthur and his nephew Mordred. We will not look at it. We will stand far away, on a hill, and see only the mist rising over the valley, as the evening falls.

[pause]

When it is over, Arthur is badly wounded. Only one of his knights is still with him. Sir Bedivere, loyal to the very end. Arthur asks Bedivere to take his sword, Excalibur, to a lake nearby, and throw it into the water. Bedivere goes to the lake. But when he looks at the beautiful sword, with its jewels shining in the moonlight, he cannot bear to throw it away. So he hides it under a tree, and goes back to Arthur.

[pause]

{img:lake-moonlight-sword-spinning-through-air-hand-rising-water-silver}
Arthur asks him what he saw. Bedivere says that he saw only the waves and the wind. Arthur knows that he is not telling the truth. He sends him back. Again, Bedivere cannot do it. The third time, Arthur speaks to him sternly. And this time, Bedivere takes the sword, and throws it, as far as he can, out over the lake. And an arm, dressed in white silk, rises from the water, and catches the sword by the handle. It waves the sword three times in the moonlight. And then it sinks back into the lake, without a sound.

[pause]

Bedivere goes back to Arthur, and tells him what he saw. Arthur nods, and smiles. Then he asks Bedivere to carry him down to the water's edge. And there, in the mist, a little boat is waiting. A black barge, gliding silently over the water. In it, there are several ladies, dressed in black, with veils over their heads. Among them is a queen. And when they see Arthur, they weep softly.

[long-pause]

{img:black-barge-gliding-misty-lake-queens-carrying-king-to-avalon-twilight}
The ladies lift Arthur gently into the barge. One of them takes his head in her lap. Bedivere stands on the shore, and asks Arthur what will become of him, now that he is alone. Arthur tells him to be comforted. He says that he is going to the isle of Avalon, to be healed of his wound. And that if Bedivere never hears of him again, he should pray for him. Then the barge glides away across the water, into the mist, and is gone.

[pause]

Avalon is a magical island. In the oldest stories, it is called the Island of Apples. It is a beautiful place, where apple trees grow without being planted, and the fields give their harvest without being ploughed. It is ruled by a wise lady, called Morgan, who knows all the secrets of healing. There is no winter there, no sickness and no sorrow. It is hidden somewhere in the western sea, or in the mists of a lake, where ordinary people can never find it.

[pause]

{img:avalon-island-apple-orchard-blossom-mist-gentle-light-peaceful-sleep}
And Arthur? The stories say that he did not die. He sleeps in Avalon, healed of his wounds, resting in the apple orchards. Or, in other versions, he sleeps in a cave deep under a hill, with his knights sleeping around him, waiting. And one day, when Britain is in great need, he will wake up, and come back. On an old tomb, an old story says, there are words in Latin. Here lies Arthur, the once and future king.

[long-pause]

The once and future king. It is a beautiful idea. A king who was, and who will be again. A good time that is not gone forever, but only sleeping, and will return. Perhaps that is why the legend of Arthur has lasted so long. Because it ends not with goodbye, but with a promise. And all over Britain, in caves and under hills, there are places where people say Arthur and his knights are sleeping still, waiting for the day they are needed.

[long-pause]

{img:glastonbury-tor-rising-over-flooded-levels-mist-dawn-tower-on-top}
In the county of Somerset, not far from the hill fort of Cadbury, there is a strange, steep hill, rising out of the flat land. It is called Glastonbury Tor. On its top, there is a stone tower, all that remains of an old church. And at its foot, there is the town of Glastonbury, with the ruins of a great abbey. For hundreds of years, people have believed that Glastonbury was the real Avalon.

[long-pause]

Long ago, the land around Glastonbury was very different. It was low and marshy, with rivers, lakes and reed beds. In winter, the water rose and flooded everything, and the Tor became an island, surrounded by water and mist. People travelled by boat between the islands. So it was easy to imagine a magical island, rising out of the mist, where a wounded king was carried in a barge.

[pause]

Glastonbury was also one of the oldest Christian places in Britain. There was a very old church there, made of wood and wattle. And around it, a great abbey grew, with monks, books and treasures. Legends said that Joseph of Arimathea came here, long ago, carrying the Holy Grail. And that he planted his walking stick in the ground, and it grew into a thorn tree, which flowers every year at Christmas.

[pause]

{img:glastonbury-abbey-ruins-tall-arches-green-lawn-trees-peaceful-sunlight}
In the year 1184, there was a terrible fire at Glastonbury Abbey. The old church and many of the buildings burned down. The monks needed money to rebuild. And then, in 1191, something extraordinary happened. The monks announced that they had found the grave of King Arthur, in their graveyard.

[pause]

They said that they had dug deep into the ground, between two old stone pillars. About two metres down, they found a stone slab, and under it, a cross of lead, with words written on it in Latin. Here lies buried the famous King Arthur, in the isle of Avalon. And under that, even deeper, they found a huge coffin, made from a hollowed tree trunk. Inside were the bones of a very tall man. And beside him, the smaller bones of a woman, with a lock of golden hair.

[long-pause]

{img:monks-digging-graveyard-glastonbury-1191-lead-cross-found-torchlight}
Was it really Arthur? Most historians today think it probably was not. They think the monks may have arranged the discovery, to bring pilgrims and money to the abbey, so they could rebuild after the fire. And it worked. Pilgrims came from everywhere to see the grave of Arthur. But we cannot know for sure. Perhaps the monks found an old grave, and truly believed it was Arthur's.

[pause]

In 1278, King Edward the First and his queen came to Glastonbury. The bones were placed in a beautiful black marble tomb, in front of the high altar of the abbey church. The king and queen carried the bones themselves, wrapped in fine cloth. And for more than two hundred and fifty years, pilgrims came to pray at the tomb of King Arthur and Queen Guinevere.

[pause]

{img:glastonbury-abbey-site-of-arthurs-tomb-sign-grass-ruins-today-quiet}
In 1539, King Henry the Eighth closed all the monasteries in England. Glastonbury Abbey was closed, and its buildings were pulled down. The black marble tomb was destroyed, and the bones were lost. The lead cross was seen by a writer some years later, and drawn in a book. Then it too disappeared. Nobody knows where it is today.

[long-pause]

But the ruins of the abbey are still there. Tall broken walls and arches, standing on green lawns, among old trees. In the middle, where the high altar once stood, there is a small sign in the grass. It says that this is the site of King Arthur's tomb. Visitors come and stand there quietly. And above the town, the Tor still rises against the sky, and on misty mornings, it still looks like an island, floating in a sea of white.

[long-pause]

{img:breton-storyteller-harp-french-castle-hall-ladies-listening-12th-century}
In the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, the stories of Arthur travelled all over Europe. They were carried by storytellers, poets and singers, from castle to castle, from country to country. And in every country, people added their own ideas, their own knights and their own adventures. The legend grew and grew, like a great tree spreading its branches.

[long-pause]

Some of the first to carry the stories were the Bretons. Brittany is a part of the northwest of France, where many Britons had settled, long ago, around the time of Arthur. They spoke a language very similar to Welsh and Cornish, and they knew the old stories. Breton storytellers travelled to the castles of France, playing the harp and telling tales of Arthur. The French nobles loved them.

[pause]

In France, a poet called Chrétien de Troyes wrote five long poems about the knights of Arthur, around the year 1170 to 1190. He was the first to write about Lancelot, about Camelot and about the Grail. He lived at the court of a countess in Champagne, a lady called Marie, who loved stories of love and chivalry. Chrétien's poems were full of adventure, romance and beautiful descriptions. They were copied and read everywhere.

[pause]

{img:carved-stone-archway-italian-cathedral-knights-arthur-sculpture-modena}
The stories even travelled as far as Italy. In the town of Modena, there is a cathedral. And over one of its doors, there is a carved stone arch, made around the year 1120 or 1130. It shows a group of knights on horseback, riding to rescue a lady from a castle. Above the knights, there are names carved in the stone. One of them is Artus de Bretania. Arthur of Britain. It is one of the oldest pictures of Arthur in the world. And it was made before Geoffrey of Monmouth even wrote his book.

[pause]

In the south of Italy, in the town of Otranto, there is a cathedral with a huge mosaic on the floor, made in the year 1165. It shows the tree of life, animals, monsters and Bible stories. And among them, there is a little figure riding on a goat, with a crown on his head. Next to him, there is a name. Rex Arturus. King Arthur. Nobody is quite sure why he is riding a goat. But there he is, in a mosaic, at the other end of Europe from Britain.

[long-pause]

{img:german-knight-poet-writing-parzival-castle-window-forest-13th-century}
In Germany, a knight called Wolfram von Eschenbach wrote a great poem about Percival, around the year 1210. He called him Parzival. In his story, the Grail is not a cup, but a magical stone, which gives food and long life. It is guarded by knights in a castle in the mountains. Hundreds of years later, a German composer, Richard Wagner, turned this story into a famous opera.

[pause]

Stories of Arthur were also told in Norway, in Iceland, in Spain, in Portugal and in the Netherlands. In each country, they were translated and changed. Kings named their sons Arthur, and their daughters Guinevere or Isolde. Painters painted the knights on the walls of castles. Ladies embroidered them on tapestries. Children played at being Lancelot and Gawain in castle courtyards all over Europe.

[pause]

{img:castle-wall-painting-arthurian-knights-colourful-fresco-medieval-room}
In a castle in the north of Italy, called Runkelstein, there are wall paintings from around the year 1400, showing scenes from the stories of Tristan and Isolde, and of the Round Table. In a castle in Germany, there were paintings of the knights of Arthur on the walls of the great hall. Wherever there were knights and ladies, there were stories of Arthur.

[long-pause]

So the little hero of the Welsh bards, sung about by the fire in a hill fort in the west of Britain, had become the hero of all Europe. If Nest, the blacksmith's daughter of Cadbury, could have seen this, she would have been amazed. The stories she heard from the old bard, by the fire in the great hall, had travelled further than she could ever have imagined. Further than the ships that came to Tintagel, from the far side of the sea.

[long-pause]

{img:thomas-malory-writing-prison-cell-window-light-manuscript-1460s}
Around the year 1470, an English knight called Sir Thomas Malory finished a very long book. He had gathered together all the stories of Arthur that he could find, in French and in English. And he retold them in English prose, in one great book, from the birth of Arthur to his passing to Avalon. It is called Le Morte d'Arthur, which means the Death of Arthur.

[long-pause]

Malory was not a quiet scholar. He had a very adventurous and troubled life. He was a soldier, and a member of parliament. But he was also arrested many times, and spent many years in prison. Historians are not sure if he was guilty of the crimes he was accused of. It was a time of civil war in England, called the Wars of the Roses, and many people were imprisoned for being on the wrong side. Malory probably wrote much of his book in prison.

[pause]

At the end of his book, Malory asked his readers to pray for him, that God might send him good deliverance, which probably meant freedom from prison. He wrote that he was a knight prisoner. And he passed away in 1471, perhaps still in prison, before his book was printed. He never knew how famous it would become.

[pause]

{img:caxton-printing-press-westminster-1485-workers-printed-pages-book}
In 1485, fourteen years after Malory's death, his book was printed by William Caxton. Caxton was the first person to print books in England. He had set up his printing press near Westminster Abbey, in London, a few years before. He printed many books. But the Morte d'Arthur became one of his most famous. In his preface, Caxton wrote that the book was full of noble chivalry, courtesy, kindness and friendship, and that readers should do after the good and leave the evil.

[pause]

Printing changed everything for the stories of Arthur. Before, every book had to be copied by hand, and only rich people could have one. Now, hundreds of copies could be made quickly. More and more people could read the stories. Malory's book was printed again and again, for the next hundred and fifty years. And it became the most important version of the legend in English.

[long-pause]

{img:reader-by-fireplace-reading-morte-darthur-old-book-cosy-evening}
Malory's book is the source of most of the stories that English-speaking people know today. The sword in the stone. The Lady of the Lake. The Round Table and the Siege Perilous. Lancelot and Guinevere. The quest for the Grail. Bedivere and Excalibur. The barge to Avalon. The once and future king. All of them are in Malory, told in beautiful, simple, old English.

[pause]

Malory's language is gentle and musical. When Lancelot hears that Arthur is gone, an old knight says of him that he was the courteoust knight that ever bare shield, and the truest friend to his lover that ever bestrode horse, and the kindest man that ever struck with sword. It is one of the most beautiful passages in English literature. People still read it aloud today.

[pause]

{img:old-library-shelf-antique-books-malory-volume-dust-light-beam}
For a long time, only one copy of Caxton's printed book was known, in an American library. Then, in 1934, a schoolteacher at Winchester College found an old manuscript in the library of the school. It was Malory's own text, copied by hand, before Caxton printed it. It was a sensational discovery. It showed how Malory had really written his book, before Caxton changed some parts of it.

[long-pause]

And Winchester, the same city where the great round table hangs on the wall of the castle. So Winchester has both the round table of King Edward and King Henry, and the lost manuscript of Sir Thomas Malory. It is a city full of Arthur. And Malory himself wrote, in his book, that Camelot was Winchester. Perhaps they were right. Perhaps not. But it is a lovely thought.

[long-pause]

{img:victorian-painting-style-lady-of-shalott-boat-river-willows-later}
After Malory, the stories of Arthur were less popular for a while. In the sixteen and seventeen hundreds, people preferred stories of ancient Greece and Rome. Some writers said that the old tales of knights and magic were silly and old-fashioned. But Malory's book was never completely forgotten. And in the eighteen hundreds, the legend of Arthur came back, more popular than ever.

[long-pause]

The great English poet Alfred Tennyson wrote a series of long poems about Arthur, called the Idylls of the King. They were published between 1859 and 1885, and were hugely popular. Queen Victoria loved them. Tennyson also wrote a famous poem called The Lady of Shalott, about a lady who lives in a tower on an island, near Camelot, weaving a magic tapestry. She can only see the world in a mirror. And when she sees Lancelot riding by, she looks out of the window, and floats down the river to Camelot in a little boat.

[pause]

At the same time, a group of English painters, called the Pre-Raphaelites, painted beautiful pictures of the legend. Knights and ladies in rich colours, in gardens and forests and on rivers. The Lady of Shalott in her boat. Lancelot and Guinevere. Galahad and the Grail. Their paintings are still in museums today, and are among the most loved paintings in Britain. People bought prints of them for their homes, and dreamed of Camelot.

[pause]

{img:victorian-family-reading-arthur-stories-children-fireside-1880s-later}
In America, the writer Mark Twain wrote a funny book in 1889, called A Connecticut Yankee in King Arthur's Court. It is about an American man who wakes up, after a blow on the head, in the time of King Arthur. He uses his modern knowledge to amaze the people of Camelot. It is a comic story, but it also has serious thoughts about kings, knights and ordinary people.

[pause]

In the twentieth century, the stories of Arthur became more popular than ever. The English writer T. H. White wrote a series of books, called The Once and Future King. The first one, The Sword in the Stone, tells about Arthur's childhood, when he was a boy called Wart, and Merlin taught him by turning him into a fish, a hawk, an ant and a badger. It is a warm, funny and wise book, loved by children and adults.

[long-pause]

{img:theatre-stage-camelot-musical-castle-set-actors-costumes-later}
The legend appeared in films, plays, musicals and cartoons. There was a famous musical called Camelot, in 1960. There was a cartoon film of The Sword in the Stone, in 1963. There were serious films, funny films, and very silly films. There were television series, comic books and computer games. Every generation told the story of Arthur in its own way, for its own time.

[pause]

The name Camelot even became a word for a golden time. When the young American president John F. Kennedy was in office, from 1961 to 1963, his wife Jacqueline later said that those years were like Camelot. A brief, shining moment. The phrase came from the musical. And after that, people often called the Kennedy years Camelot.

[pause]

{img:young-readers-library-children-reading-knights-books-sunny-window-later}
Historians and archaeologists also kept searching for the real Arthur. They dug at Cadbury, at Tintagel and at Glastonbury. They studied the old Welsh poems and the old Latin books. Some believed that Arthur was a real British leader around the year 500. Others thought he was a legend from the start. The debate goes on today. And perhaps it will never be settled.

[long-pause]

But perhaps that does not matter very much. Because whether Arthur was real or not, his story is real. It has been told for fifteen hundred years. It has inspired poets and painters, kings and children. It has taught people about courage, kindness, friendship and loyalty. And it has given everyone a dream of a better kingdom, a Round Table where everyone is equal, and a king who will come again when he is needed.

[long-pause]

{img:tintagel-today-footbridge-headland-visitors-sea-cliffs-sunny-day}
Today, people from all over the world come to visit the places of the Arthur legend. In Cornwall, Somerset, Wales and Scotland, there are hills, lakes, caves and stones that are connected to his name. Some of them have old stories. Some of them have new ones. And all of them have a little magic.

[long-pause]

At Tintagel, in Cornwall, visitors walk across a new footbridge, opened in 2019, high above the sea, from the mainland to the headland. They walk among the ruins of the old castle and the even older buildings from around the year 500. They go down to the beach, to see Merlin's Cave. And on the cliff top, they find a tall bronze statue, of a king with a crown and a sword. It is called Gallos, which means power in Cornish. It is not exactly Arthur. But everyone calls it King Arthur.

[pause]

The statue is made in a special way. Parts of it are cut away, so that you can see through it, to the sea and the sky behind. It looks as if the king is half there and half not there. A ghost, or a memory, or a legend. On a misty day, it seems to fade into the air. Visitors stand beside it and look out over the sea, just as the lords of Tintagel did fifteen hundred years ago.

[pause]

{img:gallos-statue-tintagel-bronze-king-sword-see-through-sea-behind}
At Cadbury Castle, in Somerset, you can walk up the steep path to the top of the hill fort, through the old banks and ditches, now covered with grass and trees. On the top, there is a wide, flat field, where cows graze. Nothing remains of the great hall, except in the ground. But the view is wonderful. And on a clear day, far away to the northwest, you can see the shape of Glastonbury Tor, rising out of the flat land.

[pause]

At Glastonbury, visitors climb the Tor, on a winding path, to the old tower at the top. From there, the whole of Somerset is spread out below. In the town, they visit the ruins of the abbey, and the site of Arthur's tomb. And they visit the Chalice Well, a spring at the foot of the Tor, whose water is tinted red by iron. Some say that the Grail is hidden there. It is a peaceful garden, with flowers, trees and the sound of trickling water.

[long-pause]

{img:snowdonia-lake-mountains-mist-still-water-legend-of-excalibur-today}
In Wales, there are many lakes and mountains connected to Arthur. In the mountains of Snowdonia, there is a lake called Llyn Llydaw, where some say Bedivere threw Excalibur into the water. There is a pass in the mountains called Bwlch y Saethau, the pass of the arrows, where some say Arthur fell. And there is a cave, high in the mountains, where Arthur's knights are said to be sleeping, waiting for his return.

[pause]

In Scotland, in the middle of Edinburgh, there is a great hill, called Arthur's Seat. It is an old volcano, rising high above the city. Nobody knows exactly why it has that name. But people walk up to the top every day, to look out over the city and the sea. And at sunrise on the first of May, some people climb it to wash their faces in the dew, for good luck and beauty.

[pause]

{img:arthurs-seat-edinburgh-hill-sunrise-walkers-city-below-today}
In Winchester, the great round table still hangs on the wall of the castle hall, with King Arthur at the top, and the names of his knights around the edge. And in Winchester College, there is still the library where the lost manuscript of Malory was found. Visitors can see a copy of it. And in the cathedral nearby, there is a statue of a knight, kneeling in prayer.

[long-pause]

And all over the world, every day, someone is reading the story of Arthur for the first time. A child, curled up in bed with a book. A student in a library. A grandmother reading to her grandchildren. The sword in the stone. The Lady of the Lake. The Round Table. The Grail. The barge to Avalon. The story goes on, and on, and it is new again for every reader.

[long-pause]

{img:hill-fort-evening-sunset-mist-valleys-glastonbury-tor-distant-island}
So we leave the world of King Arthur tonight, around the year 500, as the evening falls. On the hill fort of Cadbury, the sun is setting over the flat lands of Somerset. The mist is rising from the marshes, filling the valleys with white. And far away, Glastonbury Tor is rising out of the mist, like an island in a pale sea. Like Avalon.

[long-pause]

In the great hall on the hill, the fire is lit. The lord and his people are gathering for the evening meal. Smoke rises through the roof into the still evening air. The cattle are in their pens. The gates are closed. And on the bank of the hill fort, a girl of ten is sitting with her knees pulled up, looking at the Tor in the distance, as the light fades.

[pause]

{img:old-bard-harp-firelight-hall-evening-girl-listening-stories-begin}
Inside the hall, the old bard is tuning his harp. He plucks a few soft notes, and the sound drifts out through the open door, into the evening. Soon, he will begin to sing. About the old heroes, about the brave and the generous, about the great leader who brought peace to the land. And the girl will come in, and sit by the fire, and listen, and remember every word.

[long-pause]

She does not know that the story she will hear tonight will travel further than any ship. That it will be written down by monks, and copied by scholars, and sung by poets in France, and carved in stone in Italy, and printed in London, and read by children all over the world, for fifteen hundred years. She only knows that it is a good story, and that she loves it.

[pause]

{img:moonlit-lake-mist-sword-arm-gentle-silver-water-calm-night}
