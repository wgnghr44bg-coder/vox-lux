{img:domremy-1428-small-village-meuse-river-church-tower-autumn-fields-mist}
Tonight, we travel back almost six hundred years, to the autumn of the year 1428. We go to the east of France, to a small village on the banks of a quiet river, called the Meuse. The village is called Domrémy. It is a tiny place, of perhaps thirty or forty families. Stone houses, with steep roofs, along a single street. A little church, with a square tower. Gardens, and orchards, and meadows, running down to the river. And all around, the gentle green hills, and the dark oak woods.

[pause]

It is a peaceful place, in many ways. The people are farmers, and shepherds. They grow wheat, and oats, and hemp. They keep cows, and sheep, and pigs, and chickens. They work hard, from dawn until dusk, through the seasons, as their parents, and grandparents, did before them. They go to church on Sundays, and on the many holy days. And they know everyone in the village, and everyone in the next village, and the one after that.

[long-pause]

But France, in 1428, is not a peaceful country. It has been at war for almost a hundred years. A long, slow, terrible war, against the English, who claim to be the rightful kings of France. English armies, and their allies, the Burgundians, have taken half of the country. Paris, the capital, is in their hands. And now, this very autumn, an English army has surrounded the great city of Orléans, on the river Loire. If Orléans falls, the rest of France may fall too.

[pause]

{img:village-girl-1428-red-dress-spinning-wool-doorway-cottage-autumn-light}
In Domrémy, there is a girl of sixteen. Her name is Jeanne. Her father is a farmer, and one of the leading men of the village. She is strong, and healthy, and good at spinning, and sewing, and looking after the animals. She is kind, and cheerful, and very religious. She goes to church more often than anyone else in the village. The other young people tease her about it, a little. But they like her.

[pause]

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

{img:young-peasant-girl-kneeling-in-garden-light-through-trees-church-bells}
Jeanne has a secret. For about three years, since she was thirteen, she has been hearing voices. She believes they are the voices of saints, sent by God. At first, they told her to be good, and to go to church. But now, they are telling her something much bigger. Something impossible. They are telling her that she must leave her village, and go to the king, and help him to save France. And she has begun to believe that she must obey.

[pause]

In the next few months, this girl from a tiny village will do something that nobody has ever done before. She will travel across France, through enemy country, to meet the uncrowned king. She will ride at the head of his army, in armour, with a white banner. She will help to lift the siege of Orléans. And she will stand beside the king, in the great cathedral of Reims, when he is crowned. Today, we know her as Joan of Arc.

[pause]

{img:white-banner-fleur-de-lis-golden-light-cathedral-distance-hopeful}
This will be a gentle telling of her story. We will spend much of our time in her village, in the autumn, at harvest time, and in the quiet places she knew. And we will follow her journey, from a distance, without dwelling on the battles, or the sad things. We will see the France of her time. The villages, and the roads, and the castles, and the cities. And the ordinary people, who prayed, and hoped, and waited, for peace.

[pause]

So let us go to Domrémy, in the autumn of 1428. Let us walk along the village street, in the early morning, as the mist rises from the river, and the church bell rings. And let us meet Jeanne, and her family, as they begin another day, in the fields.

[long-pause]

{img:map-style-medieval-france-1428-divided-english-burgundian-french-lands}
The war that has troubled France for so long is called, today, the Hundred Years' War. It actually lasted even longer than a hundred years, from 1337 to 1453. But it was not a hundred years of constant fighting. There were long periods of peace, or truce, and then fighting again. And it was not really a war between two countries, as we think of them today. It was a war between two royal families, both of whom claimed the throne of France.

[pause]

The kings of England, in those days, were not quite English, as we would think of it. They were descended from French nobles, who had conquered England, three hundred years before. They spoke French, at their court. They owned great lands in France. And through their family, they believed that they had a right to be kings of France too. The French did not agree. And so the war began.

[long-pause]

{img:french-countryside-medieval-burned-farm-abandoned-fields-ruins-quiet}
For the ordinary people of France, the war was a long misery. Armies marched back and forth across the country, for generations. Not just the English, and the French, but bands of soldiers, without pay, who robbed, and burned, and stole, wherever they went. Villages were burned. Fields were left empty. Many people fled to the towns, behind the walls. In some parts of France, whole regions were almost deserted.

[pause]

In 1415, the English king, Henry the Fifth, won a great victory, at a place called Agincourt. Then he conquered Normandy, and much of the north of France. The duke of Burgundy, a powerful French prince, became his ally. And in 1420, the mad French king, Charles the Sixth, signed a treaty. It said that his own son, Charles, would never be king. Instead, King Henry of England would become king of France, after Charles the Sixth passed away.

[pause]

{img:young-dauphin-charles-1428-castle-chinon-uncertain-face-plain-robes}
But in 1422, both King Henry, and King Charles, passed away, within a few weeks of each other. The new English king was Henry's son, Henry the Sixth, a baby, only a few months old. The English said that the baby was now king of England, and king of France. But many of the French said no. They said that the rightful king was Charles, the son of the old French king. They called him the Dauphin, which is the title of the heir to the French throne.

[pause]

The Dauphin Charles was nineteen, in 1422. He was not a strong, or brave, or impressive young man. He was thin, and nervous, and uncertain. He had never been crowned. The kings of France were always crowned in the great cathedral of Reims. But Reims was deep in enemy territory, in the hands of the English, and the Burgundians. So Charles could not go there. And without a coronation, many people did not believe he was a real king.

[long-pause]

So, by 1428, France was divided in two. In the north, the English, and the Burgundians. In the south, the Dauphin, and his supporters. In between, along the river Loire, the great city of Orléans, which was still loyal to the Dauphin. It was the key to the south. If the English took it, they could cross the Loire, and march into the heart of the Dauphin's lands.

[pause]

{img:orleans-1428-walled-city-loire-bridge-towers-english-forts-around}
In October 1428, the English came to Orléans, with an army, and surrounded it. They built forts, around the city, and blocked the roads, and the river. The people of Orléans shut their gates, and prepared to defend themselves. And the whole of France waited, to see what would happen.

[pause]

Domrémy was a long way from Orléans. Hundreds of kilometres, to the east. But the news travelled, slowly, from village to village, along the roads, with merchants, and pilgrims, and soldiers. And in Domrémy, too, people talked about Orléans, and the English, and the Dauphin. And Jeanne listened. And she prayed.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-farmhouse-domremy-stone-house-family-outside-father-mother-sons-girl}
Jeanne's father is Jacques d'Arc. He is a farmer, about fifty years old. He owns about twenty hectares of land, fields, and meadows, and woods, around the village. That is quite a lot, for a peasant. He is not rich. But he is comfortable, and respected. He is one of the leaders of the village, and speaks for the villagers, when there is business with the lord, or the officials. He is a serious, careful, hard-working man.

[pause]

Jeanne's mother is Isabelle. People call her Isabelle Romée, perhaps because she once made a pilgrimage to Rome, or to another holy place. She is a pious, gentle woman, who taught Jeanne her prayers. The Our Father, the Hail Mary, and the Creed. Jeanne said, later, that everything she knew about her faith, she learned from her mother. She could not read, or write. Neither could her mother. But she knew her prayers.

[long-pause]

{img:mother-teaching-daughter-prayers-by-hearth-fire-medieval-kitchen-evening}
Jeanne has three brothers. Jacquemin, the eldest, who is married, and lives in a nearby village. Jean, and Pierre, who are a little older than her, and still live at home, and work on the farm with their father. Pierre is cheerful, and brave, and close to Jeanne. He will later go with her, on her great journey, and fight beside her. There was also a sister, Catherine, who married, and passed away young. Jeanne loved her very much.

[pause]

The family's house stands next to the church, in the middle of the village. It is a solid stone house, with a steep roof, and thick walls, and small windows. Downstairs, there is a big room, with a great fireplace, where the family cooks, and eats, and sits in the evenings. There are a few smaller rooms, for sleeping. And behind the house, there is a garden, with vegetables, and herbs, and fruit trees, running down toward the church.

[pause]

{img:girl-herding-cows-meadow-by-river-meuse-willows-morning-mist}
Jeanne's days are full of work. She helps her mother in the house. She spins wool, and flax, on her spindle, and her spinning wheel. She is very good at it. She said, later, that she was not afraid of any woman in Rouen, when it came to spinning, and sewing. She cooks, and cleans, and fetches water from the well. And she helps on the farm. She takes the cows, and the sheep, to the meadows, by the river. She helps with the harvest. She works in the garden.

[pause]

When it is Jacques d'Arc's turn, in the village, to watch over the animals, the family's cows, and the neighbours' cows, are taken out together, to graze in the meadows. Then Jeanne sometimes goes too, and sits by the river, watching the animals, and spinning, and praying. It is a quiet, peaceful job. She likes it. She likes the river, and the meadows, and the silence, and the sound of the bells, drifting across the fields.

[long-pause]

Jeanne's best friend is a girl called Hauviette. She lives next door, and is a few years younger. They have played together since they were small. They have run in the meadows, and picked flowers, and danced around the old tree in the woods, and slept in each other's houses. Hauviette loves Jeanne very much. Many years later, when she was an old woman, Hauviette said that Jeanne was good, and simple, and gentle, and that she often went to church, and gave to the poor.

[pause]

{img:two-village-girls-friends-walking-arm-in-arm-lane-flowers-laughing}
Jeanne gives to the poor, whenever she can. When a poor traveller comes to the village, she asks her father to let him sleep in the house, by the fire, and she sleeps in the loft herself. She looks after the sick, in the village, and sits with them, and brings them food. When she has a little money, she gives it to the church, for masses, or to the poor. People say that she has a good heart.

[pause]

She also loves the bells. In the evenings, when the church bell rings for the end of the day, Jeanne kneels down, wherever she is, in the field, or the garden, and says a prayer. Sometimes, the bell ringer forgets to ring, or rings too late. Then Jeanne scolds him, gently, and promises him some little cakes, if he remembers to ring on time. And he does.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-autumn-domremy-ploughing-oxen-sowing-winter-wheat-fields-mist}
In the autumn of 1428, there is much to do on the farm. The summer harvest of wheat, and oats, and barley, is over, and the grain has been threshed, on the barn floor, with flails, and stored. Now, it is time to plough the fields again, and sow the winter wheat. Jacques d'Arc and his sons plough with oxen, two great patient beasts, pulling a heavy wooden plough, through the brown earth, back and forth, all day long.

[pause]

Behind them walks the sower. A man with a bag of seed, slung over his shoulder. He takes a handful of seed, and throws it, in a wide arc, across the ploughed earth. Then another handful, and another, step by step, along the furrows. The crows follow, hoping to steal some of the seed. The children run after them, and chase them away, shouting, and waving their arms.

[long-pause]

{img:villagers-picking-apples-orchard-ladders-baskets-autumn-sunlight-medieval}
In the orchards, it is time to pick the fruit. Apples, and pears, and plums, and quinces. The whole family helps, with ladders, and baskets. The best apples are stored in the loft, on beds of straw, for the winter. The others are pressed, in the village press, to make cider. Jeanne loves the apple harvest. She climbs the ladders, and fills the baskets, and eats an apple, now and then, when nobody is looking.

[pause]

In the woods, there are nuts, to be gathered. Walnuts, and hazelnuts, and beechnuts, and acorns. The pigs of the village are taken into the oak woods, in the autumn, to eat the acorns. A boy goes with them, to watch them, and to knock down the acorns from the trees, with a long stick. The pigs grow fat, on the acorns. And in the winter, some of them will be killed, and salted, for meat, to last until the spring.

[pause]

{img:pigs-in-oak-forest-autumn-swineherd-boy-stick-acorns-golden-leaves}
There are grapes, too, in a few small vineyards, on the sunny slopes above the river. They are not very good grapes. The wine of Lorraine is thin, and sour. But it is wine. And in October, the families pick the grapes, and tread them, in big wooden tubs, with their bare feet, and press them, and make their wine. The children love treading the grapes. Their legs turn purple, up to the knees.

[pause]

The hemp is harvested too. Hemp is a tall plant, whose stems are used to make rope, and coarse cloth. The stems are cut, and tied in bundles, and soaked in the river, for weeks, until they are soft. Then they are dried, and beaten, to separate the fibres. Then the women spin the fibres into thread, in the long winter evenings, by the fire. Jeanne spins a great deal of hemp, and flax, and wool, every winter.

[long-pause]

{img:family-eating-together-long-table-medieval-farmhouse-bread-soup-candle}
In the evenings, when the work is done, the family sits together, by the great fireplace, in the big room of the house. They eat their supper. Soup, with bread, and vegetables, and a little bacon, or cheese. And sometimes, fish from the river. They talk about the day. About the fields, and the animals, and the weather. And about the news from outside. The war, and the English, and the soldiers, and Orléans.

[pause]

After supper, the women spin, and the men mend tools, and harness, by the light of the fire. Jacques d'Arc tells stories, sometimes, of the old days, and of the wars, and the troubles. Isabelle sings, softly, an old song, or a hymn. And Jeanne spins, and listens, and looks into the fire, and thinks. She is very quiet, this autumn. Her mother notices. She wonders what her daughter is thinking about.

[pause]

At night, the family goes to bed early. Candles are expensive, and the work begins again at dawn. The fire is covered with ashes, to keep it alive until the morning. The house grows dark, and quiet. Outside, the wind blows through the bare trees, and the river runs softly past the village, in the dark. And the stars shine over the sleeping fields.

[long-pause]

{img:great-old-beech-tree-ladies-tree-hillside-spring-water-children-garlands}
Not far from Domrémy, on the hillside above the village, at the edge of an oak wood, there is a very old tree. A great beech tree, enormous, and ancient, with a wide spreading crown of branches, and roots like great twisted snakes, running across the ground. The people of the village call it the Ladies' Tree. Or the Fairies' Tree. Nobody knows how old it is. The oldest people in the village say that it was already old, when their grandparents were children.

[pause]

Near the tree, there is a spring. A little fountain of clear, cold water, bubbling up from the ground, among the rocks. People say that the water is good for fevers. When someone in the village is ill, their family comes to the spring, and fills a jug with water, and takes it home, for them to drink. And many of them get better. Whether it is the water, or the prayers, nobody knows.

[long-pause]

{img:village-children-dancing-around-old-tree-spring-garlands-bread-laughing}
The tree is the centre of an old custom. Every year, in the spring, on a special Sunday, the young people of the village go up to the tree, to celebrate. They make garlands of flowers, and hang them on the branches. They bring bread, and little cakes, and eat them, under the tree. They drink water from the spring. And they dance, and sing, around the great trunk, holding hands, in a big circle. It is one of the happiest days of the year.

[pause]

The old people say that long ago, fairies lived at the tree. Beautiful, mysterious ladies, who came out on summer nights, and danced under the branches. Jeanne's godmother says that she once saw them, when she was young. But the priest does not approve of such stories. He says that there are no fairies. He goes up to the tree, every year, on a special day, and reads the Gospel there, to bless it, and to drive away any bad spirits.

[pause]

{img:girl-hanging-flower-garland-on-low-branch-ancient-beech-dappled-light}
Jeanne has gone to the tree, with the other children, every spring, since she was small. She has danced around it, and made garlands, and eaten cakes, and drunk the water from the spring. But she says that she never saw any fairies. She has also made garlands, for the statue of the Virgin Mary, in the little chapel nearby, at Bermont, in the woods. She loves that chapel. She goes there often, on Saturdays, to pray, and to light a candle.

[pause]

Much later, in a very different place, men would ask Jeanne many questions about the Ladies' Tree. They would try to make her say that she had met the fairies there, and that her voices came from them, and not from God. But Jeanne answered them simply, and honestly. She said that she had danced there, as a child, as all the children did. But that she had never seen fairies, and that her voices came from God.

[long-pause]

In the autumn of 1428, the great tree is turning golden. Its leaves are yellow, and orange, and brown, and they fall slowly, in the wind, and cover the ground, like a carpet. The beechnuts fall too, and the children gather them, in their pockets. The spring still bubbles, cold, and clear, among the fallen leaves. And the oak woods behind it are dark, and quiet, and smell of damp earth, and mushrooms.

[pause]

{img:autumn-beech-tree-golden-leaves-falling-girl-sitting-at-roots-praying-quiet}
Jeanne goes up to the tree, one afternoon, alone. She sits at the foot of the great trunk, among the roots, and the fallen leaves, and looks down at the village, and the river, and the valley. She can see her house, beside the church. And the smoke rising from the chimneys. And the cows in the meadows, by the river. And the road, winding away, to the north, toward Vaucouleurs, and beyond.

[pause]

She sits there for a long time, thinking, and praying. The wind moves the golden branches above her. The leaves fall around her, softly. The spring murmurs, beside her. And she listens. For her voices. And for her courage. Because she knows, now, that she must soon leave this place. The tree, the spring, the meadows, the river, her family. And she does not know if she will ever come back.

[long-pause]

{img:girl-thirteen-in-fathers-garden-summer-noon-bright-light-from-church-side}
Jeanne first heard her voices when she was about thirteen years old. It was a summer day, at noon, in her father's garden, beside the church. She heard a voice, coming from the right, from the direction of the church. And she saw a great light, at the same time. She was very frightened. She did not know what it was. But the voice was kind, and gentle. It told her to be good, and to go to church often.

[pause]

She heard the voice again, many times, after that. At first, she did not know who it was. Then she came to believe that it was the voice of Saint Michael, the archangel, the great warrior angel, who leads the armies of heaven. Later, she heard two other voices too. The voices of Saint Catherine, and Saint Margaret. Two saints, who were young women, long ago, and who were famous for their courage, and their faith.

[long-pause]

{img:painted-church-statues-saint-michael-saint-catherine-saint-margaret-candles}
These saints were very well known, in Jeanne's time. There were statues of them, and paintings, in many churches. Saint Michael with his sword, and his wings, and his armour. Saint Catherine with her wheel, and her crown. Saint Margaret with a little dragon, at her feet. There was a statue of Saint Margaret in the church at Domrémy. Jeanne had looked at it, every Sunday, since she was a child.

[pause]

Jeanne said that she heard the voices two or three times a week. Sometimes more. Often, when the church bells were ringing. Or when she was alone, in the fields, or the woods, or the garden. She said that she saw them, sometimes, too, and that they were beautiful, and that they smelled sweet. She said that when they left her, she cried, and wished that they had taken her with them.

[pause]

{img:young-woman-praying-alone-small-chapel-bermont-candle-woods-window}
Nobody knows exactly what Jeanne's voices were. People have argued about it, for six hundred years. Some people believe that they were truly the voices of saints, sent by God. Others think that they came from Jeanne's own mind, from her deep faith, and her imagination, and her longing to help her country. We cannot know. But we do know that Jeanne believed in them, completely, with all her heart. And that they gave her extraordinary courage.

[pause]

For a long time, Jeanne told nobody about her voices. Not her mother, or her father, or her brothers, or Hauviette, or the priest. She kept them secret, in her heart. She was afraid that people would not believe her. Or that her father would stop her. She made a promise, to her voices, that she would stay pure, and good, and serve God, all her life, as long as it pleased Him.

[long-pause]

As she grew older, the voices began to tell her about France. About the great sadness of the kingdom, and the war, and the English. And they told her that she must go to France. In those days, the people of Domrémy said they were going to France when they went west, toward the lands of the king, because their own little region, on the border, felt a little separate. The voices told her that she must go to the Dauphin, and help him, and lead him to Reims, to be crowned.

[pause]

{img:girl-looking-at-distant-road-from-hill-dusk-thoughtful-courage-wind}
Jeanne was very frightened. She said to the voices that she was only a poor girl, who did not know how to ride a horse, or how to fight in a war. But the voices said that God would help her. And that she must go. Again, and again, they told her. Go to France. Go to the Dauphin. Go to Vaucouleurs, to the captain there, Robert de Baudricourt, and he will give you men, to take you to the Dauphin.

[pause]

In the autumn of 1428, when the news came that Orléans was under siege, the voices became more urgent. They told her that she must go, soon. That she must help to save Orléans. And Jeanne, who was sixteen, and had never been further than the next town, began to make her plans. She decided that she would go to Vaucouleurs again, as she had once before. And this time, she would find a way, somehow, to reach the Dauphin.

[long-pause]

{img:vaucouleurs-castle-1428-hilltop-fortress-meuse-valley-small-town-walls}
In fact, Jeanne had already tried once. In May 1428, before the siege of Orléans, she had gone to Vaucouleurs, a small fortified town, about twenty kilometres north of Domrémy, along the river. It was the nearest town that was still loyal to the Dauphin, in that part of France. Its captain was a knight called Robert de Baudricourt. A tough, practical soldier, who held the town for the Dauphin, surrounded by enemies.

[pause]

Jeanne went to Vaucouleurs with her uncle, Durand Laxart, who was married to her mother's cousin. She told her father that she was going to help her cousin, who was expecting a baby. Which was true. But when she got to Vaucouleurs, she went to the castle, and asked to see the captain. And she told him that God had sent her, to go to the Dauphin, and help him.

[long-pause]

{img:gruff-knight-captain-castle-hall-laughing-at-peasant-girl-red-dress-uncle}
Robert de Baudricourt laughed at her. A peasant girl, of sixteen, in a red dress, saying that God had sent her to save France? He told her uncle to take her home to her father, and to box her ears. So Jeanne went home, to Domrémy, disappointed. But she was not discouraged. She knew that she would go back. Her voices had told her that it would take three tries.

[pause]

A few weeks later, in July 1428, trouble came to Domrémy. A band of Burgundian soldiers was riding through the region, burning, and stealing. The people of Domrémy were warned that they were coming. So the whole village packed up their things, and gathered their animals, and fled, together, to the walled town of Neufchâteau, a few kilometres away, where they would be safe, behind the walls.

[pause]

{img:villagers-fleeing-with-cattle-carts-road-to-walled-town-summer-dust}
It was a strange time for Jeanne. The whole family stayed in Neufchâteau, for about two weeks, in an inn, kept by a kind woman, called La Rousse. Jeanne helped at the inn, with the work, and looked after the family's animals, in the fields outside the town. She went to church, every day. And she prayed for her village, and her home.

[pause]

When it was safe, the villagers went home. And they found that the soldiers had been there. The church had been set on fire, and damaged. Some of the houses had been burned. The fields had been trampled. Some of the animals that had been left behind had been taken. It was a sad homecoming. But the people set to work, at once, to repair the damage, and to bring in the harvest. They had been through this kind of thing before.

[long-pause]

For Jeanne, the burned church was a terrible sight. She had loved that church all her life. She had been baptised there. She had prayed there, every day. Now its roof was blackened, and its walls were scorched. She knelt in the ruins, and prayed. And she felt, more strongly than ever, that the war must end. That France must have peace. And that she must do something to help.

[pause]

{img:village-church-repaired-roof-villagers-working-together-autumn-carpenters}
The villagers repaired the church, as best they could, through the late summer, and the autumn. The men cut new timbers, for the roof. The women cleaned the inside, and scrubbed away the soot. The priest blessed it again. And by the autumn, it was being used again, for mass, on Sundays. It was not as beautiful as before. But it was standing.

[pause]

Jeanne's father, at about this time, had a bad dream. He dreamed that his daughter went away, with soldiers. He was very upset. He told his sons, very sternly, that they must never let such a thing happen. It showed how much he feared for her. And Jeanne knew that she could not tell him what she planned to do.

[long-pause]

{img:orleans-1428-city-walls-loire-river-bridge-tourelles-fort-boats-autumn}
While Jeanne was in Domrémy, bringing in the harvest, and praying at the old tree, the siege of Orléans had begun. In October 1428, an English army arrived at the city, and began to surround it. Orléans is a great city, on the north bank of the river Loire, one of the biggest rivers in France. In 1428, it had about thirty thousand people. It was rich, and proud, and well defended, with strong walls, and towers, and gates.

[pause]

A long stone bridge crossed the river, from the city to the south bank. At the southern end of the bridge, there was a fortress, with two towers, called Les Tourelles. The English attacked it first, and captured it, after a few days. So now, the English held the southern end of the bridge. And the people of Orléans broke down part of the bridge, so that the English could not cross it, into the city.

[long-pause]

{img:english-wooden-forts-bastilles-around-medieval-city-winter-smoke-camps}
Then the English built a ring of small wooden forts, called bastilles, around the city, on both sides of the river. They did not have enough soldiers to surround the city completely. There were gaps between the forts, through which people could sometimes come and go, carefully, with supplies. But the English hoped that, slowly, over the winter, the city would run out of food, and be forced to surrender.

[pause]

Life inside the city was difficult, but it went on. The people of Orléans were brave, and determined. The men took turns to guard the walls, day and night. The women carried stones, and water, and food, to the defenders. The children helped too. The bakers baked bread. The churches held services, and processions, and prayed for help. And the great cathedral bells rang out, over the city, every day, as if to say, we are still here.

[pause]

{img:people-of-orleans-on-city-walls-watching-river-candles-church-procession}
In charge of the defence of Orléans was a brave young nobleman, called Jean, the Bastard of Orléans. In those days, the word bastard simply meant a son born outside of marriage, and it was not an insult. He was the half-brother of the Duke of Orléans, who was a prisoner in England. He was clever, and cheerful, and a good soldier. Later, he would become a great friend of Jeanne's, and a hero of France. People today know him by his later title, Dunois.

[pause]

The winter of 1428 was long, and hard, in Orléans. Food became scarce, and expensive. The English attacked the walls, from time to time, and the French made raids against the English forts. The cannons on both sides fired, now and then, across the river, and over the walls. People said that a great English commander had been killed, early in the siege, by a cannonball fired from the city. But still the siege went on.

[long-pause]

In February 1429, the French tried to stop an English supply convoy, carrying food to the soldiers around Orléans. The convoy was carrying barrels of herrings, because it was Lent, when people did not eat meat. The French attacked it, but they were defeated. It was called the Battle of the Herrings. It was a great disappointment, for the people of Orléans. They began to lose hope.

[pause]

{img:winter-night-orleans-cathedral-towers-snow-city-lights-quiet-hope}
News of the siege spread all over France. People everywhere talked about it, and prayed for Orléans. They knew that if Orléans fell, the Dauphin's cause might be lost. And there was an old prophecy, that people repeated, in these dark days. It said that France would be lost by a woman, and saved by a maiden, from the borders of Lorraine. People said that the woman was the queen who had signed away her son's right to the throne. And they wondered who the maiden could be.

[pause]

In Domrémy, near the borders of Lorraine, Jeanne had heard the prophecy too. And when she heard it, she was certain. She was the maiden. She must go. In the middle of the winter, in January 1429, she left her home, and went to Vaucouleurs again. This time, she did not come back.

[long-pause]

{img:girl-leaving-village-winter-morning-uncle-cart-looking-back-snow-domremy}
In January 1429, Jeanne left Domrémy for the last time. Her uncle, Durand Laxart, came to fetch her, saying that his wife needed help with the new baby. Jeanne said goodbye to her family. But she did not tell them where she was really going. As she walked out of the village, she passed the house of her friend Hauviette. But she did not stop, to say goodbye. She said, later, that she did not want to cry. Hauviette cried, for a long time, when she found out.

[pause]

Jeanne went first to her uncle's house, and then to Vaucouleurs. There, she stayed with a family she knew, a wheelwright, called Henri le Royer, and his wife, Catherine. She helped Catherine with the spinning, and the housework. And every day, she went to the little church, by the castle, and prayed, for hours, before a statue of the Virgin Mary, in the crypt, below the chapel.

[long-pause]

{img:vaucouleurs-street-medieval-townspeople-watching-girl-in-red-dress-walk}
And she went to see Robert de Baudricourt, again. And again. She told him, every time, that she must go to the Dauphin. That God had sent her. That she would save Orléans, and lead the Dauphin to Reims, to be crowned. And every time, Baudricourt sent her away. But this time, the people of Vaucouleurs began to listen to her. They talked about the girl from Domrémy, and the prophecy, and the maiden from Lorraine. They began to believe in her.

[pause]

One day, a young soldier, called Jean de Metz, met Jeanne in the street, and asked her, kindly, what she was doing there. She told him. She said that she must go to the Dauphin, before the middle of Lent, even if she had to wear her legs down to her knees, walking there. She said that nobody in the world could save the kingdom of France, except her. Not kings, nor dukes, nor anyone else. Jean de Metz was so moved that he promised to take her to the Dauphin himself.

[pause]

{img:young-soldier-kneeling-promising-to-help-peasant-girl-street-vaucouleurs}
Another young soldier, Bertrand de Poulengy, also offered to go with her. Both of them were knights, of good families. They believed in her, completely. And slowly, even Robert de Baudricourt began to change his mind. Perhaps he was impressed by her faith, and her determination. Perhaps he was impressed by the people of the town, who believed in her. Perhaps he thought, why not? The situation of France is so bad, that nothing could make it worse.

[pause]

The people of Vaucouleurs helped Jeanne to prepare for her journey. They gave her men's clothes, to wear. A tunic, and leggings, and boots, and a cloak, and a cap. For a girl travelling with soldiers, through dangerous country, it was much safer to dress as a boy. She cut her long dark hair short, around her head, like a boy's. And they bought her a horse. Robert de Baudricourt gave her a sword.

[long-pause]

{img:girl-in-boys-clothes-short-hair-cloak-mounting-horse-castle-gate-winter}
On the evening of the twenty-third of February, 1429, Jeanne set out from Vaucouleurs, with six companions. Jean de Metz, and Bertrand de Poulengy, and their servants, and a royal messenger, and an archer. The people of the town gathered at the gate, called the Gate of France, to see them off. Some of them were crying. Robert de Baudricourt made the men swear to look after her. Then he said to Jeanne, go, and come what may.

[pause]

Jeanne turned her horse, and rode out through the gate, into the darkness, and the cold. She was seventeen years old. She had never ridden so far, or been so far from home. Ahead of her, there were more than five hundred kilometres of road, through winter countryside, much of it held by the enemy. Behind her, there was everything she had ever known. But she did not look back.

[pause]

In Domrémy, her mother and father heard the news a few days later. They were shocked, and worried, and frightened for her. They did not understand. But Jeanne sent them a letter, later, asking them to forgive her. And they did. And later still, they would be very proud of her. But for now, in the cold February of 1429, they could only wait, and pray, and hope that their daughter was safe.

[long-pause]

{img:riders-crossing-winter-countryside-flooded-river-ford-grey-sky-medieval}
The journey from Vaucouleurs to the Dauphin took eleven days. They travelled west, across France, through the regions of Champagne, and Burgundy, and into the valley of the Loire. It was winter, and the weather was cold, and wet. The roads were muddy, and the rivers were swollen with rain, and snow. Some of the bridges were broken, or guarded by enemy soldiers. They had to cross several big rivers, by fords, with the cold water up to the horses' bellies.

[pause]

Much of the country they passed through was held by the English, or the Burgundians. So they had to be careful. They often travelled by night, and slept by day, hidden in the woods, or in barns, or in abbeys. They avoided the big towns, and the main roads. They wrapped themselves in their cloaks, and lay on the ground, and slept, and then rode on, through the dark, under the stars.

[long-pause]

{img:night-camp-woods-small-fire-travellers-sleeping-cloaks-girl-praying}
Jean de Metz and Bertrand de Poulengy said, later, that Jeanne was brave, and calm, and cheerful, all through the journey. She never complained, about the cold, or the hunger, or the long hours in the saddle. She slept in her clothes, between the two knights, wrapped in her cloak. And they said that they never had any wrong thought about her, because she was so good, and pure, and holy. They felt a kind of awe, in her presence.

[pause]

Jeanne wanted to go to mass every day. But it was too dangerous, to go into the towns, and the churches. She managed to go twice, during the journey. Once at the great abbey of Saint Urbain, where they stayed the first night. And once at Auxerre, in the great cathedral, where she stood quietly at the back, among the other people, hoping that nobody would notice her.

[pause]

{img:medieval-abbey-gatehouse-night-monks-lanterns-welcoming-riders}
Her companions sometimes wondered if they were doing the right thing. Sometimes they asked her if she was sure. And she always answered, calmly, that they must not be afraid. That God was with them, and would show them the way. That her brothers in paradise told her what to do. They did not quite understand. But they trusted her.

[pause]

At last, they crossed the river Loire, at a town called Gien, into the lands loyal to the Dauphin. Now they were safer. They could travel by day, on the roads, and stay at inns. And news of their coming spread ahead of them. People came out of their houses, to see the maiden from Lorraine, who was going to save France. Some of them believed in her. Others laughed. But everyone was curious.

[long-pause]

{img:pilgrimage-chapel-sainte-catherine-de-fierbois-candles-girl-praying-dawn}
On the last stage of the journey, they stopped at a little village, called Sainte Catherine de Fierbois, where there was a famous chapel, dedicated to Saint Catherine. Saint Catherine was one of Jeanne's saints. So Jeanne was very happy to be there. She went to mass three times, in one day, in the chapel. And she dictated a letter to the Dauphin, saying that she had travelled a long way to help him, and that she had many good things to tell him.

[pause]

The next day, on the sixth of March, 1429, they rode into the town of Chinon, on the river Vienne. Above the town, on a high ridge, stood a great castle, with tall walls, and towers. It was one of the Dauphin's favourite castles. And the Dauphin was there, now. Jeanne looked up at the castle, and at the towers, against the sky. She had come so far. And now, at last, she was here.

[pause]

The little group found an inn, in the town, at the foot of the castle. Jeanne washed, and rested, and prayed. And she waited, to be called. For a day or two, the Dauphin's advisers argued about whether he should see her at all. And then, one evening, word came. The Dauphin would receive her.

[long-pause]

{img:chinon-castle-great-hall-1429-torches-crowd-of-courtiers-fireplace-evening}
In the evening, Jeanne climbed up the steep path, from the town, to the castle of Chinon. It was dark, and the castle was lit with torches. She was led through the great gate, and across the courtyard, and up the stairs, into the great hall of the castle. It was a long, high room, with a huge fireplace, at one end, and tall windows. It was lit by fifty torches, people said. And it was full of people.

[pause]

Three hundred lords, and knights, and ladies, and officials, of the Dauphin's court, had come to see the strange girl from Lorraine. They were dressed in their finest clothes, in silk, and velvet, and fur, and jewels. They talked, and laughed, and whispered, as Jeanne came in. She was dressed in her simple boy's clothes, of black and grey, with her short dark hair. She looked very small, and very plain, among all the fine people.

[long-pause]

{img:peasant-girl-kneeling-before-plain-dressed-dauphin-among-courtiers-surprise}
The story says that the Dauphin had decided to test her. He did not sit on his throne. He dressed in ordinary clothes, and hid among the courtiers, in the crowd, while another man sat on the throne, pretending to be him. If Jeanne was really sent by God, he thought, she would know which one was the real Dauphin. And if she did not, she was a fraud.

[pause]

Jeanne had never seen the Dauphin before. There were no photographs, in those days, and very few pictures of him. But she walked straight past the man on the throne, without stopping. She looked around the crowd, for a moment. Then she walked up to the Dauphin, among the courtiers, and knelt in front of him, and said, God give you a good life, gentle king. The Dauphin said, I am not the king. And he pointed to the man on the throne. But Jeanne said, in God's name, gentle prince, it is you, and nobody else.

[pause]

{img:dauphin-and-girl-speaking-privately-window-alcove-candle-serious}
Then Jeanne told him why she had come. She said that she was sent by God, to lift the siege of Orléans, and to lead him to Reims, to be crowned and anointed, as the true king of France. The Dauphin took her aside, to a window, away from the crowd, and they talked privately, for a long time. Nobody knows exactly what they said. But when they came back, the Dauphin looked very different. People said that he looked radiant, as if he had heard something wonderful.

[pause]

Later, people said that Jeanne had told him a secret. Something that only he knew. Perhaps a prayer, that he had said, alone, in his chapel, asking God to show him if he was really the true heir to the throne. And Jeanne had told him that he was. Nobody knows if this is true. But something she said convinced him. From that moment, the Dauphin began to believe in her.

[long-pause]

The Dauphin's advisers were not so sure. Some of them thought she was mad. Others thought she might be a witch, or a spy, sent by the English. Others thought she might be useful, whatever she was, because the people believed in her, and the soldiers might follow her. They argued about it, for days. In the meantime, Jeanne stayed in a tower, in the castle, with a lady to look after her.

[pause]

{img:young-duke-of-alencon-meeting-joan-tilting-yard-horse-lance-spring-sun}
While she was at Chinon, Jeanne met a young nobleman, the Duke of Alençon. He was a cousin of the Dauphin, about twenty-two years old, handsome, and cheerful. He had been a prisoner of the English, for several years, and had just been released. He came to Chinon to meet the maiden. He liked her at once. And she liked him. She called him, my fair duke. He would become one of her closest friends, and fight beside her, in the months to come.

[pause]

One day, the duke watched Jeanne ride, with a lance, in the meadow, below the castle. She rode so well, and handled the lance so skilfully, that he was amazed. He gave her a fine horse, as a gift. The girl from Domrémy, who had looked after cows, by the river, was learning to be a knight. And she was learning very fast.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-poitiers-1429-university-churchmen-robes-hall-girl-answering-questions}
Before the Dauphin would give Jeanne an army, he wanted to be sure about her. So he sent her to the city of Poitiers, about a hundred kilometres south of Chinon, to be examined by a group of learned churchmen. Bishops, and priests, and professors of theology, from the university. They would ask her questions, about her life, and her faith, and her voices. And they would decide whether she was truly sent by God.

[pause]

The examination lasted about three weeks. Jeanne stayed in the house of a lawyer, and his wife, in Poitiers. Every day, the learned men came to see her, and asked her questions. They were very clever, and very serious, and they had studied for many years. And she was a peasant girl, of seventeen, who could not read, or write. But she answered all their questions, simply, and honestly, and sometimes with great wit.

[long-pause]

{img:wise-old-cleric-smiling-at-simple-girl-answer-wooden-table-books}
One of the churchmen, a friar, asked her what language her voices spoke. He had a strong accent, from his own part of France. Jeanne looked at him, and said, a better one than yours. Everyone laughed, including the friar. Another asked her if she believed in God. She said, yes, better than you do. Another said that if God wanted to save France, He did not need soldiers. Jeanne said, in God's name, the soldiers will fight, and God will give the victory.

[pause]

They asked her for a sign. A miracle, to prove that she was sent by God. Jeanne said that she had not come to Poitiers to make signs. She said, take me to Orléans, and I will show you the sign for which I was sent. The sign would be the lifting of the siege. If she could do that, everyone would know that she was sent by God.

[pause]

{img:noble-ladies-examining-girl-kindly-private-room-candles-respect}
The churchmen also asked some noble ladies, including the Dauphin's mother-in-law, Queen Yolande, to talk with Jeanne, and to check that she was really a young maiden, as she said. They did, kindly, and confirmed it. Queen Yolande was a wise and powerful woman, who supported the Dauphin. She believed in Jeanne, and helped her a great deal.

[pause]

At the end of the three weeks, the churchmen gave their verdict. They said that they had found nothing bad in her. Only goodness, humility, honesty, simplicity, and devotion. They said that, in the desperate situation of the kingdom, the Dauphin should not refuse her help, but should let her go to Orléans, and see if her sign would come true. To refuse her, they said, might be to refuse the help of God.

[long-pause]

While she was at Poitiers, Jeanne dictated a letter to the English. She could not write, so she told a clerk what to say, and he wrote it down. It was addressed to the King of England, and his commanders, at Orléans. It told them, very firmly, to leave France, and go home, and to give back the towns they had taken. If they did, she said, they could go in peace. If they did not, she would drive them out. She signed it, Jeanne the Maiden.

[pause]

{img:clerk-writing-letter-quill-dictating-girl-standing-window-poitiers}
The English, when they received the letter, were very angry. They called her rude names, and said they would burn her messengers. They did not believe that a peasant girl could do anything against them. But Jeanne had warned them. And she meant every word.

[pause]

The people of Poitiers came to see her, in great numbers, while she was there. They came to the lawyer's house, to look at her, and talk to her, and touch her hands. Many of them said that they believed she was sent by God. And the news spread, all over the Dauphin's France. A maiden had come, from Lorraine, to save the kingdom. And the learned men of Poitiers had said that she was good. People began to hope again, for the first time in years.

[long-pause]

{img:armourer-workshop-tours-1429-fitting-white-plate-armour-on-young-woman}
After Poitiers, Jeanne went to the city of Tours, on the Loire, to prepare for the journey to Orléans. The Dauphin gave her a household, of her own, like a knight, or a captain. A squire, called Jean d'Aulon, a sensible, brave, older soldier, who would look after her. Two pages, young boys, to carry her things, and help her with her armour. A chaplain, a young friar called Jean Pasquerel, who would hear her confessions, and say mass for her. And messengers, and servants.

[pause]

Her brothers, Jean and Pierre, came from Domrémy, to join her. She was very happy to see them. They brought news from home, from her parents, and the village, and Hauviette. And they stayed with her, as part of her household, all through the months that followed. Pierre especially was always close to her side.

[long-pause]

{img:white-armour-gleaming-on-stand-workshop-light-helmet-gauntlets-polished}
The Dauphin ordered a suit of armour for her. It was made by the best armourer in Tours. A full suit of plate armour, made of polished steel, fitted exactly to her body. Breastplate, and backplate, and arm pieces, and leg pieces, and gauntlets for her hands, and a helmet. It was white armour, which means that it was plain, and polished, and shining, without paint, or decoration. It was beautiful. When she wore it, she shone like silver, in the sun.

[pause]

Jeanne needed a sword, too. Her voices told her that there was a sword, buried behind the altar, in the chapel of Saint Catherine at Fierbois, where she had prayed, on her way to Chinon. She sent a messenger there, to look for it. The priests dug behind the altar, and found an old sword, rusty, with five crosses on the blade. When they cleaned it, the rust fell away easily. They sent it to Jeanne, with a fine scabbard. People said it was a miracle.

[pause]

{img:painters-workshop-making-white-banner-lilies-figure-of-christ-angels}
But Jeanne's favourite thing was not her sword. It was her banner. She had it made by a painter, in Tours, following the instructions of her voices. It was a long banner, of white linen, with a fringe of silk. On it, there was a picture of Christ, sitting in judgement, holding the world, with an angel on each side. And the names, Jesus, Maria. And the background was scattered with golden lilies, the flowers of the French kings.

[pause]

Jeanne said, later, that she loved her banner forty times more than her sword. She carried it herself, in her hand, into every battle, so that she would never have to use her sword, against anyone. She said that she never killed anyone. She carried the banner, high, so that the soldiers could see it, and follow it. And they did.

[long-pause]

{img:young-woman-white-armour-on-white-horse-holding-banner-crowd-cheering-tours}
On the day she first put on her armour, and mounted her horse, and took her banner in her hand, the people of Tours came out, in crowds, to see her. She rode through the streets, on a great white horse, in her shining armour, with her banner flying. People cheered, and wept, and crossed themselves. They said that she looked like an angel. Or like Saint Michael himself, come down from heaven.

[pause]

Jeanne was still a peasant girl, from Domrémy, at heart. She was still simple, and humble, and kind. She still prayed, many times a day. She still went to mass, every morning. She still gave to the poor. She did not like the fine clothes, and the attention, very much. But she knew that she must play her part. The soldiers, and the people, needed something to believe in. And she was ready to give it to them.

[pause]

At Blois, a town on the Loire, an army was gathering, to take supplies of food to Orléans. Jeanne went there, to join it. She was horrified by the soldiers. They swore, and drank, and gambled, and fought, and behaved very badly. So she told them to stop. She told them to go to confession, and to mass, and to stop swearing. And, to everyone's amazement, many of them did. The priests marched at the head of the army, singing hymns. It was the strangest army anyone had ever seen.

[long-pause]

{img:boats-crossing-loire-night-supplies-wind-change-sails-orleans-walls-distance}
At the end of April, 1429, the army set out from Blois, along the south bank of the Loire, toward Orléans. With it went a long convoy of carts, and cattle, and sheep, carrying food, and supplies, for the hungry city. Jeanne rode at the front, in her white armour, on her horse, with her banner. The priests walked ahead, singing. And the soldiers followed, quietly, behind.

[pause]

When they reached Orléans, they found that they were on the wrong side of the river. The city was on the north bank. The supplies had to be carried across the river, in boats. But the wind was blowing the wrong way, against the boats, and they could not sail upstream. Jean, the Bastard of Orléans, came across the river, to meet Jeanne. He explained the problem. Jeanne was impatient. But then, suddenly, the wind changed. It began to blow the other way. And the boats could sail.

[long-pause]

{img:night-entry-into-orleans-torches-crowds-girl-on-white-horse-banner-joy}
People said it was a miracle. Perhaps it was. Perhaps it was just the wind. But the boats sailed, loaded with food, up the river, and into the city, past the English forts. And on the evening of the twenty-ninth of April, 1429, Jeanne herself crossed the river, and rode into Orléans, through the eastern gate, the Burgundy Gate, at about eight o'clock at night.

[pause]

The whole city came out to meet her. Thousands of people, men, and women, and children, crowded into the streets, with torches, and candles, to see the maiden. They had been besieged for seven months. They were hungry, and tired, and frightened. And now, at last, help had come. They cheered, and cried, and pushed forward, to touch her, or her horse. It was like a festival, said a man who was there. As if God himself had come down among them.

[pause]

Jeanne rode slowly through the streets, in her armour, with her banner, surrounded by the crowd. At one moment, a torch came too close to her banner, and set it on fire. Jeanne calmly turned her horse, and put out the flames, with her hand, as if she had been riding all her life. The people cheered even louder. She rode to the cathedral first, to give thanks to God. And then to the house where she would stay.

[pause]

She stayed in the house of a rich and respected citizen, the treasurer of the Duke of Orléans, called Jacques Boucher. He lived with his wife, and his daughter, Charlotte, who was nine years old. Jeanne slept in the same bed as Charlotte, every night, while she was in Orléans. Charlotte loved her. She said, later, that Jeanne was kind, and gentle, and that she prayed, every night, before she went to sleep.

[long-pause]

{img:quiet-bedroom-medieval-house-girl-and-young-woman-praying-before-sleep-candle}
For the next few days, Jeanne waited, while the captains of the army made their plans. She did not like waiting. She wanted to attack the English, at once. But the captains wanted to wait, for more soldiers. So Jeanne spent her time going to church, and riding around the city, so that the people could see her, and taking messages to the English, asking them to leave.

[pause]

She went to the end of the broken bridge, and called across the gap, to the English soldiers, in the Tourelles fortress, on the other side. She told them to go home, in God's name, or they would be sorry. The English soldiers shouted rude things back at her. They called her a cowgirl, and told her to go back to her cows. Jeanne was upset by their rudeness. But she did not give up.

[pause]

{img:girl-in-armour-at-end-of-broken-bridge-calling-across-river-fortress}
The people of Orléans followed her everywhere. When she went to church, the church was full. When she rode through the streets, the streets were full. Children ran after her. Women brought her flowers, and food. Old men blessed her, as she passed. For the first time in months, the people of Orléans smiled. They felt hope. They believed that the maiden would save them.

[pause]

And in the English forts, around the city, the English soldiers watched, and listened, and began to feel uneasy. They had heard the stories about the maiden. Some of them were afraid of her. They said that she was a witch. Or that she had magic powers. And for the first time in a long time, the English soldiers were not quite so sure that they would win.

[long-pause]

{img:orleans-may-1429-distant-view-smoke-over-forts-river-city-walls-dawn}
In the first week of May, the fighting began. We will watch it from a distance, as the people of Orléans did, from the city walls, and the church towers. Jeanne rode out, with the soldiers, to attack the English forts, one by one. She carried her banner, at the front, where everyone could see it. And the French soldiers, who had lost so many battles, and had almost given up hope, followed her, with new courage.

[pause]

On the fourth of May, they took the first English fort, on the east side of the city. On the sixth, they crossed the river, and took more forts, on the south bank. Each time, the people of Orléans watched from the walls, and prayed, and rang the church bells, when the news of victory came. The English were surprised. They had not expected the French to fight like this.

[long-pause]

On the seventh of May, the French attacked the great fortress of the Tourelles, at the southern end of the bridge. It was the strongest of all the English forts. The attack began in the morning, and went on all day. During the fighting, Jeanne was wounded, by an arrow, which struck her near the shoulder. She was carried away, to rest. She had known, beforehand, that she would be hurt. Her voices had told her. She wept, a little, from the pain. But the wound was not too serious.

[pause]

The captains thought that they should stop the attack, for the day. The soldiers were tired, and it was getting late. But Jeanne asked them to wait a little. She went away, alone, into a vineyard nearby, and prayed, for a quarter of an hour. Then she came back, and took her banner, and went back to the front. And when the soldiers saw her banner again, they took heart, and attacked once more.

[pause]

{img:sunset-over-loire-tourelles-fortress-banner-raised-on-walls-distant-victory}
By the evening, the Tourelles had fallen. The French held both ends of the bridge again. The people of Orléans quickly laid planks across the broken part of the bridge, so that the soldiers could come back into the city. And that night, all the church bells of Orléans rang, and rang, in joy. The people sang hymns, in the streets, and gave thanks to God. Jeanne went back to the Bouchers' house, and had her wound dressed, and ate a little bread, soaked in wine, and went to sleep.

[pause]

The next morning, Sunday, the eighth of May, the English came out of their remaining forts, on the north side of the city, and lined up, in battle order, in a field, facing the city. The French came out too, and lined up, facing them. For an hour, the two armies stood there, looking at each other. But it was Sunday. And Jeanne said that they should not fight on a Sunday, unless the English attacked first.

[long-pause]

{img:english-army-marching-away-morning-mist-fields-orleans-people-watching-walls}
So, instead of fighting, Jeanne had an altar set up, in front of the French army, and a priest said mass, in the open air. The soldiers knelt, and prayed. And when the mass was over, Jeanne asked, are the English facing us, or have they turned their backs? And they told her that the English had turned around. They were marching away. Leaving their forts. The siege was over.

[pause]

The siege of Orléans, which had lasted for seven months, was lifted in just nine days, after Jeanne arrived. It was an extraordinary achievement. All over France, people heard the news, and could hardly believe it. The maiden from Lorraine had done what she said she would do. She had shown the sign, for which she was sent. And from that day on, people called her the Maid of Orléans.

[pause]

{img:orleans-procession-may-8-thanksgiving-crowds-churches-banners-joy}
The people of Orléans have never forgotten. Almost every year, since 1429, around the eighth of May, they hold a great festival, in her honour. There is a procession, through the streets, with banners, and music, and a young girl from the city, chosen to play Jeanne, riding on a horse, in armour. It is one of the oldest festivals in France. Almost six hundred years old. And it still takes place, today.

[long-pause]

{img:loire-valley-june-1429-army-on-road-castles-river-summer-fields}
After Orléans, Jeanne wanted to go straight to Reims, to crown the Dauphin. But first, the English must be driven out of the other towns along the Loire. So, in June 1429, the French army, with Jeanne, and the Duke of Alençon, marched along the river, and took the towns, one by one. Jargeau, and Meung, and Beaugency. Each time, the people of the towns welcomed the French, and the maiden, with joy.

[pause]

On the eighteenth of June, near a village called Patay, the French army met the English army, in open country. The English were surprised, before they were ready. The French cavalry charged, and the English were defeated. It was a great victory. The English commander, Lord Talbot, a famous and brave old soldier, was taken prisoner. Jeanne was not in the front of the fighting, that day. She came afterward, and was sad, to see the wounded, and comforted them.

[long-pause]

{img:girl-in-armour-comforting-wounded-soldier-field-evening-gentle-sad}
Jeanne was always sad, after a battle. She did not like fighting, or seeing people hurt, even the English. She wept for the dead, on both sides. She once held the head of a wounded English soldier, in her lap, and comforted him, and asked a priest to come and hear his confession. She believed that the English should go home. But she did not hate them. She prayed for them.

[pause]

After Patay, the road to Reims was open. But Reims was far away, about three hundred kilometres to the north-east, through country still held by the English, and the Burgundians. The Dauphin's advisers were worried. They said it was too dangerous. That the army should go somewhere else, and attack Normandy, or Paris. But Jeanne insisted. She said that the Dauphin must be crowned, at Reims, as soon as possible. Once he was crowned, everyone would know that he was the true king.

[pause]

{img:royal-army-procession-countryside-dauphin-and-maid-riding-banners-summer}
At last, the Dauphin agreed. At the end of June, he set out, with Jeanne, and a large army, for Reims. It was a strange journey. They marched through enemy country, but they hardly had to fight at all. Town after town opened its gates to them, without a battle. Auxerre gave them food, and supplies. Troyes, the town where the terrible treaty had been signed, surrendered after a few days. Châlons opened its gates. The people came out to cheer.

[pause]

In many of the towns, people came to see Jeanne. They crowded around her horse, and touched her, and asked her to bless them, and their children. Some of them brought her rosaries, and medals, to touch, so that they would be holy. Jeanne laughed, kindly, and said, touch them yourselves. They will be just as good, from your touch, as from mine. She did not want people to treat her like a saint.

[long-pause]

At one village, on the road, Jeanne met some people from Domrémy. They had walked a long way, to see her. Among them was her godfather, and some of her old neighbours. She was overjoyed to see them. She asked about her family, and the village, and everyone she knew. She gave them gifts, and asked them to take her love to her parents. And for a little while, she was just a girl from Domrémy again, talking with her friends about home.

[pause]

The army marched on, in the summer heat, through the fields of Champagne. The wheat was golden, and ripe, in the fields. The peasants stopped their harvesting, to watch the army pass, and wave. In the evenings, the army camped in the fields, and the soldiers sat around their fires, and sang. And Jeanne prayed, in her tent, and thanked God for every day, and asked Him to bring them safely to Reims.

[pause]

{img:distant-view-reims-cathedral-towers-across-golden-wheat-fields-july}
On the sixteenth of July, 1429, the army came over a hill, and saw, in the distance, across the golden fields, the great towers of the cathedral of Reims, rising above the city. The English garrison had already left. The people of Reims opened the gates, and welcomed the Dauphin, and the maiden, with cheers, and bells, and flowers. And that night, the whole city prepared, for the great ceremony of the next day.

[long-pause]

{img:reims-cathedral-1429-coronation-interior-crowds-candles-stained-glass-light}
On Sunday, the seventeenth of July, 1429, the Dauphin was crowned King of France, in the cathedral of Reims. The cathedral is one of the greatest Gothic churches in the world. Tall, and vast, and full of light, with great stained glass windows, and thousands of carved stone statues, on the outside, of saints, and angels, and kings. For hundreds of years, almost all the kings of France had been crowned there.

[pause]

The ceremony began early in the morning, and lasted for five hours. The cathedral was packed with people. Nobles, and knights, and churchmen, and the people of Reims. There were candles everywhere, and incense, and music, and singing. Everyone was dressed in their best clothes. And the bells of the cathedral rang, and rang, until the whole city shook with the sound.

[long-pause]

{img:holy-ampulla-oil-archbishop-anointing-kneeling-king-golden-light}
There was an ancient custom, for the coronation. A little glass bottle, called the Holy Ampulla, contained a sacred oil. The legend said that it had been brought down from heaven, by a dove, more than nine hundred years before, for the baptism of the first Christian king of the Franks, Clovis. The archbishop of Reims took a little of this oil, and anointed the Dauphin with it, on his head, and his breast, and his hands. It was this anointing, more than the crown, that made him truly king, in the eyes of the people.

[pause]

Then the archbishop placed the crown on his head. And the Dauphin became King Charles the Seventh of France. The people cheered, and the trumpets sounded, and the bells rang. They said that the sound of the trumpets was so loud that it seemed the roof of the cathedral would fall in. Everyone shouted, Noël, Noël, which was the cry of joy, in those days. Many people wept.

[pause]

{img:maid-standing-beside-crowned-king-holding-banner-cathedral-altar-light}
Jeanne stood beside the king, near the altar, during the whole ceremony. She was in her armour, holding her banner. Later, people asked her why her banner was allowed in the cathedral, when the banners of the great lords were not. She answered with words that became famous. She said, it had been through the hard work. It was only right that it should have the honour.

[pause]

When the ceremony was over, Jeanne knelt before the king, and held his knees, and wept. And she said, gentle king, now God's will is done. He wanted me to raise the siege of Orléans, and to bring you to this city of Reims, to receive your holy anointing, to show that you are the true king, to whom the kingdom of France belongs. Everyone who saw her wept too. She had done it. Everything that her voices had told her to do.

[long-pause]

{img:father-and-daughter-reunion-inn-reims-old-farmer-embracing-girl-in-armour}
And in the crowd, at Reims, there was someone very special. Jeanne's father, Jacques d'Arc, had come from Domrémy, to see the coronation. With him came Durand Laxart, the uncle who had helped her. They stayed at an inn, called the Striped Donkey, near the cathedral. When Jeanne heard they were there, she went to see them, at once. And she embraced her father, and they both cried.

[pause]

It had been six months since she had left home, secretly, without telling him. He had been angry, and frightened, and worried. But now, seeing her, in her armour, beside the king, he was only proud. He did not understand everything. But he knew that his daughter had done something wonderful. They talked for a long time, about home, and the family, and her mother, and the village.

[pause]

The king asked Jeanne what reward she wanted, for everything she had done. She could have asked for gold, or jewels, or land, or titles, for herself. But she asked for only one thing. She asked that the people of her village, Domrémy, and the next village, Greux, should never have to pay taxes again. The king agreed, gladly. And for more than three hundred years, until the French Revolution, the tax collectors' books had a note, next to the names of those two villages. It said, nothing, for the Maid.

[long-pause]

{img:domremy-summer-1429-villagers-gathered-around-traveller-news-church-square}
In Domrémy, the news came slowly, along the roads, with travellers, and messengers. First, that Jeanne had reached the Dauphin, at Chinon. Then, that she had been given armour, and a banner, and an army. Then, in May, that she had saved Orléans. And the whole village was amazed. Their own Jeanne, the quiet girl, who spun wool, and minded the cows, and went to church so often. It seemed impossible.

[pause]

When the news of the coronation came, in July, the whole village celebrated. The church bell was rung, for hours. The priest said a special mass, of thanksgiving. There was a feast, in the village, with food, and wine, and music, and dancing. And everyone talked about Jeanne. They remembered things about her, when she was small. How kind she was, and how good, and how she always went to church.

[long-pause]

{img:mother-isabelle-kneeling-in-village-church-candle-prayer-for-daughter}
Isabelle, Jeanne's mother, stayed at home, while her husband went to Reims. She went to church every day, to pray for her daughter. She was proud of her, and frightened for her, at the same time. She knew that Jeanne was in great danger, among the soldiers, and the battles. She lit a candle, every day, before the statue of the Virgin Mary, and asked her to protect her girl.

[pause]

When Jacques came home, from Reims, the whole village gathered around him, to hear the news. He told them about the coronation, and the cathedral, and the trumpets, and the crowds. And about Jeanne, standing beside the king, in her armour, with her banner. And how she had asked the king to free the village from taxes. The villagers cheered. No more taxes. They could hardly believe it.

[pause]

{img:old-friend-hauviette-sitting-under-ladies-tree-autumn-thinking-of-joan}
Hauviette, Jeanne's best friend, listened to all the news, and wept, with joy, and with sadness. She missed her friend very much. She went up to the Ladies' Tree, sometimes, alone, and sat under it, where they had danced together, as children, and thought about Jeanne, far away, with the king, and the soldiers. She wondered if Jeanne would ever come home again.

[pause]

That autumn, in 1429, the harvest in Domrémy was good. The people worked in the fields, as they always had. They ploughed, and sowed, and picked the apples, and gathered the nuts, and made the wine. The pigs grew fat, in the oak woods. The great beech tree turned golden, and the leaves fell. Life went on, in the village, as it always had. But everything felt different. Because one of their own had changed the world.

[long-pause]

The people of the villages around came to Domrémy, to see the house where the Maid was born. They stood outside, and looked at it, and asked the neighbours about her. Some of them went into the church, to pray where she had prayed. Some of them went up to the Ladies' Tree, and the spring. Domrémy, the tiny village that nobody had ever heard of, was becoming famous.

[pause]

{img:domremy-evening-autumn-1429-smoke-from-chimneys-church-bells-golden-fields}
In the evenings, Jacques and Isabelle sat by the fire, in the big room of their house, and talked about their daughter. They wondered where she was, and what she was doing, and whether she was safe, and well. They wondered whether she was eating properly, and sleeping enough. Whether she was cold, in the autumn rain. They were her parents. Even if she was the Maid of Orléans, and the saviour of France, she was still their little girl.

[pause]

And far away, in the north of France, Jeanne thought about them too. In the evenings, when the soldiers were singing around their fires, she sometimes sat alone, and thought about Domrémy. About her mother, and her father, and the house beside the church, and the meadows, by the river, and the cows, and the old tree. She said, once, that she wished she could go home, and look after her father's sheep, with her mother and her brothers. But she could not. Not yet.

[long-pause]

{img:paris-walls-1429-autumn-distant-view-army-camp-grey-sky-towers}
After the coronation, Jeanne wanted to go on, and free Paris, and the rest of France, from the English. But the new king, Charles, was cautious. His advisers wanted to make peace, by talking, with the Duke of Burgundy. So the army waited, and wandered, and lost time. In September 1429, they finally attacked Paris. But the attack failed. Jeanne was wounded, again, by an arrow, in the leg. And the king ordered the army to retreat.

[pause]

It was the first time Jeanne had failed. She was very sad. She hung up her armour, in the great abbey church of Saint Denis, near Paris, as an offering to God. And the army went back to the Loire. That winter, Jeanne stayed with the court, in various castles, and towns. The king gave her family a great honour. He made them nobles. From then on, they had a coat of arms, with a sword, holding up a crown, and two golden lilies.

[long-pause]

But Jeanne was not happy at court. She did not like the politics, and the plotting, and the waiting. She wanted to be doing something. In the spring of 1430, she left the court, with a small group of soldiers, and went north, to help the towns that were still fighting against the Burgundians. Her voices told her, around this time, that she would be captured, before the summer. She was afraid. But she went on.

[pause]

In May 1430, she went to help the town of Compiègne, which was being besieged by the Burgundians. On the evening of the twenty-third of May, she rode out of the town, with some soldiers, to attack the enemy camp. But the attack went wrong. The enemy came from all sides. Jeanne and her soldiers had to retreat, back toward the town. She stayed at the back, to protect the others, as they retreated.

[pause]

{img:compiegne-bridge-evening-1430-town-gate-closing-distant-riders-sunset}
When she reached the town, the gate had been closed, and the bridge raised, to keep the enemy out. Jeanne was left outside. A Burgundian archer pulled her from her horse, by her cloak. And she was taken prisoner. She was eighteen years old. The Burgundians were delighted. They had captured the famous Maid of Orléans. They took her away, to one of their castles.

[pause]

For several months, Jeanne was kept as a prisoner, in castles belonging to the Burgundians. She was treated fairly well, at first. In one castle, at Beaurevoir, there were three kind ladies, who looked after her, and gave her women's clothes, and were very fond of her. She was allowed to walk in the garden, and to hear mass. But she was a prisoner. And she longed for freedom.

[long-pause]

{img:castle-tower-beaurevoir-window-girl-looking-out-over-fields-autumn}
The king of France, Charles, did nothing to help her. He did not pay a ransom for her, or try to rescue her, as kings sometimes did for their important prisoners. Nobody really knows why. Perhaps his advisers told him not to. Perhaps he was busy with other things. Perhaps he no longer believed in her. It is one of the sad parts of the story. The king she had crowned did not come to save her.

[pause]

The English wanted her very much. They believed that she was a witch, who had used evil magic to defeat them. They wanted to put her on trial, and to show the world that she was not sent by God. If they could prove that, they could prove that Charles was not a true king, because he had been crowned with her help. So they paid the Burgundians a great sum of money, ten thousand gold coins, and the Burgundians sold Jeanne to the English.

[pause]

{img:prisoner-transport-cart-guards-autumn-road-normandy-grey-sky-solemn}
In the winter of 1430, Jeanne was taken to the city of Rouen, in Normandy, the capital of the English part of France. She was kept in a castle, in a tower, guarded by English soldiers, day and night. It was cold, and dark, and lonely. She was far from her family, and her friends, and her village. But she still prayed, every day. And her voices, she said, still came to comfort her.

[long-pause]

{img:rouen-1431-medieval-city-cathedral-spires-seine-river-grey-winter-morning}
In January 1431, Jeanne's trial began, in Rouen. We will tell this part of her story gently, and briefly, because it is a sad one. The judges were churchmen, who worked with the English. The most important of them was a bishop, called Pierre Cauchon. He was clever, and ambitious, and determined to find her guilty. There were dozens of learned men, priests, and professors, who asked her questions, for weeks, and weeks.

[pause]

Jeanne had no lawyer, and no one to help her. She was nineteen years old, alone, against all of these clever, powerful men. But she answered their questions with extraordinary courage, and wisdom, and simplicity. Again, and again, they tried to trap her, with difficult questions. And again, and again, she answered in a way that surprised them, and left them with nothing to say.

[long-pause]

{img:young-woman-standing-before-many-judges-chapel-light-calm-courage}
The most famous of her answers was to a very difficult question. They asked her if she was in the grace of God. It was a trap. If she said yes, they would say that she was proud, because nobody can be sure of that. If she said no, they would say that she had admitted she was a sinner, and not sent by God. Jeanne thought for a moment. Then she said, if I am not, may God put me there. And if I am, may God keep me there. The judges were astonished. There was nothing they could say.

[pause]

They asked her about her voices, and the saints, and the Ladies' Tree, and the fairies. They asked her about her men's clothes, and her banner, and her sword. They asked her about the king, and his secret. She answered what she could, and refused to answer what she should not. She said, several times, you say that you are my judge. Be careful what you are doing. Because I am truly sent by God.

[pause]

In the end, the judges found her guilty, as they had always intended. On the thirtieth of May, 1431, in the old market square of Rouen, Jeanne passed away. She was nineteen years old. She asked for a cross, and a soldier made her a small one, from two pieces of wood. A priest held another cross up before her, from the nearby church, so that she could see it. And her last word was the name of Jesus.

[pause]

Many people who were there wept. Even some of the English soldiers, and some of the judges. One of the English officials is said to have walked away, saying, we are lost. We have killed a saint. Her ashes were scattered in the river Seine, which flows through Rouen, to the sea.

[long-pause]

{img:river-seine-rouen-evening-calm-water-flowing-to-sea-soft-light-peace}
It is a sad ending, to a short life. But it was not really the end. Her story did not end in Rouen. It was only just beginning. Because the people of France never forgot her. And the truth about her, and about her trial, would come out, in time. And her name would be cleared, and honoured, all over the world.

[pause]

The river Seine flows on, through Rouen, as it did then, slowly, and calmly, out to the sea. And in the old market square, where she passed away, there is now a church, named after her. It is a modern church, with a roof like a great wave, or a flame. And inside, there are beautiful old stained glass windows, full of light. People come there, from all over the world, to light a candle, and remember her.

[pause]

{img:modern-church-saint-joan-rouen-market-square-wave-roof-stained-glass-glow}
In Domrémy, when the news came, her mother and father wept, for a long time. And the whole village mourned. The priest said a mass, for her soul. And Hauviette went up to the Ladies' Tree, alone, and sat under it, among the roots, and remembered her friend. The girl who had danced there, as a child, and spun wool, and minded the cows, and gone away, one winter morning, to save France.

[long-pause]

{img:french-army-1450s-normandy-recaptured-castle-banners-lilies-peace-returning}
After Jeanne passed away, the war went on, for another twenty-two years. But the tide had turned. Jeanne had given the French hope, and courage, and a crowned king. Slowly, the French won back their country. In 1435, the Duke of Burgundy made peace with King Charles, and left the English. In 1436, Paris opened its gates to the French. In 1450, Normandy was freed. And in 1453, the last English army in the south of France was defeated. The Hundred Years' War was over.

[pause]

After so many years of war, France was at peace again. Slowly, the villages were rebuilt, and the fields were ploughed again, and the roads became safe. The people could go back to their lives, and their work, without fear. Many of them remembered Jeanne, and said that it was thanks to her. King Charles the Seventh came to be called Charles the Well-Served, because he had been served so well, by so many people. Most of all by the Maid.

[long-pause]

{img:elderly-mother-isabelle-1450s-travelling-to-paris-church-petition-dignified}
In 1450, when Rouen was French again, King Charles ordered an inquiry into Jeanne's trial. Perhaps he felt guilty, that he had not saved her. And perhaps he did not want people to say that he had been crowned with the help of a witch. In 1455, Jeanne's mother, Isabelle, who was an old woman now, asked the Pope to look at her daughter's trial again. The Pope agreed. And a new trial began, to examine the old one.

[pause]

Isabelle travelled to Paris, to the great cathedral of Notre Dame, to ask for justice for her daughter. She was more than sixty years old. She knelt before the judges, and wept, and asked them to clear Jeanne's name. Everyone who saw her was moved. Her husband, Jacques, had passed away, some years before. But Isabelle had lived to fight for her daughter.

[pause]

{img:old-villagers-giving-testimony-scribe-writing-1456-domremy-memories}
The judges of the new trial asked many people about Jeanne. More than a hundred people who had known her. Her old neighbours in Domrémy, and her friends, including Hauviette. Her godparents. The soldiers who had travelled with her, to Chinon. Jean de Metz, and Bertrand de Poulengy. Her squire, Jean d'Aulon, and her chaplain. The Duke of Alençon. Jean, the Bastard of Orléans. Charlotte, the little girl from Orléans, who had shared her bed. All of them told what they remembered.

[pause]

Their memories still exist, today, written down by the clerks of the court. And they give us a wonderful picture of Jeanne, as a person. A good, kind, simple, brave, cheerful, pious girl, who loved God, and her family, and her friends, and her country. Much of what we know about her childhood, in Domrémy, comes from these memories. Without them, we would know very little.

[long-pause]

{img:cathedral-rouen-1456-proclamation-crowd-bells-justice-summer-light}
In July 1456, the judges announced their decision. The first trial had been unjust, and wrong, and full of lies, and tricks. They declared that it was null and void. Jeanne was innocent. Her name was cleared. The decision was read out in public, in Rouen, and in other cities, and there were processions, and sermons, in her honour. Isabelle had lived to see her daughter's name cleared. She passed away two years later, in peace.

[pause]

Over the centuries, Jeanne became more and more famous. Writers, and painters, and musicians, told her story, again, and again. In 1909, the Catholic Church declared her blessed. And in 1920, almost five hundred years after she passed away, she was declared a saint. Saint Joan of Arc. She became one of the patron saints of France. And she is honoured, and loved, by people all over the world, of every faith, and of none, as a symbol of courage, and faith, and hope.

[pause]

Not bad, for a girl from a tiny village, who could not read or write, and who spent most of her life spinning wool, and looking after cows, by a quiet river, in the east of France.

[long-pause]

{img:today-domremy-joan-of-arc-birthplace-house-stone-church-next-door-visitors}
Today, Domrémy is still a small village, in the east of France. It is called Domrémy-la-Pucelle now. Domrémy of the Maid. About a hundred and fifty people live there. And the house where Jeanne was born is still there, beside the church. It is a simple stone house, with a steep roof, and thick walls, and small windows. Visitors can go inside, and see the rooms where she lived, as a child. The big room, with the great fireplace. And the little room where, people say, she slept.

[pause]

Above the door, there is a carved coat of arms, and a little statue of Jeanne, kneeling in prayer. Next to the house is the church, where she was baptised, and where she prayed, every day. Some parts of it are still the same as in her time. There is the old stone font, where she was baptised. And there is an old statue of Saint Margaret, which may be the very statue that Jeanne looked at, every Sunday.

[long-pause]

{img:today-basilica-bois-chenu-hillside-above-domremy-autumn-trees-view}
On the hillside above the village, near where the Ladies' Tree once stood, there is now a great church, built about a hundred years ago, in Jeanne's honour. It is called the Basilica of the Bois Chenu, the old oak wood. Pilgrims come there, from all over the world. From the terrace, there is a beautiful view, over the valley of the Meuse, and the village, and the meadows, where Jeanne once watched her father's cows.

[pause]

The old Ladies' Tree is gone now. It died, long ago. But the spring is still there, in the woods, near the basilica. And people still come to it, to drink the water, and to remember the girl who danced there, as a child. In the autumn, the woods are golden, and the leaves fall, and the beechnuts, and the acorns, just as they did in 1428.

[pause]

{img:today-orleans-fetes-johanniques-procession-girl-on-horse-banners-crowd}
In Orléans, every year, in the spring, there is still the great festival of Jeanne, around the eighth of May. A young girl from the city is chosen to play Jeanne, and she rides through the streets, on a horse, in armour, with a banner, in a great procession, with music, and soldiers, and churchmen, and thousands of people. At night, the cathedral is lit up with beautiful pictures, projected onto its walls, telling the story of the siege.

[pause]

In Reims, the great cathedral still stands, where the king was crowned, with Jeanne beside him. Outside, in the square, there is a statue of Jeanne, on horseback, in armour, holding her sword high. And inside, there is a beautiful modern statue of her, in a chapel, where people light candles. Many people come, every year, to see the place where her greatest moment happened.

[long-pause]

In Paris, there is a famous golden statue of Jeanne, on horseback, with her banner, in a little square, near the Louvre. It shines in the sun. And all over France, in towns, and villages, there are statues of her, and streets named after her, and churches, and schools. She is one of the most famous people in French history. And in many other countries too. There are statues of her in America, and Canada, and Australia, and many other places.

[pause]

There have been hundreds of books, and plays, and films, and operas, and songs, about her. Great writers, like Shakespeare, and Schiller, and Mark Twain, and George Bernard Shaw, have written about her. Mark Twain said that she was the most extraordinary person the human race has ever produced. He spent twelve years writing a book about her, and said it was the best thing he ever wrote.

[pause]

{img:today-meuse-river-meadows-domremy-cows-grazing-autumn-evening-mist}
But perhaps the best place to remember her is not in a great cathedral, or a famous square. It is by the river Meuse, at Domrémy, on an autumn evening. When the mist is rising from the water, and the cows are grazing in the meadows, and the church bell is ringing, for the end of the day. Just as it was, when a girl of sixteen sat there, with her spindle, and listened, and prayed, and dreamed of saving France.

[long-pause]

{img:domremy-autumn-1428-evening-church-bell-girl-kneeling-in-field-sunset}
So we leave Domrémy tonight, in the autumn of 1428, as the evening falls. Before all the great events. Before Vaucouleurs, and Chinon, and Orléans, and Reims. When Jeanne was still just a girl from a village, who spun wool, and minded the cows, and helped with the harvest. And who heard voices, in her father's garden, and kept them secret, in her heart.

[pause]

The sun is setting over the hills, to the west, and the sky is turning gold, and pink, and grey. The mist is rising from the river Meuse, white, and soft, drifting over the meadows. The cows are walking slowly home, from the fields, with their bells clinking. The ploughmen are unhitching their oxen. And the smoke is rising, straight up, from the chimneys of the village, into the still evening air.

[long-pause]

{img:girl-and-friend-walking-home-lane-cows-ahead-church-tower-dusk-mist}
Then the church bell begins to ring, for the end of the day. It rings out over the village, and the fields, and the river, and the woods. And in the field, by the river, Jeanne kneels down, in the grass, and says her prayer, as she always does, when the bell rings. Hauviette kneels beside her. Then they get up, and walk home together, arm in arm, along the lane, behind the cows, talking quietly.

[pause]

In the big room of the house beside the church, the fire is burning. Isabelle is stirring the soup, in the great pot, hanging over the flames. Jacques is sitting by the fire, mending a harness. Jean and Pierre come in from the fields, tired, and hungry. And when Jeanne comes in, her mother smiles, and gives her a bowl of soup, and a piece of bread. And the family sits down together, to eat.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-family-by-fire-evening-spinning-wheel-mother-girl-father-mending}
After supper, the women spin, by the fire, and the men mend their tools. Jacques tells a story. Isabelle sings, softly, an old hymn. And Jeanne spins, and listens, and looks into the flames. Then, one by one, they go to bed. The fire is covered with ashes. The candle is blown out. And the house grows dark, and quiet.

[pause]

Outside, the night is still. The stars are coming out, over the valley, bright, and clear. The river runs softly past the village, in the dark. Up on the hillside, the great beech tree stands silent, with its golden leaves, falling, one by one, onto the spring. An owl calls, from the oak woods. And in the little house beside the church, a girl of sixteen is asleep, dreaming of a long road, and a white banner, and a king.
