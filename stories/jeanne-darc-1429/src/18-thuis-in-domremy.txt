In Domrémy, the news came slowly, along the roads, with travellers, and messengers. First, that Jeanne had reached the Dauphin, at Chinon. Then, that she had been given armour, and a banner, and an army. Then, in May, that she had saved Orléans. And the whole village was amazed. Their own Jeanne, the quiet girl, who spun wool, and minded the cows, and went to church so often. It seemed impossible.

When the news of the coronation came, in July, the whole village celebrated. The church bell was rung, for hours. The priest said a special mass, of thanksgiving. There was a feast, in the village, with food, and wine, and music, and dancing. And everyone talked about Jeanne. They remembered things about her, when she was small. How kind she was, and how good, and how she always went to church.

--

{img:mother-isabelle-kneeling-in-village-church-candle-prayer-for-daughter}
Isabelle, Jeanne's mother, stayed at home, while her husband went to Reims. She went to church every day, to pray for her daughter. She was proud of her, and frightened for her, at the same time. She knew that Jeanne was in great danger, among the soldiers, and the battles. She lit a candle, every day, before the statue of the Virgin Mary, and asked her to protect her girl.

When Jacques came home, from Reims, the whole village gathered around him, to hear the news. He told them about the coronation, and the cathedral, and the trumpets, and the crowds. And about Jeanne, standing beside the king, in her armour, with her banner. And how she had asked the king to free the village from taxes. The villagers cheered. No more taxes. They could hardly believe it.

{img:old-friend-hauviette-sitting-under-ladies-tree-autumn-thinking-of-joan}
Hauviette, Jeanne's best friend, listened to all the news, and wept, with joy, and with sadness. She missed her friend very much. She went up to the Ladies' Tree, sometimes, alone, and sat under it, where they had danced together, as children, and thought about Jeanne, far away, with the king, and the soldiers. She wondered if Jeanne would ever come home again.

That autumn, in 1429, the harvest in Domrémy was good. The people worked in the fields, as they always had. They ploughed, and sowed, and picked the apples, and gathered the nuts, and made the wine. The pigs grew fat, in the oak woods. The great beech tree turned golden, and the leaves fell. Life went on, in the village, as it always had. But everything felt different. Because one of their own had changed the world.

--

The people of the villages around came to Domrémy, to see the house where the Maid was born. They stood outside, and looked at it, and asked the neighbours about her. Some of them went into the church, to pray where she had prayed. Some of them went up to the Ladies' Tree, and the spring. Domrémy, the tiny village that nobody had ever heard of, was becoming famous.

{img:domremy-evening-autumn-1429-smoke-from-chimneys-church-bells-golden-fields}
In the evenings, Jacques and Isabelle sat by the fire, in the big room of their house, and talked about their daughter. They wondered where she was, and what she was doing, and whether she was safe, and well. They wondered whether she was eating properly, and sleeping enough. Whether she was cold, in the autumn rain. They were her parents. Even if she was the Maid of Orléans, and the saviour of France, she was still their little girl.

And far away, in the north of France, Jeanne thought about them too. In the evenings, when the soldiers were singing around their fires, she sometimes sat alone, and thought about Domrémy. About her mother, and her father, and the house beside the church, and the meadows, by the river, and the cows, and the old tree. She said, once, that she wished she could go home, and look after her father's sheep, with her mother and her brothers. But she could not. Not yet.
