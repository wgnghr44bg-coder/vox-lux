In the first week of May, the fighting began. We will watch it from a distance, as the people of Orléans did, from the city walls, and the church towers. Jeanne rode out, with the soldiers, to attack the English forts, one by one. She carried her banner, at the front, where everyone could see it. And the French soldiers, who had lost so many battles, and had almost given up hope, followed her, with new courage.

On the fourth of May, they took the first English fort, on the east side of the city. On the sixth, they crossed the river, and took more forts, on the south bank. Each time, the people of Orléans watched from the walls, and prayed, and rang the church bells, when the news of victory came. The English were surprised. They had not expected the French to fight like this.

--

On the seventh of May, the French attacked the great fortress of the Tourelles, at the southern end of the bridge. It was the strongest of all the English forts. The attack began in the morning, and went on all day. During the fighting, Jeanne was wounded, by an arrow, which struck her near the shoulder. She was carried away, to rest. She had known, beforehand, that she would be hurt. Her voices had told her. She wept, a little, from the pain. But the wound was not too serious.

The captains thought that they should stop the attack, for the day. The soldiers were tired, and it was getting late. But Jeanne asked them to wait a little. She went away, alone, into a vineyard nearby, and prayed, for a quarter of an hour. Then she came back, and took her banner, and went back to the front. And when the soldiers saw her banner again, they took heart, and attacked once more.

{img:sunset-over-loire-tourelles-fortress-banner-raised-on-walls-distant-victory}
By the evening, the Tourelles had fallen. The French held both ends of the bridge again. The people of Orléans quickly laid planks across the broken part of the bridge, so that the soldiers could come back into the city. And that night, all the church bells of Orléans rang, and rang, in joy. The people sang hymns, in the streets, and gave thanks to God. Jeanne went back to the Bouchers' house, and had her wound dressed, and ate a little bread, soaked in wine, and went to sleep.

The next morning, Sunday, the eighth of May, the English came out of their remaining forts, on the north side of the city, and lined up, in battle order, in a field, facing the city. The French came out too, and lined up, facing them. For an hour, the two armies stood there, looking at each other. But it was Sunday. And Jeanne said that they should not fight on a Sunday, unless the English attacked first.

--

{img:english-army-marching-away-morning-mist-fields-orleans-people-watching-walls}
So, instead of fighting, Jeanne had an altar set up, in front of the French army, and a priest said mass, in the open air. The soldiers knelt, and prayed. And when the mass was over, Jeanne asked, are the English facing us, or have they turned their backs? And they told her that the English had turned around. They were marching away. Leaving their forts. The siege was over.

The siege of Orléans, which had lasted for seven months, was lifted in just nine days, after Jeanne arrived. It was an extraordinary achievement. All over France, people heard the news, and could hardly believe it. The maiden from Lorraine had done what she said she would do. She had shown the sign, for which she was sent. And from that day on, people called her the Maid of Orléans.

{img:orleans-procession-may-8-thanksgiving-crowds-churches-banners-joy}
The people of Orléans have never forgotten. Almost every year, since 1429, around the eighth of May, they hold a great festival, in her honour. There is a procession, through the streets, with banners, and music, and a young girl from the city, chosen to play Jeanne, riding on a horse, in armour. It is one of the oldest festivals in France. Almost six hundred years old. And it still takes place, today.
