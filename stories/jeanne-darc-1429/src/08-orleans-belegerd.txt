While Jeanne was in Domrémy, bringing in the harvest, and praying at the old tree, the siege of Orléans had begun. In October 1428, an English army arrived at the city, and began to surround it. Orléans is a great city, on the north bank of the river Loire, one of the biggest rivers in France. In 1428, it had about thirty thousand people. It was rich, and proud, and well defended, with strong walls, and towers, and gates.

A long stone bridge crossed the river, from the city to the south bank. At the southern end of the bridge, there was a fortress, with two towers, called Les Tourelles. The English attacked it first, and captured it, after a few days. So now, the English held the southern end of the bridge. And the people of Orléans broke down part of the bridge, so that the English could not cross it, into the city.

--

{img:english-wooden-forts-bastilles-around-medieval-city-winter-smoke-camps}
Then the English built a ring of small wooden forts, called bastilles, around the city, on both sides of the river. They did not have enough soldiers to surround the city completely. There were gaps between the forts, through which people could sometimes come and go, carefully, with supplies. But the English hoped that, slowly, over the winter, the city would run out of food, and be forced to surrender.

Life inside the city was difficult, but it went on. The people of Orléans were brave, and determined. The men took turns to guard the walls, day and night. The women carried stones, and water, and food, to the defenders. The children helped too. The bakers baked bread. The churches held services, and processions, and prayed for help. And the great cathedral bells rang out, over the city, every day, as if to say, we are still here.

{img:people-of-orleans-on-city-walls-watching-river-candles-church-procession}
In charge of the defence of Orléans was a brave young nobleman, called Jean, the Bastard of Orléans. In those days, the word bastard simply meant a son born outside of marriage, and it was not an insult. He was the half-brother of the Duke of Orléans, who was a prisoner in England. He was clever, and cheerful, and a good soldier. Later, he would become a great friend of Jeanne's, and a hero of France. People today know him by his later title, Dunois.

The winter of 1428 was long, and hard, in Orléans. Food became scarce, and expensive. The English attacked the walls, from time to time, and the French made raids against the English forts. The cannons on both sides fired, now and then, across the river, and over the walls. People said that a great English commander had been killed, early in the siege, by a cannonball fired from the city. But still the siege went on.

--

In February 1429, the French tried to stop an English supply convoy, carrying food to the soldiers around Orléans. The convoy was carrying barrels of herrings, because it was Lent, when people did not eat meat. The French attacked it, but they were defeated. It was called the Battle of the Herrings. It was a great disappointment, for the people of Orléans. They began to lose hope.

{img:winter-night-orleans-cathedral-towers-snow-city-lights-quiet-hope}
News of the siege spread all over France. People everywhere talked about it, and prayed for Orléans. They knew that if Orléans fell, the Dauphin's cause might be lost. And there was an old prophecy, that people repeated, in these dark days. It said that France would be lost by a woman, and saved by a maiden, from the borders of Lorraine. People said that the woman was the queen who had signed away her son's right to the throne. And they wondered who the maiden could be.

In Domrémy, near the borders of Lorraine, Jeanne had heard the prophecy too. And when she heard it, she was certain. She was the maiden. She must go. In the middle of the winter, in January 1429, she left her home, and went to Vaucouleurs again. This time, she did not come back.
