On Sunday, the seventeenth of July, 1429, the Dauphin was crowned King of France, in the cathedral of Reims. The cathedral is one of the greatest Gothic churches in the world. Tall, and vast, and full of light, with great stained glass windows, and thousands of carved stone statues, on the outside, of saints, and angels, and kings. For hundreds of years, almost all the kings of France had been crowned there.

The ceremony began early in the morning, and lasted for five hours. The cathedral was packed with people. Nobles, and knights, and churchmen, and the people of Reims. There were candles everywhere, and incense, and music, and singing. Everyone was dressed in their best clothes. And the bells of the cathedral rang, and rang, until the whole city shook with the sound.

There was an ancient custom, for the coronation. A little glass bottle, called the Holy Ampulla, contained a sacred oil. The legend said that it had been brought down from heaven, by a dove, more than nine hundred years before, for the baptism of the first Christian king of the Franks, Clovis. The archbishop of Reims took a little of this oil, and anointed the Dauphin with it, on his head, and his breast, and his hands. It was this anointing, more than the crown, that made him truly king, in the eyes of the people.

Then the archbishop placed the crown on his head. And the Dauphin became King Charles the Seventh of France. The people cheered, and the trumpets sounded, and the bells rang. They said that the sound of the trumpets was so loud that it seemed the roof of the cathedral would fall in. Everyone shouted, Noël, Noël, which was the cry of joy, in those days. Many people wept.

{img:maid-standing-beside-crowned-king-holding-banner-cathedral-altar-light}
Jeanne stood beside the king, near the altar, during the whole ceremony. She was in her armour, holding her banner. Later, people asked her why her banner was allowed in the cathedral, when the banners of the great lords were not. She answered with words that became famous. She said, it had been through the hard work. It was only right that it should have the honour.

When the ceremony was over, Jeanne knelt before the king, and held his knees, and wept. And she said, gentle king, now God's will is done. He wanted me to raise the siege of Orléans, and to bring you to this city of Reims, to receive your holy anointing, to show that you are the true king, to whom the kingdom of France belongs. Everyone who saw her wept too. She had done it. Everything that her voices had told her to do.

And in the crowd, at Reims, there was someone very special. Jeanne's father, Jacques d'Arc, had come from Domrémy, to see the coronation. With him came Durand Laxart, the uncle who had helped her. They stayed at an inn, called the Striped Donkey, near the cathedral. When Jeanne heard they were there, she went to see them, at once. And she embraced her father, and they both cried.

It had been six months since she had left home, secretly, without telling him. He had been angry, and frightened, and worried. But now, seeing her, in her armour, beside the king, he was only proud. He did not understand everything. But he knew that his daughter had done something wonderful. They talked for a long time, about home, and the family, and her mother, and the village.

The king asked Jeanne what reward she wanted, for everything she had done. She could have asked for gold, or jewels, or land, or titles, for herself. But she asked for only one thing. She asked that the people of her village, Domrémy, and the next village, Greux, should never have to pay taxes again. The king agreed, gladly. And for more than three hundred years, until the French Revolution, the tax collectors' books had a note, next to the names of those two villages. It said, nothing, for the Maid.
