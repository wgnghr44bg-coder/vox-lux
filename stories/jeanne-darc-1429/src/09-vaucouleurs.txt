In January 1429, Jeanne left Domrémy for the last time. Her uncle, Durand Laxart, came to fetch her, saying that his wife needed help with the new baby. Jeanne said goodbye to her family. But she did not tell them where she was really going. As she walked out of the village, she passed the house of her friend Hauviette. But she did not stop, to say goodbye. She said, later, that she did not want to cry. Hauviette cried, for a long time, when she found out.

Jeanne went first to her uncle's house, and then to Vaucouleurs. There, she stayed with a family she knew, a wheelwright, called Henri le Royer, and his wife, Catherine. She helped Catherine with the spinning, and the housework. And every day, she went to the little church, by the castle, and prayed, for hours, before a statue of the Virgin Mary, in the crypt, below the chapel.

--

{img:vaucouleurs-street-medieval-townspeople-watching-girl-in-red-dress-walk}
And she went to see Robert de Baudricourt, again. And again. She told him, every time, that she must go to the Dauphin. That God had sent her. That she would save Orléans, and lead the Dauphin to Reims, to be crowned. And every time, Baudricourt sent her away. But this time, the people of Vaucouleurs began to listen to her. They talked about the girl from Domrémy, and the prophecy, and the maiden from Lorraine. They began to believe in her.

One day, a young soldier, called Jean de Metz, met Jeanne in the street, and asked her, kindly, what she was doing there. She told him. She said that she must go to the Dauphin, before the middle of Lent, even if she had to wear her legs down to her knees, walking there. She said that nobody in the world could save the kingdom of France, except her. Not kings, nor dukes, nor anyone else. Jean de Metz was so moved that he promised to take her to the Dauphin himself.

{img:young-soldier-kneeling-promising-to-help-peasant-girl-street-vaucouleurs}
Another young soldier, Bertrand de Poulengy, also offered to go with her. Both of them were knights, of good families. They believed in her, completely. And slowly, even Robert de Baudricourt began to change his mind. Perhaps he was impressed by her faith, and her determination. Perhaps he was impressed by the people of the town, who believed in her. Perhaps he thought, why not? The situation of France is so bad, that nothing could make it worse.

The people of Vaucouleurs helped Jeanne to prepare for her journey. They gave her men's clothes, to wear. A tunic, and leggings, and boots, and a cloak, and a cap. For a girl travelling with soldiers, through dangerous country, it was much safer to dress as a boy. She cut her long dark hair short, around her head, like a boy's. And they bought her a horse. Robert de Baudricourt gave her a sword.

On the evening of the twenty-third of February, 1429, Jeanne set out from Vaucouleurs, with six companions. Jean de Metz, and Bertrand de Poulengy, and their servants, and a royal messenger, and an archer. The people of the town gathered at the gate, called the Gate of France, to see them off. Some of them were crying. Robert de Baudricourt made the men swear to look after her. Then he said to Jeanne, go, and come what may.

Jeanne turned her horse, and rode out through the gate, into the darkness, and the cold. She was seventeen years old. She had never ridden so far, or been so far from home. Ahead of her, there were more than five hundred kilometres of road, through winter countryside, much of it held by the enemy. Behind her, there was everything she had ever known. But she did not look back.

In Domrémy, her mother and father heard the news a few days later. They were shocked, and worried, and frightened for her. They did not understand. But Jeanne sent them a letter, later, asking them to forgive her. And they did. And later still, they would be very proud of her. But for now, in the cold February of 1429, they could only wait, and pray, and hope that their daughter was safe.
