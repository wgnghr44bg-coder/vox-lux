In the autumn of 1428, there is much to do on the farm. The summer harvest of wheat, and oats, and barley, is over, and the grain has been threshed, on the barn floor, with flails, and stored. Now, it is time to plough the fields again, and sow the winter wheat. Jacques d'Arc and his sons plough with oxen, two great patient beasts, pulling a heavy wooden plough, through the brown earth, back and forth, all day long.

Behind them walks the sower. A man with a bag of seed, slung over his shoulder. He takes a handful of seed, and throws it, in a wide arc, across the ploughed earth. Then another handful, and another, step by step, along the furrows. The crows follow, hoping to steal some of the seed. The children run after them, and chase them away, shouting, and waving their arms.

In the orchards, it is time to pick the fruit. Apples, and pears, and plums, and quinces. The whole family helps, with ladders, and baskets. The best apples are stored in the loft, on beds of straw, for the winter. The others are pressed, in the village press, to make cider. Jeanne loves the apple harvest. She climbs the ladders, and fills the baskets, and eats an apple, now and then, when nobody is looking.

In the woods, there are nuts, to be gathered. Walnuts, and hazelnuts, and beechnuts, and acorns. The pigs of the village are taken into the oak woods, in the autumn, to eat the acorns. A boy goes with them, to watch them, and to knock down the acorns from the trees, with a long stick. The pigs grow fat, on the acorns. And in the winter, some of them will be killed, and salted, for meat, to last until the spring.

{img:pigs-in-oak-forest-autumn-swineherd-boy-stick-acorns-golden-leaves}
There are grapes, too, in a few small vineyards, on the sunny slopes above the river. They are not very good grapes. The wine of Lorraine is thin, and sour. But it is wine. And in October, the families pick the grapes, and tread them, in big wooden tubs, with their bare feet, and press them, and make their wine. The children love treading the grapes. Their legs turn purple, up to the knees.

The hemp is harvested too. Hemp is a tall plant, whose stems are used to make rope, and coarse cloth. The stems are cut, and tied in bundles, and soaked in the river, for weeks, until they are soft. Then they are dried, and beaten, to separate the fibres. Then the women spin the fibres into thread, in the long winter evenings, by the fire. Jeanne spins a great deal of hemp, and flax, and wool, every winter.

In the evenings, when the work is done, the family sits together, by the great fireplace, in the big room of the house. They eat their supper. Soup, with bread, and vegetables, and a little bacon, or cheese. And sometimes, fish from the river. They talk about the day. About the fields, and the animals, and the weather. And about the news from outside. The war, and the English, and the soldiers, and Orléans.

After supper, the women spin, and the men mend tools, and harness, by the light of the fire. Jacques d'Arc tells stories, sometimes, of the old days, and of the wars, and the troubles. Isabelle sings, softly, an old song, or a hymn. And Jeanne spins, and listens, and looks into the fire, and thinks. She is very quiet, this autumn. Her mother notices. She wonders what her daughter is thinking about.

At night, the family goes to bed early. Candles are expensive, and the work begins again at dawn. The fire is covered with ashes, to keep it alive until the morning. The house grows dark, and quiet. Outside, the wind blows through the bare trees, and the river runs softly past the village, in the dark. And the stars shine over the sleeping fields.
