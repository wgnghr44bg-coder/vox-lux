At the end of April, 1429, the army set out from Blois, along the south bank of the Loire, toward Orléans. With it went a long convoy of carts, and cattle, and sheep, carrying food, and supplies, for the hungry city. Jeanne rode at the front, in her white armour, on her horse, with her banner. The priests walked ahead, singing. And the soldiers followed, quietly, behind.

When they reached Orléans, they found that they were on the wrong side of the river. The city was on the north bank. The supplies had to be carried across the river, in boats. But the wind was blowing the wrong way, against the boats, and they could not sail upstream. Jean, the Bastard of Orléans, came across the river, to meet Jeanne. He explained the problem. Jeanne was impatient. But then, suddenly, the wind changed. It began to blow the other way. And the boats could sail.

People said it was a miracle. Perhaps it was. Perhaps it was just the wind. But the boats sailed, loaded with food, up the river, and into the city, past the English forts. And on the evening of the twenty-ninth of April, 1429, Jeanne herself crossed the river, and rode into Orléans, through the eastern gate, the Burgundy Gate, at about eight o'clock at night.

The whole city came out to meet her. Thousands of people, men, and women, and children, crowded into the streets, with torches, and candles, to see the maiden. They had been besieged for seven months. They were hungry, and tired, and frightened. And now, at last, help had come. They cheered, and cried, and pushed forward, to touch her, or her horse. It was like a festival, said a man who was there. As if God himself had come down among them.

Jeanne rode slowly through the streets, in her armour, with her banner, surrounded by the crowd. At one moment, a torch came too close to her banner, and set it on fire. Jeanne calmly turned her horse, and put out the flames, with her hand, as if she had been riding all her life. The people cheered even louder. She rode to the cathedral first, to give thanks to God. And then to the house where she would stay.

She stayed in the house of a rich and respected citizen, the treasurer of the Duke of Orléans, called Jacques Boucher. He lived with his wife, and his daughter, Charlotte, who was nine years old. Jeanne slept in the same bed as Charlotte, every night, while she was in Orléans. Charlotte loved her. She said, later, that Jeanne was kind, and gentle, and that she prayed, every night, before she went to sleep.

For the next few days, Jeanne waited, while the captains of the army made their plans. She did not like waiting. She wanted to attack the English, at once. But the captains wanted to wait, for more soldiers. So Jeanne spent her time going to church, and riding around the city, so that the people could see her, and taking messages to the English, asking them to leave.

She went to the end of the broken bridge, and called across the gap, to the English soldiers, in the Tourelles fortress, on the other side. She told them to go home, in God's name, or they would be sorry. The English soldiers shouted rude things back at her. They called her a cowgirl, and told her to go back to her cows. Jeanne was upset by their rudeness. But she did not give up.

{img:girl-in-armour-at-end-of-broken-bridge-calling-across-river-fortress}
The people of Orléans followed her everywhere. When she went to church, the church was full. When she rode through the streets, the streets were full. Children ran after her. Women brought her flowers, and food. Old men blessed her, as she passed. For the first time in months, the people of Orléans smiled. They felt hope. They believed that the maiden would save them.

And in the English forts, around the city, the English soldiers watched, and listened, and began to feel uneasy. They had heard the stories about the maiden. Some of them were afraid of her. They said that she was a witch. Or that she had magic powers. And for the first time in a long time, the English soldiers were not quite so sure that they would win.
