Not far from Domrémy, on the hillside above the village, at the edge of an oak wood, there is a very old tree. A great beech tree, enormous, and ancient, with a wide spreading crown of branches, and roots like great twisted snakes, running across the ground. The people of the village call it the Ladies' Tree. Or the Fairies' Tree. Nobody knows how old it is. The oldest people in the village say that it was already old, when their grandparents were children.

Near the tree, there is a spring. A little fountain of clear, cold water, bubbling up from the ground, among the rocks. People say that the water is good for fevers. When someone in the village is ill, their family comes to the spring, and fills a jug with water, and takes it home, for them to drink. And many of them get better. Whether it is the water, or the prayers, nobody knows.

--

{img:village-children-dancing-around-old-tree-spring-garlands-bread-laughing}
The tree is the centre of an old custom. Every year, in the spring, on a special Sunday, the young people of the village go up to the tree, to celebrate. They make garlands of flowers, and hang them on the branches. They bring bread, and little cakes, and eat them, under the tree. They drink water from the spring. And they dance, and sing, around the great trunk, holding hands, in a big circle. It is one of the happiest days of the year.

The old people say that long ago, fairies lived at the tree. Beautiful, mysterious ladies, who came out on summer nights, and danced under the branches. Jeanne's godmother says that she once saw them, when she was young. But the priest does not approve of such stories. He says that there are no fairies. He goes up to the tree, every year, on a special day, and reads the Gospel there, to bless it, and to drive away any bad spirits.

{img:girl-hanging-flower-garland-on-low-branch-ancient-beech-dappled-light}
Jeanne has gone to the tree, with the other children, every spring, since she was small. She has danced around it, and made garlands, and eaten cakes, and drunk the water from the spring. But she says that she never saw any fairies. She has also made garlands, for the statue of the Virgin Mary, in the little chapel nearby, at Bermont, in the woods. She loves that chapel. She goes there often, on Saturdays, to pray, and to light a candle.

Much later, in a very different place, men would ask Jeanne many questions about the Ladies' Tree. They would try to make her say that she had met the fairies there, and that her voices came from them, and not from God. But Jeanne answered them simply, and honestly. She said that she had danced there, as a child, as all the children did. But that she had never seen fairies, and that her voices came from God.

--

In the autumn of 1428, the great tree is turning golden. Its leaves are yellow, and orange, and brown, and they fall slowly, in the wind, and cover the ground, like a carpet. The beechnuts fall too, and the children gather them, in their pockets. The spring still bubbles, cold, and clear, among the fallen leaves. And the oak woods behind it are dark, and quiet, and smell of damp earth, and mushrooms.

{img:autumn-beech-tree-golden-leaves-falling-girl-sitting-at-roots-praying-quiet}
Jeanne goes up to the tree, one afternoon, alone. She sits at the foot of the great trunk, among the roots, and the fallen leaves, and looks down at the village, and the river, and the valley. She can see her house, beside the church. And the smoke rising from the chimneys. And the cows in the meadows, by the river. And the road, winding away, to the north, toward Vaucouleurs, and beyond.

She sits there for a long time, thinking, and praying. The wind moves the golden branches above her. The leaves fall around her, softly. The spring murmurs, beside her. And she listens. For her voices. And for her courage. Because she knows, now, that she must soon leave this place. The tree, the spring, the meadows, the river, her family. And she does not know if she will ever come back.
