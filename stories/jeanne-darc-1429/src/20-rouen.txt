In January 1431, Jeanne's trial began, in Rouen. We will tell this part of her story gently, and briefly, because it is a sad one. The judges were churchmen, who worked with the English. The most important of them was a bishop, called Pierre Cauchon. He was clever, and ambitious, and determined to find her guilty. There were dozens of learned men, priests, and professors, who asked her questions, for weeks, and weeks.

Jeanne had no lawyer, and no one to help her. She was nineteen years old, alone, against all of these clever, powerful men. But she answered their questions with extraordinary courage, and wisdom, and simplicity. Again, and again, they tried to trap her, with difficult questions. And again, and again, she answered in a way that surprised them, and left them with nothing to say.

--

{img:young-woman-standing-before-many-judges-chapel-light-calm-courage}
The most famous of her answers was to a very difficult question. They asked her if she was in the grace of God. It was a trap. If she said yes, they would say that she was proud, because nobody can be sure of that. If she said no, they would say that she had admitted she was a sinner, and not sent by God. Jeanne thought for a moment. Then she said, if I am not, may God put me there. And if I am, may God keep me there. The judges were astonished. There was nothing they could say.

They asked her about her voices, and the saints, and the Ladies' Tree, and the fairies. They asked her about her men's clothes, and her banner, and her sword. They asked her about the king, and his secret. She answered what she could, and refused to answer what she should not. She said, several times, you say that you are my judge. Be careful what you are doing. Because I am truly sent by God.

In the end, the judges found her guilty, as they had always intended. On the thirtieth of May, 1431, in the old market square of Rouen, Jeanne passed away. She was nineteen years old. She asked for a cross, and a soldier made her a small one, from two pieces of wood. A priest held another cross up before her, from the nearby church, so that she could see it. And her last word was the name of Jesus.

Many people who were there wept. Even some of the English soldiers, and some of the judges. One of the English officials is said to have walked away, saying, we are lost. We have killed a saint. Her ashes were scattered in the river Seine, which flows through Rouen, to the sea.

--

{img:river-seine-rouen-evening-calm-water-flowing-to-sea-soft-light-peace}
It is a sad ending, to a short life. But it was not really the end. Her story did not end in Rouen. It was only just beginning. Because the people of France never forgot her. And the truth about her, and about her trial, would come out, in time. And her name would be cleared, and honoured, all over the world.

The river Seine flows on, through Rouen, as it did then, slowly, and calmly, out to the sea. And in the old market square, where she passed away, there is now a church, named after her. It is a modern church, with a roof like a great wave, or a flame. And inside, there are beautiful old stained glass windows, full of light. People come there, from all over the world, to light a candle, and remember her.

{img:modern-church-saint-joan-rouen-market-square-wave-roof-stained-glass-glow}
In Domrémy, when the news came, her mother and father wept, for a long time. And the whole village mourned. The priest said a mass, for her soul. And Hauviette went up to the Ladies' Tree, alone, and sat under it, among the roots, and remembered her friend. The girl who had danced there, as a child, and spun wool, and minded the cows, and gone away, one winter morning, to save France.
