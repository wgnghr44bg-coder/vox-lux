After Jeanne passed away, the war went on, for another twenty-two years. But the tide had turned. Jeanne had given the French hope, and courage, and a crowned king. Slowly, the French won back their country. In 1435, the Duke of Burgundy made peace with King Charles, and left the English. In 1436, Paris opened its gates to the French. In 1450, Normandy was freed. And in 1453, the last English army in the south of France was defeated. The Hundred Years' War was over.

After so many years of war, France was at peace again. Slowly, the villages were rebuilt, and the fields were ploughed again, and the roads became safe. The people could go back to their lives, and their work, without fear. Many of them remembered Jeanne, and said that it was thanks to her. King Charles the Seventh came to be called Charles the Well-Served, because he had been served so well, by so many people. Most of all by the Maid.

In 1450, when Rouen was French again, King Charles ordered an inquiry into Jeanne's trial. Perhaps he felt guilty, that he had not saved her. And perhaps he did not want people to say that he had been crowned with the help of a witch. In 1455, Jeanne's mother, Isabelle, who was an old woman now, asked the Pope to look at her daughter's trial again. The Pope agreed. And a new trial began, to examine the old one.

Isabelle travelled to Paris, to the great cathedral of Notre Dame, to ask for justice for her daughter. She was more than sixty years old. She knelt before the judges, and wept, and asked them to clear Jeanne's name. Everyone who saw her was moved. Her husband, Jacques, had passed away, some years before. But Isabelle had lived to fight for her daughter.

{img:old-villagers-giving-testimony-scribe-writing-1456-domremy-memories}
The judges of the new trial asked many people about Jeanne. More than a hundred people who had known her. Her old neighbours in Domrémy, and her friends, including Hauviette. Her godparents. The soldiers who had travelled with her, to Chinon. Jean de Metz, and Bertrand de Poulengy. Her squire, Jean d'Aulon, and her chaplain. The Duke of Alençon. Jean, the Bastard of Orléans. Charlotte, the little girl from Orléans, who had shared her bed. All of them told what they remembered.

Their memories still exist, today, written down by the clerks of the court. And they give us a wonderful picture of Jeanne, as a person. A good, kind, simple, brave, cheerful, pious girl, who loved God, and her family, and her friends, and her country. Much of what we know about her childhood, in Domrémy, comes from these memories. Without them, we would know very little.

--

In July 1456, the judges announced their decision. The first trial had been unjust, and wrong, and full of lies, and tricks. They declared that it was null and void. Jeanne was innocent. Her name was cleared. The decision was read out in public, in Rouen, and in other cities, and there were processions, and sermons, in her honour. Isabelle had lived to see her daughter's name cleared. She passed away two years later, in peace.

Over the centuries, Jeanne became more and more famous. Writers, and painters, and musicians, told her story, again, and again. In 1909, the Catholic Church declared her blessed. And in 1920, almost five hundred years after she passed away, she was declared a saint. Saint Joan of Arc. She became one of the patron saints of France. And she is honoured, and loved, by people all over the world, of every faith, and of none, as a symbol of courage, and faith, and hope.

Not bad, for a girl from a tiny village, who could not read or write, and who spent most of her life spinning wool, and looking after cows, by a quiet river, in the east of France.
