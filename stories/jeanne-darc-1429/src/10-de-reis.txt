The journey from Vaucouleurs to the Dauphin took eleven days. They travelled west, across France, through the regions of Champagne, and Burgundy, and into the valley of the Loire. It was winter, and the weather was cold, and wet. The roads were muddy, and the rivers were swollen with rain, and snow. Some of the bridges were broken, or guarded by enemy soldiers. They had to cross several big rivers, by fords, with the cold water up to the horses' bellies.

Much of the country they passed through was held by the English, or the Burgundians. So they had to be careful. They often travelled by night, and slept by day, hidden in the woods, or in barns, or in abbeys. They avoided the big towns, and the main roads. They wrapped themselves in their cloaks, and lay on the ground, and slept, and then rode on, through the dark, under the stars.

Jean de Metz and Bertrand de Poulengy said, later, that Jeanne was brave, and calm, and cheerful, all through the journey. She never complained, about the cold, or the hunger, or the long hours in the saddle. She slept in her clothes, between the two knights, wrapped in her cloak. And they said that they never had any wrong thought about her, because she was so good, and pure, and holy. They felt a kind of awe, in her presence.

Jeanne wanted to go to mass every day. But it was too dangerous, to go into the towns, and the churches. She managed to go twice, during the journey. Once at the great abbey of Saint Urbain, where they stayed the first night. And once at Auxerre, in the great cathedral, where she stood quietly at the back, among the other people, hoping that nobody would notice her.

{img:medieval-abbey-gatehouse-night-monks-lanterns-welcoming-riders}
Her companions sometimes wondered if they were doing the right thing. Sometimes they asked her if she was sure. And she always answered, calmly, that they must not be afraid. That God was with them, and would show them the way. That her brothers in paradise told her what to do. They did not quite understand. But they trusted her.

At last, they crossed the river Loire, at a town called Gien, into the lands loyal to the Dauphin. Now they were safer. They could travel by day, on the roads, and stay at inns. And news of their coming spread ahead of them. People came out of their houses, to see the maiden from Lorraine, who was going to save France. Some of them believed in her. Others laughed. But everyone was curious.

On the last stage of the journey, they stopped at a little village, called Sainte Catherine de Fierbois, where there was a famous chapel, dedicated to Saint Catherine. Saint Catherine was one of Jeanne's saints. So Jeanne was very happy to be there. She went to mass three times, in one day, in the chapel. And she dictated a letter to the Dauphin, saying that she had travelled a long way to help him, and that she had many good things to tell him.

The next day, on the sixth of March, 1429, they rode into the town of Chinon, on the river Vienne. Above the town, on a high ridge, stood a great castle, with tall walls, and towers. It was one of the Dauphin's favourite castles. And the Dauphin was there, now. Jeanne looked up at the castle, and at the towers, against the sky. She had come so far. And now, at last, she was here.

The little group found an inn, in the town, at the foot of the castle. Jeanne washed, and rested, and prayed. And she waited, to be called. For a day or two, the Dauphin's advisers argued about whether he should see her at all. And then, one evening, word came. The Dauphin would receive her.
