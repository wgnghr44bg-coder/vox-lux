Jeanne's father is Jacques d'Arc. He is a farmer, about fifty years old. He owns about twenty hectares of land, fields, and meadows, and woods, around the village. That is quite a lot, for a peasant. He is not rich. But he is comfortable, and respected. He is one of the leaders of the village, and speaks for the villagers, when there is business with the lord, or the officials. He is a serious, careful, hard-working man.

Jeanne's mother is Isabelle. People call her Isabelle Romée, perhaps because she once made a pilgrimage to Rome, or to another holy place. She is a pious, gentle woman, who taught Jeanne her prayers. The Our Father, the Hail Mary, and the Creed. Jeanne said, later, that everything she knew about her faith, she learned from her mother. She could not read, or write. Neither could her mother. But she knew her prayers.

Jeanne has three brothers. Jacquemin, the eldest, who is married, and lives in a nearby village. Jean, and Pierre, who are a little older than her, and still live at home, and work on the farm with their father. Pierre is cheerful, and brave, and close to Jeanne. He will later go with her, on her great journey, and fight beside her. There was also a sister, Catherine, who married, and passed away young. Jeanne loved her very much.

The family's house stands next to the church, in the middle of the village. It is a solid stone house, with a steep roof, and thick walls, and small windows. Downstairs, there is a big room, with a great fireplace, where the family cooks, and eats, and sits in the evenings. There are a few smaller rooms, for sleeping. And behind the house, there is a garden, with vegetables, and herbs, and fruit trees, running down toward the church.

{img:girl-herding-cows-meadow-by-river-meuse-willows-morning-mist}
Jeanne's days are full of work. She helps her mother in the house. She spins wool, and flax, on her spindle, and her spinning wheel. She is very good at it. She said, later, that she was not afraid of any woman in Rouen, when it came to spinning, and sewing. She cooks, and cleans, and fetches water from the well. And she helps on the farm. She takes the cows, and the sheep, to the meadows, by the river. She helps with the harvest. She works in the garden.

When it is Jacques d'Arc's turn, in the village, to watch over the animals, the family's cows, and the neighbours' cows, are taken out together, to graze in the meadows. Then Jeanne sometimes goes too, and sits by the river, watching the animals, and spinning, and praying. It is a quiet, peaceful job. She likes it. She likes the river, and the meadows, and the silence, and the sound of the bells, drifting across the fields.

--

Jeanne's best friend is a girl called Hauviette. She lives next door, and is a few years younger. They have played together since they were small. They have run in the meadows, and picked flowers, and danced around the old tree in the woods, and slept in each other's houses. Hauviette loves Jeanne very much. Many years later, when she was an old woman, Hauviette said that Jeanne was good, and simple, and gentle, and that she often went to church, and gave to the poor.

{img:two-village-girls-friends-walking-arm-in-arm-lane-flowers-laughing}
Jeanne gives to the poor, whenever she can. When a poor traveller comes to the village, she asks her father to let him sleep in the house, by the fire, and she sleeps in the loft herself. She looks after the sick, in the village, and sits with them, and brings them food. When she has a little money, she gives it to the church, for masses, or to the poor. People say that she has a good heart.

She also loves the bells. In the evenings, when the church bell rings for the end of the day, Jeanne kneels down, wherever she is, in the field, or the garden, and says a prayer. Sometimes, the bell ringer forgets to ring, or rings too late. Then Jeanne scolds him, gently, and promises him some little cakes, if he remembers to ring on time. And he does.
