The war that has troubled France for so long is called, today, the Hundred Years' War. It actually lasted even longer than a hundred years, from 1337 to 1453. But it was not a hundred years of constant fighting. There were long periods of peace, or truce, and then fighting again. And it was not really a war between two countries, as we think of them today. It was a war between two royal families, both of whom claimed the throne of France.

The kings of England, in those days, were not quite English, as we would think of it. They were descended from French nobles, who had conquered England, three hundred years before. They spoke French, at their court. They owned great lands in France. And through their family, they believed that they had a right to be kings of France too. The French did not agree. And so the war began.

--

{img:french-countryside-medieval-burned-farm-abandoned-fields-ruins-quiet}
For the ordinary people of France, the war was a long misery. Armies marched back and forth across the country, for generations. Not just the English, and the French, but bands of soldiers, without pay, who robbed, and burned, and stole, wherever they went. Villages were burned. Fields were left empty. Many people fled to the towns, behind the walls. In some parts of France, whole regions were almost deserted.

In 1415, the English king, Henry the Fifth, won a great victory, at a place called Agincourt. Then he conquered Normandy, and much of the north of France. The duke of Burgundy, a powerful French prince, became his ally. And in 1420, the mad French king, Charles the Sixth, signed a treaty. It said that his own son, Charles, would never be king. Instead, King Henry of England would become king of France, after Charles the Sixth passed away.

{img:young-dauphin-charles-1428-castle-chinon-uncertain-face-plain-robes}
But in 1422, both King Henry, and King Charles, passed away, within a few weeks of each other. The new English king was Henry's son, Henry the Sixth, a baby, only a few months old. The English said that the baby was now king of England, and king of France. But many of the French said no. They said that the rightful king was Charles, the son of the old French king. They called him the Dauphin, which is the title of the heir to the French throne.

The Dauphin Charles was nineteen, in 1422. He was not a strong, or brave, or impressive young man. He was thin, and nervous, and uncertain. He had never been crowned. The kings of France were always crowned in the great cathedral of Reims. But Reims was deep in enemy territory, in the hands of the English, and the Burgundians. So Charles could not go there. And without a coronation, many people did not believe he was a real king.

--

So, by 1428, France was divided in two. In the north, the English, and the Burgundians. In the south, the Dauphin, and his supporters. In between, along the river Loire, the great city of Orléans, which was still loyal to the Dauphin. It was the key to the south. If the English took it, they could cross the Loire, and march into the heart of the Dauphin's lands.

{img:orleans-1428-walled-city-loire-bridge-towers-english-forts-around}
In October 1428, the English came to Orléans, with an army, and surrounded it. They built forts, around the city, and blocked the roads, and the river. The people of Orléans shut their gates, and prepared to defend themselves. And the whole of France waited, to see what would happen.

Domrémy was a long way from Orléans. Hundreds of kilometres, to the east. But the news travelled, slowly, from village to village, along the roads, with merchants, and pilgrims, and soldiers. And in Domrémy, too, people talked about Orléans, and the English, and the Dauphin. And Jeanne listened. And she prayed.
