After Poitiers, Jeanne went to the city of Tours, on the Loire, to prepare for the journey to Orléans. The Dauphin gave her a household, of her own, like a knight, or a captain. A squire, called Jean d'Aulon, a sensible, brave, older soldier, who would look after her. Two pages, young boys, to carry her things, and help her with her armour. A chaplain, a young friar called Jean Pasquerel, who would hear her confessions, and say mass for her. And messengers, and servants.

Her brothers, Jean and Pierre, came from Domrémy, to join her. She was very happy to see them. They brought news from home, from her parents, and the village, and Hauviette. And they stayed with her, as part of her household, all through the months that followed. Pierre especially was always close to her side.

--

The Dauphin ordered a suit of armour for her. It was made by the best armourer in Tours. A full suit of plate armour, made of polished steel, fitted exactly to her body. Breastplate, and backplate, and arm pieces, and leg pieces, and gauntlets for her hands, and a helmet. It was white armour, which means that it was plain, and polished, and shining, without paint, or decoration. It was beautiful. When she wore it, she shone like silver, in the sun.

Jeanne needed a sword, too. Her voices told her that there was a sword, buried behind the altar, in the chapel of Saint Catherine at Fierbois, where she had prayed, on her way to Chinon. She sent a messenger there, to look for it. The priests dug behind the altar, and found an old sword, rusty, with five crosses on the blade. When they cleaned it, the rust fell away easily. They sent it to Jeanne, with a fine scabbard. People said it was a miracle.

{img:painters-workshop-making-white-banner-lilies-figure-of-christ-angels}
But Jeanne's favourite thing was not her sword. It was her banner. She had it made by a painter, in Tours, following the instructions of her voices. It was a long banner, of white linen, with a fringe of silk. On it, there was a picture of Christ, sitting in judgement, holding the world, with an angel on each side. And the names, Jesus, Maria. And the background was scattered with golden lilies, the flowers of the French kings.

Jeanne said, later, that she loved her banner forty times more than her sword. She carried it herself, in her hand, into every battle, so that she would never have to use her sword, against anyone. She said that she never killed anyone. She carried the banner, high, so that the soldiers could see it, and follow it. And they did.

On the day she first put on her armour, and mounted her horse, and took her banner in her hand, the people of Tours came out, in crowds, to see her. She rode through the streets, on a great white horse, in her shining armour, with her banner flying. People cheered, and wept, and crossed themselves. They said that she looked like an angel. Or like Saint Michael himself, come down from heaven.

Jeanne was still a peasant girl, from Domrémy, at heart. She was still simple, and humble, and kind. She still prayed, many times a day. She still went to mass, every morning. She still gave to the poor. She did not like the fine clothes, and the attention, very much. But she knew that she must play her part. The soldiers, and the people, needed something to believe in. And she was ready to give it to them.

At Blois, a town on the Loire, an army was gathering, to take supplies of food to Orléans. Jeanne went there, to join it. She was horrified by the soldiers. They swore, and drank, and gambled, and fought, and behaved very badly. So she told them to stop. She told them to go to confession, and to mass, and to stop swearing. And, to everyone's amazement, many of them did. The priests marched at the head of the army, singing hymns. It was the strangest army anyone had ever seen.
