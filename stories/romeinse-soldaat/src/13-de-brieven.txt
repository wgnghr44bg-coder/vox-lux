Roman soldiers wrote letters. Many of them could read and write, at least a little. And those who could not, asked a friend to write for them. They wrote to their families at home, to friends in other forts, and to the officers. Most of these letters have been lost long ago. But in one place in Britain, by a lucky chance, hundreds of them have survived.

--

The place is called Vindolanda. It was a Roman fort in the north of Britain, near where Hadrian's Wall would later be built. In the 1970s, archaeologists digging there found something extraordinary. In the wet, muddy ground, where there was no air, they found hundreds of thin wooden tablets, about the size of a postcard. They were covered with writing in ink. They were letters, lists and notes, written by the soldiers and their families, around the years 90 to 120.

These are some of the oldest handwritten documents ever found in Britain. And they tell us things about the daily lives of Roman soldiers that no history book ever mentioned. They are not about great battles or emperors. They are about ordinary things. Shopping lists. Requests for leave. Complaints about the roads. And birthday parties.

{img:roman-woman-writing-letter-birthday-invitation-wooden-tablet-garden}
The most famous of the Vindolanda letters is a birthday invitation. It was written by a woman called Claudia Severa, the wife of an officer at another fort. She writes to her friend Sulpicia Lepidina, the wife of the commander at Vindolanda. She invites her to her birthday party, on the eleventh of September. She writes that the day will be much more enjoyable if her friend is there. And at the end, in her own handwriting, she adds, farewell, sister, my dearest soul.

It is the oldest known writing by a woman in Latin. And it is so warm and so ordinary. Two friends, at the cold edge of the empire, far from home, keeping in touch and inviting each other to parties. Reading it, we suddenly feel very close to them, even after almost two thousand years.

--

{img:soldier-opening-parcel-woollen-socks-sandals-barracks-delighted}
Another letter was written to a soldier by someone at home. It says, I have sent you pairs of socks, two pairs of sandals and two pairs of underpants. In the cold, wet north of Britain, socks were very welcome. The soldiers from the warm south must have been very grateful. Some people at home clearly worried that their sons and brothers were cold.

Other letters are about everyday problems. A soldier asks his officer for leave, to visit somewhere. Someone complains that the roads are very bad, and that he does not want to damage his carts. A list counts the soldiers in a unit, and says how many are sick, or away on duties. One famous note describes the local Britons, using a rude little nickname, Brittunculi, which means something like the wretched little Britons.

{img:soldier-writing-letter-home-stylus-wax-tablet-lamp-barracks-night}
Lucius writes letters too. He writes home to his family in Nemausus. He tells them about the long journey, the sea crossing, the fortress and his friends. He tells them about the green hills and the rain. He tells them not to worry. He does not know if the letters arrive. They travel for weeks, with traders and messengers, across the sea and through Gaul.

--

And one day, after almost a year, a letter arrives for him. It is from his mother. She writes that everyone is well. His sister is going to be married. The olive harvest was good. His grandfather sends his greetings, and says he is proud. And there is a small package with the letter. Inside, there are two pairs of thick woollen socks. Lucius laughs out loud. Then he reads the letter again, three times. And that night, he puts on the socks, and sleeps with warm feet for the first time since he came to Britain.

Writing letters is not always easy. Paper is rare and expensive. So most soldiers write on thin slices of wood, with ink, or on wooden tablets covered with wax, with a pointed stylus. The wax can be smoothed out and used again. Quintus helps the soldiers who cannot write well. He writes their letters for them in the evenings, by lamplight, while they tell him what to say. In return, they buy him a cup of wine.
