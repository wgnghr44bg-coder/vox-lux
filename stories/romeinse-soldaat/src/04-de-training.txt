The training of a Roman recruit lasts about four months. It is very hard. The Romans believe that a well-trained soldier is worth ten untrained ones. And so the recruits train every day, from morning until evening, in all kinds of weather. A Roman writer later said that their training was like a battle without blood, and their battles were like training with blood.

--

The first thing the recruits learn is to march. They must learn to walk in step, in neat rows, all moving together. They march for hours, round and round the parade ground. Then they march along the roads. Three times a month, they must march about thirty kilometres in five hours, carrying a heavy pack. At first, Lucius's feet are covered in blisters. But slowly, his legs get stronger, and his feet get harder.

Then they learn to fight. But not with real weapons, at first. They are given wooden swords and shields made of woven willow branches. These are twice as heavy as real ones. So when the recruits later use real weapons, they feel light and easy. They practise against a wooden post, as tall as a man, set in the ground. They strike at it again and again, for hours, learning to attack and to protect themselves with the shield.

{img:recruits-swimming-river-roman-training-officer-watching-summer}
The recruits also learn to swim. Many rivers have no bridges, and the army must be able to cross them. So the recruits swim in the river near the camp, in the summer, while the instructors shout from the bank. Lucius is a good swimmer. As a boy, he swam in the river near Nemausus every summer. But some of the other recruits have never swum before. Lucius helps them, holding them up in the water until they learn.

They learn to throw the javelin, called the pilum, at a target. They learn to jump and to climb. They learn to ride a horse, and to get on and off quickly, even in full armour. They learn to dig ditches and build walls of earth. And they learn to obey orders instantly, without thinking. When the trumpet sounds, they must react at once.

--

{img:roman-recruits-forming-shield-wall-testudo-practice-dust-sunlight}
One of the most important things they learn is to fight together, in formation. The soldiers stand close together, shoulder to shoulder, with their big shields touching. They move forward together, as one. They learn the famous tortoise formation, where the soldiers hold their shields over their heads and on all sides, like the shell of a tortoise. Then arrows and stones just bounce off.

The instructors are tough and strict. They shout a lot. If a recruit is lazy or makes a mistake, he must do extra work, or stand guard all night. If he does very badly, he gets barley bread instead of wheat bread, which is a great shame. But the instructors also want the recruits to succeed. And when a recruit does well, they nod and say a short word of praise. It means more than any reward.

{img:tired-recruits-evening-campfire-rubbing-sore-legs-laughing-together}
In the evenings, the recruits are exhausted. They eat their supper and fall onto their beds. Their muscles ache everywhere. But they are also proud of themselves. Every day, they are a little stronger, a little faster and a little better. And they are becoming friends. They help each other, joke together and complain together about the instructors.

--

At the end of four months, the training is finished. The recruits are now real soldiers. They receive their real weapons and armour. And they are told where they will serve. Lucius has been assigned to the Second Legion, called Augusta, in the province of Britain. Britain! At the very edge of the world. Lucius has heard that it is cold and wet and foggy there. He swallows. But he holds his head high, and says nothing.

On the last evening of their training, the recruits sit together around a fire. They are tired, but proud. They talk about where they hope to be sent. Some want to go to the east, to the great cities of Syria. Some want to stay close to home, on the Rhine. Lucius does not mind where he goes. He only hopes that his new friends will be sent to the same place. And, as it turns out, some of them are.
