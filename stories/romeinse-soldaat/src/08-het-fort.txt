A Roman legionary fortress is like a small town, built for about five thousand soldiers. The fortress at Isca covers about twenty hectares. It has the shape of a playing card, a rectangle with rounded corners. Around it, there is a strong wall, a ditch, and a gate in the middle of each side. All Roman forts, everywhere in the empire, are built to a similar plan. A soldier who moves from one fort to another always knows his way around.

--

Two main streets cross the fortress. One runs from the front gate to the back gate. The other runs from side to side. Where they meet, in the middle of the fortress, there is the most important building. It is called the principia, the headquarters. It has a big courtyard, surrounded by covered walkways, and a great hall, where the commander speaks to the officers.

At the back of the headquarters, there is a small, sacred room. It is called the shrine of the standards. Here, the golden eagle of the legion is kept, together with the other standards, and a statue of the emperor. It is the holiest place in the whole fortress. A guard stands in front of it, day and night. Below the shrine, there is a strongroom, where the soldiers' savings are kept safe.

{img:principia-courtyard-headquarters-columns-officers-clerks-morning}
Next to the headquarters, there is the house of the commander, the legate. It is a big, comfortable house, like the house of a rich man in Italy, with many rooms around a garden courtyard, mosaic floors, painted walls and underfloor heating. The legate is a senator from Rome. He usually stays only a few years, and then goes back to Rome to continue his career. He brings his wife and children, and many servants.

Most of the fortress is filled with barracks. Long, narrow buildings, in neat rows. Each barrack block houses one century, eighty men. It is divided into ten pairs of rooms, one pair for each contubernium. At one end of the block, there are the larger rooms of the centurion, where he lives with his family and servants. Lucius's barrack block is near the western wall, close to the bakery ovens.

--

{img:granary-raised-floor-stone-buttresses-soldiers-carrying-grain-sacks}
There are many other buildings. Granaries, with raised floors and thick walls, to keep the grain dry and safe from mice. A hospital, with small rooms for the sick, around a quiet garden. Workshops, where blacksmiths, carpenters and leather workers make and repair equipment. Stables for the horses and mules. Storehouses for food, weapons and tools. And along the inside of the walls, there are rows of bread ovens, where the soldiers bake their bread.

Outside the walls, there is the great bathhouse, which we will visit later. And there is something special. An amphitheatre, an oval arena with seats all around it, for about six thousand people. Here, the soldiers practise their drills, and hold parades and ceremonies. Sometimes, there are shows and games for the soldiers and the people of the town.

{img:amphitheatre-caerleon-oval-arena-grass-seats-soldiers-drilling}
Around the fortress, a town has grown up. It is full of the families of the soldiers, and of traders, craftsmen and innkeepers. Officially, ordinary soldiers are not allowed to marry. But many of them have wives and children anyway, who live in the town outside the walls. When the soldiers retire, they marry officially, and their children become Roman citizens.

--

Lucius likes the fortress. It is well organised and clean. Everything has its place. There is fresh water from pipes, toilets that are flushed with running water, and hot baths. It is more comfortable than many towns. In the evening, he walks along the wall with Quintus, and looks out over the river and the green hills. The light is soft and golden. A boat is drifting down the river. And Lucius thinks that perhaps Britain is not so bad after all.

There is also a hospital in the fortress, with doctors and orderlies. The Roman army doctors are very skilled. They know how to set broken bones, clean wounds and treat fevers. They use herbs, honey, vinegar and wine to keep wounds clean. They have fine instruments of bronze and iron, many of which look surprisingly like modern ones. A sick soldier in a Roman fortress is better cared for than most people in the empire.
