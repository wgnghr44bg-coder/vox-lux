Tonight we travel back almost nineteen hundred years, to the time around the year 120. We go to the far north of the Roman Empire, to a land of green hills, grey skies and soft rain. It is Britain. And on a low hill, beside a little stream, there is a Roman fort, where several hundred soldiers are just waking up.

[long-pause]

It is early morning. A cool mist lies in the valleys. The fort is square, with stone walls and a gate in the middle of each side. Above the gates, there are wooden towers, where sentries stand watch, wrapped in their cloaks. Inside the walls, there are long, neat rows of buildings with red tiled roofs. Smoke is rising from the bakery ovens. A trumpet sounds. And all over the fort, men are getting up from their beds.

[pause]

Outside the gates, there is a little village. Its houses are made of wood, with thatched roofs. Here live the wives and children of the soldiers, and shopkeepers, craftsmen and traders. A woman is opening the shutters of a small tavern. A dog is barking. A cart loaded with barrels is rolling slowly up the road toward the fort. And beyond the village, the green hills stretch away into the mist, toward the wild lands of the north.

[pause]

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

{img:young-roman-legionary-tunic-cloak-standing-barracks-doorway-morning}
Tonight, we will follow the life of one Roman soldier. His name is Lucius. He is eighteen years old when our story begins. He comes from the warm south of Gaul, the land that is now France, where olive trees and vineyards grow in the sun. He has just joined the Roman army, as a legionary. And he will serve for twenty-five years.

[pause]

We will see how he becomes a soldier. How he trains, what he wears and what he carries. We will meet the seven friends who share his tent and his barrack room. We will follow him on the long journey north, to the edge of the empire, in Britain. And we will see what daily life was like in a Roman fort, from the morning trumpet to the evening watch.

[pause]

{img:roman-soldiers-marching-road-green-hills-shields-packs-distance}
We will see him march along the straight Roman roads, and build a camp for the night. We will visit the bathhouse, the bakery and the little temple. We will read some of the letters that real soldiers and their families wrote to each other, almost two thousand years ago. And we will watch him help build one of the most famous walls in the world, across the whole width of Britain.

[long-pause]

Being a Roman soldier was sometimes dangerous. There were wars and battles. But we will look at those only from a distance. Most of a soldier's life was not spent fighting. It was spent training, building, marching, guarding, cooking, mending and waiting. It was a life of routine, of hard work and of friendship. And on the quiet northern frontier, around the year 120, it was a life that was mostly peaceful.

[pause]

{img:sunrise-over-roman-fort-hills-britain-golden-light-sentry-tower}
So let us begin. Not yet in Britain, but far away in the south, in a sunny town in Gaul, on a spring morning. A young man is standing in front of a Roman officer, nervous, with his heart beating fast. He wants to become a soldier of Rome. And his long journey is about to begin.

[pause]

On the parade ground, a centurion is already walking up and down, looking at the sky. He is an old soldier, with grey hair and a weathered face. He has seen many mornings like this, in many lands. He sniffs the air, and decides it will rain later. He is almost always right. Then he turns and walks toward the barracks, where his men are pulling on their boots and buckling their belts.

[long-pause]

{img:roman-empire-120ad-feeling-legions-across-many-lands-roads-forts}
Around the year 120, the Roman Empire is at its greatest. It stretches from Britain in the north to Egypt in the south, and from Spain in the west to Mesopotamia in the east. About sixty million people live inside its borders. They speak many languages, and worship many gods. And all of them are protected by the Roman army.

[long-pause]

The emperor at this time is called Hadrian. He has just come to power, in the year 117. He is a thoughtful, restless man, about forty years old, with a short beard. He was the first emperor to wear a beard, and soon all the fashionable men of Rome copied him. Hadrian loves Greek culture, architecture and poetry. And he has decided that the empire is big enough. He does not want to conquer new lands. He wants to protect the lands Rome already has.

[pause]

So the army's main job, in Hadrian's time, is to guard the borders. Along the edges of the empire, there is a long line of forts, watchtowers, roads and walls. Soldiers live in these forts all their lives. They watch the frontier, patrol the roads, collect taxes, build bridges and keep the peace. It is a long, quiet, careful job.

[pause]

{img:roman-legion-on-parade-standards-eagle-rows-of-soldiers-sunlight}
The Roman army has two main kinds of soldiers. First, there are the legionaries. They are the heart of the army. To become a legionary, you must be a Roman citizen. There are about thirty legions in the whole empire. Each legion has about five thousand men. They are heavy infantry, well trained, and well equipped. They are also the best builders and engineers in the world.

[pause]

Second, there are the auxiliaries. They are soldiers from the conquered peoples of the empire, who are not yet Roman citizens. They come from many lands. Spain, Gaul, Germany, Syria, North Africa. Some are archers, some are horsemen, some are foot soldiers. They serve for twenty-five years. And when they retire, they receive a great reward. They become Roman citizens, and so do their children.

[long-pause]

{img:centurion-crest-helmet-vine-stick-inspecting-soldiers-line}
A legion is divided into smaller groups. The smallest group is the contubernium, eight men who share a tent and a room. Ten of these groups make a century, eighty men, led by an officer called a centurion. Six centuries make a cohort. And ten cohorts make a legion. At the head of the legion, there is a commander, called a legate. And the symbol of the legion is a golden eagle on a pole, which the soldiers guard with their lives.

[pause]

The centurions are the most important officers. They are tough, experienced soldiers, who have risen from the ranks. They wear a helmet with a crest from side to side, so their men can see them. And they carry a stick made of vine wood, as a sign of their rank. Some centurions are kind and fair. Some are very strict. Every soldier hopes for a good centurion.

[pause]

{img:roman-soldiers-building-road-stones-levelling-engineers-countryside}
Roman soldiers do not only fight. In fact, they spend much more time building. They build forts, walls, roads, bridges, aqueducts and canals. Many of the roads they built are still used today, or still lie under modern roads. A Roman legion is like a moving town, with carpenters, blacksmiths, surveyors, doctors and clerks. Everything the army needs, it can make itself.

[long-pause]

In Britain, around the year 120, there are three legions. One is at Isca, in the west, near what is now Wales. One is at Deva, which is now the city of Chester. And one is at Eboracum, which is now York. Between them and around them, there are dozens of smaller forts, with auxiliary soldiers. Altogether, about fifty thousand soldiers guard the province of Britain. It is one of the most heavily guarded parts of the whole empire.

[long-pause]

{img:nemausus-roman-town-gaul-temple-columns-olive-trees-sunny-street}
Lucius was born in a town called Nemausus, in the south of Gaul. Today it is the French city of Nîmes. It is a beautiful Roman town, with a fine temple, a big amphitheatre, and an aqueduct that brings fresh water from the hills. Around the town, there are olive groves, vineyards and fields of wheat. The sun shines almost every day, and the air smells of herbs.

[long-pause]

Lucius's family are Roman citizens. His grandfather was a soldier, who served in the legions and was given land near Nemausus when he retired. His father is a potter. He makes jars, bowls and lamps in a little workshop near the town gate. Lucius has grown up there, playing in the clay, helping with the kiln and listening to his grandfather's stories about the army.

[pause]

His grandfather told him about faraway lands. About the cold forests of Germany, the deserts of Africa, the mountains of Spain. About marching for days along straight roads, and building camps in the wilderness. About friendship around the campfire. And about the day he retired, with his savings and his own piece of land. Lucius listened with shining eyes. He decided, as a little boy, that he would be a soldier too.

[pause]

{img:young-man-before-roman-officer-recruitment-table-scribe-writing}
Now he is eighteen. One spring morning, he walks to the office where new soldiers are recruited. He carries a letter of recommendation from an important man of the town, a friend of his father. Without such a letter, it is hard to join the legions. Behind a table sits a Roman officer, with a scribe beside him. The officer reads the letter. Then he looks Lucius up and down.

[pause]

First, Lucius must prove that he is a Roman citizen, free and born of free parents. Then comes the examination. The officer measures his height. A legionary should be tall, at least about one metre seventy. Lucius is just tall enough. A doctor looks at his eyes, his teeth and his feet. He must be healthy, with good eyesight. The officer asks him if he can read and write. Lucius says yes, a little. That is good. The army needs men who can read.

[long-pause]

{img:recruits-swearing-oath-standards-raised-hands-roman-parade-ground}
Lucius passes the examination. His name is written down on a list. He is given a small lead tag, with his name on it, to wear on a cord around his neck. And he receives some money for his journey to the legion, which is called viaticum. From this moment, he belongs to the army.

[pause]

A few weeks later, together with the other new recruits, he swears the military oath. It is called the sacramentum. The recruits stand in front of the standards of the legion, and raise their right hands. They swear by the gods to obey the emperor, to follow their officers, and never to leave their post. The oath is very serious. The Romans believe that the gods themselves will punish anyone who breaks it.

[pause]

{img:mother-saying-goodbye-son-roman-house-doorway-bundle-morning-sun}
Before he leaves, Lucius goes home one last time. His mother cries. She knows she may not see him for many years. His father gives him a small clay lamp, made in his own workshop, with a picture of a ship on it. His little sister gives him a woollen scarf she has woven herself. And his old grandfather, who can hardly walk now, gives him something very precious. His own old army belt buckle, made of bronze, which he has kept for forty years.

[long-pause]

Lucius leaves Nemausus on a bright spring morning, with a group of other recruits. They walk north along the great Roman road, toward the camp where they will be trained. Lucius looks back once, at the town on its hills, at the temple shining in the sun. Then he turns and walks on. Twenty-five years is a very long time. But he is young, and the world is wide, and he is not afraid.

[pause]

That evening, Lucius sits with his grandfather in the little garden behind the house, under the fig tree. The old man holds his hand. He says that the army will be hard, but that it will also give him the best friends of his life. He says that Lucius must always look after his feet, always obey his centurion, and always write to his mother. Lucius promises. And the old man smiles, and pats his hand.

[long-pause]

{img:roman-recruits-training-wooden-swords-wicker-shields-wooden-posts-field}
The training of a Roman recruit lasts about four months. It is very hard. The Romans believe that a well-trained soldier is worth ten untrained ones. And so the recruits train every day, from morning until evening, in all kinds of weather. A Roman writer later said that their training was like a battle without blood, and their battles were like training with blood.

[long-pause]

The first thing the recruits learn is to march. They must learn to walk in step, in neat rows, all moving together. They march for hours, round and round the parade ground. Then they march along the roads. Three times a month, they must march about thirty kilometres in five hours, carrying a heavy pack. At first, Lucius's feet are covered in blisters. But slowly, his legs get stronger, and his feet get harder.

[pause]

Then they learn to fight. But not with real weapons, at first. They are given wooden swords and shields made of woven willow branches. These are twice as heavy as real ones. So when the recruits later use real weapons, they feel light and easy. They practise against a wooden post, as tall as a man, set in the ground. They strike at it again and again, for hours, learning to attack and to protect themselves with the shield.

[pause]

{img:recruits-swimming-river-roman-training-officer-watching-summer}
The recruits also learn to swim. Many rivers have no bridges, and the army must be able to cross them. So the recruits swim in the river near the camp, in the summer, while the instructors shout from the bank. Lucius is a good swimmer. As a boy, he swam in the river near Nemausus every summer. But some of the other recruits have never swum before. Lucius helps them, holding them up in the water until they learn.

[pause]

They learn to throw the javelin, called the pilum, at a target. They learn to jump and to climb. They learn to ride a horse, and to get on and off quickly, even in full armour. They learn to dig ditches and build walls of earth. And they learn to obey orders instantly, without thinking. When the trumpet sounds, they must react at once.

[long-pause]

{img:roman-recruits-forming-shield-wall-testudo-practice-dust-sunlight}
One of the most important things they learn is to fight together, in formation. The soldiers stand close together, shoulder to shoulder, with their big shields touching. They move forward together, as one. They learn the famous tortoise formation, where the soldiers hold their shields over their heads and on all sides, like the shell of a tortoise. Then arrows and stones just bounce off.

[pause]

The instructors are tough and strict. They shout a lot. If a recruit is lazy or makes a mistake, he must do extra work, or stand guard all night. If he does very badly, he gets barley bread instead of wheat bread, which is a great shame. But the instructors also want the recruits to succeed. And when a recruit does well, they nod and say a short word of praise. It means more than any reward.

[pause]

{img:tired-recruits-evening-campfire-rubbing-sore-legs-laughing-together}
In the evenings, the recruits are exhausted. They eat their supper and fall onto their beds. Their muscles ache everywhere. But they are also proud of themselves. Every day, they are a little stronger, a little faster and a little better. And they are becoming friends. They help each other, joke together and complain together about the instructors.

[long-pause]

At the end of four months, the training is finished. The recruits are now real soldiers. They receive their real weapons and armour. And they are told where they will serve. Lucius has been assigned to the Second Legion, called Augusta, in the province of Britain. Britain! At the very edge of the world. Lucius has heard that it is cold and wet and foggy there. He swallows. But he holds his head high, and says nothing.

[pause]

On the last evening of their training, the recruits sit together around a fire. They are tired, but proud. They talk about where they hope to be sent. Some want to go to the east, to the great cities of Syria. Some want to stay close to home, on the Rhine. Lucius does not mind where he goes. He only hopes that his new friends will be sent to the same place. And, as it turns out, some of them are.

[long-pause]

{img:roman-legionary-full-kit-segmented-armour-helmet-shield-standing-calm}
When Lucius becomes a real soldier, he receives his equipment. Some of it is given to him by the army. But he must pay for it himself, out of his wages, a little each month. So he takes very good care of everything. A Roman soldier's equipment is heavy, well made and very practical. Let us look at it, piece by piece.

[long-pause]

First, his clothes. He wears a tunic of wool, red or off-white, which comes down to his knees. In the cold north, he will wear two tunics, one on top of the other, and woollen trousers underneath, like the local people. Over his shoulders, he wears a thick woollen cloak, fastened with a brooch. The cloak is also his blanket at night. And around his waist, he wears a belt, decorated with metal plates, with a little apron of leather strips hanging down in front, which jingle as he walks.

[pause]

On his feet, he wears the famous soldier's boots, called caligae. They are a kind of heavy sandal, made of leather, with many straps, and with iron nails in the soles. The nails make them last longer, and grip the ground. When a group of soldiers marches on a stone road, you can hear the crunch of their boots from far away. In winter, the soldiers stuff the boots with wool, or wear socks underneath.

[pause]

{img:caligae-hobnailed-roman-boots-close-leather-straps-stone-road}
For armour, Lucius has a body armour made of strips of iron, held together with leather straps and little bronze hooks. It covers his chest, his back and his shoulders. It is flexible, so he can move and bend easily. But it is heavy, about nine kilos. It takes some practice to put on and take off. The soldiers help each other with the buckles. The armour must be kept oiled and polished, or it rusts in the wet British air.

[pause]

On his head, he wears an iron helmet, with a wide guard at the back to protect his neck, and two hinged plates on the sides to protect his cheeks. There is a ridge across the front, to protect his face from blows. Inside, the helmet is lined with felt, to make it comfortable. On parade days, the soldiers fix a crest of horsehair on top.

[long-pause]

{img:roman-shield-curved-red-painted-designs-gladius-pilum-display}
His shield is big and curved, like a section of a tube. It is about a metre high, made of thin layers of wood glued together, covered with leather, and painted red. On the front, there is a design, the same for the whole legion, often with lightning bolts or wings. In the middle, there is a round metal boss, which protects the hand. When marching, the shield is protected from the rain by a leather cover.

[pause]

His weapons are a short sword, called the gladius, which hangs at his right side. A dagger at his left side. And two javelins, called pila. A pilum has a long, thin iron point, attached to a wooden shaft. It is designed so that the iron bends when it hits something, so the enemy cannot throw it back. Lucius keeps his sword very sharp, and very clean.

[pause]

{img:soldier-pack-on-pole-furca-cooking-pot-tools-bag-marching}
And then there is the pack. On the march, a Roman soldier carries everything he needs on a pole over his shoulder, called a furca. On the pole hang a leather bag with his clothes, a cooking pot, a water bottle, a small sack of grain for three days, a cloak bag, and tools. Every soldier carries a tool for digging and building. A pickaxe, a spade, a saw or a basket for carrying earth. The whole load can weigh more than thirty kilos.

[long-pause]

Because of these heavy loads, the soldiers were sometimes called Marius's mules, after a famous general who made them carry all their own equipment. The soldiers did not mind the joke very much. They were proud of their strength. And Lucius, after a few months, carries his heavy pack as if it were nothing at all. He even whistles as he marches.

[long-pause]

{img:eight-roman-soldiers-around-campfire-tent-evening-friends-talking}
In the Roman army, the most important group is the smallest one. It is the contubernium, the eight men who share a tent on the march, and a room in the barracks. They eat together, sleep together, work together and fight together. They are like brothers. And for twenty-five years, they will be the closest thing Lucius has to a family.

[long-pause]

The eight men also share a mule. The mule carries their leather tent, their cooking equipment and their millstone for grinding grain. It is a patient, grumpy animal, with long ears and a loud voice. Lucius's group has called it Cicero, after a famous Roman speaker who talked too much. Cicero the mule does not like to be hurried. But he is strong, and he never gets lost.

[pause]

The leader of Lucius's group is a soldier called Titus. He is about thirty-five years old, and has served for fifteen years. He has a scar on his chin and a deep, calm voice. He has been to Germany and to Africa. He is not an officer, but everyone listens to him. He teaches the young soldiers how to survive. How to keep your feet dry. How to light a fire in the rain. How to keep out of trouble with the centurion.

[pause]

{img:veteran-soldier-scar-chin-teaching-young-soldier-sharpen-sword}
There is Marcus, a big, cheerful man from Spain, who laughs at everything and is always hungry. There is Quintus, from Italy, a thin, serious young man, who can read and write very well, and wants to become a clerk. There is Felix, from North Africa, with dark skin and a beautiful singing voice. And there are two brothers from Gaul, Sextus and Gnaeus, who are always arguing and always together.

[pause]

The eighth man is a quiet soldier called Aulus. He is from Syria, at the other end of the empire. He has been a soldier for ten years. He does not talk much about himself. But he knows a lot about medicine and herbs. When someone in the group is sick or hurt, Aulus looks after him. He carries a little box of herbs and ointments in his pack.

[long-pause]

{img:roman-barracks-room-bunks-shields-on-wall-soldiers-relaxing}
In the barracks, the eight men share two small rooms. The front room is for their equipment. Armour, shields, weapons and packs are hung on the walls and stored on shelves. The back room is where they sleep. There are bunk beds along the walls, with straw mattresses and woollen blankets. There is a small hearth, or a brazier, for warmth and cooking. And a little window, high up, which lets in some light.

[pause]

It is crowded. Eight grown men, with all their things, in two small rooms. It smells of leather, wool, smoke and sweat. There is no privacy at all. But the men get used to it. They learn to put up with each other's habits. Marcus snores. Quintus reads by lamplight late at night. The two brothers argue in the middle of the night. And Felix sings in the morning, whether anyone wants him to or not.

[pause]

{img:soldiers-sharing-meal-barracks-bowls-bread-lamp-laughter}
They do everything together. They cook their meals together, taking turns. They clean their equipment together. They play dice together in the evenings. When one of them is ill, the others take over his duties. When one of them gets a letter from home, he reads it aloud to the others. And when one of them is sad, the others notice, and find a way to cheer him up.

[long-pause]

Lucius is the youngest in the group. At first, the others tease him a lot. They call him puer, the boy. They give him the worst jobs, like cleaning the cooking pot and fetching water. But Lucius does not complain. He works hard, and learns quickly. And after a few months, they stop calling him the boy. Titus puts a hand on his shoulder one evening, and says that he is one of them now. Lucius feels warm all over.

[pause]

In the evenings, when the work is done, the eight men often sit outside their barrack door, on a wooden bench, in the last light of the day. They watch the other soldiers come and go. They talk about the day, about home and about the future. Quintus wants to become a clerk. Marcus wants to open a tavern. Felix wants to see Rome. And Lucius, when they ask him, says that he just wants to see everything.

[long-pause]

{img:roman-soldiers-marching-north-gaul-straight-road-poplar-trees-spring}
The journey to Britain is long. Lucius and the other new soldiers for the British legions travel together, under the command of a centurion. They march north through Gaul, along the great Roman roads. Every day, they march about twenty-five kilometres. At night, they sleep in army posts along the road, or in their tents. It takes them more than a month to cross the whole of Gaul.

[long-pause]

Along the way, Lucius sees the country change. In the south, there were olive trees and vineyards, and the sun was hot. As they go north, the olive trees disappear. The land becomes greener and flatter. There are big forests of oak and beech, and wide fields of wheat. The weather becomes cooler, and it rains more often. The houses have steeper roofs. And the people speak with different accents.

[pause]

They pass through big cities. Lugdunum, which is now Lyon, the great capital of Gaul, built where two rivers meet. Durocortorum, now Reims, with its wide streets and triumphal arch. And many smaller towns, with their markets and temples and baths. In each town, the soldiers rest for a night, and spend a little of their money in the taverns.

[pause]

{img:roman-lighthouse-gesoriacum-coast-ships-harbour-grey-sea-cliffs}
At last, they reach the coast, at a port called Gesoriacum, which is now Boulogne, in France. Here, the Roman fleet of Britain has its base. There is a busy harbour, full of ships. And on a hill above the harbour, there is a tall lighthouse, built by the emperor Caligula. At night, a great fire burns at the top, to guide the ships across the sea. Lucius has never seen the sea so grey and wild.

[pause]

On a clear day, from the cliffs, you can just see a pale line on the horizon. The white cliffs of Britain. Lucius stands on the cliff for a long time, looking at it. To the Romans of the south, Britain was a strange, mysterious island, at the very edge of the world. Beyond it, they believed, there was only the endless ocean.

[long-pause]

{img:roman-transport-ship-crossing-channel-soldiers-on-deck-waves-sails}
They cross the sea in a big transport ship, with a square sail and a team of rowers. The crossing takes most of a day. The sea is rough, and the ship rolls and pitches. Many of the soldiers are seasick, leaning over the side. Lucius feels sick too, but he holds on, staring at the horizon, as the sailors told him to. Marcus, the big Spaniard, is the sickest of all. He says he would rather fight ten battles than cross the sea again.

[pause]

In the evening, they arrive at Dubris, which is now Dover, under the white cliffs. There is another lighthouse there, on the cliff top, answering the one in Gaul. Lucius steps off the ship onto the stone quay. His legs are still wobbly. It is raining softly. The air smells of salt and seaweed and wood smoke. He is in Britain.

[pause]

{img:soldiers-arriving-dover-quay-white-cliffs-rain-lighthouse-evening}
From Dubris, they march west, across the south of Britain, for about two weeks. They pass through Londinium, which is now London. It is already a big, busy city, with a long wooden bridge over the river Thames, a forum, a basilica and warehouses full of goods from all over the empire. Then they march on, through the green countryside, past farms and villas and small towns.

[long-pause]

At last, they reach the fortress of the Second Legion Augusta, at a place called Isca. Today it is called Caerleon, in Wales. It lies on a river, surrounded by green hills. As they march through the great gate, the soldiers of the legion come out to watch the new arrivals. Some of them shout jokes. Some of them nod in welcome. And Lucius looks around him at the long streets and the stone buildings, and thinks that this will be his home.

[long-pause]

{img:legionary-fortress-isca-caerleon-playing-card-shape-walls-river-hills}
A Roman legionary fortress is like a small town, built for about five thousand soldiers. The fortress at Isca covers about twenty hectares. It has the shape of a playing card, a rectangle with rounded corners. Around it, there is a strong wall, a ditch, and a gate in the middle of each side. All Roman forts, everywhere in the empire, are built to a similar plan. A soldier who moves from one fort to another always knows his way around.

[long-pause]

Two main streets cross the fortress. One runs from the front gate to the back gate. The other runs from side to side. Where they meet, in the middle of the fortress, there is the most important building. It is called the principia, the headquarters. It has a big courtyard, surrounded by covered walkways, and a great hall, where the commander speaks to the officers.

[pause]

At the back of the headquarters, there is a small, sacred room. It is called the shrine of the standards. Here, the golden eagle of the legion is kept, together with the other standards, and a statue of the emperor. It is the holiest place in the whole fortress. A guard stands in front of it, day and night. Below the shrine, there is a strongroom, where the soldiers' savings are kept safe.

[pause]

{img:principia-courtyard-headquarters-columns-officers-clerks-morning}
Next to the headquarters, there is the house of the commander, the legate. It is a big, comfortable house, like the house of a rich man in Italy, with many rooms around a garden courtyard, mosaic floors, painted walls and underfloor heating. The legate is a senator from Rome. He usually stays only a few years, and then goes back to Rome to continue his career. He brings his wife and children, and many servants.

[pause]

Most of the fortress is filled with barracks. Long, narrow buildings, in neat rows. Each barrack block houses one century, eighty men. It is divided into ten pairs of rooms, one pair for each contubernium. At one end of the block, there are the larger rooms of the centurion, where he lives with his family and servants. Lucius's barrack block is near the western wall, close to the bakery ovens.

[long-pause]

{img:granary-raised-floor-stone-buttresses-soldiers-carrying-grain-sacks}
There are many other buildings. Granaries, with raised floors and thick walls, to keep the grain dry and safe from mice. A hospital, with small rooms for the sick, around a quiet garden. Workshops, where blacksmiths, carpenters and leather workers make and repair equipment. Stables for the horses and mules. Storehouses for food, weapons and tools. And along the inside of the walls, there are rows of bread ovens, where the soldiers bake their bread.

[pause]

Outside the walls, there is the great bathhouse, which we will visit later. And there is something special. An amphitheatre, an oval arena with seats all around it, for about six thousand people. Here, the soldiers practise their drills, and hold parades and ceremonies. Sometimes, there are shows and games for the soldiers and the people of the town.

[pause]

{img:amphitheatre-caerleon-oval-arena-grass-seats-soldiers-drilling}
Around the fortress, a town has grown up. It is full of the families of the soldiers, and of traders, craftsmen and innkeepers. Officially, ordinary soldiers are not allowed to marry. But many of them have wives and children anyway, who live in the town outside the walls. When the soldiers retire, they marry officially, and their children become Roman citizens.

[long-pause]

Lucius likes the fortress. It is well organised and clean. Everything has its place. There is fresh water from pipes, toilets that are flushed with running water, and hot baths. It is more comfortable than many towns. In the evening, he walks along the wall with Quintus, and looks out over the river and the green hills. The light is soft and golden. A boat is drifting down the river. And Lucius thinks that perhaps Britain is not so bad after all.

[pause]

There is also a hospital in the fortress, with doctors and orderlies. The Roman army doctors are very skilled. They know how to set broken bones, clean wounds and treat fevers. They use herbs, honey, vinegar and wine to keep wounds clean. They have fine instruments of bronze and iron, many of which look surprisingly like modern ones. A sick soldier in a Roman fortress is better cared for than most people in the empire.

[long-pause]

{img:roman-fortress-morning-trumpet-soldiers-assembling-street-dawn}
Every day in a Roman fortress follows the same pattern. It begins at dawn, with the sound of a trumpet. The soldiers get up from their bunks, wash their faces in cold water, and get dressed. They eat a quick breakfast of bread and cheese. And then they go out into the street in front of their barracks, to hear the orders for the day.

[long-pause]

Every morning, the centurion of each century gives out the duties. He reads them from a wooden tablet, written by his clerk. Some soldiers will guard the gates. Some will work in the workshops. Some will go out to collect firewood, or to fetch grain from a nearby farm. Some will patrol the roads. Some will train on the parade ground. And some will do the cleaning, which nobody likes.

[pause]

Each morning, the officers also receive the watchword for the day. It is a secret word, written on a small wooden tablet, which the soldiers on guard must use to recognise each other at night. Anyone who does not know the watchword cannot pass. The watchword changes every day. It is often something simple, like the name of a god, or a good wish, like victory, or health.

[pause]

{img:roman-sentries-gate-tower-watching-road-countryside-cloaks-spears}
Today, Lucius is on guard duty at the western gate. He stands on the wall above the gate, with his shield and his spear, and watches the road. People come and go. Farmers with carts of vegetables. Traders with mules carrying goods. A messenger on a fast horse, with letters for the commander. Soldiers coming back from patrol. Everyone must be checked. Lucius asks each of them where they are going and why.

[pause]

Guard duty is long and often boring. Lucius stands for hours, watching the road and the hills. The rain comes and goes. The clouds drift across the sky. A hawk circles over the fields. In the river, a fisherman pulls in his net. Lucius thinks about home, about Nemausus and the warm sun. Then he shakes himself awake. A good sentry must always be alert. Sleeping on guard duty is one of the worst crimes a soldier can commit.

[long-pause]

{img:soldiers-working-workshop-blacksmith-forge-repairing-armour}
On other days, Lucius works in the fortress. He helps the blacksmiths in the workshop, carrying iron and working the bellows. He helps the carpenters repair a roof. He helps the clerks in the headquarters, because he can read a little. Every soldier has a skill, and the army uses it. Some soldiers with special skills, like surveyors, doctors and engineers, are excused from the heavy work. They are called immunes, the exempt ones.

[pause]

In the afternoon, there is often training. The soldiers practise with their weapons on the parade ground, in the hot sun or the cold rain. They practise marching in formation. They practise building walls and digging ditches. The centurions watch everything, with their vine sticks in their hands. Even soldiers who have served for many years must keep training, every day, to stay strong and ready.

[pause]

{img:soldiers-evening-barracks-cleaning-armour-oil-cloth-lamplight}
In the evening, the soldiers come back to their barracks. They clean their equipment. Armour must be oiled. Swords must be sharpened. Boots must be cleaned and greased. Then they cook their supper, and eat together. After supper, there is some free time. The soldiers talk, play dice, mend their clothes, write letters or go to the bathhouse. Some go to the town outside the walls, to visit friends or family.

[long-pause]

At night, the trumpet sounds again. The gates are closed. Lamps are put out. The night guards take their places on the walls. Every few hours, the guards change, and an officer walks around the walls to check that everyone is awake. Lucius lies in his bunk, listening to the rain on the roof, and the slow footsteps of the guard outside. Marcus is snoring. And Lucius falls asleep, thinking of nothing at all.

[pause]

Once a month, there is a big inspection. The soldiers line up in full armour, and the officers walk slowly along the rows, looking at everything. A dirty helmet, a rusty buckle or a loose strap means extra duties. So the night before an inspection, every barrack room is full of soldiers polishing and scrubbing by lamplight, helping each other, and checking every little detail twice.

[long-pause]

{img:roman-soldiers-grinding-grain-hand-quern-barracks-doorway-morning}
A Roman soldier eats well. The army knows that a hungry soldier is a weak soldier. So it makes sure that every man gets enough food. The most important food of all is grain, mostly wheat. Each soldier receives about one kilo of grain a day. It is taken from his wages. And he and his tent mates must turn it into food themselves.

[long-pause]

First, the grain must be ground into flour. Each contubernium has its own hand mill, called a quern. It is two round, heavy stones, one on top of the other. The top stone has a wooden handle. One soldier pours grain into a hole in the top, and turns the handle. The stone grinds the grain into flour, which comes out at the sides. It takes a long time. The soldiers take turns.

[pause]

From the flour, they bake bread, in the big ovens along the walls of the fortress. Each century has its own ovens. The bread is round and flat, often divided into eight pieces, one for each man in the tent group. On the march, they bake a kind of hard biscuit, called bucellatum, which lasts for a long time. Or they simply make porridge, boiling the flour with water and a little salt.

[pause]

{img:round-roman-bread-loaves-eight-sections-oven-soldier-baker}
Besides bread and porridge, the soldiers eat many other things. Bacon is a favourite. Cheese, eggs, beans, lentils, cabbage, onions and garlic. In Britain, they also eat a lot of meat. Beef, pork and mutton, and sometimes deer, wild boar and hare, from hunting. They eat fish from the rivers and the sea, and oysters, which are very popular. The archaeologists have found thousands of oyster shells at Roman forts in Britain.

[pause]

The soldiers also love a strong, salty fish sauce, called garum. It is made from fish left to ferment in salt, in the sun, for months. It smells terrible. But the Romans put it on almost everything, like we use salt or ketchup. It comes all the way from Spain, in big clay jars called amphorae. Olive oil also comes from Spain, and wine from Gaul and Italy.

[long-pause]

{img:roman-soldiers-supper-table-barracks-stew-bread-cups-olive-oil-lamp}
To drink, the soldiers have a sour drink called posca. It is made of cheap sour wine, mixed with water and sometimes herbs. It is refreshing, and safer than plain water. On special days, they have real wine. And in Britain, they also drink the local beer, made from barley, called cervesa. The letters found at the fort of Vindolanda, in the north, mention beer quite often. One officer complains that his men have no beer left, and asks for more to be sent.

[pause]

The eight men of the contubernium cook together, in their room or outside on a small fire. Each man takes his turn as cook. Some are good cooks. Some are terrible. Marcus, the big Spaniard, is the best. He makes a stew of beans, bacon and herbs that everyone loves. Felix, from Africa, adds spices that the others have never tasted. Lucius is not a good cook. The first time he cooked, he burned the porridge so badly that Cicero the mule refused to eat it.

[pause]

{img:officers-dining-room-couches-roman-meal-fine-plates-wine}
The officers eat better. The legate and the centurions have their own kitchens and their own cooks. They eat at tables with fine plates and glasses, lying on couches in the Roman way. They have imported food from all over the empire. Dates and figs from Africa, peppers from India, olives and fine wine from Italy. The letters from Vindolanda mention lists of food for an officer's household, with chickens, geese, apples and eggs by the hundred.

[long-pause]

But even the ordinary soldiers eat better than many poor people in the empire. They have bread every day, and meat and cheese often. After a long day of work, sitting together with their friends around a pot of hot stew, they feel content. Titus says that the army has three good things. The friends, the food and the pay. And that the pay is the least important of the three.

[long-pause]

{img:roman-fortress-baths-caerleon-vaulted-hall-pool-steam-soldiers}
Just inside the walls of the fortress at Isca, there is a big, beautiful building. It is the bathhouse. For the soldiers, it is one of the most important places of all. It is where they get clean, relax, meet their friends and forget about their work for a while. The Romans believe that bathing is good for the body and the mind. And they bring their love of baths with them, wherever they go, even to the cold, wet edge of the empire.

[long-pause]

The bathhouse at Isca is very large, almost like a palace. It has high, vaulted ceilings, painted walls and mosaic floors. There is an open-air swimming pool, about forty metres long, in a courtyard surrounded by columns. And there is an exercise yard, where the soldiers can play ball games, wrestle and lift weights. When Lucius first sees it, he can hardly believe it. It is bigger than the baths in Nemausus.

[pause]

The soldiers go to the baths in the afternoon or evening, after their work. They pay a small fee, or nothing at all. First, they take off their clothes in a changing room, and leave them on shelves. A slave or a friend keeps an eye on them, because sometimes clothes are stolen. Then they do some exercise in the yard, to warm up. They throw a ball, or wrestle, or run.

[pause]

{img:roman-bath-hot-room-steam-soldiers-relaxing-marble-benches}
Then they go through a series of rooms, each warmer than the last. First, the cold room, with a cold plunge pool. Then the warm room, pleasantly warm, where they sit and talk. Then the hot room, which is very hot and full of steam, like a sauna. Here, they sweat. The floors of these rooms are heated from below, by hot air from a furnace, which flows under the floor and up through the walls. The floor is so hot that the bathers must wear wooden sandals.

[pause]

Romans do not use soap. Instead, they rub olive oil on their skin, and then scrape it off with a curved metal tool, called a strigil. The oil takes the dirt and sweat with it. The soldiers help each other with the hard-to-reach places, on the back. Then they go back into the warm room, and finally into the cold pool, to close the skin. They come out feeling fresh, clean and relaxed.

[long-pause]

{img:hairpins-gems-found-in-roman-bath-drain-archaeology-small-objects}
The baths are noisy and lively. The soldiers talk, laugh, joke and gossip. They talk about their work, about their officers, about the news from Rome. They play board games on the benches. There are snack sellers, selling sausages, bread and sweet cakes. There is a barber, who shaves the soldiers and cuts their hair. Some soldiers spend hours at the baths every day. It is like a club.

[pause]

Archaeologists have dug up the drains of the baths at Isca. And in the mud of the drains, they found many small things that people had lost while bathing. Little engraved gemstones from rings. Hairpins. Beads. Small bones from meals. Even baby teeth. Because the families of the soldiers also came to the baths, at certain times, and the children lost their teeth there, just like children everywhere.

[pause]

{img:soldiers-relaxing-around-swimming-pool-columns-evening-light}
Lucius loves the baths. After a long day of guard duty in the cold rain, there is nothing better than sitting in the warm room, with the steam rising around him, and his friends beside him. Felix sings softly. Marcus tells jokes. Quintus reads a little book. Titus closes his eyes and seems to sleep. And Lucius leans back against the warm wall, and feels the cold and the tiredness slowly leave his body.

[long-pause]

When they come out of the baths, it is evening. The air is cool and fresh. The lamps are being lit in the streets of the fortress. The soldiers walk slowly back to their barracks, clean and warm and a little sleepy, with their hair still wet. And for a moment, Lucius thinks that being a soldier is the best life in the world.

[long-pause]

{img:roman-column-marching-road-hills-wales-standards-mules-carts}
In the second year, Lucius's cohort is sent out on a long march, through the hills of what is now Wales. They are to visit the smaller forts in the area, check the roads and show the local people that Rome is strong and watchful. It is a peaceful mission. But they must be ready for anything.

[long-pause]

The column of soldiers sets out early in the morning. At the front, there are scouts on horseback, riding ahead to check the way. Then come the surveyors, who choose the place for the camp each night. Then the standard bearers, carrying the standards high. Then the soldiers, marching in rows of six, with their packs on their shoulders. Then the mules and carts, with the tents and the baggage. And at the back, more soldiers, as a rear guard.

[pause]

They march along the Roman roads. The roads are straight, wherever possible, going up and over the hills instead of around them. They are well built, with layers of stones and gravel, and a curved surface so that the rain runs off. Every Roman mile, about one and a half kilometres, there is a stone pillar, called a milestone, with the distance to the next town carved on it.

[pause]

{img:roman-soldiers-building-marching-camp-ditch-rampart-stakes-evening}
In the afternoon, after about twenty-five kilometres, the column stops. The surveyors have chosen a place for the night. A flat, open field near a stream. And now, something amazing happens. In just a few hours, the soldiers build a complete camp. Some dig a ditch all around the field. The earth from the ditch is piled up on the inside, to make a wall. On top of the wall, they put wooden stakes, which every soldier carries in his pack.

[pause]

Inside the camp, the tents are put up in neat rows, exactly the same as in every other camp. Every soldier knows where his tent goes. The commander's tent is in the middle. There are streets between the tents, and four gates, one on each side. By the time the sun sets, the camp is finished. A small, safe, fortified town in the middle of the wilderness. And the next morning, they pull it all down again, and march on.

[long-pause]

{img:leather-tent-roman-camp-night-campfire-eight-soldiers-cooking}
Lucius's group puts up their leather tent. It is made of goat skins, sewn together, and supported by wooden poles. Eight men sleep inside, side by side, on the ground, wrapped in their cloaks. It is tight and warm. Outside, they light a fire, and cook their porridge in the big pot, with some bacon. Cicero the mule munches grass nearby, and occasionally makes a loud noise, to remind everyone that he exists.

[pause]

On the march, they pass through beautiful country. Green valleys with fast, clear rivers. High hills covered with heather and bracken. Little farms, with round houses and thatched roofs, where the local people live. The local people look at the soldiers as they pass. Some wave. Some stare silently. Some of them are friendly, and come to sell food, wool and leather. Others keep their distance.

[pause]

{img:welsh-hills-heather-round-houses-smoke-roman-soldiers-passing-valley}
One evening, they camp near a lake, high in the hills. The water is perfectly still. The mountains are reflected in it, upside down. The sunset turns the sky pink and gold. Lucius sits on a rock by the water, after supper, and looks at it for a long time. He has never seen such a beautiful place. Titus comes and sits beside him. He says that the gods must have made this place on a good day. Lucius agrees.

[long-pause]

After three weeks, the cohort marches back to Isca. Their boots are worn, their cloaks are muddy and their legs are tired. But they have seen the country, visited the forts and met the people. Nothing bad has happened. And Lucius feels that he has become a real soldier, at last. A soldier who can march all day, build a camp in an evening, and sleep anywhere in the world.

[pause]

On the last night of the march, it rains. The soldiers put up their tents in the mud, and huddle inside, wet and cold. Nobody can light a fire. They eat hard biscuits and cold bacon. Then Felix starts to sing, very softly, in the dark. One by one, the others join in. The rain drums on the leather tent. And eight soldiers sing together in the darkness, until they fall asleep.

[long-pause]

{img:vindolanda-wooden-writing-tablets-thin-wood-ink-letters-close}
Roman soldiers wrote letters. Many of them could read and write, at least a little. And those who could not, asked a friend to write for them. They wrote to their families at home, to friends in other forts, and to the officers. Most of these letters have been lost long ago. But in one place in Britain, by a lucky chance, hundreds of them have survived.

[long-pause]

The place is called Vindolanda. It was a Roman fort in the north of Britain, near where Hadrian's Wall would later be built. In the 1970s, archaeologists digging there found something extraordinary. In the wet, muddy ground, where there was no air, they found hundreds of thin wooden tablets, about the size of a postcard. They were covered with writing in ink. They were letters, lists and notes, written by the soldiers and their families, around the years 90 to 120.

[pause]

These are some of the oldest handwritten documents ever found in Britain. And they tell us things about the daily lives of Roman soldiers that no history book ever mentioned. They are not about great battles or emperors. They are about ordinary things. Shopping lists. Requests for leave. Complaints about the roads. And birthday parties.

[pause]

{img:roman-woman-writing-letter-birthday-invitation-wooden-tablet-garden}
The most famous of the Vindolanda letters is a birthday invitation. It was written by a woman called Claudia Severa, the wife of an officer at another fort. She writes to her friend Sulpicia Lepidina, the wife of the commander at Vindolanda. She invites her to her birthday party, on the eleventh of September. She writes that the day will be much more enjoyable if her friend is there. And at the end, in her own handwriting, she adds, farewell, sister, my dearest soul.

[pause]

It is the oldest known writing by a woman in Latin. And it is so warm and so ordinary. Two friends, at the cold edge of the empire, far from home, keeping in touch and inviting each other to parties. Reading it, we suddenly feel very close to them, even after almost two thousand years.

[long-pause]

{img:soldier-opening-parcel-woollen-socks-sandals-barracks-delighted}
Another letter was written to a soldier by someone at home. It says, I have sent you pairs of socks, two pairs of sandals and two pairs of underpants. In the cold, wet north of Britain, socks were very welcome. The soldiers from the warm south must have been very grateful. Some people at home clearly worried that their sons and brothers were cold.

[pause]

Other letters are about everyday problems. A soldier asks his officer for leave, to visit somewhere. Someone complains that the roads are very bad, and that he does not want to damage his carts. A list counts the soldiers in a unit, and says how many are sick, or away on duties. One famous note describes the local Britons, using a rude little nickname, Brittunculi, which means something like the wretched little Britons.

[pause]

{img:soldier-writing-letter-home-stylus-wax-tablet-lamp-barracks-night}
Lucius writes letters too. He writes home to his family in Nemausus. He tells them about the long journey, the sea crossing, the fortress and his friends. He tells them about the green hills and the rain. He tells them not to worry. He does not know if the letters arrive. They travel for weeks, with traders and messengers, across the sea and through Gaul.

[long-pause]

And one day, after almost a year, a letter arrives for him. It is from his mother. She writes that everyone is well. His sister is going to be married. The olive harvest was good. His grandfather sends his greetings, and says he is proud. And there is a small package with the letter. Inside, there are two pairs of thick woollen socks. Lucius laughs out loud. Then he reads the letter again, three times. And that night, he puts on the socks, and sleeps with warm feet for the first time since he came to Britain.

[pause]

Writing letters is not always easy. Paper is rare and expensive. So most soldiers write on thin slices of wood, with ink, or on wooden tablets covered with wax, with a pointed stylus. The wax can be smoothed out and used again. Quintus helps the soldiers who cannot write well. He writes their letters for them in the evenings, by lamplight, while they tell him what to say. In return, they buy him a cup of wine.

[long-pause]

{img:roman-altar-stone-carved-inscription-fort-soldiers-offering-incense}
Roman soldiers are very religious. They believe that the gods are everywhere, and that they must be honoured and kept happy. The army has many official festivals and ceremonies. And the soldiers also have their own private gods, which they pray to for luck, health and protection.

[long-pause]

The most important god of the army is Jupiter, the king of the gods. Every year, on the first of January, the soldiers set up a new stone altar to Jupiter, on the parade ground. They make a sacrifice and renew their oath to the emperor. Many of these altars have been found in Britain, buried in rows near the forts. Each one has a carved inscription, saying which unit set it up, and in which year.

[pause]

The soldiers also honour the emperor himself, and his family. And they honour the standards of the legion, especially the golden eagle, which is almost a god in itself. On the birthday of the legion, there is a great festival. The standards are decorated with flowers. There are sacrifices, parades, a feast and games. Lucius's legion, the Second Augusta, has the sign of the Capricorn, a goat with the tail of a fish.

[pause]

{img:capricorn-emblem-legio-ii-augusta-standard-decorated-roses-festival}
But the soldiers also believe in many other gods. Mars, the god of war. Fortuna, the goddess of luck, who has an altar in every bathhouse, because the baths are a place where luck is needed. Aesculapius, the god of healing, in the hospital. And the genius of the century, a protecting spirit, who looks after each group of soldiers.

[pause]

Many soldiers also worship the gods of the places where they come from. Soldiers from Germany bring their own gods. Soldiers from Syria bring theirs. And in Britain, they also begin to worship the local British gods. They believe that every river, every hill and every spring has its own spirit. So they pray to the spirits of the land where they now live, to be on the safe side.

[long-pause]

{img:mithraeum-dark-underground-temple-torchlight-benches-small-group}
Some soldiers belong to a secret religion, called the cult of Mithras. Mithras was a god from Persia. His followers met in small, dark temples, built partly underground, like caves. Inside, there were stone benches along the walls, and at the end, a carved picture of Mithras. Only men could join. They had to pass through seven levels of initiation, with secret ceremonies. Nobody outside knew exactly what happened inside.

[pause]

Titus belongs to the cult of Mithras. He never talks about it. Sometimes, in the evening, he goes out quietly, and comes back late. When Lucius asks him about it, he just smiles and says that some things are not for talking about. One day, Lucius thinks, he might join too. But for now, he prefers the bright, open temples, with the sun shining on them.

[pause]

{img:soldier-praying-at-small-shrine-household-gods-lamp-barracks-corner}
In the corner of their barrack room, the eight soldiers have a little shrine. It is a small shelf, with tiny statues of their favourite gods, made of bronze and clay. There is a little lamp, which is lit on special days. Each man has put something there. Lucius has put his father's little clay lamp with the ship on it. Felix has put a small statue of a goddess from Africa. And Aulus has put a little carved hand, which is a sign of good luck in his homeland of Syria.

[long-pause]

Every morning, before going out to work, the soldiers touch the shrine, or say a short prayer. It is a little habit, like saying good morning. Lucius always prays for the same things. For his family in Nemausus. For his friends in the contubernium. And for good weather, which, in Britain, is the hardest thing of all to get.

[long-pause]

{img:emperor-hadrian-arriving-britain-ship-harbour-officials-welcoming-122ad}
In the year 122, a great piece of news spreads through all the forts of Britain. The emperor himself is coming. Hadrian has been travelling around his empire for some time, visiting the provinces and the armies. He has been to Gaul and to Germany. And now, he is crossing the sea to Britain. It is the first time in many years that an emperor has visited the island.

[long-pause]

Hadrian is a most unusual emperor. He does not like to stay in Rome. He prefers to travel, and to see things with his own eyes. He spends more than half of his reign travelling, to almost every province of the empire. He visits the forts and the cities, inspects the soldiers, talks to the governors and orders new buildings. Wherever he goes, he leaves new temples, bridges, roads and walls behind him.

[pause]

And Hadrian likes soldiers. He lives like them, when he visits. He eats their simple food, bread, bacon, cheese and sour wine. He marches with them, on foot, bareheaded, in all weathers, sometimes thirty kilometres a day. He knows the names of many old soldiers. He visits the sick in the hospitals. The soldiers love him for it. But he is also very strict. He expects them to train hard, and to keep their equipment perfect.

[pause]

{img:hadrian-inspecting-troops-parade-ground-bearded-emperor-walking-ranks}
When the news arrives at Isca, the whole fortress gets busy. Everything is cleaned and repaired. The walls are whitewashed. The streets are swept. The soldiers polish their armour until it shines like silver. They oil their boots, sharpen their swords and paint their shields. The centurions inspect everything three times. Nobody wants the emperor to find anything wrong.

[pause]

The emperor does not come to Isca himself. He goes north. But a large part of the legion is ordered to march north too, to meet him, and to begin a great new work. Lucius's cohort is among them. They march for weeks, along the roads, through the middle of Britain, all the way to the north. And there, near the river Tyne, they see the emperor.

[long-pause]

{img:soldiers-parade-for-emperor-standards-raised-shields-gleaming-crowd}
The soldiers stand in long rows on a field, in their best armour, with their standards raised. The emperor rides slowly along the rows, on a fine horse. He is about forty-six years old, tall and strong, with curly hair and a short beard. He looks carefully at the soldiers as he passes. When he reaches Lucius's century, he stops. He asks the centurion a few questions. Then he looks straight at Lucius. Lucius stands as still as a statue, and does not breathe.

[pause]

The emperor asks him where he comes from. Lucius answers, from Nemausus, in Gaul. The emperor nods. He says that he knows Nemausus. It is a fine town, with a fine temple. Then he rides on. The whole thing takes only a few seconds. But Lucius will remember it for the rest of his life. The emperor of Rome spoke to him. He knew his town.

[pause]

{img:hadrian-speaking-to-officers-map-table-tent-plans-for-wall}
After the inspection, the emperor speaks to the officers. He tells them about his great plan. He wants to build a wall across the whole of Britain, from sea to sea. It will mark the northern border of the empire. It will control who comes and goes. It will protect the province from raids from the north. And it will show everyone, the Britons and the Romans, exactly where the Roman Empire ends.

[long-pause]

A Roman writer later said that Hadrian built the wall to separate the Romans from the barbarians. But the wall was not only a barrier. It also had many gates, where people could pass through, and trade, and pay customs. It was a way of watching and controlling the frontier, more than closing it. And it was a great sign of Roman power. And now, Lucius and his friends would help to build it.

[long-pause]

{img:hadrians-wall-under-construction-soldiers-building-stone-wall-hills-122ad}
Hadrian's Wall is one of the greatest building projects of the Roman world. It runs for about one hundred and seventeen kilometres, across the whole width of northern Britain, from the river Tyne in the east to the Solway Firth in the west. It crosses hills and valleys, rivers and cliffs. And it is built almost entirely by the soldiers of the three British legions.

[long-pause]

The plan is simple and clever. The wall is made of stone, about three metres wide and about five metres high. In front of it, on the north side, there is a deep ditch. Every Roman mile, there is a small fort, called a milecastle, with a gate and a few soldiers. Between each pair of milecastles, there are two small towers. And later, there will be bigger forts along the wall, with hundreds of soldiers each.

[pause]

The work is divided between the legions. Each legion builds its own sections of the wall. And each century in the legion builds a small piece. When they finish a piece, they carve a small stone with the name of their century and their legion, and set it into the wall. Many of these stones have been found. They are called centurial stones. They show us exactly who built which part.

[pause]

{img:legionaries-quarrying-stone-shaping-blocks-carrying-baskets-mortar}
Lucius's century works on a section in the middle of the wall, where it climbs over steep, rocky hills. The work is very hard. First, the stone must be cut from quarries nearby. The soldiers split the rock with iron wedges and hammers, and shape the blocks with chisels. Each block is about the size of a big loaf of bread. Then the blocks are carried to the wall in carts, or in baskets on the soldiers' backs.

[pause]

At the wall, the builders lay the stones in neat rows, on both sides, with mortar between them. The space in the middle is filled with rubble and clay. Slowly, row by row, the wall rises. The surveyors check that it is straight, with plumb lines and measuring rods. The centurions watch everything. And the soldiers work from dawn till dusk, in sun, wind and rain.

[long-pause]

{img:soldiers-resting-on-wall-looking-north-wild-hills-wind-clouds}
In the evenings, the soldiers sit on top of the half-built wall and look north. The view is wide and wild. Green and brown hills, stretching away to the horizon. Little lakes shining in the valleys. Clouds racing across the sky, and their shadows racing across the hills. No houses, no roads, no towns. Just the wind, and the birds, and the open land. It is the very edge of the Roman world.

[pause]

Lucius loves these evenings. He sits with Titus and Felix on the wall, eating their bread, and watching the sun go down over the western hills. The sky turns red and gold. A curlew calls, with its long, sad, beautiful cry. Felix sings a song from Africa, very softly. And Lucius thinks about how far he has come, from the warm south of Gaul, to this windy wall at the end of the world.

[pause]

{img:centurial-stone-carved-inscription-set-in-wall-soldier-proud}
After several months, his century finishes its section. They carve their centurial stone, with the name of their centurion and their legion. Quintus, who writes best, draws the letters. Lucius helps to carve them. They set the stone into the wall with great care. Then they stand back and look at it. Titus says that their stone will still be there in a thousand years. They all laugh. But he is right.

[long-pause]

The whole wall takes about six years to build, with thousands of soldiers working on it. When it is finished, it is painted white, perhaps, so that it shines across the hills. It is the most famous border in the whole Roman Empire. And for almost three hundred years, Roman soldiers will live and work along it, guarding the edge of the world.

[pause]

Not all the work is done by the legionaries. Local people are hired to bring food and materials. Carts loaded with lime for the mortar come from kilns nearby. Timber comes from the forests for the scaffolding and the gates. And at the end of each day, the builders count how much of the wall they have finished, and the clerks write it all down on their wooden tablets.

[long-pause]

{img:roman-fort-hadrians-wall-snow-winter-smoke-chimneys-grey-sky}
When the building season is over, part of Lucius's cohort stays in the north for the winter. They live in a fort near the wall. And they discover what the British winter is really like, so far north. For a young man from the warm south of Gaul, it is a shock.

[long-pause]

The days are very short. The sun rises late, around eight in the morning, and sets early, around four in the afternoon. And often, it hardly comes out at all. The sky is grey for days on end. The wind blows from the north and the east, cold and wet. It rains, and then it sleets, and then it snows. The hills turn white. The streams freeze. And the soldiers on guard duty on the wall stand in the wind, wrapped in every piece of clothing they own.

[pause]

The soldiers learn to keep warm. They wear two tunics, and woollen trousers, like the Britons. They wrap their legs in strips of wool. They wear thick cloaks with hoods. They stuff their boots with wool, and wear the socks that their families have sent. They wear woollen caps under their helmets. Marcus, the Spaniard, wears so many clothes that the others say he looks like a walking sheep.

[pause]

{img:barracks-room-winter-brazier-glowing-soldiers-wrapped-blankets-dice}
In the barracks, there are small hearths, and charcoal braziers, to keep the rooms warm. The soldiers sit close to the fire in the long evenings. They mend their clothes, clean their equipment, play dice and tell stories. The small window is closed with a wooden shutter. The room is smoky and cosy. Outside, the wind howls around the fort, and the snow piles up against the doors.

[pause]

Some of the officers' houses have something special. Underfloor heating, like in the bathhouse. A furnace outside the house sends hot air under the floor and up through the walls. The officers' rooms are warm and comfortable, even in the coldest winter. The ordinary soldiers are a little jealous. But they have their own warm place too. The bathhouse. In winter, they go there almost every day.

[long-pause]

{img:soldiers-on-wall-snowy-hills-guard-duty-hooded-cloaks-breath-steam}
Guard duty in winter is the hardest. The soldiers stand on the wall or in the towers, for hours, in the dark and the cold. They stamp their feet and blow on their hands. Their breath makes clouds in the air. They watch the white hills in the moonlight, where nothing moves except, sometimes, a fox or a hare. When their watch is over, they hurry back to the barracks, and sit as close to the fire as they can.

[pause]

One winter night, Lucius is on guard in a tower on the wall. It is snowing softly. The world is completely silent. The snow falls on his cloak, on his helmet, on the stones of the wall. He can see nothing but white flakes, drifting down in the light of his little lamp. He feels very alone, at the very end of the world. And then, out of the darkness, he hears footsteps. It is Titus, coming to take over, with a jug of hot spiced wine in his hand.

[pause]

{img:two-soldiers-sharing-hot-drink-tower-snow-falling-lamp-glow}
They stand together in the tower for a while, drinking the hot wine, and watching the snow. Titus says that he has served in many places. In the deserts of Africa, where the sun is like fire. In the forests of Germany, where it rains for months. And here, in the north of Britain, where it snows. He says that every place is hard in its own way. But that a good friend and a hot drink make anywhere bearable. Lucius thinks this is very wise.

[long-pause]

And then, slowly, the winter ends. The days get longer. The snow melts. The streams run fast and full. The first flowers appear on the hillsides, little white snowdrops and yellow primroses. The birds come back and start to sing. And one morning, the sun comes out, warm and bright, for the first time in months. The soldiers come out of their barracks, blinking, and turn their faces up to it, like flowers.

[pause]

On the darkest days of winter, the soldiers celebrate the Saturnalia, and later, in the spring, the festivals of the legion. These feasts are like little candles in the long darkness. Everyone looks forward to them for weeks. And when they come, the barracks are full of music, laughter and the smell of roasting meat, while outside the snow falls quietly on the wall.

[long-pause]

{img:vicus-village-outside-fort-shops-tavern-women-children-traders}
Outside the gates of every fort along the wall, a little village grows up. The Romans call it a vicus. It is full of people who live from the soldiers. Shopkeepers, craftsmen, innkeepers, traders and farmers. And the wives, children and families of the soldiers. It is a lively, busy little place, with wooden houses, workshops, a tavern and a small market.

[long-pause]

Many of the people in the vicus are local Britons. They have come to live near the fort, because there is work and money there. They sell food to the soldiers. Grain, meat, vegetables, eggs and beer. They make leather goods, pottery and cloth. Some of them work for the army, as guides, messengers or carters. Slowly, they learn to speak Latin, and to wear Roman clothes, and to live in a Roman way.

[pause]

Others come from far away. There are traders from Gaul, selling wine and pottery. There are craftsmen from Germany. There are people from Syria and North Africa, who came with the army. One gravestone found near the wall belongs to a woman called Regina. She was a Briton, from the south, who married a trader from Palmyra, in Syria, at the other end of the empire. His name was Barates. He made her gravestone, with a carving of her, sitting in a chair, with her spinning and her jewellery box. And he wrote her name in Latin, and again in his own language, Aramaic.

[pause]

{img:british-woman-market-stall-selling-wool-cloth-soldiers-buying}
In the vicus, Lucius meets a young British woman called Brica. She sells woollen cloaks and blankets at a stall in the market, which her mother weaves. She has red hair, freckles and a quick laugh. She speaks a little Latin, with a strong British accent. Lucius buys a blanket from her, and then comes back the next day to buy another one, although he does not really need it.

[pause]

Soon, he goes to her stall every day. They talk. She teaches him some words of her own language, which is a Celtic language, related to modern Welsh. He teaches her more Latin. She tells him about her people, who have lived in these hills for many hundreds of years. About their gods, the spirits of the rivers and the springs. About the old hill forts, where her grandparents lived before the Romans came.

[long-pause]

{img:young-roman-soldier-british-woman-walking-by-river-spring-evening}
Officially, Lucius cannot marry while he is in the army. That is the law. But many soldiers have partners and children in the vicus anyway. The army looks the other way. And when the soldiers retire, they can marry officially. So after a while, Lucius and Brica decide to be together. They have a little house in the vicus, and Lucius visits her whenever he is not on duty.

[pause]

The other soldiers tease him about it. But they also like Brica very much. She brings them bread and cheese, and mends their cloaks. Felix sings at their little celebration. Marcus cooks a feast. Titus makes a small speech, which makes Brica laugh and cry at the same time. And Lucius thinks that he has never been so happy.

[pause]

{img:small-house-vicus-evening-lamp-window-couple-baby-cradle}
Over the years, life on the frontier becomes a mixture of Roman and British. The soldiers drink British beer and wear British trousers. The Britons drink Roman wine and use Roman pottery. Children grow up speaking both languages. People worship both Roman and British gods. And slowly, the soldiers who came from far away begin to feel that this northern land, with its green hills and grey skies, is their home.

[long-pause]

A year later, Brica has a baby. A little boy, with his mother's red hair and his father's brown eyes. They call him Gaius. Lucius holds him in his arms for the first time, very carefully, as if he were made of glass. And he thinks of his own father, in the workshop in Nemausus, and wishes he could show him his grandson.

[long-pause]

{img:roman-soldiers-playing-board-game-barracks-table-counters-dice-cup}
A soldier's life is not only work. There is also free time. And the Roman soldiers know very well how to enjoy it. In the evenings, on holidays and on rainy days, they find many ways to pass the time.

[long-pause]

The most popular pastime is gambling, with dice. The soldiers throw the dice from a little cup, onto the table, and bet small coins on the result. They shout and laugh when they win, and groan when they lose. The officers do not really approve. But they cannot stop it. Dice have been found at almost every Roman fort. Some of them are even weighted, to cheat. Clearly, not every soldier was honest.

[pause]

They also play board games. One game is like a mixture of chess and draughts. It is played on a board with squares, scratched into a stone or a piece of wood, with little round counters of glass or bone. The players try to trap each other's pieces. Quintus is the best player in the group. He beats everyone. Only Aulus, the quiet Syrian, can sometimes beat him.

[pause]

{img:roman-soldiers-hunting-deer-forest-dogs-spears-misty-morning}
Another favourite is hunting. The north of Britain is full of wild animals. Deer, wild boar, hares and birds. On free days, the soldiers go out into the forests and hills with spears, nets and hunting dogs. The British hunting dogs are famous. The Romans admire them, and even send them back to Italy. The hunters bring back meat for the cooking pot, and stories for the evening.

[pause]

One officer at a fort in the north set up an altar to the god Silvanus, the god of the forests, to thank him for a wonderful hunt. He wrote on it that he had caught a wild boar of extraordinary size, which many before him had failed to catch. We can still read his proud words today. Clearly, even two thousand years ago, hunters liked to tell everyone about their biggest catch.

[long-pause]

{img:saturnalia-feast-soldiers-wearing-felt-caps-gifts-candles-december}
The best holiday of the year is the Saturnalia, in December. It is a festival of the god Saturn, and it lasts for several days. During the Saturnalia, the normal rules are turned upside down. Work stops. Everyone eats, drinks and celebrates. People give each other small gifts, like candles, little clay figures and sweets. Masters serve their slaves at dinner. And everyone wears a soft felt cap, as a sign of freedom.

[pause]

In the fort, the soldiers decorate their barracks with green branches. They have feasts, with roast meat, wine and cakes. They play games, sing songs and tell jokes. Each group chooses a king of the Saturnalia, by throwing dice. The king can give silly orders to everyone, and they must obey. One year, Marcus is chosen as king. He orders Titus to sing a love song. Titus, red in the face, sings it. Everyone laughs so much that they cannot breathe.

[pause]

{img:soldiers-gathered-around-storyteller-fire-winter-night-listening}
In the long winter evenings, the soldiers also tell stories. Stories about their homelands, about famous battles, about gods and heroes. Felix tells stories about the deserts of Africa, about lions and camels and cities of white stone. Aulus tells stories about Syria, about the great cities of the east, with their markets full of silk and spices. The two Gaulish brothers tell stories about their village, and argue about every detail.

[long-pause]

And sometimes there are visitors. Travelling entertainers come to the vicus. Jugglers, acrobats, musicians and actors. Once, a troupe of actors puts on a funny play in the courtyard of the fort, about a greedy old man and a clever slave. The soldiers laugh until they cry. Another time, a man comes with a trained bear, which dances to a flute. The children of the vicus follow him all over the village. Life on the frontier is far from home. But it is never boring.

[pause]

Some soldiers spend their free time making things. Little carvings of wood or bone. Rings and brooches. Leather pouches and belts. Lucius learns to make small clay lamps, like his father, using clay from the riverbank and a little kiln in the vicus. He makes one for each of his friends, with a different picture on each. Felix gets a lion. Marcus gets a pot of stew. Everyone laughs at that one.

[long-pause]

{img:older-roman-soldier-grey-hair-teaching-young-recruits-fort-courtyard}
The years go by. Lucius serves in the army for twenty-five years in all. He serves at Isca and on the wall, and in other forts in Britain. He marches thousands of kilometres. He builds walls, roads and bridges. He guards gates and towers, in sun and snow. And slowly, he grows older, and wiser, and more experienced.

[long-pause]

He is promoted. First, he becomes the leader of his contubernium, like Titus was. Then he becomes an optio, the assistant of a centurion. It is an important job. He helps to train the soldiers, check the guards and keep the records. Because he can read and write well, he is very useful. And finally, after almost twenty years, he becomes a centurion himself. He wears the crest from side to side, and carries the vine stick.

[pause]

As a centurion, Lucius tries to be the kind of officer he would have liked when he was young. Strict but fair. He knows the names of all his soldiers, and something about their families. He teaches them what Titus taught him. How to keep your feet dry. How to light a fire in the rain. How to keep out of trouble. And he never forgets what it was like to be eighteen, far from home, with blisters on his feet.

[pause]

{img:old-friends-roman-veterans-sitting-bench-sunlight-laughing-together}
The friends of the old contubernium go different ways. Quintus becomes a clerk at the headquarters, as he always wanted. Marcus marries a woman from the vicus, and opens a little tavern after he retires, famous for his bean stew. Felix is sent to another legion, in Germany, and writes letters for many years. The two Gaulish brothers go home to Gaul together, still arguing. And Aulus becomes a medical orderly in the hospital, helping the doctors.

[pause]

Titus retires after twenty-five years, when Lucius has served for ten. On his last day, the whole contubernium gives him a farewell feast. Titus gives Lucius his old dagger, as a gift. He says that Lucius will make a good soldier, and a good man. Then he walks out of the gate, with his pack on his shoulder, and settles on a small farm not far away. Lucius visits him every year, until the old man passes away peacefully, in his sleep, at a great age.

[long-pause]

{img:discharge-ceremony-veteran-receiving-reward-officers-standards}
And at last, after twenty-five years, Lucius's own service comes to an end. He is forty-three years old. There is a ceremony in the courtyard of the fortress. He receives his honourable discharge, and a large sum of money, about ten years' pay. It is enough to buy a good piece of land, and to live comfortably for the rest of his life. His name and his good service are written down in the records of the legion, so that nobody can ever doubt that he served Rome well.

[pause]

Now, at last, he can officially marry Brica. They have been together for more than twenty years. Their son Gaius is grown up, and has joined the army himself. They also have a daughter, called Lucia, with red hair like her mother. There is a small wedding, in the vicus, with all their friends. Marcus cooks. Quintus writes the marriage contract. And Lucius and Brica stand together, a little grey, a little wrinkled, and very happy.

[pause]

{img:retired-veteran-farm-britain-green-hills-wife-grandchildren-evening}
Lucius does not go back to Gaul. He has been away too long. Britain is his home now. With his money, he buys a small farm near the wall, with fields, a few cows, some sheep and an orchard. He builds a house in the Roman style, with a tiled roof and a little bath. He plants an olive tree in a pot, by the door, to remind him of Nemausus. It never grows very well, in the British weather. But he looks after it anyway.

[long-pause]

In the evenings, he sits in front of his house with Brica, looking out over the green hills. On the horizon, he can see the long white line of the wall, which he helped to build, running across the land. His grandchildren play in the orchard. The sun goes down. And Lucius thinks about the young man who left Nemausus long ago, with his grandfather's belt buckle. He has had a good life. A soldier's life. And he would not change any of it.

[long-pause]

{img:antonine-wall-turf-rampart-scotland-hills-later-roman-soldiers}
Hadrian died in the year 138. The next emperor, Antoninus Pius, decided to push the frontier further north. His soldiers built a new wall, about a hundred and sixty kilometres to the north, across the narrow middle of Scotland. It was called the Antonine Wall. It was made mostly of turf, on a stone base. But it was only held for about twenty years. Then the soldiers went back to Hadrian's Wall, which became the frontier once more.

[long-pause]

For almost three hundred years, Roman soldiers lived along Hadrian's Wall. Generation after generation. Sons followed their fathers into the army. Families lived in the villages outside the forts. The soldiers came from all over the empire, and many of them stayed in Britain when they retired. Slowly, the people of the frontier became a mixture of all these peoples. Britons, Gauls, Germans, Syrians, Africans and Italians, all living together.

[pause]

There were peaceful times and difficult times. Sometimes, peoples from the north attacked the wall. Sometimes, there were wars between Roman generals, who wanted to become emperor. One emperor, Septimius Severus, came to Britain himself, with a great army, around the year 208. He was born in North Africa. He stayed in Britain for three years, and died at Eboracum, which is now York, in 211.

[pause]

{img:late-roman-fort-wall-fewer-soldiers-evening-quiet-later}
In the later years, the Roman Empire grew weaker. There were troubles everywhere, in many provinces. The soldiers on the wall were not paid as regularly as before. Fewer new soldiers came. The forts became smaller and quieter. The soldiers became more like local farmers, who also guarded the wall. Their families lived inside the forts now.

[pause]

Then, around the year 410, the Roman army left Britain. The emperor needed all his soldiers to defend Italy and Gaul. He told the people of Britain that they would have to look after themselves. Some of the soldiers on the wall may have stayed, because Britain was their home. They had lived there for generations. But they were no longer part of the Roman army.

[long-pause]

{img:hadrians-wall-ruins-medieval-shepherd-sheep-stones-taken-later}
After the Romans left, the wall slowly fell into ruin. Over the centuries, people took its stones to build farms, churches and castles. Many farmhouses and churches near the wall are built partly from Roman stones. Some of them still have Roman inscriptions on them, built into their walls, sometimes upside down. In some places, the wall disappeared completely. In others, it stood, slowly crumbling, covered with grass and moss.

[pause]

But the wall was never forgotten. People in the Middle Ages told stories about it. Some thought it was built by giants. Some called it the Picts' Wall. And in the eighteen hundreds, a man called John Clayton, who lived near the wall, began to buy the land along it, to protect it. He paid workers to dig out the old forts and repair the wall. Thanks to him, many parts of the wall survive today.

[pause]

{img:old-roman-coin-found-in-field-farmer-hand-earth-later}
Over the years, farmers along the wall found many Roman things in their fields. Coins, pottery, brooches, weapons and tools. Altars and gravestones with inscriptions. Each find told a little more about the lives of the soldiers who had lived there. And in the twentieth century, the great discovery of the Vindolanda tablets brought their voices back to life.

[long-pause]

As for Lucius and his family, they were not real people. But they were like thousands of real soldiers who lived along the wall. Soldiers from far away, who came to the edge of the world, built a wall, made friends, fell in love and grew old in a land that was not theirs at first, but became their home. Their names are on the centurial stones, the altars and the gravestones. And their stories are still there, in the stones of the wall, if you know how to look.

[long-pause]

{img:hadrians-wall-today-walkers-path-following-wall-over-crags-sunshine}
Today, Hadrian's Wall is one of the most famous ancient monuments in the world. It is a World Heritage Site. And every year, thousands of people come to walk along it. There is a long footpath, which follows the whole wall, from coast to coast, for about one hundred and thirty-five kilometres. Most walkers take about a week to walk the whole way.

[long-pause]

The most beautiful part of the wall is in the middle, where Lucius's century worked. Here, the wall climbs up and down over steep, rocky hills. It runs along the top of high cliffs, called crags, with lakes below. The views are wide and wild. On a clear day, you can see for kilometres in every direction. The wind blows, the clouds race across the sky, and the curlews call, just as they did two thousand years ago.

[pause]

In one famous place, the wall dips down into a little valley, where a lonely sycamore tree once stood, right beside the wall. It was one of the most photographed trees in Britain. Sadly, it was cut down in 2023. But people still come to the place, and a new tree is growing from a part of the old one.

[pause]

{img:housesteads-fort-ruins-today-stone-foundations-hillside-visitors}
Along the wall, visitors can explore the ruins of the old forts. At Housesteads, on a steep hillside, you can walk through the stone foundations of the barracks, the headquarters, the granaries and the hospital. You can even see the Roman toilets, with their long stone channels for running water. Children love them. And from the walls of the fort, the view over the hills is breathtaking.

[pause]

At Vindolanda, the excavations are still going on. Every summer, archaeologists and volunteers dig in the ground, and every year they find new things. Shoes, combs, toys, tools, and sometimes new writing tablets. In the museum at Vindolanda, you can see hundreds of Roman shoes, of every size, from big soldiers' boots to tiny children's sandals. They have survived in the wet ground for almost two thousand years.

[long-pause]

{img:vindolanda-museum-roman-shoes-display-children-sandals-today}
The most precious Vindolanda tablets, including the birthday invitation of Claudia Severa, are kept in the British Museum in London. They are displayed in a dark case, to protect the faded ink. People stand in front of them for a long time, reading the translations. The socks, the beer, the birthday party. It is one of the most moving things in the whole museum.

[pause]

At Caerleon, in Wales, where Lucius first arrived, you can still see the remains of the legionary fortress. There are the barracks, with their rows of rooms for the soldiers. There is the amphitheatre, a great oval of grassy banks, where the soldiers once trained. And there is the bathhouse, now covered with a modern roof, with its great swimming pool. In the museum next to it, you can see the little gemstones and the baby teeth found in its drains.

[pause]

{img:caerleon-amphitheatre-today-grassy-oval-arena-families-walking}
In Nîmes, in the south of France, the old town of Nemausus, the Roman temple still stands, almost perfectly preserved, after two thousand years. It is called the Maison Carrée, the square house. The great amphitheatre is still there too, and is still used for concerts and shows. And outside the town, the great aqueduct bridge, the Pont du Gard, still crosses the river valley, with its three rows of arches.

[long-pause]

So the two homes of Lucius, the warm south and the cold north, are both still there. And between them, the old Roman roads still run, under the modern roads, all the way across France and England. If you travel from Nîmes to Hadrian's Wall today, you follow, almost exactly, the same long road that thousands of young soldiers took, long ago, to the edge of the world.

[long-pause]

{img:hadrians-wall-evening-sunset-milecastle-soldier-silhouette-hills}
So we leave the Roman frontier tonight, around the year 120, as the evening falls. The sun is going down behind the western hills. Its last golden light is shining on the long stone wall, as it runs across the land, over the hills and down into the valleys. The sky is turning pink and purple. And the wind, which has been blowing all day, is slowly dying down.

[long-pause]

In the fort, the evening trumpet sounds. The gates are closed. The lamps are being lit in the barracks. Smoke rises from the bakery ovens, and the smell of fresh bread drifts through the streets. The soldiers are coming back from the bathhouse, clean and warm, with their hair still wet. They are talking quietly, laughing a little, on their way to supper.

[pause]

{img:barracks-evening-soldiers-eating-together-lamp-light-warm-room}
In a small barrack room, eight soldiers are sitting together around a pot of stew. Marcus has cooked tonight, so it is good. Felix is humming a song from Africa. Quintus is reading a letter by the light of the lamp. The two brothers from Gaul are arguing quietly about something. Aulus is mending a cloak. Titus is leaning back against the wall, with his eyes half closed. And the youngest of them, a young man from the south, is writing a letter home to his mother.

[long-pause]

Outside, on the wall, a sentry is standing in a tower, wrapped in his cloak. He is looking out over the dark hills to the north. The first stars are coming out. Far away, an owl is calling. Down in the valley, a little stream is murmuring over the stones. Everything is quiet and peaceful. The sentry leans on the wall, and breathes in the cool night air, and waits patiently for his watch to end.

[pause]

{img:vicus-night-small-houses-lamps-windows-fort-walls-behind-stars}
In the village outside the gates, the lamps are glowing in the windows of the little houses. A mother is singing a lullaby to her baby, in a language that is half British and half Latin. The tavern is closing. A dog is curled up on a doorstep. And in the stable, Cicero the mule is standing with his eyes closed, dreaming whatever mules dream about.

[long-pause]

A mother there is looking up at the stars too, thinking of her son at the edge of the world. And somehow, under the same sky, they do not feel quite so far apart.
