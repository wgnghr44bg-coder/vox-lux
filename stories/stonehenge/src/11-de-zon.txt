Why was Stonehenge built? We may never know everything about it. But one thing is very clear. It is lined up with the sun.

If you stand in the middle of Stonehenge on the shortest day of the year, in winter, and look south-west, you see the sun go down. It sets exactly between the stones of the tallest trilithon. It is as if the stones were made to frame it, like a picture.

--

And if you stand in the middle on the longest day of the year, in summer, and look the other way, toward the north-east, you see the sun come up. It rises right along the path that leads into the circle. It comes up over a large stone that stands outside the ring. This stone is called the Heel Stone.

The midwinter sunset and the midsummer sunrise lie on the same line. The builders lined up the whole monument along this line. They did it on purpose, and with great care.

{img:midsummer-sunrise-heel-stone-avenue-golden-rays-mist-dawn-ancient}
For farming people, the year was everything. The seasons told them when to plant and when to harvest. They told them when the animals would have their young, and when the cold would come. And the turning points of the year were the solstices, the longest day and the shortest day.

In spring, the farmers watched for the first warm days to sow their barley. In summer, they watched the grain grow tall and turn golden. In autumn, they cut it with flint sickles and stored it carefully. And in winter, they waited by the fire for the light to come back.

--

Midwinter must have been a time of quiet worry. The days get shorter and shorter, and the sun stays lower and lower in the sky. It is cold and dark, and the stores of food are getting smaller. What if the sun kept going away, people might have wondered? What if it never came back? But at midwinter, the sun seems to stop for a few days. And then it turns. The days start to get longer again. The light comes back, a little more each day.

Many experts think midwinter was the most important time at Stonehenge. The great feast at Durrington Walls was held at midwinter. The pig bones tell us that. Perhaps people gathered at Stonehenge to watch the last sunset of the old year. They came to say goodbye to the old sun. And they came to welcome the new one.

{img:people-watching-solstice-silhouettes-stones-sky-orange-quiet-awe}
Imagine standing in the circle with thousands of others, watching the sun sink between the great stones. You feel the cold on your face, and the darkness coming. But you know that tomorrow, the light will begin to return. It must have been a powerful feeling. Even today, people who stand there at the solstice say they feel it too.

Afterwards, people walked home slowly in the dusk, talking quietly. Some held hands, and some sang softly. The children skipped ahead and ran back again. And behind them, the great stones stood dark against the last glow of the sky. The longest night had begun, but tomorrow would be a little brighter.
