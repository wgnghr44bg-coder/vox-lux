After the great stones were raised, Stonehenge went on changing.

The bluestones were moved around several times over the centuries. First they stood in one shape, and then in another. Sometimes they made a ring, and sometimes a horseshoe, inside the great sarsen circle. People kept rearranging them, as if they were still looking for the best way. Stonehenge was never quite finished. It was always being worked on.

--

In the age of bronze that followed, people built hundreds of round burial mounds on the hills around Stonehenge. They look like little round hills. They are still there today, and you can see them on the skyline all around. Important people were laid to rest in them, with bronze daggers and gold, amber and beads. People wanted to rest close to the great stones.

Carvings of bronze axes and daggers were even cut into some of the sarsens. They are hard to see now, worn smooth by time and weather. But on a sunny evening, when the light is low, you can find them if you know where to look. There are little shapes of axe heads, carved on the old grey stones.

{img:carvings-bronze-axe-heads-on-sarsen-stone-low-evening-sun-faint}
Then slowly, people stopped coming to Stonehenge. Around three thousand years ago, the great gatherings came to an end. Nobody knows exactly why. Times changed, and beliefs changed, and new ways of life came. Stonehenge stood on the plain, quiet and empty. Sheep grazed among the stones, and birds nested on the lintels.

--

Over the centuries, some of the stones fell over. Storms and frost slowly pushed them down. Some were taken away for building. Some were broken. In the Middle Ages, people told stories about how Stonehenge was made. One famous story said that the wizard Merlin moved the stones by magic, all the way from Ireland. Others said that giants had built it. Nobody remembered the real builders anymore.

{img:medieval-shepherd-sheep-among-fallen-stones-stonehenge-moonlight-alone}
Imagine a shepherd in the Middle Ages, on a moonlit night. He sits among the fallen stones with his dog, wrapped in his cloak. His sheep graze quietly around him. He looks up at the great stones in the moonlight and wonders who put them there. Perhaps he thinks of giants, or wizards. And then he pulls his hood up and dozes against a stone until morning.

But the stones stayed, most of them. They stood tall and grey on the empty plain. They stood through Roman times and Saxon times and Norman times. They stood all through the Middle Ages. They were waiting for people to come back and try to understand them.

--

In later centuries, travellers on horseback stopped to look at the stones on their way across the plain. Some carved their names into the soft surfaces, which we would not do today. Painters came to paint the stones in storms and in sunsets. Poets came and wrote about them. And slowly, people began to ask again who had built them, and why.

For a while, Stonehenge even belonged to private owners. In 1915, it was sold at an auction. A local man bought it, as a present for his wife. She did not much like it, people say. So a few years later, he gave it to the nation. Since then, it has belonged to everyone.
