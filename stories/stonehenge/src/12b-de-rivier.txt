The river Avon flows between Durrington Walls and Stonehenge. And some archaeologists think the river was at the heart of everything.

Here is one idea about it. Durrington Walls, with its wooden circles and its houses and its feasts, was the place of the living. Wood is alive, and it grows and changes, just like people. And Stonehenge, with its stone circles and the ashes of the ancestors, was the place of those who had gone before. Stone is cold and hard, and it lasts forever. It does not change, just as the memory of the ancestors does not change.

--

And the river joined these two places together. From Durrington Walls, an avenue leads down to the river. From the river, the Stonehenge Avenue leads up to the stones. So a journey from the land of the living to the land of the ancestors went down one road. Then it went along the river, and up the other road.

Perhaps, when someone passed away, their ashes were carried on this journey. Perhaps the river was like a border between the two worlds. Many peoples in history have thought of rivers in that way. A river to cross, they said, between this life and what comes after.

{img:neolithic-small-boat-river-carrying-offering-flowers-dawn-gentle-reeds}
We cannot know this for sure, because the people left no words. But the shape of the land and the monuments tells a story. It is a careful and thoughtful story. They built their monuments to make these journeys, and to mark them.

--

The river was also simply very useful in everyday life. It was a road for small boats made of hollowed-out logs. Wood and food and people could travel along it. It gave water for drinking and cooking and washing. There were fish and eels to catch, and reeds to cut for roofs. There were ducks and geese to hunt. In the cold winter mornings, mist rose from the water like smoke. Swans glided slowly past, white and silent. And herons stood perfectly still in the shallows, waiting.

On the morning after the midwinter ceremony, the family walks back along the river to Durrington Walls. The grandmother is very tired after the long night. She walks slowly, and Ula holds her arm. They stop to rest on the river bank, on a fallen tree. The grandmother looks at the water for a long time, without speaking. The mist is rising, and the sun is pale and low.

One day, she says quietly, you will carry me down this river, and up to the stones. To be with my mother and my father. Ula squeezes her hand gently. Not yet, she says. Not for a long time. The grandmother smiles and pats her hand. No, she says, not yet. A heron lifts slowly from the shallows and flies off down the river. And they walk on, together, in the cold bright morning.

Back at the house, Wren makes her grandmother a warm drink of herbs and honey. The grandmother sits by the fire, wrapped in furs, and sips it slowly. Soon she is asleep, with a little smile on her face.
