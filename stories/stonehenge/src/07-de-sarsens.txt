The big stones at Stonehenge are called sarsens.

They are enormous. The tallest one stands more than seven metres high. That is as tall as a house with two floors. And more of it is hidden underground. The biggest sarsens weigh around thirty tons, and some weigh even more. That is as heavy as five elephants, each one.

--

Sarsen is a very hard, grey sandstone. It formed millions of years ago, deep in the ground. Then, slowly, the softer rocks around it washed away. And the sarsens were left lying on the surface of the land. They lay scattered across the hills like giant grey sheep. People call them grey wethers. Wether is an old word for a sheep. And from a distance, they really do look like a flock of sheep lying in the grass.

There are many sarsens on the hills about twenty-five kilometres north of Stonehenge. For a long time, people thought the Stonehenge sarsens must have come from there. But nobody knew exactly where.

{img:scientists-analysing-sarsen-core-sample-laboratory-microscope-later}
Then, a few years ago, scientists found the answer, thanks to a small surprise. In the 1950s, workers repairing Stonehenge drilled right through one of the sarsens. They took out a long cylinder of stone, called a core. One of the workers kept it as a souvenir. He took it home and kept it in his office for many years. Sixty years later, when he was a very old man, he gave it back.

--

The scientists studied this core very carefully. They measured the tiny amounts of different substances inside it. Every place where sarsens are found has its own special mix, like a fingerprint. Then they tested sarsens from all over the area. And they found the match. It was a place called West Woods, about twenty-five kilometres north of Stonehenge. Most of the great sarsens came from there.

Today, West Woods is a quiet forest of tall beech trees. In spring, the ground is covered with bluebells, like a soft blue carpet. A few big grey sarsens still lie among the trees, covered with green moss. They are the ones that were left behind.

{img:west-woods-bluebells-spring-mossy-sarsen-boulders-beech-trees}
Choosing the right stones must have been careful work. The builders needed long, straight ones for the uprights. They needed flatter ones for the lintels that would lie across the top. They walked the hills and looked at every stone. They measured them with ropes and sticks. Which one is long enough, they asked each other? Which one is strong enough? Which one has no hidden crack inside? A mistake would cost months of work.

We can imagine an old stone-chooser walking slowly among the grey boulders. He taps each one with a small stone and listens. He runs his hand along the surface, feeling for cracks. He paces out the length and nods to himself. When he finds a good one, he ties a strip of red cloth around it.

--

There is one more sarsen mystery. A few of the stones at Stonehenge are slightly different from the others. Scientists think they came from a different place, one that has not been found yet. Somewhere on the hills, there may be another old quarry. It may be hidden under the grass or the trees, waiting to be discovered.
