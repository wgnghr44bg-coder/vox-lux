About three kilometres north-east of Stonehenge, beside the river Avon, there is a great gathering place. Today it is called Durrington Walls.

It is huge, a giant ring of earth and ditch, about five hundred metres across. It is much bigger than Stonehenge itself. Inside it, in the time of the builders, there were hundreds of houses. There may have been a thousand houses, or even more. It may have been the biggest settlement in the whole of northern Europe at that time.

--

But people did not live there all year round. Archaeologists think it was used mainly at the great festivals, in midwinter and in midsummer. People came from all over Britain to be there. They stayed for a few weeks. They feasted and held ceremonies and met old friends. They worked together on the great stones. And then they went home again to their own villages.

In the middle of Durrington Walls, there were great circles of wooden posts. Huge tree trunks stood upright in rings, one inside the other. They were like Stonehenge, but made of wood instead of stone. Some people think these wooden circles were for the living. And the stone circle at Stonehenge was for the ancestors. Wood grows and changes, like life that passes. Stone stays the same, like the ancestors who are always there.

{img:timber-circle-durrington-giant-oak-posts-rings-people-ceremony-dusk}
From Durrington Walls, a road leads down to the river Avon. It is paved with flint and lined with banks of earth. People could walk from the houses down to the river. Then they could go along the river and up another road, to Stonehenge. It was a journey from the land of the living to the land of the ancestors.

--

When archaeologists dug at Durrington Walls, they found the floors of houses. They were square houses with chalk floors and hearths in the middle. They were just like the family's house in our story. They found the marks of beds and boxes in the floors. And they found piles and piles of rubbish. There were broken pots and flint tools. And there were bones, lots and lots of bones. Most of them were the bones of pigs.

{img:archaeologists-excavating-house-floor-chalk-hearth-durrington-trench}
The archaeologists worked slowly and carefully, with small trowels and soft brushes. They brushed away the earth, a little at a time, until the white chalk floors appeared. They drew every hole and every stone in their notebooks. They put every bone and every piece of pot into a little bag with a label. In the evenings, they sat together and talked about what they had found that day.

Those bones tell an amazing story. It is a story about the greatest feasts of the Stone Age. And it is a story about where the builders came from.

When we think about Durrington Walls, we can imagine it in midwinter. Smoke rises from hundreds of roofs into the cold sky. Children run between the houses, and dogs bark at every stranger. The smell of roasting pork drifts across the whole valley. People call to each other in many different accents. And down by the river, more families are arriving every day, with their pigs and their baskets. Soon the great feast will begin.
