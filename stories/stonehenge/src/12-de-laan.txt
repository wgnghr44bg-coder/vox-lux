From the entrance of Stonehenge, a wide path leads away across the plain. It is called the Avenue.

The Avenue is about twenty metres wide, with a low bank and a ditch on each side. It starts at the north-east entrance of Stonehenge. It runs straight for about five hundred metres, along the line of the midsummer sunrise and the midwinter sunset. Then it turns, and curves east across the plain. It goes down the slope and all the way to the river Avon. It is almost three kilometres long.

--

When archaeologists dug at the start of the Avenue, they found something surprising. There were natural ridges in the chalk. These long, low ridges were made thousands of years before, by ice and water. And by chance, these ridges run exactly along the line of the midsummer sunrise and the midwinter sunset.

Imagine the first people who noticed this. They stood on the plain at sunrise on the longest day. And they saw the sun come up right at the end of these strange natural lines in the ground. It was as if the earth itself were pointing at the sun. It must have seemed like a sign, a holy place made by the earth and the sky. Maybe that is why Stonehenge was built exactly here.

{img:natural-chalk-ridges-aligned-sunrise-grass-dew-dawn-plain}
The Avenue turned those natural lines into a road. It was a road for processions, for people walking together slowly. They walked from the river up to the stones, or from the stones down to the river. They sang as they walked, and they carried torches and gifts. Sometimes they carried the ashes of those who had passed away.

--

At the far end of the Avenue, beside the river, archaeologists found another small stone circle. They call it Bluestonehenge, because it was made of bluestones. Perhaps the bluestones first stood there, beside the river. Perhaps that was where the stones arrived, after coming by water. And later, they were moved up the Avenue to Stonehenge itself.

On the night of the midwinter feast, the family walks along the Avenue with thousands of others. It is dark, and people carry burning torches. The flames flicker and smoke in the cold air. The torches make a long river of fire, winding slowly up the hill toward the stones. Somewhere ahead, drums are beating softly. Somewhere behind, people are singing an old song. Wren holds her grandmother's hand, and Pip holds his father's. Nobody speaks very much. It is too special and too solemn for words. And at the top of the hill, black against the stars, the great stones are waiting.

When they reach the stones, the drums stop. For a moment there is complete silence, except for the crackle of the torches. Then an old woman begins to sing, alone, in a high clear voice. One by one, others join in. The song rises up between the great stones and into the starry sky. Wren will remember this moment for the rest of her life.
