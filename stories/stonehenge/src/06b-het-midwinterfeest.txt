It is midwinter, and the family has arrived at Durrington Walls.

The journey took them three days. They walked through cold rain and frosty mornings, along the paths across the hills. They drove their pigs ahead of them, with Pip running back and forth to keep them together. The grandmother walked slowly, leaning on her stick. Sometimes Bran carried her for a while on his back. She grumbled about it, but she let him. And at last, on the third evening, they came over a hill. And there it was below them.

--

They saw the great ring of earth, white and green in the fading light. Inside it, there were hundreds of houses, with smoke rising from every roof. There were fires everywhere and people everywhere. There were more people than the children had ever seen in their lives. There were thousands of them, from all over the land. They spoke in different ways and wore different clothes. But they had all come for the same feast.

The family finds a place to stay. Relatives from another settlement have a house there, and they make room. Everyone squeezes in together. It is crowded and warm and noisy. Pip and Wren can hardly sleep that first night. There is too much to see and too many sounds. There is music and laughter, dogs barking and pigs squealing.

{img:crowded-house-durrington-relatives-sharing-hearth-children-excited-night}
The children meet cousins they have never met before. They swap little treasures, a shell for a bead, a feather for a pretty stone. They whisper and giggle under the furs until the grown-ups tell them to hush.

The archaeologists found the bones of thousands of pigs at Durrington Walls. They studied the pigs' teeth carefully. And they found that most of the pigs were about nine months old when they were eaten. Pigs are born in the spring. So nine months later is midwinter. That is how we know the great feasts were held at midwinter.

--

And the pig bones tell us something more. Scientists studied the tiny traces in the bones. These show where each pig grew up. And the pigs came from all over Britain. They came from the south-west and from Wales, from the north of England and even from Scotland. People drove their pigs hundreds of kilometres to bring them to the feast. That shows how important the feast was. People came from every corner of the island.

{img:pigs-being-driven-along-path-chalk-hills-people-winter-journey}
The feast lasts for many days. Pigs are roasted over great fires, and the smell fills the air. People eat until they are full. They drink something sweet, made from honey or grain. They sing and dance and tell stories. Old friends meet again and hug each other. Young people meet for the first time and smile shyly. Families arrange marriages for the summer. Traders swap stone axes and pots and beads.

--

And on the shortest day of the year, everyone goes to the stones. They walk down to the river and along it. Then they walk up the long road to Stonehenge. Thousands of people walk in a long line, as the sun goes down. They gather around the great ring. And they watch.

{img:crowd-watching-midwinter-sunset-through-stones-stonehenge-golden-cold}
The grandmother stands with her family, holding Wren's hand. She is tired from the long walk, but her eyes are bright. The sun sinks lower and lower in the sky. And it sets exactly between the tallest stones. Golden light shines through the gap, onto the faces of the crowd. Everyone is silent. Then a great soft sound goes up from the people. The sun has gone, and the darkest day is here. And from tomorrow, the days will grow longer again.
