Standing the uprights was hard work. But the next part was even harder. Now the lintels had to be lifted on top.

A lintel is a flat stone that lies across the tops of two uprights, like the top of a doorway. At Stonehenge, the great outer circle of uprights was joined all the way around by lintels. It made a ring of stone, floating high in the air. And inside it, five giant pairs of stones each had their own lintel. These are called trilithons, which means three stones. There are two standing stones, with one lying across the top.

--

The lintels weigh about seven tons each. And they had to be lifted more than five metres into the air. How could anyone do that, without machines?

Nobody knows for sure. But one good idea is a wooden platform. The workers build a stack of timber beside the uprights. It looks like a square tower made of logs, laid crossways over each other. They lever the lintel up onto the first layer. Then they add another layer of logs underneath it. And they lever it up again. Layer by layer, the lintel slowly rises. It goes higher and higher, until it is level with the tops of the uprights.

{img:workers-on-timber-platform-levering-lintel-stone-high-up-careful}
Then they slide it across, very carefully, onto the tops of the two uprights. Workers stand on the wooden platform with levers and ropes. Every movement is slow and careful and calm. Down below, everyone watches and waits quietly.

The children watch from a safe distance, sitting on the grass with their parents. Pip asks his father how long it will take. Bran says it might take all day, or even longer. Pip decides he will watch the whole time. But after a while, he falls asleep in the winter sunshine.

--

And when the lintel finally settles into place, there is a soft click. That is because it fits, exactly.

The builders made special joints to hold the stones together. On top of each upright, they carved a little round bump. On the underside of each lintel, they carved a matching hollow. When the lintel comes down, the bump fits neatly into the hollow. It is like a peg fitting into a hole. So the lintel cannot slide off.

{img:mortise-tenon-joint-carved-stone-top-of-upright-close-detail}
And the lintels of the outer circle fit to each other as well. Each end of a lintel is cut with either a groove or a tongue. The tongue of one lintel fits into the groove of the next. It is like the pieces of a wooden puzzle. So the whole ring is locked together. And the lintels are gently curved, so together they make a perfect circle in the sky.

--

It is the only stone circle in the world where the lintels are joined like this. Nowhere else did anyone build quite like Stonehenge. The builders were doing something completely new, something nobody had tried before. And they did it so well that much of it still stands, four and a half thousand years later.

When the last lintel of the outer ring was put in place, there must have been a great feast. People must have stood back and looked up at the ring of stone in the sky. They must have felt very proud of what they had made together.
