How did the bluestones travel from the hills of Wales to Stonehenge? They had to go two hundred and twenty-five kilometres, with no wheels, no metal and no horses.

Nobody knows for certain how it was done. But people have thought about it a great deal. And some have even tried it for themselves.

--

Each stone was probably tied onto a wooden sledge. A sledge is a strong flat frame, made of tree trunks tied together. Teams of people pulled the sledge along with long ropes. The ropes were made of plant fibres or animal hide, twisted together tightly. Under the sledge, they may have laid wooden rails or logs. Or they may have greased the path with animal fat, to make it slippery.

A few years ago, a group of students tried it themselves. They put a stone as heavy as a bluestone on a wooden sledge. They laid wooden rails in front of it. And they pulled. They found that about ten people could pull a stone this heavy, quite easily, on flat ground. They were surprised by this. It was much easier than they had expected.

{img:team-of-people-pulling-sledge-hill-slope-ropes-straining-autumn}
But the journey from Wales is not flat at all. There are hills and valleys, rivers and marshes and thick forests. The people had to find the best way through. They went along open ridges and down river valleys. They went around the steepest places. Some people think the stones might have gone partly by water. Perhaps they travelled on rafts or boats, along the coast or up the rivers. Others think they went all the way by land.

--

Either way, it must have taken a very long time. It took weeks for each stone, and perhaps months. Dozens or even hundreds of people helped. And there were about eighty stones.

Imagine it for a moment. A long line of people winds slowly through the forest, pulling a great grey stone on a sledge. They sing to keep the rhythm of the pulling. At night they stop and make a fire, and they eat and sleep. People from villages along the way come out to watch. Some of them help to pull for a day or two. Some bring baskets of food, bread and nuts and dried meat. Children walk alongside, wide-eyed, and touch the stone for luck.

{img:camp-night-fire-beside-bluestone-on-sledge-forest-people-resting-stars}
Evenings in the camp were warm and friendly. The pullers sat around the fire with sore hands and tired legs. Someone played a bone flute, and others told stories. The stone sat quietly on its sledge in the firelight, as if it were listening too.

There must have been difficult moments along the way. There were steep slopes where the stone could slide back. There were rivers where the sledge could sink into the mud. Rain turned the paths into mud, and the nights were cold. Sometimes, perhaps, a rope snapped, and everyone jumped back.

--

But they did it, again and again. Every one of the bluestones reached the plain at last. It is one of the most amazing journeys of the ancient world. It was not done by one strong man. It was done by many people working together for a long time, for something they believed in.

{img:bluestones-arriving-plain-stonehenge-crowd-welcoming-dusk-celebration}
When each stone finally arrived on the plain, there must have been a great celebration. There was food and music and big fires. And then the stone was set down gently on the grass. It was ready to be raised in the circle, after its long journey from the hills of the west.
