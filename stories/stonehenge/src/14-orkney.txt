If the Altar Stone came from the far north, let us travel there for a while. Let us visit the Orkney Islands.

Orkney is a group of islands off the very top of Scotland. They are wild and windy, and surrounded on every side by the sea. There are almost no trees there at all. But there is plenty of flat stone that splits easily into slabs. And the people of Orkney in the Stone Age used it to build amazing things.

--

On one island, between two lakes, there is a place called the Ness of Brodgar. Archaeologists have been digging there for twenty years. They have found big stone buildings with thick walls. Some had roofs made of stone tiles. Some had painted walls, in red and yellow and black. That is the oldest known paint in Britain. It was a great gathering place, like Durrington Walls in the south. But it was even older.

Nearby stand two great stone circles. The Ring of Brodgar is a huge ring of tall, thin stones. It stands on a narrow strip of land between the lakes. The Stones of Stenness have a few great slabs still standing tall. Both of them are older than the stone circle at Stonehenge.

{img:ring-of-brodgar-tall-thin-stones-heather-loch-sunset-orkney}
In late summer, the heather around the Ring of Brodgar turns purple. The lakes on either side shine silver and blue. Swans and geese float on the water, and curlews call across the moor. At sunset, the tall stones cast long shadows over the heather.

And on the coast, there is a whole village from the Stone Age. It is called Skara Brae. A great storm long ago blew the sand off it, and there it was. There are little stone houses, joined together by covered passages. Inside, the furniture is still there, made of stone. There are stone beds and stone shelves. There are stone boxes set into the floor, for keeping fish or bait. And there is a stone dresser against the back wall, facing the door. That is where the family showed their best things.

--

Grooved ware pottery, the same kind found at Stonehenge, was first made in Orkney. So it seems that ideas travelled from the north to the south. Perhaps the way of building circles travelled too. Perhaps the way of making pots. And perhaps the way of watching the sun.

{img:skara-brae-stone-house-interior-dresser-beds-hearth-sea-light}
Imagine an evening in one of the houses at Skara Brae. The fire burns in the stone hearth in the middle of the room. A mother is mending a fishing net, and a father is sharpening a bone needle. The children are lying on the stone beds, wrapped in sealskins. Outside, the waves are breaking on the beach, and the wind is whistling over the roof.

Now imagine a traveller from Orkney, arriving at the midwinter feast on the plain. He has made a long, long journey. He speaks a little differently from the people of the south. He brings stories from the islands of the north. He tells of stone villages by the sea, and of great circles between the lakes. The people of the plain gather round the fire to listen. They are amazed that the world is so big. And they are amazed that people so far away believe the same things they do.
