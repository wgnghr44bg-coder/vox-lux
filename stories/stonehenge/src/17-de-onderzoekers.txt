About three hundred years ago, people started to study Stonehenge seriously.

One of the first was a man called William Stukeley. He was a doctor who loved old things and old places. He spent many summers at Stonehenge, with his notebooks and his measuring tapes. He drew it carefully and measured every stone. And he noticed something important. The monument was lined up with the midsummer sunrise. He was one of the first people to see it.

--

But Stukeley also had some wrong ideas. He thought Stonehenge was built by the druids, the priests of the Celtic people. We now know that the druids lived two thousand years after Stonehenge was built. But the idea stuck, and many people still believe it. Druids did not build Stonehenge. But druids today do gather there at the solstice to celebrate, dressed in long white robes.

In the 1900s, archaeologists began to dig carefully at Stonehenge. They measured and recorded everything they found. They found the antler picks and the hammerstones. They worked out the different stages of building, one after another. And a new science helped them. It was a way to tell how old things are, by measuring tiny changes in anything that was once alive. It works on bone and antler and wood. That is how we know the ditch was dug around 3000 before Christ. And it is how we know the great stones were raised around 2500.

{img:archaeologists-1950s-excavating-stonehenge-trench-stones-measuring-poles}
In the 1950s and 1960s, some of the fallen stones were lifted and set upright again, using cranes. Some people did not like this. Others said it helped to save the monument for the future.

The archaeologists of those days worked in old jumpers and wide hats. They dug long trenches with spades and trowels. They drank tea from flasks and ate sandwiches sitting on the stones. In the evenings, they wrote up their notes by lamplight in the village inn.

--

In the last twenty years, the discoveries have come faster and faster. There was the great settlement at Durrington Walls, and the pig bones from the midwinter feasts. There were the quarries in Wales, and Bluestonehenge by the river. There was the source of the sarsens in West Woods. There was the Altar Stone from Scotland, and the Amesbury Archer from the Alps. Scientists now have new machines to help them. Some machines can see under the ground without digging. Others can read the tiny traces in teeth and stones. Bit by bit, the story of the builders is coming back to us.

{img:ground-penetrating-radar-survey-field-stonehenge-modern-team-later}
Today, small teams walk slowly across the fields around Stonehenge, pushing machines like lawnmowers. The machines send signals into the ground and listen to the echoes. On a computer screen, hidden pits and ditches and post holes appear. In this way, the plain gives up its secrets without a single spade.

And still, there are many mysteries left. Why exactly did they build it? What did they believe? What songs did they sing as they pulled the stones? What did they call it? We will probably never know these things. But we know much more than anyone did a hundred years ago. And every year, the plain gives up a few more of its secrets.
