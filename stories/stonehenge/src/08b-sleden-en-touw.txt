How many people does it take to pull a stone as heavy as a sarsen? In our own time, people have tried to find out.

Archaeologists and volunteers have done experiments to test it. They made a copy of a big stone, out of concrete, about the same weight as a sarsen. They put it on a wooden sledge. They laid wooden rails in front of it, just as the builders might have done. And then they pulled.

--

In one famous experiment, they found that about two hundred people could pull a very heavy stone up a gentle slope. That sounds like a lot of people. But at Durrington Walls, there were thousands of people gathered together. So there were more than enough hands to do the work.

The volunteers enjoyed it very much. They came from all walks of life, students and teachers, grandparents and children. They laughed when the sledge would not move, and cheered when it did. At the end of the day, they understood the builders a little better. They had felt the weight on the ropes and the joy of moving it together.

They also found some useful tricks. If the rails are greased with animal fat, the sledge slides much more easily. If the path is carefully chosen, avoiding steep hills, the work goes much faster. And if everyone pulls together, in rhythm, the stone moves smoothly. Teamwork matters more than strength.

{img:greased-timber-rails-sledge-runner-close-fat-shine-chalk-path}
The builders probably chose a route that went around the steepest hills. They may have waited for winter, when the ground was frozen hard. A frozen path is firmer than a muddy one, and the sledge does not sink. Some people think they even waited for snow. On snow and ice, a heavy sledge can slide quite well.

--

The ropes were very important to the whole work. Rope made from lime tree bark is strong, but it wears out with use. It frays and weakens and can snap. So the rope makers must have worked all the time. They made new rope and checked the old rope, every day. A snapping rope could cause the stone to slide back. So everyone kept a careful eye on the ropes.

{img:rope-makers-checking-frayed-rope-camp-fire-pile-of-coils-night}
In the evenings, the rope makers sat by the fire with the day's ropes across their knees. They ran their fingers slowly along every length, feeling for weak places. When they found one, they cut it out and spliced in a new piece. Their fingers were rough and quick from years of practice. Behind them, the new coils of rope were piled up, ready for the morning.

How long did it take to bring all the sarsens to Stonehenge? There were about eighty of them. Each one took weeks to move twenty-five kilometres. Some experts think the whole job took many years, perhaps ten or more. Perhaps people worked on it every winter, after the harvest was in. That was when there was time to spare, and food from the feasts. And there were thousands of hands, all gathered together on the plain.
