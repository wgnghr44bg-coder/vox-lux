Once a good sarsen was chosen, it had to be freed from the ground and made ready for its long journey.

Many sarsens lay half buried in the earth. The workers dug all around them with antler picks. Then they dug underneath them. They pushed long, strong wooden levers under the stone. Many people pulled down on the levers at the same moment. And slowly, the great stone lifted a little. They pushed stones and blocks of wood underneath to hold it up. Then they lifted again. Bit by bit, the stone came up out of the ground.

--

Sometimes the stone had to be shaped a little before the journey. The workers used big round hammerstones. These were lumps of very hard stone, held in both hands or dropped from a height. They pounded the sarsen to knock off bumps and corners. It was very slow work, because sarsen is extremely hard. Each blow knocked off only a little dust. Hundreds of hammerstones have been found at Stonehenge. Some of them are worn smooth from so much use.

{img:hammerstone-pounding-sarsen-surface-dust-chips-worker-close-hands}
The workers took turns with the hammerstones, because it made their arms ache. While some pounded, others rested and drank water and talked. The steady thump, thump, thump of the stones echoed through the woods all day. The birds soon got used to it and went on singing.

Then the stone had to be moved onto a sledge. The sledge was a huge wooden frame, strong enough to carry thirty tons. The workers built ramps of earth and timber beside the stone. They levered it onto the sledge, a tiny bit at a time. Then they tied it down firmly with thick ropes. Everyone held their breath while this was done. If the stone rolled the wrong way, it could crack. So everyone moved slowly and carefully, and listened to the leader.

--

All of this needed a great many people. And those people needed a great deal of food. It also needed a lot of wood. Whole groves of trees were cut down for levers and rollers and sledges. Ropes had to be made from the inner bark of lime trees. The bark was soaked and twisted and plaited until the ropes were thick and strong. It took many hands and many days.

{img:rope-making-lime-bark-fibres-women-men-twisting-plaiting-camp}
Rope making was quiet, patient work, often done by the older people. They sat in a circle in the shade, twisting the fibres between their hands. They talked and sang and told old jokes while they worked. Long coils of new rope grew slowly beside them, like sleeping snakes.

At West Woods, there was probably a big camp. People came for weeks at a time to work there. They cut wood and made rope and freed the stones. They ate and slept beside the fires. Children played around the stones, and dogs barked. And each time a great sarsen was ready on its sledge, a cheer went up from the whole camp. That was another one done, ready for the long pull south.

In the evenings, the camp was a cheerful place. People cooked meat and porridge over the fires. Someone always had a story to tell, or a song to sing. And above the tall trees, the stars came out one by one.
