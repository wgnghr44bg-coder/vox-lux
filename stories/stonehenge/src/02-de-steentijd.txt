The people who built Stonehenge lived in a time we call the New Stone Age. Its other name is the Neolithic.

It is called the Stone Age because people did not use metal yet. Their tools were made of stone and wood, bone and antler. Their axes were made of stone, polished until they were smooth and shining. Their knives and scrapers were made of flint, a hard stone that breaks into very sharp edges. They had no iron and no bronze and no copper. That would all come later.

--

And it is called the New Stone Age because something new had happened in Britain, and that new thing was farming. For a very long time before, people in Britain had lived by hunting and gathering. They followed the deer through the forests and gathered nuts and berries. They fished in the rivers and the sea. Then, about one and a half thousand years before our story, farming came to Britain from across the sea.

People began to grow wheat and barley in small fields. They kept cattle and pigs and sheep. They cleared parts of the forest to make room for their fields. They built houses and stayed in one place for longer. And life slowly changed. More food meant more people. More people meant bigger groups, and bigger groups could do bigger things together. Like building great monuments.

{img:polished-stone-axe-flint-tools-antler-pick-wooden-handle-close}
A good stone axe was a precious thing. It took many days to grind and polish one until it was smooth. People looked after their axes carefully and passed them down to their children. Some of the most beautiful axes were never used at all. They were given as gifts, or kept as treasures.

In 2500 before Christ, Britain is a land of forests and grasslands and rivers. People live in small groups, scattered across the land. They farm and herd and gather. They make pots and tools and clothes. They trade with each other, exchanging axes and beads and furs. And they gather together at special places for feasts and ceremonies.

--

They have no writing at all. So we do not know their names, or their language, or their stories. Everything we know about them comes from what they left behind. We have their tools and their pots, their houses and their graves. We have their bones. And we have their great monuments.

{img:grooved-ware-pottery-decorated-spirals-lines-neolithic-hearth}
They make a special kind of pottery. They make big pots with flat bottoms, decorated with grooves and lines and spirals. Archaeologists call it grooved ware. It is found all over Britain, from the far north of Scotland to the south of England. The same kind of pots are found everywhere. That tells us something important about these people. They were connected to each other, across the whole island. Ideas and styles travelled from place to place. And so, perhaps, did people.

--

Life is not easy in the Stone Age. The winters are cold, and illness is common. People do not live very long, by our standards. Forty or fifty years is old. But the people are clever and skilled and strong. They know their land intimately, every hill and stream and tree. They know when the salmon swim up the rivers and when the hazelnuts are ripe. And they work together, always. Because alone, nobody could live for long.
