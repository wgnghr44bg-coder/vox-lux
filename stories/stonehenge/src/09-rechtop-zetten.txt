When a great sarsen finally reaches Stonehenge, the hardest part begins. Now it must be stood upright.

First, the workers dig a pit, a deep hole in the chalk, using their antler picks. The hole is shaped very carefully. One side goes straight down. The other side slopes gently, like a ramp. The pit must be exactly the right depth. If it is too shallow, the stone will not stand firmly. If it is too deep, the stone will be too short.

--

Then the stone is pulled on its sledge until one end sits over the sloping side of the pit. The workers lever it forward, slowly, slowly. The heavy end tips down into the pit. It slides down the slope and stops against the straight side. Now the stone is leaning at an angle. It is half down in the pit and half up in the air.

Next, it must be pulled upright. Wooden frames are built beside it. Ropes are tied around the top of the stone. Teams of people pull on the ropes from the other side. Others push with long wooden poles from underneath. Slowly, very slowly, the great stone rises. It rises higher and higher. Everyone pulls together, and the leader calls out the rhythm. And at last, the stone stands straight up.

{img:workers-packing-stones-chalk-around-base-upright-sarsen-pit}
Quickly, the workers fill the gap around the bottom of the stone. They pack it with broken stones and chalk. They pound it down hard with heavy wooden posts. This holds the stone firmly in place. It must not move, and it must not lean. It must stand for ever.

--

This is an exciting moment for everyone watching. If the ropes slip, the stone could tip back down. If the pit is not deep enough, it could wobble. If the angle is wrong, the stone will lean to one side. Everyone watches closely. Some people whisper prayers. Some hold their breath. And when the stone is finally standing straight, with its base packed tight, the whole crowd cheers. Another stone stands.

After the cheering, there is always a small celebration. Bowls of honey drink are passed around, and someone plays a drum. The workers sit down in the grass, tired and happy. Children run up to touch the new stone, and they press their hands against its cold, rough side.

Some of the great stones at Stonehenge are still standing in their holes today. They stand exactly where they were raised, four and a half thousand years ago. Archaeologists have dug carefully around their bases. They have found the packing stones and the antler picks. And they have even found a few broken hammerstones, dropped by the workers when the job was done.

--

The builders must have planned the whole circle very carefully before they raised a single stone. They needed the right number of stones and the right heights. They needed exactly the right spacing between them. They probably marked out the circle with a long rope, tied to a post in the middle. They walked round and round, holding the rope tight. And they marked the places for the holes on the ground. It is just the way a child might draw a circle with a string and a stick.

We can imagine the planners on a quiet morning, before the work began. An old woman and two men walk slowly around the circle, talking softly. They push small pegs into the ground where each stone will go. They step back and look, and move a peg a little. When they are happy, they nod to each other and smile.
