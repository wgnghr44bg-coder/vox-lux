Tonight we go about four and a half thousand years back. We go to a wide green plain in the south of England. The year is around 2500 before Christ. And on that plain, people are raising great stones toward the sky.

[long-pause]

It is early morning. Mist lies over the rolling grassland. The sky in the east is turning pink. And in the middle of the plain, there is a ring. A ring of huge grey stones, standing upright. Some are taller than three people. Some have great flat stones lying across their tops, like the lintel of a door. Some are not finished yet. Wooden frames lean against them. Ropes lie coiled on the grass. Smoke rises from a fire nearby, where people are waking up.

[pause]

This is Stonehenge. One of the most famous places in the world. And one of the most mysterious.

[pause]

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

{img:neolithic-people-hauling-sarsen-sledge-ropes-many-people-chalk-plain}
Who built it? How? Why? For hundreds of years, people have wondered. Some said it was built by giants. Some said a wizard flew the stones there by magic. Some said it was built by Romans, or by druids, or by people from far away. None of that was true.

[pause]

It was built by the people who lived here. Farmers and herders. People with no metal tools, no wheels, no writing. Just stone and wood and antler and rope. And their own strength. And their own cleverness. And a great shared dream.

[pause]

{img:midsummer-sunrise-through-stonehenge-trilithons-golden-light-ancient}
In the last fifty years, archaeologists have learned an amazing amount. They have found where the stones came from. Some from not far away. Some from the mountains of Wales, two hundred kilometres away. One even from the far north of Scotland. They have found the great village where the builders gathered for their feasts. They have found the bones of the pigs they ate. And they have learned that Stonehenge is lined up with the sun.

[long-pause]

{img:neolithic-family-walking-across-plain-toward-stones-winter-morning-cattle}
Tonight we will walk on this plain in the time of the builders. We will meet a family who comes here for the midwinter feast. We will follow the long journey of the bluestones from Wales. We will help pull a great sarsen stone across the hills. We will watch the stones being raised and the lintels lifted. And at the end, we will stand inside the ring and watch the sun rise on the longest day of the year.

[long-pause]

{img:neolithic-britain-farmers-fields-cattle-woodland-clearing-round-houses}
The people who built Stonehenge lived in a time we call the New Stone Age. Or the Neolithic.

[pause]

It is called the Stone Age because people did not use metal yet. Their tools were made of stone and wood and bone and antler. Their axes were made of polished stone. Their knives and scrapers were made of flint, a hard stone that breaks into sharp edges. They had no iron, no bronze, no copper. Not yet.

[long-pause]

And it is called the New Stone Age because something new had happened. Farming. For a long time before, people in Britain had lived by hunting and gathering. They followed the deer and gathered nuts and berries and fished in the rivers. Then, about a thousand five hundred years before our story, farming came to Britain. From across the sea.

[pause]

People began to grow wheat and barley. They kept cattle and pigs and sheep. They cleared forests to make fields. They built houses and stayed in one place. Life changed. More food meant more people. More people meant bigger groups. And bigger groups could do bigger things together. Like building great monuments.

[pause]

{img:polished-stone-axe-flint-tools-antler-pick-wooden-handle-close}
In 2500 before Christ, Britain is a land of forests and grasslands and rivers. People live in small groups, scattered across the land. They farm and herd. They make pots and tools and clothes. They trade with each other. And they gather together at special places for feasts and ceremonies.

[long-pause]

They have no writing. So we do not know their names or their language or their stories. Everything we know comes from what they left behind. Their tools and pots. Their houses and graves. Their bones. And their monuments.

[pause]

{img:grooved-ware-pottery-decorated-spirals-lines-neolithic-hearth}
They make a special kind of pottery. Big flat-bottomed pots, decorated with grooves and lines and spirals. Archaeologists call it grooved ware. It is found all over Britain. From the far north of Scotland to the south of England. The same kind of pots. That tells us something important. These people were connected. Ideas and styles travelled across the whole island. And so, perhaps, did people.

[long-pause]

Life is not easy. Winters are cold. Illness is common. Many babies do not grow up. People do not live very long, by our standards. Forty or fifty is old. But people are clever and skilled and strong. They know their land intimately. Every hill and stream and tree. They know when the salmon run and when the hazelnuts ripen. And they work together. Because alone, nobody could survive for long.

[long-pause]

{img:salisbury-plain-rolling-chalk-downs-grass-sky-barrows-distant-river}
Stonehenge stands on a wide open land of gentle hills. Today it is called Salisbury Plain.

[pause]

Under the grass, the ground is chalk. Soft white rock, made long ago from the tiny shells of sea creatures. The chalk makes the land dry and the grass short and sweet. Good for grazing cattle and sheep. When you dig into it, it is bright white. In the time of the builders, many monuments were dug into the chalk. When they were new, they shone white across the green land. You could see them from far away.

[long-pause]

The plain is not empty. There are already many monuments here, built long before Stonehenge. Long mounds where the ancestors lie. A great long ditch, almost three kilometres long, that runs across the land. Rings of earth and ditches. And later, many round mounds will be built on the hilltops.

[pause]

It is a sacred landscape. A place of the ancestors. People come here from far away, again and again, for hundreds of years.

[pause]

{img:river-avon-wiltshire-valley-willows-water-meadow-dawn-neolithic}
To the east of Stonehenge flows the river Avon. A gentle river winding through a green valley. It flows south to the sea. In the time of the builders, the river is an important road. People and goods travel along it by boat. And it may have been important in other ways too.

[long-pause]

The weather on the plain can be wild. In winter, cold winds blow across the open land. Rain and snow sweep over the hills. There are few trees to shelter behind. In summer, the sun shines warm on the grass, and skylarks sing high above. Big skies. Long views. A feeling of being close to the heavens.

[pause]

{img:skylark-singing-over-grassland-summer-sky-chalk-downs-flowers}
Maybe that is why the people chose this place. The open sky. The wide horizon. Where you can see the sun rise and set without anything in the way. Where you can feel the seasons turning. A place between the earth and the sky.

[long-pause]

{img:early-stonehenge-3000bc-circular-ditch-bank-chalk-white-ring-plain}
Stonehenge was not built all at once. It was built and changed and rebuilt over many hundreds of years. Like an old church that keeps getting new parts.

[pause]

It began about five hundred years before our story. Around the year 3000 before Christ. And at first, it had no great stones at all.

[long-pause]

The first Stonehenge was a ring of earth. People dug a round ditch in the chalk, about a hundred metres across. They piled the white chalk into a bank beside it. They left two openings, like doors. One faced north-east. One faced south. Inside the ring, they dug a circle of fifty-six holes. Some of these holes may have held wooden posts. Or small stones.

[pause]

How did they dig the ditch? With picks made from the antlers of red deer. They hacked at the chalk with the antler picks. They scooped up the loose chalk with the shoulder bones of cattle, like shovels. And they carried it away in baskets. Archaeologists have found dozens of these antler picks in the ditch. Left behind by the diggers. Some still have the marks of their hands.

[pause]

{img:antler-picks-cattle-shoulder-blade-shovels-chalk-ditch-digging}
Inside the ring, people laid their dead to rest. Not buried, but after cremation, the ashes placed in the holes and in the ground. Many people. Over hundreds of years. Men and women. Some of them came from far away. Perhaps they were important people. Leaders or priests. Perhaps they were the families of those who built it. We remember them gently.

[long-pause]

So from the very beginning, Stonehenge was a place of the dead. A place to honour the ancestors. And the circle and the openings may already have been linked to the sun.

[pause]

{img:white-chalk-bank-ring-evening-people-gathering-fire-smoke-ancient}
For five hundred years, the ring of earth stood on the plain. People came and went. Ceremonies were held. The chalk bank slowly turned green with grass. And then, around the time of our story, something new began. People decided to build with stone. Huge stone. And they would bring it from very far away.

[long-pause]

Why a circle? Perhaps because the sky looks round. Perhaps because the sun and the moon are round. Perhaps because a circle has no beginning and no end, like the turning seasons. Or perhaps because, in a circle, everyone stands as equals. Facing the middle. Facing each other. Nobody knows. But people all over the world, in many times, have gathered in circles to remember and to celebrate.

[long-pause]

{img:preseli-hills-wales-rocky-outcrops-spotted-dolerite-pillars-misty}
At Stonehenge, there are two kinds of stone. The big ones and the small ones.

[pause]

The small ones are called bluestones. When they are wet, they have a soft blue-grey colour. There were once about eighty of them. Each one is about two metres tall and weighs about two to four tons. As heavy as two or three cars. They are not as big as the great stones. But where they came from is one of the most amazing parts of the whole story.

[long-pause]

The bluestones do not come from anywhere near Stonehenge. They come from the Preseli Hills in the west of Wales. About two hundred and twenty-five kilometres away. That is a long, long way. Over hills and rivers and through forests. With no roads and no wheels.

[pause]

Scientists found out exactly where they came from. They studied the tiny crystals inside the stones. Then they searched the Welsh hills for rock that matched. And they found it. On rocky outcrops on the tops of the hills. At one place, the rock grows naturally in tall pillars, standing side by side. Like organ pipes. The builders only had to lever them free.

[pause]

{img:neolithic-quarry-welsh-hillside-wooden-wedges-pillar-stone-being-freed}
Archaeologists even found the old quarries. They found the stone tools the workers used. They found little platforms of stone where the pillars were lowered down. They found the remains of fires where the workers cooked their food. And they found that the stones were taken from here around the time Stonehenge was being built.

[long-pause]

But why? Why bring stones from so far away? There are plenty of stones much closer to Stonehenge. Nobody knows for sure. Maybe the bluestones were special. Maybe the Preseli Hills were a holy place. Some of the stones make a ringing sound when you hit them. Like a bell. Maybe that was important. Maybe the stones carried the spirit of the place they came from. Or the spirit of the ancestors.

[pause]

{img:people-tapping-bluestone-listening-ringing-sound-hilltop-wales-wind}
One idea is that the people of Wales moved east, to the plain. And they brought their sacred stones with them. Like bringing a piece of home. Scientists have even found what may be an older stone circle in Wales. Where some of the bluestones may have stood first. Before they were taken down. And carried away. To Stonehenge.

[long-pause]

{img:bluestone-on-wooden-sledge-rollers-team-pulling-welsh-valley-forest}
How did the bluestones travel from the hills of Wales to Stonehenge? Two hundred and twenty-five kilometres. With no wheels and no metal and no horses.

[pause]

Nobody knows for sure. But people have thought about it a lot. And some have even tried it.

[long-pause]

Each stone was probably tied onto a wooden sledge. A strong flat frame of tree trunks. Then teams of people pulled the sledge with ropes. Ropes made of plant fibres or animal hide, twisted together. Under the sledge, they may have laid wooden rails. Or logs to roll on. Or they may have greased the path with animal fat to make it slippery.

[pause]

A few years ago, a group of students tried it. They put a stone as heavy as a bluestone on a wooden sledge. They laid wooden rails in front. And they pulled. They found that about ten people could pull a stone this heavy. Quite easily, on flat ground. They were surprised. It was easier than they had thought.

[pause]

{img:team-of-people-pulling-sledge-hill-slope-ropes-straining-autumn}
But the journey from Wales is not flat. There are hills and valleys and rivers and marshes and forests. The people had to find the best way. Over open ridges. Along river valleys. Around the steep places. Some think the stones might have gone partly by water. On rafts or boats, along the coast or up the rivers. Others think they went all the way by land.

[long-pause]

Either way, it must have taken a long time. Weeks for each stone. Maybe months. With dozens or hundreds of people helping. And there were about eighty stones.

[pause]

Imagine it. A long line of people winding through the forest. Pulling a great grey stone on a sledge. Singing to keep the rhythm. Stopping at night to make a fire and eat and sleep. Villages along the way coming out to watch. Maybe to help. Maybe to bring food.

[pause]

{img:camp-night-fire-beside-bluestone-on-sledge-forest-people-resting-stars}
There must have been dangers. Steep slopes where the stone could slide back. Rivers where the sledge could sink in the mud. Rain that turned the paths into mud. Cold nights. Tired people. Sometimes, perhaps, a rope snapped. And everyone jumped back.

[long-pause]

But they did it. Again and again. Every one of the bluestones reached the plain. It is one of the most amazing journeys of the ancient world. Not because one strong man did it. But because many people worked together for a long time. For something they believed in.

[pause]

{img:bluestones-arriving-plain-stonehenge-crowd-welcoming-dusk-celebration}
When each stone finally arrived on the plain, there must have been a great celebration. Food and music and fires. And the stone was set down. Ready to be raised in the circle. After its long journey from the hills of the west.

[long-pause]

{img:neolithic-family-group-father-mother-children-grandmother-wool-cloaks}
Let us meet a family from the time of the builders.

[pause]

They live about a day's walk north of Stonehenge, in a small settlement by a stream. There are four houses there, close together. About twenty people altogether. Parents and grandparents and children and cousins. They farm a few small fields. They keep cattle and pigs and a few sheep. And they gather food from the woods and the stream.

[long-pause]

The father is called Bran. That is not his real name. We do not know any real names from this time. But we will call him Bran. He is a herder. He knows every one of his cattle. He can tell them apart by their colour and their horns and the way they walk. He is strong. He has helped to pull great stones before.

[pause]

The mother is called Ula. She makes pots and weaves cloth. She grows barley and wheat in the little fields. She knows every plant in the woods. Which ones heal and which ones harm. Which ones are good to eat in spring and which in autumn. People in the settlement come to her when they are ill.

[pause]

{img:mother-making-grooved-pottery-coil-clay-hut-doorway-children-watching}
There are three children. A girl of twelve called Wren. A boy of eight called Pip. And a baby, a little girl, who is always laughing. And there is the grandmother. She is old for this time. Perhaps fifty. Her back is bent and her hands are stiff. But she remembers everything. All the stories. All the songs. All the ways of the ancestors.

[long-pause]

It is late autumn. The harvest is in. The leaves are falling. The days are getting shorter and colder. And soon the family will make a journey. South, to the great gathering on the plain. To the midwinter feast. Bran will bring pigs to share. Ula will bring pots of food. The children are very excited. They have heard so many stories.

[pause]

{img:family-preparing-journey-pigs-baskets-pots-autumn-leaves-settlement}
But the grandmother is worried. The journey is long and cold. She is old. Will she be strong enough this year? She does not want to stay behind. Every midwinter of her life, she has gone to the plain. To see the sun go down between the great stones. She is not going to miss it now. Bran says he will carry her on his back if he has to. She laughs and says she is not a sack of barley. But she is grateful.

[long-pause]

{img:neolithic-square-house-chalk-floor-central-hearth-wooden-furniture-dim}
The family lives in a simple house. Let us look inside.

[pause]

It is a square house, about five metres on each side. The walls are made of woven branches of hazel, like a big basket. The gaps are filled with clay mixed with straw and dung. When it dries, it is hard and keeps out the wind. The roof is thick and steep, covered with straw or reeds or turf. Smoke from the fire seeps out through the roof.

[long-pause]

The floor is made of chalk, beaten flat and hard. It is white and clean. In the middle there is a hearth. A round fireplace, with a low rim of clay. The fire burns day and night in winter. It gives warmth and light. And everything is cooked on it. Pots of stew and porridge. Meat on sticks. Flat bread on hot stones.

[pause]

Along the walls, there are wooden boxes and beds. Archaeologists have found the marks in the chalk floors where they stood. Little slots where wooden planks were set into the ground. Like the furniture in the stone houses of Orkney, far to the north. The family sleeps on beds of straw and furs. Wrapped in woollen blankets and skins.

[pause]

{img:family-eating-around-hearth-pots-meat-bread-evening-firelight-house}
There is not much in the house. A few pots. Baskets. Tools of stone and bone and wood. Furs and skins. Bags of grain hanging from the roof beams, away from the mice. A loom for weaving. A grinding stone for the grain. Everything has a use.

[long-pause]

In the evening, the family sits around the fire. The baby sleeps in a fur bag. The grandmother tells stories. About the old days. About the ancestors. About the sun and the moon. About the great stones on the plain and why they were raised. The children listen with big eyes in the firelight.

[pause]

{img:grandmother-telling-story-firelight-children-wide-eyes-neolithic-house}
Outside, the wind blows across the hills. An owl calls. The cattle shift in their pen. And the family falls asleep in the warm smoky darkness, close together. Dreaming of the journey to come.

[long-pause]

Around the house, there is a little yard. A pen for the pigs, made of woven hazel. A shelter for the cattle in the worst weather. A pit for storing grain, lined with clay. A pile of firewood. A place where flint is knapped into tools, with sharp flakes lying all around. And beyond, the small fields, the stream and the woods. A whole world, within a short walk.

[long-pause]

{img:neolithic-children-playing-stream-catching-fish-hands-summer-woodland}
What is life like for children in the time of Stonehenge?

[pause]

They work and they play, like children everywhere. As soon as they can walk, they help. They fetch water from the stream. They gather sticks for the fire. They pick berries and nuts in the woods. They watch the smaller children. They scare birds away from the fields.

[long-pause]

As they get older, they learn the skills of the grown-ups. Boys and girls learn to herd cattle and pigs. They learn to grind grain and make bread. They learn to make pots from clay and to fire them. They learn to make tools from flint, knocking off sharp flakes with a stone. They learn to make cloth and rope. They learn which plants are good to eat. And which are dangerous.

[pause]

Wren, the girl of twelve, is learning to make pots. Her mother shows her how. You roll the clay into long snakes. You coil them round and round to build up the sides. You smooth them with wet fingers. Then you press patterns into the clay with a stick or a bone. Lines and grooves and spirals. Wren's first pot was lopsided. It cracked in the fire. She cried. Her mother hugged her and said, every potter's first pot cracks. Now her pots are getting better.

[pause]

{img:girl-learning-pottery-coiling-clay-mother-guiding-hands-outside-hut}
Pip, the boy of eight, is learning to herd the pigs. Pigs are clever and naughty. They run off into the woods to look for acorns. Pip runs after them with a stick. Sometimes he catches them. Sometimes they lead him a long chase through the trees. He comes home muddy and scratched. But proud.

[long-pause]

The children play too. They swim in the stream in summer. They climb trees. They play chasing games in the long grass. They make little figures out of clay. They make toys out of sticks and string. They listen to stories. And they dream.

[pause]

{img:boy-chasing-pigs-oak-woodland-acorns-autumn-laughing-mud}
And this year, for the first time, Wren and Pip will help to pull a stone. At the great gathering on the plain, everyone helps. Even the children. Their father says they can hold the very end of the rope. Pip has talked about nothing else for weeks.

[long-pause]

{img:durrington-walls-huge-earthwork-circle-many-houses-smoke-gathering-winter}
About three kilometres north-east of Stonehenge, beside the river Avon, there is a great gathering place. Today it is called Durrington Walls.

[pause]

It is huge. A giant ring of earth and ditch, about five hundred metres across. Much bigger than Stonehenge. Inside it, at the time of the builders, there were hundreds of houses. Maybe a thousand. Maybe more. It may have been the biggest settlement in the whole of northern Europe at that time.

[long-pause]

But people did not live there all year. Archaeologists think it was used mainly in winter. And in summer. At the great festivals. People came from all over Britain. They stayed for a few weeks. They feasted and held ceremonies. They worked on the great stones. Then they went home.

[pause]

In the middle of Durrington Walls, there were great circles of wooden posts. Huge tree trunks standing upright in rings. Like Stonehenge, but in wood. Some think these wooden circles were for the living. And the stone circle at Stonehenge was for the dead. Wood that rots, for life that passes. Stone that lasts, for the ancestors who are forever.

[pause]

{img:timber-circle-durrington-giant-oak-posts-rings-people-ceremony-dusk}
From Durrington Walls, a road leads down to the river Avon. It is paved with flint and lined with banks. People could walk from the houses down to the river. And then, along the river and up another road, to Stonehenge. A journey from the land of the living to the land of the ancestors.

[long-pause]

When archaeologists dug at Durrington Walls, they found the floors of houses. Square houses with chalk floors and hearths in the middle. Just like the family's house. They found the marks of beds and boxes. And they found piles and piles of rubbish. Broken pots. Flint tools. And bones. Lots and lots of bones. Mostly pigs.

[pause]

{img:archaeologists-excavating-house-floor-chalk-hearth-durrington-trench}
Those bones tell an amazing story. A story about the greatest feasts of the Stone Age. And about where the builders came from.

[long-pause]

{img:midwinter-feast-durrington-walls-roasting-pigs-fires-crowds-snow-dusk}
It is midwinter. The family has arrived at Durrington Walls.

[pause]

The journey took three days. They walked through cold rain and frosty mornings. They drove their pigs along the paths. The grandmother walked slowly, leaning on her stick. Sometimes Bran carried her for a while on his back. She grumbled. But she let him. And at last, on the third evening, they came over a hill. And they saw it.

[long-pause]

The great ring of earth, white and green. Inside it, hundreds of houses, with smoke rising from every roof. Fires everywhere. People everywhere. More people than the children have ever seen in their lives. Thousands of them. From all over the land. Speaking in different ways. Wearing different clothes. All gathered for the same feast.

[pause]

The family finds a place to stay. Relatives from another settlement have a house there. They squeeze in. It is crowded and warm and noisy. Pip and Wren can hardly sleep that night. There is too much to see. Too many sounds. Music and laughter and dogs barking and pigs squealing.

[pause]

{img:crowded-house-durrington-relatives-sharing-hearth-children-excited-night}
The archaeologists found the bones of thousands of pigs at Durrington Walls. They studied the pigs' teeth. And they found that most of the pigs were about nine months old when they were eaten. Pigs are born in spring. So nine months later is midwinter. That is how we know the great feasts were at midwinter.

[long-pause]

And the pig bones tell us something else. Scientists studied the chemicals in the bones. They show where each pig grew up. And the pigs came from all over Britain. From the south-west, from Wales, from the north of England, even from Scotland. People drove their pigs hundreds of kilometres to bring them to the feast. That shows how important it was. People came from every corner of the island.

[pause]

{img:pigs-being-driven-along-path-chalk-hills-people-winter-journey}
The feast lasts for days. Pigs are roasted over great fires. The smell fills the air. People eat until they are full. They drink something made from honey or grain. They sing and dance and tell stories. Old friends meet again. Young people meet for the first time. Families arrange marriages. Traders swap axes and pots and beads.

[long-pause]

And on the shortest day of the year, everyone goes to the stones. They walk down to the river. Along the river. And up the long road to Stonehenge. Thousands of people in a long line, as the sun goes down. They gather around the great ring. And they watch.

[pause]

{img:crowd-watching-midwinter-sunset-through-stones-stonehenge-golden-cold}
The grandmother stands with her family. She is tired from the long walk. But her eyes are bright. The sun sinks lower and lower. And it sets exactly between the tallest stones. Golden light shines through the gap. Everyone is silent. And then a great sound goes up from the crowd. The sun has gone. The darkest day is here. And from tomorrow, the days will grow longer again.

[long-pause]

{img:sarsen-stones-scattered-downs-west-woods-grey-boulders-grass-sheep}
The big stones at Stonehenge are called sarsens.

[pause]

They are huge. The tallest stands more than seven metres high. That is as tall as a two-storey house. And more of it is hidden underground. The biggest sarsens weigh around thirty tons. Some even more. That is as heavy as five elephants. Each one.

[long-pause]

Sarsen is a very hard grey sandstone. It formed millions of years ago. Then, slowly, the softer rocks around it washed away. And the sarsens were left lying on the surface of the land. Scattered across the hills like giant grey sheep. People call them grey wethers. Wether is an old word for sheep. From a distance, they really do look like a flock of sheep lying on the grass.

[pause]

There are many sarsens on the hills about twenty-five kilometres north of Stonehenge. For a long time, people thought the Stonehenge sarsens must have come from there. But nobody knew exactly where.

[pause]

{img:scientists-analysing-sarsen-core-sample-laboratory-microscope-later}
Then, a few years ago, scientists found the answer. In the 1950s, workers repairing Stonehenge drilled through one of the sarsens. They took out a long cylinder of stone, called a core. One of the workers kept it. He took it home. And sixty years later, when he was very old, he gave it back.

[long-pause]

Scientists studied the core. They measured the tiny amounts of different chemicals inside it. Each sarsen site has its own special mix. Like a fingerprint. Then they tested sarsens from all over the area. And they found the match. A place called West Woods, about twenty-five kilometres north of Stonehenge. Most of the great sarsens came from there.

[pause]

Today, West Woods is a quiet forest. In spring it is full of bluebells. A few big grey sarsens still lie among the trees. Covered with moss. The ones that were left behind.

[pause]

{img:west-woods-bluebells-spring-mossy-sarsen-boulders-beech-trees}
Choosing the stones must have been careful work. The builders needed long straight ones for the uprights. Flatter ones for the lintels. They walked the hills and looked at the stones. They measured them with ropes and sticks. Which one is long enough? Which one is strong enough? Which one has no hidden crack? A mistake would cost months of work.

[long-pause]

There is one more sarsen mystery. Some of the stones at Stonehenge are slightly different from the others. Scientists think they came from a different place. One that has not been found yet. Somewhere on the hills, there may be another old quarry, hidden under the grass or the trees. Waiting to be discovered.

[long-pause]

{img:workers-levering-sarsen-from-ground-wooden-levers-ropes-team-west-woods}
Once a good sarsen was chosen, it had to be freed from the ground and made ready for its journey.

[pause]

Many sarsens lay half buried in the earth. The workers dug around them with antler picks. They dug under them. Then they pushed long wooden levers underneath. Many people pulled down on the levers at once. And slowly, the great stone lifted a little. They pushed stones and wood underneath it to hold it up. Then they lifted again. Bit by bit, the stone came up out of the ground.

[long-pause]

Sometimes the stone had to be shaped a little before the journey. The workers used big round hammerstones. Lumps of very hard stone, held in both hands or dropped from a height. They pounded the sarsen to knock off bumps and corners. It is very slow work. Sarsen is extremely hard. Each blow knocks off only a little dust. Hundreds of hammerstones have been found at Stonehenge. Some are worn smooth from use.

[pause]

{img:hammerstone-pounding-sarsen-surface-dust-chips-worker-close-hands}
Then the stone had to be moved onto a sledge. A huge wooden frame, strong enough to carry thirty tons. The workers built ramps of earth and timber. They levered the stone onto the sledge, inch by inch. They tied it down with thick ropes. Everyone held their breath. If the stone rolled off, it could crush someone. Or crack.

[long-pause]

All of this needed a lot of people. And a lot of food for those people. And a lot of wood. Whole groves of trees were cut down for levers and rollers and sledges. Ropes had to be made from the inner bark of lime trees. Twisted and plaited until they were thick and strong. Many hands. Many days.

[pause]

{img:rope-making-lime-bark-fibres-women-men-twisting-plaiting-camp}
At West Woods, there was probably a big camp. People came for weeks at a time. They cut wood and made rope and freed stones. They ate and slept by the fires. Children played around the stones. Dogs barked. And each time a great sarsen was ready on its sledge, a cheer went up from the camp. Another one. Ready for the long pull south.

[long-pause]

{img:great-sarsen-sledge-hundreds-people-ropes-pulling-chalk-downs-winter}
The family has come to help pull a stone.

[pause]

It is the morning after the midwinter feast. Bran and Ula and the children walk north with many others. To where a great sarsen is waiting on its sledge, half way between West Woods and Stonehenge. It has been pulled this far already. Now it must go the rest of the way.

[long-pause]

There are hundreds of people. Maybe two hundred. Maybe more. Long ropes run from the sledge in many lines. People line up along the ropes. Strong men at the front. Women and older children behind. And at the very end of one rope, Pip and Wren. Holding on tight. Hearts pounding.

[pause]

In front of the sledge, workers have laid a track of wooden rails. Logs laid in a long line. The sledge will slide along them. Behind it, other workers pick up the rails it has already passed. They carry them round to the front and lay them down again. Over and over.

[pause]

{img:wooden-rail-track-sledge-runners-workers-carrying-logs-forward-frost}
A man stands on top of the stone. He is the leader. He raises his arms. Everyone grips the ropes. He calls out a word. And everyone pulls. The ropes creak. The sledge does not move. He calls again. They pull again. Harder. Pip's feet slip in the frosty grass. And then, with a groan, the sledge slides forward. Just a little. A cheer goes up.

[long-pause]

Then they find their rhythm. The leader calls. Everyone pulls. Rest. Call. Pull. Rest. Someone starts a song. A pulling song, with a beat. Everyone joins in. The song carries across the hills. The great grey stone moves slowly forward. A little with every pull.

[pause]

{img:people-singing-while-pulling-ropes-leader-on-stone-arms-raised}
On flat ground, it goes well. But then comes a slope. Going up is very hard. Everyone strains. The leader shouts. Some people fall in the mud. They get up again. Going down is even more dangerous. The stone wants to rush ahead. People hold the ropes from behind to slow it down. Wooden blocks are pushed under the runners. Slowly. Carefully. One wrong move and the stone could slip.

[long-pause]

By the end of the day, they have moved the stone perhaps a kilometre. Maybe a little more. Everyone is exhausted. Their hands are raw from the ropes. Their legs shake. But they are proud. They sit by the fires and eat and laugh and tell stories about the day.

[pause]

{img:exhausted-pullers-resting-fires-evening-stone-sledge-behind-stars}
Pip falls asleep with his head in his mother's lap, still holding a little piece of rope. Wren sits by her father. Her hands hurt. But she cannot stop smiling. I helped, she says. I helped move a stone. Bran puts his arm around her. Yes, he says. You did. And one day you will tell your grandchildren.

[long-pause]

{img:experimental-archaeology-volunteers-pulling-replica-stone-sledge-today}
How many people does it take to pull a stone as heavy as a sarsen? People have tried to find out.

[pause]

In our own time, archaeologists and volunteers have done experiments. They made a copy of a big stone, out of concrete, about the same weight as a sarsen. They put it on a wooden sledge. They laid wooden rails in front of it. And they pulled.

[long-pause]

In one famous experiment, they found that about two hundred people could pull a very heavy stone up a gentle slope. That sounds like a lot. But at Durrington Walls, there were thousands of people. So there were more than enough hands.

[pause]

They also found some tricks. If the rails are greased with animal fat, the sledge slides much more easily. If the path is carefully chosen, avoiding steep hills, it is much faster. And if everyone pulls together, in rhythm, the stone moves smoothly. Teamwork matters more than strength.

[pause]

{img:greased-timber-rails-sledge-runner-close-fat-shine-chalk-path}
The builders probably chose a route that avoided the steepest hills. They may have waited for winter, when the ground was frozen hard. A frozen path is firmer than a muddy one. Some think they waited for snow. On snow and ice, a heavy sledge can slide quite well.

[long-pause]

The ropes were very important. Rope made of lime tree bark is strong. But it wears out. It frays and snaps. So rope makers must have worked all the time. Making new rope. Checking the old. A snapping rope could be dangerous. The stone could slide back. People could fall.

[pause]

{img:rope-makers-checking-frayed-rope-camp-fire-pile-of-coils-night}
How long did it take to bring all the sarsens? There were about eighty of them. Each one took weeks to move twenty-five kilometres. Some experts think the whole job took many years. Maybe ten. Maybe more. Perhaps people worked on it every winter, after the harvest. When there was time. And food from the feasts. And thousands of hands, gathered on the plain.

[long-pause]

{img:sarsen-being-raised-into-pit-a-frame-timber-ropes-crowd-pulling}
When a great sarsen reaches Stonehenge, the hardest part begins. It must be stood upright.

[pause]

First, the workers dig a pit. A deep hole in the chalk, with antler picks. The hole is shaped carefully. One side is straight down. The other side slopes gently, like a ramp. The pit must be just the right depth. Too shallow, and the stone will fall over. Too deep, and it will be too short.

[long-pause]

Then the stone is pulled on its sledge until one end sits over the sloping side of the pit. The workers lever it forward, slowly, slowly. The heavy end tips down into the pit. It slides down the slope. And then it stops against the straight side. Now the stone is leaning at an angle. Half in the pit. Half up in the air.

[pause]

Next, it must be pulled upright. Wooden frames are built. Ropes are tied to the top of the stone. Teams of people pull on the ropes from the other side. Others push with long wooden poles. Slowly, very slowly, the great stone rises. Higher. Higher. Everyone pulls together. The leader calls out. And at last, the stone stands straight up.

[pause]

{img:workers-packing-stones-chalk-around-base-upright-sarsen-pit}
Quickly, the workers fill the gap around the bottom of the stone. They pack it with broken stones and chalk. They pound it down hard with wooden posts. That holds the stone firmly in place. It must not move. It must not lean. It must stand for ever.

[long-pause]

This is a nervous moment. If the ropes slip, the stone could fall. If the pit is not deep enough, it could topple. If the angle is wrong, the stone will lean. Everyone watches. Some people pray. Some hold their breath. And when the stone is finally standing straight and the base is packed tight, the whole crowd cheers. Another stone stands.

[pause]

{img:upright-sarsen-standing-new-crowd-cheering-sky-dramatic-clouds}
Some of the great stones at Stonehenge are still standing in their holes today, exactly where they were raised four and a half thousand years ago. Archaeologists have dug around their bases. They have found the packing stones. The antler picks. And even a few of the broken hammerstones, dropped by the workers when the job was done.

[long-pause]

The builders must have planned the whole circle very carefully before they raised a single stone. They needed the right number of stones. The right heights. The right spacing. They probably marked out the circle with a rope tied to a post in the middle. Walking round and round. Marking the places for the holes. Just as a child might draw a circle with a string and a stick.

[long-pause]

{img:lintel-being-raised-timber-crib-platform-between-uprights-ropes-levers}
Standing the uprights was hard. But the next part was even harder. Putting the lintels on top.

[pause]

A lintel is a flat stone that lies across the top of two uprights. Like the top of a doorway. At Stonehenge, the great outer circle of uprights was joined all the way around by lintels. A ring of stone floating high in the air. And inside, five giant pairs of stones each had their own lintel. These are called trilithons. That means three stones. Two standing and one lying across the top.

[long-pause]

The lintels weigh about seven tons each. And they had to be lifted more than five metres into the air. How?

[pause]

Nobody knows for sure. But one good idea is a wooden platform. The workers build a stack of timber beside the uprights. Like a square tower of logs, crossed over each other. They lever the lintel up onto the first layer. Then they add another layer of logs underneath. And lever it up again. Layer by layer, the lintel rises. Higher and higher. Until it is level with the tops of the uprights.

[pause]

{img:workers-on-timber-platform-levering-lintel-stone-high-up-careful}
Then they slide it across. Very carefully. Onto the tops of the two uprights. If it slips, it could fall. Workers stand on the platform with levers and ropes. Every move is slow and careful. Everyone below watches and waits.

[long-pause]

And when the lintel settles into place, there is a soft click. Because it fits. Exactly.

[pause]

The builders made special joints to hold the stones together. On top of each upright, they carved a little bump. On the underside of each lintel, they carved a matching hollow. When the lintel goes down, the bump fits into the hollow. Like a peg in a hole. So the lintel cannot slide off.

[pause]

{img:mortise-tenon-joint-carved-stone-top-of-upright-close-detail}
And the lintels of the outer circle fit to each other too. Each end of a lintel is cut with a groove or a tongue. The tongue of one fits into the groove of the next. Like the pieces of a wooden puzzle. So the whole ring is locked together. And the lintels are gently curved, so together they make a perfect circle in the sky.

[long-pause]

It is the only stone circle in the world where the lintels are joined like this. Nowhere else did anyone build quite like Stonehenge. The builders were doing something completely new. Something nobody had tried before. And they did it so well that much of it still stands, four and a half thousand years later.

[long-pause]

{img:sarsen-surfaces-being-smoothed-pounding-grinding-workers-dust-sunlight}
Those joints are very strange, if you think about it. A bump fitting into a hollow. A tongue fitting into a groove. Those are joints for wood. Not for stone.

[pause]

Carpenters use these joints to build wooden houses and wooden frames. You cut a peg on one beam and a hole in another, and fit them together. It is called a mortise and tenon. And the tongue and groove is used to join planks side by side.

[long-pause]

So it seems the builders of Stonehenge thought like carpenters. They had built great circles of wooden posts before, like the one at Durrington Walls. They knew how to join timber. And when they built in stone, they used the same ideas. As if they were building a wooden house. But out of stone that would last forever.

[pause]

Carving these joints in hard sarsen was very slow work. The workers pounded the stone with hammerstones, again and again. Each blow knocked off a tiny bit. It may have taken weeks to carve one joint.

[pause]

{img:worker-pounding-joint-hollow-lintel-hammerstone-patience-dust}
The builders also shaped and smoothed the stones. The insides of the great stones, the sides facing the middle, were made smoother and flatter than the outsides. They were pounded and then ground with sand and water. Over and over. Until they were smooth. It is as if the inside of Stonehenge was the best side. The side facing the centre. Where the ceremonies happened.

[long-pause]

Some of the uprights are shaped to be a little wider at the top than in the middle. That makes them look straight from the ground. Without this trick, tall stones can look like they get thinner at the top. The builders knew this. They cared about how it looked.

[pause]

{img:stonehenge-inner-faces-smooth-sarsen-close-texture-morning-light}
All this shows great skill. And great care. The builders were not just piling up rocks. They were making something beautiful. Something precise. And it must have taken many skilled workers many years. Pounding and grinding. Measuring and checking. With patience and pride.

[long-pause]

{img:midwinter-sunset-stonehenge-sun-between-great-trilithon-gold-frost}
Why was Stonehenge built? We may never know everything. But one thing is clear. It is lined up with the sun.

[pause]

If you stand in the middle of Stonehenge on the shortest day of the year, in winter, and look south-west, you see the sun go down. Exactly between the stones of the tallest trilithon. As if the stones were made to frame it.

[long-pause]

And if you stand in the middle on the longest day of the year, in summer, and look the other way, north-east, you see the sun come up. Right along the path that leads into the circle. Over a large stone that stands outside the ring. The Heel Stone.

[pause]

The midwinter sunset and the midsummer sunrise. They are on the same line. The builders lined the whole monument up along this line. On purpose. With great care.

[pause]

{img:midsummer-sunrise-heel-stone-avenue-golden-rays-mist-dawn-ancient}
For farming people, the year was everything. The seasons told them when to plant and when to harvest. When the animals would have their young. When the cold would come. And the turning points of the year were the solstices. The longest day and the shortest day.

[long-pause]

Midwinter must have been a time of worry. The days get shorter and shorter. The sun gets lower and lower. It is cold and dark. Food is running low. What if the sun kept going away? What if it never came back? At midwinter, the sun seems to stop. And then it turns. The days start to get longer again. The light comes back.

[pause]

Many experts think midwinter was the most important time at Stonehenge. The great feast at Durrington Walls was at midwinter. The pig bones tell us that. Perhaps people gathered at Stonehenge to watch the last sunset of the dying year. To say goodbye to the old sun. And to welcome the new one.

[pause]

{img:people-watching-solstice-silhouettes-stones-sky-orange-quiet-awe}
Standing in the circle, with thousands of others. Watching the sun sink between the great stones. Feeling the cold. Feeling the dark coming. And knowing that tomorrow, the light would begin to return. It must have been a powerful feeling. Even today, people who stand there at the solstice say they feel it too.

[long-pause]

{img:full-moon-rising-over-stonehenge-night-stars-cold-clear-ancient}
The sun was not the only thing in the sky the builders watched. They watched the moon too. And the stars.

[pause]

The moon moves in a more complicated way than the sun. It rises and sets in different places over the months. And over a long cycle of about eighteen and a half years, its path swings from far north to far south and back. Some people think the builders knew about this. And that some of the stones may point to where the moon rises at its furthest points.

[long-pause]

That is still being argued about. But it is certain that the builders watched the sky carefully. Night after night. Year after year. For generations. They had no writing to record what they saw. So they remembered. In stories. In songs. In the positions of stones and posts.

[pause]

Without city lights, the night sky in the Stone Age was amazing. Thousands and thousands of stars. The Milky Way stretching across the sky like a river of light. The planets moving slowly among the stars. Shooting stars. The moon, bright enough to cast shadows.

[pause]

{img:milky-way-over-neolithic-plain-wooden-posts-silhouette-people-gazing}
For people who lived close to nature, the sky was a great clock and a great calendar. The stars told you the time of night. The moon told you the time of the month. The sun told you the time of year. When certain stars rose at dawn, it was time to plant. When others appeared, it was time to bring the animals in.

[long-pause]

The grandmother of our family knows all this. On the walk home from the feast, she points to the stars and teaches the children. That bright one is the herder's star, she says. When it rises early, the calves are born. Those seven little ones close together are the sisters. When they go away in spring, it is time to plant. The children look up. And they try to remember.

[pause]

{img:grandmother-pointing-stars-children-night-walk-frost-breath}
Pip asks why the stars do not fall down. The grandmother laughs. Because the sky holds them, she says. Like the earth holds the stones. Pip thinks about that for a long time as they walk. His breath makes little clouds in the cold air. And above them, the stars shine on.

[long-pause]

{img:stonehenge-avenue-chalk-banks-ditches-leading-to-river-procession-dusk}
From the entrance of Stonehenge, a path leads away across the plain. It is called the Avenue.

[pause]

The Avenue is a wide path, about twenty metres across, with a low bank and a ditch on each side. It starts at the north-east entrance of Stonehenge. It runs straight for about five hundred metres along the line of the midsummer sunrise and the midwinter sunset. Then it turns. It curves east across the plain. Down the slope. And all the way to the river Avon. It is almost three kilometres long.

[long-pause]

When archaeologists dug at the start of the Avenue, they found something surprising. Natural ridges in the chalk. Long low ridges made thousands of years before by ice and water. And by chance, these ridges run exactly along the line of the midsummer sunrise and midwinter sunset.

[pause]

Imagine the first people who noticed this. Standing on the plain at sunrise on the longest day. And seeing the sun come up right at the end of these strange natural lines in the ground. As if the earth itself was pointing at the sun. It must have seemed like a sign. A holy place, made by the gods. Maybe that is why Stonehenge was built exactly here.

[pause]

{img:natural-chalk-ridges-aligned-sunrise-grass-dew-dawn-plain}
The Avenue made those natural lines into a road. A road for processions. People walking slowly from the river up to the stones. Or from the stones down to the river. Singing. Carrying torches. Carrying offerings. Or carrying the ashes of the dead.

[long-pause]

At the end of the Avenue, beside the river, archaeologists found another small stone circle. They call it Bluestonehenge. It was made of bluestones. Perhaps the bluestones first stood there, by the river. Perhaps that was where the stones arrived, after coming by water. And later they were moved up the Avenue to Stonehenge itself.

[pause]

{img:bluestone-circle-riverside-avon-small-stones-ring-reeds-misty-morning}
On the night of the midwinter feast, the family walks along the Avenue with thousands of others. It is dark. People carry burning torches. The flames flicker and smoke. The torches make a long river of fire winding up the hill toward the stones. Somewhere ahead, drums are beating. Somewhere behind, people are singing. Wren holds her grandmother's hand. Pip holds his father's. Nobody speaks much. It is too special. Too solemn. And at the top of the hill, black against the stars, the great stones are waiting.

[long-pause]

{img:river-avon-winter-dusk-mist-boats-torches-people-on-banks-neolithic}
The river Avon flows between Durrington Walls and Stonehenge. And some archaeologists think the river was at the heart of everything.

[pause]

Here is one idea. Durrington Walls, with its wooden circles and its houses and its feasts, was the place of the living. Wood is alive. It grows. It rots. Like people. And Stonehenge, with its stone circles and the ashes of the dead, was the place of the ancestors. Stone is cold and hard and lasts forever. Like the dead, who never change.

[long-pause]

And the river joined them. From Durrington Walls, an avenue leads down to the river. From the river, the Stonehenge Avenue leads up to the stones. So a journey from the land of the living to the land of the dead went down one road. Along the river. And up the other road.

[pause]

Perhaps, when someone passed away, their ashes were carried on this journey. Perhaps the river was like a boundary between the two worlds. Many peoples in history have thought of rivers that way. A river to cross, between life and what comes after.

[pause]

{img:neolithic-small-boat-river-carrying-offering-flowers-dawn-gentle-reeds}
We cannot know for sure. The people left no words. But the shape of the land and the monuments tells a story. A careful, deliberate story. They built their monuments to make these journeys. And to mark them.

[long-pause]

The river was also simply useful. It was a road for boats. Wood and food and people could travel along it. It gave water for drinking and cooking. Fish and eels. Reeds for roofs. Birds to hunt. In the cold winter mornings, mist rose from the water. Swans glided past. Herons stood still in the shallows.

[pause]

{img:heron-standing-river-mist-winter-morning-willows-calm-water}
On the morning after the midwinter ceremony, the family walks back along the river to Durrington Walls. The grandmother is very tired. She walks slowly. Ula holds her arm. They stop to rest on the bank. The grandmother looks at the water for a long time. One day, she says quietly, you will carry me down this river. And up to the stones. To be with my mother and my father. Ula squeezes her hand. Not yet, she says. Not for a long time. The grandmother smiles. No, she says. Not yet. And they walk on, together, in the cold bright morning.

[long-pause]

{img:woodhenge-concentric-rings-tall-oak-posts-painted-carved-sky-neolithic}
Just south of Durrington Walls, there is another great circle. But this one was made of wood. Today it is called Woodhenge.

[pause]

It was made of six rings of tall wooden posts. One inside the other. Like the rings of a tree. Huge oak trunks, standing upright in deep holes in the chalk. The tallest may have been seven or eight metres high. As tall as the great stones at Stonehenge.

[long-pause]

For a long time, nobody knew Woodhenge was there. The wood had rotted away thousands of years ago. Nothing was left above the ground. Then, about a hundred years ago, a pilot flying over the plain looked down. In the fields below, he saw strange rings of dark spots in the crops. The crops grew differently over the old post holes. From the air, the pattern showed clearly. Archaeologists came and dug. And they found the holes. Rings and rings of them.

[pause]

{img:aerial-view-crop-marks-rings-dark-spots-field-1920s-biplane-later}
Today, concrete posts mark where the wooden ones stood. Short posts in rings, on the grass. You can walk among them and imagine the great trunks rising above you.

[long-pause]

Woodhenge was lined up with the sun too. Like Stonehenge. Its entrance faces the midsummer sunrise. Perhaps it was a place for ceremonies at the start of summer. While Stonehenge was for the end of winter.

[pause]

Some people think the wooden posts were carved and painted. With patterns and faces and colours. We cannot know. The wood is gone. But many peoples around the world have carved and painted their great wooden posts. It is easy to imagine the Woodhenge posts bright with colour. Red and white and black. Tall against the sky.

[pause]

{img:people-dancing-among-timber-posts-woodhenge-summer-evening-music}
Imagine walking into Woodhenge. Ring after ring of tall dark posts around you. Like walking into a forest of giant trees. With the sky above, and the light flickering between the trunks. Drums beating. People dancing. The smell of fresh oak and woodsmoke. It must have felt very different from the cold grey stones. Warm and alive. A place of the living.

[long-pause]

Wood does not last. After a few hundred years, the posts rotted and fell. The rings disappeared. Only the holes remained, hidden under the grass. Waiting for a pilot to fly over on a summer day, thousands of years later. And see them.

[pause]

In the middle of Woodhenge, archaeologists found a small grave. A child, about three years old, laid to rest at the very centre of the rings. We do not know who the child was, or why they lay there. We think of them gently. A little one, at the heart of the great wooden circle, under the summer sky.

[long-pause]

{img:altar-stone-fallen-under-trilithon-stonehenge-green-sandstone-centre}
In the very middle of Stonehenge, there is a big flat stone. It is lying down, under two fallen stones from the great trilithon. It is called the Altar Stone.

[pause]

It is different from the others. It is not a grey sarsen. It is not a bluestone. It is a greenish sandstone, with little sparkles in it. It is about five metres long and weighs about six tons. Nobody knows exactly what it was for. The name Altar Stone was given much later. It may once have stood upright, like the others.

[long-pause]

For a long time, people thought it came from Wales, like the bluestones. But in 2024, scientists made an amazing discovery. They studied tiny grains inside the stone. Grains of minerals that hold a record of where and when the rock was formed. And they found it did not come from Wales at all.

[pause]

It came from the far north of Scotland. From the area around the Orkney Islands and the north-east tip of Scotland. More than seven hundred kilometres away. Twice as far as the bluestones.

[pause]

{img:northern-scotland-coast-orkney-sandstone-cliffs-sea-grey-sky-wild}
How could a six-ton stone travel seven hundred kilometres four and a half thousand years ago? Over mountains, through forests, across rivers? Or perhaps by sea? Down the long coast of Britain in some kind of great boat? Nobody knows. It is one of the newest mysteries of Stonehenge. And one of the most amazing.

[long-pause]

What we do know is this. The people of Stonehenge were connected to people far, far away. From Wales in the west to Orkney in the far north. The same pottery. The same ideas. Perhaps the same beliefs. Perhaps even the same families, related by marriage across the whole island.

[pause]

{img:neolithic-boat-coast-sea-paddlers-heavy-stone-cargo-dawn-calm}
Maybe the Altar Stone was a gift. From the people of the north to the people of the south. A piece of their holy land, carried all the way to the great gathering place. To stand in the very centre. Joining the far corners of Britain together in one sacred circle.

[long-pause]

{img:ness-of-brodgar-orkney-stone-buildings-walls-lochs-standing-stones-sky}
If the Altar Stone came from the far north, let us travel there for a moment. To the Orkney Islands.

[pause]

Orkney is a group of islands off the very top of Scotland. Wild and windy and surrounded by the sea. There are almost no trees. But there is plenty of flat stone that splits easily into slabs. And the people of Orkney in the Stone Age used it to build amazing things.

[long-pause]

On one island, between two lakes, there is a place called the Ness of Brodgar. Archaeologists have been digging there for twenty years. They have found big stone buildings with thick walls. Some had roofs of stone tiles. Some had painted walls, in red and yellow and black. The oldest known paint in Britain. It was a great gathering place. Like Durrington Walls in the south. But older.

[pause]

Nearby stand two great stone circles. The Ring of Brodgar, a huge ring of tall thin stones, standing on a strip of land between the lakes. And the Stones of Stenness, with a few great slabs still standing tall. They are older than the stone circle at Stonehenge.

[pause]

{img:ring-of-brodgar-tall-thin-stones-heather-loch-sunset-orkney}
And on the coast, there is a whole village from the Stone Age. It is called Skara Brae. A great storm long ago blew the sand off it. And there it was. Little stone houses joined by covered passages. Inside, the furniture is still there. Made of stone. Stone beds. Stone shelves. Stone boxes in the floor, for keeping fish or bait. And a stone dresser against the back wall, facing the door. Where the family's best things were shown.

[long-pause]

Grooved ware pottery, the same kind found at Stonehenge, was first made in Orkney. Ideas seem to have travelled from the north to the south. The way of building circles. The way of making pots. Maybe the way of watching the sun.

[pause]

{img:skara-brae-stone-house-interior-dresser-beds-hearth-sea-light}
Imagine a traveller from Orkney, arriving at the midwinter feast on the plain. After a long, long journey. Speaking a little differently. Bringing stories from the islands of the north. Of stone villages by the sea. Of circles between the lakes. And the people of the plain gathering round to listen. Amazed that the world was so big. And that people so far away believed the same things they did.

[long-pause]

{img:amesbury-archer-burial-goods-copper-knives-gold-ornaments-beakers-museum}
About two hundred years after the great stones were raised, a man came to Stonehenge from very far away. We call him the Amesbury Archer.

[pause]

He was found in 2002, when builders were digging for new houses near Stonehenge. Archaeologists came to look first. And they found a grave. A man lying on his side, his knees bent, as if asleep. And around him, more things than had ever been found in a Stone Age grave in Britain.

[long-pause]

There were arrowheads made of flint. Many of them. That is why he is called the Archer. There were two stone wrist guards. Archers wear them to protect their arm from the bowstring. There were copper knives. Some of the first metal objects in Britain. There were five pots of a new kind, shaped like a bell or a beaker. And there were two little hair ornaments made of gold. The oldest gold ever found in Britain.

[pause]

Who was he? Scientists studied his teeth. And they found he had grown up far away. In the Alps, in the middle of Europe. Probably in what is now Switzerland or Germany. He had travelled all the way to Britain. Over mountains and rivers and the sea.

[pause]

{img:traveller-archer-crossing-sea-small-boat-white-cliffs-ahead-bronze-age}
He also had a bad knee. An old injury had left him limping. So he walked with difficulty for many years. And yet he made that long journey.

[long-pause]

Why did he come? Perhaps because of Stonehenge. Maybe its fame had spread across Europe. Maybe he came to see it. Or to be healed there. Some people think the bluestones were believed to have healing powers. Maybe he came hoping to cure his leg.

[pause]

Or perhaps he came because he knew something new. How to work metal. Copper and gold. People in Britain did not know how yet. He may have been one of the first to bring this knowledge. A metalworker. A man with a new and precious skill.

[pause]

{img:early-metalworker-crucible-fire-copper-pouring-mould-curious-onlookers}
He was a sign of changing times. After him, more people came from Europe. With their bell-shaped pots and their metal. The Stone Age was ending. The age of metal was beginning. Bronze would come soon. And life on the plain would change. But Stonehenge would stay. People would keep coming to it. And keep changing it. For another thousand years.

[long-pause]

{img:bronze-age-round-barrows-hilltops-near-stonehenge-summer-grass-sheep}
After the great stones were raised, Stonehenge kept changing.

[pause]

The bluestones were moved around several times over the centuries. First in one shape. Then in another. A ring. A horseshoe. Inside the great sarsen circle. People kept rearranging them, as if they were still working out the best way. Stonehenge was never quite finished. It was always being worked on.

[long-pause]

In the age of bronze that followed, people built hundreds of round burial mounds on the hills around Stonehenge. Like little round hills. They are still there today. You can see them on the skyline all around. Important people were buried in them. With bronze daggers and gold and amber and beads. People wanted to be buried near the great stones.

[pause]

Carvings of bronze axes and daggers were even cut into some of the sarsens. They are hard to see now, worn smooth by time and weather. But on a low sunny evening, if you know where to look, you can find them. Little shapes of axe heads, carved on the old grey stones.

[pause]

{img:carvings-bronze-axe-heads-on-sarsen-stone-low-evening-sun-faint}
Then slowly, people stopped coming. Around three thousand years ago, the great gatherings ended. Nobody knows exactly why. Times changed. Beliefs changed. New ways of life came. Stonehenge stood on the plain, quiet and empty. Sheep grazed among the stones.

[long-pause]

Over the centuries, some stones fell over. Storms and frost pushed them. Some were taken away for building. Some were broken. In the Middle Ages, people told stories about how Stonehenge was made. One famous story said the wizard Merlin moved the stones by magic from Ireland. Others said giants built it. Nobody remembered the real builders anymore.

[pause]

{img:medieval-shepherd-sheep-among-fallen-stones-stonehenge-moonlight-alone}
But the stones stayed. Most of them. Tall and grey on the empty plain. Through Roman times and Saxon times and Norman times. Through the Middle Ages. Waiting for people to come back. And try to understand.

[long-pause]

For a while, Stonehenge even belonged to private owners. In 1915, it was sold at an auction. A local man bought it, as a present for his wife. She did not much like it. So a few years later, he gave it to the nation. Since then, it has belonged to everyone.

[long-pause]

{img:antiquarian-1720s-drawing-stonehenge-measuring-tape-notebook-wig-coat}
About three hundred years ago, people started to study Stonehenge seriously.

[pause]

One of the first was a man called William Stukeley. He was a doctor who loved old things. He spent many summers at Stonehenge. He drew it carefully and measured it. And he noticed something important. The monument was lined up with the midsummer sunrise. He was one of the first to see it.

[long-pause]

But he also had some wrong ideas. He thought Stonehenge was built by the druids, the priests of the Celtic people. We now know the druids lived two thousand years after Stonehenge was built. But the idea stuck. Even today, some people think druids built it. They did not. But druids today do gather there at the solstice to celebrate. Dressed in white robes.

[pause]

In the 1900s, archaeologists began to dig carefully. They measured and recorded everything. They found the antler picks and the hammerstones. They worked out the different stages of building. And a new science helped them. A way to tell how old things are, by measuring tiny changes in anything that was once alive. Bone, antler, wood. That is how we know the ditch was dug around 3000 before Christ. And the great stones were raised around 2500.

[pause]

{img:archaeologists-1950s-excavating-stonehenge-trench-stones-measuring-poles}
In the 1950s and 1960s, some of the fallen stones were lifted and set upright again. Using cranes. And concrete. Some people did not like it. Others said it helped save the monument.

[long-pause]

In the last twenty years, the discoveries have come faster and faster. The great settlement at Durrington Walls. The pig bones and the midwinter feasts. The quarries in Wales. Bluestonehenge by the river. The source of the sarsens in West Woods. The Altar Stone from Scotland. The Amesbury Archer from the Alps. Scientists with new machines. Machines that can see under the ground without digging. Machines that read the chemicals in teeth and stones. Bit by bit, the story of the builders is coming back.

[pause]

{img:ground-penetrating-radar-survey-field-stonehenge-modern-team-later}
And still there are mysteries. Why exactly did they build it? What did they believe? What songs did they sing as they pulled the stones? What did they call it? We will probably never know. But we know much more than anyone did a hundred years ago. And every year, the plain gives up a few more of its secrets.

[long-pause]

{img:stonehenge-today-visitors-path-around-stones-summer-sky-plain}
Today, Stonehenge is one of the most famous places in the world. More than a million people visit it every year.

[pause]

Visitors walk along a path around the stones. Most days they cannot go inside the circle. The stones must be protected. Too many feet could wear away the ground. But they walk slowly around, looking. And they are amazed at how big the stones are. And how old.

[long-pause]

Near Stonehenge there is a visitor centre. Inside, you can see the bones and antler picks and pots that the builders left behind. You can see the face of a man who lived nearby five and a half thousand years ago. Rebuilt by scientists from his skull. And outside, there are copies of the Stone Age houses from Durrington Walls. Square houses with chalk floors and hearths in the middle. And a big sarsen stone on a wooden sledge, with ropes. Visitors can try to pull it. It is very hard. Unless a lot of people pull together.

[pause]

{img:modern-solstice-crowd-sunrise-stonehenge-people-celebrating-june}
And twice a year, something special happens. At the summer solstice and the winter solstice, the circle is opened. Anyone can come inside. Thousands of people gather in the dark. Druids and families. Young and old. People from all over the world. They wait through the night. And when the sun rises on the longest day, they cheer. And drums beat. And people dance. Just as their ancestors may have done, four and a half thousand years ago.

[long-pause]

So we leave the plain tonight, in the time of the builders, as the evening falls.

[pause]

The midwinter sun has set between the great stones. The sky is turning from gold to rose to deep blue. The first stars come out over the plain. At Durrington Walls, the fires are burning and the pigs are roasting. The smell of smoke drifts on the cold air. In a crowded house, Wren and Pip are falling asleep, with their hands still sore from the rope. Bran and Ula sit by the hearth, talking quietly. The grandmother lies wrapped in her furs, smiling in her sleep. Outside, on the plain, the great stones stand silent in the frost. Grey and tall and patient. And over them, slowly, the stars turn. As they always have. As they always will.
