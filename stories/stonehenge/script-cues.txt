Tonight we travel about four and a half thousand years back in time. We go to a wide green plain in the south of England. The year is around 2500 before Christ. And on that plain, people are raising great stones toward the sky.

[long-pause]

It is early morning, and the air is cold and still. Mist lies low over the rolling grassland, soft and white. In the east, the sky is slowly turning pink. And in the middle of the plain, there is a ring of huge grey stones, standing upright. Some of them are taller than three people standing on each other's shoulders. Some have great flat stones lying across their tops, like the top of a doorway. Some are not finished yet. Wooden frames lean against them, and thick ropes lie coiled on the grass. Smoke rises from a fire nearby, where people are waking up and stretching.

[pause]

This is Stonehenge, one of the most famous places in the world. And it is also one of the most mysterious.

[pause]

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

{img:neolithic-people-hauling-sarsen-sledge-ropes-many-people-chalk-plain}
Who built Stonehenge, and how did they do it, and why? For hundreds of years, people have wondered about these questions. Some said it was built by giants long ago. Some said a wizard flew the stones there by magic. Some said it was built by the Romans, or by druids, or by people from far away. None of that was true.

[pause]

It was built by the people who lived here, on and around the plain. They were farmers and herders, ordinary people with families. They had no metal tools, no wheels and no writing. They had only stone and wood, antler and rope. And they had their own strength, their own cleverness, and a great dream they all shared.

[pause]

{img:midsummer-sunrise-through-stonehenge-trilithons-golden-light-ancient}
In the last fifty years, archaeologists have learned an amazing amount about Stonehenge. They have found out where the stones came from. Some came from not very far away. Some came from the hills of Wales, about two hundred kilometres away. One great stone even came from the far north of Scotland. They have found the great village where the builders gathered for their feasts. They have found the bones of the pigs they roasted and ate. And they have learned that Stonehenge is carefully lined up with the sun.

[long-pause]

{img:neolithic-family-walking-across-plain-toward-stones-winter-morning-cattle}
Tonight we will walk on this plain in the time of the builders. We will meet a family who comes here every year for the great midwinter feast. There is a father and a mother, two children, a baby and an old grandmother. We will follow the long journey of the bluestones from the hills of Wales. We will help to pull a great grey stone across the hills on a wooden sledge. We will watch the stones being raised, and the heavy lintels lifted into place. And at the end, we will stand in the circle and watch the sun go down on the shortest day of the year.

[pause]

So let us begin, on a cold morning, long ago. A family is walking slowly across the plain toward the stones. Their cattle walk beside them, breathing little clouds into the frosty air. And the great grey ring is waiting for them on the hill.

[long-pause]

{img:neolithic-britain-farmers-fields-cattle-woodland-clearing-round-houses}
The people who built Stonehenge lived in a time we call the New Stone Age. Its other name is the Neolithic.

[pause]

It is called the Stone Age because people did not use metal yet. Their tools were made of stone and wood, bone and antler. Their axes were made of stone, polished until they were smooth and shining. Their knives and scrapers were made of flint, a hard stone that breaks into very sharp edges. They had no iron and no bronze and no copper. That would all come later.

[long-pause]

And it is called the New Stone Age because something new had happened in Britain, and that new thing was farming. For a very long time before, people in Britain had lived by hunting and gathering. They followed the deer through the forests and gathered nuts and berries. They fished in the rivers and the sea. Then, about one and a half thousand years before our story, farming came to Britain from across the sea.

[pause]

People began to grow wheat and barley in small fields. They kept cattle and pigs and sheep. They cleared parts of the forest to make room for their fields. They built houses and stayed in one place for longer. And life slowly changed. More food meant more people. More people meant bigger groups, and bigger groups could do bigger things together. Like building great monuments.

[pause]

{img:polished-stone-axe-flint-tools-antler-pick-wooden-handle-close}
A good stone axe was a precious thing. It took many days to grind and polish one until it was smooth. People looked after their axes carefully and passed them down to their children. Some of the most beautiful axes were never used at all. They were given as gifts, or kept as treasures.

[pause]

In 2500 before Christ, Britain is a land of forests and grasslands and rivers. People live in small groups, scattered across the land. They farm and herd and gather. They make pots and tools and clothes. They trade with each other, exchanging axes and beads and furs. And they gather together at special places for feasts and ceremonies.

[long-pause]

They have no writing at all. So we do not know their names, or their language, or their stories. Everything we know about them comes from what they left behind. We have their tools and their pots, their houses and their graves. We have their bones. And we have their great monuments.

[pause]

{img:grooved-ware-pottery-decorated-spirals-lines-neolithic-hearth}
They make a special kind of pottery. They make big pots with flat bottoms, decorated with grooves and lines and spirals. Archaeologists call it grooved ware. It is found all over Britain, from the far north of Scotland to the south of England. The same kind of pots are found everywhere. That tells us something important about these people. They were connected to each other, across the whole island. Ideas and styles travelled from place to place. And so, perhaps, did people.

[long-pause]

Life is not easy in the Stone Age. The winters are cold, and illness is common. People do not live very long, by our standards. Forty or fifty years is old. But the people are clever and skilled and strong. They know their land intimately, every hill and stream and tree. They know when the salmon swim up the rivers and when the hazelnuts are ripe. And they work together, always. Because alone, nobody could live for long.

[long-pause]

{img:salisbury-plain-rolling-chalk-downs-grass-sky-barrows-distant-river}
Stonehenge stands on a wide, open land of gentle hills. Today it is called Salisbury Plain.

[pause]

Under the grass, the ground is made of chalk. Chalk is a soft white rock, made long ago from the tiny shells of sea creatures. The chalk makes the land dry, and the grass short and sweet. It is good for grazing cattle and sheep. When you dig into the chalk, it is bright white underneath. In the time of the builders, many monuments were dug into the chalk. When they were new, they shone white across the green land. You could see them from far away.

[long-pause]

The plain is not empty, even in the time of our story. There are already many monuments here, built long before Stonehenge. There are long mounds where the ancestors rest. There is a great long ditch, almost three kilometres long, that runs across the land. There are rings of earth and ditches. And later, many round mounds will be built on the hilltops as well.

[pause]

It is a sacred landscape, a place of the ancestors. People come here from far away, again and again, for hundreds of years.

[pause]

{img:river-avon-wiltshire-valley-willows-water-meadow-dawn-neolithic}
To the east of Stonehenge flows the river Avon. It is a gentle river, winding slowly through a green valley. Willow trees lean over the water, and reeds grow along the banks. The river flows south, all the way to the sea. In the time of the builders, it is an important road. People and goods travel along it in small boats. And it may have been important in other ways too.

[pause]

In the early mornings, the river is very beautiful. Mist rises from the water and drifts over the meadows. Herons stand still in the shallows, waiting for fish. Ducks paddle among the reeds, and a kingfisher flashes past like a little blue spark.

[long-pause]

The weather on the plain can be wild. In winter, cold winds blow across the open land. Rain and snow sweep over the hills, and there are few trees to shelter behind. People pull their cloaks tight and hurry home to their fires. But in summer, the sun shines warmly on the grass. Skylarks sing high above, so high you can hardly see them. There are big skies and long views. And there is a feeling of being very close to the heavens.

[pause]

{img:skylark-singing-over-grassland-summer-sky-chalk-downs-flowers}
In summer, the plain is full of flowers. There are yellow buttercups and purple orchids, and little blue harebells nodding in the wind. Butterflies drift from flower to flower. Sheep graze slowly across the hillsides, and their bells clink softly.

[pause]

Shepherds bring their flocks up onto the plain in the mornings and down again in the evenings. They sit on the warm grass with their dogs and watch the clouds drift by. Sometimes they play a little tune on a flute made from a bird's bone. The sound carries a long way across the quiet hills.

[pause]

Maybe that is why the people chose this place for their great monument. There is the open sky and the wide horizon. You can see the sun rise and set, without anything in the way. You can feel the seasons turning, one after another. It is a place between the earth and the sky.

[long-pause]

{img:early-stonehenge-3000bc-circular-ditch-bank-chalk-white-ring-plain}
Stonehenge was not built all at once. It was built and changed and rebuilt over many hundreds of years. It is a little like an old church that keeps getting new parts added to it.

[pause]

It began about five hundred years before our story, around the year 3000 before Christ. And at first, it had no great stones at all.

[long-pause]

The first Stonehenge was a ring of earth. People dug a round ditch in the chalk, about a hundred metres across. They piled the white chalk into a bank beside it. They left two openings in the ring, like doors. One opening faced north-east, and the other faced south. Inside the ring, they dug a circle of fifty-six small holes. Some of these holes may have held wooden posts, or small stones.

[pause]

How did they dig the ditch without metal tools? They used picks made from the antlers of red deer. They hacked at the hard chalk with these antler picks. They scooped up the loose chalk with the shoulder bones of cattle, using them like shovels. And they carried it away in baskets on their backs. Archaeologists have found dozens of these antler picks lying in the ditch. They were left behind by the diggers, all those years ago. Some of them still show the marks where their hands held them.

[pause]

{img:antler-picks-cattle-shoulder-blade-shovels-chalk-ditch-digging}
Digging the ditch was hard and dusty work. The diggers worked in small teams, each team digging its own part. At midday, they sat on the bank and ate bread and cheese and drank water. Children carried baskets of chalk away and tipped them onto the growing bank. By evening, everyone was covered in white dust, from their hair to their feet.

[pause]

Inside the ring, people laid their dead to rest. Their ashes were placed in the holes and in the ground. There were many people, over hundreds of years, both men and women. Some of them had come from far away. Perhaps they were important people, leaders or wise ones. Perhaps they were the families of those who built the ring. We remember them gently.

[long-pause]

So from the very beginning, Stonehenge was a place for the ancestors. It was a place to honour those who had gone before. And the circle and its openings may already have been linked to the sun.

[pause]

{img:white-chalk-bank-ring-evening-people-gathering-fire-smoke-ancient}
For five hundred years, the ring of earth stood on the plain. People came and went, and ceremonies were held there. The white chalk bank slowly turned green as the grass grew over it. And then, around the time of our story, something new began. People decided to build with stone, huge stone. And some of it would come from very far away.

[long-pause]

Why did they build a circle? Perhaps because the sky looks round, like a great bowl over the earth. Perhaps because the sun and the moon are round. Perhaps because a circle has no beginning and no end, like the turning seasons. Or perhaps because, in a circle, everyone stands as equals. Everyone faces the middle, and everyone faces each other. Nobody knows for sure. But people all over the world, in many different times, have gathered in circles. They have gathered to remember, and to celebrate, and to be together.

[long-pause]

{img:preseli-hills-wales-rocky-outcrops-spotted-dolerite-pillars-misty}
At Stonehenge, there are two kinds of stone, the big ones and the small ones.

[pause]

The small ones are called bluestones. When they are wet with rain, they have a soft blue-grey colour. There were once about eighty of them. Each one is about two metres tall and weighs about two to four tons. That is as heavy as two or three cars. They are not as big as the great stones. But where they came from is one of the most amazing parts of the whole story.

[long-pause]

The bluestones do not come from anywhere near Stonehenge. They come from the Preseli Hills in the west of Wales. That is about two hundred and twenty-five kilometres away. It is a long, long way to travel, over hills and rivers and through forests. And there were no roads and no wheels to help.

[pause]

Scientists found out exactly where the bluestones came from. They studied the tiny crystals inside the stones. Then they searched the Welsh hills for rock that matched. And at last, they found it. It was on rocky outcrops on the tops of the hills. At one place, the rock grows naturally in tall pillars, standing side by side. They look like the pipes of a great organ. The builders only had to loosen them and lift them free.

[pause]

{img:neolithic-quarry-welsh-hillside-wooden-wedges-pillar-stone-being-freed}
Archaeologists even found the old quarries where the work was done. They found the stone tools the workers used. They found little platforms of stone, where the pillars were lowered down gently. They found the remains of fires, where the workers cooked their food. And they found that the stones were taken from there around the time Stonehenge was being built.

[pause]

We can imagine the workers on the hilltop on a misty morning. They push wooden wedges into the cracks between the pillars and tap them in. Slowly the cracks widen, and a pillar begins to lean. Others hold ropes to lower it gently onto a bed of logs. When it is down, everyone stops to rest and drink and laugh.

[long-pause]

But why did they do all this? Why bring stones from so far away? There are plenty of stones much closer to Stonehenge. Nobody knows for sure. Maybe the bluestones were special in some way. Maybe the Preseli Hills were a holy place. Some of the stones make a ringing sound when you tap them with another stone. They ring a little like a bell. Maybe that was important to the builders. Maybe the stones carried the spirit of the place they came from. Or the spirit of the ancestors.

[pause]

{img:people-tapping-bluestone-listening-ringing-sound-hilltop-wales-wind}
One idea is that some people of Wales moved east, to the plain. And they brought their sacred stones with them, like bringing a piece of home. Scientists have even found what may be an older stone circle in Wales. Some of the bluestones may have stood there first, for many years. Then they were taken down carefully, and carried all the way to Stonehenge. Perhaps the people sang as they took them down, and said goodbye to the hills. And perhaps they sang again when the stones stood up on the plain.

[long-pause]

{img:bluestone-on-wooden-sledge-rollers-team-pulling-welsh-valley-forest}
How did the bluestones travel from the hills of Wales to Stonehenge? They had to go two hundred and twenty-five kilometres, with no wheels, no metal and no horses.

[pause]

Nobody knows for certain how it was done. But people have thought about it a great deal. And some have even tried it for themselves.

[long-pause]

Each stone was probably tied onto a wooden sledge. A sledge is a strong flat frame, made of tree trunks tied together. Teams of people pulled the sledge along with long ropes. The ropes were made of plant fibres or animal hide, twisted together tightly. Under the sledge, they may have laid wooden rails or logs. Or they may have greased the path with animal fat, to make it slippery.

[pause]

A few years ago, a group of students tried it themselves. They put a stone as heavy as a bluestone on a wooden sledge. They laid wooden rails in front of it. And they pulled. They found that about ten people could pull a stone this heavy, quite easily, on flat ground. They were surprised by this. It was much easier than they had expected.

[pause]

{img:team-of-people-pulling-sledge-hill-slope-ropes-straining-autumn}
But the journey from Wales is not flat at all. There are hills and valleys, rivers and marshes and thick forests. The people had to find the best way through. They went along open ridges and down river valleys. They went around the steepest places. Some people think the stones might have gone partly by water. Perhaps they travelled on rafts or boats, along the coast or up the rivers. Others think they went all the way by land.

[long-pause]

Either way, it must have taken a very long time. It took weeks for each stone, and perhaps months. Dozens or even hundreds of people helped. And there were about eighty stones.

[pause]

Imagine it for a moment. A long line of people winds slowly through the forest, pulling a great grey stone on a sledge. They sing to keep the rhythm of the pulling. At night they stop and make a fire, and they eat and sleep. People from villages along the way come out to watch. Some of them help to pull for a day or two. Some bring baskets of food, bread and nuts and dried meat. Children walk alongside, wide-eyed, and touch the stone for luck.

[pause]

{img:camp-night-fire-beside-bluestone-on-sledge-forest-people-resting-stars}
Evenings in the camp were warm and friendly. The pullers sat around the fire with sore hands and tired legs. Someone played a bone flute, and others told stories. The stone sat quietly on its sledge in the firelight, as if it were listening too.

[pause]

There must have been difficult moments along the way. There were steep slopes where the stone could slide back. There were rivers where the sledge could sink into the mud. Rain turned the paths into mud, and the nights were cold. Sometimes, perhaps, a rope snapped, and everyone jumped back.

[long-pause]

But they did it, again and again. Every one of the bluestones reached the plain at last. It is one of the most amazing journeys of the ancient world. It was not done by one strong man. It was done by many people working together for a long time, for something they believed in.

[pause]

{img:bluestones-arriving-plain-stonehenge-crowd-welcoming-dusk-celebration}
When each stone finally arrived on the plain, there must have been a great celebration. There was food and music and big fires. And then the stone was set down gently on the grass. It was ready to be raised in the circle, after its long journey from the hills of the west.

[long-pause]

{img:neolithic-family-group-father-mother-children-grandmother-wool-cloaks}
Now let us meet a family from the time of the builders.

[pause]

They live about a day's walk north of Stonehenge, in a small settlement beside a stream. There are four houses there, standing close together. About twenty people live there altogether. There are parents and grandparents, children and cousins. They farm a few small fields of wheat and barley. They keep cattle and pigs and a few sheep. And they gather food from the woods and the stream.

[long-pause]

The father of the family is called Bran. That is not his real name, because we do not know any real names from this time. But we will call him Bran. He is a herder, and he knows every one of his cattle. He can tell them apart by their colour, their horns and the way they walk. He is tall and strong, and he has helped to pull great stones before.

[pause]

The mother is called Ula. She makes pots and weaves cloth, and she grows barley and wheat in the little fields. She knows every plant in the woods. She knows which ones heal and which ones are harmful. She knows which ones are good to eat in spring and which in autumn. People in the settlement come to her when they are feeling unwell. She gives them a warm drink of herbs and a kind word.

[pause]

{img:mother-making-grooved-pottery-coil-clay-hut-doorway-children-watching}
There are three children in the family. There is a girl of twelve called Wren, and a boy of eight called Pip. And there is a baby girl who is always laughing and waving her little fists. There is also the grandmother. She is old for this time, perhaps fifty years old. Her back is bent, and her hands are stiff in the mornings. But she remembers everything. She knows all the stories and all the songs, and all the ways of the ancestors.

[long-pause]

It is late autumn now. The harvest is in, and the leaves are falling from the trees. The days are getting shorter and colder. And soon the family will make a journey. They will walk south, to the great gathering on the plain, for the midwinter feast. Bran will bring pigs to share with the others. Ula will bring pots of food and baskets of hazelnuts. The children are very excited. They have heard so many stories about the feast.

[pause]

{img:family-preparing-journey-pigs-baskets-pots-autumn-leaves-settlement}
For days, the whole settlement is busy getting ready. Ula grinds grain for flat bread and packs it in leather bags. Wren helps to tie bundles of dried meat and wrap up the best pots in grass. Pip is given the job of feeding the pigs extra acorns, so they will be fat for the feast. He takes it very seriously.

[pause]

But the grandmother is a little worried. The journey is long and cold, and she is getting old. Will she be strong enough this year, she wonders? She does not want to stay behind. Every midwinter of her whole life, she has gone to the plain. She has always watched the sun go down between the great stones. She is not going to miss it now. Bran says he will carry her on his back if he has to. She laughs and says she is not a sack of barley. But she is grateful, all the same.

[long-pause]

{img:neolithic-square-house-chalk-floor-central-hearth-wooden-furniture-dim}
The family lives in a simple house. Let us go inside and look around.

[pause]

It is a square house, about five metres long on each side. The walls are made of hazel branches woven together, like a big basket. The gaps are filled with a mix of clay and straw and dung. When it dries, it becomes hard and keeps out the wind and the rain. The roof is thick and steep, covered with straw or reeds or turf. Smoke from the fire seeps slowly out through the roof.

[long-pause]

The floor is made of chalk, beaten flat and hard. It is white and clean, and the family sweeps it with a bundle of twigs. In the middle there is a hearth, a round fireplace with a low rim of clay. In winter, the fire burns day and night. It gives warmth and light, and everything is cooked on it. There are pots of stew and porridge. There is meat roasted on sticks, and flat bread baked on hot stones.

[pause]

Along the walls there are wooden boxes and beds. Archaeologists have found the marks in the chalk floors where they once stood. They found little slots where wooden planks were set into the ground. The furniture was a little like the stone furniture in the houses of Orkney, far to the north. The family sleeps on beds of straw and furs. They wrap themselves in woollen blankets and soft animal skins.

[pause]

{img:family-eating-around-hearth-pots-meat-bread-evening-firelight-house}
There is not very much in the house. There are a few pots and baskets. There are tools of stone and bone and wood. There are furs and skins, folded on the beds. Bags of grain hang from the roof beams, out of reach of the mice. There is a loom for weaving and a big flat stone for grinding grain. Everything in the house has a use.

[pause]

In the evening, the family eats together around the hearth. Ula ladles out bowls of thick stew with barley and pork. Bran tears the warm flat bread into pieces and passes them round. The baby sits on Wren's lap and tries to grab the bread. Everyone laughs, and the fire crackles cheerfully.

[long-pause]

After supper, the family sits close around the fire. The baby sleeps in a soft fur bag. And the grandmother tells stories. She tells about the old days and about the ancestors. She tells about the sun and the moon, and how they chase each other across the sky. She tells about the great stones on the plain, and why they were raised. The children listen with big eyes in the firelight.

[pause]

{img:grandmother-telling-story-firelight-children-wide-eyes-neolithic-house}
Outside, the wind blows softly across the hills. An owl calls from the edge of the woods. The cattle shift and sigh in their pen. And the family falls asleep in the warm, smoky darkness, all close together. They dream of the journey to come.

[long-pause]

Around the house, there is a little yard. There is a pen for the pigs, made of woven hazel. There is a shelter for the cattle in the worst weather. There is a pit for storing grain, lined with clay to keep it dry. There is a big pile of firewood, stacked neatly against the wall. There is a place where flint is chipped into tools, with sharp flakes lying all around. And beyond the yard are the small fields, the stream and the woods. It is a whole world, within a short walk.

[long-pause]

{img:neolithic-children-playing-stream-catching-fish-hands-summer-woodland}
What was life like for children in the time of Stonehenge?

[pause]

They worked and they played, like children everywhere in the world. As soon as they could walk, they began to help. They fetched water from the stream in small pots. They gathered sticks for the fire. They picked berries and nuts in the woods. They watched over the smaller children. And they scared the birds away from the fields of barley.

[long-pause]

As they got older, they learned the skills of the grown-ups. Boys and girls learned to herd the cattle and pigs. They learned to grind grain and bake bread. They learned to make pots from clay and to fire them. They learned to make tools from flint, knocking off sharp flakes with another stone. They learned to make cloth and rope. And they learned which plants were good to eat, and which ones to leave alone.

[pause]

Wren, the girl of twelve, is learning to make pots. Her mother shows her how, sitting beside her at the door of the house. First you roll the clay into long snakes, she says. Then you coil them round and round to build up the sides. You smooth them gently with wet fingers. Then you press patterns into the clay with a stick or a bone. You make lines and grooves and spirals. Wren's first pot was lopsided, and it cracked in the fire. She cried a little. Her mother hugged her and said, every potter's first pot cracks. Now her pots are getting better and better.

[pause]

{img:girl-learning-pottery-coiling-clay-mother-guiding-hands-outside-hut}
Wren loves the feel of the cool clay in her hands. She loves the moment when a pot comes out of the fire, hard and warm. Her favourite pattern is a spiral, like a snail shell. She puts one on every pot she makes, so everyone knows it is hers.

[pause]

Pip, the boy of eight, is learning to herd the pigs. Pigs are clever and rather naughty. They run off into the woods to look for acorns under the oak trees. Pip runs after them with a long stick, calling their names. Sometimes he catches them quickly. Sometimes they lead him on a long chase through the trees. He comes home muddy and scratched, with leaves in his hair. But he is always proud.

[long-pause]

The children play as well, whenever they can. In summer they swim in the stream and try to catch fish with their hands. They climb trees and build little shelters of branches. They play chasing games in the long grass. They make little animals out of clay. They make toys out of sticks and string. They listen to stories by the fire. And they dream about what they will do when they are grown.

[pause]

{img:boy-chasing-pigs-oak-woodland-acorns-autumn-laughing-mud}
And this year, for the very first time, Wren and Pip will help to pull a stone. At the great gathering on the plain, everyone helps, even the children. Their father says they may hold the very end of a rope. Pip has talked about nothing else for weeks. He practises pulling on the gate of the pig pen every evening. Wren pretends not to care, but she is just as excited as he is.

[long-pause]

{img:durrington-walls-huge-earthwork-circle-many-houses-smoke-gathering-winter}
About three kilometres north-east of Stonehenge, beside the river Avon, there is a great gathering place. Today it is called Durrington Walls.

[pause]

It is huge, a giant ring of earth and ditch, about five hundred metres across. It is much bigger than Stonehenge itself. Inside it, in the time of the builders, there were hundreds of houses. There may have been a thousand houses, or even more. It may have been the biggest settlement in the whole of northern Europe at that time.

[long-pause]

But people did not live there all year round. Archaeologists think it was used mainly at the great festivals, in midwinter and in midsummer. People came from all over Britain to be there. They stayed for a few weeks. They feasted and held ceremonies and met old friends. They worked together on the great stones. And then they went home again to their own villages.

[pause]

In the middle of Durrington Walls, there were great circles of wooden posts. Huge tree trunks stood upright in rings, one inside the other. They were like Stonehenge, but made of wood instead of stone. Some people think these wooden circles were for the living. And the stone circle at Stonehenge was for the ancestors. Wood grows and changes, like life that passes. Stone stays the same, like the ancestors who are always there.

[pause]

{img:timber-circle-durrington-giant-oak-posts-rings-people-ceremony-dusk}
From Durrington Walls, a road leads down to the river Avon. It is paved with flint and lined with banks of earth. People could walk from the houses down to the river. Then they could go along the river and up another road, to Stonehenge. It was a journey from the land of the living to the land of the ancestors.

[long-pause]

When archaeologists dug at Durrington Walls, they found the floors of houses. They were square houses with chalk floors and hearths in the middle. They were just like the family's house in our story. They found the marks of beds and boxes in the floors. And they found piles and piles of rubbish. There were broken pots and flint tools. And there were bones, lots and lots of bones. Most of them were the bones of pigs.

[pause]

{img:archaeologists-excavating-house-floor-chalk-hearth-durrington-trench}
The archaeologists worked slowly and carefully, with small trowels and soft brushes. They brushed away the earth, a little at a time, until the white chalk floors appeared. They drew every hole and every stone in their notebooks. They put every bone and every piece of pot into a little bag with a label. In the evenings, they sat together and talked about what they had found that day.

[pause]

Those bones tell an amazing story. It is a story about the greatest feasts of the Stone Age. And it is a story about where the builders came from.

[pause]

When we think about Durrington Walls, we can imagine it in midwinter. Smoke rises from hundreds of roofs into the cold sky. Children run between the houses, and dogs bark at every stranger. The smell of roasting pork drifts across the whole valley. People call to each other in many different accents. And down by the river, more families are arriving every day, with their pigs and their baskets. Soon the great feast will begin.

[long-pause]

{img:midwinter-feast-durrington-walls-roasting-pigs-fires-crowds-snow-dusk}
It is midwinter, and the family has arrived at Durrington Walls.

[pause]

The journey took them three days. They walked through cold rain and frosty mornings, along the paths across the hills. They drove their pigs ahead of them, with Pip running back and forth to keep them together. The grandmother walked slowly, leaning on her stick. Sometimes Bran carried her for a while on his back. She grumbled about it, but she let him. And at last, on the third evening, they came over a hill. And there it was below them.

[long-pause]

They saw the great ring of earth, white and green in the fading light. Inside it, there were hundreds of houses, with smoke rising from every roof. There were fires everywhere and people everywhere. There were more people than the children had ever seen in their lives. There were thousands of them, from all over the land. They spoke in different ways and wore different clothes. But they had all come for the same feast.

[pause]

The family finds a place to stay. Relatives from another settlement have a house there, and they make room. Everyone squeezes in together. It is crowded and warm and noisy. Pip and Wren can hardly sleep that first night. There is too much to see and too many sounds. There is music and laughter, dogs barking and pigs squealing.

[pause]

{img:crowded-house-durrington-relatives-sharing-hearth-children-excited-night}
The children meet cousins they have never met before. They swap little treasures, a shell for a bead, a feather for a pretty stone. They whisper and giggle under the furs until the grown-ups tell them to hush.

[pause]

The archaeologists found the bones of thousands of pigs at Durrington Walls. They studied the pigs' teeth carefully. And they found that most of the pigs were about nine months old when they were eaten. Pigs are born in the spring. So nine months later is midwinter. That is how we know the great feasts were held at midwinter.

[long-pause]

And the pig bones tell us something more. Scientists studied the tiny traces in the bones. These show where each pig grew up. And the pigs came from all over Britain. They came from the south-west and from Wales, from the north of England and even from Scotland. People drove their pigs hundreds of kilometres to bring them to the feast. That shows how important the feast was. People came from every corner of the island.

[pause]

{img:pigs-being-driven-along-path-chalk-hills-people-winter-journey}
The feast lasts for many days. Pigs are roasted over great fires, and the smell fills the air. People eat until they are full. They drink something sweet, made from honey or grain. They sing and dance and tell stories. Old friends meet again and hug each other. Young people meet for the first time and smile shyly. Families arrange marriages for the summer. Traders swap stone axes and pots and beads.

[long-pause]

And on the shortest day of the year, everyone goes to the stones. They walk down to the river and along it. Then they walk up the long road to Stonehenge. Thousands of people walk in a long line, as the sun goes down. They gather around the great ring. And they watch.

[pause]

{img:crowd-watching-midwinter-sunset-through-stones-stonehenge-golden-cold}
The grandmother stands with her family, holding Wren's hand. She is tired from the long walk, but her eyes are bright. The sun sinks lower and lower in the sky. And it sets exactly between the tallest stones. Golden light shines through the gap, onto the faces of the crowd. Everyone is silent. Then a great soft sound goes up from the people. The sun has gone, and the darkest day is here. And from tomorrow, the days will grow longer again.

[long-pause]

{img:sarsen-stones-scattered-downs-west-woods-grey-boulders-grass-sheep}
The big stones at Stonehenge are called sarsens.

[pause]

They are enormous. The tallest one stands more than seven metres high. That is as tall as a house with two floors. And more of it is hidden underground. The biggest sarsens weigh around thirty tons, and some weigh even more. That is as heavy as five elephants, each one.

[long-pause]

Sarsen is a very hard, grey sandstone. It formed millions of years ago, deep in the ground. Then, slowly, the softer rocks around it washed away. And the sarsens were left lying on the surface of the land. They lay scattered across the hills like giant grey sheep. People call them grey wethers. Wether is an old word for a sheep. And from a distance, they really do look like a flock of sheep lying in the grass.

[pause]

There are many sarsens on the hills about twenty-five kilometres north of Stonehenge. For a long time, people thought the Stonehenge sarsens must have come from there. But nobody knew exactly where.

[pause]

{img:scientists-analysing-sarsen-core-sample-laboratory-microscope-later}
Then, a few years ago, scientists found the answer, thanks to a small surprise. In the 1950s, workers repairing Stonehenge drilled right through one of the sarsens. They took out a long cylinder of stone, called a core. One of the workers kept it as a souvenir. He took it home and kept it in his office for many years. Sixty years later, when he was a very old man, he gave it back.

[long-pause]

The scientists studied this core very carefully. They measured the tiny amounts of different substances inside it. Every place where sarsens are found has its own special mix, like a fingerprint. Then they tested sarsens from all over the area. And they found the match. It was a place called West Woods, about twenty-five kilometres north of Stonehenge. Most of the great sarsens came from there.

[pause]

Today, West Woods is a quiet forest of tall beech trees. In spring, the ground is covered with bluebells, like a soft blue carpet. A few big grey sarsens still lie among the trees, covered with green moss. They are the ones that were left behind.

[pause]

{img:west-woods-bluebells-spring-mossy-sarsen-boulders-beech-trees}
Choosing the right stones must have been careful work. The builders needed long, straight ones for the uprights. They needed flatter ones for the lintels that would lie across the top. They walked the hills and looked at every stone. They measured them with ropes and sticks. Which one is long enough, they asked each other? Which one is strong enough? Which one has no hidden crack inside? A mistake would cost months of work.

[pause]

We can imagine an old stone-chooser walking slowly among the grey boulders. He taps each one with a small stone and listens. He runs his hand along the surface, feeling for cracks. He paces out the length and nods to himself. When he finds a good one, he ties a strip of red cloth around it.

[long-pause]

There is one more sarsen mystery. A few of the stones at Stonehenge are slightly different from the others. Scientists think they came from a different place, one that has not been found yet. Somewhere on the hills, there may be another old quarry. It may be hidden under the grass or the trees, waiting to be discovered.

[long-pause]

{img:workers-levering-sarsen-from-ground-wooden-levers-ropes-team-west-woods}
Once a good sarsen was chosen, it had to be freed from the ground and made ready for its long journey.

[pause]

Many sarsens lay half buried in the earth. The workers dug all around them with antler picks. Then they dug underneath them. They pushed long, strong wooden levers under the stone. Many people pulled down on the levers at the same moment. And slowly, the great stone lifted a little. They pushed stones and blocks of wood underneath to hold it up. Then they lifted again. Bit by bit, the stone came up out of the ground.

[long-pause]

Sometimes the stone had to be shaped a little before the journey. The workers used big round hammerstones. These were lumps of very hard stone, held in both hands or dropped from a height. They pounded the sarsen to knock off bumps and corners. It was very slow work, because sarsen is extremely hard. Each blow knocked off only a little dust. Hundreds of hammerstones have been found at Stonehenge. Some of them are worn smooth from so much use.

[pause]

{img:hammerstone-pounding-sarsen-surface-dust-chips-worker-close-hands}
The workers took turns with the hammerstones, because it made their arms ache. While some pounded, others rested and drank water and talked. The steady thump, thump, thump of the stones echoed through the woods all day. The birds soon got used to it and went on singing.

[pause]

Then the stone had to be moved onto a sledge. The sledge was a huge wooden frame, strong enough to carry thirty tons. The workers built ramps of earth and timber beside the stone. They levered it onto the sledge, a tiny bit at a time. Then they tied it down firmly with thick ropes. Everyone held their breath while this was done. If the stone rolled the wrong way, it could crack. So everyone moved slowly and carefully, and listened to the leader.

[long-pause]

All of this needed a great many people. And those people needed a great deal of food. It also needed a lot of wood. Whole groves of trees were cut down for levers and rollers and sledges. Ropes had to be made from the inner bark of lime trees. The bark was soaked and twisted and plaited until the ropes were thick and strong. It took many hands and many days.

[pause]

{img:rope-making-lime-bark-fibres-women-men-twisting-plaiting-camp}
Rope making was quiet, patient work, often done by the older people. They sat in a circle in the shade, twisting the fibres between their hands. They talked and sang and told old jokes while they worked. Long coils of new rope grew slowly beside them, like sleeping snakes.

[pause]

At West Woods, there was probably a big camp. People came for weeks at a time to work there. They cut wood and made rope and freed the stones. They ate and slept beside the fires. Children played around the stones, and dogs barked. And each time a great sarsen was ready on its sledge, a cheer went up from the whole camp. That was another one done, ready for the long pull south.

[pause]

In the evenings, the camp was a cheerful place. People cooked meat and porridge over the fires. Someone always had a story to tell, or a song to sing. And above the tall trees, the stars came out one by one.

[long-pause]

{img:great-sarsen-sledge-hundreds-people-ropes-pulling-chalk-downs-winter}
The family has come to help pull a stone.

[pause]

It is the morning after the midwinter feast. Bran and Ula and the children walk north with many others. They walk to where a great sarsen is waiting on its sledge, halfway between West Woods and Stonehenge. It has been pulled this far already. Now it must travel the rest of the way. The grandmother stays behind at Durrington Walls, by the fire, with the baby.

[long-pause]

There are hundreds of people gathered around the stone, perhaps two hundred or more. Long ropes run out from the sledge in many lines. People stand along the ropes, one behind the other. Strong men stand at the front, and women and older children behind them. And at the very end of one rope stand Pip and Wren. They hold on tightly, and their hearts are beating fast.

[pause]

In front of the sledge, workers have laid a track of wooden rails. These are logs laid end to end in a long line. The sledge will slide along them. Behind the sledge, other workers pick up the rails it has already passed. They carry them round to the front and lay them down again. They do this over and over, all day long.

[pause]

{img:wooden-rail-track-sledge-runners-workers-carrying-logs-forward-frost}
A man climbs up and stands on top of the stone. He is the leader of the pull. He raises his arms high, and everyone grips the ropes. He calls out a word, and everyone pulls together. The ropes creak and stretch, but the sledge does not move. He calls again, and they pull again, harder this time. Pip's feet slip on the frosty grass. And then, with a long low groan, the sledge slides forward. It moves only a little, but a great cheer goes up.

[long-pause]

Then they find their rhythm. The leader calls, and everyone pulls, and then they rest. Call, pull, rest. Someone starts to sing a pulling song, with a strong steady beat. Everyone joins in, and the song carries across the hills. The great grey stone moves slowly forward, a little with every pull.

[pause]

{img:people-singing-while-pulling-ropes-leader-on-stone-arms-raised}
On flat ground, everything goes well. But then they come to a slope. Going uphill is very hard work. Everyone leans forward and pulls with all their strength. The leader shouts encouragement. A few people slip in the mud, and they get up again, laughing. Going downhill needs even more care. The stone wants to slide ahead too fast. So some people hold the ropes from behind to slow it down. Wooden blocks are pushed under the runners. Everything is done slowly and carefully.

[pause]

At midday, they stop to rest and eat. Ula has brought bread and cold pork wrapped in leaves. The children sit on the frosty grass, with their cheeks pink from the cold. Their hands are red and sore, but they do not complain.

[long-pause]

By the end of the day, they have moved the stone perhaps a kilometre, or maybe a little more. Everyone is very tired. Their hands are sore from the ropes, and their legs are shaking. But they are proud of what they have done. They sit by the fires and eat and laugh and tell stories about the day.

[pause]

{img:exhausted-pullers-resting-fires-evening-stone-sledge-behind-stars}
Pip falls asleep with his head in his mother's lap, still holding a little piece of rope. Wren sits close to her father by the fire. Her hands hurt, but she cannot stop smiling. I helped, she says softly. I helped to move a stone. Bran puts his arm around her shoulders. Yes, he says, you did. And one day you will tell your grandchildren about it.

[long-pause]

{img:experimental-archaeology-volunteers-pulling-replica-stone-sledge-today}
How many people does it take to pull a stone as heavy as a sarsen? In our own time, people have tried to find out.

[pause]

Archaeologists and volunteers have done experiments to test it. They made a copy of a big stone, out of concrete, about the same weight as a sarsen. They put it on a wooden sledge. They laid wooden rails in front of it, just as the builders might have done. And then they pulled.

[long-pause]

In one famous experiment, they found that about two hundred people could pull a very heavy stone up a gentle slope. That sounds like a lot of people. But at Durrington Walls, there were thousands of people gathered together. So there were more than enough hands to do the work.

[pause]

The volunteers enjoyed it very much. They came from all walks of life, students and teachers, grandparents and children. They laughed when the sledge would not move, and cheered when it did. At the end of the day, they understood the builders a little better. They had felt the weight on the ropes and the joy of moving it together.

[pause]

They also found some useful tricks. If the rails are greased with animal fat, the sledge slides much more easily. If the path is carefully chosen, avoiding steep hills, the work goes much faster. And if everyone pulls together, in rhythm, the stone moves smoothly. Teamwork matters more than strength.

[pause]

{img:greased-timber-rails-sledge-runner-close-fat-shine-chalk-path}
The builders probably chose a route that went around the steepest hills. They may have waited for winter, when the ground was frozen hard. A frozen path is firmer than a muddy one, and the sledge does not sink. Some people think they even waited for snow. On snow and ice, a heavy sledge can slide quite well.

[long-pause]

The ropes were very important to the whole work. Rope made from lime tree bark is strong, but it wears out with use. It frays and weakens and can snap. So the rope makers must have worked all the time. They made new rope and checked the old rope, every day. A snapping rope could cause the stone to slide back. So everyone kept a careful eye on the ropes.

[pause]

{img:rope-makers-checking-frayed-rope-camp-fire-pile-of-coils-night}
In the evenings, the rope makers sat by the fire with the day's ropes across their knees. They ran their fingers slowly along every length, feeling for weak places. When they found one, they cut it out and spliced in a new piece. Their fingers were rough and quick from years of practice. Behind them, the new coils of rope were piled up, ready for the morning.

[pause]

How long did it take to bring all the sarsens to Stonehenge? There were about eighty of them. Each one took weeks to move twenty-five kilometres. Some experts think the whole job took many years, perhaps ten or more. Perhaps people worked on it every winter, after the harvest was in. That was when there was time to spare, and food from the feasts. And there were thousands of hands, all gathered together on the plain.

[long-pause]

{img:sarsen-being-raised-into-pit-a-frame-timber-ropes-crowd-pulling}
When a great sarsen finally reaches Stonehenge, the hardest part begins. Now it must be stood upright.

[pause]

First, the workers dig a pit, a deep hole in the chalk, using their antler picks. The hole is shaped very carefully. One side goes straight down. The other side slopes gently, like a ramp. The pit must be exactly the right depth. If it is too shallow, the stone will not stand firmly. If it is too deep, the stone will be too short.

[long-pause]

Then the stone is pulled on its sledge until one end sits over the sloping side of the pit. The workers lever it forward, slowly, slowly. The heavy end tips down into the pit. It slides down the slope and stops against the straight side. Now the stone is leaning at an angle. It is half down in the pit and half up in the air.

[pause]

Next, it must be pulled upright. Wooden frames are built beside it. Ropes are tied around the top of the stone. Teams of people pull on the ropes from the other side. Others push with long wooden poles from underneath. Slowly, very slowly, the great stone rises. It rises higher and higher. Everyone pulls together, and the leader calls out the rhythm. And at last, the stone stands straight up.

[pause]

{img:workers-packing-stones-chalk-around-base-upright-sarsen-pit}
Quickly, the workers fill the gap around the bottom of the stone. They pack it with broken stones and chalk. They pound it down hard with heavy wooden posts. This holds the stone firmly in place. It must not move, and it must not lean. It must stand for ever.

[long-pause]

This is an exciting moment for everyone watching. If the ropes slip, the stone could tip back down. If the pit is not deep enough, it could wobble. If the angle is wrong, the stone will lean to one side. Everyone watches closely. Some people whisper prayers. Some hold their breath. And when the stone is finally standing straight, with its base packed tight, the whole crowd cheers. Another stone stands.

[pause]

After the cheering, there is always a small celebration. Bowls of honey drink are passed around, and someone plays a drum. The workers sit down in the grass, tired and happy. Children run up to touch the new stone, and they press their hands against its cold, rough side.

[pause]

{img:upright-sarsen-standing-new-crowd-cheering-sky-dramatic-clouds}
Some of the great stones at Stonehenge are still standing in their holes today. They stand exactly where they were raised, four and a half thousand years ago. Archaeologists have dug carefully around their bases. They have found the packing stones and the antler picks. And they have even found a few broken hammerstones, dropped by the workers when the job was done.

[long-pause]

The builders must have planned the whole circle very carefully before they raised a single stone. They needed the right number of stones and the right heights. They needed exactly the right spacing between them. They probably marked out the circle with a long rope, tied to a post in the middle. They walked round and round, holding the rope tight. And they marked the places for the holes on the ground. It is just the way a child might draw a circle with a string and a stick.

[pause]

We can imagine the planners on a quiet morning, before the work began. An old woman and two men walk slowly around the circle, talking softly. They push small pegs into the ground where each stone will go. They step back and look, and move a peg a little. When they are happy, they nod to each other and smile.

[long-pause]

{img:lintel-being-raised-timber-crib-platform-between-uprights-ropes-levers}
Standing the uprights was hard work. But the next part was even harder. Now the lintels had to be lifted on top.

[pause]

A lintel is a flat stone that lies across the tops of two uprights, like the top of a doorway. At Stonehenge, the great outer circle of uprights was joined all the way around by lintels. It made a ring of stone, floating high in the air. And inside it, five giant pairs of stones each had their own lintel. These are called trilithons, which means three stones. There are two standing stones, with one lying across the top.

[long-pause]

The lintels weigh about seven tons each. And they had to be lifted more than five metres into the air. How could anyone do that, without machines?

[pause]

Nobody knows for sure. But one good idea is a wooden platform. The workers build a stack of timber beside the uprights. It looks like a square tower made of logs, laid crossways over each other. They lever the lintel up onto the first layer. Then they add another layer of logs underneath it. And they lever it up again. Layer by layer, the lintel slowly rises. It goes higher and higher, until it is level with the tops of the uprights.

[pause]

{img:workers-on-timber-platform-levering-lintel-stone-high-up-careful}
Then they slide it across, very carefully, onto the tops of the two uprights. Workers stand on the wooden platform with levers and ropes. Every movement is slow and careful and calm. Down below, everyone watches and waits quietly.

[pause]

The children watch from a safe distance, sitting on the grass with their parents. Pip asks his father how long it will take. Bran says it might take all day, or even longer. Pip decides he will watch the whole time. But after a while, he falls asleep in the winter sunshine.

[long-pause]

And when the lintel finally settles into place, there is a soft click. That is because it fits, exactly.

[pause]

The builders made special joints to hold the stones together. On top of each upright, they carved a little round bump. On the underside of each lintel, they carved a matching hollow. When the lintel comes down, the bump fits neatly into the hollow. It is like a peg fitting into a hole. So the lintel cannot slide off.

[pause]

{img:mortise-tenon-joint-carved-stone-top-of-upright-close-detail}
And the lintels of the outer circle fit to each other as well. Each end of a lintel is cut with either a groove or a tongue. The tongue of one lintel fits into the groove of the next. It is like the pieces of a wooden puzzle. So the whole ring is locked together. And the lintels are gently curved, so together they make a perfect circle in the sky.

[long-pause]

It is the only stone circle in the world where the lintels are joined like this. Nowhere else did anyone build quite like Stonehenge. The builders were doing something completely new, something nobody had tried before. And they did it so well that much of it still stands, four and a half thousand years later.

[pause]

When the last lintel of the outer ring was put in place, there must have been a great feast. People must have stood back and looked up at the ring of stone in the sky. They must have felt very proud of what they had made together.

[long-pause]

{img:sarsen-surfaces-being-smoothed-pounding-grinding-workers-dust-sunlight}
Those joints are rather strange, if you think about it. There is a bump fitting into a hollow, and a tongue fitting into a groove. Those are joints for wood, not for stone.

[pause]

Carpenters use these joints to build wooden houses and wooden frames. You cut a peg on one beam and a hole in another, and fit them together. It is called a mortise and tenon. And the tongue and groove is used to join planks side by side.

[long-pause]

So it seems that the builders of Stonehenge thought like carpenters. They had built great circles of wooden posts before, like the one at Durrington Walls. They knew very well how to join timber together. And when they built in stone, they used the same ideas. It was as if they were building a wooden house, but out of stone that would last forever.

[pause]

Carving these joints in hard sarsen was very slow work. The workers pounded the stone with hammerstones, again and again. Each blow knocked off only a tiny bit. It may have taken weeks to carve a single joint.

[pause]

{img:worker-pounding-joint-hollow-lintel-hammerstone-patience-dust}
The workers who carved the joints were probably the most skilled of all. They had learned their craft over many years, perhaps from their fathers and mothers. They checked their work again and again with sticks and measuring cords. They worked slowly, with great patience, never rushing. A joint that did not fit would mean starting all over again.

[pause]

The builders also shaped and smoothed the stones. The insides of the great stones, the sides facing the middle, were made smoother and flatter than the outsides. They were pounded first, and then ground with sand and water. This was done over and over, until the stone was smooth. It is as if the inside of Stonehenge was the best side. It was the side facing the centre, where the ceremonies took place.

[long-pause]

Some of the uprights are shaped to be a little wider at the top than in the middle. This makes them look straight when you look up from the ground. Without this clever trick, tall stones can look as if they get thinner at the top. The builders knew this. They cared very much about how their monument looked.

[pause]

{img:stonehenge-inner-faces-smooth-sarsen-close-texture-morning-light}
All of this shows great skill and great care. The builders were not just piling up rocks. They were making something beautiful and exact. And it must have taken many skilled workers many years. They pounded and ground, they measured and checked. And they did it all with patience and pride.

[pause]

Today, if you could touch the inside faces of the great stones, you would feel how smooth they are. In the early morning light, they glow softly, grey and gold. You can still see the faint marks of the hammerstones, after all these years. Each little mark was made by a person, long ago, working with care. And together, those many small marks made something that has lasted for thousands of years.

[long-pause]

{img:midwinter-sunset-stonehenge-sun-between-great-trilithon-gold-frost}
Why was Stonehenge built? We may never know everything about it. But one thing is very clear. It is lined up with the sun.

[pause]

If you stand in the middle of Stonehenge on the shortest day of the year, in winter, and look south-west, you see the sun go down. It sets exactly between the stones of the tallest trilithon. It is as if the stones were made to frame it, like a picture.

[long-pause]

And if you stand in the middle on the longest day of the year, in summer, and look the other way, toward the north-east, you see the sun come up. It rises right along the path that leads into the circle. It comes up over a large stone that stands outside the ring. This stone is called the Heel Stone.

[pause]

The midwinter sunset and the midsummer sunrise lie on the same line. The builders lined up the whole monument along this line. They did it on purpose, and with great care.

[pause]

{img:midsummer-sunrise-heel-stone-avenue-golden-rays-mist-dawn-ancient}
For farming people, the year was everything. The seasons told them when to plant and when to harvest. They told them when the animals would have their young, and when the cold would come. And the turning points of the year were the solstices, the longest day and the shortest day.

[pause]

In spring, the farmers watched for the first warm days to sow their barley. In summer, they watched the grain grow tall and turn golden. In autumn, they cut it with flint sickles and stored it carefully. And in winter, they waited by the fire for the light to come back.

[long-pause]

Midwinter must have been a time of quiet worry. The days get shorter and shorter, and the sun stays lower and lower in the sky. It is cold and dark, and the stores of food are getting smaller. What if the sun kept going away, people might have wondered? What if it never came back? But at midwinter, the sun seems to stop for a few days. And then it turns. The days start to get longer again. The light comes back, a little more each day.

[pause]

Many experts think midwinter was the most important time at Stonehenge. The great feast at Durrington Walls was held at midwinter. The pig bones tell us that. Perhaps people gathered at Stonehenge to watch the last sunset of the old year. They came to say goodbye to the old sun. And they came to welcome the new one.

[pause]

{img:people-watching-solstice-silhouettes-stones-sky-orange-quiet-awe}
Imagine standing in the circle with thousands of others, watching the sun sink between the great stones. You feel the cold on your face, and the darkness coming. But you know that tomorrow, the light will begin to return. It must have been a powerful feeling. Even today, people who stand there at the solstice say they feel it too.

[pause]

Afterwards, people walked home slowly in the dusk, talking quietly. Some held hands, and some sang softly. The children skipped ahead and ran back again. And behind them, the great stones stood dark against the last glow of the sky. The longest night had begun, but tomorrow would be a little brighter.

[long-pause]

{img:full-moon-rising-over-stonehenge-night-stars-cold-clear-ancient}
The sun was not the only thing in the sky that the builders watched. They watched the moon too, and the stars.

[pause]

The moon moves in a more complicated way than the sun. It rises and sets in different places over the months. And over a long cycle of about eighteen and a half years, its path swings from far north to far south and back again. Some people think the builders knew about this. They think some of the stones may point to where the moon rises at its furthest points.

[long-pause]

That is still being discussed by the experts. But it is certain that the builders watched the sky carefully. They watched it night after night, year after year, for many generations. They had no writing to record what they saw. So they remembered it instead. They remembered it in stories and in songs, and in the places of stones and posts.

[pause]

Without any city lights, the night sky in the Stone Age was amazing. There were thousands and thousands of stars. The Milky Way stretched across the sky like a river of light. The planets moved slowly among the stars. There were shooting stars, flashing and gone in a moment. And there was the moon, bright enough to cast shadows on the grass.

[pause]

{img:milky-way-over-neolithic-plain-wooden-posts-silhouette-people-gazing}
For people who lived close to nature, the sky was a great clock and a great calendar. The stars told you the time of night. The moon told you the time of the month. The sun told you the time of the year. When certain stars rose at dawn, it was time to plant. When others appeared, it was time to bring the animals in from the hills.

[long-pause]

On clear nights at home, the family sometimes sits outside the house to look at the sky. Bran points out the moon rising over the woods, round and orange. Ula wraps the baby warmly and rocks her gently. The cattle are lying down in their pen, chewing slowly. And everything is still, except for an owl calling softly far away.

[pause]

The grandmother of our family knows all of this very well. On the walk home from the feast, she points to the stars and teaches the children. That bright one is the herder's star, she says. When it rises early, the calves are born. Those seven little ones close together are the sisters. When they go away in spring, it is time to plant the barley. The children look up and try very hard to remember.

[pause]

The night is clear and very cold. Frost sparkles on the grass in the starlight. The family walks slowly along the path, wrapped in their cloaks and furs. Bran carries the baby on his chest, inside his cloak. Ula walks beside the grandmother, holding her arm.

[pause]

{img:grandmother-pointing-stars-children-night-walk-frost-breath}
Pip asks why the stars do not fall down from the sky. The grandmother laughs softly. Because the sky holds them, she says, just as the earth holds the stones. Pip thinks about that for a long time as they walk along. His breath makes little white clouds in the cold air. Wren asks if their ancestors are up there among the stars. Perhaps they are, says the grandmother. Perhaps they are watching over us tonight. And above them, the stars shine on, quietly, as they always have.

[long-pause]

{img:stonehenge-avenue-chalk-banks-ditches-leading-to-river-procession-dusk}
From the entrance of Stonehenge, a wide path leads away across the plain. It is called the Avenue.

[pause]

The Avenue is about twenty metres wide, with a low bank and a ditch on each side. It starts at the north-east entrance of Stonehenge. It runs straight for about five hundred metres, along the line of the midsummer sunrise and the midwinter sunset. Then it turns, and curves east across the plain. It goes down the slope and all the way to the river Avon. It is almost three kilometres long.

[long-pause]

When archaeologists dug at the start of the Avenue, they found something surprising. There were natural ridges in the chalk. These long, low ridges were made thousands of years before, by ice and water. And by chance, these ridges run exactly along the line of the midsummer sunrise and the midwinter sunset.

[pause]

Imagine the first people who noticed this. They stood on the plain at sunrise on the longest day. And they saw the sun come up right at the end of these strange natural lines in the ground. It was as if the earth itself were pointing at the sun. It must have seemed like a sign, a holy place made by the earth and the sky. Maybe that is why Stonehenge was built exactly here.

[pause]

{img:natural-chalk-ridges-aligned-sunrise-grass-dew-dawn-plain}
The Avenue turned those natural lines into a road. It was a road for processions, for people walking together slowly. They walked from the river up to the stones, or from the stones down to the river. They sang as they walked, and they carried torches and gifts. Sometimes they carried the ashes of those who had passed away.

[long-pause]

At the far end of the Avenue, beside the river, archaeologists found another small stone circle. They call it Bluestonehenge, because it was made of bluestones. Perhaps the bluestones first stood there, beside the river. Perhaps that was where the stones arrived, after coming by water. And later, they were moved up the Avenue to Stonehenge itself.

[pause]

{img:bluestone-circle-riverside-avon-small-stones-ring-reeds-misty-morning}
On the night of the midwinter feast, the family walks along the Avenue with thousands of others. It is dark, and people carry burning torches. The flames flicker and smoke in the cold air. The torches make a long river of fire, winding slowly up the hill toward the stones. Somewhere ahead, drums are beating softly. Somewhere behind, people are singing an old song. Wren holds her grandmother's hand, and Pip holds his father's. Nobody speaks very much. It is too special and too solemn for words. And at the top of the hill, black against the stars, the great stones are waiting.

[pause]

When they reach the stones, the drums stop. For a moment there is complete silence, except for the crackle of the torches. Then an old woman begins to sing, alone, in a high clear voice. One by one, others join in. The song rises up between the great stones and into the starry sky. Wren will remember this moment for the rest of her life.

[long-pause]

{img:river-avon-winter-dusk-mist-boats-torches-people-on-banks-neolithic}
The river Avon flows between Durrington Walls and Stonehenge. And some archaeologists think the river was at the heart of everything.

[pause]

Here is one idea about it. Durrington Walls, with its wooden circles and its houses and its feasts, was the place of the living. Wood is alive, and it grows and changes, just like people. And Stonehenge, with its stone circles and the ashes of the ancestors, was the place of those who had gone before. Stone is cold and hard, and it lasts forever. It does not change, just as the memory of the ancestors does not change.

[long-pause]

And the river joined these two places together. From Durrington Walls, an avenue leads down to the river. From the river, the Stonehenge Avenue leads up to the stones. So a journey from the land of the living to the land of the ancestors went down one road. Then it went along the river, and up the other road.

[pause]

Perhaps, when someone passed away, their ashes were carried on this journey. Perhaps the river was like a border between the two worlds. Many peoples in history have thought of rivers in that way. A river to cross, they said, between this life and what comes after.

[pause]

{img:neolithic-small-boat-river-carrying-offering-flowers-dawn-gentle-reeds}
We cannot know this for sure, because the people left no words. But the shape of the land and the monuments tells a story. It is a careful and thoughtful story. They built their monuments to make these journeys, and to mark them.

[long-pause]

The river was also simply very useful in everyday life. It was a road for small boats made of hollowed-out logs. Wood and food and people could travel along it. It gave water for drinking and cooking and washing. There were fish and eels to catch, and reeds to cut for roofs. There were ducks and geese to hunt. In the cold winter mornings, mist rose from the water like smoke. Swans glided slowly past, white and silent. And herons stood perfectly still in the shallows, waiting.

[pause]

{img:heron-standing-river-mist-winter-morning-willows-calm-water}
On the morning after the midwinter ceremony, the family walks back along the river to Durrington Walls. The grandmother is very tired after the long night. She walks slowly, and Ula holds her arm. They stop to rest on the river bank, on a fallen tree. The grandmother looks at the water for a long time, without speaking. The mist is rising, and the sun is pale and low.

[pause]

One day, she says quietly, you will carry me down this river, and up to the stones. To be with my mother and my father. Ula squeezes her hand gently. Not yet, she says. Not for a long time. The grandmother smiles and pats her hand. No, she says, not yet. A heron lifts slowly from the shallows and flies off down the river. And they walk on, together, in the cold bright morning.

[pause]

Back at the house, Wren makes her grandmother a warm drink of herbs and honey. The grandmother sits by the fire, wrapped in furs, and sips it slowly. Soon she is asleep, with a little smile on her face.

[long-pause]

{img:woodhenge-concentric-rings-tall-oak-posts-painted-carved-sky-neolithic}
Just south of Durrington Walls, there is another great circle. But this one was made of wood. Today it is called Woodhenge.

[pause]

It was made of six rings of tall wooden posts, one inside the other, like the rings of a tree. Huge oak trunks stood upright in deep holes in the chalk. The tallest posts may have been seven or eight metres high. That is as tall as the great stones at Stonehenge.

[long-pause]

For a very long time, nobody knew that Woodhenge was there. The wood had rotted away thousands of years before. Nothing was left above the ground at all. Then, about a hundred years ago, a pilot flying over the plain looked down. In the fields below, he saw strange rings of dark spots in the crops. The crops grew differently over the old post holes. From the air, the pattern showed up clearly. Archaeologists came and dug in that field. And they found the holes, rings and rings of them.

[pause]

{img:aerial-view-crop-marks-rings-dark-spots-field-1920s-biplane-later}
The pilot was very excited by what he had seen. He flew round and round the field, taking photographs. When he landed, he hurried to show them to his friends. Nobody had ever thought of looking for old monuments from the air before. After that, many more were found in the same way.

[pause]

Today, short concrete posts mark the places where the wooden ones once stood. They stand in rings on the grass. You can walk among them and imagine the great trunks rising high above you.

[long-pause]

Woodhenge was lined up with the sun too, just like Stonehenge. Its entrance faces the midsummer sunrise. Perhaps it was a place for ceremonies at the beginning of summer. And Stonehenge was for the end of winter.

[pause]

Some people think the wooden posts were carved and painted. They may have had patterns and faces and bright colours. We cannot know, because the wood is gone. But many peoples around the world have carved and painted their great wooden posts. It is easy to imagine the Woodhenge posts bright with colour. They might have been red and white and black, standing tall against the sky.

[pause]

{img:people-dancing-among-timber-posts-woodhenge-summer-evening-music}
Imagine walking into Woodhenge on a summer evening. Ring after ring of tall dark posts stand all around you. It is like walking into a forest of giant trees. The sky is above you, and the light flickers between the trunks. Drums are beating, and people are dancing. You can smell fresh oak and woodsmoke. It must have felt very different from the cold grey stones. It felt warm and alive, a place for the living.

[long-pause]

Wood does not last forever. After a few hundred years, the posts rotted and fell down. The rings disappeared, one by one. Only the holes remained, hidden under the grass. They waited there for a pilot to fly over on a summer day, thousands of years later. And then he saw them.

[pause]

In the middle of Woodhenge, archaeologists found a small grave. A young child had been laid to rest there, at the very centre of the rings. We do not know who the child was, or why they were placed there. We think of them gently. A little one, at the heart of the great wooden circle, under the summer sky.

[long-pause]

{img:altar-stone-fallen-under-trilithon-stonehenge-green-sandstone-centre}
In the very middle of Stonehenge, there is a big flat stone. It lies on the ground, under two fallen stones from the great trilithon. It is called the Altar Stone.

[pause]

It is different from all the others. It is not a grey sarsen, and it is not a bluestone. It is a greenish sandstone, with tiny sparkles in it that catch the light. It is about five metres long and weighs about six tons. Nobody knows exactly what it was used for. The name Altar Stone was given to it much later. It may once have stood upright, like the other stones.

[long-pause]

For a long time, people thought it came from Wales, like the bluestones. But in 2024, scientists made an amazing discovery. They studied tiny grains inside the stone. These grains hold a record of where and when the rock was formed. And they found that it did not come from Wales at all.

[pause]

It came from the far north of Scotland. It came from the area around the Orkney Islands and the north-east tip of Scotland. That is more than seven hundred kilometres away from Stonehenge. It is more than three times as far as the bluestones travelled.

[pause]

{img:northern-scotland-coast-orkney-sandstone-cliffs-sea-grey-sky-wild}
The coast of the far north is wild and beautiful. Tall cliffs of red and grey sandstone rise straight out of the sea. Seabirds nest on the ledges, and seals rest on the rocks below. The wind blows almost all the time, and the sky changes every hour.

[pause]

How could a stone weighing six tons travel seven hundred kilometres, four and a half thousand years ago? Did it go over mountains, through forests and across rivers? Or perhaps it went by sea, down the long coast of Britain, in some kind of great boat? Nobody knows. It is one of the newest mysteries of Stonehenge, and one of the most amazing.

[long-pause]

Perhaps the people who brought the stone stayed on the plain for a while. Perhaps they shared the midwinter feast and slept in the crowded houses at Durrington Walls. Perhaps they made new friends and told stories of their faraway islands. And when spring came, perhaps they began the long journey home.

[pause]

But we do know this. The people of Stonehenge were connected to people far, far away. They were connected from Wales in the west to Orkney in the far north. They used the same kind of pottery and shared the same ideas. Perhaps they shared the same beliefs. Perhaps even the same families were related by marriage across the whole island.

[pause]

{img:neolithic-boat-coast-sea-paddlers-heavy-stone-cargo-dawn-calm}
Imagine a long boat, paddled slowly along the coast on a calm morning. The paddlers dip their paddles in time, singing a low song. In the middle of the boat, carefully tied down, lies a great greenish stone. At night they pull the boat up onto a beach and make a fire. People from the nearby village come down to look at the stone and wonder.

[pause]

Maybe the Altar Stone was a gift. Maybe it was sent from the people of the north to the people of the south. It was a piece of their holy land, carried all the way to the great gathering place. It was placed in the very centre of the circle. And in this way, it joined the far corners of Britain together in one sacred ring.

[long-pause]

{img:ness-of-brodgar-orkney-stone-buildings-walls-lochs-standing-stones-sky}
If the Altar Stone came from the far north, let us travel there for a while. Let us visit the Orkney Islands.

[pause]

Orkney is a group of islands off the very top of Scotland. They are wild and windy, and surrounded on every side by the sea. There are almost no trees there at all. But there is plenty of flat stone that splits easily into slabs. And the people of Orkney in the Stone Age used it to build amazing things.

[long-pause]

On one island, between two lakes, there is a place called the Ness of Brodgar. Archaeologists have been digging there for twenty years. They have found big stone buildings with thick walls. Some had roofs made of stone tiles. Some had painted walls, in red and yellow and black. That is the oldest known paint in Britain. It was a great gathering place, like Durrington Walls in the south. But it was even older.

[pause]

Nearby stand two great stone circles. The Ring of Brodgar is a huge ring of tall, thin stones. It stands on a narrow strip of land between the lakes. The Stones of Stenness have a few great slabs still standing tall. Both of them are older than the stone circle at Stonehenge.

[pause]

{img:ring-of-brodgar-tall-thin-stones-heather-loch-sunset-orkney}
In late summer, the heather around the Ring of Brodgar turns purple. The lakes on either side shine silver and blue. Swans and geese float on the water, and curlews call across the moor. At sunset, the tall stones cast long shadows over the heather.

[pause]

And on the coast, there is a whole village from the Stone Age. It is called Skara Brae. A great storm long ago blew the sand off it, and there it was. There are little stone houses, joined together by covered passages. Inside, the furniture is still there, made of stone. There are stone beds and stone shelves. There are stone boxes set into the floor, for keeping fish or bait. And there is a stone dresser against the back wall, facing the door. That is where the family showed their best things.

[long-pause]

Grooved ware pottery, the same kind found at Stonehenge, was first made in Orkney. So it seems that ideas travelled from the north to the south. Perhaps the way of building circles travelled too. Perhaps the way of making pots. And perhaps the way of watching the sun.

[pause]

{img:skara-brae-stone-house-interior-dresser-beds-hearth-sea-light}
Imagine an evening in one of the houses at Skara Brae. The fire burns in the stone hearth in the middle of the room. A mother is mending a fishing net, and a father is sharpening a bone needle. The children are lying on the stone beds, wrapped in sealskins. Outside, the waves are breaking on the beach, and the wind is whistling over the roof.

[pause]

Now imagine a traveller from Orkney, arriving at the midwinter feast on the plain. He has made a long, long journey. He speaks a little differently from the people of the south. He brings stories from the islands of the north. He tells of stone villages by the sea, and of great circles between the lakes. The people of the plain gather round the fire to listen. They are amazed that the world is so big. And they are amazed that people so far away believe the same things they do.

[long-pause]

{img:amesbury-archer-burial-goods-copper-knives-gold-ornaments-beakers-museum}
About two hundred years after the great stones were raised, a man came to Stonehenge from very far away. We call him the Amesbury Archer.

[pause]

He was found in 2002, when builders were getting ready to build new houses near Stonehenge. Archaeologists came to look at the ground first. And they found a grave. A man was lying on his side, with his knees bent, as if he were asleep. Around him there were more things than had ever been found in a Stone Age grave in Britain.

[long-pause]

There were many arrowheads made of flint, and that is why he is called the Archer. There were two stone wrist guards. Archers wear these to protect their arm from the bowstring. There were copper knives, some of the first metal objects ever found in Britain. There were five pots of a new kind, shaped like a bell or a beaker. And there were two little hair ornaments made of gold. They are the oldest gold ever found in Britain.

[pause]

Who was this man? Scientists studied his teeth very carefully. And they found that he had grown up far away, in the Alps, in the middle of Europe. He probably came from what is now Switzerland or Germany. He had travelled all the way to Britain, over mountains and rivers and across the sea.

[pause]

{img:traveller-archer-crossing-sea-small-boat-white-cliffs-ahead-bronze-age}
He also had a bad knee. An old injury had left him limping, so walking was difficult for him for many years. And yet he made that very long journey.

[pause]

We can imagine him crossing the sea in a small boat, on a grey morning. He sits in the boat with his bow across his knees, looking ahead. And then the white cliffs of Britain appear out of the mist. He has never seen this land before. He wonders what he will find there.

[long-pause]

Why did he come all this way? Perhaps it was because of Stonehenge. Maybe its fame had spread across Europe, and he came to see it. Some people think the bluestones were believed to have healing powers. Maybe he came hoping to make his leg better.

[pause]

Or perhaps he came because he knew how to do something new. He knew how to work metal, copper and gold. People in Britain did not know how to do this yet. He may have been one of the first to bring this knowledge. Perhaps he was a metalworker, a man with a new and precious skill.

[pause]

{img:early-metalworker-crucible-fire-copper-pouring-mould-curious-onlookers}
Imagine him showing the people of the plain how it was done. He builds a small, very hot fire and puts lumps of green stone in a clay pot. The people gather round to watch, curious. Slowly, the stone melts into shining liquid metal. He pours it into a mould of stone. And when it cools, there is a bright new knife. The people gasp and murmur in wonder.

[pause]

He was a sign of changing times. After him, more people came from Europe, with their bell-shaped pots and their metal. The Stone Age was coming to an end, and the age of metal was beginning. Bronze would come soon, and life on the plain would change. But Stonehenge would stay. People would keep coming to it, and keep changing it, for another thousand years.

[long-pause]

{img:bronze-age-round-barrows-hilltops-near-stonehenge-summer-grass-sheep}
After the great stones were raised, Stonehenge went on changing.

[pause]

The bluestones were moved around several times over the centuries. First they stood in one shape, and then in another. Sometimes they made a ring, and sometimes a horseshoe, inside the great sarsen circle. People kept rearranging them, as if they were still looking for the best way. Stonehenge was never quite finished. It was always being worked on.

[long-pause]

In the age of bronze that followed, people built hundreds of round burial mounds on the hills around Stonehenge. They look like little round hills. They are still there today, and you can see them on the skyline all around. Important people were laid to rest in them, with bronze daggers and gold, amber and beads. People wanted to rest close to the great stones.

[pause]

Carvings of bronze axes and daggers were even cut into some of the sarsens. They are hard to see now, worn smooth by time and weather. But on a sunny evening, when the light is low, you can find them if you know where to look. There are little shapes of axe heads, carved on the old grey stones.

[pause]

{img:carvings-bronze-axe-heads-on-sarsen-stone-low-evening-sun-faint}
Then slowly, people stopped coming to Stonehenge. Around three thousand years ago, the great gatherings came to an end. Nobody knows exactly why. Times changed, and beliefs changed, and new ways of life came. Stonehenge stood on the plain, quiet and empty. Sheep grazed among the stones, and birds nested on the lintels.

[long-pause]

Over the centuries, some of the stones fell over. Storms and frost slowly pushed them down. Some were taken away for building. Some were broken. In the Middle Ages, people told stories about how Stonehenge was made. One famous story said that the wizard Merlin moved the stones by magic, all the way from Ireland. Others said that giants had built it. Nobody remembered the real builders anymore.

[pause]

{img:medieval-shepherd-sheep-among-fallen-stones-stonehenge-moonlight-alone}
Imagine a shepherd in the Middle Ages, on a moonlit night. He sits among the fallen stones with his dog, wrapped in his cloak. His sheep graze quietly around him. He looks up at the great stones in the moonlight and wonders who put them there. Perhaps he thinks of giants, or wizards. And then he pulls his hood up and dozes against a stone until morning.

[pause]

But the stones stayed, most of them. They stood tall and grey on the empty plain. They stood through Roman times and Saxon times and Norman times. They stood all through the Middle Ages. They were waiting for people to come back and try to understand them.

[long-pause]

In later centuries, travellers on horseback stopped to look at the stones on their way across the plain. Some carved their names into the soft surfaces, which we would not do today. Painters came to paint the stones in storms and in sunsets. Poets came and wrote about them. And slowly, people began to ask again who had built them, and why.

[pause]

For a while, Stonehenge even belonged to private owners. In 1915, it was sold at an auction. A local man bought it, as a present for his wife. She did not much like it, people say. So a few years later, he gave it to the nation. Since then, it has belonged to everyone.

[long-pause]

{img:antiquarian-1720s-drawing-stonehenge-measuring-tape-notebook-wig-coat}
About three hundred years ago, people started to study Stonehenge seriously.

[pause]

One of the first was a man called William Stukeley. He was a doctor who loved old things and old places. He spent many summers at Stonehenge, with his notebooks and his measuring tapes. He drew it carefully and measured every stone. And he noticed something important. The monument was lined up with the midsummer sunrise. He was one of the first people to see it.

[long-pause]

But Stukeley also had some wrong ideas. He thought Stonehenge was built by the druids, the priests of the Celtic people. We now know that the druids lived two thousand years after Stonehenge was built. But the idea stuck, and many people still believe it. Druids did not build Stonehenge. But druids today do gather there at the solstice to celebrate, dressed in long white robes.

[pause]

In the 1900s, archaeologists began to dig carefully at Stonehenge. They measured and recorded everything they found. They found the antler picks and the hammerstones. They worked out the different stages of building, one after another. And a new science helped them. It was a way to tell how old things are, by measuring tiny changes in anything that was once alive. It works on bone and antler and wood. That is how we know the ditch was dug around 3000 before Christ. And it is how we know the great stones were raised around 2500.

[pause]

{img:archaeologists-1950s-excavating-stonehenge-trench-stones-measuring-poles}
In the 1950s and 1960s, some of the fallen stones were lifted and set upright again, using cranes. Some people did not like this. Others said it helped to save the monument for the future.

[pause]

The archaeologists of those days worked in old jumpers and wide hats. They dug long trenches with spades and trowels. They drank tea from flasks and ate sandwiches sitting on the stones. In the evenings, they wrote up their notes by lamplight in the village inn.

[long-pause]

In the last twenty years, the discoveries have come faster and faster. There was the great settlement at Durrington Walls, and the pig bones from the midwinter feasts. There were the quarries in Wales, and Bluestonehenge by the river. There was the source of the sarsens in West Woods. There was the Altar Stone from Scotland, and the Amesbury Archer from the Alps. Scientists now have new machines to help them. Some machines can see under the ground without digging. Others can read the tiny traces in teeth and stones. Bit by bit, the story of the builders is coming back to us.

[pause]

{img:ground-penetrating-radar-survey-field-stonehenge-modern-team-later}
Today, small teams walk slowly across the fields around Stonehenge, pushing machines like lawnmowers. The machines send signals into the ground and listen to the echoes. On a computer screen, hidden pits and ditches and post holes appear. In this way, the plain gives up its secrets without a single spade.

[pause]

And still, there are many mysteries left. Why exactly did they build it? What did they believe? What songs did they sing as they pulled the stones? What did they call it? We will probably never know these things. But we know much more than anyone did a hundred years ago. And every year, the plain gives up a few more of its secrets.

[long-pause]

{img:stonehenge-today-visitors-path-around-stones-summer-sky-plain}
Today, Stonehenge is one of the most famous places in the world. More than a million people visit it every year.

[pause]

Visitors walk along a path around the stones. On most days, they cannot go inside the circle, because the stones must be protected. Too many feet could wear away the ground. But they walk slowly around the outside, looking. And they are amazed at how big the stones are, and how very old.

[long-pause]

Near Stonehenge, there is a visitor centre. Inside, you can see the bones and antler picks and pots that the builders left behind. You can see the face of a man who lived nearby, five and a half thousand years ago. Scientists rebuilt his face from his skull. Outside, there are copies of the Stone Age houses from Durrington Walls. They are square houses with chalk floors and hearths in the middle. And there is a big stone on a wooden sledge, with long ropes. Visitors can try to pull it. It is very hard to move, unless a lot of people pull together.

[pause]

{img:modern-solstice-crowd-sunrise-stonehenge-people-celebrating-june}
And twice a year, something special happens. At the summer solstice and the winter solstice, the circle is opened to everyone. Anyone can come inside the ring. Thousands of people gather in the dark. There are druids and families, young people and old people, from all over the world. They wait through the night together. And when the sun rises on the longest day, they cheer. Drums beat, and people dance. They celebrate just as their ancestors may have done, four and a half thousand years ago.

[long-pause]

So we leave the plain tonight, in the time of the builders, as the evening falls.

[pause]

The midwinter sun has set between the great stones. The sky is turning from gold to rose to a deep, soft blue. The first stars come out over the plain, one by one. At Durrington Walls, the fires are burning and the last pigs are roasting. The smell of woodsmoke drifts on the cold air.

[pause]
