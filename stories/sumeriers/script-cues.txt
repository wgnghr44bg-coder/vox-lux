{img:ur-2100bc-city-ziggurat-dawn-euphrates-palms-reed-boats-mist}
Tonight we go more than four thousand years back. We go to the land of Sumer, in the south of what is now Iraq. The year is around 2100 before Christ. And we go to one of the oldest cities in the world. Its name is Ur.

[long-pause]

It is early morning. The sun rises over a flat land of rivers and palm trees and reeds. A great river flows slowly past the city. Boats made of reeds glide on the water. Herons stand in the shallows. And above the city, a huge stepped tower rises into the pale sky. On its top stands a small temple. The first light of the sun touches it and turns it gold.

[pause]

The people of Ur are waking up. Women light their ovens. Men lead donkeys to the fields. Boys run down to the river to fish. Priests climb the tower for the morning prayers. It is a day like any other. And yet these people are doing something new in the history of the world. They are living in cities.

[pause]

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

{img:sumerian-people-market-ur-wool-skirts-baskets-donkeys-morning}
The Sumerians are often called the first civilization. That is a big word. It means people living together in cities. With laws and writing and kings. With temples and schools and trade. The Sumerians were among the very first people on earth to do all this.

[pause]

They wrote the first writing. They built some of the first cities. They made some of the first wheels and ploughs. They wrote the first stories and the first laws. They counted time in sixties, and we still do. So much of what we take for granted began here, in this flat hot land between two rivers.

[pause]

{img:sumerian-scribe-reed-stylus-clay-tablet-courtyard-shade-ur}
And yet for thousands of years, the Sumerians were completely forgotten. Their cities turned to mounds of earth. Their language was no longer spoken. Nobody even remembered their name. Only about a hundred and fifty years ago did people start to find them again.

[long-pause]

Tonight we will walk through Ur in the time of its greatest king. We will climb toward the great tower. We will visit a family in their house. We will sit in a school where boys learn to write on clay. We will sail to faraway lands with the traders. We will hear the oldest story in the world. And we will learn how a whole lost world was dug back out of the earth.

[pause]

{img:great-ziggurat-ur-reconstructed-stairs-temple-top-golden-light}
So let us walk along the river bank toward the walls of Ur. The air is warm and soft. The palm leaves rustle. The smell of baking bread drifts out from the city. And the great tower stands waiting in the morning sun.

[pause]

Down by the canal, the first boats are already moving. They are long and narrow, made of reeds tied tightly together and coated with black tar. A man pushes his boat along with a long pole, loaded with baskets of fish. On the bank, women are filling clay jars with water and chatting. A boy drives a small flock of goats through the city gate. And from somewhere inside the walls comes the smell of fresh bread, baking in a clay oven.

[long-pause]

{img:sumer-landscape-flat-plain-canals-palm-groves-cities-distance}
Sumer is the southern part of a land called Mesopotamia. That name means the land between the rivers. The two rivers are the Tigris and the Euphrates. Today most of this land is in Iraq.

[pause]

It is a flat, hot, dry land. It hardly ever rains. In summer, it is so hot that the air shimmers. And yet people have lived here for thousands of years. Because of the rivers.

[long-pause]

The rivers bring water and rich mud from the mountains far to the north. Long ago, people learned to dig canals. They led the river water out to the fields. With water, the dry land became green and rich. Barley grew tall. Date palms grew thick. Sheep and goats grew fat. There was more food than people needed. And that changed everything.

[pause]

When there is extra food, not everyone has to farm. Some people can become potters or weavers or builders. Some can become priests or writers or traders. Villages grow into towns. Towns grow into cities. And that is what happened here, in Sumer, more than five thousand years ago.

[pause]

{img:sumerian-city-states-map-like-ur-uruk-lagash-nippur-rivers}
Sumer is not one country with one king, at least not always. It is a land of many cities. Ur and Uruk. Lagash and Nippur. Eridu and Kish. Each city has its own god and its own temple. Each has its own ruler. They trade with each other. They marry into each other's families. And sometimes they argue over water and land.

[long-pause]

The people call themselves the black-headed people. Nobody knows exactly why. Maybe because of their black hair. Their language is unlike any other language we know. It is not related to any language spoken today. It is a puzzle.

[pause]

{img:sumerian-man-woman-woollen-fringed-robes-shaved-head-long-hair}
The men often shave their heads and wear long beards. Or they shave everything. The women wear their hair long and pinned up. Both wear long wrap-around robes of wool. Some robes have rows of fringes, like the fur of a sheep. Rich people wear jewellery of gold and blue stone. And in summer, everyone looks for shade.

[pause]

Most people in Sumer are farmers, herders and fishermen. They live in the cities or in small villages nearby. They get up with the sun and go to bed when it gets dark. They work in the fields and along the canals all day. In the evenings, they sit together outside their houses and talk. The children play in the dust, and the old people tell stories. Life is simple, but it has its own quiet rhythm.

[pause]

The land of Sumer is flat and hot, with very few trees. There is no stone and very little wood. But there is plenty of mud and clay along the rivers. So the Sumerians build almost everything from mud. They make bricks and dry them in the sun. They make pots and jars and little figures. They even write on tablets of clay. It is a land built from the earth itself, by patient and clever hands.

[long-pause]

{img:southern-iraq-marshes-reed-houses-mudhif-canoe-water-buffalo-dawn}
South of Ur, where the rivers come close to the sea, there are great marshes.

[pause]

They are a world of water and reeds. The reeds grow tall, taller than a man. Narrow channels of water wind between them. The water is still and clear. Fish swim in it. Birds of every kind live there. Herons and ducks and pelicans. Kingfishers flash blue between the stems.

[long-pause]

The marsh people live on little islands. Some of the islands are made by people. They pile up reeds and mud until it is high enough to build on. Their houses are made of reeds too. They tie bundles of tall reeds into great arches. They bend them over and tie them together at the top. Then they cover the frame with woven reed mats. The result is a long tunnel-shaped house. Cool in summer. Dry in the rain.

[pause]

Some of these reed houses are very big. A great reed hall can be twenty or thirty metres long. Villages meet there. Guests are welcomed there. And they are beautiful, with patterns woven into the mats.

[pause]

{img:reed-arch-house-interior-woven-mats-light-through-pattern-calm}
The marsh people travel in long thin boats. They push them along with poles through the channels. They fish with spears and nets. They keep water buffalo, big dark animals that wallow in the water. The buffalo give milk. And their dung is dried and burned as fuel.

[long-pause]

The amazing thing is this. Pictures from Sumer, more than four thousand years old, show exactly these reed houses and these boats. And in the marshes of southern Iraq, people built the very same houses in the same way until quite recently. The same arches. The same mats. The same long boats. For five thousand years, almost nothing changed.

[pause]

{img:marsh-boy-poling-canoe-reeds-channel-heron-taking-off-morning}
For the people of Ur, the marshes are close by. Reeds are one of their most useful materials. They make houses and boats and baskets and mats from them. And they use one thin reed for something very special. They cut it to a point. And with it, they write.

[pause]

In the marshes, people live in houses made entirely of reeds. They tie great bundles of tall reeds together and bend them into arches. The houses look like long tunnels with rounded roofs. Inside, it is cool and shady, even on the hottest days. The people fish and hunt birds and keep water buffalo. They travel everywhere by little reed boats, gliding silently between the green walls of reeds. And in the evenings, the marsh is full of the sound of frogs and birds.

[pause]

The two great rivers bring life to the whole land. Their names are the Tigris and the Euphrates. The farmers dig long canals to carry the water from the rivers to their fields. Every year, the canals must be cleaned of mud, or they will fill up. So in the cool months, whole villages work together with baskets and hoes. They sing as they dig, and the mud piles up along the banks. Without the canals, nothing would grow.

[long-pause]

{img:ur-city-walls-mudbrick-harbours-river-boats-houses-dense-aerial}
Ur is a big city for its time. Perhaps thirty or forty thousand people live here. Maybe more.

[pause]

It is shaped like an oval. A thick wall of mud brick runs all around it. On the west side runs the river Euphrates. On the north side there is a harbour, where boats come in from the river and the sea. A second harbour lies on the west. Canals lead in and out. Ur is a city of water as well as of land.

[long-pause]

Inside the walls, the city is crowded. Narrow winding streets run between the houses. They are made of beaten earth. They are dusty in summer and muddy when it rains. Rubbish is thrown into the street. Over the years, the street level rises. Some houses have steps going down into them from the street.

[pause]

In the north of the city, behind a second wall, is the holy area. That is where the great tower stands. Around it are temples and store rooms and the houses of priests. And the palace of the king.

[pause]

{img:narrow-winding-street-ur-mudbrick-walls-doorways-people-donkey}
The rest of the city is for ordinary people. Houses packed close together. Little shops and workshops. Small shrines at the street corners. Bakers and potters and weavers. Coppersmiths hammering. The smell of bread and dung and spices. Children playing. Dogs sleeping in the shade. And everywhere, donkeys with loads on their backs.

[long-pause]

Everything is built of mud brick. There is hardly any stone in Sumer. And almost no good timber. But there is mud. Endless mud from the rivers. People mix it with straw and water. They press it into wooden moulds. They let the bricks dry in the sun. And with these bricks, they build everything. Houses and walls and temples and towers.

[pause]

{img:brickmakers-ur-moulds-straw-mud-rows-drying-sun-river}
Sun-dried brick does not last forever. Rain slowly washes it away. So walls must be repaired again and again. Over hundreds of years, old houses fall down. New houses are built on top of them. The city slowly rises up on its own ruins. That is why the old cities of Sumer are now big mounds of earth. Layer upon layer upon layer. Each layer is a page of history.

[long-pause]

At night, the gates of Ur are closed. Guards stand on the walls with torches. The streets grow dark and quiet. Only the dogs bark now and then. And from the holy area, the soft sound of the priests' evening hymns drifts over the rooftops. The city sleeps inside its walls, safe until morning.

[pause]

In the busy streets of Ur, there are little shops and workshops everywhere. A potter sits at his wheel in an open doorway, shaping a jar. A baker sells round flat loaves, still warm from the oven. A barber shaves a man's head on a stool in the street. Donkeys carry heavy loads, and their bells jingle as they walk. Children run between the people, and dogs sleep in the shade. And everyone seems to be talking at once.

[long-pause]

{img:ziggurat-ur-nammu-three-stairways-meeting-terraces-trees-blue-sky}
Above everything in Ur rises the great tower. It is called a ziggurat.

[pause]

A ziggurat is a stepped tower built of brick. It has several levels, one on top of another. Each level is a little smaller than the one below. At the very top stands a small temple. It looks like a mountain made by people. And that is just what it is meant to be. A holy mountain in the flat land.

[long-pause]

The great ziggurat of Ur was built by King Ur-Nammu, the father of the present king. It is huge. Its base is about sixty metres long and forty-five metres wide. Inside, it is solid mud brick. Outside, it is covered with a thick skin of baked bricks, set in black tar to keep out the water. Millions of bricks went into it.

[pause]

Three great stairways lead up the front. One in the middle and one on each side. They meet at a gate high up on the first level. From there, more stairs lead up to the second level and the third. And at the very top is the temple of the moon god.

[pause]

{img:priests-climbing-ziggurat-stairs-procession-offerings-morning}
The builders were clever. They knew a huge brick tower could collapse if water got inside. So they left little holes in the walls. They placed layers of reed mats between the bricks. Rain water could drain out. The tower could breathe. And it stood strong.

[long-pause]

They had another trick too. The walls of the ziggurat are not perfectly straight. They lean in very slightly, and they curve a tiny bit outward in the middle. You cannot see it if you do not know. But it makes the tower look stronger and taller. The Greeks used the same trick much later in their temples. The Sumerians thought of it first.

[pause]

{img:ziggurat-terraces-planted-trees-gardens-green-on-brick-steps}
Some people think there were trees on the terraces. Green trees on a brown tower, like a holy hill. Holes for soil have been found on the levels. Imagine it. A green mountain rising out of the city, with a temple shining on the very top.

[long-pause]

Only priests may climb all the way to the top. Ordinary people watch from below. On feast days, they see processions going up the great stairs. They hear music and singing. They smell incense drifting down. And they look up at the temple at the top, where their god lives, close to the sky.

[pause]

{img:ziggurat-at-night-moon-rising-behind-temple-top-city-dark-below}
At night, the moon rises behind the ziggurat. It hangs above the temple on the top. And for the people of Ur, that is the most beautiful sight of all. Because the moon is their god.

[pause]

Early in the morning, before the city wakes, the priests climb the great stairway of the ziggurat. They carry jars of water, baskets of bread and bowls of incense. At the top, they wash the shrine and sweep the floor. They light the lamps and say the morning prayers. From up there, they can see the whole city spread out below them. They can see the river and the fields and the marshes. And they watch the sun come up over the land.

[long-pause]

{img:crescent-moon-over-ur-ziggurat-night-sky-stars-reed-houses}
Every city in Sumer has its own god. The god of Ur is Nanna, the god of the moon.

[pause]

The Sumerians believe the moon is a god who sails across the night sky in a boat. Some nights his boat is thin like a sliver. Some nights it is round and full. He watches over the cattle and the herds. He keeps the calendar, because the months follow the moon. And above all, he watches over Ur.

[long-pause]

Nanna is a gentle god in many stories. He is wise and calm and fair. His wife is the goddess Ningal. Her name means great lady. Their children are the sun god and the goddess of the morning star. So the moon is the father of the sun, in Sumerian belief. The night came first. And the day was born from it.

[pause]

In the temple at the top of the ziggurat, and in the big temple at its foot, Nanna lives. Not just in the sky. The Sumerians believe the god really lives in his temple, in the form of a statue. The statue is dressed in fine clothes. It is given food every day. Bread and beer and meat and fruit. It is washed and cared for, like a great lord.

[pause]

{img:temple-of-nanna-courtyard-priests-offering-food-statue-incense}
Hundreds of people work for the temple. Priests and singers. Cooks and bakers and brewers. Weavers and herdsmen. The temple owns fields and flocks and fishing boats. It is not only a place of prayer. It is one of the biggest businesses in the city.

[long-pause]

The people of Ur love their god. They bring him gifts. They pray to him when they are sick or sad. They thank him when things go well. And every month, when the new moon appears as a thin silver line in the evening sky, there is a little festival. The god has come back. Everyone goes out to look.

[pause]

The darkest nights are a little worrying. When the moon disappears completely, for a night or two, people wonder. Where has the god gone? Will he come back? The priests say prayers and make offerings. And then, there it is again. A thin silver curve low in the west, just after sunset. People point and smile. Nanna has returned.

[pause]

{img:family-on-roof-pointing-at-new-moon-evening-ur-crescent}
In a few weeks, the city will hold its greatest festival for the moon. The statue of Nanna will be carried out of his temple. He will travel by boat to visit the father of the gods, in another city. And all of Ur is getting ready. Because if the journey goes well, the whole year will be blessed.

[pause]

On nights of the full moon, many families in Ur go up to their roofs to look at it. The moon rises big and golden over the city walls. It slowly turns silver as it climbs higher into the sky. The grandmother tells the children that Nanna is sailing across the sky in his boat. The children look up and try to see the boat. The little one says he can see it. Everyone smiles. And they sit quietly together in the silver light.

[pause]

In the temple of Nanna, the priestesses look after the god's daily needs. Twice a day, they bring him meals of bread and beer, meat and fruit. They dress his statue in fine clothes and decorate it with jewels. They play music for him and sing his songs. People believe the god enjoys these things, just as people do. And they hope that, in return, he will watch over the city and keep it safe.

[long-pause]

{img:king-shulgi-ur-seated-throne-wool-robe-beard-officials-bowing}
The king of Ur in our story is called Shulgi. He is one of the greatest kings Sumer ever had.

[pause]

His father was Ur-Nammu. Ur-Nammu made Ur strong again after a time of trouble. He joined many cities of Sumer under his rule. He built the great ziggurat. He dug canals and made roads safe. And then, sadly, he fell in battle far from home. Shulgi was young when he became king. Everyone wondered. Could he hold his father's kingdom together?

[long-pause]

He could. Shulgi ruled for almost fifty years. And under him, Ur became the centre of a great kingdom. Its power reached across all of Sumer and beyond. To the mountains in the east. And up the rivers to the north.

[pause]

Shulgi was a great organiser. He made everyone in the kingdom use the same weights and measures. A bag of barley weighed the same in Ur as in Nippur. He set up a calendar that everyone followed. He built roads with rest houses along them for travellers. Every day, at these stations, messengers could find food and water and a place to sleep.

[pause]

{img:ur-iii-scribes-office-thousands-tablets-counting-sheep-barley}
And he loved writing. Under Shulgi, there were more scribes than ever before. They wrote down everything. How many sheep came to the temple. How much barley each worker received. How many days a man worked in the fields. Tens of thousands of these clay tablets have been found. They are the most careful records from the ancient world. Some people have called this kingdom the first great office of history.

[long-pause]

Shulgi also liked to boast. He had songs written about himself. In them, he says he is the best at everything. The fastest runner. The best singer. The wisest judge. He says he can speak five languages. He says he can play every instrument. He says he went to school as a boy and was the best student of all.

[pause]

{img:shulgi-playing-lyre-court-musicians-listening-hall-lamps}
Some of that is probably true. Some of it is probably bragging. But one of his boasts was so famous that people sang about it for hundreds of years. It was about a great run. And it is a story worth hearing.

[long-pause]

But being king is not only songs and boasting. Every morning, Shulgi meets his officials. Reports come in from every city. A canal has silted up near Lagash. A flock of sheep has fallen sick near Umma. Messengers from the mountains in the east bring news of trouble on the border. The king listens. He gives orders. He sends workers and soldiers and silver. A good king must keep the whole land running. And the land is big.

[pause]

In the palace, King Shulgi has many servants and officials. Scribes write down his orders on clay tablets. Cooks prepare great feasts of bread and meat and dates. Musicians play for him in the evenings. But the old songs say that Shulgi also liked to be among ordinary people. He visited the temples and the schools. He talked with farmers and soldiers. And he wanted people to remember him as a wise and kind king.

[pause]

Shulgi also built roads and rest houses for travellers, all across his kingdom. Every few hours of walking, there was a place to stop. Travellers could find water and shade and a place to sleep. Messengers could get fresh food before they ran on. It made the roads safer and the journeys easier. Traders were glad of it, and so were the farmers taking their goods to market.

[long-pause]

{img:king-running-road-sumer-dawn-palms-way-stations-from-nippur}
This is the story of King Shulgi's great run.

[pause]

It was a festival day. Shulgi was in the holy city of Nippur, in the north of Sumer. That is where the father of the gods has his temple. And on the same day, there was also a festival in Ur, for the moon god. Shulgi wanted to be at both. But Ur and Nippur are far apart. About a hundred and sixty kilometres. A whole week's walk for most people.

[long-pause]

So Shulgi said, I will run. He rose before dawn in Nippur. He made his offerings in the temple there. Then he tied his belt tight and set out along the road to Ur. Alone, or with a few guards. Down the long straight road beside the canal.

[pause]

He ran past fields and villages. Past palm groves and reed beds. The sun came up and climbed higher. It grew hot. He ran on. At each rest station, he stopped for a drink of water. Then he ran on again. Farmers in the fields looked up as he passed. Was that the king? Running? They stared after him.

[pause]

{img:farmers-in-field-looking-up-runner-passing-dust-road-midday}
In the afternoon, clouds gathered. Rain began to fall. Then hail. Heavy hard balls of ice pounding down on the road. The wind howled. It was hard to see. Most people would have stopped. Taken shelter. Waited. But the song says Shulgi did not stop. He ran through the storm like a lion. Like a donkey of the mountains. Like a wild horse.

[long-pause]

And that evening, as the sun went down, he reached Ur. He ran up to the temple of the moon god. And he made his offerings there too. Both festivals, on one day. The people of Ur cheered him. Then he turned around. And the song says he ran all the way back to Nippur that same night.

[pause]

Did it really happen? Maybe. Maybe not all of it. Running from Nippur to Ur in one day would be an amazing feat. Running there and back would be almost impossible. But the song was sung for generations. Children learned it at school. And every king after Shulgi wanted to be as strong and fast as he was.

[pause]

{img:king-arriving-ur-temple-evening-torches-crowd-cheering-tired}
Today, some long distance runners have tried to run the same distance in a day. It can be done. It takes great strength and training. Shulgi was probably a good runner. And a very good storyteller about his own running.

[pause]

The story of Shulgi's great run was told for many years in Sumer. Children in the schools copied it onto their clay tablets. Old men told it in the evenings by the fire. Boys raced each other along the canal banks, pretending to be the king. The one who won would shout, I am Shulgi! And everyone would laugh and race again. In this way, the story stayed alive, long after the king himself was gone.

[long-pause]

{img:ur-house-two-storey-courtyard-wooden-gallery-stairs-family}
Let us visit a family in their house in Ur.

[pause]

The house is on a narrow street in the middle of the city. From outside, you see only a plain wall of mud brick with a small wooden door. There are no windows on the street. That keeps out the heat and the dust and the noise. And it keeps the family private.

[long-pause]

Inside the door there is a small room for washing your feet. Then you step into the courtyard. It is open to the sky, paved with baked bricks. In the middle is a drain, so the rain can run away. Around the courtyard are the rooms. A kitchen with a clay oven. A store room for jars of barley and oil and dates. A room where guests are welcomed. And at the back, a small family chapel.

[pause]

Some houses in Ur have two floors. A wooden gallery runs around the courtyard on the upper level. Stairs of brick lead up to it. The bedrooms are up there. And above them is the flat roof. On hot summer nights, the whole family sleeps on the roof.

[pause]

{img:family-chapel-small-altar-clay-figures-lamp-ancestors-below-floor}
The family chapel is a special room. There is a little altar and a few clay figures of the family's gods. And under the floor of the chapel, the family buries its dead. Grandparents and great-grandparents lie there, in brick tombs. The family believes they are still part of the household. Every month they pour water and leave a little food for them.

[long-pause]

This family is a family of traders. The father buys and sells copper and cloth. He travels sometimes by boat down to the sea. He keeps his records on clay tablets in a box in his room. Thousands of letters and contracts have been found in houses like this one in Ur.

[pause]

{img:trader-father-reading-clay-tablets-box-letters-courtyard-room}
The mother runs the house. She has a few servants to help her. She manages the store room and the kitchen and the money while her husband is away. Their house is busy. Two sons, one daughter, a grandmother. A servant girl. A cat that catches mice in the store room. And a little dog that barks at every visitor.

[long-pause]

Right now, the father is far away. He left two months ago on a boat for a land across the sea. He should be back soon. The mother checks the harbour every few days. The children ask every morning, is father home today? Not yet, she says. But soon.

[pause]

In the evening, the mother walks slowly through the house and checks that everything is in order. She closes the store room and makes sure the jars are covered. She lights a small lamp in the family chapel. She looks in on the children, already half asleep. Then she climbs the stairs to the roof and looks out over the city. Somewhere far away, across the sea, her husband is sleeping on a ship. She wishes him a safe journey home. Then she lies down and closes her eyes.

[long-pause]

{img:woman-grinding-barley-courtyard-dawn-clay-oven-flatbread-ur}
Let us spend a day with this family.

[pause]

The day starts early. Before the sun is up, the servant girl lights the clay oven in the courtyard. The mother grinds barley on a flat stone, rolling a smaller stone back and forth. Soon there is flour. She mixes it with water and salt. She slaps flat round pieces of dough onto the hot inside wall of the oven. They bake in a few minutes. She peels them off. Warm bread for breakfast.

[long-pause]

They eat in the courtyard as the sky turns pink. Bread and dates. A little cheese made from goat's milk. And beer. Thick, cloudy, a little sweet. Everyone drinks beer in Sumer. Even children drink a weak kind. It is safer than the river water.

[pause]

Then the day begins. The eldest son, who is twelve, goes off to school. He carries a little basket with his clay tablet and his reed pen. He is not happy about it. School is hard. The younger son, who is seven, goes with the grandmother to the market. The daughter, who is ten, stays home and helps her mother spin wool.

[pause]

{img:girl-spinning-wool-spindle-courtyard-mother-weaving-loom-shade}
Wool is everywhere in Sumer. There are huge flocks of sheep. Their wool is spun into thread and woven into cloth. The cloth is sold far and wide. Every woman in Ur knows how to spin. The daughter holds a stick wrapped with wool in one hand. She twists the thread with the other, onto a spinning stick that hangs and turns. Round and round. Her thread is still a little lumpy. But it is getting better.

[long-pause]

At the market, the grandmother buys onions and garlic and fish. She bargains hard. The seller sighs and gives in. The little boy watches a man with a monkey that does tricks. He laughs so hard he hiccups. On the way home, the grandmother buys him a little cake with honey and sesame.

[pause]

{img:market-ur-grandmother-boy-fish-seller-onions-baskets-monkey-trick}
At midday, it is too hot to do anything. Everyone rests in the cool dark rooms. The streets go quiet. Only the flies are busy.

[long-pause]

In the late afternoon, the eldest son comes home from school. His hand hurts. Did you get in trouble, his mother asks. A little, he says. I talked when I should have been writing. She shakes her head and gives him a piece of bread.

[pause]

{img:family-supper-roof-evening-ur-ziggurat-silhouette-sunset-lamps}
In the evening, the family eats on the roof. Lentil stew with onions. Bread. Fish. Cucumbers. The sun goes down behind the ziggurat. The sky turns red and gold. Swallows dart overhead. The grandmother tells a story about the moon god. And as the stars come out, the mother looks toward the harbour. Still no ship. Maybe tomorrow.

[pause]

At the market, the grandmother and the younger son buy onions and a fish. The boy carries the basket proudly, even though it is heavy. They stop at a stall where an old man sells little clay animals. There are sheep and goats and tiny donkeys. The boy looks at them for a long time. His grandmother smiles and buys him a little clay goat. He holds it carefully all the way home, and that night he puts it beside his bed.

[pause]

The servant girl spends much of her day in the kitchen with the mother. She grinds barley on a flat stone, pushing a smaller stone back and forth. She fetches water from the well in the street. She sweeps the courtyard with a broom of palm leaves. In the afternoon, when the work is done, she sits in the shade and plays with the cat. The cat purrs and stretches out on the warm bricks. And for a little while, everything is quiet.

[long-pause]

{img:clay-tokens-shapes-cones-spheres-envelope-early-counting-uruk}
The Sumerians gave the world one of its greatest gifts. Writing.

[pause]

It did not start with poems or stories. It started with counting. Long before writing, people in this land used little clay tokens to keep track of things. A small cone for a measure of barley. A ball for a sheep. A disc for a jar of oil. If you owed someone ten sheep, you gave them ten little clay balls.

[long-pause]

Then people started to put the tokens inside a hollow ball of clay, like an envelope. And they pressed the tokens on the outside first, so you could see what was inside without breaking it. Then someone had a clever idea. Why bother with the tokens at all? Just press the marks into a flat piece of clay. And that was the start of writing.

[pause]

It happened about five thousand two hundred years ago. In the city of Uruk, not far from Ur. The earliest tablets from Uruk are mostly lists. Barley and sheep and workers and beer. Little pictures pressed or drawn into clay. A head of barley for barley. A sheep's head for sheep. And marks for numbers.

[pause]

{img:earliest-uruk-tablet-pictographs-barley-sheep-numbers-close-light}
Over hundreds of years, the pictures changed. Writing with a reed on wet clay is easier if you just press. So the pictures turned into little groups of wedge-shaped marks. Today we call this writing cuneiform. It means wedge-shaped.

[long-pause]

Slowly, the signs came to stand for sounds too, not only for things. Then people could write names. And sentences. And letters. And songs. And laws. And stories. Writing could do anything that speech could do. And it could last. A spoken word disappears. A word written in clay can last for thousands of years.

[pause]

{img:scribe-writing-letter-clay-envelope-seal-rolling-courtyard}
At first, only a few people could read and write. Writers who worked for the temples and the palace. But writing changed everything. Kings could send orders far away. Traders could make contracts. Priests could write down prayers. And for the first time, people could talk to the future.

[long-pause]

That is exactly what they did. Hundreds of thousands of their clay tablets have been found. We can read them today. We can hear their voices across five thousand years. A trader complaining about bad copper. A mother writing to her son. A boy who hated school. The Sumerians are talking to us still.

[pause]

There is one more thing about writing on clay. Clay can break. A tablet dropped on the floor can shatter. A tablet left in the rain can melt back into mud. So important tablets are baked hard in ovens. And sometimes fires do the baking by accident. When a library burns, the clay tablets inside do not burn. They bake. And they become as hard as brick. So a fire that destroys a city can save its writing for thousands of years.

[pause]

Writing in clay is different from writing on paper. The scribe holds a soft tablet of damp clay in one hand. With the other hand, he presses a reed pen into the clay. Each press makes a little wedge-shaped mark. Many wedges together make one sign. When the tablet is finished, it is left to dry in the sun. Then it becomes hard, and the writing stays for a very long time. Some tablets have lasted more than four thousand years.

[long-pause]

{img:sumerian-tablet-house-school-boys-benches-clay-tablets-teacher}
The eldest son goes to school. The Sumerians call it the tablet house.

[pause]

It is not a grand building. Usually it is a room in a teacher's house. The boys sit on low benches of brick, or on the floor. Each has a lump of wet clay and a reed pen. The teacher is called the father of the tablet house. And the older pupils who help him are called big brothers.

[long-pause]

School starts at sunrise and ends at sunset. Every day, for years. The boys first learn to make a good tablet. Smooth and flat and the right size. Then they learn to press the wedges. Long ones, short ones, upright ones, slanting ones. Then they learn the signs, one by one. There are hundreds of them.

[pause]

They copy everything. The teacher writes a model on one side of the tablet. The boy copies it on the other side. Again and again. Lists of words. Lists of trees and animals and fish and birds. Lists of gods and cities and jobs. Then proverbs and short stories and hymns. If he makes a mistake, he smooths the clay with his thumb and starts again.

[pause]

{img:pupil-copying-teacher-model-tablet-crooked-wedges-close-hands}
It is hard. And the teachers are strict. We know this because the Sumerians wrote about it. One old story is told by a schoolboy himself. He writes about a terrible day at school. He was late. So he was punished. He talked in class. Punished. He stood up without permission. Punished. His writing was messy. Punished again. He went home very unhappy.

[long-pause]

So he had an idea. He told his father to invite the teacher home for dinner. The father did. He gave the teacher the best seat. He served him a fine meal. He gave him a new robe and a ring. And the teacher, full and happy, suddenly said nice things. Your son is a fine pupil, he said. He will go far.

[pause]

It is a funny story. And very human. Schoolboys and teachers and parents four thousand years ago. Not so different from today. Students copied this story for hundreds of years. Perhaps it made them feel better on bad days.

[pause]

{img:boy-walking-home-from-school-evening-street-tablet-basket-tired}
The eldest son of our family knows the story well. Today he talked in class. His teacher was not pleased. He thinks about telling his father to invite the teacher to dinner. But his father is away at sea. So he will just have to try harder tomorrow.

[pause]

Not every day at school is hard. Sometimes the teacher tells the boys old stories and proverbs. A good word is a friend to everyone, he says. A friend for a day, but a relative forever. The boys copy the proverbs onto their tablets and learn them by heart. At midday, they eat their bread in the courtyard and play games. And at the end of the day, they run home through the streets, glad to be free.

[pause]

The teacher has an assistant, an older student whom the boys call big brother. He checks their tablets and helps them when they make mistakes. He shows them how to hold the reed pen at the right angle. He is patient with the youngest ones and strict with the lazy ones. The eldest son of our family likes him very much. One day, he thinks, I want to be a big brother too.

[long-pause]

{img:barley-fields-sumer-canals-workers-harvesting-sickles-spring}
The most important food in Sumer is barley.

[pause]

Barley is a grain, a bit like wheat. It grows well in the hot dry land, if it gets enough water. And it does not mind a little salt in the soil. That matters here. Because the river water carries tiny bits of salt. Over the years, the salt builds up in the fields. Wheat cannot grow in salty soil. Barley can, at least for a while.

[long-pause]

Barley is everything. People eat it as bread and porridge. They drink it as beer. Workers are paid in barley. Taxes are paid in barley. Prices are counted in barley. If you want to know how rich someone is, you ask how much barley they have.

[pause]

The fields lie in long narrow strips beside the canals. In autumn, the farmers plough them with oxen. Then they sow the barley. In spring, the barley grows green and then golden. In late spring, it is harvested. The workers cut it with curved knives made of clay or copper. They tie it in bundles. They carry it to the threshing floor. Oxen walk round and round on it to shake out the grain. And then the wind blows away the husks.

[pause]

{img:threshing-floor-oxen-walking-circles-barley-grain-wind-chaff}
There is an old Sumerian book of advice for farmers. A father gives instructions to his son. When to open the canals. How deep to plough. How to sow the seed. How to scare away the birds. How to protect the young barley from pests. It is one of the oldest farming books in the world. And the father ends it by saying, these are the teachings of the god of farming.

[long-pause]

The second great food of Sumer is dates. The date palm grows everywhere along the canals. It gives sweet sticky fruit, full of energy. Dates can be eaten fresh or dried. They can be pressed into syrup, sweet like honey. The palm gives wood and leaves and fibre too. Nothing is wasted.

[pause]

{img:date-palm-grove-canal-climber-rope-harvesting-dates-baskets}
Under the palms, in their shade, people grow gardens. Onions and garlic and leeks. Lentils and chickpeas. Cucumbers and lettuce. Apples and figs and pomegranates. A Sumerian garden is like an oasis. Cool and green and full of birds, in the middle of a hot brown land.

[long-pause]

But there is a danger growing slowly in these fields. Every year, a little more salt. Every year, the barley grows a little less well. Farmers notice. They leave some fields to rest. They try to wash the salt away with extra water. For now, the harvests are still good. But over hundreds of years, the salt will win. And the fields of the south will turn white and bare.

[pause]

At harvest time, the whole family helps in the fields. The men cut the barley with sickles of baked clay or copper. The women and children gather it into bundles and tie them. Then the bundles are carried to the threshing floor. Oxen walk round and round over the barley, to shake the grain loose. The grain is tossed into the air, and the wind blows the chaff away. And the clean golden grain falls back down in a soft heap.

[long-pause]

{img:sumerians-drinking-beer-long-straws-big-jar-sitting-together}
The Sumerians love beer. They have dozens of kinds. Light beer and dark beer. Sweet beer and strong beer. Beer with dates and beer with honey.

[pause]

Beer is drunk every day by everyone. Workers get beer as part of their pay. Temples give beer to the gods. Kings drink beer at feasts. It is not very strong. It is thick and cloudy and filling. More like a food than a drink.

[long-pause]

The beer is so thick that bits of grain float on top. So people drink it through long straws. Old pictures show people sitting around a big jar together. Each one has a long reed straw. They all sip from the same jar and talk. Rich people have straws with metal tips that act like filters. One beautiful straw of gold and blue stone was found in a royal tomb at Ur.

[pause]

There is even a goddess of beer. Her name is Ninkasi. And there is a song for her. It is a hymn of praise. But it is also a recipe. It tells how to make beer, step by step. Soaking the barley. Baking the beer bread. Mixing it with water and sweet dates. Pouring it into the big jar. Letting it bubble.

[pause]

{img:women-brewers-ninkasi-hymn-big-jars-mixing-bread-dates-courtyard}
The song says, you are the one who pours out the filtered beer. It is like the flow of the two great rivers. People sang it while they brewed. That way they remembered every step. It is one of the oldest recipes in the world. In our own time, brewers have tried to make beer using the song. It turned out quite nice. A little like cider.

[long-pause]

Brewing is often women's work. Many brewers and tavern keepers in Sumer are women. They make the beer and sell it from their houses. People gather there in the evening. They drink and talk and laugh. The tavern is a place to hear the news. And to meet friends.

[pause]

{img:tavern-keeper-woman-evening-lamps-customers-straws-ur-house}
There is an old Sumerian proverb. It says, when you do not drink beer, you do not know what is good. Another one is less happy. It says, beer is good, but the road home is long. The Sumerians knew both sides.

[long-pause]

In our family, the mother brews beer once a week. The kitchen smells of baked bread and sweet dates for days. When the beer is ready, the grandmother tastes it first. She sips through her straw and thinks for a moment. Good, she says. A little sweeter than last week. Your husband will like it. And the mother smiles. And she hopes he comes home soon to taste it.

[pause]

The mother has her own way of making beer, which she learned from her own mother. She keeps it secret, even from the neighbours. She bakes special loaves of bread and crumbles them into a large jar. She adds water and a little date syrup for sweetness. Then she covers the jar and waits for a few days. The daughter is allowed to help stir it. Her mother says that one day she will teach her the secret too.

[long-pause]

{img:sumerian-solid-wooden-wheel-cart-onagers-pulling-road-dust}
The Sumerians did not only invent writing. They made many other things the world still uses.

[pause]

One of the most famous is the wheel. Some of the oldest wheels in the world were made in this land. The first wheels were solid. They were made of three planks of wood, joined together and cut into a circle. They were heavy and slow. But they worked.

[long-pause]

At first, wheels were probably used by potters. A potter's wheel is a flat round disc that spins. You put a lump of clay in the middle and spin it. Then you shape it with your hands. It makes round pots quickly and evenly. Before that, every pot was built slowly by hand.

[pause]

Then someone thought of putting wheels on a cart. Now heavy loads could be pulled over land. Carts and wagons were pulled by oxen or by wild donkeys. Later, kings rode in chariots in their parades. Wheels changed the way people moved things for ever.

[pause]

{img:potter-spinning-wheel-shaping-jar-workshop-ur-hands-clay}
The Sumerians also improved the plough. Ploughs are tools that cut and turn the soil before planting. The Sumerians made a clever plough with a funnel on top. As the ox pulls the plough, the farmer drops seeds into the funnel. The seeds fall down a tube right into the new furrow. Ploughing and sowing in one go. It saved a lot of time and seed.

[long-pause]

They also built sailboats. They made bronze, by mixing copper with tin. Bronze is much harder than copper alone. They made bricks baked in ovens, strong and waterproof. They used tar from the ground to glue and seal things. They made glass, or something close to it. They made the first maps. They counted in sixties and divided the circle into parts. Every time you look at a clock with sixty minutes, you are using an idea from Sumer.

[pause]

{img:sumerian-seed-plough-funnel-ox-farmer-dropping-seeds-field}
Nobody knows the names of the people who invented these things. They were potters and farmers and builders and boatmen. Ordinary clever people trying to make their work a little easier. But their ideas spread to the whole world. And we still use them every single day.

[long-pause]

In our family's house, the grandmother has an old spinning wheel for clay. Not for wool. Her father was a potter. When she was a girl, she sat beside him and watched him spin pots. Bowls and jars and cups, round and smooth. She tried it herself. Her first pot was crooked and lumpy. Her father laughed and kept it anyway. It still stands on a shelf in her room. She shows it to her grandchildren sometimes. Every potter starts with a crooked pot, she tells them.

[pause]

On the farms around Ur, the farmers start ploughing in the autumn, when the heat is over. The oxen walk slowly in front of the plough, their heads low. The farmer walks behind, holding the handles steady. His son walks beside the oxen, talking to them gently. The soil turns over in long dark lines behind them. Birds follow the plough, looking for worms. And by evening, a whole field is ready for the seed.

[pause]

Some of the first wheels in the world were made in this part of the world. They were solid wheels, made from planks of wood fixed together. They were heavy and slow, but they were very useful. Carts with wheels could carry much more than a donkey. Potters also used a kind of wheel, spinning it to shape their pots. It was a simple idea, but it changed the world forever.

[long-pause]

{img:sumerian-sailing-ship-gulf-sea-traders-copper-bales-wind}
Sumer has rich soil and plenty of barley and wool. But it has almost nothing else. No stone. No good wood. No metal. No precious stones. So the Sumerians must trade for everything.

[pause]

And many of their traders go by sea.

[long-pause]

From the harbours of Ur, ships sail down the river to the sea. Then they sail south along the coast of the Gulf. Their first big stop is an island called Dilmun. Today it is called Bahrain. Dilmun is a green island in the sea. It has fresh water springs bubbling up from the ground. Palm trees and gardens. The Sumerians think of it as a kind of paradise. In their old stories, Dilmun is a pure land where nobody grows old and no lion hunts.

[pause]

Dilmun is also a great trading place. Ships from many lands meet there. Copper comes there from the mountains of Oman, further south. Stone and timber and ivory and pearls come too. The traders of Ur bring barley and wool and cloth and oil. And they swap them for copper.

[pause]

{img:dilmun-island-fresh-springs-palms-harbour-ships-trading-goods}
This trade is risky. The sea can be rough. Ships can sink. Pirates can attack. Traders borrow money to buy their goods. If the ship is lost, they lose everything. So traders pray to the gods before every voyage. And their families wait anxiously at home.

[long-pause]

We know about one trader from Ur who lived a little later than our story. His name was Ea-nasir. He sold copper. And he was famous for one reason. His customers complained. A lot. One customer wrote him an angry letter on a clay tablet. You promised me good copper, he wrote. But you gave me bad copper. You treated my messenger rudely. Who do you think I am? Give me back my money!

[pause]

{img:angry-clay-tablet-letter-complaint-copper-trader-ea-nasir-museum}
This letter has survived for nearly four thousand years. It is now in a museum in London. People call it the oldest customer complaint in the world. And Ea-nasir kept all these angry letters in his house. Maybe he thought he would deal with them later.

[long-pause]

In our family, the father has gone to Dilmun. He took wool and cloth on a big boat with other traders. He should have been back weeks ago. Every evening, the mother goes up on the roof and looks toward the harbour. She sees the masts of ships. But not his.

[pause]

On the long journey to Dilmun, the sailors live on the deck of their ship. They sleep under the stars on mats of woven reed. They eat dates and dried fish and bread, and they drink water from big clay jars. In the evenings, they sit together and sing songs about the sea and their homes. The father of our family sits among them. He looks at the stars and thinks about his wife and children far away in Ur. And he counts the days until he can go home.

[long-pause]

{img:indus-valley-ship-arriving-ur-harbour-strange-cargo-carnelian-beads}
Far beyond Dilmun, much further to the east, there is a land the Sumerians call Meluhha.

[pause]

Meluhha is far away across the sea. Today we think it was the land along the river Indus, in what is now Pakistan and western India. Great cities stood there at the same time as Sumer. Cities with straight streets and brick houses and covered drains. People there had their own writing. But nobody today can read it.

[long-pause]

Ships from Meluhha sometimes come all the way to the harbours of Sumer. They bring wonderful things. Beads of a deep orange-red stone called carnelian. Some are long and thin and have white patterns burned into them. Ivory and fine wood. Gold. And peacocks and monkeys and dogs, strange animals the Sumerians have never seen.

[pause]

In the royal tombs of Ur, archaeologists found beads that must have come from Meluhha. Long red beads with white lines. The same kind of beads have been found in the cities of the Indus. Proof that the two lands traded with each other, across thousands of kilometres of sea.

[pause]

{img:carnelian-beads-etched-white-lines-necklace-ur-tomb-close-light}
There were even people from Meluhha living in Sumer. A writer from Ur mentions a village of Meluhha people near the city. Perhaps traders who settled there. One clay seal from Sumer belonged to a man whose job was translator for the language of Meluhha. Imagine that. Four thousand years ago, people were already learning foreign languages to do business.

[long-pause]

From the east, over the mountains, comes another treasure. A deep blue stone called lapis lazuli. It comes from far away mountains in what is now Afghanistan. It travels for months by donkey and by boat. The Sumerians love it more than any other stone. It is the colour of the night sky with little flecks of gold, like stars. They use it in jewellery and statues and the eyes of gods.

[pause]

{img:lapis-lazuli-blue-stone-gold-flecks-caravan-mountains-donkeys}
So Ur is connected to the whole known world. Ships and caravans come and go. Goods and ideas and people travel with them. A woman in Ur might wear a necklace with stone from Afghanistan and beads from India and gold from the mountains. She has never seen any of those places. But they have come to her.

[long-pause]

And one evening, from the roof, the mother of our family sees a ship coming up the river toward the harbour. It is low in the water, heavy with cargo. She stares at it. Is it him? She calls the children. They run to the edge of the roof and look. The ship comes closer. And the daughter shouts. That is father's ship! I can see the red cloth on the mast!

[pause]

The traders of Meluhha bring wonderful things to the markets of Sumer. There are beads of red carnelian, polished until they shine like little drops of fire. There are pieces of ivory and fine wood. There are little stone seals, carved with pictures of animals. Sometimes, people say, they even bring monkeys and colourful birds. The children of Ur crowd around to look. They have never seen such things before. And they wonder about the faraway land where they come from.

[pause]

Meluhha was probably the land we now call the Indus Valley, far to the east. Its people built great cities with straight streets and covered drains. They had their own writing, which nobody can read today. Their ships sailed across the sea to trade with Sumer and Dilmun. So, more than four thousand years ago, people from faraway lands were already meeting and trading. They shared goods and ideas, and perhaps a few stories too.

[long-pause]

{img:ship-docking-ur-harbour-family-running-father-stepping-ashore}
The whole family runs down to the harbour. The grandmother cannot run, so she walks as fast as she can. The ship is tying up at the quay. Men are shouting and throwing ropes. And there, at the front of the ship, is the father. He is thinner and his face is brown from the sun. But he is smiling.

[pause]

He steps off the ship. The children throw their arms around him. The mother holds his hands and does not say anything for a long time. The grandmother arrives last, out of breath. She touches his face. You are late, she says. I know, he says. There was a storm. We waited on Dilmun for two weeks. But I am home.

[long-pause]

He has brought copper, good copper this time. And a necklace of red beads for his wife. A little ivory comb for his daughter. Dates from Dilmun, the sweetest in the world, for the grandmother. And for the boys, a clay whistle shaped like a bird. That evening, the family eats on the roof. The father tells them about the sea and the island and the springs. And the mother brings out her new beer. He sips it through his straw. It is good, he says. The best I have had in months.

[pause]

{img:temple-weaving-workshop-ur-many-women-looms-wool-bundles-overseer}
Some of the wool the father traded came from the great temple workshops of Ur. Let us visit one.

[long-pause]

The temples and the palace of Ur own huge flocks of sheep. Tens of thousands of them. Their wool is brought to big workshops in the city. And there, hundreds of women and girls work at spinning and weaving. Some workshops have more than a thousand weavers.

[pause]

The work is carefully organised. Some women wash the wool. Some comb it. Some spin it into thread. Some weave it into cloth on big looms. Overseers check the work. Scribes write down how much wool comes in and how much cloth goes out. Every worker gets a ration of barley and oil and wool every month.

[pause]

{img:spinners-weavers-ur-workshop-children-helping-wool-baskets-light}
The cloth is of many kinds. Fine soft cloth for the gods and the king. Heavy rough cloth for workers. Cloth in natural colours, white and brown and grey. And cloth dyed in bright colours. Red and blue and yellow. It is sold all over Sumer. And it is carried by ships to far lands, to be traded for copper and stone.

[long-pause]

The weavers' lives are not easy. The days are long. The pay is small. Some of the workers are widows or orphans with nowhere else to go. Some are women taken from other lands. But the workshops give them food and a place. Mothers bring their small children with them. The children play between the looms. And when they are old enough, they learn to spin too.

[pause]

That night, the children cannot sleep for a long time. They lie on the roof and whisper about the presents. The boys blow softly on their clay bird whistle, until their mother tells them to hush. The daughter runs the little ivory comb through her hair again and again. The grandmother eats one more sweet date from Dilmun, very slowly. And the father and mother sit together by the lamp, talking quietly until very late. The whole family is together again at last.

[long-pause]

{img:goldsmith-workshop-ur-gold-leaf-lapis-beads-craftsman-careful}
Ur is full of skilled craftsmen. And some of the most beautiful things in the ancient world were made here.

[pause]

In the workshops of the goldsmiths, men work with thin sheets of gold. They hammer the gold until it is as thin as a leaf. They cut it into shapes. Leaves and flowers and stars. They make headdresses of golden leaves for the great ladies. They make cups and bowls and rings. They combine gold with blue lapis and red carnelian, so it shines in three colours.

[long-pause]

The stone workers carve seals. A seal is a small stone cylinder, about the size of your finger. Tiny pictures are carved into it. Gods and animals and heroes and people. Every important person has a seal. To sign a tablet, you roll your seal across the wet clay. It leaves a little picture, like a signature. Nobody else has a seal quite like yours.

[pause]

{img:cylinder-seal-rolling-wet-clay-tiny-scene-impression-close}
Seal carvers work with tiny tools and great patience. The pictures are so small, some of them only a few centimetres long. But they are full of detail. A hero fighting a lion. A row of goats walking past a tree. A god on a throne with worshippers before him. Each one is like a tiny work of art.

[long-pause]

There are carpenters and boat builders. There are potters at their spinning wheels. There are metal workers who melt copper and tin together to make bronze. They pour it into clay moulds to make tools and bowls and statues. There are reed weavers and leather workers and perfume makers.

[pause]

One famous object from Ur shows the life of the city. It is a wooden box about the size of a shoebox, covered with little pictures. The pictures are made of shell and red stone and blue lapis, set in black tar. On one side there is a great feast. The king sits drinking. A musician plays a lyre. Servants bring animals and fish and bundles of goods. People call it the Standard of Ur. Nobody knows what it was for. But it is beautiful.

[pause]

{img:standard-of-ur-mosaic-feast-side-lapis-shell-rows-figures-museum}
The craftsmen of Ur pass their skills from father to son. And from mother to daughter. A boy might start as a helper at seven or eight. He sweeps the floor and fetches water. He watches. Slowly he is allowed to try simple things. After many years, he becomes a master himself. And one day, he will teach his own son.

[pause]

In the workshops of Ur, there are many skilled craftsmen. There are metalworkers who make tools and bowls of copper and bronze. There are jewellers who string beads of gold and blue lapis lazuli. There are carpenters who make chairs and chests and boats. There are weavers who make fine cloth on large looms. Many of them work in open doorways, so people in the street can watch them. And children often stop to stare, fascinated by their quick and clever hands.

[long-pause]

{img:enheduanna-high-priestess-ur-disc-procession-flounced-robe-headdress}
Here is a question. Who was the first writer in history whose name we know?

[pause]

Not a king, boasting about himself. A real writer. Someone who wrote poems and signed them with their own name. The answer is a woman. And she lived at Ur.

[long-pause]

Her name was Enheduanna. She lived about two hundred years before our story. She was the daughter of a great king called Sargon, who ruled much of this land from the north. Sargon made his daughter the high priestess of the moon god at Ur. It was one of the most important jobs in the whole country. She lived in a special house near the temple. She led the ceremonies. She looked after the temple and its people for many years.

[pause]

And she wrote. She wrote hymns to the gods. Long beautiful poems of praise. She wrote a set of forty-two hymns to the temples of Sumer, one for each great temple. And at the end of them, she wrote her own name. The person who put this tablet together is Enheduanna, she wrote. No one has made anything like this before.

[pause]

{img:enheduanna-writing-hymn-tablet-temple-room-lamp-night-thinking}
She also wrote poems about her own life. In one, she tells of a hard time. A rebel took power in Ur. She was forced out of the temple. She had to leave her home. She was afraid and alone. And she prayed to the goddess Inanna for help. In the poem, she writes about her fear and her sadness. And then about her hope. And in the end, the poem says, the goddess heard her. And she was able to return.

[long-pause]

It is one of the first times in history that a person writes about their own feelings. Not about a king's victories. About fear and hope and faith. And it was a woman who did it.

[pause]

We even know what she looked like. In 1927, archaeologists at Ur found a round disc of white stone. On it is a carved picture of a procession. And in the middle walks a woman in a long robe with many layers. She wears a special headdress. Next to her, writing names her. It is Enheduanna.

[pause]

{img:alabaster-disc-enheduanna-carved-procession-museum-soft-light}
After she passed away, her poems were copied by schoolboys for hundreds of years. Students in the tablet houses learned them by heart. Long after the kings of her time were forgotten, her words were still being read. And today, more than four thousand years later, they are being read again. Enheduanna was right. Nobody had made anything like it before.

[pause]

Enheduanna lived in a fine house beside the temple, with gardens and fountains. She had many servants and priestesses to help her. But she also liked quiet times alone. In the evenings, she would sit in her garden and look at the moon. She would think about the words for her next song. Then she would call a scribe and speak the words slowly, while he wrote them down in clay. Her songs were sung in the temples for hundreds of years.

[pause]

Enheduanna is the first writer in history whose name we know. She lived more than four thousand years ago, as the high priestess of the moon god at Ur. She was the daughter of a great king. Her songs and poems were copied by students for hundreds of years. Some of them are still read today, in many languages. They speak of the moon and the stars, of joy and sorrow, and of the gods she loved.

[long-pause]

{img:gilgamesh-king-uruk-strong-bearded-city-walls-dawn-epic}
The Sumerians told many stories. The most famous of all is about a king called Gilgamesh.

[pause]

Gilgamesh was the king of Uruk, the great city not far from Ur. He may have been a real king who lived long, long ago. But over the centuries, the stories about him grew and grew. Until he became the greatest hero of ancient times.

[long-pause]

In the stories, Gilgamesh is strong and proud. Too proud. He is part god and part man. He builds the great walls of Uruk. But he is also a bully. He makes his people work too hard. So the people pray to the gods for help. And the gods make a wild man to be his equal. His name is Enkidu.

[pause]

Enkidu lives in the wilderness with the animals. He runs with the gazelles and drinks at the water holes. He is as strong as Gilgamesh. When the two of them finally meet, they fight. They wrestle in the street of Uruk. The walls shake. Doors break. Neither one can win. And at last, both stop. And they look at each other. And they laugh. And from that day, they are best friends.

[pause]

{img:gilgamesh-enkidu-wrestling-then-embracing-uruk-street-friends}
Together they go on great adventures. They travel to a faraway forest of huge cedar trees, guarded by a giant. They face him together and win. They return home as heroes.

[long-pause]

But then sorrow comes. Enkidu falls ill. Gilgamesh sits beside him day and night. But he cannot help. Enkidu passes away. And Gilgamesh is heartbroken. He weeps for his friend for seven days. And for the first time, he is afraid. Afraid that one day, he too will die.

[pause]

So he sets out on a long journey. He wants to find the secret of living forever. He crosses mountains and deserts. He walks through a long dark tunnel under a mountain. He crosses the waters of death. He is looking for one man. A man who, the stories say, was given everlasting life by the gods. A man who survived the great flood.

[pause]

{img:gilgamesh-alone-crossing-dark-waters-boat-poles-mist-journey}
What he learns from that man is another story. The story of the flood.

[long-pause]

The story of Gilgamesh was told and retold for two thousand years. In Sumerian and later in other languages. It was written on clay tablets and copied in schools. Then it was forgotten. Until about a hundred and fifty years ago, when people found the tablets again and learned to read them. Today it is called the oldest great story in the world. And it is still read all over the world. Because its questions are still our questions. Friendship. Loss. And how to live, knowing that life does not last forever.

[pause]

At the end of his long journey, Gilgamesh comes home to Uruk. He has not found a way to live forever. But he looks at his city. At its walls and its gardens and its people. And he understands. This is what lasts. What we build. And the people we love.

[pause]

The story of Gilgamesh was told and retold for many centuries. In the evenings, storytellers would sit by the fire and tell it to a circle of listeners. They told of his friendship with Enkidu, and of their long journeys together. They told of his search for the secret of living forever. The listeners would lean forward and hold their breath at the exciting parts. And at the end, they would nod quietly, thinking about the lesson of the story.

[long-pause]

{img:ancient-flood-rising-waters-plain-reed-boat-large-storm-clouds}
At the end of his journey, Gilgamesh finds the man he is looking for. His name is Utnapishtim. And he tells Gilgamesh a story. The story of the great flood.

[pause]

Long ago, Utnapishtim says, the gods decided to send a flood over the whole earth. People had become too many and too noisy. The gods could not sleep. So they would wash the world clean with water.

[long-pause]

But one god loved people. He was Ea, the god of the sweet waters and of wisdom. He did not want everyone to be lost. But he had promised to keep the plan secret. So he found a clever way. He went to the reed house where Utnapishtim lived. And he did not speak to Utnapishtim. He spoke to the wall of the house. Reed wall, listen. Build a boat. Leave your things behind. Save life.

[pause]

Utnapishtim heard it through the wall. And he understood. He built a great boat. Huge and square, with many decks and rooms. He covered it with tar to keep the water out. He loaded it with food and gold and silver. He brought his family on board. And the craftsmen who had helped him. And animals of every kind, wild and tame.

[pause]

{img:huge-boat-being-built-tar-workers-animals-waiting-reed-houses}
Then the storm came. Black clouds rose from the horizon. Thunder roared. The rain fell and fell. For six days and seven nights the wind blew and the water rose. It covered the fields and the cities and the hills. Even the gods were frightened. They crouched like dogs against the walls of heaven.

[long-pause]

On the seventh day, the storm grew calm. The sea was still. Utnapishtim opened a window. Light fell on his face. Everywhere there was water. And he sat down and wept.

[pause]

The boat came to rest on top of a mountain. Utnapishtim waited seven more days. Then he sent out a dove. The dove flew around and came back. There was nowhere to land. He sent out a swallow. It came back too. Then he sent out a raven. The raven did not come back. It had found dry land. So Utnapishtim opened the boat. And everyone came out into the new world.

[pause]

{img:dove-flying-from-boat-mountain-top-calm-sea-sunrise-hope}
Does this story sound familiar? It is very like the story of Noah in the Bible. A great flood. A man who is warned. A boat full of animals. Birds sent out to find land. When people first read the Sumerian story, about a hundred and fifty years ago, they were amazed. The story was much older than anyone had thought.

[long-pause]

Why did the people of Sumer tell stories about a great flood? Maybe because floods were real here. The rivers flood every spring. Sometimes they flood very badly. Whole towns are lost. Archaeologists digging at Ur found a thick layer of clean mud in the ground. No pots, no bricks, nothing. Just river mud, laid down by a great flood long ago. People who lived here knew water could be wonderful. And terrible.

[pause]

{img:archaeologists-pit-ur-thick-clean-mud-layer-flood-1929-ladders}
So they told the story. And at the end, the gods were sorry for what they had done. They promised it would not happen again. And Utnapishtim and his wife were given life without end, far away at the mouth of the rivers.

[pause]

Floods were a real part of life in Sumer. The rivers could rise suddenly in the spring, when the snow melted far away in the mountains. Then the water spread over the fields and sometimes reached the villages. People built their houses on small hills, to keep them dry. They built strong banks along the rivers and canals. And when the water went down again, they cleaned their houses, planted their fields, and began again.

[pause]

The old story of the great flood was told in many lands for thousands of years. In the Sumerian version, a wise man is warned by a kind god. He builds a great boat and fills it with his family and with animals of every kind. The rain falls for many days, and the water covers the land. When the rain stops, he sends out a bird to look for dry land. And at last, the boat comes to rest, and life begins again.

[long-pause]

{img:lyre-of-ur-golden-bull-head-lapis-beard-wooden-frame-strings}
The Sumerians love music. Music is played in the temples and the palace. At feasts and funerals. In the fields and in the taverns. And some of the oldest musical instruments in the world come from Ur.

[pause]

The most famous are the lyres. A lyre is a stringed instrument. It has a wooden sound box at the bottom. Two arms rise up from it. A bar joins the arms at the top. And strings run from the box up to the bar. You pluck the strings with your fingers.

[long-pause]

The lyres of Ur are works of art. On the front of the sound box, there is often the head of a bull. Made of gold, with a beard of blue lapis. Its eyes are shell and stone. It looks out at you, calm and noble. Below the head, there are little pictures of animals, set in shell. A dog carrying a table. A lion carrying a jar. A donkey playing a lyre. A bear dancing. They look like a funny story, but we do not know what story it is.

[pause]

Some lyres are big. As big as a person. The musician sits or stands next to it to play. Others are smaller and can be held in the arms.

[pause]

{img:musician-playing-big-lyre-temple-courtyard-singers-listening}
The lyres were found in the royal tombs of Ur. They were made of wood, and after four and a half thousand years the wood had rotted away. But the archaeologists were clever. They poured plaster into the holes left in the earth. When the plaster set, they had the shape of the lyre. Then they rebuilt it. Today you can see them in museums. And some musicians have made copies that really play.

[long-pause]

What did Sumerian music sound like? Nobody knows exactly. But we have some clues. A clay tablet from a nearby land, a little later, has the oldest written music ever found. It is a hymn to a goddess. It has words, and special signs that tell the player which strings to pluck. Scholars have tried to play it. It sounds soft and slow and a little sad.

[pause]

{img:harpist-and-singer-evening-palace-garden-lamps-soft-music}
There are other instruments too. Drums and flutes and pipes. Harps. Little rattles and clappers. Bells. At temple ceremonies, big drums boom and singers chant hymns to the gods. At feasts in the palace, lyres and harps play softly. Singers sing of the gods and heroes and the beauty of the city.

[long-pause]

In our family, the grandmother can play a small lyre. She learned it when she was a girl. On warm evenings, she takes it out on the roof. She plucks the strings gently. The notes float out over the dark rooftops. The father, home again, closes his eyes and listens. The children lie on their backs and look at the stars. And for a little while, nobody speaks.

[pause]

In the evenings, music can be heard all over Ur. From the temples comes the sound of harps and drums and singing. From the houses comes the sound of simple flutes and clapping hands. On the roof of our family's house, the father sometimes plays a small lyre that he brought back from his travels. He is not very good at it. But the children love to listen, and the grandmother sings along softly. And the music drifts out over the roofs into the warm night.

[long-pause]

{img:royal-game-of-ur-board-squares-shell-rosettes-pieces-dice}
After the music, the father and his eldest son play a game. It is the favourite game of Sumer. Today we call it the Royal Game of Ur.

[pause]

The board is shaped a little like a long box with a narrow middle. It has twenty squares. Some are decorated with flowers called rosettes. Each player has seven pieces. Round flat counters, white for one player and black for the other. And instead of dice with dots, they throw little pyramid-shaped dice. Each one has two corners marked. You throw four of them and count how many marked corners point up.

[long-pause]

The goal is to race all your pieces around the board and off the end. Before your opponent does. Your pieces go in at one side and come out at the other. In the middle, both players share the same path. And if you land on your opponent's piece there, you knock it off. It has to start all over again.

[pause]

The rosette squares are special. If you land on one, you are safe. Nobody can knock you off. And you get another turn. So everyone wants to reach the rosettes. And the middle rosette is the most important square of all.

[pause]

{img:father-son-playing-game-of-ur-rooftop-lamplight-evening-laughing}
It is a game of luck. But also of thinking. Which piece do you move? Do you risk it? Do you wait? It is exciting. One lucky throw can change everything. Players groan and cheer and laugh.

[long-pause]

How do we know the rules? For a long time, nobody knew. Beautiful boards were found in the royal tombs of Ur. But no rulebook. Then, in a museum in London, an expert found a clay tablet. It had been written about two thousand years after our story, by a Babylonian writer. And it explained how to play. So the game was still being played then. For thousands of years.

[pause]

{img:game-board-carved-on-gate-stone-lamassu-guards-later-graffiti}
People played it everywhere. Guards at a palace gate in the north scratched a game board into the stone they stood on. To pass the time on long boring watches. Boards have been found from Egypt to India. It was the most popular game of the ancient world.

[long-pause]

Tonight, the father is winning. He has three pieces off the board already. His son has only one. Then the boy throws. Four marked corners. The best throw there is. He moves his piece onto the middle rosette. He throws again. He knocks his father's piece off the board. The father groans. The son grins. And the grandmother laughs so hard she has to put down her lyre.

[pause]

In our family, the eldest son and his father play the Game of Ur in the evenings. They sit on the roof with the board between them, and the lamp beside them. The daughter watches and cheers for her brother. The younger son tries to help by moving the pieces when nobody is looking. Everyone laughs when he is caught. Sometimes the father wins, and sometimes the son wins. And sometimes, the grandmother plays and beats them both.

[pause]

The Game of Ur was played all over the ancient world for thousands of years. Boards have been found in many places, carved in wood and stone and clay. Some were simple, scratched into a step or a wall. Others were beautiful, decorated with shells and red stone and blue lapis. Today, people still play it, using the rules found on an old clay tablet. And it is still great fun.

[long-pause]

{img:law-tablet-ur-nammu-clay-cuneiform-columns-museum-light}
Shulgi's father, King Ur-Nammu, did something important. He wrote down laws.

[pause]

It is the oldest written set of laws we have found. It is about three hundred years older than the more famous laws of a later king called Hammurabi. Pieces of it were found on clay tablets. Not all of it has survived. But enough to understand what kind of laws they were.

[long-pause]

At the beginning, the king explains why he made the laws. He says he did it so that the orphan would not be handed over to the rich man. So that the widow would not be handed over to the powerful man. So that the man with one sheep would not be handed over to the man with a hundred sheep. He wanted fairness. He wanted the weak to be protected.

[pause]

And then come the laws themselves. If someone does this, then that happens. They are short and clear. Many of them are about money. If you break someone's bone, you pay them a fixed amount of silver. If you cut off someone's nose, you pay more. If you steal, you pay back. If you lie in court, you pay a fine.

[pause]

{img:judges-sitting-temple-gate-ur-hearing-case-witnesses-scribe}
This was quite gentle for its time. Many later laws in other lands were much harsher. An eye for an eye. But in Ur-Nammu's laws, most wrongs could be made right with silver. The idea was to fix the harm. Not just to punish.

[long-pause]

There are judges in Ur. They often sit at the gates of the temple. People come to them with their quarrels. About land and debts and marriages and stolen sheep. The judges listen to both sides. They hear witnesses. They look at the contracts on clay tablets. And they decide. A scribe writes everything down.

[pause]

{img:two-neighbours-arguing-before-judges-clay-contract-held-up-calm}
Sometimes, when nobody can prove who is right, the judges ask someone to swear an oath before the god. That is very serious. People believe the god will punish a lie. So many people would rather settle the quarrel than swear falsely. Often, at the last moment, someone gives in.

[long-pause]

Most problems are settled without judges. Families and neighbours talk things out. An old man of the street might help. Everyone meets in a courtyard in the evening. They argue. They drink a little beer. And in the end, they usually agree. And then they go home to sleep.

[pause]

There is a famous Sumerian saying about this. It says, a friendly word is a friend to everyone. Another says, do not quarrel with your neighbour, for tomorrow you may need his help. The people of Ur lived close together. They knew that getting along was worth more than winning.

[pause]

In the city, there are judges who listen to quarrels and decide what is fair. They sit at the city gate or in a room near the temple. People come to them with all kinds of problems. One man says his neighbour's goat ate his barley. Another says a trader did not pay him enough. The judges listen carefully to both sides. Then they decide, and a scribe writes the decision on a clay tablet. That way, nobody can forget what was agreed.

[long-pause]

{img:festival-boat-carrying-moon-god-statue-canal-ur-to-nippur-crowds}
And now the day of the great festival has come. The moon god Nanna will travel to Nippur.

[pause]

Nippur is the holy city of Sumer. It is where Enlil lives, the father of the gods. Every year, the gods of the other cities travel to Nippur to visit him. They bring gifts. They ask for his blessing. And Nanna of Ur makes the journey too. By boat.

[long-pause]

For weeks, Ur has been getting ready. The god's boat has been repaired and painted. It is decorated with gold and silver and blue stone. Its prow is high and curved. Gifts are loaded on board. Fine cloth and butter and cheese and fish. Lambs and goats. Jars of oil and beer. Baskets of dates. All the best things of Ur, to give to the father of the gods.

[pause]

On the day of the journey, the whole city comes out. The priests carry the statue of Nanna out of the temple. He is dressed in his finest robe. Music plays. Drums beat. People throw flowers. The statue is placed carefully in the boat. Will it go well? Every year, everyone worries a little. If the god's journey is troubled, it could be a bad sign for the year.

[pause]

{img:priests-carrying-dressed-statue-to-boat-music-drums-flowers}
Then the boat pushes off. Rowers pull at the oars. The boat moves slowly up the canal, out of the city. People follow along the banks, singing and waving. Children run beside it. For a long way, the crowds walk with their god. Then they stop and watch the boat grow smaller and smaller. Until it disappears among the palm trees.

[long-pause]

The journey takes several days. A Sumerian poem tells about it. At each city along the way, the boat stops. The people of that city come out to greet the moon god. They offer gifts. And the god blesses them. Then he sails on.

[pause]

At last he reaches Nippur. The father of the gods welcomes him. There is a great feast. Nanna gives his gifts. And Enlil gives him something in return. A blessing for Ur. Good harvests and fat sheep and full rivers. Long life for the king.

[pause]

{img:moon-god-boat-returning-ur-crowds-joy-evening-lanterns-banks}
Then the boat comes home. And the people of Ur are waiting. They see it coming up the canal in the evening. They cheer and run to meet it. The god is back. He has brought the blessing. The year will be good. And that night there is feasting in every street of Ur, under the shining moon.

[pause]

During the festival, the streets of Ur are full of people in their best clothes. Women wear necklaces of coloured beads and long woollen dresses. Men wear fringed skirts and carry walking sticks. Children have flowers tucked behind their ears. There is music on every corner, and the smell of roasting meat everywhere. Our family walks together through the crowds, holding hands so nobody gets lost. And the little one sits on his father's shoulders, to see over everyone's heads.

[pause]

When the festival is over, the family walks home slowly through the quiet streets. The lamps are going out one by one. The little one has fallen asleep on his father's shoulder. The daughter yawns and leans against her mother. The grandmother walks slowly, holding the eldest son's arm. At home, they climb up to the roof and lie down on their mats. And above them, the moon of Nanna shines down on the sleeping city.

[long-pause]

{img:leonard-woolley-excavation-ur-1920s-workers-baskets-mound-trenches}
How do we know so much about Ur? Because about a hundred years ago, someone dug it up.

[pause]

His name was Leonard Woolley. He was a British archaeologist. In 1922, he came to a big mound of earth in the desert of southern Iraq. Local people called it the mound of tar. Woolley believed the old city of Ur lay underneath. He was right.

[long-pause]

For twelve years, Woolley dug at Ur. Hundreds of local workers helped him. They carried away the earth in baskets. Layer by layer, they uncovered the city. The walls and the streets. The houses and the temples. The great ziggurat. They found thousands of clay tablets. And then, in one corner of the city, they found the royal tombs.

[pause]

These tombs were older than our story. Much older. About four hundred years. They belonged to kings and queens of an earlier time. And they were full of treasures. Gold and silver and blue stone. Headdresses and cups and lyres and games. The most dazzling things ever found from the ancient world, until then.

[pause]

{img:queen-puabi-headdress-gold-leaves-flowers-lapis-carnelian-museum}
One tomb belonged to a queen called Puabi. Her name was on her seal. She was buried in a beautiful headdress made of golden leaves and flowers. Long golden ribbons. Huge golden earrings. Necklaces of gold and lapis and carnelian. A cape made of beads. When Woolley's team found her, the beads and gold were lying just as they had been placed, more than four thousand years earlier.

[long-pause]

Woolley worked very carefully for his time. He drew and photographed everything. He knew the finds were precious. Sometimes he let a tomb lie untouched for years, until he had trained his workers well enough to dig it properly. He was patient.

[pause]

His wife Katharine helped him. She drew the finds and helped rebuild them. One of her jobs was to rebuild Puabi's headdress. She studied every golden leaf and bead. And she fitted them back together on a model head. Today you can see it in a museum in America. It shines as brightly as ever.

[pause]

{img:katharine-woolley-drawing-finds-dig-house-lamp-table-careful}
Woolley also wrote books about Ur for ordinary people. They were exciting and easy to read. Millions of people around the world learned about the Sumerians from him. Before Woolley, almost nobody had heard of them. After Woolley, everyone knew the name of Ur.

[long-pause]

One visitor to the dig became one of the most famous writers in the world. Her name was Agatha Christie. She met one of Woolley's young assistants there. They fell in love and married. She wrote one of her mystery novels while staying near the ruins. The desert and the mounds and the old secrets inspired her stories.

[pause]

Among the treasures found in the royal graves, there are beautiful everyday things too. There are combs and hairpins of gold, and little boxes for makeup. There are cups and bowls for drinking, and game boards for playing. There are lyres, decorated with the heads of bulls made of gold. These things show us that the people of Ur loved beauty and music and games. Just like people today, they enjoyed the small pleasures of life.

[long-pause]

{img:ur-autumn-palms-quiet-ziggurat-distant-end-of-era-dusk}
Shulgi ruled Ur for almost fifty years. His kingdom was rich and strong. But nothing lasts forever.

[pause]

After Shulgi, his sons ruled. At first, all was well. Then troubles began. Harvests failed. Wandering peoples came from the west and the north. The great kingdom started to break apart. Cities stopped obeying Ur. And about a hundred years after our story, an army from the mountains in the east came down to Ur.

[long-pause]

The city was taken. The temples were damaged. The statue of the moon god was carried away. We will leave those sad days there, gently, in the past.

[pause]

The people of Sumer wrote a long poem about it. It is called the Lament for Ur. A lament is a song of sadness. In it, the goddess Ningal, the wife of the moon god, weeps for her city. She walks through the ruined streets. She mourns for her people. It is one of the saddest poems ever written. And one of the most beautiful.

[pause]

{img:ningal-goddess-weeping-ruined-ur-street-gentle-sorrow-poem}
But Ur did not disappear. People came back. They rebuilt the temples and the houses. Later kings repaired the ziggurat. For many centuries more, Ur was still a city. Much later, the Bible says that Abraham came from a place called Ur of the Chaldees. Many people believe it is this same Ur.

[long-pause]

But the Sumerian language slowly faded. People in the land began to speak another language instead. Sumerian was no longer spoken in the streets. But it was still used in the temples and schools, for prayers and old texts. A bit like Latin in Europe. For two thousand years, scribes still learned it.

[pause]

{img:later-babylonian-scribe-copying-old-sumerian-hymns-temple-library}
And slowly, the land changed. The salt in the fields grew worse. The rivers changed their course. The Euphrates moved away from Ur. Without the river, the city could not live. The harbours dried up. The canals filled with sand. One by one, the people left. And at last, Ur was empty. The wind blew sand over the houses and the temples. And the great ziggurat became a mound in the desert.

[long-pause]

For two thousand years, Ur lay sleeping under the sand. Nobody remembered it. Nobody could read its writing. Even the name Sumer was forgotten. Until the archaeologists came. And slowly, from the clay and the dust and the broken bricks, the first civilization came back to life.

[pause]

Many things we still use today began in Sumer. The idea of dividing an hour into sixty minutes comes from their way of counting. So does the idea of a circle with three hundred and sixty degrees. They had some of the first written laws and some of the first schools. They told some of the first stories ever written down. And so, in many small ways, the Sumerians are still with us, every single day.

[long-pause]

{img:ziggurat-ur-today-restored-lower-levels-desert-iraq-sunset}
Today, Ur lies in the desert of southern Iraq, about fifteen kilometres from the river. The river moved away long ago.

[pause]

The great ziggurat is still there. Its lower levels were partly rebuilt about fifty years ago. You can climb the great middle stairway. From the top, you look out over the desert. You see the mounds of the old city. The ruins of houses and temples. And far away, the line of green where the river now flows.

[long-pause]

Visitors come to Ur, though not as many as to some other places. It is quiet there. Mostly wind and sand and birds. Sometimes a family of local people. Sometimes a group of students. Sometimes a few travellers from far away. In 2021, the head of the Catholic Church visited Ur. He prayed there with leaders of many faiths. Because this is said to be the home of Abraham. A father to many people.

[pause]

{img:visitors-climbing-ziggurat-ur-stairs-today-desert-wind-light}
The treasures of Ur are spread around the world. Some are in Baghdad, in the national museum of Iraq. Some are in London. Some are in America. Queen Puabi's golden headdress. The lyres with their golden bulls. The game boards with their rosettes. The Standard of Ur. People stand in front of them and wonder at the skill of hands that lived four and a half thousand years ago.

[long-pause]

And the gifts of Sumer are all around us. We write. We use wheels. We count our hours in sixty minutes. We have laws written down for everyone to read. We tell stories about friendship and loss and floods. Every city in the world owes something to the first cities, here, between the rivers.

And in the marshes south of Ur, where the water has come back after hard times, people still live in reed houses. They still push long boats through the channels. They still keep water buffalo. Just as their ancestors did five thousand years ago.

In the villages near Ur today, families still bake flat bread in round clay ovens, much like the ovens of long ago. Children still play in the dust and chase the goats. And in the marshes to the east, people still build houses of reeds and glide through the water in narrow boats. Some things have changed very little in four thousand years.
