The Sumerians gave the world one of its greatest gifts. Writing.

It did not start with poems or stories. It started with counting. Long before writing, people in this land used little clay tokens to keep track of things. A small cone for a measure of barley. A ball for a sheep. A disc for a jar of oil. If you owed someone ten sheep, you gave them ten little clay balls.

--

Then people started to put the tokens inside a hollow ball of clay, like an envelope. And they pressed the tokens on the outside first, so you could see what was inside without breaking it. Then someone had a clever idea. Why bother with the tokens at all? Just press the marks into a flat piece of clay. And that was the start of writing.

It happened about five thousand two hundred years ago. In the city of Uruk, not far from Ur. The earliest tablets from Uruk are mostly lists. Barley and sheep and workers and beer. Little pictures pressed or drawn into clay. A head of barley for barley. A sheep's head for sheep. And marks for numbers.

{img:earliest-uruk-tablet-pictographs-barley-sheep-numbers-close-light}
Over hundreds of years, the pictures changed. Writing with a reed on wet clay is easier if you just press. So the pictures turned into little groups of wedge-shaped marks. Today we call this writing cuneiform. It means wedge-shaped.

--

Slowly, the signs came to stand for sounds too, not only for things. Then people could write names. And sentences. And letters. And songs. And laws. And stories. Writing could do anything that speech could do. And it could last. A spoken word disappears. A word written in clay can last for thousands of years.

{img:scribe-writing-letter-clay-envelope-seal-rolling-courtyard}
At first, only a few people could read and write. Writers who worked for the temples and the palace. But writing changed everything. Kings could send orders far away. Traders could make contracts. Priests could write down prayers. And for the first time, people could talk to the future.

--

That is exactly what they did. Hundreds of thousands of their clay tablets have been found. We can read them today. We can hear their voices across five thousand years. A trader complaining about bad copper. A mother writing to her son. A boy who hated school. The Sumerians are talking to us still.

There is one more thing about writing on clay. Clay can break. A tablet dropped on the floor can shatter. A tablet left in the rain can melt back into mud. So important tablets are baked hard in ovens. And sometimes fires do the baking by accident. When a library burns, the clay tablets inside do not burn. They bake. And they become as hard as brick. So a fire that destroys a city can save its writing for thousands of years.

Writing in clay is different from writing on paper. The scribe holds a soft tablet of damp clay in one hand. With the other hand, he presses a reed pen into the clay. Each press makes a little wedge-shaped mark. Many wedges together make one sign. When the tablet is finished, it is left to dry in the sun. Then it becomes hard, and the writing stays for a very long time. Some tablets have lasted more than four thousand years.
