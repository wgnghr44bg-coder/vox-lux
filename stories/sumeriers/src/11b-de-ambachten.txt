Ur is full of skilled craftsmen. And some of the most beautiful things in the ancient world were made here.

In the workshops of the goldsmiths, men work with thin sheets of gold. They hammer the gold until it is as thin as a leaf. They cut it into shapes. Leaves and flowers and stars. They make headdresses of golden leaves for the great ladies. They make cups and bowls and rings. They combine gold with blue lapis and red carnelian, so it shines in three colours.

--

The stone workers carve seals. A seal is a small stone cylinder, about the size of your finger. Tiny pictures are carved into it. Gods and animals and heroes and people. Every important person has a seal. To sign a tablet, you roll your seal across the wet clay. It leaves a little picture, like a signature. Nobody else has a seal quite like yours.

{img:cylinder-seal-rolling-wet-clay-tiny-scene-impression-close}
Seal carvers work with tiny tools and great patience. The pictures are so small, some of them only a few centimetres long. But they are full of detail. A hero fighting a lion. A row of goats walking past a tree. A god on a throne with worshippers before him. Each one is like a tiny work of art.

--

There are carpenters and boat builders. There are potters at their spinning wheels. There are metal workers who melt copper and tin together to make bronze. They pour it into clay moulds to make tools and bowls and statues. There are reed weavers and leather workers and perfume makers.

One famous object from Ur shows the life of the city. It is a wooden box about the size of a shoebox, covered with little pictures. The pictures are made of shell and red stone and blue lapis, set in black tar. On one side there is a great feast. The king sits drinking. A musician plays a lyre. Servants bring animals and fish and bundles of goods. People call it the Standard of Ur. Nobody knows what it was for. But it is beautiful.

{img:standard-of-ur-mosaic-feast-side-lapis-shell-rows-figures-museum}
The craftsmen of Ur pass their skills from father to son. And from mother to daughter. A boy might start as a helper at seven or eight. He sweeps the floor and fetches water. He watches. Slowly he is allowed to try simple things. After many years, he becomes a master himself. And one day, he will teach his own son.

In the workshops of Ur, there are many skilled craftsmen. There are metalworkers who make tools and bowls of copper and bronze. There are jewellers who string beads of gold and blue lapis lazuli. There are carpenters who make chairs and chests and boats. There are weavers who make fine cloth on large looms. Many of them work in open doorways, so people in the street can watch them. And children often stop to stare, fascinated by their quick and clever hands.
