The Sumerians did not only invent writing. They made many other things the world still uses.

One of the most famous is the wheel. Some of the oldest wheels in the world were made in this land. The first wheels were solid. They were made of three planks of wood, joined together and cut into a circle. They were heavy and slow. But they worked.

--

At first, wheels were probably used by potters. A potter's wheel is a flat round disc that spins. You put a lump of clay in the middle and spin it. Then you shape it with your hands. It makes round pots quickly and evenly. Before that, every pot was built slowly by hand.

Then someone thought of putting wheels on a cart. Now heavy loads could be pulled over land. Carts and wagons were pulled by oxen or by wild donkeys. Later, kings rode in chariots in their parades. Wheels changed the way people moved things for ever.

{img:potter-spinning-wheel-shaping-jar-workshop-ur-hands-clay}
The Sumerians also improved the plough. Ploughs are tools that cut and turn the soil before planting. The Sumerians made a clever plough with a funnel on top. As the ox pulls the plough, the farmer drops seeds into the funnel. The seeds fall down a tube right into the new furrow. Ploughing and sowing in one go. It saved a lot of time and seed.

--

They also built sailboats. They made bronze, by mixing copper with tin. Bronze is much harder than copper alone. They made bricks baked in ovens, strong and waterproof. They used tar from the ground to glue and seal things. They made glass, or something close to it. They made the first maps. They counted in sixties and divided the circle into parts. Every time you look at a clock with sixty minutes, you are using an idea from Sumer.

{img:sumerian-seed-plough-funnel-ox-farmer-dropping-seeds-field}
Nobody knows the names of the people who invented these things. They were potters and farmers and builders and boatmen. Ordinary clever people trying to make their work a little easier. But their ideas spread to the whole world. And we still use them every single day.

--

In our family's house, the grandmother has an old spinning wheel for clay. Not for wool. Her father was a potter. When she was a girl, she sat beside him and watched him spin pots. Bowls and jars and cups, round and smooth. She tried it herself. Her first pot was crooked and lumpy. Her father laughed and kept it anyway. It still stands on a shelf in her room. She shows it to her grandchildren sometimes. Every potter starts with a crooked pot, she tells them.
