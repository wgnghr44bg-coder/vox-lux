South of Ur, where the rivers come close to the sea, there are great marshes.

They are a world of water and reeds. The reeds grow tall, taller than a man. Narrow channels of water wind between them. The water is still and clear. Fish swim in it. Birds of every kind live there. Herons and ducks and pelicans. Kingfishers flash blue between the stems.

--

The marsh people live on little islands. Some of the islands are made by people. They pile up reeds and mud until it is high enough to build on. Their houses are made of reeds too. They tie bundles of tall reeds into great arches. They bend them over and tie them together at the top. Then they cover the frame with woven reed mats. The result is a long tunnel-shaped house. Cool in summer. Dry in the rain.

Some of these reed houses are very big. A great reed hall can be twenty or thirty metres long. Villages meet there. Guests are welcomed there. And they are beautiful, with patterns woven into the mats.

{img:reed-arch-house-interior-woven-mats-light-through-pattern-calm}
The marsh people travel in long thin boats. They push them along with poles through the channels. They fish with spears and nets. They keep water buffalo, big dark animals that wallow in the water. The buffalo give milk. And their dung is dried and burned as fuel.

--

The amazing thing is this. Pictures from Sumer, more than four thousand years old, show exactly these reed houses and these boats. And in the marshes of southern Iraq, people built the very same houses in the same way until quite recently. The same arches. The same mats. The same long boats. For five thousand years, almost nothing changed.

{img:marsh-boy-poling-canoe-reeds-channel-heron-taking-off-morning}
For the people of Ur, the marshes are close by. Reeds are one of their most useful materials. They make houses and boats and baskets and mats from them. And they use one thin reed for something very special. They cut it to a point. And with it, they write.
