After the music, the father and his eldest son play a game. It is the favourite game of Sumer. Today we call it the Royal Game of Ur.

The board is shaped a little like a long box with a narrow middle. It has twenty squares. Some are decorated with flowers called rosettes. Each player has seven pieces. Round flat counters, white for one player and black for the other. And instead of dice with dots, they throw little pyramid-shaped dice. Each one has two corners marked. You throw four of them and count how many marked corners point up.

--

The goal is to race all your pieces around the board and off the end. Before your opponent does. Your pieces go in at one side and come out at the other. In the middle, both players share the same path. And if you land on your opponent's piece there, you knock it off. It has to start all over again.

The rosette squares are special. If you land on one, you are safe. Nobody can knock you off. And you get another turn. So everyone wants to reach the rosettes. And the middle rosette is the most important square of all.

{img:father-son-playing-game-of-ur-rooftop-lamplight-evening-laughing}
It is a game of luck. But also of thinking. Which piece do you move? Do you risk it? Do you wait? It is exciting. One lucky throw can change everything. Players groan and cheer and laugh.

--

How do we know the rules? For a long time, nobody knew. Beautiful boards were found in the royal tombs of Ur. But no rulebook. Then, in a museum in London, an expert found a clay tablet. It had been written about two thousand years after our story, by a Babylonian writer. And it explained how to play. So the game was still being played then. For thousands of years.

{img:game-board-carved-on-gate-stone-lamassu-guards-later-graffiti}
People played it everywhere. Guards at a palace gate in the north scratched a game board into the stone they stood on. To pass the time on long boring watches. Boards have been found from Egypt to India. It was the most popular game of the ancient world.

--

Tonight, the father is winning. He has three pieces off the board already. His son has only one. Then the boy throws. Four marked corners. The best throw there is. He moves his piece onto the middle rosette. He throws again. He knocks his father's piece off the board. The father groans. The son grins. And the grandmother laughs so hard she has to put down her lyre.

In our family, the eldest son and his father play the Game of Ur in the evenings. They sit on the roof with the board between them, and the lamp beside them. The daughter watches and cheers for her brother. The younger son tries to help by moving the pieces when nobody is looking. Everyone laughs when he is caught. Sometimes the father wins, and sometimes the son wins. And sometimes, the grandmother plays and beats them both.

The Game of Ur was played all over the ancient world for thousands of years. Boards have been found in many places, carved in wood and stone and clay. Some were simple, scratched into a step or a wall. Others were beautiful, decorated with shells and red stone and blue lapis. Today, people still play it, using the rules found on an old clay tablet. And it is still great fun.
