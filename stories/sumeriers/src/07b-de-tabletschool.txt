The eldest son goes to school. The Sumerians call it the tablet house.

It is not a grand building. Usually it is a room in a teacher's house. The boys sit on low benches of brick, or on the floor. Each has a lump of wet clay and a reed pen. The teacher is called the father of the tablet house. And the older pupils who help him are called big brothers.

--

School starts at sunrise and ends at sunset. Every day, for years. The boys first learn to make a good tablet. Smooth and flat and the right size. Then they learn to press the wedges. Long ones, short ones, upright ones, slanting ones. Then they learn the signs, one by one. There are hundreds of them.

They copy everything. The teacher writes a model on one side of the tablet. The boy copies it on the other side. Again and again. Lists of words. Lists of trees and animals and fish and birds. Lists of gods and cities and jobs. Then proverbs and short stories and hymns. If he makes a mistake, he smooths the clay with his thumb and starts again.

{img:pupil-copying-teacher-model-tablet-crooked-wedges-close-hands}
It is hard. And the teachers are strict. We know this because the Sumerians wrote about it. One old story is told by a schoolboy himself. He writes about a terrible day at school. He was late. So he was punished. He talked in class. Punished. He stood up without permission. Punished. His writing was messy. Punished again. He went home very unhappy.

--

So he had an idea. He told his father to invite the teacher home for dinner. The father did. He gave the teacher the best seat. He served him a fine meal. He gave him a new robe and a ring. And the teacher, full and happy, suddenly said nice things. Your son is a fine pupil, he said. He will go far.

It is a funny story. And very human. Schoolboys and teachers and parents four thousand years ago. Not so different from today. Students copied this story for hundreds of years. Perhaps it made them feel better on bad days.

{img:boy-walking-home-from-school-evening-street-tablet-basket-tired}
The eldest son of our family knows the story well. Today he talked in class. His teacher was not pleased. He thinks about telling his father to invite the teacher to dinner. But his father is away at sea. So he will just have to try harder tomorrow.

Not every day at school is hard. Sometimes the teacher tells the boys old stories and proverbs. A good word is a friend to everyone, he says. A friend for a day, but a relative forever. The boys copy the proverbs onto their tablets and learn them by heart. At midday, they eat their bread in the courtyard and play games. And at the end of the day, they run home through the streets, glad to be free.

The teacher has an assistant, an older student whom the boys call big brother. He checks their tablets and helps them when they make mistakes. He shows them how to hold the reed pen at the right angle. He is patient with the youngest ones and strict with the lazy ones. The eldest son of our family likes him very much. One day, he thinks, I want to be a big brother too.
