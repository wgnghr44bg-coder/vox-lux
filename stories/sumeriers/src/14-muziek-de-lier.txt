The Sumerians love music. Music is played in the temples and the palace. At feasts and funerals. In the fields and in the taverns. And some of the oldest musical instruments in the world come from Ur.

The most famous are the lyres. A lyre is a stringed instrument. It has a wooden sound box at the bottom. Two arms rise up from it. A bar joins the arms at the top. And strings run from the box up to the bar. You pluck the strings with your fingers.

--

The lyres of Ur are works of art. On the front of the sound box, there is often the head of a bull. Made of gold, with a beard of blue lapis. Its eyes are shell and stone. It looks out at you, calm and noble. Below the head, there are little pictures of animals, set in shell. A dog carrying a table. A lion carrying a jar. A donkey playing a lyre. A bear dancing. They look like a funny story, but we do not know what story it is.

Some lyres are big. As big as a person. The musician sits or stands next to it to play. Others are smaller and can be held in the arms.

{img:musician-playing-big-lyre-temple-courtyard-singers-listening}
The lyres were found in the royal tombs of Ur. They were made of wood, and after four and a half thousand years the wood had rotted away. But the archaeologists were clever. They poured plaster into the holes left in the earth. When the plaster set, they had the shape of the lyre. Then they rebuilt it. Today you can see them in museums. And some musicians have made copies that really play.

--

What did Sumerian music sound like? Nobody knows exactly. But we have some clues. A clay tablet from a nearby land, a little later, has the oldest written music ever found. It is a hymn to a goddess. It has words, and special signs that tell the player which strings to pluck. Scholars have tried to play it. It sounds soft and slow and a little sad.

{img:harpist-and-singer-evening-palace-garden-lamps-soft-music}
There are other instruments too. Drums and flutes and pipes. Harps. Little rattles and clappers. Bells. At temple ceremonies, big drums boom and singers chant hymns to the gods. At feasts in the palace, lyres and harps play softly. Singers sing of the gods and heroes and the beauty of the city.

--

In our family, the grandmother can play a small lyre. She learned it when she was a girl. On warm evenings, she takes it out on the roof. She plucks the strings gently. The notes float out over the dark rooftops. The father, home again, closes his eyes and listens. The children lie on their backs and look at the stars. And for a little while, nobody speaks.

In the evenings, music can be heard all over Ur. From the temples comes the sound of harps and drums and singing. From the houses comes the sound of simple flutes and clapping hands. On the roof of our family's house, the father sometimes plays a small lyre that he brought back from his travels. He is not very good at it. But the children love to listen, and the grandmother sings along softly. And the music drifts out over the roofs into the warm night.
