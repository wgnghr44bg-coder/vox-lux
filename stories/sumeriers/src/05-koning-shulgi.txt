The king of Ur in our story is called Shulgi. He is one of the greatest kings Sumer ever had.

His father was Ur-Nammu. Ur-Nammu made Ur strong again after a time of trouble. He joined many cities of Sumer under his rule. He built the great ziggurat. He dug canals and made roads safe. And then, sadly, he fell in battle far from home. Shulgi was young when he became king. Everyone wondered. Could he hold his father's kingdom together?

--

He could. Shulgi ruled for almost fifty years. And under him, Ur became the centre of a great kingdom. Its power reached across all of Sumer and beyond. To the mountains in the east. And up the rivers to the north.

Shulgi was a great organiser. He made everyone in the kingdom use the same weights and measures. A bag of barley weighed the same in Ur as in Nippur. He set up a calendar that everyone followed. He built roads with rest houses along them for travellers. Every day, at these stations, messengers could find food and water and a place to sleep.

{img:ur-iii-scribes-office-thousands-tablets-counting-sheep-barley}
And he loved writing. Under Shulgi, there were more scribes than ever before. They wrote down everything. How many sheep came to the temple. How much barley each worker received. How many days a man worked in the fields. Tens of thousands of these clay tablets have been found. They are the most careful records from the ancient world. Some people have called this kingdom the first great office of history.

--

Shulgi also liked to boast. He had songs written about himself. In them, he says he is the best at everything. The fastest runner. The best singer. The wisest judge. He says he can speak five languages. He says he can play every instrument. He says he went to school as a boy and was the best student of all.

{img:shulgi-playing-lyre-court-musicians-listening-hall-lamps}
Some of that is probably true. Some of it is probably bragging. But one of his boasts was so famous that people sang about it for hundreds of years. It was about a great run. And it is a story worth hearing.

--

But being king is not only songs and boasting. Every morning, Shulgi meets his officials. Reports come in from every city. A canal has silted up near Lagash. A flock of sheep has fallen sick near Umma. Messengers from the mountains in the east bring news of trouble on the border. The king listens. He gives orders. He sends workers and soldiers and silver. A good king must keep the whole land running. And the land is big.

In the palace, King Shulgi has many servants and officials. Scribes write down his orders on clay tablets. Cooks prepare great feasts of bread and meat and dates. Musicians play for him in the evenings. But the old songs say that Shulgi also liked to be among ordinary people. He visited the temples and the schools. He talked with farmers and soldiers. And he wanted people to remember him as a wise and kind king.

Shulgi also built roads and rest houses for travellers, all across his kingdom. Every few hours of walking, there was a place to stop. Travellers could find water and shade and a place to sleep. Messengers could get fresh food before they ran on. It made the roads safer and the journeys easier. Traders were glad of it, and so were the farmers taking their goods to market.
