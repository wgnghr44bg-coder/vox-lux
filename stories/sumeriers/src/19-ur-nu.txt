Today, Ur lies in the desert of southern Iraq, about fifteen kilometres from the river. The river moved away long ago.

The great ziggurat is still there. Its lower levels were partly rebuilt about fifty years ago. You can climb the great middle stairway. From the top, you look out over the desert. You see the mounds of the old city. The ruins of houses and temples. And far away, the line of green where the river now flows.

--

Visitors come to Ur, though not as many as to some other places. It is quiet there. Mostly wind and sand and birds. Sometimes a family of local people. Sometimes a group of students. Sometimes a few travellers from far away. In 2021, the head of the Catholic Church visited Ur. He prayed there with leaders of many faiths. Because this is said to be the home of Abraham. A father to many people.

{img:visitors-climbing-ziggurat-ur-stairs-today-desert-wind-light}
The treasures of Ur are spread around the world. Some are in Baghdad, in the national museum of Iraq. Some are in London. Some are in America. Queen Puabi's golden headdress. The lyres with their golden bulls. The game boards with their rosettes. The Standard of Ur. People stand in front of them and wonder at the skill of hands that lived four and a half thousand years ago.

--

And the gifts of Sumer are all around us. We write. We use wheels. We count our hours in sixty minutes. We have laws written down for everyone to read. We tell stories about friendship and loss and floods. Every city in the world owes something to the first cities, here, between the rivers.

{img:marsh-arabs-today-reed-house-boat-iraq-marshes-sunset-calm}
And in the marshes south of Ur, where the water has come back after hard times, people still live in reed houses. They still push long boats through the channels. They still keep water buffalo. Just as their ancestors did five thousand years ago.

--

In the villages near Ur today, families still bake flat bread in round clay ovens, much like the ovens of long ago. Children still play in the dust and chase the goats. And in the marshes to the east, people still build houses of reeds and glide through the water in narrow boats. Some things have changed very little in four thousand years.

So we leave Ur tonight, in the time of King Shulgi, as the evening falls.

The sun sets behind the palm groves. The great ziggurat turns gold and then red and then dark. On the rooftops, families spread their mats for the night. The father is home. The children are falling asleep under the stars. The grandmother is playing her lyre, softly. In the temple at the top of the tower, the priests light their lamps. Down by the harbour, the boats rock gently on the water. And over the river and the reeds and the sleeping city, the moon rises. Silver and full and calm. Nanna, the god of Ur, sailing across the night sky in his boat.
