Shulgi ruled Ur for almost fifty years. His kingdom was rich and strong. But nothing lasts forever.

After Shulgi, his sons ruled. At first, all was well. Then troubles began. Harvests failed. Wandering peoples came from the west and the north. The great kingdom started to break apart. Cities stopped obeying Ur. And about a hundred years after our story, an army from the mountains in the east came down to Ur.

--

The city was taken. The temples were damaged. The statue of the moon god was carried away. We will leave those sad days there, gently, in the past.

The people of Sumer wrote a long poem about it. It is called the Lament for Ur. A lament is a song of sadness. In it, the goddess Ningal, the wife of the moon god, weeps for her city. She walks through the ruined streets. She mourns for her people. It is one of the saddest poems ever written. And one of the most beautiful.

{img:ningal-goddess-weeping-ruined-ur-street-gentle-sorrow-poem}
But Ur did not disappear. People came back. They rebuilt the temples and the houses. Later kings repaired the ziggurat. For many centuries more, Ur was still a city. Much later, the Bible says that Abraham came from a place called Ur of the Chaldees. Many people believe it is this same Ur.

--

But the Sumerian language slowly faded. People in the land began to speak another language instead. Sumerian was no longer spoken in the streets. But it was still used in the temples and schools, for prayers and old texts. A bit like Latin in Europe. For two thousand years, scribes still learned it.

{img:later-babylonian-scribe-copying-old-sumerian-hymns-temple-library}
And slowly, the land changed. The salt in the fields grew worse. The rivers changed their course. The Euphrates moved away from Ur. Without the river, the city could not live. The harbours dried up. The canals filled with sand. One by one, the people left. And at last, Ur was empty. The wind blew sand over the houses and the temples. And the great ziggurat became a mound in the desert.

--

For two thousand years, Ur lay sleeping under the sand. Nobody remembered it. Nobody could read its writing. Even the name Sumer was forgotten. Until the archaeologists came. And slowly, from the clay and the dust and the broken bricks, the first civilization came back to life.

Many things we still use today began in Sumer. The idea of dividing an hour into sixty minutes comes from their way of counting. So does the idea of a circle with three hundred and sixty degrees. They had some of the first written laws and some of the first schools. They told some of the first stories ever written down. And so, in many small ways, the Sumerians are still with us, every single day.
