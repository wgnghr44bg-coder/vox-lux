Shulgi's father, King Ur-Nammu, did something important. He wrote down laws.

It is the oldest written set of laws we have found. It is about three hundred years older than the more famous laws of a later king called Hammurabi. Pieces of it were found on clay tablets. Not all of it has survived. But enough to understand what kind of laws they were.

--

At the beginning, the king explains why he made the laws. He says he did it so that the orphan would not be handed over to the rich man. So that the widow would not be handed over to the powerful man. So that the man with one sheep would not be handed over to the man with a hundred sheep. He wanted fairness. He wanted the weak to be protected.

And then come the laws themselves. If someone does this, then that happens. They are short and clear. Many of them are about money. If you break someone's bone, you pay them a fixed amount of silver. If you cut off someone's nose, you pay more. If you steal, you pay back. If you lie in court, you pay a fine.

{img:judges-sitting-temple-gate-ur-hearing-case-witnesses-scribe}
This was quite gentle for its time. Many later laws in other lands were much harsher. An eye for an eye. But in Ur-Nammu's laws, most wrongs could be made right with silver. The idea was to fix the harm. Not just to punish.

--

There are judges in Ur. They often sit at the gates of the temple. People come to them with their quarrels. About land and debts and marriages and stolen sheep. The judges listen to both sides. They hear witnesses. They look at the contracts on clay tablets. And they decide. A scribe writes everything down.

{img:two-neighbours-arguing-before-judges-clay-contract-held-up-calm}
Sometimes, when nobody can prove who is right, the judges ask someone to swear an oath before the god. That is very serious. People believe the god will punish a lie. So many people would rather settle the quarrel than swear falsely. Often, at the last moment, someone gives in.

--

Most problems are settled without judges. Families and neighbours talk things out. An old man of the street might help. Everyone meets in a courtyard in the evening. They argue. They drink a little beer. And in the end, they usually agree. And then they go home to sleep.

There is a famous Sumerian saying about this. It says, a friendly word is a friend to everyone. Another says, do not quarrel with your neighbour, for tomorrow you may need his help. The people of Ur lived close together. They knew that getting along was worth more than winning.
