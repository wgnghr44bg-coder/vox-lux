The most important food in Sumer is barley.

Barley is a grain, a bit like wheat. It grows well in the hot dry land, if it gets enough water. And it does not mind a little salt in the soil. That matters here. Because the river water carries tiny bits of salt. Over the years, the salt builds up in the fields. Wheat cannot grow in salty soil. Barley can, at least for a while.

--

Barley is everything. People eat it as bread and porridge. They drink it as beer. Workers are paid in barley. Taxes are paid in barley. Prices are counted in barley. If you want to know how rich someone is, you ask how much barley they have.

The fields lie in long narrow strips beside the canals. In autumn, the farmers plough them with oxen. Then they sow the barley. In spring, the barley grows green and then golden. In late spring, it is harvested. The workers cut it with curved knives made of clay or copper. They tie it in bundles. They carry it to the threshing floor. Oxen walk round and round on it to shake out the grain. And then the wind blows away the husks.

{img:threshing-floor-oxen-walking-circles-barley-grain-wind-chaff}
There is an old Sumerian book of advice for farmers. A father gives instructions to his son. When to open the canals. How deep to plough. How to sow the seed. How to scare away the birds. How to protect the young barley from pests. It is one of the oldest farming books in the world. And the father ends it by saying, these are the teachings of the god of farming.

--

The second great food of Sumer is dates. The date palm grows everywhere along the canals. It gives sweet sticky fruit, full of energy. Dates can be eaten fresh or dried. They can be pressed into syrup, sweet like honey. The palm gives wood and leaves and fibre too. Nothing is wasted.

{img:date-palm-grove-canal-climber-rope-harvesting-dates-baskets}
Under the palms, in their shade, people grow gardens. Onions and garlic and leeks. Lentils and chickpeas. Cucumbers and lettuce. Apples and figs and pomegranates. A Sumerian garden is like an oasis. Cool and green and full of birds, in the middle of a hot brown land.

--

But there is a danger growing slowly in these fields. Every year, a little more salt. Every year, the barley grows a little less well. Farmers notice. They leave some fields to rest. They try to wash the salt away with extra water. For now, the harvests are still good. But over hundreds of years, the salt will win. And the fields of the south will turn white and bare.
