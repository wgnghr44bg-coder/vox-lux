Sumer has rich soil and plenty of barley and wool. But it has almost nothing else. No stone. No good wood. No metal. No precious stones. So the Sumerians must trade for everything.

And many of their traders go by sea.

--

From the harbours of Ur, ships sail down the river to the sea. Then they sail south along the coast of the Gulf. Their first big stop is an island called Dilmun. Today it is called Bahrain. Dilmun is a green island in the sea. It has fresh water springs bubbling up from the ground. Palm trees and gardens. The Sumerians think of it as a kind of paradise. In their old stories, Dilmun is a pure land where nobody grows old and no lion hunts.

Dilmun is also a great trading place. Ships from many lands meet there. Copper comes there from the mountains of Oman, further south. Stone and timber and ivory and pearls come too. The traders of Ur bring barley and wool and cloth and oil. And they swap them for copper.

{img:dilmun-island-fresh-springs-palms-harbour-ships-trading-goods}
This trade is risky. The sea can be rough. Ships can sink. Pirates can attack. Traders borrow money to buy their goods. If the ship is lost, they lose everything. So traders pray to the gods before every voyage. And their families wait anxiously at home.

--

We know about one trader from Ur who lived a little later than our story. His name was Ea-nasir. He sold copper. And he was famous for one reason. His customers complained. A lot. One customer wrote him an angry letter on a clay tablet. You promised me good copper, he wrote. But you gave me bad copper. You treated my messenger rudely. Who do you think I am? Give me back my money!

{img:angry-clay-tablet-letter-complaint-copper-trader-ea-nasir-museum}
This letter has survived for nearly four thousand years. It is now in a museum in London. People call it the oldest customer complaint in the world. And Ea-nasir kept all these angry letters in his house. Maybe he thought he would deal with them later.

--

In our family, the father has gone to Dilmun. He took wool and cloth on a big boat with other traders. He should have been back weeks ago. Every evening, the mother goes up on the roof and looks toward the harbour. She sees the masts of ships. But not his.

On the long journey to Dilmun, the sailors live on the deck of their ship. They sleep under the stars on mats of woven reed. They eat dates and dried fish and bread, and they drink water from big clay jars. In the evenings, they sit together and sing songs about the sea and their homes. The father of our family sits among them. He looks at the stars and thinks about his wife and children far away in Ur. And he counts the days until he can go home.
