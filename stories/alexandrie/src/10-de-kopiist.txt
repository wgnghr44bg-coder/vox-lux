Let us spend a day with Demetrios, at his work in the great library.

He sets off from home early in the morning, just after sunrise. He walks through the streets to the royal quarter, past the harbour and the palace. At the gate of the library, the guard knows him and nods. He goes to the copying room, a long room with big windows that let in plenty of light. There are about twenty copyists working there, each at his own desk.

--

Demetrios sits down at his desk. In front of him, there is the scroll he is copying, held open by little weights. Beside it, there is a new, blank roll of papyrus. He has a pot of black ink and several reed pens. He cuts the tip of a pen with a little knife, to make it sharp. Then he dips it in the ink, and begins to write.

{img:scribe-close-hand-writing-greek-letters-reed-pen-papyrus-ink-pot}
He writes in Greek, in neat, even capital letters. In those days, words are written without any spaces between them, and with very little punctuation. He must be very careful. He reads a few words from the old scroll, and then writes them on the new one. Then a few more words. Line by line, column by column. A good copyist can copy perhaps a few hundred lines in a day.

--

Mistakes are very easy to make. Your eyes can jump from one line to another. You can miss a word, or write a word twice. So copyists must concentrate very hard, all day long. When Demetrios makes a mistake, he can sometimes wash the ink off the papyrus with a wet sponge, while it is still fresh. When a scroll is finished, another copyist checks it against the original, word by word.

{img:scribes-checking-copy-two-men-reading-aloud-comparing-scrolls-table}
Today, Demetrios is copying a book of poems by Sappho, a famous woman poet who lived on the island of Lesbos, long ago. Her poems are about love and friendship, flowers and the moon. They are very beautiful. Demetrios reads them as he copies them, and sometimes he smiles. He thinks he will tell Kallisto about them tonight.

--

At midday, the copyists stop for a meal. They eat bread and cheese, olives and dried figs, in a little courtyard with a fountain. They talk about their work and the latest news from the city. Some of them are old men, who have been copying for thirty years. Some are young men, just starting. They tease each other and laugh. Then they go back to their desks for the afternoon.

{img:copyists-lunch-courtyard-fountain-bread-olives-figs-talking-shade}
In the evening, Demetrios walks home through the busy streets. His eyes are tired, and his fingers are stained with ink. But he is content. Today he has copied three columns of Sappho's poems. In a few weeks, the whole book will be finished. A new copy, for the great library. And perhaps, he thinks, someone will read it in a hundred years, or a thousand. He likes to think of that. Then he opens the door of his house, and Honey the cat comes running to meet him.

When a new scroll is finished, Demetrios writes his name at the very end, in small letters. It is a tradition among copyists. He also writes how many lines he has copied. Copyists are paid by the line, so this is important. Then the scroll is rolled up, and a little label is attached. And it goes onto the shelves of the great library, to wait for its first reader.

Sometimes Demetrios takes Kallisto with him to the library, on a quiet day. She sits beside him at his desk, very still, and watches him write. She looks at the long rows of shelves, full of scrolls. She breathes in the smell of papyrus and ink. And she thinks that this must be the most wonderful place in the whole world. Her father lets her copy a few lines on a spare piece of papyrus.
