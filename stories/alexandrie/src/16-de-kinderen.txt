What is life like for children in Alexandria, around 240 before Christ?

Children of Greek families often go to school, from about the age of seven. Boys especially, but sometimes girls too. They go to a little school run by a teacher, called a grammatistes, a teacher of letters. They learn to read and write Greek. They write on wax tablets with a pointed stick, or on broken pieces of pottery with ink. Later, they read the poems of Homer and learn them by heart.

--

Theon goes to a little school in the next street. There are about fifteen boys there, of different ages. The teacher is a thin old man with a long beard and a stick. He is strict, but fair. The boys sit on benches and copy the letters he writes. They recite poems and practise counting. Theon is not very good at sitting still. He would rather be at the harbour. But he is good at counting, and he likes stories about the sea.

{img:children-playing-knucklebones-courtyard-alexandria-afternoon-shade-laughing}
Children in Alexandria also love to play. They play with balls and hoops, and with spinning tops. They play a game with little bones from the ankles of sheep, called knucklebones, throwing and catching them. They play tag and hide and seek in the streets. Girls play with dolls made of clay and cloth, with little arms and legs that move. Children also keep pets: cats and dogs, birds and even monkeys.

--

Egyptian children learn mostly at home, from their parents. Boys learn their father's work, and girls learn their mother's. Some Egyptian boys go to schools at the temples, where they learn the old Egyptian writing, hieroglyphs. It is very difficult, with hundreds of different signs. It takes many years to learn. The priests teach it, so that the old prayers and stories will never be forgotten.

{img:grandmother-teaching-girl-hieroglyphs-drawing-in-sand-courtyard-evening}
Grandmother Tiye cannot read or write. But she knows some of the old hieroglyphs, from the temples of her childhood. She draws them for Kallisto in the sand of the courtyard, with a stick. This one is an eye, she says, and this one is a bird. This one is water, and this one is the sun. Kallisto copies them carefully. She loves the little pictures. She thinks they are much more beautiful than Greek letters.

--

Kallisto has a best friend called Miriam, who lives in the Jewish quarter. Her father is a merchant who buys papyrus from Merit. The two girls met at the market, when they were small. Now they meet whenever they can. They walk along the beach and collect shells. They sit in the shade and read to each other. Miriam reads stories from her people's holy books, in Greek. Kallisto reads Homer. They tell each other about their families and their festivals.

{img:two-girls-friends-walking-beach-collecting-shells-alexandria-sea-sunset}
On hot summer days, the children swim in the sea. There are little beaches along the coast, with soft sand and calm water. The children splash and dive and float on their backs, looking up at the blue sky. Theon is a very good swimmer. He says he will need to be, when he is a sailor. Kallisto prefers to sit on the sand with a scroll. But even she cannot resist the cool water, on the hottest days.

The children of Alexandria also love the animals of the city. There are cats everywhere, sleeping in the sun and hunting mice. There are dogs in the streets, and donkeys carrying loads. There are birds in cages in the market, singing. And there are the animals of the royal zoo, which children can sometimes glimpse through the gates. Theon once saw an elephant there, and he talked about it for weeks.

In the evenings, the children of the street often gather in someone's courtyard to play games and tell stories. They tell ghost stories and funny stories, stories of heroes and monsters. They ask each other riddles. Theon is very good at riddles. His favourite is an old one about a creature that walks on four legs in the morning, two at noon, and three in the evening. The answer is a person: a baby, a grown-up, and an old person with a stick.
