In our own time, divers and archaeologists have found remains of ancient Alexandria, under the sea.

Over the centuries, the coast of Alexandria has sunk a little. Earthquakes and the rising sea covered parts of the old city with water. The royal quarter, with the palace, is now partly under the sea. So is part of the island of Pharos. For many years, nobody knew what was down there. Then, about thirty years ago, divers began to explore.

--

What they found was amazing. In the shallow water near the old harbour, there are thousands of great stone blocks, columns and statues. Some of them are very big. There are sphinxes, with the bodies of lions and the heads of kings. There are statues of kings and queens, dressed as pharaohs. There are columns of granite and pieces of great buildings. Some of the stones may have come from the great lighthouse, after it fell.

{img:giant-statue-lifted-from-sea-crane-boat-divers-alexandria-harbour-today}
Some of the statues have been lifted out of the sea, very carefully, with big cranes. They are cleaned and studied, and put in museums. One of them is a huge statue of a king, as tall as a two-storey house. Others have been left under the water, where divers can visit them. It is like an underwater museum. Fish swim around the old sphinxes, and seaweed grows on the columns.

--

On land, too, archaeologists have found traces of the old city. Under the modern streets, they have found the old cisterns, the underground water tanks. Some of them are still there, with their stone columns and arches. They have found pieces of old houses and floors with mosaics, pictures made of little stones. They have found a lecture hall, with rows of stone seats, where students once listened to their teachers.

{img:ancient-lecture-halls-kom-el-dikka-stone-seats-rows-ruins-sunlight-today}
These lecture halls are at a place called Kom el-Dikka, in the middle of the modern city. There are about twenty of them, side by side. They are small, with seats in a horseshoe shape around a raised seat for the teacher. They are from a later time than our story, from the Roman period. But they show that Alexandria remained a city of learning for many centuries.

--

Archaeologists have also found many papyrus scrolls, though not in Alexandria itself. The ground in Alexandria is too damp, and the old papyri have rotted away. But in the dry sands of the Egyptian desert, further south, papyri have survived. In one old town, people found thousands of them, in the rubbish dumps of the town. There are letters and lists, shopping notes and school exercises. There are pieces of old books, poems and plays. Some of them are books that were thought to be lost forever.

{img:papyrus-fragments-museum-case-greek-writing-old-faded-careful-light-today}
Reading these old papyri, we can hear the voices of ordinary people from long ago. A boy writes to his father, asking him to send a present. A woman writes a list of things to buy at the market. A soldier writes home to his mother, to say that he is well. A schoolchild practises writing letters, again and again. These little papers, saved by the dry desert sand, bring the people of ancient Egypt very close to us.

Archaeologists also use modern tools to study ancient Alexandria. They use special machines that can see under the sea floor and under the ground. They make maps of the old harbours and the lost buildings. They study the old stones, to find out where they came from. Bit by bit, they are drawing a picture of the great city as it once was.

One of the most exciting finds in the dry sands of Egypt was a piece of an old library catalogue. It listed books and their authors, just like the Pinakes of Callimachus. Another find was a page from a lost book about the history of Athens. Every few years, new papyri are found, read and studied. And each one tells us a little more about the world of ancient Egypt and its great city by the sea.
