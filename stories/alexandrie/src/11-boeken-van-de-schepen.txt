How did the library collect so many books? The kings of Alexandria had some very clever, and some rather cheeky, ways of doing it.

First, they bought books. They sent agents all over the Greek world, to Athens and Rhodes, to Pergamon and Antioch. The agents bought every book they could find, especially old and rare ones. They paid very good prices. The kings were willing to spend a great deal of money on books. Sometimes they paid more for an old book than for a fine house.

--

Second, they had translations made. They wanted books from every country, in Greek, so that the scholars could read them. So they invited scholars from other lands to come to Alexandria and translate their books. The most famous story is about the Jewish holy books. The old stories say that seventy-two Jewish scholars came from Jerusalem to Alexandria. They translated the books of the Hebrew Bible into Greek. This translation is called the Septuagint, which means the seventy. It is still read today.

{img:jewish-scholars-translating-scrolls-hebrew-greek-island-house-sea-light}
An Egyptian priest called Manetho wrote a history of Egypt in Greek, for the kings. He used the old records of the Egyptian temples. He divided the kings of Egypt into groups, called dynasties. We still use his dynasties today, when we talk about the history of ancient Egypt.

--

And third, there was the famous trick with the ships. Every ship that came into the harbour of Alexandria was searched by the king's officials. If they found any books on board, they took them to the library. There, the copyists made copies of them. Then the library kept the original books, and gave the copies back to the owners. The original books were labelled: from the ships.

{img:copyist-hurrying-copy-sailors-scroll-library-desk-ship-label-evening}
There is an even more famous story about the plays of Athens. The great playwrights of Athens, Aeschylus, Sophocles and Euripides, had written wonderful plays. The city of Athens kept the official copies of these plays, very carefully. King Ptolemy the Third asked if he could borrow them, to make copies. He promised to send them back, and he left a huge sum of money in silver as a promise. Athens agreed and sent the precious scrolls.

--

But the king did not send the originals back. Instead, he had beautiful new copies made, and sent those back to Athens. He kept the originals for his library. And he told Athens that they could keep the silver. He thought the old scrolls were worth much more. Athens was not happy. But there was nothing they could do. And so the original plays of the great Athenian writers ended up in Alexandria.

{img:library-receiving-new-scrolls-crates-unpacking-scholars-excited-hall}
Theon loves this story. He thinks it is very funny that the king tricked the people of Athens. He tells it to his friends at the harbour. Kallisto says it was not very honest. Demetrios says that perhaps they are both right. It was not honest, he says, but it saved the plays. Because later, the copies in Athens were lost, and only the books in Alexandria survived. Sometimes, he says, people do the wrong thing, for a good reason. Kallisto thinks about that for a long time.

Not all the books from the ships are important. Some are just letters, or lists of goods, or little books of songs. But the copyists copy them all. The kings want everything. Who knows, they say, what might be important one day? So the library is full of all kinds of writing, great and small. And the copyists are always busy.

The great library also has a rival, in a city called Pergamon, in Asia. The kings of Pergamon are building their own library, and they want it to be as great as Alexandria's. The kings of Egypt are not pleased. People say they stopped sending papyrus to Pergamon. So the people of Pergamon began to write on animal skins instead, carefully prepared. This writing material is called parchment, after the name of their city.
