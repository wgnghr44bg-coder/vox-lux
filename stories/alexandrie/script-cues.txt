Alexandria was founded by one of the most famous men in history. His name was Alexander, and people called him Alexander the Great.

[pause]

Alexander was a young king from Macedonia, in the north of Greece. When he was only twenty years old, he became king. Then he set off with his army to the east. In just a few years, he travelled further than anyone could have imagined. He went through Turkey and Syria, Egypt and Persia, all the way to the edge of India. [long-pause]

In the year 331 before Christ, Alexander came to Egypt. He was only twenty-five years old. The Egyptians welcomed him. They were tired of being ruled by the Persians. They made Alexander their pharaoh, their king. And Alexander decided to build a new city in Egypt, on the coast of the Mediterranean Sea.

He chose a place on a narrow strip of land, between the sea and a big lake. There was a little Egyptian fishing village there, and an island just off the coast. Alexander walked over the ground and showed his builders where the streets and the walls should go. There is a famous old story about this. The builders had no chalk to mark the lines on the ground. So they used barley flour instead. Then flocks of birds came down and ate the flour. Some people thought it was a bad sign. But the wise men said it meant that the city would feed many people from all over the world.

Alexander never saw his city finished. He left Egypt soon afterwards, and continued his journey east. He passed away in Babylon, eight years later, when he was only thirty-two. But his city grew and grew. And when he was gone, his body was brought back to Alexandria. He was laid to rest in a beautiful tomb in the middle of the city. People came from everywhere to visit it.

The city was planned very carefully, by an architect called Deinocrates. The streets were laid out in a grid, straight and at right angles, like a chessboard. The main streets were very wide, wider than in any other city at that time. The city was built so that the cool north wind from the sea could blow through the streets in summer. It was a modern city, for its time.

[long-pause]

Alexander's empire did not last long after him. His generals divided it among themselves. One of them was a general called Ptolemy. He had been Alexander's friend since childhood. He took Egypt as his share. He made himself king, and Alexandria became his capital. His family, the Ptolemies, will rule Egypt for almost three hundred years. The last of them will be a famous queen called Cleopatra.

In our story, around 240 before Christ, the king of Egypt is Ptolemy the Third. He is the grandson of the first Ptolemy. He is a strong king, and a lover of books, like his father and his grandfather. He wants Alexandria to be the most learned city in the world. And he is willing to pay almost anything to get the books he wants.

Before he left Egypt, Alexander made a long journey into the desert, to a famous oracle. It was at an oasis called Siwa, far to the west. There was a temple there of the god Amun. People came from far away to ask the god questions. The old stories say that the priests greeted Alexander as the son of the god. Nobody knows what he asked, or what he was told. He never said.

The new city grew very quickly. People came from all over the Greek world to live there. They came because there was work, and land, and a new beginning. Egyptians came from the villages along the Nile. Jewish families came from Judea, and traders came from Syria and beyond. Within a few years, the little fishing village had become a busy town. And within a few more, it was a great city.

The Ptolemies are Greek kings, ruling over Egypt. And that makes them a little unusual.

[pause]

In Alexandria, the kings live like Greek kings. They speak Greek, wear Greek clothes, and have Greek friends and advisers. Their palace is like a Greek palace, with columns and gardens and fountains. But for the Egyptian people, they are pharaohs, like the kings of old. In the old Egyptian temples along the Nile, they are shown in Egyptian clothes, with Egyptian crowns, making offerings to the Egyptian gods.

[long-pause]

The Ptolemies want to be liked by both the Greeks and the Egyptians. So they build new temples to the old Egyptian gods, in the old Egyptian style. Some of the most beautiful temples in Egypt were built by them. And they also create a new god, called Serapis. He looks like a Greek god, with a beard and curly hair. But he is linked to the old Egyptian gods Osiris and Apis. Greeks and Egyptians can both worship him.

[pause]

{img:serapeum-temple-serapis-alexandria-hill-columns-worshippers-sunlight}
The great temple of Serapis stands on a hill in the Egyptian part of the city. It is called the Serapeum. It is very beautiful, with columns and courtyards and statues. Inside, there is a huge statue of the god. Later, a second library will be built there, a daughter of the great library.

[long-pause]

The kings of Alexandria are very rich. Egypt is one of the richest lands in the world. The river Nile floods every summer, and leaves rich black earth on the fields. The farmers grow huge harvests of wheat and barley. The kings sell much of this grain to other countries. They also tax everything: the land, the crops, the trade. And they use their wealth to make Alexandria magnificent.

[pause]

{img:royal-palace-alexandria-gardens-harbour-columns-fountains-peacocks}
The royal palace stands beside the harbour. It is not one building, but many. There are halls and gardens, courtyards and private harbours, where the royal ships are kept. There is a zoo, with animals from all over the world: elephants and giraffes, lions and leopards, parrots and even a polar bear, some old stories say. The queens of Alexandria are important too. Some of them rule beside their husbands, and some rule alone.

[long-pause]

The queen of Ptolemy the Third is called Berenice. There is a lovely story about her. When her husband went away to war, she was worried about him. The next morning, the hair was gone. The court astronomer said that the gods had taken it up into the sky. He pointed to a little group of faint stars. That, he said, is the hair of Berenice. Kallisto, the girl in our story, loves this story. On clear nights, she goes up onto the roof of her house with her father. He shows her the stars. But when she does, she smiles, and thinks of the queen who gave away her hair for love.

The kings of Alexandria also love great celebrations and fine things. They give splendid feasts in the palace, with music and dancing, poetry and wine. They wear robes of purple and gold. They ride in golden chariots. They want everyone to see that they are rich and powerful. But they also want to be remembered as kings of learning, who made Alexandria the brightest city in the world.

[pause]

Ordinary people in Alexandria do not see the king very often. But on festival days, he rides through the city in a great procession. The people line the streets to watch him pass. They throw flowers and cheer. The children sit on their fathers' shoulders to see better. And for a moment, the king and his people are together, in the bright sunshine of the great city.

Let us walk through the streets of Alexandria, around the year 240 before Christ.

[pause]

The city lies on a long, narrow strip of land. To the north is the Mediterranean Sea. To the south is a big lake, called Lake Mareotis. A long causeway, built of stone, runs from the city out to the island of Pharos, where the lighthouse stands. It divides the harbour into two parts, the Great Harbour to the east and a smaller harbour to the west.

The main street of the city is called the Canopic Way. It runs from east to west, right through the middle of the city. Under the colonnades, there are shops of every kind.

{img:canopic-way-colonnades-shops-chariots-people-shade-afternoon-sun}
Along the Canopic Way, there are beautiful buildings. There are temples to the Greek gods, with white marble columns. There is a great gymnasium, where young men exercise and study. There is a law court, and a market square called the agora. There are fountains and statues and gardens. And in the middle of the city, near the crossing of the two main streets, there is the tomb of Alexander the Great.

[long-pause]

The city is divided into five quarters, named after the first five letters of the Greek alphabet: Alpha, Beta, Gamma, Delta and Epsilon. The royal quarter, near the harbour, has the palace and the great library. The Jewish quarter is in the east, near the sea. Many Jewish families live there, with their own synagogues and their own leaders. The Egyptian quarter is in the west, near the old fishing village, and the temple of Serapis.

{img:egyptian-quarter-alexandria-mud-brick-houses-narrow-lanes-women-water-jars}
In the Egyptian quarter, the houses are smaller and simpler, made of mud brick. The streets are narrower. People speak Egyptian, and wear Egyptian clothes, white linen. They worship the old gods of Egypt. There are little shrines to Isis and Osiris, to Bastet the cat goddess, and to Bes, the funny little god who protects homes and children. Life here is a little like life in the old villages along the Nile.

[long-pause]

In the Greek parts of the city, the houses are bigger, made of stone and brick, with courtyards inside. People speak Greek, and wear Greek tunics and cloaks. They go to the gymnasium and the theatre. They worship Zeus and Apollo, Athena and Dionysus. But in Alexandria, everything is mixed. Greeks pray to Isis. Egyptians learn Greek. People marry each other and share their customs. It is a city where many worlds meet.

Water is very important in Alexandria, because it hardly ever rains. A canal brings fresh water from the river Nile, many kilometres away. Under the city, there are hundreds of underground tanks, called cisterns, where the water is stored. Every house draws its water from these cisterns, with buckets on ropes. The cisterns are cool and dark, with stone columns. Some of them are still there today, under the modern city.

[pause]

There is a great theatre in Alexandria too, carved into a hillside, with rows of stone seats. Thousands of people come there to watch plays and hear music. They watch the old Greek tragedies and comedies, and new plays by Alexandrian writers. They laugh at the funny plays and cry at the sad ones. The theatre is one of the favourite places of the people of the city.

[pause]

At night, the streets of Alexandria are lit by little oil lamps, hanging outside the shops and houses. Watchmen walk through the city with torches. The taverns are full of sailors and merchants, talking and laughing. Music drifts from the open doors. And the great fire of the Pharos shines over everything, like a giant star at the edge of the sea.

Let us meet the family who lives in a house near the harbour, between the Greek quarter and the Egyptian quarter.

[pause]

The father is called Demetrios. He is Greek, about forty years old. His grandfather came to Alexandria from an island in the Aegean Sea, when the city was young. Demetrios is a copyist at the great library. His job is to copy books by hand, carefully, word by word. He has beautiful handwriting and very good eyes. He is quiet and gentle, and he loves books more than anything, except his family.

[pause]

The mother is called Merit. She is Egyptian. Her family has lived in the land of Egypt for as long as anyone can remember. She sells papyrus in the market. Papyrus is the paper of the ancient world, made from the reeds of the Nile. Her brother makes it, in a village on the edge of the lake. Merit is lively and practical, with dark eyes and a quick laugh. She speaks both Greek and Egyptian.

[long-pause]

They have two children. The daughter is called Kallisto, and she is twelve years old. Her name is Greek, and it means the most beautiful. She has her mother's dark eyes and her father's love of books. She can read and write in Greek, and a little in Egyptian too. She reads everything she can find. She dreams of becoming a scholar at the library, though there are very few women scholars.

{img:girl-twelve-reading-scroll-courtyard-fig-tree-sunlight-alexandria}
The son is called Theon, and he is nine. His name is Greek too. He is cheerful and energetic, and he loves ships more than anything. He spends every free moment at the harbour, watching the ships come and go. He knows the names of many of the ships and their captains. He wants to be a sailor when he grows up, like his uncle Nebamun.

[long-pause]

The grandmother lives with them. She is Merit's mother, and her name is Tiye. She is about seventy, small and wrinkled, with bright black eyes. She grew up in a village on the Nile, far to the south. She came to Alexandria when she married, long ago. She speaks only a little Greek. She tells the children stories of the old gods of Egypt, of Isis and Osiris, of Horus the falcon and Thoth the wise ibis. She knows all the old prayers and spells for good luck.

[pause]

{img:old-egyptian-grandmother-linen-dress-telling-stories-children-lamp-evening}
Merit's brother, Uncle Nebamun, is a sailor on a big grain ship. He sails from Alexandria to Rhodes and Athens, carrying wheat from Egypt. He is away for weeks at a time. When he comes home, he brings presents and stories from faraway places. Theon adores him. He wants to sail with him one day. Nebamun laughs and says: when you are older, little one.

[long-pause]

The family is not rich, but they live comfortably. Demetrios earns a good wage at the library. Merit earns a little from selling papyrus. They have a small house with a courtyard, and enough to eat. They have two cats, as many Egyptian families do. Cats are sacred in Egypt, and they are good at keeping mice away from the papyrus. One cat is grey and called Smoke. The other is golden and called Honey.

[pause]

{img:family-courtyard-evening-supper-greek-egyptian-two-cats-lamp-fig-tree}
In the evenings, the family eats together in the courtyard, under a fig tree. There is bread and lentils, onions and cheese, fish from the harbour, and dates and figs. They speak both Greek and Egyptian at the table, mixing the two languages together. Demetrios tells them about the books he is copying. Merit tells them about the market. Theon tells them about the ships. Kallisto asks questions. And Grandmother Tiye listens, and smiles, and feeds little scraps to the cats under the table.

[pause]

On their days off, the family likes to walk along the shore together. They walk out along the long causeway toward the island of Pharos. They watch the ships and the fishermen. They buy roasted nuts from a seller on the causeway. Demetrios tells the children the names of the ships and where they come from. And Grandmother Tiye walks slowly behind, holding Merit's arm, smiling at everything.

[pause]

Demetrios and Merit met at the market, many years ago, when Demetrios came to buy papyrus. He came back the next day, and the day after that, to buy more papyrus than he needed. Merit soon understood why. They married a year later. Their wedding was half Greek and half Egyptian, with music and dancing from both. Grandmother Tiye still says it was the best wedding she ever saw.

{img:alexandria-house-courtyard-fig-tree-well-rooms-around-flat-roof-sunlight}
The family's house is in a quiet street, a few minutes' walk from the harbour. It is a simple house of whitewashed mud brick, with a flat roof.

[pause]

From the street, you see only a plain white wall with a wooden door. There are no windows on the street side, only a few small openings high up for air. That keeps the house cool in the hot summer, and private. When you open the door, you step into a little entrance, and then into the courtyard. The courtyard is the heart of the house.

[long-pause]

In the middle of the courtyard, there is a fig tree, which gives shade in summer and sweet figs in autumn. There is a stone basin with water, where the cats like to drink. There are pots of herbs and flowers: mint and basil, roses and lilies. Around the courtyard, there are the rooms of the house. There is a kitchen, a storeroom, a room for eating and talking, and bedrooms.

[pause]

{img:simple-kitchen-ancient-alexandria-clay-oven-pots-jars-onions-hanging}
The kitchen has a clay oven for baking bread, and a little hearth for cooking. There are clay pots and jars on shelves, full of oil and wine, lentils and flour, dates and salted fish. Onions and garlic hang from the ceiling. There is a big stone for grinding grain. Merit and Grandmother Tiye cook here every day. The smell of fresh bread fills the house every morning.

[long-pause]

The rooms have floors of beaten earth or tiles. There is very little furniture. There are a few wooden stools and a low table, some chests for clothes, and beds made of wood and woven cord. The beds have thin mattresses of linen stuffed with wool. In hot weather, the family often sleeps on the flat roof, under the stars, where it is cooler.

The roof is one of the best places in the house. You climb up to it on a wooden ladder from the courtyard. From the roof, you can see over the other houses to the harbour. You can see the masts of the ships and the great white lighthouse. At night, you can see the fire burning at the top of the Pharos, like a star that has come down to Earth. Theon loves to lie on the roof and watch it, until he falls asleep.

[long-pause]

In one corner of the main room, there is a little shrine. It is a small wooden box, open at the front, painted with flowers. Inside, there are little statues of the household gods. There is Bes, the funny dwarf god who protects the family and frightens away bad dreams. There is Isis, the mother goddess, holding her baby son Horus. And there is a little bronze statue of Hermes, the Greek god of travellers and messengers, for Demetrios. The family lights a little lamp in front of them every evening.

[pause]

{img:household-shrine-little-statues-bes-isis-hermes-oil-lamp-flowers}
Demetrios also has a little shelf of his own scrolls. They are his greatest treasure. There are poems by Homer, which he copied himself as a young man. There is a book of fables, and a book about the stars. He keeps them carefully in a wooden box, to protect them from the damp and the mice. In the evenings, he takes one out and reads aloud to the family. And Kallisto sits beside him, following the words with her finger.

[pause]

In summer, the family spends most of its time in the courtyard. It is the coolest place in the house, in the shade of the fig tree. Merit grinds grain and cooks there. Grandmother Tiye spins linen thread there. Demetrios reads there in the evenings. The children play there. Even the cats prefer the courtyard, lying in the cool shadow of the stone basin.

[pause]

Once a month, Merit sweeps and washes the whole house, from the roof to the courtyard. She shakes out the mats and the linen. She sweeps away the sand that blows in from the street. She washes the clay pots and the floor tiles. Kallisto and Theon help her. They complain a little, but when it is finished, the house smells fresh and clean, and everyone feels good.

[long-pause]

{img:great-library-alexandria-hall-shelves-scroll-niches-scholars-reading-columns}
In the royal quarter of Alexandria, near the palace, there is the most famous library in the ancient world. It is called the Great Library of Alexandria.

[pause]

It was started by the first King Ptolemy, about sixty years before our story. He wanted to collect all the knowledge of the world in one place. His son, Ptolemy the Second, made it much bigger. And now his grandson, Ptolemy the Third, goes on collecting. By the time of our story, the library may have hundreds of thousands of scrolls. Nobody knows exactly how many. But it is more than in any other library in the world.

[long-pause]

In those days, books do not look like books today. They are scrolls, long strips of papyrus rolled up around a wooden stick. A scroll might be several metres long when it is unrolled. To read it, you hold it in both hands. You unroll it with one hand and roll it up with the other, as you read. A long book, like a poem by Homer, needs many scrolls.

[pause]

{img:scrolls-stored-pigeonholes-wooden-shelves-labels-tags-hanging-library}
In the library, the scrolls are kept on shelves, in long rows of wooden pigeonholes. Each scroll has a little label hanging from the end, with the title and the author's name. That way, you can find a scroll without unrolling it. There are scrolls in Greek, and scrolls translated from other languages: Egyptian, Hebrew, Persian, and many more.

[long-pause]

The library has scrolls about everything. There are poems and plays, histories and stories. There are books about mathematics and geometry, about the stars and the planets. There are books about medicine and plants, about animals and rocks. There are books of law and philosophy, of geography and music. The kings want everything, every book ever written, in every language.

[pause]

{img:callimachus-scholar-making-catalogue-pinakes-tablets-scrolls-desk-lamp}
To keep track of so many scrolls, the library needs a catalogue, a list of all its books. A famous poet and scholar called Callimachus made the first great catalogue, a few years before our story. It is called the Pinakes, which means the tables. It is itself very long, more than a hundred scrolls. It lists every author, with a little note about their life, and all their books. It is one of the first library catalogues in history.

[long-pause]

Not just anyone can go into the library. It is mainly for the scholars who live and work there, and for their students. But people like Demetrios, who work there, can go in every day. And visiting scholars can come too, from all over the world, if they have permission. They come to read books that they cannot find anywhere else.

[pause]

{img:library-garden-walkway-scholars-walking-discussing-columns-shade-afternoon}
The library is not just one room. It is a group of buildings, with halls and rooms, gardens and walkways. There are rooms for reading and rooms for copying. There are rooms for storing scrolls and rooms for repairing them. There is a dining hall, where the scholars eat together. And there are shady walkways with columns, where the scholars walk up and down, talking about their ideas. It is a peaceful, quiet place, full of the soft rustle of papyrus.

[pause]

There is a famous saying about the library, written on its walls, people say. It calls the library a place of healing for the soul. Reading good books, the old Greeks believed, makes people wiser and kinder and calmer. Demetrios believes this too. He says that whenever he is sad or worried, he reads a good book. And by the end, he always feels a little better.

[pause]

Repairing old scrolls is also important work in the library. Papyrus can become brittle and break as it gets old. It can be damaged by damp or by insects. So there are workers whose job is to repair the old scrolls. They glue the broken pieces carefully together. They make new copies of the most damaged ones. And they keep the scrolls in dry, safe places, away from the damp sea air.

[long-pause]

{img:mouseion-alexandria-scholars-dining-hall-long-table-discussion-evening-lamps}
The great library is part of something even bigger. It is called the Mouseion. That means the place of the Muses, the nine goddesses of the arts and learning. From this word comes our word museum. But the Mouseion of Alexandria is not a museum as we know it. It is more like a university, a place where scholars live and work together.

[pause]

The kings of Alexandria invite the best scholars from all over the Greek world to come and live at the Mouseion. They give them a house, meals, a salary, and everything they need. The scholars do not have to pay taxes. They do not have to teach if they do not want to. They are free to spend their whole lives reading, writing and studying. It is a wonderful life for a scholar.

[long-pause]

There are about thirty or forty scholars at the Mouseion at any time. They are poets and mathematicians, doctors and astronomers, geographers and philosophers. They eat together in a great dining hall every day, and talk about their work. They argue and discuss and share their ideas. Sometimes the king himself comes to listen, and asks them questions.

[pause]

{img:astronomer-instruments-armillary-sphere-observing-stars-roof-night-alexandria}
Some of the most important ideas in history were first thought of here. A mathematician called Euclid wrote a famous book about geometry, here in Alexandria. It was used to teach mathematics for more than two thousand years. An astronomer called Aristarchus, who studied here for a time, suggested that the Earth goes around the sun, not the sun around the Earth. Most people did not believe him. But he was right.

[long-pause]

Doctors at the Mouseion studied how the human body works. They learned about the heart and the blood, the brain and the nerves. They wrote books about medicine and healing plants. An engineer called Ctesibius made clever machines that worked with water and air. He made a water clock that told the time very exactly. He made a pump, and even a kind of organ that played music with water.

[pause]

{img:ctesibius-water-clock-engineer-workshop-bronze-mechanism-water-flowing}
Poets lived at the Mouseion too. Callimachus wrote beautiful short poems. A poet called Apollonius wrote a long poem about Jason and the Argonauts, who sailed to find the Golden Fleece. And a poet called Theocritus wrote poems about shepherds and the countryside, about love and summer and music. People still read them today.

[long-pause]

Some scholars spent their lives studying the old books. They compared different copies of Homer's poems, to find out which words were really his. They wrote notes and explanations. They tried to make the most correct version of every old book. Thanks to their work, we can still read Homer today, almost as he was first written down.

[pause]

{img:scholar-comparing-two-scrolls-homer-desk-notes-quiet-reading-room-light}
Demetrios does not live at the Mouseion. He is not a famous scholar. He is a copyist, who works for the scholars. But he knows many of them. He sees them every day, walking in the gardens and reading in the halls. Sometimes they ask him to copy a special book for them. And sometimes, they stop to talk with him about the book he is copying. He likes these talks very much. He tells his family about them in the evenings.

[pause]

The scholars of the Mouseion also have a zoo and a garden of plants, for their studies. There are animals from all over the world: birds and snakes, monkeys and big cats. There are plants from India and Arabia, Africa and Greece. The scholars study them, and write books about them. The children of Alexandria love to look at the animals through the gates.

[pause]

Not everyone admired the scholars. A poet from another city made fun of them. He wrote that Egypt was feeding a lot of bookworms, who quarrelled all day long in the birdcage of the Muses. The scholars of Alexandria laughed at the joke. They did argue a lot, it is true. But their arguments helped them find out what was true.

[long-pause]

{img:papyrus-reeds-nile-marsh-tall-green-stalks-boat-cutting-morning}
Every book in the great library is written on papyrus. And all of that papyrus comes from the reeds of the river Nile.

[pause]

Papyrus is a tall plant that grows in the marshes along the Nile, and around the lakes near Alexandria. It has a long, green, three-sided stem, taller than a man, with a tuft of thin leaves at the top, like a little green firework. It grows in thick clumps in the shallow water. Birds nest among the reeds, and frogs sing there at night.

[long-pause]

Merit's other brother, Uncle Hapu, makes papyrus in a little village on the shore of Lake Mareotis, outside the city. He has a small workshop by the water, and several workers. Every morning, they go out in little boats to cut the papyrus reeds. They cut them near the bottom with sharp knives, and bundle them up. Then they carry them back to the workshop.

[pause]

{img:workers-slicing-papyrus-stalks-thin-strips-workshop-lake-shore-sunlight}
First, they peel off the green outer skin of the stalk. Inside, there is a soft white pith. They cut the pith into long, thin strips, with a sharp knife. Then they lay the strips side by side on a flat board, slightly overlapping. On top of these, they lay another layer of strips, crossways. So there are two layers, one going one way and one going the other.

[long-pause]

Then they press the layers together, by beating them gently with a wooden mallet. Or they put heavy stones on top of them. The juice of the plant is sticky, and it glues the strips together. Then the sheet is left to dry in the sun. When it is dry, it is smoothed with a stone or a piece of shell, until it is smooth and white. And now it is a sheet of papyrus, ready to write on.

[pause]

{img:papyrus-sheets-drying-sun-workshop-yard-stones-smoothing-shell}
Many sheets are glued together, side by side, to make a long roll. A roll might have twenty sheets, and be several metres long. The rolls are sold to the library, to government offices, to merchants and to schools. Papyrus from Egypt is sent all over the Mediterranean world. Everyone needs it, for letters and records and books. It is one of Egypt's most important exports.

[long-pause]

Merit sells the papyrus in a little stall in the market, near the harbour. She sells it by the sheet and by the roll. Her customers are scribes and merchants, students and officials. Sometimes the library buys a whole cartload at once. She also sells reed pens, made from thin rushes, and little cakes of ink, made from soot and gum. She knows the quality of her papyrus very well. She can tell a good sheet from a bad one just by touching it.

[pause]

{img:papyrus-stall-market-woman-selling-rolls-reed-pens-ink-customers}
Sometimes Kallisto helps her mother at the stall. She likes the smell of the fresh papyrus and the feel of it under her fingers. She likes talking to the customers, especially the students from the library. They tell her what they are studying. Once, a young student showed her a map of the world that he had drawn, on her mother's papyrus. Kallisto looked at it for a long time. She wished she could draw maps like that.

[pause]

Papyrus is used for many other things, too. The Egyptians make boats from bundles of papyrus reeds, tied together tightly. They make baskets and mats, ropes and sandals from it. They chew the sweet pith of young stems, like a snack. And they burn the dried roots as firewood. Nothing is wasted. The papyrus plant is one of the most useful plants in all of Egypt.

[pause]

In the evening, after a long day at the workshop, Uncle Hapu and his workers sit by the lake and eat their supper. They watch the sun go down over the water, turning the papyrus reeds gold. Birds fly home to their nests. Frogs begin to croak. And the workers talk quietly about the day, and about their families, and about the price of papyrus in the city.

[long-pause]

{img:copying-room-library-scribes-desks-reed-pens-ink-scrolls-quiet-morning-light}
Let us spend a day with Demetrios, at his work in the great library.

[pause]

He sets off from home early in the morning, just after sunrise. He walks through the streets to the royal quarter, past the harbour and the palace. At the gate of the library, the guard knows him and nods. He goes to the copying room, a long room with big windows that let in plenty of light. There are about twenty copyists working there, each at his own desk.

[long-pause]

Demetrios sits down at his desk. In front of him, there is the scroll he is copying, held open by little weights. Beside it, there is a new, blank roll of papyrus. He has a pot of black ink and several reed pens. He cuts the tip of a pen with a little knife, to make it sharp. Then he dips it in the ink, and begins to write.

[pause]

{img:scribe-close-hand-writing-greek-letters-reed-pen-papyrus-ink-pot}
He writes in Greek, in neat, even capital letters. In those days, words are written without any spaces between them, and with very little punctuation. He must be very careful. He reads a few words from the old scroll, and then writes them on the new one. Then a few more words. Line by line, column by column. A good copyist can copy perhaps a few hundred lines in a day.

[long-pause]

Mistakes are very easy to make. Your eyes can jump from one line to another. You can miss a word, or write a word twice. So copyists must concentrate very hard, all day long. When Demetrios makes a mistake, he can sometimes wash the ink off the papyrus with a wet sponge, while it is still fresh. When a scroll is finished, another copyist checks it against the original, word by word.

[pause]

{img:scribes-checking-copy-two-men-reading-aloud-comparing-scrolls-table}
Today, Demetrios is copying a book of poems by Sappho, a famous woman poet who lived on the island of Lesbos, long ago. Her poems are about love and friendship, flowers and the moon. They are very beautiful. Demetrios reads them as he copies them, and sometimes he smiles. He thinks he will tell Kallisto about them tonight.

[long-pause]

At midday, the copyists stop for a meal. They eat bread and cheese, olives and dried figs, in a little courtyard with a fountain. They talk about their work and the latest news from the city. Some of them are old men, who have been copying for thirty years. Some are young men, just starting. They tease each other and laugh. Then they go back to their desks for the afternoon.

[pause]

{img:copyists-lunch-courtyard-fountain-bread-olives-figs-talking-shade}
In the evening, Demetrios walks home through the busy streets. His eyes are tired, and his fingers are stained with ink. But he is content. Today he has copied three columns of Sappho's poems. In a few weeks, the whole book will be finished. A new copy, for the great library. And perhaps, he thinks, someone will read it in a hundred years, or a thousand. He likes to think of that. Then he opens the door of his house, and Honey the cat comes running to meet him.

[pause]

When a new scroll is finished, Demetrios writes his name at the very end, in small letters. It is a tradition among copyists. He also writes how many lines he has copied. Copyists are paid by the line, so this is important. Then the scroll is rolled up, and a little label is attached. And it goes onto the shelves of the great library, to wait for its first reader.

[pause]

Sometimes Demetrios takes Kallisto with him to the library, on a quiet day. She sits beside him at his desk, very still, and watches him write. She looks at the long rows of shelves, full of scrolls. She breathes in the smell of papyrus and ink. And she thinks that this must be the most wonderful place in the whole world. Her father lets her copy a few lines on a spare piece of papyrus.

[long-pause]

{img:officials-boarding-ship-harbour-searching-cargo-scrolls-alexandria-quay}
How did the library collect so many books? The kings of Alexandria had some very clever, and some rather cheeky, ways of doing it.

[pause]

First, they bought books. They sent agents all over the Greek world, to Athens and Rhodes, to Pergamon and Antioch. The agents bought every book they could find, especially old and rare ones. They paid very good prices. The kings were willing to spend a great deal of money on books. Sometimes they paid more for an old book than for a fine house.

[long-pause]

Second, they had translations made. They wanted books from every country, in Greek, so that the scholars could read them. So they invited scholars from other lands to come to Alexandria and translate their books. The most famous story is about the Jewish holy books. The old stories say that seventy-two Jewish scholars came from Jerusalem to Alexandria. They translated the books of the Hebrew Bible into Greek. This translation is called the Septuagint, which means the seventy. It is still read today.

[pause]

{img:jewish-scholars-translating-scrolls-hebrew-greek-island-house-sea-light}
An Egyptian priest called Manetho wrote a history of Egypt in Greek, for the kings. He used the old records of the Egyptian temples. He divided the kings of Egypt into groups, called dynasties. We still use his dynasties today, when we talk about the history of ancient Egypt.

[long-pause]

And third, there was the famous trick with the ships. Every ship that came into the harbour of Alexandria was searched by the king's officials. If they found any books on board, they took them to the library. There, the copyists made copies of them. Then the library kept the original books, and gave the copies back to the owners. The original books were labelled: from the ships.

[pause]

{img:copyist-hurrying-copy-sailors-scroll-library-desk-ship-label-evening}
There is an even more famous story about the plays of Athens. The great playwrights of Athens, Aeschylus, Sophocles and Euripides, had written wonderful plays. The city of Athens kept the official copies of these plays, very carefully. King Ptolemy the Third asked if he could borrow them, to make copies. He promised to send them back, and he left a huge sum of money in silver as a promise. Athens agreed and sent the precious scrolls.

[long-pause]

But the king did not send the originals back. Instead, he had beautiful new copies made, and sent those back to Athens. He kept the originals for his library. And he told Athens that they could keep the silver. He thought the old scrolls were worth much more. Athens was not happy. But there was nothing they could do. And so the original plays of the great Athenian writers ended up in Alexandria.

[pause]

{img:library-receiving-new-scrolls-crates-unpacking-scholars-excited-hall}
Theon loves this story. He thinks it is very funny that the king tricked the people of Athens. He tells it to his friends at the harbour. Kallisto says it was not very honest. Demetrios says that perhaps they are both right. It was not honest, he says, but it saved the plays. Because later, the copies in Athens were lost, and only the books in Alexandria survived. Sometimes, he says, people do the wrong thing, for a good reason. Kallisto thinks about that for a long time.

[pause]

Not all the books from the ships are important. Some are just letters, or lists of goods, or little books of songs. But the copyists copy them all. The kings want everything. Who knows, they say, what might be important one day? So the library is full of all kinds of writing, great and small. And the copyists are always busy.

[pause]

The great library also has a rival, in a city called Pergamon, in Asia. The kings of Pergamon are building their own library, and they want it to be as great as Alexandria's. The kings of Egypt are not pleased. People say they stopped sending papyrus to Pergamon. So the people of Pergamon began to write on animal skins instead, carefully prepared. This writing material is called parchment, after the name of their city.

[long-pause]

{img:pharos-lighthouse-alexandria-three-tiers-square-octagon-round-statue-top-day}
On the island at the edge of the harbour stands the great lighthouse of Alexandria, the Pharos. It is one of the seven wonders of the ancient world.

[pause]

The lighthouse was finished about forty years before our story, in the time of the second Ptolemy. It is enormous, more than a hundred metres tall. That is about as tall as a thirty-storey building. For many centuries, it will be one of the tallest buildings in the world. It is built of great blocks of white limestone and granite. It gleams in the sunlight, and it can be seen from far out at sea.

[long-pause]

The lighthouse has three parts, one on top of the other. At the bottom, there is a huge square tower, with many windows. Inside, there are rooms for the keepers and the soldiers, and storerooms for fuel. On top of that, there is a smaller tower with eight sides. And on top of that, there is a round tower, with columns. At the very top, there is a great bronze statue, perhaps of Zeus, or of Poseidon, the god of the sea.

[pause]

{img:pharos-ramp-inside-donkeys-carrying-wood-spiral-climbing-keepers}
How do they get the fuel up to the top? Inside the lighthouse, there is a great ramp, winding up and up, around the inside of the tower. Donkeys carry wood and oil up the ramp, all day long. At the top, the keepers keep a great fire burning all night. Behind the fire, there is a huge mirror of polished bronze. It reflects the light of the fire far out over the sea. Sailors say they can see it from more than fifty kilometres away.

[long-pause]

The light of the Pharos is very important. The coast of Egypt is low and flat, and hard to see from the sea. There are dangerous rocks and sandbanks near the harbour. Without the lighthouse, ships could easily miss the harbour, or run onto the rocks in the dark. The Pharos guides them safely home, every night. Sailors from all over the world love the sight of it. When they see its light, they know they are close to Alexandria.

[pause]

{img:pharos-fire-night-bright-flame-mirror-ships-approaching-dark-sea}
The lighthouse was built by an architect called Sostratus, from the city of Cnidus. There is a story about him. The king wanted only his own name carved on the lighthouse. But Sostratus wanted to be remembered too. So he carved his own name into the stone, with a message to the gods, asking them to protect the sailors. Then he covered it with plaster, and carved the king's name on the plaster. Years later, the plaster fell away, and the name of Sostratus appeared.

[long-pause]

One day, Uncle Nebamun takes Theon and Kallisto to the island of Pharos. They walk across the long causeway from the city. The lighthouse grows bigger and bigger as they come closer. At its foot, they stand with their heads tipped back, looking up. It is so tall that it seems to touch the sky. Theon's mouth is wide open. Kallisto counts the windows, and loses count.

[pause]

{img:children-uncle-foot-of-pharos-looking-up-tall-white-tower-sky-amazed}
A keeper they know lets them climb a little way up the great ramp, behind the donkeys. The walls are thick and cool. Little windows look out over the sea. They climb higher and higher, until their legs ache. From a window, they can see the whole city below them, white and shining. They can see the harbour full of ships, and the lake beyond the city, and the green land of the Nile in the distance. Theon says it is the best day of his life. He says that every time something good happens. But this time, he really means it.

[pause]

The keepers of the Pharos live on the island, with their families. They work in shifts, day and night. In the day, they clean the great mirror, carry up the fuel and look after the tower. At night, they keep the fire burning. It is hard work, and lonely sometimes. But they are proud of it. They know that many sailors' lives depend on them.

[pause]

Sailors from all over the world tell stories about the Pharos. Some say that its mirror could set enemy ships on fire, from far away. Some say you could see ships in Greece in its mirror. These stories are not true. But they show how amazing the lighthouse seemed to people. Even today, in many languages, the word for lighthouse comes from the word Pharos.

[long-pause]

{img:alexandria-dawn-mother-baking-bread-clay-oven-courtyard-grandmother-cats}
Let us spend an ordinary day with Merit and her family, in the summer.

[pause]

The day begins at sunrise. Merit gets up and lights the clay oven in the courtyard. She mixes flour and water and a little old dough, and shapes flat round loaves. She bakes them in the oven, and soon the smell of fresh bread fills the house. Grandmother Tiye sits beside her, grinding grain on a flat stone. The two cats wait hopefully nearby, in case anything falls.

[long-pause]

Breakfast is warm bread, with a little honey or cheese, and some dates. Demetrios eats quickly and goes off to the library. Theon goes off to school, a little school run by a Greek teacher in the next street. Kallisto has finished school now, so she helps her mother. Together, they take the papyrus to the stall in the market, in a basket on Merit's head.

[pause]

{img:market-alexandria-stalls-fish-fruit-papyrus-mother-daughter-morning-busy}
The market near the harbour is busy and noisy. There are stalls selling fish and fruit, vegetables and bread. There are stalls selling cloth and pots, spices and perfumes. There are sellers of little lucky charms and statues of the gods. There are storytellers and musicians, jugglers and snake charmers. People shout and bargain in many languages. Merit sets up her stall and starts to sell.

[long-pause]

Kallisto helps her mother all morning. She counts the sheets of papyrus and takes the coins. She talks to the customers in Greek and in Egyptian. One of them is an old scholar from the library, who buys papyrus every week. He has a long white beard and a kind face. He asks Kallisto what she is reading these days. She tells him about Homer, and he smiles and nods. He tells her to keep reading, always.

[pause]

{img:old-scholar-buying-papyrus-talking-with-girl-stall-kind-smile}
At midday, it is very hot. Merit closes the stall, and they go home. The family eats lunch in the shade of the courtyard: bread, lentils, onions and cucumbers. Then everyone rests during the hottest part of the day. Grandmother Tiye sleeps on her bed. Kallisto reads in the shade of the fig tree. The cats sleep in the coolest corner. The whole city is quiet in the afternoon heat.

[long-pause]

In the late afternoon, when it is cooler, Kallisto and Theon go down to the harbour. Theon wants to see the ships, of course. Kallisto wants to sit on the harbour wall and read. They watch the big grain ships being loaded with wheat, and the fishing boats coming in. They watch the sun go down behind the lighthouse. The water turns gold and red. The sea breeze is cool and fresh.

[pause]

{img:brother-sister-harbour-wall-sunset-pharos-silhouette-ships-golden}
As the sun sets, they see the keepers lighting the great fire at the top of the Pharos. First a small light, then bigger and brighter, until it shines out over the darkening sea. Theon and Kallisto watch it for a while. Then they walk home through the evening streets. The lamps are being lit in the houses. The smell of cooking drifts from the windows. And at home, their mother is waiting with supper, under the fig tree.

[pause]

In the market, there are also little stalls selling food that you can eat as you walk. There are grilled fish on sticks and little meat pies. There are sweet cakes made with honey and sesame seeds. There are fresh dates and figs and slices of melon. Kallisto and Theon always beg their mother for a honey cake, and sometimes she says yes.

[pause]

Before bed, Grandmother Tiye tells the children a story. Tonight, it is the story of the cat goddess Bastet, who protects homes and families. She tells them that cats are sacred because Bastet loves them. And that is why, she says, you must always be kind to Honey and Smoke. The cats, curled up on the end of the bed, seem to agree. And the children fall asleep, listening to the cats purring.

[long-pause]

{img:great-harbour-alexandria-grain-ships-loading-sacks-porters-quay-sunny}
The harbour of Alexandria is one of the busiest in the whole world. Ships come and go every day, from every port of the Mediterranean Sea.

[pause]

The most important ships are the grain ships. They are big and wide, with one tall mast and a great square sail. They carry wheat from Egypt to other countries: to Greece, to Rhodes, to the islands and cities all around the sea. Egypt grows more wheat than any other land. The grain ships carry it away, and bring back gold and silver, wine and oil, wood and metals.

[long-pause]

On the quays, there are always crowds of workers. Porters carry heavy sacks of grain on their backs, up the gangplanks onto the ships. Others roll barrels and carry big clay jars, called amphorae, full of wine and oil. Scribes sit at little tables, writing down every sack and every jar. Officials of the king check everything and collect the taxes. It is noisy and dusty and busy, from dawn until dusk.

[pause]

{img:amphorae-stacked-quay-wine-oil-merchants-scribes-tables-harbour}
Many other goods come into Alexandria too. There is wood from Lebanon, for building ships and houses, because Egypt has very few trees. There is silver and tin from Spain. There is marble from the Greek islands. There are horses from Libya. And from the south and the east, by the Red Sea, come incense and spices, ivory and ebony, gold and precious stones from Africa and India.

[long-pause]

Alexandria is also a city of shipbuilders. In the shipyards near the harbour, carpenters build big ships and small boats. The kings of Alexandria build enormous warships, bigger than any others. A few years after our story, a king called Ptolemy the Fourth will build one with forty rows of oars, and thousands of rowers. It will be so big that it can hardly move. It will be more for show than for use. But people will come from everywhere to look at it.

[pause]

{img:shipyard-alexandria-wooden-hull-ribs-carpenters-working-timber-cedar}
Theon spends as much time at the harbour as he can. He knows many of the sailors and the porters. He runs errands for them, and they give him little coins or pieces of fruit. He watches the ships coming in and going out. He can tell where a ship comes from by the shape of its sails and the colour of its hull. He dreams of the day when he will sail on one of them.

[long-pause]

Uncle Nebamun's ship is called the Isis. It is a big grain ship, with a painted eye on each side of the front, to help it see its way across the sea. Its sail is decorated with a picture of the goddess Isis, the protector of sailors. When the Isis is in the harbour, Theon goes on board to see his uncle. The sailors let him climb a little way up the mast. They teach him to tie knots and to coil ropes.

[pause]

{img:grain-ship-isis-painted-eye-bow-sailors-boy-climbing-mast-harbour}
Before each voyage, the sailors of the Isis make an offering to the goddess. They pour a little wine into the sea, and burn some incense on a little altar on the ship. They pray for good winds and calm seas. Then they raise the anchor and the great sail. And the ship sails slowly out of the harbour, past the white lighthouse, into the open sea. Theon waves from the harbour wall until the ship is only a little speck on the horizon.

[pause]

In the evenings, the sailors gather in the taverns near the harbour. They eat fish stew and bread, and drink wine. They tell stories of their voyages, of storms and pirates, faraway ports and strange customs. They sing songs in many languages. Theon is not allowed in the taverns, because he is too young. But sometimes he sits outside the door and listens to the stories.

[long-pause]

{img:nile-river-egypt-palm-trees-fields-feluccas-villages-golden-afternoon}
Just outside Alexandria, beyond the lake, lies the land of the river Nile. And without the Nile, there would be no Egypt at all.

[pause]

The Nile is the longest river in the world. It flows from deep in Africa, through the desert, all the way north to the Mediterranean Sea. On both sides of the river, there is a narrow strip of green land. Beyond it, there is only desert, golden and empty. Almost all the people of Egypt live on that narrow green strip. The Egyptians call it the black land, because of its rich dark soil. And they call the desert the red land.

[long-pause]

Every summer, something wonderful happens. Far away in Africa, the rains fall on the mountains. The river begins to rise. It rises and rises, until it floods over its banks, and covers the fields with water. For a few weeks, the whole valley is like a great lake, with the villages standing on little hills like islands. Then the water goes down again. And it leaves behind a layer of rich black mud, which makes the fields very fertile.

[pause]

{img:nile-flood-fields-covered-water-villages-islands-palm-trees-boats}
After the flood, the farmers plant their crops in the wet black earth. They grow wheat and barley, beans and lentils, onions and garlic, flax for linen. The crops grow quickly in the warm sun. In spring, there is a great harvest. The farmers cut the wheat with sickles, and thresh it with oxen. Most of it goes to the king, as tax. Some of it is sent down the river to Alexandria, and then by ship all over the world.

[long-pause]

Grandmother Tiye grew up in a village on the Nile, far to the south. She tells the children about her life there. She tells them about the mud-brick houses and the palm trees, the water buffalo and the geese. She tells them about the flood, and the festival when the water rose. She tells them about the great temples with their painted walls, and the old gods of Egypt. Her eyes shine when she talks about it.

[pause]

{img:egyptian-village-nile-mud-brick-houses-water-buffalo-women-river-washing}
The Egyptians say that the flood comes from the tears of the goddess Isis. She weeps for her husband Osiris, who was taken from her long ago. Her tears fill the river and make it rise. And Osiris is the god of the flood and of the growing grain. Every year, when the river rises and the crops grow, he comes back to life. Tiye tells the children this story every summer, when the news of the flood reaches Alexandria.

[long-pause]

A canal links Alexandria to the Nile. Boats travel along it, carrying grain and goods to the city. Once, Merit takes the children on a little journey along the canal, to visit relatives in a village near the river. They travel in a small boat with a big triangular sail, called a felucca. The boat glides along the green canal, between palm trees and fields. Herons and ibis stand in the water. Children wave from the banks.

[pause]

{img:felucca-sailing-canal-palm-trees-family-on-board-herons-calm-water}
In the village, the children see the old Egyptian life that their grandmother remembers. They see women carrying water jars on their heads, and men working in the fields with oxen. They see children swimming in the canal, and goats wandering in the lanes. They eat bread and onions and fresh fish, cooked over a fire. And they sleep on the roof of their relatives' house, under a sky full of stars. Kallisto writes it all down in her little notebook, so she will never forget.

[pause]

The farmers of the Nile use a clever machine to lift water from the river into their fields. It is called a shaduf. It is a long wooden pole, balanced on a post, with a bucket on one end and a heavy stone on the other. The farmer pulls the bucket down into the river, fills it, and then lets the stone lift it up. Then he empties the water into a little channel that runs into his field.

[pause]

At the end of their visit to the village, the relatives give the family presents to take home. There is a basket of fresh dates, a jar of honey and a little linen cloth woven by a cousin. Grandmother Tiye cries a little when they leave. She has not been back to the river for many years. But she is happy too. She has seen the Nile again, and shown it to her grandchildren.

[long-pause]

{img:greek-school-alexandria-boys-wax-tablets-teacher-reading-homer-courtyard}
What is life like for children in Alexandria, around 240 before Christ?

[pause]

Children of Greek families often go to school, from about the age of seven. Boys especially, but sometimes girls too. They go to a little school run by a teacher, called a grammatistes, a teacher of letters. They learn to read and write Greek. They write on wax tablets with a pointed stick, or on broken pieces of pottery with ink. Later, they read the poems of Homer and learn them by heart.

[long-pause]

Theon goes to a little school in the next street. There are about fifteen boys there, of different ages. The teacher is a thin old man with a long beard and a stick. He is strict, but fair. The boys sit on benches and copy the letters he writes. They recite poems and practise counting. Theon is not very good at sitting still. He would rather be at the harbour. But he is good at counting, and he likes stories about the sea.

[pause]

{img:children-playing-knucklebones-courtyard-alexandria-afternoon-shade-laughing}
Children in Alexandria also love to play. They play with balls and hoops, and with spinning tops. They play a game with little bones from the ankles of sheep, called knucklebones, throwing and catching them. They play tag and hide and seek in the streets. Girls play with dolls made of clay and cloth, with little arms and legs that move. Children also keep pets: cats and dogs, birds and even monkeys.

[long-pause]

Egyptian children learn mostly at home, from their parents. Boys learn their father's work, and girls learn their mother's. Some Egyptian boys go to schools at the temples, where they learn the old Egyptian writing, hieroglyphs. It is very difficult, with hundreds of different signs. It takes many years to learn. The priests teach it, so that the old prayers and stories will never be forgotten.

[pause]

{img:grandmother-teaching-girl-hieroglyphs-drawing-in-sand-courtyard-evening}
Grandmother Tiye cannot read or write. But she knows some of the old hieroglyphs, from the temples of her childhood. She draws them for Kallisto in the sand of the courtyard, with a stick. This one is an eye, she says, and this one is a bird. This one is water, and this one is the sun. Kallisto copies them carefully. She loves the little pictures. She thinks they are much more beautiful than Greek letters.

[long-pause]

Kallisto has a best friend called Miriam, who lives in the Jewish quarter. Her father is a merchant who buys papyrus from Merit. The two girls met at the market, when they were small. Now they meet whenever they can. They walk along the beach and collect shells. They sit in the shade and read to each other. Miriam reads stories from her people's holy books, in Greek. Kallisto reads Homer. They tell each other about their families and their festivals.

[pause]

{img:two-girls-friends-walking-beach-collecting-shells-alexandria-sea-sunset}
On hot summer days, the children swim in the sea. There are little beaches along the coast, with soft sand and calm water. The children splash and dive and float on their backs, looking up at the blue sky. Theon is a very good swimmer. He says he will need to be, when he is a sailor. Kallisto prefers to sit on the sand with a scroll. But even she cannot resist the cool water, on the hottest days.

[pause]

The children of Alexandria also love the animals of the city. There are cats everywhere, sleeping in the sun and hunting mice. There are dogs in the streets, and donkeys carrying loads. There are birds in cages in the market, singing. And there are the animals of the royal zoo, which children can sometimes glimpse through the gates. Theon once saw an elephant there, and he talked about it for weeks.

[pause]

In the evenings, the children of the street often gather in someone's courtyard to play games and tell stories. They tell ghost stories and funny stories, stories of heroes and monsters. They ask each other riddles. Theon is very good at riddles. His favourite is an old one about a creature that walks on four legs in the morning, two at noon, and three in the evening. The answer is a person: a baby, a grown-up, and an old person with a stick.

[long-pause]

{img:eratosthenes-scholar-library-alexandria-older-man-scrolls-measuring-instruments}
Around the time of our story, a new head librarian comes to the great library of Alexandria. His name is Eratosthenes. And he is going to do something amazing.

[pause]

Eratosthenes comes from the city of Cyrene, to the west of Egypt. He is about thirty-five years old. He has studied in Athens, and he knows a great deal about almost everything. He is a mathematician and a geographer, a poet and an astronomer, a historian and a philosopher. Some people tease him, and call him Beta, the second letter. They say he is second best at everything. But in truth, he is one of the cleverest people of his time.

[long-pause]

Eratosthenes knows that the Earth is round, like a ball. Many Greek scholars already believe this. They have seen that ships disappear over the horizon from the bottom up. They have seen that the shadow of the Earth on the moon, during an eclipse, is always round. But nobody knows how big the Earth is. Eratosthenes decides to find out.

[pause]

{img:deep-well-syene-sunlight-reaching-bottom-noon-summer-reflection-water}
He has read in a book, in the library, about a town far to the south of Egypt, called Syene. Today it is called Aswan. In Syene, there is a deep well. And on the longest day of the year, in summer, at exactly midday, the sun shines straight down into the well, all the way to the bottom. That means the sun is directly overhead. There are no shadows at all.

[long-pause]

But in Alexandria, on the same day, at the same time, things are different. Eratosthenes puts a tall stick in the ground, standing perfectly straight. At midday, the stick has a short shadow. So in Alexandria, the sun is not directly overhead. It is a little to one side. Eratosthenes measures the angle of the shadow very carefully. It is about one fiftieth of a full circle.

[pause]

{img:eratosthenes-measuring-shadow-stick-noon-alexandria-courtyard-helpers}
Eratosthenes thinks: the Earth is round. The sun is so far away that its rays come in straight lines, all parallel. So the difference in the shadows must be because of the curve of the Earth. If the angle is one fiftieth of a circle, then the distance from Alexandria to Syene must be one fiftieth of the way around the whole Earth. So all he needs to know is the distance from Alexandria to Syene.

[long-pause]

That distance is known, because the king's officials have measured it. They used men called bematists, who were trained to walk with very even steps, counting them as they went. The distance is about five thousand stadia, the Greek unit of measurement. So Eratosthenes multiplies five thousand by fifty. And he gets about two hundred and fifty thousand stadia. That, he says, is the distance all the way around the Earth.

[pause]

{img:globe-earth-diagram-two-cities-sun-rays-angle-papyrus-drawing-lamp}
Today, we know that the Earth is about forty thousand kilometres around. And Eratosthenes' answer, as far as we can tell, was very close. Perhaps only a little bit off. With just a stick, a shadow, and his clever mind, he measured the whole world. More than two thousand years ago.

[pause]

When Demetrios hears about it at the library, he comes home and tells the family. He puts a stick in the courtyard and shows the children the shadow. Kallisto understands at once. Her eyes go wide. He measured the whole Earth, she says, with a shadow? Demetrios nods. Kallisto goes out to the courtyard and stands next to the stick for a long time, looking at its shadow. She has never felt so excited in her life.

[pause]

Eratosthenes also drew a map of the whole known world. He used reports from travellers, sailors and soldiers. He drew lines across the map, from east to west and from north to south, to help find places. These lines were an early form of the lines of latitude and longitude that we still use on maps today. He was one of the first true geographers.

[pause]

Eratosthenes also found a clever way to find prime numbers. These are numbers that can only be divided by one and by themselves, like two, three, five and seven. His method is called the sieve of Eratosthenes. You write down all the numbers, and then cross out the ones that are not prime, step by step. Schoolchildren still learn it today, more than two thousand years later.

[long-pause]

{img:alexandria-autumn-storm-clouds-gathering-sea-grey-waves-harbour-evening}
In the autumn, the sailing season is coming to an end. Soon the winter storms will come, and the ships will stay in the harbour until spring. Uncle Nebamun's ship, the Isis, is on its last voyage of the year. It has gone to Rhodes with a cargo of wheat. It should be home any day now.

[pause]

The family waits for it. Every day, Theon goes to the harbour and watches the sea. Every evening, he comes home and shakes his head. Not yet. Merit says a prayer to Isis every morning and evening, at the little shrine. Grandmother Tiye says that Nebamun has always come home safely, and he will this time too.

[long-pause]

Then one afternoon, the weather changes. Dark clouds pile up in the north, over the sea. The wind rises, and turns cold. The sea turns from blue to grey. Great waves begin to roll in toward the coast, crashing against the rocks and the harbour walls. The fishing boats hurry back into the harbour. The sailors tie up their ships tightly, with extra ropes. A storm is coming.

[pause]

{img:waves-crashing-harbour-wall-storm-spray-dark-clouds-pharos-standing}
Theon runs home from the harbour, wet with spray. He says there is no sign of the Isis yet. The family looks at each other. Nobody says anything. Merit lights an extra lamp at the shrine. Grandmother Tiye sits down beside it and begins to pray softly in Egyptian. Demetrios puts his arm around Kallisto.

[long-pause]

As night falls, the storm grows stronger. The wind howls through the streets of the city. Rain beats against the walls and the shutters, which is very rare in Alexandria. The sea roars against the rocks. The family climbs up to the roof, wrapped in cloaks, to look out at the sea. It is very dark. But out at the edge of the harbour, the great fire of the Pharos is burning, bright and steady, in the wind and the rain.

[pause]

{img:pharos-fire-blazing-storm-night-rain-ship-lights-approaching-waves}
The keepers of the lighthouse are working hard tonight. They feed the fire with wood and oil, again and again. They keep it burning as brightly as they can. Its light shines out across the stormy sea, far into the darkness. Any ship out there, struggling in the storm, will see it. And it will know where the harbour is, and where the dangerous rocks are.

[long-pause]

The family stays on the roof for a long time, watching the light. Then, late in the night, Theon shouts and points. Far out in the darkness, there is a tiny light, moving slowly toward the harbour. It is a ship's lantern. A ship is coming in, guided by the Pharos. Slowly, very slowly, it comes closer. It passes the lighthouse, and enters the harbour. And in the light of the great fire, they can see its sail. It has a picture of the goddess Isis on it.

[pause]

{img:family-running-harbour-night-storm-easing-ship-isis-tied-up-sailors-lanterns}
The whole family runs down to the harbour, through the wet streets. The storm is easing a little now. The ship is tied up at the quay, battered but safe. The sailors are coming ashore, tired and soaked. And there is Uncle Nebamun, with water dripping from his beard, smiling widely. Merit runs to him and hugs him. Theon hugs his legs. Grandmother Tiye cries and laughs at the same time. Nebamun looks up at the great white tower, with its fire still burning. Without the Pharos, he says quietly, we would never have found our way. And he bows his head toward the light.

[pause]

The next day, the sun comes out again, and the sea is calm. The family has a feast to welcome Nebamun home. Merit cooks a big fish stew, with onions and herbs. Grandmother Tiye makes honey cakes. Nebamun tells them all about the storm, and about Rhodes and its great harbour. He has brought presents: a little bronze bracelet for Kallisto, and a toy ship carved from wood for Theon.

[long-pause]

{img:festival-procession-alexandria-canopic-way-floats-music-crowds-flowers-banners}
Alexandria is a city of festivals. There are Greek festivals and Egyptian festivals, Jewish festivals and royal festivals. Some of them are among the most magnificent in the whole world.

[pause]

The greatest festival of all is the Ptolemaieia, held every four years in honour of the royal family. A writer described one of these festivals, from about forty years before our story. There was a great procession through the city that lasted a whole day. There were hundreds of people in costumes, playing music and dancing. There were huge floats, carried on wheels.

[long-pause]

One float carried a statue of the god Dionysus, the god of wine, five metres tall. Another carried a giant wine press, with men treading grapes, and wine running into the street for everyone. There were golden thrones and golden crowns, silver bowls and jewels. There were animals from all over the world: elephants, camels, ostriches, lions, giraffes and even a rhinoceros. People could hardly believe their eyes.

[pause]

{img:elephants-camels-giraffe-procession-street-alexandria-crowds-amazed-ancient}
The Egyptian festivals are different, but just as lovely. There are festivals for Isis and Osiris, for Serapis and for the Nile. At the festival of the Nile flood, people go down to the water's edge. They throw flowers and gifts into the river, and sing songs of thanks. At night, there are lamps everywhere, along the river and the canals. The water glitters with thousands of little lights.

[long-pause]

One of the most beautiful festivals is the festival of the lamps. On that night, every family in the Egyptian quarter puts little oil lamps outside their houses, all night long. The streets glow with thousands of small flames. People walk through the streets in the warm night, visiting friends and family. Children carry little lamps of their own. It is quiet and peaceful and very beautiful.

[pause]

{img:festival-of-lamps-egyptian-quarter-night-small-oil-lamps-doorways-families}
The family celebrates both Greek and Egyptian festivals. For the Greek festivals, Demetrios takes them to the theatre, to see plays and hear music. For the Egyptian festivals, Merit and Grandmother Tiye take them to the temple of Serapis, and to the little Egyptian shrines. The children love all of them. They say they are lucky to have twice as many festivals as other children.

[long-pause]

On the night of the festival of the lamps, the family puts out little lamps all along the front of their house. Grandmother Tiye lights them, one by one, saying a little prayer for each. Then they walk through the glowing streets together. They visit Uncle Hapu's family and Uncle Nebamun. They eat honey cakes and sweet dates. They listen to musicians playing harps and flutes on the street corners.

[pause]

{img:family-walking-glowing-lamp-street-night-grandmother-children-holding-hands}
Late at night, they come home and sit on the roof. Below them, the whole Egyptian quarter is glowing with little lights, like a sea of stars. Above them, the real stars are shining. And far away, at the edge of the harbour, the great fire of the Pharos burns brightest of all. Grandmother Tiye says that on this night, the lights of the Earth and the lights of the sky meet. Kallisto leans against her and watches, until her eyes close.

[pause]

The Jewish families of Alexandria have their own festivals too. Miriam invites Kallisto to her home for the festival of lights, in the winter. Miriam's family lights little lamps, one more each night, for eight nights. They sing songs and eat special food, fried in oil. Kallisto thinks it is very beautiful. She tells her family about it, and Grandmother Tiye says that light is precious in every faith.

[pause]

At the Greek festivals, there are also contests of music and poetry. Poets read their new poems aloud, and musicians play the lyre and the flute. The best of them win crowns of leaves and prizes of silver. Demetrios takes Kallisto to hear the poets one year. She listens to every word, with her eyes shining. On the way home, she says she wants to write poems too.

[long-pause]

{img:cleopatra-queen-alexandria-palace-scrolls-harbour-view-50bc-later}
Alexandria remained a great city for many hundreds of years after our story.

[pause]

The Ptolemies ruled Egypt for about two hundred more years. The last of them was a famous queen called Cleopatra. She was clever and learned, and she spoke many languages. She was the first of her family to learn to speak Egyptian. She loved Alexandria and its library. But in her time, the great power of Rome was growing. And after Cleopatra, Egypt became part of the Roman Empire.

[long-pause]

Under the Romans, Alexandria remained a great city of trade and learning. The library and the Mouseion went on for a long time. Scholars still came from all over the world to study there. A famous astronomer called Ptolemy, not one of the kings, wrote a great book about the stars here. A woman scholar called Hypatia taught mathematics and philosophy here, many centuries after our story. She was one of the most famous teachers of her time.

[pause]

{img:hypatia-teaching-students-alexandria-scrolls-astrolabe-late-roman-later}
What happened to the great library? Nobody knows exactly. There are many stories. Part of it may have been damaged by fire, during a war in the time of Julius Caesar. Over the centuries, there were other troubles and changes. The kings and emperors who came later did not care as much for the library. Less money was spent on it. Fewer scholars came. Scrolls grew old and were not copied again. Slowly, over hundreds of years, the great library faded away.

[long-pause]

But much of what was in the library was not lost. Many of the books had been copied, again and again, and sent to other libraries and other cities. Scholars in other places kept them and copied them in their turn. Later, scholars in Baghdad and in Constantinople, and in the monasteries of Europe, kept copying the old books. Thanks to them, we can still read Homer and Euclid, Sappho and many others today.

[pause]

{img:medieval-scholars-copying-old-greek-books-baghdad-library-lamps-later}
The great lighthouse stood for more than a thousand years. Sailors went on being guided home by its light, for century after century. But Egypt sometimes has earthquakes. Over the years, earthquakes damaged the lighthouse, little by little. In the end, about seven hundred years ago, it fell. Later, a fortress was built on the same spot, using some of its stones. That fortress still stands there today.

[long-pause]

What about the family? We can only imagine. Perhaps Theon became a sailor, and later a captain, sailing to Rhodes and Athens and beyond. Perhaps he always looked for the light of the Pharos, when he came home. Perhaps Demetrios went on copying books for the rest of his life, and some of his copies were read a thousand years later.

[pause]

{img:older-woman-kallisto-teaching-children-shadow-stick-courtyard-alexandria-later}
And perhaps Kallisto became a teacher. Perhaps she taught the children of Alexandria to read Homer, and to count the stars, and to draw maps of the world. And perhaps, every summer, on the longest day, she took them out into a courtyard at midday. She put a stick in the ground, and showed them its shadow. And she told them about a clever man who once measured the whole Earth, with nothing but a stick and a shadow, and his own mind.

[pause]

Alexandria also became one of the great centres of early Christianity. Famous teachers and scholars lived there, and wrote many books. Later still, it became part of the lands of Islam, and Arab scholars studied there too. Through all these changes, the city remained a place where different peoples and ideas met, by the edge of the sea.

[pause]

The name of Alexandria was given to many other cities too. Alexander the Great founded more than a dozen cities called Alexandria, across his empire, from Egypt to India. Some of them have disappeared. But some are still there today, with new names. And in many languages, the name Alexander is still very common, all over the world.

[long-pause]

{img:divers-underwater-alexandria-harbour-ancient-stones-statues-sphinx-light-rays-today}
In our own time, divers and archaeologists have found remains of ancient Alexandria, under the sea.

[pause]

Over the centuries, the coast of Alexandria has sunk a little. Earthquakes and the rising sea covered parts of the old city with water. The royal quarter, with the palace, is now partly under the sea. So is part of the island of Pharos. For many years, nobody knew what was down there. Then, about thirty years ago, divers began to explore.

[long-pause]

What they found was amazing. In the shallow water near the old harbour, there are thousands of great stone blocks, columns and statues. Some of them are very big. There are sphinxes, with the bodies of lions and the heads of kings. There are statues of kings and queens, dressed as pharaohs. There are columns of granite and pieces of great buildings. Some of the stones may have come from the great lighthouse, after it fell.

[pause]

{img:giant-statue-lifted-from-sea-crane-boat-divers-alexandria-harbour-today}
Some of the statues have been lifted out of the sea, very carefully, with big cranes. They are cleaned and studied, and put in museums. One of them is a huge statue of a king, as tall as a two-storey house. Others have been left under the water, where divers can visit them. It is like an underwater museum. Fish swim around the old sphinxes, and seaweed grows on the columns.

[long-pause]

On land, too, archaeologists have found traces of the old city. Under the modern streets, they have found the old cisterns, the underground water tanks. Some of them are still there, with their stone columns and arches. They have found pieces of old houses and floors with mosaics, pictures made of little stones. They have found a lecture hall, with rows of stone seats, where students once listened to their teachers.

[pause]

{img:ancient-lecture-halls-kom-el-dikka-stone-seats-rows-ruins-sunlight-today}
These lecture halls are at a place called Kom el-Dikka, in the middle of the modern city. There are about twenty of them, side by side. They are small, with seats in a horseshoe shape around a raised seat for the teacher. They are from a later time than our story, from the Roman period. But they show that Alexandria remained a city of learning for many centuries.

[long-pause]

Archaeologists have also found many papyrus scrolls, though not in Alexandria itself. The ground in Alexandria is too damp, and the old papyri have rotted away. But in the dry sands of the Egyptian desert, further south, papyri have survived. In one old town, people found thousands of them, in the rubbish dumps of the town. There are letters and lists, shopping notes and school exercises. There are pieces of old books, poems and plays. Some of them are books that were thought to be lost forever.

[pause]

{img:papyrus-fragments-museum-case-greek-writing-old-faded-careful-light-today}
Reading these old papyri, we can hear the voices of ordinary people from long ago. A boy writes to his father, asking him to send a present. A woman writes a list of things to buy at the market. A soldier writes home to his mother, to say that he is well. A schoolchild practises writing letters, again and again. These little papers, saved by the dry desert sand, bring the people of ancient Egypt very close to us.

[pause]

Archaeologists also use modern tools to study ancient Alexandria. They use special machines that can see under the sea floor and under the ground. They make maps of the old harbours and the lost buildings. They study the old stones, to find out where they came from. Bit by bit, they are drawing a picture of the great city as it once was.

[pause]

One of the most exciting finds in the dry sands of Egypt was a piece of an old library catalogue. It listed books and their authors, just like the Pinakes of Callimachus. Another find was a page from a lost book about the history of Athens. Every few years, new papyri are found, read and studied. And each one tells us a little more about the world of ancient Egypt and its great city by the sea.

[long-pause]

{img:bibliotheca-alexandrina-today-tilted-disc-roof-sea-corniche-sunny-today}
Today, Alexandria is still a great city, the second biggest in Egypt. More than five million people live there. It stretches along the coast of the Mediterranean Sea, for many kilometres.

[pause]

Along the sea, there is a long, curving road, called the Corniche. In the evenings, people walk along it, enjoying the cool breeze from the sea. Families sit on the sea wall and eat ice cream and roasted corn. Fishermen cast their lines into the water. Little boats bob in the harbour. And the sun sets over the sea, just as it did in the time of Kallisto and Theon.

[long-pause]

On the Corniche, near where the old royal quarter used to be, there is a remarkable modern building. It is called the Bibliotheca Alexandrina, the new library of Alexandria. It was opened about twenty-five years ago. It was built to remember the great ancient library, and to carry on its spirit. It is a library for the whole world.

[pause]

{img:bibliotheca-alexandrina-interior-reading-room-terraces-light-readers-today}
The building is shaped like a giant tilted disc, rising out of the ground, facing the sea. It is meant to look like the sun rising. On its outside walls, there are letters and signs from all the alphabets of the world, carved into the grey granite. Inside, there is a huge reading room, on many levels, with light coming in through the roof. There is room for millions of books. There are museums and a planetarium, where people can learn about the stars.

[long-pause]

At the end of the harbour, where the Pharos once stood, there is now an old stone fortress. It is called the Citadel of Qaitbay. It was built about five hundred years ago, by a sultan of Egypt, partly from the stones of the fallen lighthouse. It is a beautiful fortress, with thick walls and towers, standing on the rocks at the edge of the sea. Visitors can walk through it and look out over the harbour, as the keepers of the Pharos once did.

[pause]

{img:citadel-qaitbay-harbour-sunset-fishing-boats-seagulls-calm-water-today}
In the city, there are still traces of the old Alexandria. There is a tall column of red granite, standing on a hill, called Pompey's Pillar. It was put up by the Romans, near the place where the temple of Serapis once stood. There are underground tombs, carved into the rock, with a mix of Greek and Egyptian decorations. And there is a museum full of statues, coins and objects from the city's long history.

[long-pause]

Alexandria is still a city of many peoples and many cultures. Over the centuries, people from all around the Mediterranean have lived here: Greeks and Italians, Armenians and Jews, Lebanese and many others. Many writers and poets have loved the city, and written about it. It is still a city by the sea, a city of trade and ships, and a city of books and learning.

[pause]

{img:alexandria-corniche-evening-families-walking-sea-wall-lights-sunset-today}
In the evenings, the people of Alexandria still love to sit by the sea. They drink tea in the cafés along the Corniche, and talk with their friends. Children play on the beach. Young people walk along the sea wall, laughing. The lights of the city come on, one by one, reflected in the dark water. And out at the edge of the harbour, the old fortress stands quietly in the dusk, on the place where the great light once guided the ships home.

[pause]

The new library also holds old manuscripts and rare books, from many centuries. Scholars come from all over the world to study them. There are special rooms where old papers are repaired and preserved, just as the ancient scrolls were long ago. And there are rooms full of computers, where people can read books from libraries all around the world. The spirit of the old library lives on.

[pause]

In the old markets of Alexandria, there are still stalls selling spices and fish, fruit and bread. Sellers still call out to passers-by. People still bargain and laugh and talk in the busy streets. And in the little cafés, old men still sit and play games and drink tea, watching the world go by, just as people did in the time of Merit and Kallisto.

[long-pause]

{img:alexandria-evening-240bc-sunset-harbour-pharos-fire-lit-ships-golden-sea}
So we leave Alexandria tonight, around the year 240 before Christ, as the evening falls.

[pause]

The sun is setting over the sea, to the west. The sky turns gold, then pink, then a deep soft blue. The white buildings of the city glow warm in the last light. The ships in the harbour rock gently at anchor, their sails rolled up for the night. Seagulls settle on the masts. And on the island at the edge of the harbour, the keepers are lighting the great fire at the top of the Pharos.

[long-pause]

In the great library, the copyists have gone home. The reading rooms are quiet and dim. The scrolls rest on their shelves, in their thousands, with their little labels hanging down. A last scholar walks slowly along the colonnade, deep in thought. And in the garden, the fountain splashes softly in the evening air.

[pause]

{img:library-colonnade-dusk-scholar-walking-alone-scrolls-shelves-quiet-lamps}
In the family's house near the harbour, the lamps are lit in the courtyard. Merit is clearing away the supper, under the fig tree. Grandmother Tiye is sitting by the little shrine, saying her evening prayers in Egyptian. The two cats are curled up together on a mat. And Demetrios is sitting on a stool, with a scroll in his lap, reading aloud from the poems of Homer.

[long-pause]

Theon is lying on a mat, half asleep, listening to the story of Odysseus and his long voyage home across the sea. He dreams of ships and islands and faraway shores. Kallisto sits beside her father, following the words on the scroll with her finger. When the story is over, she asks if they can go up to the roof, to look at the stars. Her father smiles and says yes.

[pause]

{img:father-daughter-flat-roof-night-looking-at-stars-pharos-light-harbour}
On the roof, the night is warm and still. The stars are coming out, more and more, until the whole sky is full of them. Demetrios points to the faint little cluster called Berenice's Hair, and Kallisto finds it at last. Then she looks out toward the harbour.
