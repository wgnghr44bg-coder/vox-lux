{img:001-man-walking-to-work-before-dawn}

London. The thirty-first of August, 1888. It is almost four o'clock in the morning. A man is walking to work through the dark streets of Whitechapel. The gas lamps are low. The fog is cold and wet.

[long-pause]

{img:002-bucks-row-narrow-street-night}

In a narrow street called Buck's Row, he sees something on the ground. At first, he thinks it is an old piece of cloth. Then he looks closer. It is a woman. And she is not moving.

[long-pause]

{img:003-woman-leaving-lodging-house-door}

Her name is Mary Ann Nichols. She is forty-three years old. A few hours earlier, she was at a lodging house, but she had no money for a bed. She smiled and said she would soon be back with the money. She never came back.

[long-pause]

{img:004-letter-red-ink-on-desk}

Over the next ten weeks, four more women will die in these same streets. The whole city will be afraid. Letters will arrive, written by someone who says he is the killer. Detectives will question more than two thousand people.

[long-pause]

{img:005-constable-lantern-foggy-alley}

And the man behind it all will simply disappear. He will never be caught. No one will ever know his real name. But the world will remember the name he was given. Jack the Ripper.

[long-pause]

{img:006-five-candles-dark-room}

This is the story of the autumn of terror. Of the narrow streets, the fear, and the people of Whitechapel. Of five women who deserve to be remembered. And of a mystery that is still unsolved, more than a hundred and thirty years later.

[long-pause]

{img:007-gas-lamp-in-fog}

So turn down the lights. Get comfortable. And let us go back to the autumn of 1888.

[long-pause]

{img:008-london-skyline-1888-thames-dusk}

In 1888, London was the biggest city in the world. More than five million people lived there. It was the heart of a great empire. Ships came up the River Thames from every corner of the globe. They brought tea from India, wool from Australia, and cotton from America. In the West End, there were grand theatres, fine shops and wide, clean streets. Rich families rode in shiny carriages to parties and concerts. Queen Victoria had been on the throne for more than fifty years. For many people, it was a time of wealth and pride.

[long-pause]

{img:009-east-end-crowded-street-dusk}

But only a few miles to the east, the city looked very different. This was the East End. Here, the streets were narrow and dirty. The houses were old and crowded. Many families lived in a single room, with one window and no running water. The air smelled of smoke, horses and the river. Children played barefoot in the mud. The people of the East End worked hard, but they were very poor. And at the centre of the East End was a district called Whitechapel.

[long-pause]

{img:010-whitechapel-high-street-market-day}

Whitechapel was full of life. In the daytime, the main road was crowded with carts, market stalls and shoppers. People sold fish, fruit, old clothes and hot potatoes. There were butchers, bakers, tailors and pubs on every corner. Men worked at the docks, in the breweries and in small workshops. Women worked long hours sewing clothes or making matchboxes at home, for very little money. Everyone was busy. Everyone was trying to earn enough for one more day.

[long-pause]

{img:011-immigrant-family-arriving-with-bundles}

Whitechapel was also a place of newcomers. In the 1880s, thousands of Jewish families arrived from Russia and Poland. They had escaped violence and hunger in their home countries. They came with almost nothing, carrying their bundles through the streets. Many of them worked as tailors or bootmakers. They spoke Yiddish, and they opened small shops and prayer houses. Irish families lived here too, and sailors from many lands. Whitechapel was crowded, noisy and full of different voices.

[long-pause]

{img:012-common-lodging-house-dormitory-beds}

For the poorest people, home was a common lodging house. These were large, cheap houses where anyone could rent a bed for one night. In Whitechapel alone, there were more than two hundred of them. Every night, around eight thousand people slept there. A single bed cost fourpence. A double bed cost eightpence. The rooms were long and crowded, with rows of narrow beds. There was a kitchen downstairs with a big fire, where people cooked and talked late into the night.

[long-pause]

{img:013-lodging-house-kitchen-fire-night}

But the rule was simple. No money, no bed. Every evening, the deputy of the lodging house stood at the door and asked for the fourpence. If you could not pay, you had to go back out into the street. Many people walked all night, or slept in doorways and on stairs. Some women had no other way to earn the money than to sell themselves to strangers. It was dangerous, and everybody knew it. But for many, there was simply no other choice.

[long-pause]

{img:014-police-constable-on-beat-night}

The streets were watched by the Metropolitan Police. Constables walked slow, regular beats through the district, all through the night. Each man carried a lantern, a wooden truncheon and a whistle. They checked doors and windows, and they knew the faces of the people on their streets. But the area was large, the alleys were dark, and there were not many of them. A constable might pass the same corner only once every half an hour. A lot can happen in half an hour.

[long-pause]

{img:015-constable-checking-door-handle-night}

Imagine one of those constables on a long night in Whitechapel. He starts his beat at ten in the evening. He walks slowly, about two and a half miles an hour, along the same streets, again and again. He tries the handles of shop doors to make sure they are locked. He looks into dark doorways with his lantern. He hears the pubs closing, the drunk men singing, the cats on the walls, and the trains passing on the railway bridges. Towards dawn, the streets are almost silent. Then, at six in the morning, his long night is over.

[long-pause]

{img:016-thick-yellow-fog-street-lamps}

And then there was the fog. In the autumn and winter, London was famous for it. Thousands of chimneys burned coal day and night, and the smoke mixed with the damp air from the river. On some nights, the fog was so thick and yellow that a man could not see his own hand in front of his face. Londoners called it a pea-souper. Sounds became soft and strange. Footsteps seemed to come from nowhere. A person could pass within a few feet of you and never be seen.

[long-pause]

{img:017-dark-courtyard-george-yard-buildings}

In the spring and summer of 1888, there had already been trouble. In April, a woman named Emma Smith was attacked by a group of young men, and she later died. In August, a woman named Martha Tabram was found dead on a staircase in George Yard Buildings. The police never found who did it. The people of Whitechapel were worried. But violence was not new in these streets. Nobody yet knew that something much darker was about to begin.

[long-pause]

{img:018-petticoat-lane-sunday-market-stalls}

On Sunday mornings, the streets near Whitechapel turned into one great market. The most famous was Petticoat Lane. Hundreds of stalls stood side by side, from one end of the street to the other. You could buy almost anything there. Old coats, new shoes, pots and pans, birds in cages, toys, buttons and lace. The sellers shouted their prices. Buyers argued and laughed. For a few hours every week, the poorest people in London could feel that the whole world was for sale on their own doorstep.

[long-pause]

{img:019-street-food-seller-baked-potatoes-night}

Food in Whitechapel was simple and cheap. Most people lived on bread, butter or dripping, and strong, sweet tea. On the street corners, sellers stood beside small iron ovens, selling hot baked potatoes for a halfpenny. There were stalls with jellied eels, pea soup and hot coffee. In the evening, the smell of fried fish came from the little shops. For a working family, meat was a treat, often only once a week, on a Sunday.

[long-pause]

{img:020-music-hall-stage-gaslights-audience}

In the evenings, people looked for a little joy. The pubs were warm and bright, with gas lamps and polished wood. In the music halls, singers and comedians performed on small stages, and the whole audience sang along to the songs they knew. For a few pennies, a tired worker could forget his troubles for an hour. When the halls closed late at night, the crowds poured out into the dark streets, singing and talking on their way home.

[long-pause]

{img:021-children-in-ragged-school-classroom}

There were many children in Whitechapel. Some went to school. Others worked from a very young age, selling matches, running errands, or helping their mothers at home. Some had no home at all. Kind people tried to help them. A young doctor named Thomas Barnardo opened homes for children who lived on the streets. Ragged schools gave free lessons to the poorest boys and girls. It was not enough. But it was a beginning.

[long-pause]

{img:022-salvation-army-band-in-street}

Whitechapel was also a place of hope. Some years before, a preacher named William Booth had started holding meetings in a tent in Whitechapel. His movement became the Salvation Army. Its members marched through the streets with drums and brass instruments, singing hymns and offering soup and a bed to those who needed them. Near the main road, a house called Toynbee Hall had opened, where young men from the universities lived and worked among the poor, teaching classes and trying to understand their lives.

[long-pause]

{img:023-young-women-factory-gate-match-factory}

In the summer of 1888, something happened that surprised all of London. At a match factory in the East End, hundreds of young women and girls walked out of their jobs. They were angry about low pay, long hours and unfair fines. And they were sick from the chemicals used to make the matches. The match girls' strike made headlines. After two weeks, the owners agreed to many of their demands. The poorest workers in London had shown that they too had a voice.

[long-pause]

{img:024-woman-in-bonnet-walking-away-fog}

Let us go back to the woman in Buck's Row. Mary Ann Nichols was born in London in 1845. Her friends called her Polly. Her father was a locksmith. When she was a young woman, she married a printer named William Nichols. Together, they had five children. For a while, they lived a simple and ordinary life, in a small house south of the river. There was work, there was food, and there was a family around the table.

[long-pause]

{img:025-workhouse-building-grey-day}

But the marriage did not last. Around 1880, Polly and William separated. The children stayed with their father. Polly was alone, and she had very little money. For the next eight years, she moved from place to place. She stayed in workhouses, where the poorest people of London were given food and a bed in return for hard work. She stayed with friends. She slept in lodging houses. Life became harder every year.

[long-pause]

{img:026-large-victorian-house-servant-kitchen}

In the spring of 1888, it looked as if her luck might change. She found a job as a servant in a family home in Wandsworth. She wrote a letter to her father, saying that her new employers were kind people, and that she was settling in. But the new life did not last. After only a few months, she left the house. She took some clothes with her. Soon she was back in the lodging houses of Whitechapel, with nothing.

[long-pause]

{img:027-thrawl-street-lodging-house-door-night}

On the night of the thirtieth of August, Polly was at a lodging house in Thrawl Street. It was very late, and she had been drinking. She did not have the fourpence for her bed. The deputy sent her away. Polly did not seem worried. She laughed and pointed to her hat. It was a new black straw bonnet. She said she would soon get her money, and she told them to keep a bed for her. Then she walked out into the night.

[long-pause]

{img:028-shared-bedroom-lodging-house-two-beds}

In her last weeks, Polly shared a room with a few other women in a lodging house in Thrawl Street. One of them was Emily Holland, an older woman who became her friend. They shared a bed, as many poor women did, to save money. Emily later said that Polly was a quiet woman, who kept herself to herself, and who seemed to be a clean and decent person when she was not drinking. Just before the end of August, Polly moved to another lodging house nearby. But she and Emily stayed friends.

[long-pause]

{img:029-street-corner-church-clock-night}

At about half past two in the morning, a friend of hers named Emily Holland saw her near the corner of Whitechapel Road. The bell of a nearby church was striking the hour. Polly was tired and unsteady. She told Emily that she had already earned the money for her bed three times that day, and spent it every time. Emily tried to bring her home, but Polly would not come. She said she would be back soon. Then she walked away, along the dark road. Emily was the last friend to see her alive.

[long-pause]

{img:030-two-men-in-dark-street-lantern}

About an hour later, a carman named Charles Cross was on his way to work. In Buck's Row, he saw the shape on the ground, beside a gateway. Another worker, Robert Paul, came walking up the street. The two men looked together. It was too dark to see much. They decided to go and find a policeman. A few minutes later, a constable named John Neil came down Buck's Row on his beat. He shone his lantern on the ground, and he understood at once that something terrible had happened.

[long-pause]

{img:031-doctor-bag-and-lantern-on-cobblestones}

A doctor was called from his house nearby. He arrived quickly, and he said that the woman had died only a short time before. She was taken to a small mortuary nearby. At first, nobody knew who she was. The only clue was in her clothes. Her petticoat had a mark from the Lambeth Workhouse. Through that mark, the police slowly found her name. Her husband came to see her, and so did her father. Her husband said quietly that he forgave her for everything.

[long-pause]

{img:032-coroner-court-room-wooden-benches}

The police were puzzled. There had been no scream. Nobody living in Buck's Row had heard a sound. A woman sleeping in a house only a few steps away had heard nothing at all. The killer had come and gone in the dark, in a street where a constable walked past every half an hour. And he had left no weapon, no footprint, and no trace behind. How was that possible? And would he do it again?

[long-pause]

{img:033-coroner-court-wooden-benches-window}

A few days later, an inquest was held. This was a public hearing to find out how Polly had died. It was led by the coroner, a man named Wynne Baxter. He listened to the doctor, to the policemen, and to the men who had found her. Reporters wrote down every word. The inquest went on for several days. In the end, the jury could only say that she had been murdered by a person unknown. Polly was buried a week after her death, in a cemetery in the east of London.

[long-pause]

{img:034-newspaper-boy-shouting-street-corner}

The next morning, the newspapers were full of the story. Boys ran through the streets, shouting the headlines. Reporters came to Whitechapel and asked questions in every pub and lodging house. The police began their search. They spoke to the people in Buck's Row. They checked the nearby slaughterhouses, where men worked with knives all night. But they found nothing. Slowly, life in Whitechapel went on. One week passed. And then the killer struck again.

[long-pause]

{img:035-small-cottage-windsor-countryside}

Annie Chapman was born in 1840. When she was young, she married a coachman named John Chapman. For some years, they lived a quiet life outside London, near the town of Windsor. John worked for a rich gentleman, driving his carriage. They had three children. But sadness came early. Their oldest daughter died of an illness when she was only twelve. Their son was born disabled. The family struggled, and Annie began to drink.

[long-pause]

{img:036-woman-selling-flowers-street-basket}

In time, Annie and John separated. For a while, John sent her ten shillings every week, to help her live. Then, at Christmas in 1886, he died. The money stopped. Annie moved to Whitechapel. She tried to earn a living in honest ways. She made small things with crochet, and she sold flowers in the street. Her friends called her Dark Annie. They said she was a clever woman, and kind, when life was not too hard on her.

[long-pause]

{img:037-two-women-talking-near-church-railings}

In the days before her death, Annie felt very ill. On the Monday, she met her friend Amelia Palmer in the street near Christ Church. Annie had a bruise on her face from a fight. She said she felt unwell, and that she might go to the hospital. Amelia gave her twopence to buy a cup of tea, and told her not to spend it on rum. On the Friday afternoon, they met again. Annie said she felt too ill to do anything. But a little later, she said, "It's no use giving way. I must pull myself together and go out and get some money, or I shall have no lodgings."

[long-pause]

{img:038-dorset-street-lodging-house-night}

By September 1888, Annie was ill. She was living in a lodging house at thirty-five Dorset Street. On the night of the seventh of September, she sat in the kitchen there until very late. She did not have money for her bed. Just before two in the morning, the deputy asked her to pay. Annie told him not to give her bed to anyone else. She said she would soon be back with the money. Then she walked out into the street.

[long-pause]

{img:039-hanbury-street-houses-early-morning}

At about half past five that morning, a woman named Elizabeth Long was walking down Hanbury Street, on her way to the market. The sky was getting light. She saw a man and a woman talking quietly outside number twenty-nine. She saw only the man's back. She said he wore a dark coat and a brown hat, and that he looked like a shabby gentleman. She heard him ask a question. She heard the woman say yes. Elizabeth Long walked on. She did not think about it again, until later that day.

[long-pause]

{img:040-backyard-wooden-fence-dawn}

Number twenty-nine Hanbury Street was an old house, where seventeen people lived. A passage ran through the house to a small backyard. At about six o'clock, an old carman named John Davis came down the stairs to go to work. He opened the back door. In the yard, beside the steps, he found Annie Chapman. He ran into the street, calling for help. Within an hour, the whole street was full of people.

[long-pause]

{img:041-crowd-outside-house-morning}

This time, the fear spread fast. Annie had been killed when it was almost daylight, in a yard surrounded by windows. Again, nobody had heard a cry. Again, the killer had walked away without being seen. The neighbours in Hanbury Street charged people a penny to look out of their windows into the yard. Crowds stood outside all day. People began to say that this was no ordinary criminal. This was a monster.

[long-pause]

{img:042-leather-apron-on-workbench}

Soon, a rumour spread through Whitechapel. People said the killer was a man they called Leather Apron. They said he was a shoemaker who frightened women in the streets. The newspapers loved the story. A few days later, the police arrested a Jewish bootmaker named John Pizer, who was known by that name. But Pizer could prove where he had been on the nights of the murders. He was released. He was innocent. Yet the story had already done harm. Some people in Whitechapel began to blame their Jewish neighbours.

[long-pause]

{img:043-doctor-desk-medical-books-lamp}

At the inquest for Annie Chapman, the police doctor, George Bagster Phillips, gave his evidence. He said something that frightened people even more. He believed that the killer had some knowledge of the human body, the kind of knowledge that a doctor or a butcher might have. Was the killer a medical man? A slaughterman? The idea spread quickly through the newspapers. Later, other doctors disagreed with him. But the image of a skilled and cold-blooded killer stayed in people's minds.

[long-pause]

{img:044-men-meeting-in-pub-back-room}

The people of the East End felt that nobody was protecting them. So they decided to act for themselves. A group of local shopkeepers and businessmen formed a committee. It was called the Mile End Vigilance Committee, and its leader was a builder named George Lusk. The men raised money and hired helpers to walk the streets at night. They asked the government to offer a reward for catching the killer. But the government refused. Rewards, they said, only brought false information.

[long-pause]

{img:045-central-news-agency-office-desk}

Then, at the end of September, a letter arrived at the Central News Agency in London. It was addressed to "Dear Boss." It was written in red ink. The writer said he was the Whitechapel killer. He laughed at the police. He said they would never catch him. And he signed it with a name that nobody had ever used before. "Yours truly, Jack the Ripper." The agency sent the letter to Scotland Yard. For a few days, the police kept it quiet. But that was about to change.

[long-pause]

{img:046-berner-street-night-club-windows-lit}

The night of the twenty-ninth of September was wet and windy. In Berner Street, there was a club for working men. Many of them were Jewish immigrants. That evening, there was a meeting and a talk, and later the members sang songs in their own language. The windows upstairs were lit, and music came down into the dark yard beside the building. The yard was called Dutfield's Yard. It had two large wooden gates, which stood open onto the street.

[long-pause]

{img:047-swedish-village-church-coast}

Elizabeth Stride was born in Sweden in 1843, in a small farming village near the city of Gothenburg. Her name then was Elisabeth Gustafsdotter. As a young woman, she worked as a servant in the city. In 1866, she came to London. She married a carpenter named John Stride, and for a few years, they ran a small coffee shop together. She was tall, and she spoke English with a Swedish accent. Her friends called her Long Liz.

[long-pause]

{img:048-swedish-church-london-interior}

Elizabeth often told a sad story about her life. She said that her husband and her children had drowned in a terrible accident on the River Thames, when a pleasure boat sank. It was not true. Her husband had died of illness, and she had no children with him. Perhaps the story helped her to get charity. Perhaps it was easier than the real story. She sometimes received help from the Swedish Church in London. She cleaned houses, and she did sewing work when she could find it.

[long-pause]

{img:049-woman-cleaning-room-bucket-window}

On the twenty-ninth of September, Elizabeth spent the afternoon cleaning rooms at her lodging house in Flower and Dean Street. The deputy paid her sixpence for the work. In the evening, she borrowed a clothes brush from a man who lived there, to make herself look neat. She was wearing a black jacket with a small red flower pinned to it. She went out at about seven o'clock. She seemed cheerful. Nobody at the lodging house knew that they would never see her again.

[long-pause]

{img:050-pony-and-cart-entering-dark-yard}

At one o'clock in the morning, a man named Louis Diemschutz drove his pony and cart into Dutfield's Yard. He was the steward of the club, coming home from a day of selling at a market. As he turned through the gates, his pony suddenly stopped and shied to the left. It would not go on. Diemschutz looked down into the dark. There was something on the ground beside the wall. He struck a match. In its small, shaking light, he saw a woman.

[long-pause]

{img:051-men-running-out-of-club-door}

Diemschutz ran into the club for help. The men came out with a candle. It was Elizabeth Stride. She had died only minutes before. Many people believe that the arrival of the pony and cart had disturbed the killer, and that he was still in the yard, hidden in the dark, when Diemschutz arrived. Perhaps he slipped out through the gates while the men ran for help. Nobody saw him go. But the night was not over yet.

[long-pause]

{img:052-police-station-cell-door-lamp}

Catherine Eddowes was born in 1842, in the town of Wolverhampton, in the middle of England. Her father worked with tin. She came to London as a child, with her family. For many years, she lived with a man named Thomas Conway, and they had three children. Later, she lived with a kind market worker named John Kelly. In the summer of 1888, the two of them went to Kent, outside London, to pick hops in the fields. It was hard work, but the countryside was green and quiet. They came back to London at the end of September, with no money left.

[long-pause]

{img:053-pawnshop-window-boots-gaslight}

When Catherine and John Kelly came back from Kent, they had almost nothing left. On the last Saturday of September, John pawned his own boots to buy them some breakfast. In the afternoon, Catherine said she would go and find her daughter, who lived on the other side of the river. She hoped her daughter would give her a little money. She promised John that she would be back by four o'clock. He told her to be careful, because of the killer. Catherine laughed. She said that she knew how to look after herself.

[long-pause]

{img:054-police-cell-bench-lamp-night}

On the evening of the twenty-ninth of September, Catherine was found drunk in the street in Aldgate. Two City policemen took her to Bishopsgate police station, and put her in a cell to sleep. At about one o'clock in the morning, she was sober enough to go home. The officer on duty let her go. She gave him a false name. As she walked out of the door, she said, "Good night, old cock." She turned towards Aldgate. That was the wrong way to go home.

[long-pause]

{img:055-mitre-square-dark-corner-lamp}

Mitre Square was a small, quiet square, surrounded by warehouses and a few houses. At night it was empty and very dark, with only one lamp in the corner. A City policeman named Edward Watkins walked through the square every fifteen minutes. At half past one, he saw nothing unusual. At a quarter to two, he came back with his lantern. In the darkest corner of the square, he found Catherine Eddowes. He ran to a warehouse nearby and called the night watchman, who blew his whistle for help.

[long-pause]

{img:056-church-passage-narrow-archway}

Ten minutes before Catherine was found, three men had left a club near Mitre Square. One of them, Joseph Lawende, saw a man and a woman standing together at the end of a narrow passage that led into the square. The woman had her hand on the man's chest. Lawende did not see the woman's face. But the police later showed him her clothes, and he believed it was Catherine. The man was a little taller than her, with a small fair moustache and a cloth cap. Lawende said he would not know him again. Many people believe that, for a moment, Lawende may have seen the killer.

[long-pause]

{img:057-dark-stairwell-doorway-goulston-street}

Two murders in one night, less than an hour apart, and less than a mile from each other. But there was one more clue. At about three o'clock in the morning, a constable named Alfred Long was walking down Goulston Street. In a dark doorway, he found a torn piece of cloth. It was a piece of Catherine's apron. Above it, on the wall, someone had written a message in chalk. The words were strange, and they seemed to blame the Jews of Whitechapel.

[long-pause]

{img:058-wet-brick-wall-chalk-washed-away}

The head of the Metropolitan Police, Sir Charles Warren, came to Goulston Street himself before dawn. He was afraid that when the market opened and crowds arrived, the message would cause riots against the Jewish people living there. So he ordered the writing to be washed away, before anyone could take a photograph of it. Later, many people said it was a terrible mistake. Was the message written by the killer? Or was it there long before? Nobody knows.

[long-pause]

{img:059-postcard-on-wooden-table-lamp}

On the first of October, a postcard arrived at the same news agency. It was written in the same red ink, and in a similar hand. The writer called himself Saucy Jacky. He spoke of a double event. He said that this time, he had not had time to finish his work. The police now decided to show the letters to the public. They put copies on posters outside every police station, hoping that someone would recognise the handwriting. From that day on, the whole world knew a new name. Jack the Ripper.

[long-pause]

{img:060-printing-press-newspaper-sheets}

The newspapers had never seen a story like this. In 1888, cheap newspapers were new, and millions of people could read them. Papers like The Star printed new editions every few hours, with more and more dramatic stories. Some reporters invented details. Some blamed the police for everything. Sales went up and up. One magazine printed a famous cartoon of a policeman wearing a blindfold, turning in circles while criminals laughed around him. The story of Jack the Ripper was perhaps the first great media story of the modern age.

[long-pause]

{img:061-cemetery-gate-autumn-trees-mourners}

Elizabeth Stride and Catherine Eddowes were buried in the first week of October. Elizabeth's funeral was quiet and simple, paid for by the parish. Catherine's was very different. Thousands of people came to watch her coffin pass through the streets. John Kelly, the man she had lived with for seven years, walked behind it, together with her sisters. Crowds stood in silence along the road. For one day, the people of the East End came together to say goodbye to one of their own.

[long-pause]

{img:062-scotland-yard-office-papers-lamp}

After the night of the double event, the hunt became the biggest police search London had ever seen. Scotland Yard sent its best detectives to Whitechapel. One of them was Inspector Frederick Abberline. He had worked in Whitechapel for many years, and he knew its streets and its people very well. At Scotland Yard, Chief Inspector Donald Swanson read every report. Every statement, every letter and every clue passed across his desk.

[long-pause]

{img:063-old-army-officer-portrait-room-maps}

The man at the head of the Metropolitan Police was Sir Charles Warren. He was an army officer and an engineer. As a young man, he had worked in Jerusalem, exploring the tunnels and water shafts under the old city, and one of them still carries his name today. He was brave and clever, but he was a soldier, not a detective. Many newspapers did not like him. They said he cared more about marching and order than about catching criminals.

[long-pause]

{img:064-detective-hat-coat-on-hook-office}

Inspector Frederick Abberline was a very different kind of man. He was quiet, careful and patient. He had grown up in a small town in Dorset, and he had joined the police as a young man. He spent most of his working life walking the streets of the East End, and he knew many of the people there by name. After the murders, he never said who he believed the killer was. When he left the police in 1892, he worked for a private detective agency, and later he retired to the seaside, where he lived quietly until he was eighty-six.

[long-pause]

{img:065-detective-with-notebook-in-street}

But the detectives of 1888 had very few tools. There was no way to take fingerprints. That would only come to Scotland Yard many years later. There were no cameras in the streets, no telephones in most homes, and no way to test blood to see whom it belonged to. A detective had only his eyes, his notebook and his legs. He had to knock on doors, talk to witnesses, and hope that somebody had seen something.

[long-pause]

{img:066-constables-knocking-on-doors-morning}

And so the police knocked on doors. They went from house to house through the streets of Whitechapel, asking questions. They visited every lodging house. They printed eighty thousand leaflets, asking people to come forward with any information. They questioned sailors from the ships in the docks. They checked the butchers and the slaughtermen. They spoke to more than two thousand people. Many men were brought to the police station and later released. But nobody was charged.

[long-pause]

{img:067-two-bloodhounds-in-park-autumn}

Some ideas were new and unusual. In October, Sir Charles Warren tested two bloodhounds, named Barnaby and Burgho, in a London park. The plan was to use them to follow the killer's scent, if there was another murder. Newspapers wrote that Warren himself had taken part in the test, running across the grass. Some people laughed at the idea. In the end, the dogs were never used in Whitechapel.

[long-pause]

{img:068-flood-of-letters-on-desk}

Meanwhile, the letters kept coming. After the newspapers printed the Dear Boss letter, hundreds of people wrote to the police and the newspapers, pretending to be the killer. Some were jokes. Some were cruel. Some were written by people who wanted attention. The police had to read them all. Today, most historians believe that the famous Dear Boss letter itself was not written by the killer, but by a journalist who wanted to keep the story alive. One letter, however, was different.

[long-pause]

{img:069-small-cardboard-box-wrapped-paper}

On the sixteenth of October, George Lusk, the leader of the Vigilance Committee, received a small box in the post. Inside the box was a letter, and a piece of a human kidney. The letter was badly written and full of spelling mistakes. It did not start with "Dear Boss." It started with the words, "From hell." The writer said the kidney came from one of the women. Was it real, or was it a terrible joke by a medical student? Doctors at the time did not agree. And the answer is still not known.

[long-pause]

{img:070-theatre-poster-wall-gaslight-crowd}

By now, the whole country was talking about the murders. Newspapers printed drawings of the dark streets and the frightened people. Stories spread about a doctor, a butcher, a foreign sailor, or a gentleman from the West End. At a theatre in London, the play Doctor Jekyll and Mister Hyde was playing to full houses. It was the story of a respectable man who turns into a monster at night. Many people felt that the play had come to life in Whitechapel.

[long-pause]

{img:071-quiet-whitechapel-street-october-rain}

And then, for a few weeks, nothing happened. October passed. The police went on searching. The vigilance men went on walking the streets with their lanterns. The newspapers began to talk about other things. Some people hoped that it was over. Perhaps the killer had left London. Perhaps he had died. Perhaps he had been frightened away by all the police. In the lodging houses, the women went back to their old ways. They had no other choice.

[long-pause]

{img:072-dorset-street-narrow-court-entrance}

Dorset Street was a short street near Spitalfields Market. People called it the worst street in London. It was full of lodging houses, small rented rooms and dark little courts. One of these courts was called Miller's Court. You reached it through a narrow passage, only about a metre wide. Inside were a few small houses, painted white, around a tiny yard. The rooms were rented by a man named John McCarthy, who also ran a shop on the street.

[long-pause]

{img:073-small-room-fireplace-single-bed}

In room number thirteen of Miller's Court lived a young woman named Mary Jane Kelly. She was about twenty-five years old. She said she was born in Ireland, and that she had grown up in Wales. She came to London a few years before 1888. People who knew her said she was pretty, with fair hair, and that she was cheerful and liked to sing. Some said she had once lived a better life in the West End.

[long-pause]

{img:074-coal-mine-valley-wales-mist}

Mary Jane's story of her early life is hard to check. According to Joseph Barnett, she told him that she had married young, in Wales, to a coal miner named Davies. She said that her husband had died in an explosion in a mine, only a few years after their marriage. After that, she moved to Cardiff, and later to London. Some of this may be true. Some of it may not. Historians have searched the records in Ireland and Wales for many years. They have never found her for certain.

[long-pause]

{img:075-fish-market-early-morning-porters}

For a while, Mary Jane lived in that small room with a man named Joseph Barnett. He was a fish porter at Billingsgate Market. But Barnett had lost his job, money was short, and at the end of October the two of them argued. He moved out. He still visited her almost every day. By November, Mary Jane owed her landlord twenty-nine shillings in rent. It was a lot of money. And she was alone.

[long-pause]

{img:076-court-window-candlelight-night}

On the night of the eighth of November, Mary Jane was seen in the streets, and in the pubs nearby. Just before midnight, a neighbour named Mary Ann Cox saw her go into her room with a man. A little later, Mary Ann Cox heard her singing. It was an old song, called A Violet from Mother's Grave. The singing went on for a long time. Later, a man named George Hutchinson said he had seen Mary Jane at about two o'clock in the morning with a well-dressed stranger. He described him in great detail. Some people believe him. Some do not.

[long-pause]

{img:077-lord-mayors-procession-flags-street}

The ninth of November was a special day in London. It was the day of the Lord Mayor's Show. A great procession of coaches, soldiers and bands would pass through the city. Crowds were already gathering in the streets. At a quarter to eleven that morning, John McCarthy sent his assistant, Thomas Bowyer, to Miller's Court, to ask Mary Jane for the rent. Bowyer knocked on her door. There was no answer.

[long-pause]

{img:078-broken-window-pane-curtain-court}

One of the small windows of room thirteen was broken. Bowyer reached through the broken glass and pushed aside the curtain. He looked inside. Then he ran back to his master. McCarthy came and looked for himself. He sent for the police at once. Mary Jane Kelly was dead. She was the last of the five women. And of all of them, she was the only one who died indoors, in her own room, behind her own door.

[long-pause]

{img:079-crowd-in-street-november-fog}

Outside, the crowds of the Lord Mayor's Show were cheering. In Whitechapel, there was shock and fear. Around the same time, the news came that Sir Charles Warren had resigned as head of the Metropolitan Police. A few days later, the government did something new. It offered a pardon to anyone who had helped the killer, if they came forward and named him. But nobody came forward. The police went on searching. They found nothing.

[long-pause]

{img:080-palace-writing-desk-telegram}

Even Queen Victoria was worried. After the murder in Miller's Court, she sent an urgent message to the Prime Minister, Lord Salisbury. She wrote that this new murder showed that something must be done, and quickly. All the dark courts, she said, must be lit, and the detectives must be improved. The Queen of half the world was asking questions about a small dark court in Whitechapel. It showed how far the fear had spread.

[long-pause]

{img:081-funeral-carriage-black-horses-crowd}

Mary Jane Kelly was buried on the nineteenth of November, in a Catholic cemetery in Leytonstone, in the east of London. Thousands of people lined the streets to watch her coffin pass. Many of them were poor women from Whitechapel. They wept as the carriage went by. Some threw flowers. The funeral was paid for by a kind man who worked at a church nearby. The people of the East End had not known Mary Jane. But they knew that her life could have been their own.

[long-pause]

{img:082-empty-street-winter-gas-lamp}

After Mary Jane Kelly, the murders seemed to stop. There were other killings in Whitechapel in the next few years. In 1889, a woman named Alice McKenzie was found dead. In 1891, a young woman named Frances Coles was found under a railway arch. The police kept their files open until 1892. But most experts believe that these later deaths were the work of other people. The five women of the autumn of 1888 are usually seen as the victims of one man. And then he was gone.

[long-pause]

{img:083-vigilance-men-with-lanterns-night-patrol}

What happened to the men who had searched for the killer? The Vigilance Committee slowly stopped its night patrols, when the murders stopped. George Lusk went back to his work as a builder, but for a long time he received cruel letters from people pretending to be the killer. The police went back to their ordinary work. In the lodging houses, the fear faded, little by little. By the spring of 1889, the streets of Whitechapel looked much the same as before.

[long-pause]

{img:084-old-notebook-handwritten-names}

So who was he? For more than a hundred and thirty years, people have tried to answer this question. Over one hundred names have been suggested. Some of them were real suspects, named by the police at the time. Some were suggested much later, by writers and researchers. Not one of them has ever been proven to be Jack the Ripper. Let us look at a few of the best-known names, calmly and carefully. Not to accuse anyone. Only to understand the mystery.

[long-pause]

{img:085-river-thames-chiswick-winter-mist}

In 1894, a senior police officer named Sir Melville Macnaghten wrote down the names of three men who he said were more likely to be the killer than others. The first was Montague John Druitt. He was a young lawyer and teacher from a good family. At the beginning of December 1888, he disappeared. A few weeks later, his body was found in the River Thames. Macnaghten believed that Druitt's family suspected him. But there was no real evidence.

[long-pause]

{img:086-asylum-building-garden-wall}

The second name was Aaron Kosminski. He was a Polish Jewish hairdresser who lived in Whitechapel with his family. He was mentally ill, and in 1891 he was taken to an asylum, where he stayed for the rest of his life. Some senior police officers believed that he was the killer, and that a witness had recognised him. But the witness, they said, refused to give evidence. Kosminski was never charged with any crime.

[long-pause]

{img:087-ship-leaving-port-night-america}

The third name was Michael Ostrog, a Russian-born thief and confidence man who used many false names. Later research showed that he was probably in prison in France during the murders. Other suspects were named over the years. One was an American doctor named Francis Tumblety, who was in London in 1888, and who left the country in a hurry that November. Others were a famous painter, a cotton merchant from Liverpool, and even a member of the royal family. Most of these ideas have been strongly questioned.

[long-pause]

{img:088-shawl-folded-in-glass-case}

In recent years, people have also used science. In 2014, a writer said that a shawl, believed to have been found near Catherine Eddowes, carried traces of DNA linked to Aaron Kosminski. It made headlines around the world. But many scientists and historians disagree. Nobody knows for sure where the shawl came from, or who handled it in the many years since 1888. Once again, the answer slipped away.

[long-pause]

{img:089-whitechapel-street-renamed-sign-free}

And Whitechapel itself slowly changed. Buck's Row was given a new name, because the people living there did not want to live on the street of the murders. Dorset Street was rebuilt, and the courts and lodging houses disappeared. Charities and new laws brought better homes, more lamps, and cleaner streets. The East End was still poor for many years. But the dark world of the autumn of 1888 slowly faded away.

[long-pause]

{img:090-old-books-on-shelf-candle}

The story of Jack the Ripper never went away. Soon after 1888, the first books about the murders were written. Then came plays, and later films and television programmes. Today there are hundreds of books about the case. People who study it call themselves Ripperologists. They meet, they argue, and they search old archives for new clues. Every year, visitors from all over the world walk through the streets of Whitechapel at night, following guides with lanterns, listening to the story once more.

[long-pause]

{img:091-old-photographs-census-papers-desk}

In 2019, a historian named Hallie Rubenhold wrote a book called The Five. It did not try to find the killer. Instead, it told the life stories of the five women, from their childhoods to their last days. She searched old records, letters and census papers. She showed that these women were not just names in a crime story. They had grown up, fallen in love, raised children, worked and struggled. Many readers said that the book changed the way they saw the whole case.

[long-pause]

{img:092-five-flowers-on-wooden-table-candle}

For a long time, the story of Jack the Ripper was told as the story of the killer. But in recent years, writers and historians have turned to the five women. Mary Ann Nichols, who wrote to her father about her new job. Annie Chapman, who made crochet work and sold flowers. Elizabeth Stride, who came from a village in Sweden. Catherine Eddowes, who picked hops in the green fields of Kent. And Mary Jane Kelly, who sang an old song on her last night. They were daughters, wives and mothers. They deserve to be remembered.

[long-pause]

{img:093-spitalfields-church-spire-dawn-fog}

Today, if you walk through Whitechapel on a quiet evening, it is hard to imagine the old streets. There are new houses and offices now. The traffic hums. The market stalls still sell food and clothes, in many languages. But the great white church of Christ Church Spitalfields still stands, just as it did in 1888, looking down over the streets where it all happened.

[long-pause]

{img:094-fog-lifting-over-rooftops-morning}

The fog has lifted from Whitechapel. The gas lamps are gone. The constables with their lanterns walk their beats no more. The lodging houses are quiet, and the voices of 1888 have faded away. Only the questions remain. Who was he? Where did he go? And why did he stop?

[long-pause]

{img:095-single-gas-lamp-going-out-dawn}

Perhaps we will never know. Perhaps the answer is written in an old notebook, in an attic, somewhere. Or perhaps it was lost long ago, like the chalk on the wall in Goulston Street, washed away in the rain before morning. The mystery stays, quiet and patient, like the old river at night.

[long-pause]

{img:096-quiet-river-thames-night-calm}

Thank you for walking with me through the streets of old Whitechapel tonight. Rest now. Let the old streets fade away, and let the night be calm and quiet. Good night.
