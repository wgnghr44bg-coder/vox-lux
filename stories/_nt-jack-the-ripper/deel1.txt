London. The thirty-first of August, 1888. It is almost four o'clock in the morning. A man is walking to work through the dark streets of Whitechapel. The gas lamps are low. The fog is cold and wet.

[long-pause]

{img:02-bucks-row-narrow-street-night}

In a narrow street called Buck's Row, he sees something on the ground. At first, he thinks it is an old piece of cloth. Then he looks closer. It is a woman. And she is not moving.

[long-pause]

{img:03-woman-leaving-lodging-house-door}

Her name is Mary Ann Nichols. She is forty-three years old. A few hours earlier, she was at a lodging house, but she had no money for a bed. She smiled and said she would soon be back with the money. She never came back.

[long-pause]

{img:04-letter-red-ink-on-desk}

Over the next ten weeks, four more women will die in these same streets. The whole city will be afraid. Letters will arrive, written by someone who says he is the killer. Detectives will question more than two thousand people.

[long-pause]

{img:05-constable-lantern-foggy-alley}

And the man behind it all will simply disappear. He will never be caught. No one will ever know his real name. But the world will remember the name he was given. Jack the Ripper.

[long-pause]

{img:06-five-candles-dark-room}

This is the story of the autumn of terror. Of the narrow streets, the fear, and the people of Whitechapel. Of five women who deserve to be remembered. And of a mystery that is still unsolved, more than a hundred and thirty years later.

[long-pause]

{img:07-gas-lamp-in-fog}

So turn down the lights. Get comfortable. And let us go back to the autumn of 1888.

[long-pause]

{img:08-london-skyline-1888-thames-dusk}

In 1888, London was the biggest city in the world. More than five million people lived there. It was the heart of a great empire. Ships came up the River Thames from every corner of the globe. They brought tea from India, wool from Australia, and cotton from America. In the West End, there were grand theatres, fine shops and wide, clean streets. Rich families rode in shiny carriages to parties and concerts. Queen Victoria had been on the throne for more than fifty years. For many people, it was a time of wealth and pride.

[long-pause]

{img:09-east-end-crowded-street-dusk}

But only a few miles to the east, the city looked very different. This was the East End. Here, the streets were narrow and dirty. The houses were old and crowded. Many families lived in a single room, with one window and no running water. The air smelled of smoke, horses and the river. Children played barefoot in the mud. The people of the East End worked hard, but they were very poor. And at the centre of the East End was a district called Whitechapel.

[long-pause]

{img:10-whitechapel-high-street-market-day}

Whitechapel was full of life. In the daytime, the main road was crowded with carts, market stalls and shoppers. People sold fish, fruit, old clothes and hot potatoes. There were butchers, bakers, tailors and pubs on every corner. Men worked at the docks, in the breweries and in small workshops. Women worked long hours sewing clothes or making matchboxes at home, for very little money. Everyone was busy. Everyone was trying to earn enough for one more day.

[long-pause]

{img:11-immigrant-family-arriving-with-bundles}

Whitechapel was also a place of newcomers. In the 1880s, thousands of Jewish families arrived from Russia and Poland. They had escaped violence and hunger in their home countries. They came with almost nothing, carrying their bundles through the streets. Many of them worked as tailors or bootmakers. They spoke Yiddish, and they opened small shops and prayer houses. Irish families lived here too, and sailors from many lands. Whitechapel was crowded, noisy and full of different voices.

[long-pause]

{img:12-common-lodging-house-dormitory-beds}

For the poorest people, home was a common lodging house. These were large, cheap houses where anyone could rent a bed for one night. In Whitechapel alone, there were more than two hundred of them. Every night, around eight thousand people slept there. A single bed cost fourpence. A double bed cost eightpence. The rooms were long and crowded, with rows of narrow beds. There was a kitchen downstairs with a big fire, where people cooked and talked late into the night.

[long-pause]

{img:13-lodging-house-kitchen-fire-night}

But the rule was simple. No money, no bed. Every evening, the deputy of the lodging house stood at the door and asked for the fourpence. If you could not pay, you had to go back out into the street. Many people walked all night, or slept in doorways and on stairs. Some women had no other way to earn the money than to sell themselves to strangers. It was dangerous, and everybody knew it. But for many, there was simply no other choice.

[long-pause]

{img:14-police-constable-on-beat-night}

The streets were watched by the Metropolitan Police. Constables walked slow, regular beats through the district, all through the night. Each man carried a lantern, a wooden truncheon and a whistle. They checked doors and windows, and they knew the faces of the people on their streets. But the area was large, the alleys were dark, and there were not many of them. A constable might pass the same corner only once every half an hour. A lot can happen in half an hour.

[long-pause]

{img:15-dark-courtyard-george-yard-buildings}

In the spring and summer of 1888, there had already been trouble. In April, a woman named Emma Smith was attacked by a group of young men, and she later died. In August, a woman named Martha Tabram was found dead on a staircase in George Yard Buildings. The police never found who did it. The people of Whitechapel were worried. But violence was not new in these streets. Nobody yet knew that something much darker was about to begin.

[long-pause]

{img:16-woman-in-bonnet-walking-away-fog}

Let us go back to the woman in Buck's Row. Mary Ann Nichols was born in London in 1845. Her friends called her Polly. Her father was a locksmith. When she was a young woman, she married a printer named William Nichols. Together, they had five children. For a while, they lived a simple and ordinary life, in a small house south of the river. There was work, there was food, and there was a family around the table.

[long-pause]

{img:17-workhouse-building-grey-day}

But the marriage did not last. Around 1880, Polly and William separated. The children stayed with their father. Polly was alone, and she had very little money. For the next eight years, she moved from place to place. She stayed in workhouses, where the poorest people of London were given food and a bed in return for hard work. She stayed with friends. She slept in lodging houses. Life became harder every year.

[long-pause]

{img:18-large-victorian-house-servant-kitchen}

In the spring of 1888, it looked as if her luck might change. She found a job as a servant in a family home in Wandsworth. She wrote a letter to her father, saying that her new employers were kind people, and that she was settling in. But the new life did not last. After only a few months, she left the house. She took some clothes with her. Soon she was back in the lodging houses of Whitechapel, with nothing.

[long-pause]

{img:19-thrawl-street-lodging-house-door-night}

On the night of the thirtieth of August, Polly was at a lodging house in Thrawl Street. It was very late, and she had been drinking. She did not have the fourpence for her bed. The deputy sent her away. Polly did not seem worried. She laughed and pointed to her hat. It was a new black straw bonnet. She said she would soon get her money, and she told them to keep a bed for her. Then she walked out into the night.

[long-pause]

{img:20-street-corner-church-clock-night}

At about half past two in the morning, a friend of hers named Emily Holland saw her near the corner of Whitechapel Road. The bell of a nearby church was striking the hour. Polly was tired and unsteady. She told Emily that she had already earned the money for her bed three times that day, and spent it every time. Emily tried to bring her home, but Polly would not come. She said she would be back soon. Then she walked away, along the dark road. Emily was the last friend to see her alive.

[long-pause]

{img:21-two-men-in-dark-street-lantern}

About an hour later, a carman named Charles Cross was on his way to work. In Buck's Row, he saw the shape on the ground, beside a gateway. Another worker, Robert Paul, came walking up the street. The two men looked together. It was too dark to see much. They decided to go and find a policeman. A few minutes later, a constable named John Neil came down Buck's Row on his beat. He shone his lantern on the ground, and he understood at once that something terrible had happened.

[long-pause]

{img:22-doctor-bag-and-lantern-on-cobblestones}

A doctor was called from his house nearby. He arrived quickly, and he said that the woman had died only a short time before. She was taken to a small mortuary nearby. At first, nobody knew who she was. The only clue was in her clothes. Her petticoat had a mark from the Lambeth Workhouse. Through that mark, the police slowly found her name. Her husband came to see her, and so did her father. Her husband said quietly that he forgave her for everything.

[long-pause]

{img:23-coroner-court-room-wooden-benches}

The police were puzzled. There had been no scream. Nobody living in Buck's Row had heard a sound. A woman sleeping in a house only a few steps away had heard nothing at all. The killer had come and gone in the dark, in a street where a constable walked past every half an hour. And he had left no weapon, no footprint, and no trace behind. How was that possible? And would he do it again?
