### NA 14
{img:14a-constable-checking-door-handle-night}

Imagine one of those constables on a long night in Whitechapel. He starts his beat at ten in the evening. He walks slowly, about two and a half miles an hour, along the same streets, again and again. He tries the handles of shop doors to make sure they are locked. He looks into dark doorways with his lantern. He hears the pubs closing, the drunk men singing, the cats on the walls, and the trains passing on the railway bridges. Towards dawn, the streets are almost silent. Then, at six in the morning, his long night is over.

[long-pause]

{img:14b-thick-yellow-fog-street-lamps}

And then there was the fog. In the autumn and winter, London was famous for it. Thousands of chimneys burned coal day and night, and the smoke mixed with the damp air from the river. On some nights, the fog was so thick and yellow that a man could not see his own hand in front of his face. Londoners called it a pea-souper. Sounds became soft and strange. Footsteps seemed to come from nowhere. A person could pass within a few feet of you and never be seen.

[long-pause]

### NA 19
{img:19a-shared-bedroom-lodging-house-two-beds}

In her last weeks, Polly shared a room with a few other women in a lodging house in Thrawl Street. One of them was Emily Holland, an older woman who became her friend. They shared a bed, as many poor women did, to save money. Emily later said that Polly was a quiet woman, who kept herself to herself, and who seemed to be a clean and decent person when she was not drinking. Just before the end of August, Polly moved to another lodging house nearby. But she and Emily stayed friends.

### NA 26
{img:26a-two-women-talking-near-church-railings}

In the days before her death, Annie felt very ill. On the Monday, she met her friend Amelia Palmer in the street near Christ Church. Annie had a bruise on her face from a fight. She said she felt unwell, and that she might go to the hospital. Amelia gave her twopence to buy a cup of tea, and told her not to spend it on rum. On the Friday afternoon, they met again. Annie said she felt too ill to do anything. But a little later, she said, "It's no use giving way. I must pull myself together and go out and get some money, or I shall have no lodgings."

### NA 36
{img:36a-woman-cleaning-room-bucket-window}

On the twenty-ninth of September, Elizabeth spent the afternoon cleaning rooms at her lodging house in Flower and Dean Street. The deputy paid her sixpence for the work. In the evening, she borrowed a clothes brush from a man who lived there, to make herself look neat. She was wearing a black jacket with a small red flower pinned to it. She went out at about seven o'clock. She seemed cheerful. Nobody at the lodging house knew that they would never see her again.

### NA 39
{img:39a-pawnshop-window-boots-gaslight}

When Catherine and John Kelly came back from Kent, they had almost nothing left. On the last Saturday of September, John pawned his own boots to buy them some breakfast. In the afternoon, Catherine said she would go and find her daughter, who lived on the other side of the river. She hoped her daughter would give her a little money. She promised John that she would be back by four o'clock. He told her to be careful, because of the killer. Catherine laughed. She said that she knew how to look after herself.

### NA 46
{img:46a-old-army-officer-portrait-room-maps}

The man at the head of the Metropolitan Police was Sir Charles Warren. He was an army officer and an engineer. As a young man, he had worked in Jerusalem, exploring the tunnels and water shafts under the old city, and one of them still carries his name today. He was brave and clever, but he was a soldier, not a detective. Many newspapers did not like him. They said he cared more about marching and order than about catching criminals.

[long-pause]

{img:46b-detective-hat-coat-on-hook-office}

Inspector Frederick Abberline was a very different kind of man. He was quiet, careful and patient. He had grown up in a small town in Dorset, and he had joined the police as a young man. He spent most of his working life walking the streets of the East End, and he knew many of the people there by name. After the murders, he never said who he believed the killer was. When he left the police in 1892, he worked for a private detective agency, and later he retired to the seaside, where he lived quietly until he was eighty-six.

### NA 55
{img:55a-coal-mine-valley-wales-mist}

Mary Jane's story of her early life is hard to check. According to Joseph Barnett, she told him that she had married young, in Wales, to a coal miner named Davies. She said that her husband had died in an explosion in a mine, only a few years after their marriage. After that, she moved to Cardiff, and later to London. Some of this may be true. Some of it may not. Historians have searched the records in Ireland and Wales for many years. They have never found her for certain.

### NA 62
{img:62a-vigilance-men-with-lanterns-night-patrol}

What happened to the men who had searched for the killer? The Vigilance Committee slowly stopped its night patrols, when the murders stopped. George Lusk went back to his work as a builder, but for a long time he received cruel letters from people pretending to be the killer. The police went back to their ordinary work. In the lodging houses, the fear faded, little by little. By the spring of 1889, the streets of Whitechapel looked much the same as before.
