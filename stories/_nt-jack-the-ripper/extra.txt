### NA 15
{img:15a-petticoat-lane-sunday-market-stalls}

On Sunday mornings, the streets near Whitechapel turned into one great market. The most famous was Petticoat Lane. Hundreds of stalls stood side by side, from one end of the street to the other. You could buy almost anything there. Old coats, new shoes, pots and pans, birds in cages, toys, buttons and lace. The sellers shouted their prices. Buyers argued and laughed. For a few hours every week, the poorest people in London could feel that the whole world was for sale on their own doorstep.

[long-pause]

{img:15b-street-food-seller-baked-potatoes-night}

Food in Whitechapel was simple and cheap. Most people lived on bread, butter or dripping, and strong, sweet tea. On the street corners, sellers stood beside small iron ovens, selling hot baked potatoes for a halfpenny. There were stalls with jellied eels, pea soup and hot coffee. In the evening, the smell of fried fish came from the little shops. For a working family, meat was a treat, often only once a week, on a Sunday.

[long-pause]

{img:15c-music-hall-stage-gaslights-audience}

In the evenings, people looked for a little joy. The pubs were warm and bright, with gas lamps and polished wood. In the music halls, singers and comedians performed on small stages, and the whole audience sang along to the songs they knew. For a few pennies, a tired worker could forget his troubles for an hour. When the halls closed late at night, the crowds poured out into the dark streets, singing and talking on their way home.

[long-pause]

{img:15d-children-in-ragged-school-classroom}

There were many children in Whitechapel. Some went to school. Others worked from a very young age, selling matches, running errands, or helping their mothers at home. Some had no home at all. Kind people tried to help them. A young doctor named Thomas Barnardo opened homes for children who lived on the streets. Ragged schools gave free lessons to the poorest boys and girls. It was not enough. But it was a beginning.

[long-pause]

{img:15e-salvation-army-band-in-street}

Whitechapel was also a place of hope. Some years before, a preacher named William Booth had started holding meetings in a tent in Whitechapel. His movement became the Salvation Army. Its members marched through the streets with drums and brass instruments, singing hymns and offering soup and a bed to those who needed them. Near the main road, a house called Toynbee Hall had opened, where young men from the universities lived and worked among the poor, teaching classes and trying to understand their lives.

[long-pause]

{img:15f-young-women-factory-gate-match-factory}

In the summer of 1888, something happened that surprised all of London. At a match factory in the East End, hundreds of young women and girls walked out of their jobs. They were angry about low pay, long hours and unfair fines. And they were sick from the chemicals used to make the matches. The match girls' strike made headlines. After two weeks, the owners agreed to many of their demands. The poorest workers in London had shown that they too had a voice.

[long-pause]

### NA 23
{img:23a-coroner-court-wooden-benches-window}

A few days later, an inquest was held. This was a public hearing to find out how Polly had died. It was led by the coroner, a man named Wynne Baxter. He listened to the doctor, to the policemen, and to the men who had found her. Reporters wrote down every word. The inquest went on for several days. In the end, the jury could only say that she had been murdered by a person unknown. Polly was buried a week after her death, in a cemetery in the east of London.

[long-pause]

### NA 31
{img:31a-doctor-desk-medical-books-lamp}

At the inquest for Annie Chapman, the police doctor, George Bagster Phillips, gave his evidence. He said something that frightened people even more. He believed that the killer had some knowledge of the human body, the kind of knowledge that a doctor or a butcher might have. Was the killer a medical man? A slaughterman? The idea spread quickly through the newspapers. Later, other doctors disagreed with him. But the image of a skilled and cold-blooded killer stayed in people's minds.

[long-pause]

### NA 45
{img:45a-printing-press-newspaper-sheets}

The newspapers had never seen a story like this. In 1888, cheap newspapers were new, and millions of people could read them. Papers like The Star printed new editions every few hours, with more and more dramatic stories. Some reporters invented details. Some blamed the police for everything. Sales went up and up. One magazine printed a famous cartoon of a policeman wearing a blindfold, turning in circles while criminals laughed around him. The story of Jack the Ripper was perhaps the first great media story of the modern age.

[long-pause]

{img:45b-cemetery-gate-autumn-trees-mourners}

Elizabeth Stride and Catherine Eddowes were buried in the first week of October. Elizabeth's funeral was quiet and simple, paid for by the parish. Catherine's was very different. Thousands of people came to watch her coffin pass through the streets. John Kelly, the man she had lived with for seven years, walked behind it, together with her sisters. Crowds stood in silence along the road. For one day, the people of the East End came together to say goodbye to one of their own.

[long-pause]

### NA 60
{img:60a-palace-writing-desk-telegram}

Even Queen Victoria was worried. After the murder in Miller's Court, she sent an urgent message to the Prime Minister, Lord Salisbury. She wrote that this new murder showed that something must be done, and quickly. All the dark courts, she said, must be lit, and the detectives must be improved. The Queen of half the world was asking questions about a small dark court in Whitechapel. It showed how far the fear had spread.

[long-pause]

### NA 68
{img:68a-old-books-on-shelf-candle}

The story of Jack the Ripper never went away. Soon after 1888, the first books about the murders were written. Then came plays, and later films and television programmes. Today there are hundreds of books about the case. People who study it call themselves Ripperologists. They meet, they argue, and they search old archives for new clues. Every year, visitors from all over the world walk through the streets of Whitechapel at night, following guides with lanterns, listening to the story once more.

[long-pause]

{img:68b-old-photographs-census-papers-desk}

In 2019, a historian named Hallie Rubenhold wrote a book called The Five. It did not try to find the killer. Instead, it told the life stories of the five women, from their childhoods to their last days. She searched old records, letters and census papers. She showed that these women were not just names in a crime story. They had grown up, fallen in love, raised children, worked and struggled. Many readers said that the book changed the way they saw the whole case.
