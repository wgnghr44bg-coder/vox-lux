A few years after the plague, Boccaccio began to write the book that would make him famous.

He called it the Decameron, which means ten days, in Greek. It is a book of a hundred stories. And it begins, not with a story, but with Florence in the summer of 1348.

--

In the opening pages, Boccaccio describes the plague in the city, honestly and simply, as he saw it.

Then he tells how, one Tuesday morning, in the middle of that terrible summer, seven young women meet by chance in the great church of Santa Maria Novella, after Mass. They are friends, all from good families, between eighteen and twenty-eight years old. They are sad and lonely, because so many of their family and friends are gone, and the city has become so quiet and so sorrowful.

The eldest of them, whom Boccaccio calls Pampinea, has an idea. Why should they stay in the city, she asks, among so much sadness? Why not go out into the countryside, to one of their families' villas, and live there quietly together for a while, among the gardens and the fields, with music and good food and good company, until the sickness has passed?

--

The others agree. But they say that they cannot go alone, without some men to accompany them.

Just at that moment, three young men come into the church. They are friends of the young women, gentle, well-mannered young men, who are also sad and lonely. The young women ask them to come too. And the three young men gladly agree.

{img:three-young-men-entering-church-sunlight}
The next morning, at dawn, the ten young people leave Florence, with a few servants, and walk out through the gates, and up into the hills, to a beautiful villa, about two miles from the city.

--

Boccaccio describes the villa with great love.

It stands on a little hill, a little way from the road, surrounded by trees and green plants. It has a courtyard in the middle, with loggias and galleries and fine rooms, all decorated with cheerful paintings. Around it there are meadows and wonderful gardens, with wells of cool, fresh water, and cellars full of good wine.

Everything has been swept and cleaned, and the beds are made, and there are flowers everywhere, of every kind of the season, and rushes strewn on the floors. The young people are delighted.

--

They decide to live a simple, happy life together.

Each day, one of them will be the king or queen of the day, and will decide what they all do. They get up early, and walk in the gardens, among the roses and the jasmine, singing and making garlands of flowers. They eat a fine meal at midday, at tables set with white linen, beside a fountain, with wine and sweetmeats.

{img:young-people-garden-table-white-linen-fountain}
Then, in the heat of the afternoon, when it is too hot to walk, they gather in a shady meadow, in the green grass, under the trees, and sit in a circle.

And each of them, in turn, tells a story.

--

Every afternoon, each of the ten tells one story, so that there are ten stories each day.

The king or queen of the day chooses a theme. One day, the stories are about people who were lucky, after many misfortunes. Another day, about people who got what they wanted through their own cleverness. Another, about lovers whose love ended happily. Another, about clever answers and quick wits.

{img:young-people-sitting-circle-meadow-shade}
The stories are of every kind. Some are funny, some are sad, some are romantic, some are a little bit naughty. There are merchants and monks, kings and peasants, lovers and tricksters, sailors and pirates, princesses and cooks. They take place in Florence and Naples, in Paris and Egypt, in Sicily and on the sea.

Many of them are stories that Boccaccio heard in the taverns and the markets and the houses of his friends, and made his own.

--

In the evenings, after supper, the young people dance in the garden, and sing songs, and play music on the lute and the viol.

At the end of each day, one of them sings a song, about love, or the spring, or the beauty of the evening. And then they go to bed, in their cool, quiet rooms, with the windows open to the night, and the sound of the fountain in the garden, and the nightingales singing in the trees.

On Fridays and Saturdays, they do not tell stories, because Friday is the day of Christ's suffering, and on Saturday the ladies wash their hair.

--

They stay for two weeks. In all, they tell one hundred stories.

Then, at the end of the tenth day of storytelling, they decide that it is time to go home. They have rested, and laughed, and been happy together, and they feel stronger now. They walk back down the hill to Florence, and say goodbye to each other at the church of Santa Maria Novella, where they met, and go home to their families.

{img:walking-down-hill-toward-florence-evening}
Boccaccio does not tell us what happened to them afterwards.

--

The Decameron was finished a few years after the plague, perhaps around 1353.

It quickly became one of the most popular books in Europe. People copied it out by hand, and read it aloud to each other, and laughed and cried over it. Later, writers all over Europe borrowed its stories: Chaucer in England, Shakespeare, and many others.

Some people think of it as a strange book to write about a time of such great sorrow. But Boccaccio knew what he was doing. He wanted to give his readers, who had suffered so much, something to make them smile again. He wrote that his book was for those who were sad, and especially for ladies in love, to comfort them.

And in a way, it is a book about how people, even in the darkest times, can still find beauty, and laughter, and friendship, and stories.
