The streets of Florence are busy from dawn to dusk.

They are narrow and paved with large, flat stones, and in many of them, two carts cannot pass each other. The upper storeys of the houses lean out over the street, held up by wooden beams, so that the sky above is only a thin blue strip, and the streets below are cool and shadowy, even in summer.

--

On the ground floors of the houses, there are shops.

Each shop is a single room, open to the street through a wide arched doorway. In the morning, the shopkeeper takes down the wooden shutter, and folds it out to make a counter, and sets out his goods on it. Behind him, in the dim room, his family and his apprentices are at work.

There are shops for everything. There are bakers and butchers, cheesemakers and grocers selling spices and salt and dried fruit. There are shoemakers and tailors, hatters and glovemakers, goldsmiths and silversmiths, booksellers and parchment-makers, apothecaries with jars of herbs and medicines, and barbers who also pull teeth and let blood.

--

The people of Florence belong to guilds.

A guild is a society of all the people who do the same kind of work: the wool merchants, the silk makers, the bankers, the doctors and apothecaries, the judges and lawyers, the butchers, the bakers, the stonemasons. Each guild has its own rules, its own officers, its own coat of arms and its own patron saint, and its own church or chapel, where its members meet and pray.

{img:guild-hall-meeting-merchants-banners}
The guilds are very powerful. Only members of the guilds can be elected to the government of the city. Every two months, the names of the men who will govern Florence are drawn from leather bags, and they move into the government palace, and live there together for their two months of office, eating and sleeping and working in the great stone building, and then go home again.

--

The greatest of all the guilds is the guild of the wool merchants.

About a third of all the people in Florence earn their living from wool, in one way or another. Most of them are poor. They are the washers and the beaters, who clean the raw wool in the river; the carders and the combers, who straighten its fibres; the spinners, mostly women working at home with their spindles and distaffs; the weavers at their great wooden looms; the dyers, with their arms stained blue and red and yellow from the great vats of dye.

{img:wool-dyers-vats-river-coloured-cloth}
Along the banks of the Arno, there are long rows of wooden frames, where the newly dyed cloth is stretched out to dry in the sun, in long bands of colour, scarlet and blue and green and deep violet, like great flags waving in the wind.

--

In the middle of the city, there is a market square, called the Mercato Vecchio, the Old Market.

Every morning, it is crowded. There are stalls selling vegetables and fruit, fresh from the countryside: cabbages and onions, beans and lettuces, figs and grapes and apples, according to the season. There are butchers and poulterers, fishmongers and cheesemakers, sellers of bread and wine and oil.

{img:mercato-vecchio-stalls-crowd-morning}
There are pedlars, and jugglers, and storytellers, and beggars sitting on the church steps. Children run between the stalls, chasing each other and the dogs and the chickens. Women stand and gossip at the well. Men stand in the doorways of the taverns, arguing about politics and prices.

--

Not far from the market, there is a strange and beautiful building, called Orsanmichele.

It is a tall, square building of stone, with wide open arches on the ground floor. Down below, it is the city's grain market, where the wheat is stored and sold. But it is also a church. Inside, among the sacks of grain, there is a beautiful painting of the Virgin Mary, which the people of Florence believe can work miracles.

Just this year, a painter named Bernardo Daddi has finished a new painting of the Madonna to hang there, glowing with gold, and every evening, people come to light candles in front of it, and kneel and pray, and sing hymns together.

--

In the late afternoon, when the work is done, the people of Florence come out into the streets and squares to walk and talk.

{img:evening-piazza-strolling-families-lanterns}
The rich merchants walk in their long gowns of fine wool, red and blue and green, lined with fur in winter. Their wives walk beside them in their best dresses, with their hair braided and covered with a fine veil. The craftsmen and their families walk in their plainer clothes. Young men stand at the corners in groups, laughing and watching the girls go by.

As the evening comes on, the bells of the churches ring for evening prayer, one after another, all over the city, and the lamps are lit in front of the little shrines on the street corners, and the shopkeepers fold up their counters and close their shutters, and go inside for supper.
