Florence is still there today, in its valley among the hills of Tuscany, on the banks of the river Arno.

From the hill of Fiesole, or from the terraces on the hills across the river, it looks very much as it did in the time of Boccaccio: a sea of red-tiled roofs, with towers and bell towers and church domes rising above them, and the river winding through the middle, crossed by its bridges, and the green hills all around, dotted with villas and cypress trees.

--

In the middle of it all, there is the cathedral, finished at last.

Its walls are covered with white and green and pink marble, in patterns of squares and stars and stripes, just as the builders of the fourteenth century planned them. And on top of it rises Brunelleschi's great dome, of red brick, with white ribs, so large that it seems to float above the city, and can be seen from everywhere in Florence, and from far out in the countryside.

Beside it stands Giotto's bell tower, tall and slender, with the little marble panels on its lowest storey, showing the ploughman and the weaver, the shepherd and the sailor and the astronomer, just as they did in 1347. Visitors can climb the more than four hundred steps to the top, and look out over the whole city.

--

The Baptistery of Saint John is still there, across the square, with Andrea Pisano's bronze doors, and the golden mosaics glittering on its domed ceiling.

{img:baptistery-golden-mosaics-visitors-quiet}
Every year, on the twenty-fourth of June, the feast of Saint John, the city still celebrates, with a procession, and a parade in medieval costume, and fireworks over the river at night.

--

The Ponte Vecchio still crosses the river, with its little shops hanging out over the water.

But the butchers and the fishmongers and the tanners are gone. Long ago, in the sixteenth century, the ruler of Florence ordered that only goldsmiths and jewellers should have shops on the bridge, because the smell of the butchers offended him. And so today, the little shops on the bridge glitter with gold and silver and precious stones.

In the evening, people stand on the bridge and watch the sun go down over the river, as Boccaccio once did, turning the water of the Arno to gold.

--

Orsanmichele still stands in the centre of the city, the tall square building that was once the grain market and the church of the Madonna.

Inside, in the dim light, Bernardo Daddi's painting of the Madonna, which the people of Florence prayed to in the summer of 1348, is still there, glowing with gold, in a beautiful marble shrine that was built around it in the years after the plague, with the money that the people gave.

{img:orsanmichele-shrine-golden-madonna-candles}
Around the outside of the building, in niches in the walls, there are statues of the patron saints of the guilds, made by the greatest sculptors of the Renaissance.

--

And the Misericordia is still there too.

More than seven hundred and fifty years after it was founded, the brotherhood still has its headquarters on the cathedral square, in Florence. Its members, men and women, are volunteers, as they always have been. They no longer wear black robes and hoods. But they still drive ambulances, and care for the sick and the old and the poor, and help people in need, all over the city, every day and every night.

{img:volunteer-ambulance-cathedral-square-today}
It is one of the oldest charities in the world, still doing the same work that its brothers did in the summer of 1348.

--

The hospital of Santa Maria Nuova, founded by the father of Dante's Beatrice, is still a hospital, in the same place, still caring for the sick, more than seven hundred years later.

In Certaldo, Boccaccio's little red brick house still stands, with its tower, and the town still holds a festival in his honour. And in libraries all over the world, people still read the Decameron, and laugh at its stories, and think about the ten young people in the villa on the hill, telling stories to each other in the summer of 1348.

In the old Laurentian Library in Florence, and in other libraries, there are still old manuscripts of the Decameron, copied out by hand in those first years after Boccaccio wrote it.

--

In the evening, Florence grows quiet.

The tourists go back to their hotels. The shops close. The swallows wheel and cry over the rooftops in the last of the light. The dome of the cathedral glows red and gold in the sunset, and then grey, and then the lamps come on, and light it from below.

{img:florence-dusk-dome-lit-swallows-sky}
Out in the hills, among the olive groves and the cypresses, the villas stand quietly in their gardens, as they did when the young people of the Decameron walked among the roses and the fountains, singing. The fireflies come out among the olive trees. The bells of the little churches ring for the evening prayer.

--

So we leave Florence for tonight, the old city of stone in its valley among the hills, with its great dome glowing softly under the stars, and the river flowing quietly beneath its bridges, as it has done for a thousand years.
