The spring of 1348 comes to Florence as it always does.

The almond trees blossom pink and white on the hillsides. The days grow longer and warmer. The swallows come back from Africa and build their nests under the eaves of the houses. In the fields, the wheat is green and growing. In the vineyards, the first leaves are unfolding on the vines.

--

In the city, people go about their business, as usual.

The wool workers are at their looms, the merchants in their offices, the shopkeepers at their counters. The children go to school. The friars preach in the squares. The government meets in its palace. The masons go on building the cathedral, and the bell tower rises a little higher, stone by stone.

But everyone is anxious. The news from Pisa, and from other cities, is not good. The sickness is there, and it is spreading. Every day, travellers come into Florence with new stories. And everyone wonders when it will reach their own city.

--

The churches are full.

People go to Mass every day, and to confession, and they pray more often and more earnestly than usual. The friars lead processions through the streets, carrying crosses and banners and holy relics, followed by long lines of people, singing hymns and praying for God to spare the city.

{img:penitent-procession-candles-hymns-street}
At Orsanmichele, the people crowd in front of the painting of the Madonna, lighting candles, and leaving gifts of money, and asking her to protect them. So much money is given at Orsanmichele in these months that the brotherhood which looks after it becomes one of the richest in the city.

--

The government of Florence does what it can.

It orders the streets to be cleaned, the rubbish to be taken away, and the drains to be cleared. It forbids the sale of rotten meat and fish. It forbids travellers from Pisa and Genoa from entering the city. It appoints officials to make sure that its orders are carried out.

The doctors of the city meet, and discuss the sickness, and write advice for the people. Eat simple food, they say. Avoid bad air. Burn sweet-smelling herbs and woods in your houses, juniper and rosemary and bay. Carry a little bunch of flowers or herbs to hold to your nose when you go out. Keep calm, and cheerful, and do not be afraid, for fear itself makes people ill.

--

In his house, Giovanni Villani goes on writing his chronicle.

He writes about the sickness in the East, and in Sicily, and in Pisa and Genoa. He writes, as he always does, carefully and calmly, setting down what he has heard, and what he has seen.

{img:old-chronicler-desk-window-spring-light}
In Boccaccio's house, the young writer is working on his books, and visiting his friends, and listening to the news. He is worried, like everyone else.

--

Some time in March or April 1348, the sickness reaches Florence.

Nobody knows exactly how it came. Perhaps it came with a traveller from Pisa, or a merchant with a bale of cloth, or a carter with a load of grain. Perhaps it came hidden in the fleas of a rat in a cart. However it came, it came quietly, and at first, almost nobody noticed.

A few people fell ill in one quarter of the city, and then a few more in another. The doctors were called, and shook their heads. The priests were called. The neighbours whispered.

And then, quite suddenly, everyone knew that the sickness was in Florence.

--

In the spring evenings, the people of Florence still come out into the streets and the squares, as they always have, as the light fades and the bells ring for evening prayer.

But they walk more quietly now, and they do not stay out as long. They speak in low voices. They look at each other with worried eyes. Some of them hold little bunches of herbs or flowers to their faces as they pass.

{img:lanterns-shrine-street-corner-dusk}
In front of the little shrines on the street corners, the lamps are burning, and there are more flowers than usual, and more candles. People stop, and kneel, and pray, and then hurry home, and close their doors behind them.

The river flows on beneath the bridges, as it always has, and the swallows wheel in the twilight over the rooftops, and the stars come out over the hills.
