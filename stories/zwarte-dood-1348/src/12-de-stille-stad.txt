By the middle of the summer of 1348, Florence had become strangely quiet.

The markets were nearly empty. Many of the shops were closed. The looms of the wool workers were silent. The masons had stopped working on the cathedral, and the cranes stood still above the half-built walls. The streets, which had once been so crowded, were almost deserted, especially in the heat of the afternoon.

--

Many people had left.

Those who could afford it had gone to their villas and farms in the countryside, where the air was fresher, and where there were fewer people, hoping that the sickness would not follow them there. Others had gone to stay with relatives in other towns. The government of the city had almost stopped meeting, because so many of its members had gone away.

Those who stayed tried to live as best they could.

--

Some people shut themselves up in their houses, with a few friends and family, and lived very quietly, eating simple food, drinking a little good wine, listening to music, and trying not to think about what was happening outside.

Others did the opposite. They decided that, since life might be short, they would enjoy it while they could, and went from tavern to tavern, eating and drinking and singing.

{img:courtyard-lute-player-quiet-family-evening}
Most people simply went on, day by day, doing what needed to be done, looking after their families and their neighbours, going to church, and hoping.

--

The sickness did not go on forever.

In the heat of July and August, it was at its worst. Then, as autumn came, and the weather grew cooler, it began, slowly, to grow less. Fewer people fell ill. More of those who fell ill recovered. By the winter, it had almost gone.

But when it was over, the city had changed.

Nobody knows exactly how many people in Florence died that year. Boccaccio wrote that it was more than a hundred thousand, but he was probably exaggerating, in his grief. Today, historians think that perhaps half of the people of the city, or a little more, were lost. Some families lost everyone. Others lost no one at all.

--

Florence was not alone.

All over Italy, the same thing happened, in city after city, town after town: in Venice and Genoa, in Siena and Pisa, in Bologna and Naples, in little villages in the mountains, and in monasteries far from any town. Everywhere, perhaps a third or a half of the people were lost.

{img:italian-hill-village-quiet-dusk-church}
And it did not stop at Italy.

--

In the summer of 1348, the sickness crossed the Alps, and spread into France and Germany and Switzerland and Austria. In June, a ship brought it to a little port called Melcombe, on the south coast of England, and from there, it spread across England, reaching London in the autumn.

In 1349, it reached Ireland and Scotland and Wales, and the Low Countries, and crossed the sea to Norway, on a ship from England. In 1350, it reached Sweden and Denmark, and the far north. And slowly, it moved eastward again, across Poland and into Russia, until, in the early 1350s, it had touched almost every corner of Europe.

Today, historians believe that between a third and a half of all the people in Europe died, in those few years. It was the greatest disaster in the history of Europe.

--

And yet, life went on.

In every city and village, the people who survived picked themselves up, and began again. They buried their dead, with prayers and with tears. They went back to their fields and their workshops. They married, and had children. They planted crops, and harvested them, and baked bread, and brewed beer, and sang songs, and told stories.

{img:farmer-ploughing-spring-field-after-loss}
And in the spring of 1349, in Florence, the almond trees blossomed on the hillsides, and the swallows came back from Africa, and the bells rang out across the city for the morning prayers, as they always had.
