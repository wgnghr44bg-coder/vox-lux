A wealthy merchant's house in Florence is built around a small courtyard.

From the street, it looks plain and strong, with thick stone walls, small barred windows on the ground floor, and a heavy wooden door studded with iron nails. But inside the door, there is a little open courtyard, paved with stone, with a well in the middle, and arches around the sides, and sometimes a lemon tree in a pot, or a climbing vine.

--

On the ground floor, around the courtyard, there are storerooms, full of bales of wool and cloth, barrels of wine and jars of oil, and the merchant's office, where he keeps his accounts in great leather-bound books, writing down every sale and every purchase in careful columns of figures.

Up a stone staircase, on the first floor, there is the main room of the house, the sala. It is a large, high room, with a great fireplace at one end, a long wooden table, benches and chests along the walls, and painted beams on the ceiling.

The walls are often painted too, with patterns of flowers and leaves, or with coats of arms, or even with pictures of gardens and trees, so that the family can feel they are sitting outdoors, even in winter.

--

Above the sala, there are the bedrooms.

The beds are very large, big enough for several people, with a wooden frame, a mattress stuffed with wool or feathers, linen sheets, woollen blankets, and in winter, a coverlet of fur. Around the bed there are curtains, to keep out the draughts. At the foot of the bed, there is a great wooden chest, painted and carved, where the family keeps its clothes and linen.

{img:canopy-bed-painted-chest-candle-bedroom}
At the very top of the house, under the roof, there is the kitchen, so that the smoke and the smell of cooking, and the danger of fire, are kept as far as possible from the rest of the house.

--

The day begins early, at sunrise, with the ringing of the church bells.

The family gets up, and washes in basins of cold water, and dresses. The father goes to his office, or to his shop, or to the guild hall. The mother runs the household, with the help of the servants: the cook, the maids, perhaps a nurse for the little children.

{img:mother-servants-kitchen-top-floor-hearth}
In the kitchen at the top of the house, there is a great open hearth, with a fire burning all day, iron pots hanging from chains over it, and a spit for roasting meat. The cook bakes bread, or sends the dough to the baker's oven down the street. She makes soups and stews, of beans and cabbage and onions, with a little meat or salted pork.

--

The main meal of the day is eaten in the late morning, after the morning's work.

The family sits at the long table in the sala. There is bread, of course, and soup, and perhaps a roast of veal or kid, or chicken, or fish from the river, or eggs. There is cheese, and fruit, and nuts, and wine mixed with water, in earthenware jugs. On fast days, which are many, there is no meat at all, only fish and vegetables and bread.

The Florentines are proud of their food. They like strong flavours: pepper and ginger, saffron and cinnamon, garlic and herbs, sour juices from unripe grapes. The rich use costly spices brought by ship from the East, and like to show them off to their guests.

--

{img:mother-teaching-daughter-spinning-window}
The children of the merchants learn to read and write and do sums.

The boys go to a school run by a teacher in the city, where they learn to read Latin, and then to a special school, called an abacus school, where they learn arithmetic and bookkeeping, so that one day they can join their father's business. By the age of fourteen or fifteen, many of them are already working as apprentices in the shops and offices of the merchants.

The girls learn at home, from their mothers, to spin and weave and sew, to run a household, and sometimes to read their prayers. Most of them are married young, at fifteen or sixteen, to men who are often much older.

--

Not every Florentine lives in such a house.

The wool workers and the labourers and the poor live in small rented rooms in the crowded quarters near the walls, or across the river, in Oltrarno. A whole family might live in one or two rooms, with a fireplace for cooking, a bed for everyone to share, a chest, a table and a few stools.

They eat bread, beans and vegetables, soup and a little cheese, and on feast days, a little meat. They fetch their water from the public fountains and wells, and buy their bread from the baker, and their wine from the tavern on the corner.

Life is hard for them, especially in years when the harvest is poor and the price of bread goes up.

--

But rich or poor, the evenings are much the same.

After supper, when it grows dark, the family gathers around the fire, or in summer, on the stone bench outside the front door, or on the roof terrace, where it is cooler. They talk about the day, and the news, and the neighbours. The grandmother tells stories. The children play. Someone sings an old song, or plays a lute.

{img:family-roof-terrace-evening-tuscan-sunset}
And then, as the bells ring out for the last prayers of the day, the candles are blown out, and the city goes to sleep under the stars, with the sound of the river flowing softly beneath the bridges, and the watchmen walking slowly through the dark streets with their lanterns.
