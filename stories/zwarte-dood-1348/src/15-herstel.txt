In the spring of 1349, Florence began to come back to life.

The people who had fled to the countryside came home. The shops opened their shutters again. The looms began to clatter in the workshops. The market in the Mercato Vecchio filled up once more with farmers and their carts. The government met again in its palace. And up on the walls of the cathedral, the masons picked up their chisels, and went back to work.

--

But the city was very different.

There were empty houses on every street. There were workshops with no one to work in them. There were fields in the countryside with no one to plough them, and vineyards with no one to prune them. Many families had lost their parents, or their children, or their brothers and sisters. There were orphans to be cared for, and widows, and old people left alone.

The people of Florence cared for them as best they could. The hospitals and the brotherhoods, which had been given so much money during the plague, used it to feed the poor, to look after the orphans, and to provide dowries for poor girls so that they could marry.

--

There were weddings, many of them.

In the year after the plague, people married in great numbers, young people who had lost their parents, widows and widowers who had lost their husbands and wives. The churches were full of wedding parties, and the streets were full of music and dancing.

{img:medieval-wedding-procession-musicians-flowers}
And soon, there were babies too, many of them, and the sound of children crying and laughing came back into the empty houses.

--

Because so many people had died, there were not enough workers.

The wool merchants needed weavers and dyers and spinners. The farmers needed ploughmen and harvesters. The builders needed masons and carpenters. And so the workers who had survived found that they could ask for more money for their work, and get it.

For the first time in many generations, ordinary working people, the labourers and the craftsmen and the peasants, found that their work was valued, and that they could earn a decent living. They ate better, and dressed better, and some of them were able to buy land of their own.

--

In England, too, the same thing happened.

There were not enough people to work the land. The lords of the manors, who owned the land, needed labourers, and the labourers began to ask for higher wages, or to move from one village to another, looking for a better master. The government tried to pass laws to stop them, and to keep wages low. But the laws did not work very well.

{img:english-peasants-harvest-field-better-times}
Over the next hundred years, the old system, in which peasants were bound to their lord and his land and could not leave, slowly began to fade away. Many people believe that the Black Death, terrible as it was, helped to bring a new and fairer world.

--

The plague also changed how people thought about medicine and health.

Doctors began to study the sickness more carefully, and to write about it. Cities began to appoint health officers, to watch for signs of illness and to keep the streets clean.

In 1377, the little city of Ragusa, on the coast of the Adriatic Sea, which today is called Dubrovnik, made a new law: that any ship or traveller coming from a place where there was sickness must wait for thirty days on a little island outside the city, before they could come in. Later, the time was made forty days, in Italian quaranta giorni. And that is where our word quarantine comes from.

--

The plague also changed art.

In the years after the Black Death, painters in Florence and Siena painted in a new, more serious way. They painted pictures of saints and of the Virgin Mary, and of heaven and the Last Judgment, and of the shortness of life and the hope of the world to come.

{img:painter-fresco-scaffold-church-wall}
But the people of Florence also wanted beauty, and joy, and life. And they wanted to give thanks for their survival. So they gave money to build and decorate churches and chapels, and to commission paintings and statues and stained glass windows. In the years that followed, the city grew more beautiful than ever.

--

The cathedral rose slowly, year by year.

In 1359, Giotto's bell tower was finally finished, tall and slender and beautiful, covered in white and green and pink marble. Today, it is one of the most beautiful bell towers in the world.

And many years later, in the 1420s and 1430s, a brilliant architect named Filippo Brunelleschi would build the great dome on top of the cathedral, the largest dome in the world, without any scaffolding from the ground, using a method that he invented himself. The people of Florence would call it simply the Cupola.

--

The plague came back, more than once, in the years that followed.

It came back in 1363, and again in 1374, and many times after that, over the next three hundred years. Each time, it was less terrible than the first time, but each time, people were afraid. And each time, they survived, and rebuilt, and went on.

{img:tuscan-countryside-recovered-farms-harvest}
And in between, there were many good years. Years of good harvests, and peace, and prosperity, when the vines grew heavy with grapes on the hills of Tuscany, and the olive trees were laden with fruit, and the markets were full, and the children played in the streets, and the bells rang out over the city for weddings and christenings and feast days.
