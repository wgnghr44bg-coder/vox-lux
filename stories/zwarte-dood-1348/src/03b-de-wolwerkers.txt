Across the river from the centre of Florence, in the quarter called Oltrarno, a family of wool workers is waking up before dawn.

They live in two small rooms on the third floor of a narrow old house, up a steep, dark wooden staircase. The father, whom we might call Piero, is a wool comber. His wife, whom we might call Lisa, is a spinner. They have four children: two boys and two girls, the eldest twelve and the youngest four.

--

There is no glass in the windows, only wooden shutters, and in the early morning, a thin grey light comes through the cracks.

Lisa gets up first, and lights the fire in the little fireplace, and warms some leftover soup in an iron pot, and cuts slices from yesterday's loaf of bread. The children wake up slowly, rubbing their eyes, in the big bed that they all share, and climb out onto the cold floor.

They eat their breakfast quickly, sitting on stools around the little table: a bowl of bean soup, a piece of bread, and a cup of watered wine.

--

Then Piero goes off to work.

He walks down the stairs, and through the narrow streets, still dark and quiet, and across the bridge to the workshop of the wool merchant who employs him. There, in a long room on the ground floor, he works with a dozen other men, combing the wool, all day long.

{img:wool-combers-workshop-long-room-morning}
It is hard, hot, dusty work. The combs are heavy iron tools with long sharp teeth, which must be heated over a little stove so that they glide through the wool. The combers pull them through the greasy, tangled fleece again and again, until the fibres are straight and smooth and shining, ready to be spun.

The workshop is noisy and full of fluff, which floats in the air and settles on everything. At midday, the men stop for a short meal, of bread and cheese and onions, sitting on the floor of the workshop or out in the street.

--

At home, Lisa spins.

The merchant's agent comes to the house every few days, bringing her a basket of combed wool, and taking away the yarn she has spun. She sits by the window, where the light is best, with her distaff tucked under her arm, holding the cloud of soft wool, and her spindle turning and turning beside her knee, drawing out the thread, fine and even.

{img:woman-spinning-wool-window-distaff-spindle}
She has spun since she was a little girl, and her hands move without thinking. While she spins, she watches the children, and talks to the neighbours across the narrow street, whose windows are only a few feet away, and sings old songs under her breath.

--

The children have their own work.

The eldest boy, who is twelve, is already working as an errand boy in a wool workshop, running between the workshops with bundles of wool and cloth. The eldest girl, who is ten, helps her mother with the spinning, and the cooking, and the little ones. The younger children play in the street with the other children of the quarter, and are sent to fetch water from the fountain, or bread from the baker.

{img:children-fetching-water-fountain-narrow-street}
In the afternoon, the eldest girl goes to the market with her mother, to buy beans and onions and a little cheese, and sometimes, on a lucky day, a little piece of meat for the soup.

--

Life is not easy for the wool workers.

They are paid by the piece, and not very much, and they have no guild of their own to speak for them. In a year of bad harvests, when the price of bread goes up, they often go hungry. Last year, the year of the famine, was very hard, and the family lived for weeks on the bread that the government gave out from the public ovens.

But the wool workers are proud of their skill, and proud of the beautiful cloth that is made from their work, which is sold all over the world. And they have their own pleasures, too: the feasts of the saints, the processions and the games, the gossip in the street, the songs in the taverns, and the warmth of their families.

--

In the evening, Piero comes home, tired and covered in fluff.

Lisa has made a supper of soup, with beans and cabbage and a little bacon, and bread, and a jug of wine. They eat together around the table, by the light of the fire, and the children tell their father about their day.

{img:family-supper-firelight-small-room-evening}
After supper, they sit for a while by the open window, in the cool of the evening, listening to the sounds of the street: the neighbours talking, a baby crying, a man singing in the tavern on the corner, the bells of Santo Spirito ringing for the evening prayer.

Then they say their prayers, and blow out the candle, and lie down together in the big bed, under their woollen blankets, and fall asleep, while the city settles into the night around them.
