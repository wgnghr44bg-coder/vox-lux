In another house in Florence, an old man is writing a history of his city.

His name is Giovanni Villani. He is about seventy years old, a grey-haired, thoughtful man, who has been a merchant and a banker, and has held many offices in the government of Florence. And for many years now, he has been writing a great book, a chronicle, which tells the whole history of Florence, year by year.

--

Villani began his chronicle in the year 1300, which was a special year, a Jubilee year, when he went on a pilgrimage to Rome.

There, he saw the great ruins of the ancient city, the Colosseum and the Forum and the aqueducts, and he thought about how the Romans had written down their history, so that people would remember them forever. And he thought that Florence, which he believed was the daughter of Rome, deserved a history too.

So he went home, and began to write.

--

His chronicle tells everything.

It tells the legends of how Florence was founded, long ago, in the time of the Romans. It tells about the wars and quarrels of the city, between the great families, and between the parties, and with the neighbouring cities of Pisa and Siena and Lucca. It tells about floods and fires, famines and comets, the deaths of popes and kings.

{img:chronicle-manuscript-pages-illuminated-letters}
But it also tells, in great detail, about the ordinary life of the city, as no other book of the time does.

--

Villani loves numbers.

He writes that Florence has about ninety thousand people living inside the walls, and that the city eats about a hundred and forty thousand bushels of grain a year. He writes that there are about two hundred workshops of the wool guild, making seventy or eighty thousand pieces of cloth a year, worth more than a million gold florins.

He writes that there are about eight to ten thousand boys and girls learning to read in the city, and about a thousand or more learning arithmetic in the abacus schools, and five or six hundred learning Latin and logic in the grammar schools. He writes that there are a hundred and ten churches in the city, and thirty hospitals, with more than a thousand beds, for the sick and the poor.

He counts the bakers, the doctors, the lawyers, the notaries. He counts the bridges, the gates, the towers. He wants future generations to know exactly what his city was like.

--

Villani has had a difficult life lately.

He was a partner in one of the banks that failed a few years ago, and he lost much of his fortune. For a while, he was even put in prison, because of the debts of the bank. Now he is an old man, living quietly, and the chronicle is his great comfort.

{img:old-man-garden-bench-reading-afternoon}
Every day, he sits at his desk, in a quiet room of his house, with his books and his papers around him, and writes, in his clear, careful hand, about the events of the past year: the weather, the harvest, the prices in the market, the news from other cities, the doings of the government.

--

In 1347, he writes about the famine of the year before, and about how the government of Florence bought grain from far away, and baked bread in public ovens, and gave it out to the hungry, so that many lives were saved.

He writes about the strange events in the world. He writes that, according to reports from merchants coming from the East, there has been a great sickness in the lands of the Tartars and the Turks, and in Syria and the countries around the Black Sea, and that many people there have died.

He writes it down calmly, as he writes everything, as a piece of news from far away.

--

In the evening, Villani puts down his pen, and closes his book, and goes to sit in the garden behind his house, in the cool air, among the fig trees and the roses.

{img:quiet-garden-twilight-fig-tree-roses}
He listens to the bells of the city ringing for the evening prayer, and the swallows calling as they swoop over the rooftops, and the voices of his neighbours in their gardens. He thinks about the history of Florence, and all the things that have happened in it, in his long life.

He has seen floods, and famines, and wars, and fires. He thinks, perhaps, that he has seen everything.
