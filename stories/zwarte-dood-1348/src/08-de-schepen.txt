Far away from Florence, on the shores of the Black Sea, there is a city called Caffa.

It is a busy trading port, on the coast of the Crimea, belonging to the merchants of Genoa, the great seafaring city on the northwest coast of Italy. Here, the Genoese buy silk and spices, furs and slaves and grain, brought from the lands of the East, along the great caravan roads across Asia, which people will one day call the Silk Road. And here, they load them onto their ships, and sail home to Italy.

--

The ships of Genoa and Venice are the finest in the world.

There are great galleys, long and narrow, with three masts and triangular sails, and banks of oars along each side, rowed by hundreds of men. There are round-bellied merchant ships, called cogs, with a single great square sail, which carry heavy cargoes of grain and wine and wool.

They sail all over the Mediterranean, from Spain to Syria, from Egypt to the Black Sea, carrying goods and passengers and news. A ship from the Black Sea to Italy takes about a month or two, stopping at many ports along the way: Constantinople, the islands of Greece, Sicily.

--

In 1346, a sickness came to Caffa.

It had come from the East, along the caravan roads, from the lands of the Golden Horde, the great empire of the Mongols, which stretched across the steppes of Asia. Nobody knew exactly where it had begun. Somewhere far away, in central Asia, people said.

{img:caravan-steppe-camels-dusk-distant}
The sickness came quickly, and it was very serious. Many people fell ill with a high fever, and with painful swellings in the neck and the arms and the legs, and a great many of them did not recover. It spread from person to person, and from town to town, along the roads and the rivers.

--

Today, we know what it was.

It was a disease caused by tiny living things, bacteria, far too small to be seen without a microscope. They lived in the fleas that lived on rats and other small animals, and when the fleas bit people, the people fell ill. Sometimes, it could also pass directly from person to person, through the air.

But in the fourteenth century, nobody knew anything about bacteria, or about fleas. Nobody knew why people fell ill, or how the sickness spread. Some people thought it came from bad air, from poisonous mists rising out of the earth. Some thought it was caused by the stars and the planets, which had come together in an unlucky way. Many believed that it was a punishment sent by God.

--

In the autumn of 1347, some Genoese ships sailed from the Black Sea, through Constantinople, toward Italy.

Without knowing it, they carried the sickness with them, in the rats and the fleas hiding among the sacks of grain and the bales of cloth in their holds, and among the sailors themselves.

{img:ships-arriving-sicily-harbour-autumn}
In October 1347, some of the ships arrived at the port of Messina, on the island of Sicily. And within a few days, the people of Messina began to fall ill.

--

From Messina, the sickness spread slowly through Sicily, and across the narrow strait to the mainland of Italy.

Other ships carried it to other ports. By the end of 1347, it had reached Genoa, and Venice, and Pisa, and Marseille in France. Then, from the ports, it began to spread inland, along the roads and the rivers, from town to town and village to village, carried by travellers and merchants and pilgrims, by carts and mules, and by the rats and the fleas that went everywhere with them.

The cities began to take what precautions they could. Some of them closed their gates to travellers from places where the sickness was. Venice chose three wise men to look after the health of the city, and ordered that ships from the East should wait outside the harbour before anyone could come ashore. But nobody really knew what to do.

--

In Florence, in the winter of 1347, the news came, from Pisa and from Genoa.

Merchants arriving from the ports told of the sickness there. Letters came from friends and business partners in other cities. People talked about it in the markets and the taverns, and in the churches, and grew anxious.

{img:florence-winter-street-worried-merchants}
The government of Florence did what it could. It ordered the streets to be cleaned, and the rubbish to be taken away. It forbade people from bringing in old clothes and cloth from places where the sickness was. It ordered prayers and processions in the churches, to ask God to spare the city.

And the winter went by, cold and grey, and the people of Florence waited, and hoped.
