In June 1348, a ship came into the little harbour of Melcombe, on the south coast of England, in the county of Dorset.

It was summer. The fields around the harbour were green, and the sheep were grazing on the downs. The fishing boats were pulled up on the beach, and the fishermen were mending their nets in the sun. Nobody in Melcombe paid much attention to the ship. Ships came in every week.

--

But the ship had come from France, where the sickness was. And within a few weeks, the people of Melcombe began to fall ill.

From there, the plague spread slowly along the coast and inland, through the villages of the West Country, along the roads and the rivers, from village to village and town to town. By the autumn, it had reached Bristol, and Oxford, and London.

England in 1348 was a land of villages. Most people lived in small communities of thatched cottages, gathered around a church and a manor house, surrounded by great open fields, divided into long strips, where the villagers grew their wheat and barley and oats and beans.

--

In the villages, the sickness came quietly, and stayed for a few weeks or months, and then moved on.

In some villages, almost everyone was lost. In others, very few. The priests did their best, and many of them, too, fell ill. In some places, there were not enough priests left, and the bishops allowed ordinary people to hear each other's confessions.

{img:village-priest-walking-lane-evening-lantern}
In the manor records, which recorded the business of each village, the clerks wrote down, in Latin, the names of those who had died, and who had inherited their land, and their cows and their sheep. Many of these records survive today, and from them, we can see village by village what happened.

--

In London, a great new cemetery was opened, outside the city walls, in a field called East Smithfield.

The King, Edward the Third, lost his young daughter Joan, who was about fourteen years old. She was travelling to Spain to marry a prince, and on the way, in the city of Bordeaux, she fell ill and died. The King wrote a sad letter to the King of Spain, saying that no fellow human being could be surprised that he was inwardly desolated by the sting of this grief.

At the King's palace, and in the great houses and the monasteries, and in the little cottages, everyone grieved.

--

In Paris, the King of France asked the doctors of the great university of Paris to explain the sickness. They met, and wrote a long, learned report.

They said that it had been caused by an unlucky meeting of three planets, Saturn, Jupiter and Mars, in the year 1345, which had poisoned the air. They advised people to avoid bad air, to burn fragrant woods, to eat light food, and to avoid hot baths and too much exercise. It was the best that anyone knew in those days.

{img:paris-scholars-gowns-meeting-hall}
In Germany and the Low Countries, groups of people called flagellants walked from town to town, in long processions, singing hymns, and praying for forgiveness. In many places, frightened people looked for someone to blame.

But in many other places, people simply helped each other, as people always have.

--

In 1349, a ship from England sailed into the harbour of Bergen, in Norway, carrying wool and a few sailors.

The sickness spread from there across Norway, through the fjords and the mountain valleys, to the farms and the fishing villages. Some of the most remote valleys of Norway were almost emptied. Stories were told for centuries afterward, of a little girl found alone in a valley, years later, living wild among the animals, or of a church found deep in the forest, overgrown with trees, with no one left to ring its bell.

{img:norwegian-fjord-wooden-church-forest-mist}
From Norway, the sickness spread to Sweden, and to Denmark, and around the shores of the Baltic, and then eastward, into Poland and Lithuania and Russia, where it reached the great city of Moscow in 1353.

--

By then, it had touched almost every part of Europe.

A few places were spared, or almost spared, perhaps because they were remote, or because of good luck. Some say the city of Milan was one, because its ruler ordered that any house where the sickness appeared should be closed off at once from the rest of the city. Some small regions of Poland and the Pyrenees mountains seem to have escaped too.

But almost everywhere else, from Sicily to Scotland, from Portugal to Russia, people had lost someone.

--

And then, slowly, it was over, at least for a while.

The sickness went away, as mysteriously as it had come. The people who had survived looked around them, at their emptied villages and towns, their silent workshops and their overgrown fields, and wondered what to do next.

{img:spring-fields-village-church-bells-rising-sun}
And then, as people always do, they began to rebuild.
