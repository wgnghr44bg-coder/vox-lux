In the spring and summer of 1348, the great sickness spread through the city of Florence.

Later, people would call it the Black Death, or the Great Mortality. In those days, people simply called it the pestilence, or the plague.

We will not tell this part of the story in great detail tonight. It was very sad, and very hard, and much has been written about it. We will tell it gently, and from a little distance, as Boccaccio himself chose to tell it.

--

The sickness came quickly.

A person might feel well in the morning, and fall ill in the afternoon, with a fever and a headache and a great tiredness. Often there were painful swellings under the arms or in the neck. Some people recovered, after many days of illness. But many did not, and often they passed away within a few days.

It seemed to strike everyone, rich and poor, young and old, good and bad, without any reason that anyone could see. In some houses, everyone fell ill. In others, nobody did. Nobody understood it, and nobody knew how to cure it.

--

The doctors did their best.

They visited the sick, and studied the sickness, and gave the medicines they knew: herbs and syrups and powders, and the letting of blood. Some of them worked tirelessly, day and night, going from house to house, and many of them fell ill themselves. Others were afraid, and stayed away.

{img:doctor-long-robe-visiting-house-door}
The priests and the friars did their best too. They visited the sick, and heard their confessions, and gave them the last rites, and prayed with them. Many of them, too, fell ill and died, because they would not leave the people who needed them.

--

In Florence, there was an old society of men, called the Misericordia, which means mercy.

It had been founded about a hundred years before, by a group of porters in the wool trade, who had decided to help people in need. Its members wore long black robes, with black hoods over their faces, so that nobody would know who they were, and they would not be thanked or praised for their good works.

In the summer of 1348, the brothers of the Misericordia went out into the streets, day after day, carrying the sick to the hospitals on stretchers, and caring for them, and helping to bury those who had died, with prayers and with dignity. Many of them, too, fell ill. But others took their places.

--

The hospitals of Florence were full.

The greatest of them was the hospital of Santa Maria Nuova, founded long ago by a rich man named Folco Portinari, the father of Beatrice, the lady whom Dante loved. It had long rooms full of beds, cared for by nuns and brothers and servants, who nursed the sick, washed them, fed them soup, and prayed with them.

{img:hospital-long-hall-beds-nuns-candles}
In that terrible summer, they worked without rest. And the people of Florence, rich and poor, gave the hospitals so much money, and so many gifts and bequests, that after the plague was over, Santa Maria Nuova became one of the richest hospitals in Europe.

--

Some time in the summer of 1348, the old chronicler Giovanni Villani fell ill.

He had been writing about the sickness in his chronicle, calmly, as he wrote about everything. In his last chapter, he wrote that the pestilence would last until, and then he left a little space, so that he could fill in the date when it was over.

{img:unfinished-chronicle-page-quill-resting}
But he never filled it in. He passed away in the summer of 1348, and the space in his book is still empty today.

His brother, Matteo, took up the pen, and went on writing the chronicle, where Giovanni had left it.

--

In Siena, a little way to the south of Florence, a shoemaker named Agnolo di Tura was also writing a chronicle of his city.

He wrote about the sickness, too. He wrote that in Siena, people said, and believed, that it was the end of the world. And he wrote, simply and sadly, that he had lost his own children.

In Siena, they had been building a new cathedral, even larger than the one in Florence. After the plague, there were not enough people left, and not enough money, to finish it. Its great unfinished walls still stand today, open to the sky, beside the old cathedral, with pigeons nesting in the arches.

--

All through that summer, it seems, Boccaccio stayed in Florence, and saw what was happening around him.

He saw the empty streets, and the closed shops, and the shuttered houses. He saw the people who had fled the city, and the people who had stayed. He saw great kindness, and great courage, and also great fear. He saw how some people helped their neighbours, and how others shut themselves away.

{img:writer-watching-from-window-empty-square}
And he decided that one day, he would write it all down, so that people would know what it was like, and he would write something else too: a book of stories, full of life and laughter and love, as a kind of answer to the sorrow.
