Tonight we are going nearly seven hundred years back, to a walled city of stone towers on the banks of a river in Tuscany, in the north of Italy.

--

It is a spring morning in the year 1347, and the sun is rising over the hills around Florence.

The first light falls on the red-tiled roofs and the tall stone towers of the city, and on the river Arno, which flows slowly through the middle of it, green and brown, between its stone embankments. Mist lies in the valley. On the hills above the city, among the olive groves and the cypress trees, the bells of little churches and monasteries are ringing for the first prayers of the day.

If you enjoy quiet stories like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe. Now, let us begin.

--

Florence is one of the greatest cities in Europe.

Perhaps a hundred thousand people live inside its walls, which is more than in London, and almost as many as in Paris. Only a few cities in the whole of Christendom are larger: Paris, and Venice, and Milan, and perhaps Genoa and Naples.

{img:city-walls-towers-gates-tuscan-hills}
The city is surrounded by a great wall of stone, recently finished, with tall towers and fortified gates, and a moat in front of it. Inside the walls, the streets are narrow and winding, and the houses stand close together, three and four and five storeys high, built of brown and grey stone, with wooden balconies and shutters, and little gardens hidden behind high walls.

--

Over the rooftops rise the towers.

Long ago, when Florence was smaller and more violent, every rich family built a tall stone tower beside its house, so that it could defend itself against its enemies. There were once more than a hundred of them, rising over the city like a forest of stone. Many have been cut down now, by order of the government, to make the city more peaceful. But many still stand.

And above them all, there are the great public buildings of the city: the tall, fortress-like palace of the government, with its slender bell tower; the churches of the friars, with their wide naves and their bell towers; and in the centre, a great new cathedral, still only half built, rising slowly, year by year, behind a forest of wooden scaffolding.

--

{img:ponte-vecchio-shops-river-1347}
The river Arno is crossed by four stone bridges.

The oldest and the busiest of them is the Ponte Vecchio, the Old Bridge, which has just been rebuilt, after a terrible flood fourteen years ago swept the old one away. On top of the new bridge, there are little shops on both sides, built of wood and stone, hanging out over the water. Butchers and fishmongers and tanners sell their goods there, and throw their scraps into the river below.

From the bridge, looking down the river, there is a wonderful view of the city: the houses rising along both banks, the towers and the domes and the bell towers, and the hills beyond, green and blue, dotted with villages and villas.

--

Florence is rich.

It is rich from wool, and from cloth. The merchants of Florence buy raw wool from England and Spain, and bring it here, and thousands of men and women in the city wash it, comb it, spin it, weave it, dye it in beautiful colours, and finish it into the finest cloth in Europe. Then the merchants sell the cloth all over the world, from London to Constantinople.

{img:wool-merchants-counting-house-florins}
And it is rich from banking. The great banking families of Florence lend money to kings and popes and princes all over Europe. Their gold coin, the florin, with a lily on one side and Saint John the Baptist on the other, is trusted everywhere, from England to Egypt.

--

But Florence has had some hard years lately.

A few years ago, two of its greatest banks, the Bardi and the Peruzzi, collapsed, because the King of England, who had borrowed enormous sums from them to pay for his wars in France, could not pay them back. Many people lost their savings. Then there were bad harvests, and food grew scarce and expensive. Last year, in 1346, many people in the city went hungry.

But this spring, the harvest looks good. The fields around the city are green, the vines are budding on the hills, and the people of Florence are hopeful.

--

{img:florence-rooftops-bell-towers-morning-light}
On this spring morning, the city is waking up.

The gates are opening, and the carts and mules are coming in from the countryside, loaded with vegetables and firewood, eggs and cheese and wine. The shopkeepers are taking down their shutters. The bakers are taking the first loaves out of their ovens. In the workshops of the wool workers, the looms are beginning to clatter.

And in a house somewhere in the city, a young writer named Giovanni Boccaccio is waking up too, and looking out of his window at the morning, and thinking about the stories he wants to write.

He does not know yet that, before long, he will write the most famous of them all, about a time in Florence that nobody who lived through it will ever forget.
