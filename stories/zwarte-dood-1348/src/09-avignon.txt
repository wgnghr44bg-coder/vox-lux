Far to the west, in the south of France, on the banks of the great river Rhône, there is the city of Avignon.

In 1347, it is one of the most important cities in Europe, because the Pope lives here. For nearly forty years, the popes have lived in Avignon instead of Rome, and they have built themselves an enormous palace, high on a rock above the river, with great stone towers and walls like a fortress.

--

The Pope in 1347 is Clement the Sixth.

He is a Frenchman, about fifty-six years old, a learned, generous, cultured man, who loves books and music and art, and likes to live in splendour. He has made his palace one of the most magnificent in the world, with great halls painted with frescoes of hunting scenes and gardens and fishponds, and chapels glowing with blue and gold.

The city around the palace is crowded with cardinals and bishops, priests and monks, lawyers and scholars, merchants and bankers, artists and musicians, from all over Europe, who have come to the papal court on business, or to seek their fortune.

--

Among them is a man who will become one of the most famous poets in history.

His name is Francesco Petrarca, whom we call Petrarch. He is forty-three years old in 1347, the son of a Florentine lawyer who was exiled from Florence, and he grew up in Avignon and the country around it. He is a tall, handsome, elegant man, a scholar who loves the ancient Latin writers more than anything, and searches the old libraries of Europe for their lost books.

{img:poet-scholar-study-books-avignon-window}
He is already famous. A few years ago, he was crowned with a wreath of laurel on the Capitoline Hill in Rome, as the greatest poet of his age.

--

Petrarch is famous above all for his poems about a lady named Laura.

He first saw her, he wrote, on Good Friday, the sixth of April, 1327, in the church of Saint Claire in Avignon. She was young and beautiful, with golden hair, and he fell in love with her at once.

But Laura was married to another man, and Petrarch could only love her from a distance. For twenty years, he wrote poems about her, hundreds of them, short, perfect poems called sonnets, about her beauty, and her goodness, and his love and his longing. They are some of the most beautiful love poems ever written, and poets all over Europe will imitate them for centuries.

--

Petrarch does not live in the city. He finds Avignon noisy and crowded and corrupt, and he calls it, unkindly, the Babylon of the West.

Instead, he has a little house in the countryside, in a quiet valley called Vaucluse, about twenty miles away, where a clear green river bursts out of a great cave at the foot of a cliff. There, he lives simply, with a few servants and a dog, among his books.

{img:vaucluse-spring-cliff-river-green-valley}
He reads and writes, and walks in the hills, and fishes in the river, and tends his little garden. And he thinks about Laura.

--

In the winter of 1347, the sickness reaches Marseille, and then, in January 1348, Avignon.

The Pope's doctor, a wise and skilful man named Guy de Chauliac, does what he can. He visits the sick and studies the disease, and writes down what he sees, carefully and honestly. He advises the Pope to stay in his rooms, and to keep great fires burning on either side of him, day and night, to purify the air. The Pope does as he is told, and he stays well.

{img:pope-seated-between-two-great-fires-calm}
Clement the Sixth does many good things during those terrible months. He pays doctors to care for the sick, and for the poor to be buried. He grants forgiveness to all who die of the sickness. And when frightened people in some towns blame the Jews for the disease, he issues letters saying that this is false, and that the Jews are suffering just as much as everyone else, and must be protected.

--

Petrarch is away from Avignon that spring, travelling in Italy, in the city of Parma.

One day in May, a letter arrives for him, from a friend in Avignon. It tells him that Laura has died, on the sixth of April, 1348, in the morning, exactly twenty-one years after the day he first saw her.

--

Petrarch has a precious book: a manuscript of the poems of Virgil, the great Roman poet, which he loves and carries everywhere with him.

On the first page of it, he writes a note in Latin, in his small, careful hand. He writes about the day he first saw Laura, and about the day she died, and about how he heard the news. He writes that he has written it in this book, which he looks at so often, so that he will always remember, and so that he will understand that there is nothing left for him in this life.

{img:old-manuscript-virgil-handwritten-note}
The book still exists today, in a library in Milan. And the note is still there, on the first page, in Petrarch's own handwriting.

--

In the years that follow, Petrarch will go on writing poems to Laura, but now they are poems of sorrow, and of memory, and of hope that he will see her again, in heaven.

He will live for another twenty-six years, and become the most famous scholar in Europe, and be honoured by kings and popes. But he will never forget the year 1348. He will write that future generations will not believe it, and will think it a fable.

And he will write: Oh happy people of the future, who have not known these miseries.
