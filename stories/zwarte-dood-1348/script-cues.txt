{img:01-florence-arno-river-dawn-1347}

In a library in Florence, there is an old handwritten book. It is a history of the city, written year by year, by a grey-haired merchant named Giovanni Villani. On one of its last pages, he began a sentence about a great sickness. He wrote that it would last until, and then he left a small empty space. He meant to fill in the date when it was over. That little space is still empty today, nearly seven hundred years later.

[pause]

Villani lived in Florence in the 1340s, in a city of stone towers, wool workshops and church bells. Then, in the spring of 1348, a sickness came across the sea that changed all of Europe. People would later call it the Black Death. So how did ordinary people live through it, the weavers and the bakers, the writers and the children? And how did they find their way back to ordinary life afterwards? Let's go and see, gently, and from a kind distance.

[pause]

Before we set out, a quiet word. Should you wish to join me on other nights, you're welcome to subscribe. Only if these slow stories suit you, and never out of duty.

[pause]

I'd also love to hear a little about your evening. Which town or village are you in, and is it late or early there? A single line in the comments is plenty.

[pause]

Now let your feet grow warm under the covers. Let your thoughts slow down, like a river in summer. And breathe out softly, as the bells of an old city ring for evening prayer.

[long-pause]

In 1347, Florence had more people than London, and almost as many as Paris.

[pause]

It is a spring morning in the year 1347. The sun is rising over the hills around Florence, in Tuscany, in the north of Italy. The first light falls on red-tiled roofs and tall stone towers. It falls on the river Arno, which flows slowly through the middle of the city, green and brown between its stone banks. Mist lies in the valley. On the hills above, among olive groves and cypress trees, the bells of little churches are ringing for the first prayers of the day. Florence was one of the greatest cities in Europe. Perhaps a hundred thousand people lived inside its walls. That was more than in London, and almost as many as in Paris. Only a few cities in all of Christian Europe were larger. Imagine standing on a hill above it, as the bells ring out one after another. The whole valley seems to hum softly, like a great beehive waking up in the sun.

[long-pause]

{img:02-city-walls-towers-gates-tuscan-hills}

Rich families in Florence once built tall stone towers beside their houses, just to defend themselves.

[pause]

Florence was surrounded by a great wall of stone, only recently finished. It had tall towers and strong gates, with a moat in front. Inside, the streets were narrow and winding. The houses stood close together, three, four or five storeys high. They were built of brown and grey stone, with wooden balconies and shutters. Little gardens hid behind high walls. And over the rooftops rose the towers. Long ago, when Florence was smaller and rougher, every rich family built a tall tower beside its house. In times of trouble, the family could climb inside and defend itself against its rivals. There were once more than a hundred of these towers. They rose over the city like a forest of stone. By 1347, the government had ordered many of them cut down, to make the city more peaceful. But many still stood, looking down on the busy streets. Some of them are still standing today.

[long-pause]

{img:03-ponte-vecchio-shops-river-1347}

On the Old Bridge, butchers and fishmongers sold their goods in little shops hanging over the river.

[pause]

The river Arno was crossed by four stone bridges. The oldest and busiest was the Ponte Vecchio, the Old Bridge. It had just been rebuilt, after a terrible flood fourteen years earlier swept the old one away. On top of the new bridge stood little shops on both sides. They were built of wood and stone and hung out over the water. Butchers, fishmongers and tanners sold their goods there. They threw their scraps straight into the river below. It was not the sweetest-smelling place in Florence. Yet the view from the bridge was wonderful. Looking down the river, you could see houses rising along both banks. You could see towers, domes and bell towers. And beyond them were the hills, green and blue, dotted with villages and villas. That same bridge still stands today, more than six hundred and seventy years later. People still stop on it in the evening to watch the river turn gold.

[long-pause]

{img:04-wool-merchants-counting-house-florins}

Florence grew rich from wool, and its little gold coin was trusted from England to Egypt.

[pause]

Florence was rich, and most of its wealth came from wool. Its merchants bought raw wool from England and Spain and brought it home. Then thousands of men and women in the city worked on it. They washed it, combed it, spun it and wove it. They dyed it in beautiful colours. Slowly, it became the finest cloth in Europe. The merchants sold that cloth all over the world, from London to Constantinople. Florence was also rich from banking. Its great banking families lent money to kings, popes and princes. Their gold coin was called the florin. It had a lily on one side and Saint John the Baptist on the other. People trusted it everywhere, from England to Egypt. A merchant in a faraway port would bite a florin, smile and accept it. Isn't it strange to think of it? A little coin from one Italian city, travelling across the whole known world.

[long-pause]

{img:05-florence-rooftops-bell-towers-morning-light}

After hungry years, the spring of 1347 made the people of Florence hopeful again.

[pause]

Florence had lived through some hard years. A few years before, two of its greatest banks had collapsed. The King of England had borrowed enormous sums from them to pay for his wars in France. Then he could not pay them back. Many people lost their savings. Then came bad harvests, and food grew scarce and expensive. In 1346, many people in the city went hungry. The government baked bread in public ovens and gave it out, and many lives were saved. But the spring of 1347 felt different. The fields around the city were green. The vines were budding on the hills. The harvest looked good. In the city, the gates opened each morning, and carts and mules came in from the countryside. They brought vegetables and firewood, eggs, cheese and wine. Shopkeepers took down their shutters. Bakers pulled the first loaves from their ovens. And people began to feel that the hard times were over.

[long-pause]

{img:06-young-writer-desk-manuscripts-window-florence}

In a house in Florence, a young writer named Boccaccio was dreaming about the stories he wanted to tell.

[pause]

In a house somewhere in the city, a young writer was waking up too. His name was Giovanni Boccaccio. He was about thirty-four, a big, round-faced man with dark eyes and a cheerful, friendly manner. He loved good food, good wine and good company, and he loved to laugh. Above all, he loved stories. His father was a merchant who had worked for one of the great banks. He had wanted his son to become a merchant too. But young Giovanni was not interested in bales of wool or columns of numbers. So his father sent him to study law instead. Giovanni was not interested in law either. He wanted to write. In the spring of 1347, he was not yet famous. People knew him as a clever writer of love poems and romances. Nobody imagined he would become one of the greatest writers in Italian history. And nobody, least of all Boccaccio, knew what he would one day write about.

[long-pause]

{img:07-narrow-florence-street-shops-awnings-medieval}

The shutters are open and the bakers are busy. Let's step down into the narrow streets, where the whole city is going about its work.

[pause]

In Florence, each shop's wooden shutter folded down to become its counter.

[pause]

The streets of Florence were busy from dawn to dusk. They were narrow and paved with large flat stones. In many of them, two carts could not pass each other. The upper storeys of the houses leaned out over the street, held up on wooden beams. So the sky above was only a thin blue strip. Even in summer, the streets stayed cool and shady. On the ground floors were the shops. Each shop was a single room, open to the street through a wide arched doorway. In the morning, the shopkeeper took down his wooden shutter. He folded it out to make a counter and set out his goods on it. Behind him, in the dim room, his family and apprentices were at work. There were shops for everything. There were bakers and butchers, grocers selling spices and dried fruit. There were shoemakers, tailors and goldsmiths. There were barbers, who also pulled teeth. You could buy almost anything in a single short walk.

[long-pause]

{img:08-guild-hall-meeting-merchants-banners}

Every two months, the names of the city's new rulers were drawn out of leather bags.

[pause]

The people of Florence belonged to guilds. A guild was a society of everyone who did the same kind of work. There were guilds of wool merchants, silk makers and bankers. There were guilds of doctors, lawyers, butchers, bakers and stonemasons. Each guild had its own rules and officers. It had its own coat of arms and its own patron saint. It had its own chapel, where members met and prayed together. The guilds were very powerful. Only guild members could be elected to govern the city. And the way they chose the government was unusual. Every two months, names were drawn from leather bags. The men whose names came out moved into the government palace. They lived there together for two months, eating, sleeping and working in the great stone building. Then they went home again, and new names were drawn. Imagine running a city for just eight weeks, and then going back to your shop.

[long-pause]

{img:09-wool-dyers-vats-river-coloured-cloth}

Along the river, newly dyed cloth dried in the sun like long flags of scarlet, blue and violet.

[pause]

The greatest of all the guilds was the guild of the wool merchants. About a third of all the people in Florence earned their living from wool, in one way or another. Most of them were poor. There were washers, who cleaned the raw wool in the river. There were carders and combers, who straightened its fibres. There were spinners, mostly women, working at home with their spindles. There were weavers at great wooden looms. And there were dyers, whose arms were stained blue, red and yellow from the great vats of dye. Along the banks of the Arno stood long rows of wooden frames. The newly dyed cloth was stretched out on them to dry in the sun. There were long bands of scarlet, blue, green and deep violet. They rippled in the wind like great flags. Can you picture it, on a bright morning? A whole riverbank shimmering with colour, and the smell of wet wool in the warm air.

[long-pause]

{img:10-mercato-vecchio-stalls-crowd-morning}

At the Old Market, children chased dogs and chickens between the stalls, while women gossiped at the well.

[pause]

In the middle of the city lay a market square called the Mercato Vecchio, the Old Market. Every morning it was crowded. Farmers' stalls sold vegetables and fruit, fresh from the countryside. There were cabbages and onions, beans and lettuces. There were figs, grapes and apples, according to the season. There were butchers and poulterers, fishmongers and cheesemakers. There were sellers of bread, wine and oil. There were pedlars, jugglers and storytellers. Beggars sat on the church steps. Children ran between the stalls, chasing each other, the dogs and the chickens. Women stood and gossiped at the well. Men stood in tavern doorways, arguing about politics and prices. It was noisy, smelly and full of life. Every bit of news in Florence passed through this square. If something happened at dawn, the whole market knew by midday. In a way, the Old Market was the beating heart of everyday Florence.

[long-pause]

{img:11-orsanmichele-grain-market-madonna-candles}

In one building in Florence, people bought grain downstairs and prayed to a golden Madonna among the sacks.

[pause]

Not far from the market stood a strange and beautiful building called Orsanmichele. It was a tall square building of stone, with wide open arches on the ground floor. Down below, it was the city's grain market. Wheat was stored and sold there. But it was also a church. Among the sacks of grain hung a painting of the Virgin Mary. The people of Florence believed it could work miracles. Just that year, a painter named Bernardo Daddi had finished a new painting of the Madonna to hang there. It glowed with gold. Every evening, people came to light candles in front of it. They knelt, prayed and sang hymns together. Imagine the scene. The smell of grain and dust, the flicker of candles, and soft voices singing among the sacks of wheat. Bread for the body and comfort for the soul, side by side. Farmers came in with their sacks in the morning. Mothers came back in the evening with a candle and a prayer. The same building served them all. That golden painting is still there today, in the very same building.

[long-pause]

{img:12-evening-piazza-strolling-families-lanterns}

When the evening bells rang, people all over Florence stopped for a moment to say a short prayer.

[pause]

In the late afternoon, when the work was done, the people of Florence came out to walk and talk. Rich merchants strolled in long gowns of fine wool, red, blue and green. In winter, the gowns were lined with fur. Their wives walked beside them in their best dresses, with braided hair under fine veils. Craftsmen and their families walked in plainer clothes. Young men stood at the corners in groups, laughing and watching the girls go by. Most people had no clocks. The church bells told them the time. They rang to wake people, to start work, to eat and to stop. At sunset, all the bells of Florence rang together for the evening prayer. All over the city, people stopped for a moment, wherever they were. They said a short prayer. Then they went on with their evening. Lamps were lit before the little shrines on the street corners. And the shopkeepers folded up their counters and went inside for supper.

[long-pause]

{img:13-merchant-house-courtyard-well-loggia}

The shutters are closed for the night now. Let's knock on a heavy wooden door, and see how a wealthy family lived inside.

[pause]

From the street, a merchant's house looked like a little fortress, but inside there was a sunny courtyard.

[pause]

A wealthy merchant's house in Florence was built around a small courtyard. From the street, it looked plain and strong. It had thick stone walls and small barred windows on the ground floor. The front door was heavy wood, studded with iron nails. But inside the door was a little open courtyard, paved with stone. A well stood in the middle. There were arches around the sides. Sometimes there was a lemon tree in a pot, or a vine climbing up the wall. Around the courtyard were storerooms. They were full of bales of wool and cloth, barrels of wine and jars of oil. There was also the merchant's office. Here he kept his accounts in great leather-bound books. He wrote down every sale and every purchase in careful columns. Florentine merchants were famous for their bookkeeping. Some of their old account books still survive today. You can read what they paid for a barrel of wine nearly seven hundred years ago.

[long-pause]

{img:14-sala-painted-walls-fireplace-long-table}

Some families painted their walls with gardens, so they could feel they were outdoors, even in winter.

[pause]

Up a stone staircase, on the first floor, was the main room of the house. It was called the sala. It was a large, high room. A great fireplace stood at one end. There was a long wooden table, with benches and chests along the walls. The ceiling beams were painted with patterns. The walls were often painted too. Some had patterns of flowers and leaves, or the family's coat of arms. Others had whole pictures of gardens and trees, with birds sitting in the branches. That way, the family could feel as if they were sitting outdoors, even in the middle of winter. Isn't that a lovely idea? On a cold, grey evening, with rain on the shutters, you could sit by the fire and look at painted roses. Here the family ate their meals, received guests and spent their evenings. The children played on the floor by the fire. And the grandmother sat in the warmest corner, spinning and telling stories.

[long-pause]

{img:15-canopy-bed-painted-chest-candle-bedroom}

Medieval beds were so big that the whole family could sleep in them, with curtains to keep out the draughts.

[pause]

Above the sala were the bedrooms. The beds were very large, big enough for several people. They had a wooden frame and a mattress stuffed with wool or feathers. There were linen sheets and woollen blankets. In winter, there was a coverlet of fur on top. Curtains hung all around the bed, to keep out the draughts. At night, you could draw them closed and make a cosy little room inside the room. At the foot of the bed stood a great wooden chest, often painted and carved. The family kept their clothes and linen inside. These chests were very precious. When a girl married, her family often gave her a beautifully painted one, full of linen and clothes. Some of those chests survive today in museums. Their painted sides show weddings, gardens and old stories. Can you imagine pulling those curtains closed on a winter night? Warm and dark and quiet, with the city asleep outside.

[long-pause]

{img:16-mother-servants-kitchen-top-floor-hearth}

In Florentine houses, the kitchen was right at the top, to keep smoke and fire far from everything else.

[pause]

At the very top of the house, under the roof, was the kitchen. It was placed there on purpose. That way, the smoke, the smell of cooking and the danger of fire were kept as far as possible from the rest of the house. In the kitchen was a great open hearth. A fire burned there all day. Iron pots hung from chains over it, and there was a spit for roasting meat. The cook baked bread, or sent the dough to the baker's oven down the street. She made soups and stews of beans, cabbage and onions, with a little meat or salted pork. The mother ran the household, with the help of the servants. There might be a cook, a couple of maids and a nurse for the little children. Every morning, she gave out the day's tasks and checked the stores. It was a big job, like running a small business. And the merchant's wife was often very good at it.

[long-pause]

{img:17-family-meal-long-table-bread-wine-jugs}

Florentines loved strong flavours, and rich families showed off costly spices to their guests.

[pause]

The main meal of the day was eaten in the late morning, after the morning's work. The family sat at the long table in the sala. There was bread, of course, and soup. There might be roast veal or kid, chicken, or fish from the river, or eggs. There was cheese, fruit and nuts. There was wine mixed with water, in earthenware jugs. On fast days, which were many, there was no meat at all. There was only fish, vegetables and bread. The Florentines were proud of their food. They liked strong flavours. They used pepper and ginger, saffron and cinnamon, garlic and herbs. They loved a sour juice made from unripe grapes. The rich used costly spices brought by ship from the East. They liked to show them off to their guests. A sprinkle of saffron said: look how well we are doing. Food, then as now, could be a way of saying something about yourself.

[long-pause]

{img:18-abacus-school-boys-counting-beads-teacher}

Merchants' sons went to a special abacus school, to learn the arithmetic they would need in business.

[pause]

The children of merchants learned to read, write and do sums. The boys first went to a school run by a teacher in the city, where they learned to read. Then many went on to a special school called an abacus school. There they learned arithmetic and bookkeeping, so that one day they could join their father's business. They learned how to work out prices, profits and exchange rates between coins from different cities. By fourteen or fifteen, many boys were already apprentices in the shops and offices of the merchants. The girls usually learned at home, from their mothers. They learned to spin, weave and sew, and to run a household. Some learned to read their prayers. Most were married young, at fifteen or sixteen, often to much older men. Florence had more schools than most cities of its time. The chronicler Villani counted thousands of children learning to read. He was very proud of that.

[long-pause]

{img:19-wool-worker-family-small-room-dawn}

Not every Florentine lived behind a heavy door, though. Let's cross the river to a crowded quarter, where a poorer family is waking before dawn.

[pause]

A family of six might live in two small rooms, with one big bed for everyone.

[pause]

Across the river from the centre lay a quarter called Oltrarno, which means beyond the Arno. Here, in two small rooms on the third floor of a narrow old house, lived a family of wool workers. Let's call the father Piero and the mother Lisa. Piero was a wool comber, and Lisa was a spinner. They had four children, two boys and two girls. The eldest was twelve, and the youngest was four. There was no glass in the windows, only wooden shutters. In the early morning, thin grey light came through the cracks. Lisa got up first and lit the fire in the little fireplace. She warmed some leftover soup in an iron pot and cut slices from yesterday's loaf. The children woke slowly in the big bed they all shared. They climbed out onto the cold floor, rubbing their eyes. They ate breakfast quickly, sitting on stools round the little table. A bowl of bean soup, a piece of bread and a cup of watered wine.

[long-pause]

{img:20-wool-combers-workshop-long-room-morning}

Wool combers heated their heavy iron combs on a stove, so they would glide through the fleece.

[pause]

Then Piero went off to work. He walked down the stairs and through the narrow streets, still dark and quiet. He crossed the bridge to the workshop of the wool merchant who employed him. There, in a long room on the ground floor, he worked with a dozen other men, combing wool all day long. It was hard, hot and dusty work. The combs were heavy iron tools with long sharp teeth. They had to be heated over a little stove, so they would glide through the wool. The combers pulled them through the greasy, tangled fleece again and again. They kept going until the fibres were straight, smooth and shining, ready to be spun. The workshop was noisy and full of fluff. It floated in the air and settled on everything, even the men's eyebrows. At midday, they stopped for a short meal of bread, cheese and onions. They sat on the floor of the workshop, or out in the sunny street.

[long-pause]

{img:21-woman-spinning-wool-window-distaff-spindle}

Spinners worked at their windows, chatting with neighbours whose windows were only a few feet away.

[pause]

At home, Lisa spun. Every few days, the merchant's agent came to the house. He brought her a basket of combed wool and took away the yarn she had spun. She sat by the window, where the light was best. Her distaff was tucked under her arm, holding a soft cloud of wool. Her spindle turned and turned beside her knee, drawing out the thread, fine and even. She had spun since she was a little girl, and her hands moved without thinking. While she spun, she watched the children. She talked to the neighbours across the narrow street, whose windows were only a few feet away. She sang old songs under her breath. The gentle whirr of the spindle was one of the most common sounds in Florence. It came from a thousand windows. If you walked through the poorer quarters in the afternoon, you could hear it everywhere, mixed with women's voices and laughter.

[long-pause]

{img:22-children-fetching-water-fountain-narrow-street}

Even the youngest children had jobs, like fetching water from the fountain or bread from the baker.

[pause]

The children had their own work. The eldest boy, who was twelve, already worked as an errand boy in a wool workshop. He ran between the workshops with bundles of wool and cloth. The eldest girl, who was ten, helped her mother with the spinning, the cooking and the little ones. The younger children played in the street with the other children of the quarter. They were sent to fetch water from the fountain or bread from the baker. In the afternoon, the eldest girl went to market with her mother. They bought beans, onions and a little cheese. Sometimes, on a lucky day, they bought a small piece of meat for the soup. Life was not easy. The wool workers were paid by the piece, and not very much. In the famine year before, the family had lived for weeks on bread given out by the city. Yet they were proud of their skill, and proud of the beautiful cloth their work became.

[long-pause]

{img:23-family-supper-firelight-small-room-evening}

In the evening, the wool worker came home covered in fluff, and the children told him about their day.

[pause]

In the evening, Piero came home, tired and covered in fluff. Lisa had made a supper of soup with beans, cabbage and a little bacon. There was bread and a jug of wine. They ate together around the table, by the light of the fire. The children told their father about their day. Who had fallen over at the fountain. What the neighbour's dog had stolen. Which boy had been chased out of the baker's. After supper, they sat for a while by the open window, in the cool evening air. They listened to the sounds of the street. Neighbours were talking. A baby was crying. A man was singing in the tavern on the corner. The bells of the church of Santo Spirito rang for evening prayer. Then the family said their prayers and blew out the candle. They lay down together in the big bed under their woollen blankets. And they fell asleep, while the city settled into the night around them.

[long-pause]

{img:24-cathedral-under-construction-scaffolding-1347}

While the wool workers sleep, the great new cathedral stands silent in the moonlight, wrapped in its wooden scaffolding. In the morning, the masons will be back.

[pause]

The Florentines wanted the largest church in Christendom, and building it took more than a century.

[pause]

In the heart of Florence, people were building a cathedral. It had been started about fifty years earlier, on the spot where an old, small church stood. The Florentines wanted it to be the largest and most beautiful church in all of Christian Europe. Everyone who came to their city should be amazed. In 1347, it was still far from finished. It would not be finished for nearly another hundred years. The walls rose slowly, of brown stone covered with white, green and pink marble. The marble was laid in patterns of squares, stripes and stars. Inside, the great hall was open to the sky in places. The floor was littered with stone blocks and piles of sand. All around, the stonemasons worked in their sheds and yards. Their chisels rang and tapped all day. Great wooden cranes lifted the stones. They were worked by men walking inside huge wooden wheels, like hamsters in a giant wheel, step by patient step.

[long-pause]

{img:25-bell-tower-rising-scaffold-marble-panels}

On the bell tower, little marble pictures showed ordinary people at work, and passers-by pointed out their own trades.

[pause]

Beside the cathedral, a bell tower was rising. It had been designed by the most famous painter in Italy, Giotto. He began it when he was already an old man. He died only three years later, when just the first storey was done. Now another master, Andrea Pisano, was carrying on the work. On the walls of the lowest storey were little carved marble panels. They showed human beings at work through the ages. There was a man ploughing a field and a weaver at his loom. There was a shepherd with his sheep and a sailor in his boat. There were a builder, a doctor, a horse rider and an astronomer looking at the stars. People stopped in the street to look at them. They pointed out their own trades with pride. A weaver would say to his son: look, that's us. It is a lovely thought. On the greatest building in the city, ordinary working people were honoured too.

[long-pause]

{img:26-baptistery-octagon-golden-mosaic-ceiling}

Every baby born in Florence was baptised in the same eight-sided building, under a ceiling of gold.

[pause]

Across the square from the cathedral stood a very old building, shaped like an octagon. It was covered with white and green marble. This was the Baptistery of Saint John, the patron saint of Florence. Every child born in the city was baptised here, in the great stone font in the middle. Rich or poor, every Florentine began life in the same place. Inside, the domed ceiling was covered with mosaics of gold and colour. They told the story of the world from the Creation to the Last Judgment. In the candlelight, the gold glittered and shimmered, as if the ceiling were alive. On its southern side, there was a pair of new bronze doors. Andrea Pisano had made them just a few years before. They showed the life of Saint John in little golden pictures. Florentines loved this building more than almost any other. The poet Dante called it his beautiful Saint John. He had been baptised there too.

[long-pause]

{img:27-feast-procession-banners-candles-street}

On the feast of Saint John, riders galloped through the streets, racing for a length of red cloth.

[pause]

On the twenty-fourth of June, the feast of Saint John, the whole city celebrated. For days beforehand, banners and tapestries hung from the windows. Shops displayed their finest goods. On the eve of the feast, there was a great procession. The clergy, the guilds and the government walked through the streets to the Baptistery. They carried candles, banners and holy relics. On the day itself, there were races and games, music and dancing in the squares. There was a horse race through the city streets, called the palio. The riders galloped wildly over the stones, while the crowds cheered and leaned out of windows. The prize was a length of costly red cloth. It sounds a small prize, but fine cloth was precious, and the honour was enormous. That festival still takes place in Florence today. Every June, there is a procession, a parade in old costumes and fireworks over the river at night.

[long-pause]

{img:28-friar-preaching-square-crowd-church}

Friars in brown and in black and white preached to great crowds in the squares, and cared for the poor.

[pause]

Florence had many other churches too. In the west of the city stood the great church of Santa Maria Novella. It belonged to the Dominican friars, who wore black and white robes. In the east stood Santa Croce. It belonged to the Franciscan friars, who wore plain brown robes tied with a rope. The friars preached in the squares in front of their churches, to great crowds. People sat on the steps and listened. A good preacher could hold a crowd for hours, making them laugh and cry. The friars also visited the sick and the poor, and gave food to the hungry. Inside their churches, the walls were covered with paintings by Giotto and his pupils. They told the stories of Saint Francis and the saints, in bright colours. For people who could not read, these paintings were like a great picture book. You could stand before a wall and follow a whole story with your eyes.

[long-pause]

{img:29-young-man-reading-garden-bay-of-naples}

The friars' sermons are over, and the bells ring the hours. In two quiet houses, two men are writing by the window.

[pause]

As a young man, Boccaccio lived for years in sunny Naples, among poets, parties and picnics by the sea.

[pause]

When Boccaccio was about fourteen, his father took him to Naples, in the south of Italy. His father's bank had an important office there. Young Giovanni stayed for many years. He lived a happy life in the sunny city by the sea, under the great volcano Vesuvius. Naples had a king and a royal court. Giovanni spent his time among young nobles, scholars and poets. He went to parties, dances and picnics by the sea. He read the old Latin poets. Above all, he read Dante, the great poet of Florence. Dante had died when Giovanni was a boy. His long poem, the Divine Comedy, was the book Giovanni loved more than any other. In Naples, he also fell in love, with a lady of the court. He wrote poems and stories about her, using a secret name: Fiammetta, which means little flame. Nobody knows for certain who she really was. He kept that secret all his life.

[long-pause]

{img:30-writer-walking-arno-bridge-sunset}

Called home to Florence, Boccaccio grumbled that he lived in a gloomy house with a gloomy old father.

[pause]

In 1341, when Boccaccio was about twenty-eight, his father's business ran into trouble. Giovanni had to come home to Florence. He was sad to leave Naples. After the sunshine, the sea and the court, Florence seemed grey and serious. He missed his friends and his old life. He wrote, rather grumpily, that he lived in a gloomy house with a gloomy old father. But he kept writing. He wrote long poems and romances in Italian. They were about love and adventure, knights and nymphs, shepherds and the lovers of ancient times. Slowly, he made friends with the scholars of Florence. And slowly, he grew to love the city again. He knew every street and square, every church and tavern. In the evenings, he liked to walk along the river and across the Ponte Vecchio. He watched the sun go down behind the hills, turning the water of the Arno to gold. Even a grumpy young man could not resist that.

[long-pause]

{img:31-tavern-storyteller-listeners-candle-laughing}

Boccaccio listened to everyone, in taverns and markets, and remembered every good story he heard.

[pause]

Boccaccio was a wonderful storyteller. He had an eye for the funny and the silly, for people's little foolishnesses. But he also noticed their kindness and their courage. He listened to everyone. He listened to merchants and priests, farmers and nobles, servants and ladies. He sat in taverns and markets and in the houses of his friends. He remembered the stories he heard there. Then he wrote them down and made them better. Some were old stories, told for hundreds of years. Some were bits of gossip, or jokes, or tales from travellers. A merchant might tell him about a clever trick in a foreign port. A cook might tell him about a lucky escape. Boccaccio would laugh, nod and file it away in his memory. Do you know someone like that? Someone who notices everything and can turn a small moment into a great story? Boccaccio was exactly that kind of person.

[long-pause]

{img:32-red-brick-town-certaldo-quiet-street-evening}

Boccaccio's family had a house in Certaldo, a little hilltop town built almost entirely of red brick.

[pause]

Boccaccio's family came from a little town called Certaldo, about twenty miles southwest of Florence. It stands on top of a hill, and it is built almost entirely of red brick. Its narrow street climbs gently between brick houses and towers. From the top, you can see for miles over the green Tuscan hills. Boccaccio spent many summers there as a young man. He loved the quiet, the views and the simple country life. He liked to walk in the hills around it, among the vineyards and olive trees. When the city felt too noisy or too sad, Certaldo was where his heart went. Many years later, he would come back to live there for good. Some people even called him by the town's name. Today, Certaldo still looks much as it did. Its red brick street still climbs the hill. In the evening light, the whole town glows warm and rosy, like a lantern on a hilltop.

[long-pause]

{img:33-old-chronicler-writing-book-study-lamp}

Giovanni Villani began writing the history of Florence after a pilgrimage to Rome in the year 1300.

[pause]

In another house in Florence, an old man was writing a history of his city. His name was Giovanni Villani. He was about seventy, grey-haired and thoughtful. He had been a merchant and a banker, and he had held many offices in the city government. For many years now, he had been writing a great book, a chronicle. It told the whole history of Florence, year by year. He had started it in the year 1300. That year, he went on a pilgrimage to Rome. There he saw the great ruins of the ancient city, the Colosseum, the Forum and the aqueducts. He thought about how the Romans had written down their history, so people would remember them forever. And he thought that Florence deserved a history too. So he went home and began to write. He told the legends of the city's founding. He told about wars, quarrels, floods, fires, famines and comets.

[long-pause]

{img:34-chronicle-manuscript-pages-illuminated-letters}

Villani loved numbers, and counted everything from bakers to bushels of grain.

[pause]

Villani's chronicle tells us about ordinary life in Florence as no other book of the time does. And Villani loved numbers. He wrote that about ninety thousand people lived inside the walls. He wrote that the city ate about a hundred and forty thousand bushels of grain a year. He counted about two hundred wool workshops, making seventy or eighty thousand pieces of cloth a year. He counted eight to ten thousand children learning to read. He counted a hundred and ten churches, and thirty hospitals with more than a thousand beds. He counted the bakers, the doctors and the lawyers. He counted the bridges, the gates and the towers. Why did he count so much? He wanted people in the future to know exactly what his city was like. And thanks to him, we do. Isn't that a gift? Seven hundred years later, we can still count the bakers of Florence, because one careful old man wrote them down.

[long-pause]

{img:35-quiet-garden-twilight-fig-tree-roses}

In the evenings, the old chronicler sat in his garden among the fig trees and listened to the bells.

[pause]

Villani had had a hard time lately. He had been a partner in one of the banks that failed. He lost much of his fortune. For a while, he was even put in prison because of the bank's debts. Now he was an old man, living quietly, and his chronicle was his great comfort. Every day, he sat at his desk with his books and papers around him. He wrote in his clear, careful hand about the past year. He wrote about the weather, the harvest and the prices in the market. He wrote about news from other cities. In the evening, he put down his pen and closed his book. He went to sit in the garden behind his house, in the cool air, among the fig trees and the roses. He listened to the bells ringing for evening prayer and the swallows calling over the rooftops. He thought about all he had seen in his long life. He had seen floods, famines, wars and fires. He thought, perhaps, that he had seen everything.

[long-pause]

{img:36-tuscan-hills-olive-groves-cypress-farmhouse}

Beyond the old man's garden wall, beyond the city gates, the hills of Tuscany roll softly away. Let's walk out among them.

[pause]

Many Tuscan farmers gave half of everything they grew to the family in the city who owned their farm.

[pause]

Outside the walls, the countryside rolled away in soft green and golden hills. The Florentines called it the contado, the country that belonged to the city. Hills were covered with olive groves, their leaves silver-green in the wind. Vineyards climbed the slopes in neat rows. Fields of wheat and barley lay in the valleys. Dark cypress trees stood along the roads like tall, quiet guards. Little stone villages perched on the hilltops. Most people in Tuscany lived in the countryside and worked the land. Many were sharecroppers. They lived on a farm owned by a rich family in the city. At the end of each year, they gave half of everything they had grown to the owner. Half the wheat, half the wine, half the olive oil. Even half the eggs, the cheese and the chickens. The other half they kept for themselves. The farmhouse had stables and storerooms below. Upstairs was a big kitchen with an open hearth, where the family lived and ate.

[long-pause]

{img:37-wheat-harvest-sickles-tuscan-summer-field}

The grape harvest was the happiest time of the farming year, when young and old went out to the vineyards.

[pause]

The farming year followed the seasons. In autumn, farmers ploughed their fields with oxen and sowed the wheat. In winter, they pruned the vines and the olive trees. They mended their tools and cut wood for the fire. In spring, they hoed the fields and planted vegetables, and the sheep had their lambs. In early summer, they cut the hay. In midsummer, under the hot sun, they harvested the wheat with sickles. They threshed it on stone floors in front of their houses. In late September came the grape harvest, the vendemmia. It was the happiest time of the year. Everyone, young and old, went out into the vineyards to pick the grapes. The grapes were trodden in great wooden tubs to make the wine. Children's feet were stained purple for days. And in November, the olives were picked and pressed. The new green oil flowed out. Everyone ate bread soaked in it, warm from the fire.

[long-pause]

{img:38-villa-garden-fountain-view-florence-hills}

In summer, rich Florentines escaped the hot, smelly city to their villas in the hills.

[pause]

The rich families of Florence loved the countryside too. Many owned villas in the hills around the city. These were comfortable country houses with gardens and orchards, fountains and fishponds. They had views over the valley of the Arno. In summer, the city grew hot, dusty and smelly. So the rich left for their villas, for weeks or months at a time. There they walked in their gardens and rode in the hills. They went hunting with hawks and dogs. In the afternoons, they sat in the shade of the trees, reading, talking and playing music. In the evenings, they ate outside, under a pergola covered with vines. Far away in the valley below, the lights of Florence twinkled in the dark. Can you picture yourself there? A cool terrace, a glass of wine, crickets singing, and a whole city glowing softly in the distance. Boccaccio knew these hills well. One day, he would set his most famous book in a villa just like this.

[long-pause]

{img:39-fiesole-hill-view-florence-valley-evening}

The little hill town above Florence was much older than the city itself, built long before the Romans.

[pause]

On a hill to the north of Florence stood a little town called Fiesole. It was much older than Florence. It had been built by the ancient Etruscans, long before the Romans came. The ruins of a Roman theatre were still there, and ancient walls. From the hill of Fiesole, there was a wonderful view of the whole of Florence spread out below. You could see the river, the walls and the towers. You could see the sea of red roofs and the half-built cathedral. On the slopes between Fiesole and the city, there were villas, gardens and monasteries. Little lanes wound between stone walls covered in ivy and wild roses. People walked up there on summer evenings to catch the cool breeze. They sat on the old stones and watched the city turn gold, then pink, then blue. That same view is still there today. Visitors still climb the hill to see it, and they still fall quiet when they reach the top. Some bring a picnic and stay until the stars come out. Below them, the lights of the city appear one by one, like embers in a hearth. It is one of the most peaceful views in all of Italy.

[long-pause]

{img:40-summer-1347-wheat-harvest-golden-hills}

After the hunger of 1346, the summer of 1347 brought a rich harvest, and the bakers' shelves were full again.

[pause]

The summer of 1347 was a good summer in Tuscany. After the hunger of the year before, the harvest was rich. The wheat around Florence grew tall and golden. In June and July, the farmers brought in the sheaves. They threshed them on the stone floors, and the grain poured into the sacks. In the city, there was relief everywhere. The price of bread fell. The bakers' shelves were full again. The market overflowed with fruit and vegetables. There were cherries and apricots, then peaches, figs and melons. People who had gone hungry the year before could eat well again. And they were grateful. The friars held services of thanksgiving. The guilds held feasts in their halls. In the heat of the afternoon, the city slowed down. Shopkeepers pulled down their awnings. People rested behind closed shutters. Only the cicadas sang in the gardens. It felt, to many, as if the hard times were finally over.

[long-pause]

{img:41-arno-riverbank-summer-evening-swimmers-poplars}

On summer evenings, boys swam in the Arno while washerwomen laughed and called to each other on the stones.

[pause]

On summer evenings, when the heat broke, everyone came out again. Young people went down to the river. Below the city walls, the banks of the Arno were shady with poplars and willows. In summer, the water was shallow and slow. Boys swam and splashed and lay on the sandy banks in the evening sun. Fishermen stood in the shallows with their nets. Washerwomen knelt on flat stones at the water's edge. They beat the clothes clean, laughing and calling to one another. Further along, great wooden waterwheels turned slowly in the current, grinding the new wheat into flour. In the squares, there was music and dancing. Young men and women danced in rings, holding hands, to pipes, drums and lutes. Storytellers sat on church steps and told old tales of knights and ladies. Old men sat on stone benches, talking about the old days. And at night, people slept with their windows open, under the summer stars.

[long-pause]

{img:42-genoese-galleys-harbour-sails-1347}

That summer, the people of Florence slept peacefully, with their windows open to the stars. Far away across the sea, though, ships were already setting sail.

[pause]

Italian merchant ships sailed all the way to the Black Sea to buy silk and spices from the East.

[pause]

Far from Florence, on the shores of the Black Sea, stood a trading city called Caffa. It belonged to the merchants of Genoa, the great seafaring city on the coast of Italy. In Caffa, the Genoese bought silk and spices, furs and grain. These goods came from the East along the great caravan roads across Asia. People would one day call those roads the Silk Road. The Genoese loaded the goods onto their ships and sailed home. The ships of Genoa and Venice were the finest of their time. There were great galleys, long and narrow, with triangular sails and many oars along each side. There were round-bellied merchant ships with a single great square sail. They sailed all over the Mediterranean, from Spain to Syria, from Egypt to the Black Sea. A voyage home took a month or two. The ships stopped at many ports along the way. And at every port, they carried goods, passengers and news.

[long-pause]

{img:43-caravan-steppe-camels-dusk-distant}

Nobody at the time knew what caused the sickness, and many thought it came from bad air or the stars.

[pause]

In 1346, a sickness came to Caffa. It had travelled from the East, along the caravan roads, from the lands of the Mongol empire. Nobody knew exactly where it had begun. Somewhere far away, people said. Today, we know what it was. It was caused by tiny living things, bacteria, far too small to see without a microscope. They lived in the fleas on rats and other small animals. When those fleas bit people, the people fell ill. Sometimes, the sickness could also pass from person to person. But in the fourteenth century, nobody knew anything about bacteria or fleas. Nobody knew why people fell ill, or how illness spread. Some thought it came from bad air rising out of the earth. Some thought the stars and planets had lined up in an unlucky way. Many believed it was sent by God. Imagine facing something like that, with no way of understanding it. People did the best they could, with what they knew.

[long-pause]

{img:44-ships-arriving-sicily-harbour-autumn}

In October 1347, merchant ships from the East sailed into the harbour of Messina, in Sicily.

[pause]

In the autumn of 1347, some Genoese ships sailed from the Black Sea toward Italy. Without knowing it, they carried the sickness with them. It hid in the rats and fleas among the sacks of grain and the bales of cloth. In October 1347, some of the ships arrived at the port of Messina, on the island of Sicily. Within a few days, people in Messina began to fall ill. From there, the sickness spread through Sicily, and across the narrow strait to the mainland of Italy. Other ships carried it to other ports. By the end of 1347, it had reached Genoa, Venice and Pisa, and Marseille in France. Then it began to move inland. It travelled along the roads and rivers, from town to town. It went with merchants, pilgrims and travellers, with carts and mules. And it went with the rats and fleas that went everywhere with them.

[long-pause]

{img:45-venice-harbour-officials-ships-waiting-lagoon}

Venice made ships from the East wait outside the harbour, one of the first public health rules in history.

[pause]

The cities did what they could. Some closed their gates to travellers from places where the sickness was. Venice chose three wise men to look after the health of the city. It ordered ships from the East to wait outside the harbour before anyone could come ashore. This was one of the first public health rules in history. Later, another city on the Adriatic coast, Ragusa, would make a similar rule. Ships would wait for thirty days before landing, and later forty. In Italian, forty days is quaranta giorni. That is where our word quarantine comes from. So a word we all know today was born in those hard years, nearly seven hundred years ago. But in 1347, nobody really knew what to do. Rules were made, broken and remade. Some helped and some did not. People argued and worried. And meanwhile, the long grey winter went slowly by.

[long-pause]

{img:46-florence-winter-street-worried-merchants}

In Florence, the news came in letters from friends in Pisa and Genoa, and the city waited and hoped.

[pause]

In Florence, in the winter of 1347, the news came from Pisa and Genoa. Merchants arriving from the ports told what they had seen. Letters came from friends and business partners in other cities. People talked about it in the markets, the taverns and the churches. They grew anxious. The government did what it could. It ordered the streets to be cleaned and the rubbish carried away. It forbade people to bring in old clothes and cloth from places where the sickness was. It ordered prayers and processions in the churches. In his house, the old chronicler Villani wrote it all down, calmly, as he wrote everything. He noted that merchants from the East reported a great sickness there. He wrote it as a piece of news from far away. And the winter went by, cold and grey. The people of Florence waited, and they hoped.

[long-pause]

{img:47-avignon-papal-palace-rhone-bridge}

Let's leave Florence for a moment, and travel west, to a city in the south of France, where a great palace stands high above a river.

[pause]

For nearly forty years, the popes lived not in Rome but in Avignon, in an enormous fortress palace.

[pause]

Far to the west, in the south of France, on the great river Rhône, lay the city of Avignon. In 1347, it was one of the most important cities in Europe, because the Pope lived there. For nearly forty years, the popes had lived in Avignon instead of Rome. They had built an enormous palace high on a rock above the river. It had great stone towers and walls like a fortress. The Pope at that time was Clement the Sixth. He was a Frenchman, about fifty-six years old. He was learned and generous, and he loved books, music and art. He liked to live in splendour. His palace had great halls painted with hunting scenes, gardens and fishponds. Its chapels glowed with blue and gold. The city around it was crowded with cardinals and bishops, lawyers and scholars, merchants and artists. They came from all over Europe on business, or to seek their fortune. The palace still stands there today, as enormous as ever.

[long-pause]

{img:48-poet-scholar-study-books-avignon-window}

The poet Petrarch fell in love with a lady named Laura at first sight, and wrote hundreds of poems about her.

[pause]

Among the crowds of Avignon was a man who would become one of the most famous poets in history. His name was Francesco Petrarca. We call him Petrarch. He was forty-three years old in 1347. He was the son of a Florentine lawyer and had grown up near Avignon. He was tall, handsome and elegant. He loved the ancient Latin writers more than anything. He searched old libraries all over Europe for their lost books. He was already famous. A few years earlier, he had been crowned with a wreath of laurel in Rome, as the greatest poet of his age. Petrarch was famous above all for his poems about a lady named Laura. He first saw her, he wrote, on Good Friday in 1327, in a church in Avignon. He fell in love at once. But she was married to someone else. So for twenty years, he loved her only from a distance. He wrote hundreds of short poems about her, called sonnets. Poets all over Europe imitated them for centuries.

[long-pause]

{img:49-vaucluse-spring-cliff-river-green-valley}

Petrarch called Avignon noisy and crowded, and lived instead in a quiet valley where a river flows out of a cave.

[pause]

Petrarch did not live in the city. He found Avignon noisy, crowded and full of greedy people. Instead, he had a little house in the countryside, in a quiet valley called Vaucluse. It lay about twenty miles away. There, a clear green river bursts out of a great cave at the foot of a cliff. It is one of the most beautiful springs in France. Petrarch lived there simply, with a few servants and a dog, among his books. He read and wrote. He walked in the hills and fished in the river. He tended his little garden. And he thought about Laura. He wrote that the valley was the only place where he could truly be at peace. You can still visit Vaucluse today. The river still pours out of its cave, cold and green, just as it did when Petrarch sat on its banks. On quiet mornings, visitors say, you can almost imagine him there, with his book and his dog.

[long-pause]

{img:50-pope-seated-between-two-great-fires-calm}

The Pope's doctor told him to sit between two great fires, day and night, to purify the air.

[pause]

In January 1348, the sickness reached Avignon. The Pope's doctor was a wise and skilful man named Guy de Chauliac. He did what he could. He visited the sick and studied the illness. He wrote down what he saw, carefully and honestly. He advised the Pope to stay in his rooms and keep two great fires burning, day and night, to purify the air. The Pope did as he was told, and he stayed well. Perhaps the heat kept the fleas away. Clement the Sixth did many good things in those months. He paid doctors to care for the sick. He paid for the poor to be buried with dignity. And when frightened people in some towns blamed the Jews for the sickness, he wrote letters saying this was false. The Jews were suffering just as much as everyone else, he wrote, and they must be protected. Guy de Chauliac caught the sickness himself, but he recovered. He later wrote that he had stayed with his patients.

[long-pause]

{img:51-old-manuscript-virgil-handwritten-note}

Petrarch wrote a note about Laura in his favourite book, and that book still exists in Milan.

[pause]

That spring, Petrarch was away from Avignon, travelling in Italy. In May, a letter reached him from a friend. It told him that Laura had died, on the sixth of April 1348. It was exactly twenty-one years after the day he first saw her. Petrarch had a precious book that he carried everywhere. It was a handwritten copy of the poems of Virgil, the great Roman poet. On its first page, he wrote a note in Latin, in his small, careful hand. He wrote about the day he first saw Laura, and the day she died. He wrote that he put it in this book, which he looked at so often, so that he would always remember. That book still exists today, in a library in Milan. The note is still there, in Petrarch's own handwriting. In the years that followed, he went on writing poems to Laura. Now they were poems of memory, and of hope that he would see her again one day.

[long-pause]

{img:52-florence-spring-1348-almond-blossom-hills}

Let's come back to Florence now. The winter is over, and the almond trees are blossoming on the hills once more.

[pause]

The spring of 1348 came to Florence as it always did, with almond blossom and swallows.

[pause]

The spring of 1348 came to Florence as it always did. The almond trees blossomed pink and white on the hillsides. The days grew longer and warmer. The swallows came back from Africa and built their nests under the eaves of the houses. In the fields, the wheat was green and growing. In the vineyards, the first leaves were unfolding on the vines. In the city, people went about their business as usual. The wool workers were at their looms and the merchants were in their offices. Shopkeepers stood at their counters, and children went to school. The friars preached in the squares. The government met in its palace. The masons went on building the cathedral, and the bell tower rose a little higher, stone by stone. And yet everyone was anxious. Every day, travellers brought new stories from other cities. Everyone wondered quietly when the sickness would reach their own streets.

[long-pause]

{img:53-penitent-procession-candles-hymns-street}

In the spring of 1348, the churches were full, and long processions moved through the streets, singing.

[pause]

The churches were full that spring. People went to Mass every day, and to confession. They prayed more often and more earnestly than ever before. The friars led processions through the streets. They carried crosses, banners and holy relics. Long lines of people followed them, singing hymns and praying for the city to be spared. At Orsanmichele, people crowded in front of the golden Madonna. They lit candles, left gifts of money and asked her to protect their families. So much money was given there in those months that the society which cared for the painting became one of the richest in Florence. Later, with that money, they built a beautiful marble shrine around the painting. It is still there today. Can you picture the evenings that spring? Thousands of small candle flames in the dusk, and quiet voices singing together, all over the city. Fear, yes. But also a great deal of hope, held together.

[long-pause]

{img:54-apothecary-shop-herbs-jars-rosemary-bundles}

The doctors of Florence advised people to burn rosemary and juniper, and above all, not to be afraid.

[pause]

The government of Florence did what it could. It ordered the streets cleaned and the drains cleared. It forbade the sale of rotten meat and fish. It stopped travellers from Pisa and Genoa from entering the city. The doctors of the city met to discuss the sickness. They wrote advice for the people. Eat simple food, they said. Avoid bad air. Burn sweet-smelling herbs and woods in your houses, like juniper, rosemary and bay. Carry a little bunch of flowers or herbs to hold to your nose when you go out. And keep calm and cheerful, they said, because fear itself makes people ill. Much of their advice could not stop the sickness. They simply did not know what caused it. But some of it, like cleaning the streets, may have helped a little. And the advice to stay calm was kind. The apothecary shops did a busy trade that spring. The whole city smelled faintly of rosemary.

[long-pause]

{img:55-lanterns-shrine-street-corner-dusk}

Some time in March or April 1348, the sickness reached Florence, quietly, and at first almost nobody noticed.

[pause]

Some time in March or April 1348, the sickness reached Florence. Nobody knows exactly how it came. Perhaps it came with a traveller from Pisa. Perhaps with a merchant and a bale of cloth, or a carter with a load of grain. However it came, it came quietly. At first, almost nobody noticed. A few people fell ill in one quarter, and then a few more in another. The doctors were called and shook their heads. The neighbours whispered. Then, quite suddenly, everyone knew. In the spring evenings, people still came out into the streets and squares as the bells rang for evening prayer. But they walked more quietly now, and did not stay out long. They spoke in low voices. In front of the little shrines on the street corners, the lamps burned. There were more flowers than usual, and more candles. People stopped, knelt and prayed. Then they hurried home and closed their doors. And the river flowed on beneath the bridges, as it always had.

[long-pause]

{img:56-florence-quiet-street-summer-1348-empty}

We will tell this part of the story gently, from a little distance, just as Boccaccio chose to tell it.

[pause]

In the spring and summer of 1348, the great sickness spread through Florence. Later, people would call it the Black Death, or the Great Mortality. At the time, they simply called it the pestilence. We will not tell this part of the story in great detail. It was a very sad time, and much has been written about it. We will tell it gently, and from a little distance, as Boccaccio himself did. The sickness came quickly. A person might feel well in the morning and ill by the afternoon, with a fever and a great tiredness. Some people recovered, after many days. But many did not. It seemed to strike rich and poor, young and old, without any reason anyone could see. In some houses, everyone fell ill. In others, nobody did. Nobody understood it, and nobody knew how to cure it. And so the city grew quieter, and the long summer days went slowly by.

[long-pause]

{img:57-brotherhood-black-robes-carrying-stretcher-street}

The brothers of the Misericordia hid their faces under black hoods, so nobody could thank them.

[pause]

In Florence, there was an old society called the Misericordia, which means mercy. It had been founded about a hundred years earlier by a group of porters in the wool trade. They had decided to help anyone in need. Its members wore long black robes, with black hoods over their faces. That way, nobody would know who they were. They did not want to be thanked or praised for their good works. In the summer of 1348, the brothers went out into the streets, day after day. They carried the sick to the hospitals on stretchers and cared for them. They helped bury those who had died, with prayers and with dignity. Many of the brothers fell ill themselves. But others stepped forward and took their places. Think of that. Ordinary porters and craftsmen, choosing to walk toward the danger, hidden under hoods so that nobody would even know their names. The Misericordia is still working in Florence today.

[long-pause]

{img:58-hospital-long-hall-beds-nuns-candles}

The great hospital of Santa Maria Nuova was founded by the father of the lady Dante loved.

[pause]

The hospitals of Florence were full that summer. The greatest of them was the hospital of Santa Maria Nuova. It had been founded long before by a rich man named Folco Portinari. He was the father of Beatrice, the lady whom the poet Dante had loved. The hospital had long halls full of beds. Nuns, lay brothers and servants nursed the sick. They washed them and fed them soup. They sat with them and prayed with them. In that hard summer, they worked without rest. Doctors and priests and friars did their best too. Many of them stayed with the sick, even when they could have left. And the people of Florence, rich and poor, gave the hospitals money and gifts. After the sickness had passed, Santa Maria Nuova was one of the richest hospitals in Europe. And here is a lovely thing. Santa Maria Nuova is still a hospital today, in the same place, caring for the sick, nearly seven hundred years later.

[long-pause]

{img:59-unfinished-chronicle-page-quill-resting}

The old chronicler left a blank space for the date the sickness would end, and it is still empty today.

[pause]

Some time that summer, the old chronicler Giovanni Villani fell ill. He had been writing about the sickness in his chronicle, calmly, as he wrote about everything. In his last chapter, he wrote that the pestilence would last until, and then he left a little blank space. He meant to fill in the date when it was over. But he never filled it in. He died in the summer of 1348, and the space in his book is still empty today. His brother Matteo picked up the pen. He went on writing the chronicle where Giovanni had left off. Later, Matteo's son carried it on too. Together, the three Villanis wrote one of the greatest histories of any medieval city. Isn't that a quiet, beautiful thing? A family passing a pen from hand to hand, so that the story of their city would not stop. That small blank space has become one of the most famous gaps in all of history.

[long-pause]

{img:60-empty-piazza-noon-heat-pigeons}

The pen passes from one brother to the next. Outside, the summer goes on, hot and still. Let's see how ordinary people lived through those strange months.

[pause]

By midsummer, the looms were silent and the cranes on the cathedral stood still.

[pause]

By the middle of the summer of 1348, Florence had become strangely quiet. The markets were nearly empty. Many shops were closed. The looms of the wool workers were silent. The masons had stopped working on the cathedral, and the great wooden cranes stood still above the half-built walls. The streets, once so crowded, were almost deserted, especially in the heat of the afternoon. Pigeons dozed by the fountains. A dog trotted across an empty square. The bells still rang the hours, but fewer people were listening. Many people had left. Those who could afford it had gone to their villas and farms in the countryside. The air was fresher there, and there were fewer people. Others went to stay with relatives in other towns. The government had almost stopped meeting, because so many of its members had gone. Yet the city did not stop completely. Bakers still baked. Water still flowed from the fountains. Somehow, life went on.

[long-pause]

{img:61-courtyard-lute-player-quiet-family-evening}

Some families shut their doors and lived quietly together, with simple food, good wine and music.

[pause]

Those who stayed in the city tried to live as best they could. Boccaccio later described how different people chose different ways. Some shut themselves up in their houses with a few friends and family. They lived very quietly. They ate simple food and drank a little good wine. They played music and talked about pleasant things, trying not to think about what was happening outside. Others did the opposite. They decided that, since life might be short, they would enjoy it while they could. They went from tavern to tavern, eating, drinking and singing. But most people simply went on, day by day. They did what needed to be done. They looked after their families and their neighbours. They went to church, and they hoped. That is often how it is, in hard times. The great stories are about heroes and villains. But most people are simply getting through each day, as kindly as they can.

[long-pause]

{img:62-neighbour-leaving-bread-basket-doorstep}

Many neighbours left bread and soup on each other's doorsteps, and looked after each other's children.

[pause]

In the old stories about the plague, people often remember the fear. But there was also a great deal of kindness. Neighbours left bread and soup on each other's doorsteps. They called up to each other's windows to ask how everyone was. They looked after each other's children when parents fell ill. Priests and friars visited the sick and sat with them. Women nursed strangers. Notaries went from house to house, writing down people's wills, so that their families would be looked after. Wills from that summer still survive in the archives of Florence. Many of them leave money to the poor, to hospitals and to orphans. In one way or another, many people were thinking of others, even in their own hardest hours. We should remember that, too. In the darkest times, there are always people who quietly keep the lamps lit for others.

[long-pause]

{img:63-writer-watching-from-window-empty-square}

Boccaccio stayed in Florence that summer, and decided that one day he would write it all down.

[pause]

All through that summer, it seems, Boccaccio stayed in Florence. He saw the empty streets, the closed shops and the shuttered houses. He saw who had fled the city and who had stayed. He saw great kindness and great courage, and he also saw great fear. He saw how some people helped their neighbours, while others shut themselves away. And he made a decision. One day, he would write it all down, honestly, so people would know what it had been like. But he would also write something else. He would write a book of stories, full of life, laughter and love. It would be a kind of answer to the sorrow. Isn't that a beautiful idea? When the world is very sad, you can describe the sadness. But you can also light a small fire of stories, for people to warm their hands by. Boccaccio chose to do both. And his book would be read for the next seven hundred years.

[long-pause]

{img:64-italian-hill-village-quiet-dusk-church}

By the cool days of autumn, the sickness had begun, slowly, to fade.

[pause]

The sickness did not go on forever. In the heat of July and August, it was at its worst. Then, as autumn came and the weather grew cooler, it began slowly to fade. Fewer people fell ill. More of those who fell ill recovered. By winter, it had almost gone. But when it was over, the city had changed. Historians today think that perhaps half of the people of Florence were lost, or a little more. Some families lost everyone. Others lost no one at all. And Florence was not alone. All over Italy, the same thing happened, in city after city and town after town. It happened in Venice and Genoa, Siena and Pisa, and in little villages high in the mountains. In Siena, people had been building a cathedral even larger than the one in Florence. Afterwards, there were not enough people or money to finish it. Its great unfinished walls still stand today, open to the sky, with pigeons nesting in the arches.

[long-pause]

{img:65-english-village-thatched-cottages-church-summer}

In England, village clerks wrote down in Latin who had inherited each family's land, cows and sheep.

[pause]

The sickness did not stop at Italy. In the summer of 1348, it crossed the Alps into France, Germany and Switzerland. In June, a ship brought it to a little port called Melcombe, on the south coast of England. Nobody in Melcombe paid much attention to the ship. Ships came in every week. From there, the sickness spread slowly along the coast and inland. By autumn, it had reached Bristol, Oxford and London. England in 1348 was a land of villages. Most people lived in small groups of thatched cottages, around a church and a manor house. Great open fields stretched around them, divided into long strips. In each village, clerks kept records of the village's business. They wrote down, in Latin, who had died. They wrote who had inherited the land, and the cows and sheep. Many of these records survive today. From them, historians can follow what happened, one village at a time. They also show something else: the fields were planted again the next spring.

[long-pause]

{img:66-norwegian-fjord-wooden-church-forest-mist}

In 1349, a ship carrying English wool brought the sickness to Bergen, in Norway.

[pause]

In 1349, a ship from England sailed into the harbour of Bergen, in Norway. It carried wool, and a few sailors. The sickness spread from there across Norway. It travelled through the fjords and the mountain valleys, to the farms and the fishing villages. Some of the most remote valleys were almost emptied. For centuries afterwards, Norwegians told old stories about those years. There was a story of a little wooden church found deep in the forest, overgrown with trees, its bell silent. From Norway, the sickness spread to Sweden and Denmark, around the Baltic Sea, and then east into Poland and Russia. By the early 1350s, it had touched almost every corner of Europe. Historians believe that between a third and a half of all the people in Europe were lost in those few years. And yet, in every place, people picked themselves up and began again. They had to. There were fields to plant, and children to raise.

[long-pause]

{img:67-milan-city-walls-towers-quiet-morning}

A few places were almost spared, perhaps by luck, or because they acted very quickly.

[pause]

A few places in Europe were spared, or almost spared. Some were very remote, like small villages high in the mountains, far from any road. Others may simply have been lucky. One of them, people say, was the great city of Milan, in the north of Italy. Its ruler gave a very strict order. If the sickness appeared in a house, that house was to be closed off from the rest of the city at once. Historians still argue about how much this helped. But Milan suffered far less than most cities that year. Some small regions of Poland and the Pyrenees mountains also seem to have escaped. Nobody at the time knew why. It must have felt like a mystery, or a blessing. Today, we understand more. Distance helped, and luck helped, and so did keeping people apart. Those lessons, learned so painfully, would be remembered and used again for hundreds of years.

[long-pause]

{img:68-paris-scholars-gowns-meeting-hall}

The doctors of Paris blamed an unlucky meeting of three planets, because it was the best they knew.

[pause]

In Paris, the King of France asked the doctors of the great university to explain the sickness. They met in their gowns and caps and talked for many days. Then they wrote a long, learned report. They said the sickness had been caused by an unlucky meeting of three planets, Saturn, Jupiter and Mars, a few years before. This, they said, had poisoned the air. They advised people to avoid bad air and burn fragrant woods. They said to eat light food and avoid hot baths and too much exercise. Today it sounds strange. But it was the best that anyone knew at the time. These were serious, clever people, doing their very best with the knowledge they had. It reminds us that understanding takes time. Bit by bit, over hundreds of years, doctors learned more. Much later, in 1894, a scientist finally saw the tiny bacteria under a microscope. The mystery that had puzzled the doctors of Paris was solved at last.

[long-pause]

{img:69-santa-maria-novella-church-morning-young-women}

Now let's return to Boccaccio, who has begun to write. His book opens in a church in Florence, on a Tuesday morning in that long summer.

[pause]

Boccaccio's most famous book begins with seven young women meeting by chance in a church.

[pause]

A few years after the plague, Boccaccio began the book that would make him famous. He called it the Decameron, which means ten days, in Greek. It is a book of a hundred stories. It begins with Florence in the summer of 1348. In the first pages, Boccaccio describes the sickness in the city, honestly and simply. Then he tells how, one Tuesday morning, seven young women met by chance in the great church of Santa Maria Novella. They were friends, all between eighteen and twenty-eight years old. They were sad and lonely, because so many of their family and friends were gone. The eldest, Pampinea, had an idea. Why stay in the city, among so much sadness? Why not go out to one of their families' villas in the countryside? They could live quietly there together for a while, with music, good food and good company. Just then, three young men they knew came into the church. They gladly agreed to come too.

[long-pause]

{img:70-villa-hilltop-gardens-fountains-roses-morning}

Boccaccio described the villa with great love: cool wells, fine rooms and flowers everywhere.

[pause]

The next morning at dawn, the ten young people left Florence with a few servants. They walked out through the gates and up into the hills, to a beautiful villa about two miles away. Boccaccio described it with great love. It stood on a little hill, a short way from the road, surrounded by trees and green plants. It had a courtyard with loggias, galleries and fine rooms, all decorated with cheerful paintings. Around it lay meadows and wonderful gardens. There were wells of cool, fresh water, and cellars full of good wine. Everything had been swept and cleaned. The beds were made. There were flowers everywhere, of every kind the season offered. Fresh rushes were strewn on the floors. The young people were delighted. Can you picture arriving there? After the quiet, sad streets of the city, suddenly you are standing in a garden full of roses, with fountains splashing and birds singing in the trees.

[long-pause]

{img:71-young-people-garden-table-white-linen-fountain}

Each day, one of the young people became king or queen for the day, and decided what everyone would do.

[pause]

The young people decided to live a simple, happy life together. Each day, one of them would be king or queen of the day. That person would decide what they all did. They got up early and walked in the gardens among the roses and jasmine. They sang and made garlands of flowers. At midday, they ate a fine meal at tables set with white linen, beside a fountain. There was wine and fruit and sweet cakes. In the evenings, after supper, they danced in the garden. They sang songs and played music on the lute and the viol. At the end of each day, one of them sang a song about love, or spring, or the beauty of the evening. Then they went to bed in their cool, quiet rooms. The windows were open to the night. They could hear the fountain splashing in the garden and the nightingales singing in the trees. It sounds a little like a dream, doesn't it? That was exactly what Boccaccio meant it to feel like.

[long-pause]

{img:72-young-people-sitting-circle-meadow-shade}

Every afternoon, in a shady meadow, each of the ten told one story, so there were ten stories every day.

[pause]

In the heat of the afternoon, when it was too hot to walk, the young people gathered in a shady meadow. They sat in a circle in the green grass, under the trees. And each of them, in turn, told a story. The king or queen of the day chose a theme. One day, the stories were about people who were lucky after many misfortunes. Another day, they were about people who got what they wanted by their own cleverness. Another, about clever answers and quick wits. The stories were of every kind. Some were funny, some were sad, some were romantic, and some were a little bit naughty. There were merchants and monks, kings and farmers, lovers and tricksters, sailors and princesses. They took place in Florence and Naples, in Paris and Egypt, and on the open sea. On Fridays and Saturdays, they told no stories. Friday was a day for prayer, and on Saturday, Boccaccio says, the ladies washed their hair.

[long-pause]

{img:73-poor-nobleman-falcon-on-glove-country-farm}

In one story, a poor young man gives away the only thing he has left, to honour the lady he loves.

[pause]

One of the most famous stories is about a young nobleman named Federigo. He was handsome, brave and kind. He fell deeply in love with a lady named Giovanna. To win her heart, he spent all his money on feasts and gifts in her honour. But Giovanna paid him no attention, and in the end, Federigo was poor. All he had left was a little farm in the countryside, and a falcon. It was one of the finest hunting birds in the world, and he loved it dearly. One day, Giovanna came to visit him at his little farm. Federigo was overjoyed. He wanted to give her a meal worthy of her. But he had nothing in the house at all. So he gave up the one precious thing he had left, to honour his guest. Giovanna was deeply moved by his great heart. Later, when she was free to marry again, she chose Federigo. She said she would rather have a man who needed riches, than riches that needed a man.

[long-pause]

{img:74-cook-roasting-crane-kitchen-fire-pretty-girl}

In another story, a quick-witted cook insists that cranes have only one leg.

[pause]

Another story is a funny one, about a cook named Chichibio. He worked for a rich gentleman named Currado, who loved hunting. One day, Currado's falcon caught a fine crane, a tall grey bird with long legs. Currado gave it to Chichibio to roast for supper. While it was roasting, a pretty girl from the village came into the kitchen. She begged Chichibio for one of the legs. To please her, he cut one off and gave it to her. That evening, the crane was served with only one leg. Where is the other leg? asked Currado. Cranes only have one leg, said Chichibio. The next morning, they rode out to the river to see. There stood a dozen cranes, all asleep in the shallows, each on one leg. Currado clapped his hands and shouted: ho, ho! The cranes put down their other legs and flew away. Chichibio thought fast. Ah, sir, he said, but you didn't shout ho, ho at the one last night. Currado laughed so much that he forgave him completely.

[long-pause]

{img:75-walking-down-hill-toward-florence-evening}

After a hundred stories, the young people felt stronger, and walked home to Florence together.

[pause]

The young people stayed in the villa for two weeks. In all, they told one hundred stories. Then, at the end of the tenth day of storytelling, they decided it was time to go home. They had rested, laughed and been happy together, and they felt stronger now. They walked back down the hill to Florence. They said goodbye at the church of Santa Maria Novella, where they had met, and went home to their families. Boccaccio does not tell us what happened to them afterwards. The Decameron was finished a few years later. It quickly became one of the most popular books in Europe. People copied it out by hand and read it aloud to each other. Later, writers all over Europe borrowed its stories, including Chaucer in England and Shakespeare. Boccaccio wrote that his book was for people who were sad, to comfort them. And in a way, it is a book about how people, even in the darkest times, can still find friendship, laughter and stories.

[long-pause]

{img:76-florence-spring-1349-workshops-reopening}

The young people have gone home, and the villa is quiet once more. Down in the valley, Florence is slowly waking up again.

[pause]

In the spring of 1349, the looms of Florence began to clatter again.

[pause]

In the spring of 1349, Florence began to come back to life. People who had fled to the countryside came home. Shops opened their shutters again. The looms began to clatter in the workshops. The Old Market filled up once more with farmers and their carts. The government met again in its palace. And up on the cathedral walls, the masons picked up their chisels and went back to work. But the city was very different. There were empty houses on every street, and workshops with no one to work in them. There were fields with no one to plough them. There were orphans to care for, and widows, and old people left alone. The hospitals and brotherhoods had been given a great deal of money during the sickness. Now they used it to feed the poor and care for the orphans. They also gave dowries to poor girls, so they could marry. Slowly, carefully, the city began to mend itself, like a torn piece of cloth.

[long-pause]

{img:77-medieval-wedding-procession-musicians-flowers}

In the year after the plague, there were so many weddings that the streets were full of music.

[pause]

There were weddings that year, a great many of them. In the year after the plague, people married in large numbers. Young people who had lost their parents married. Widows and widowers married again. The churches were full of wedding parties. The streets were full of music and dancing. Pipes and drums played, and neighbours threw flowers from the windows. And soon there were babies too, a great many of them. The sound of children crying and laughing came back into the empty houses. In the spring, the almond trees blossomed on the hills again. The swallows came back from Africa. The bells rang out over the city for weddings and christenings and feast days. Life is very stubborn, in the best possible way. Even after the hardest winter, it keeps pushing up through the cracks, like green shoots between old paving stones. Florence, like all of Europe, began again.

[long-pause]

{img:78-english-peasants-harvest-field-better-times}

Because there were so few workers left, ordinary people could ask for better pay, and get it.

[pause]

Because so many people had died, there were not enough workers. Wool merchants needed weavers, dyers and spinners. Farmers needed ploughmen and harvesters. Builders needed masons and carpenters. So the workers who had survived could ask for more money, and they often got it. For the first time in generations, ordinary working people found their work was truly valued. They ate better and dressed better. Some were able to buy land of their own. In England, the same thing happened. Labourers asked for higher wages, or moved to another village to find a better master. The government tried to pass laws to stop them and keep wages low. But the laws did not work very well. Over the next hundred years, the old system faded away. Under that system, many farm workers had been bound to their lord's land and could not leave. Many historians think the Black Death, sad as it was, helped bring about a fairer world for ordinary people.

[long-pause]

{img:79-painter-fresco-scaffold-church-wall}

To give thanks for surviving, the people of Florence paid for paintings, statues and chapels.

[pause]

The sickness also changed art. In the years that followed, painters in Florence and Siena painted in a new, more serious way. They painted saints and the Virgin Mary, and pictures of heaven. But the people of Florence also wanted beauty, joy and life. And they wanted to give thanks for having survived. So they gave money to build and decorate churches and chapels. They paid for paintings, statues and stained glass windows. Painters climbed onto wooden scaffolds and covered whole church walls with colour. Sculptors carved saints for the niches of Orsanmichele. Year by year, the city grew more beautiful than ever. The children and grandchildren of those who survived grew up surrounded by this beauty. In time, they would build the Florence of the Renaissance. It became the city of Brunelleschi and Botticelli, Leonardo and Michelangelo. Out of a very hard time grew one of the most creative cities the world has ever known.

[long-pause]

{img:80-old-surgeon-writing-large-book-candle}

The Pope's doctor wrote a great book on medicine, and doctors used it for more than two hundred years.

[pause]

Guy de Chauliac, the Pope's doctor in Avignon, had caught the sickness himself in 1348. But he recovered. He went on to write a great book about surgery and medicine, which he finished in 1363. Doctors all over Europe used it for more than two hundred years. In it, he described the sickness of 1348 carefully and honestly. He said it was the most terrible illness he had ever seen, and that doctors had been helpless before it. But he also wrote that he had stayed with his patients. He said he would have been ashamed to run away. And he encouraged other doctors to do the same. It is a quiet kind of courage, isn't it? Not the courage of a soldier, but of someone who simply stays, and keeps caring, because that is the right thing to do. Cities, too, began to learn. They appointed health officers to watch for illness and keep the streets clean.

[long-pause]

{img:81-tuscan-countryside-recovered-farms-harvest}

The sickness came back several times over the next centuries, but each time people rebuilt, and there were many good years in between.

[pause]

The sickness came back more than once in the years that followed. It returned in 1363, and again in 1374, and many times over the next three hundred years. Each time, it was less terrible than the first. But each time, people were afraid. And each time, they survived, rebuilt and went on. In between, there were many good years. There were years of good harvests, peace and plenty. The vines grew heavy with grapes on the hills of Tuscany. The olive trees were laden with fruit. The markets were full, and children played in the streets. Over time, people learned more about keeping their towns clean and safe. In the end, the great sickness faded from Europe almost completely. Today, it is very rare, and doctors can cure it with medicine. The people of 1348 could never have imagined that. Yet they kept going, year after year, so that their grandchildren could one day live in a safer world.

[long-pause]

{img:82-florence-today-dome-rooftops-morning}

Giotto's bell tower was finished in 1359, and later the cathedral got the largest dome in the world.

[pause]

The cathedral of Florence rose slowly, year by year. In 1359, Giotto's bell tower was finally finished. It was tall and slender and beautiful, covered in white, green and pink marble. Many years later, in the 1420s and 1430s, a brilliant architect named Filippo Brunelleschi built the great dome on top of the cathedral. It was the largest dome in the world. He built it without scaffolding from the ground, using a clever method he invented himself. The people of Florence called it simply the Cupola. Today, Florence still looks very much as it did in Boccaccio's time, if you see it from the hills. There is a sea of red roofs with towers and bell towers rising above them. The river winds through the middle, crossed by its bridges. And above it all floats Brunelleschi's great dome. On Giotto's bell tower, the little marble panels are still there. The ploughman, the weaver, the sailor and the astronomer are still at their work.

[long-pause]

{img:83-boccaccio-older-certaldo-study-books}

Boccaccio became friends with Petrarch, and was the first person ever to give public lectures on Dante.

[pause]

Boccaccio lived for another twenty-seven years after the plague. In 1350, he met Petrarch, whom he had admired for many years. Petrarch passed through Florence, and Boccaccio invited him to stay in his house. They became close friends. They wrote many letters to each other and visited whenever they could, for the rest of their lives. Boccaccio became a great scholar. He helped bring a Greek teacher to Florence. He helped make the first Latin translation of Homer's great poems in western Europe for many centuries. He loved Dante above all other poets. Near the end of his life, the city asked him to give public lectures on Dante's great poem in a church, for anyone who wanted to listen. He was the first person ever to do so. He spent his last years in the little red brick town of Certaldo, among the hills. His house is still there today. Visitors can climb its little tower and look out over the hills he loved.

[long-pause]

{img:84-poet-house-arqua-hills-vineyards-evening}

Petrarch died at his desk, it is said, with his head resting on a book, and his cat is still in his house.

[pause]

Petrarch lived until 1374. He travelled all over Italy and France, honoured by kings and popes. In his last years, he lived in a small house in a village called Arquà, in the hills south of Padua. It stood among vineyards and olive groves. His daughter and her family lived with him there. He died there in July 1374, at his desk, it is said, with his head resting on a manuscript of Virgil. It was as if he had simply fallen asleep while reading. His little house is still there today. You can see his chair and his desk. And in a glass case, there is something that makes visitors smile. It is his cat, which was preserved after it died. People have been coming to see it for hundreds of years. Boccaccio died a year and a half after his friend, in Certaldo. Petrarch had left him some money in his will. He said it was for a warm winter coat, to wear on cold nights while studying.

[long-pause]

{img:85-volunteer-ambulance-cathedral-square-today}

The Misericordia still helps people in Florence today, more than seven hundred and fifty years after it began.

[pause]

The Misericordia is still there in Florence today. More than seven hundred and fifty years after it was founded, it still has its headquarters on the cathedral square. Its members, men and women, are volunteers, as they always have been. They no longer wear black robes and hoods. But they still care for the sick, the old and the poor. They help people in need all over the city, day and night. It is one of the oldest charities in the world. It is still doing the same work its brothers did in the summer of 1348. And Orsanmichele still stands in the centre of the city. Inside, in the dim light, Bernardo Daddi's golden Madonna is still there. People prayed to it in the spring of 1348. It now stands in a beautiful marble shrine, built with the money they gave. Around the outside of the building are statues of the guilds' patron saints, made by the greatest sculptors of the Renaissance.

[long-pause]

{img:86-ponte-vecchio-goldsmith-shops-sunset-river}

Today, the little shops on the Old Bridge are full of gold, because a duke disliked the smell of the butchers.

[pause]

The Ponte Vecchio still crosses the river, with its little shops hanging out over the water. But the butchers, fishmongers and tanners are long gone. In the sixteenth century, the ruler of Florence ordered that only goldsmiths and jewellers could have shops on the bridge. The smell of the butchers offended him, it is said. He had to walk past them every day. So today, the little shops glitter with gold and silver and precious stones. In the evening, people still stand on the bridge to watch the sun go down over the river. The water turns gold, just as it did for Boccaccio. And all over the world, in libraries and homes, people still read the Decameron. They laugh at its stories. They think about the ten young people in the villa on the hill, telling stories to each other in the summer of 1348. Boccaccio wanted his book to comfort sad people. It still does.

[long-pause]

And so we leave Florence, resting in its valley among the hills.

[pause]

The swallows make one last circle over the rooftops. The great dome glows red and gold, then grey, and the first lamps come on below it. Out in the hills, the old villas stand quiet in their gardens. Fireflies drift between the olive trees. A fountain splashes softly in the dark, just as it did for ten young friends long ago. The river slips beneath its bridges, slow and patient.

[pause]

Perhaps you are still listening. If so, another peaceful tale is waiting on the screen to keep you company. And if tonight's journey brought you calm, a subscription will keep the door open for the next one.

[pause]

The bells have finished ringing now. Let the quiet settle over you, like a soft summer night in Tuscany.

[pause]

Sleep well, and good night.
