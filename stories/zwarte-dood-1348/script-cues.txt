Tonight we are going nearly seven hundred years back, to a walled city of stone towers on the banks of a river in Tuscany, in the north of Italy.

[long-pause]

It is a spring morning in the year 1347, and the sun is rising over the hills around Florence.

[pause]

The first light falls on the red-tiled roofs and the tall stone towers of the city, and on the river Arno, which flows slowly through the middle of it, green and brown, between its stone embankments. Mist lies in the valley. On the hills above the city, among the olive groves and the cypress trees, the bells of little churches and monasteries are ringing for the first prayers of the day.

[pause]

If you enjoy quiet stories like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe. Now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

Florence is one of the greatest cities in Europe.

[pause]

Perhaps a hundred thousand people live inside its walls, which is more than in London, and almost as many as in Paris. Only a few cities in the whole of Christendom are larger: Paris, and Venice, and Milan, and perhaps Genoa and Naples.

[pause]

{img:city-walls-towers-gates-tuscan-hills}
The city is surrounded by a great wall of stone, recently finished, with tall towers and fortified gates, and a moat in front of it. Inside the walls, the streets are narrow and winding, and the houses stand close together, three and four and five storeys high, built of brown and grey stone, with wooden balconies and shutters, and little gardens hidden behind high walls.

[long-pause]

Over the rooftops rise the towers.

[pause]

Long ago, when Florence was smaller and more violent, every rich family built a tall stone tower beside its house, so that it could defend itself against its enemies. There were once more than a hundred of them, rising over the city like a forest of stone. Many have been cut down now, by order of the government, to make the city more peaceful. But many still stand.

[pause]

And above them all, there are the great public buildings of the city: the tall, fortress-like palace of the government, with its slender bell tower; the churches of the friars, with their wide naves and their bell towers; and in the centre, a great new cathedral, still only half built, rising slowly, year by year, behind a forest of wooden scaffolding.

[long-pause]

{img:ponte-vecchio-shops-river-1347}
The river Arno is crossed by four stone bridges.

[pause]

The oldest and the busiest of them is the Ponte Vecchio, the Old Bridge, which has just been rebuilt, after a terrible flood fourteen years ago swept the old one away. On top of the new bridge, there are little shops on both sides, built of wood and stone, hanging out over the water. Butchers and fishmongers and tanners sell their goods there, and throw their scraps into the river below.

[pause]

From the bridge, looking down the river, there is a wonderful view of the city: the houses rising along both banks, the towers and the domes and the bell towers, and the hills beyond, green and blue, dotted with villages and villas.

[long-pause]

Florence is rich.

[pause]

It is rich from wool, and from cloth. The merchants of Florence buy raw wool from England and Spain, and bring it here, and thousands of men and women in the city wash it, comb it, spin it, weave it, dye it in beautiful colours, and finish it into the finest cloth in Europe. Then the merchants sell the cloth all over the world, from London to Constantinople.

[pause]

{img:wool-merchants-counting-house-florins}
And it is rich from banking. The great banking families of Florence lend money to kings and popes and princes all over Europe. Their gold coin, the florin, with a lily on one side and Saint John the Baptist on the other, is trusted everywhere, from England to Egypt.

[long-pause]

But Florence has had some hard years lately.

[pause]

A few years ago, two of its greatest banks, the Bardi and the Peruzzi, collapsed, because the King of England, who had borrowed enormous sums from them to pay for his wars in France, could not pay them back. Many people lost their savings. Then there were bad harvests, and food grew scarce and expensive. Last year, in 1346, many people in the city went hungry.

[pause]

But this spring, the harvest looks good. The fields around the city are green, the vines are budding on the hills, and the people of Florence are hopeful.

[long-pause]

{img:florence-rooftops-bell-towers-morning-light}
On this spring morning, the city is waking up.

[pause]

The gates are opening, and the carts and mules are coming in from the countryside, loaded with vegetables and firewood, eggs and cheese and wine. The shopkeepers are taking down their shutters. The bakers are taking the first loaves out of their ovens. In the workshops of the wool workers, the looms are beginning to clatter.

[pause]

And in a house somewhere in the city, a young writer named Giovanni Boccaccio is waking up too, and looking out of his window at the morning, and thinking about the stories he wants to write.

[pause]

He does not know yet that, before long, he will write the most famous of them all, about a time in Florence that nobody who lived through it will ever forget.

[long-pause]

{img:narrow-florence-street-shops-awnings-medieval}
The streets of Florence are busy from dawn to dusk.

[pause]

They are narrow and paved with large, flat stones, and in many of them, two carts cannot pass each other. The upper storeys of the houses lean out over the street, held up by wooden beams, so that the sky above is only a thin blue strip, and the streets below are cool and shadowy, even in summer.

[long-pause]

On the ground floors of the houses, there are shops.

[pause]

Each shop is a single room, open to the street through a wide arched doorway. In the morning, the shopkeeper takes down the wooden shutter, and folds it out to make a counter, and sets out his goods on it. Behind him, in the dim room, his family and his apprentices are at work.

[pause]

There are shops for everything. There are bakers and butchers, cheesemakers and grocers selling spices and salt and dried fruit. There are shoemakers and tailors, hatters and glovemakers, goldsmiths and silversmiths, booksellers and parchment-makers, apothecaries with jars of herbs and medicines, and barbers who also pull teeth and let blood.

[long-pause]

The people of Florence belong to guilds.

[pause]

A guild is a society of all the people who do the same kind of work: the wool merchants, the silk makers, the bankers, the doctors and apothecaries, the judges and lawyers, the butchers, the bakers, the stonemasons. Each guild has its own rules, its own officers, its own coat of arms and its own patron saint, and its own church or chapel, where its members meet and pray.

[pause]

{img:guild-hall-meeting-merchants-banners}
The guilds are very powerful. Only members of the guilds can be elected to the government of the city. Every two months, the names of the men who will govern Florence are drawn from leather bags, and they move into the government palace, and live there together for their two months of office, eating and sleeping and working in the great stone building, and then go home again.

[long-pause]

The greatest of all the guilds is the guild of the wool merchants.

[pause]

About a third of all the people in Florence earn their living from wool, in one way or another. Most of them are poor. They are the washers and the beaters, who clean the raw wool in the river; the carders and the combers, who straighten its fibres; the spinners, mostly women working at home with their spindles and distaffs; the weavers at their great wooden looms; the dyers, with their arms stained blue and red and yellow from the great vats of dye.

[pause]

{img:wool-dyers-vats-river-coloured-cloth}
Along the banks of the Arno, there are long rows of wooden frames, where the newly dyed cloth is stretched out to dry in the sun, in long bands of colour, scarlet and blue and green and deep violet, like great flags waving in the wind.

[long-pause]

In the middle of the city, there is a market square, called the Mercato Vecchio, the Old Market.

[pause]

Every morning, it is crowded. There are stalls selling vegetables and fruit, fresh from the countryside: cabbages and onions, beans and lettuces, figs and grapes and apples, according to the season. There are butchers and poulterers, fishmongers and cheesemakers, sellers of bread and wine and oil.

[pause]

{img:mercato-vecchio-stalls-crowd-morning}
There are pedlars, and jugglers, and storytellers, and beggars sitting on the church steps. Children run between the stalls, chasing each other and the dogs and the chickens. Women stand and gossip at the well. Men stand in the doorways of the taverns, arguing about politics and prices.

[long-pause]

Not far from the market, there is a strange and beautiful building, called Orsanmichele.

[pause]

It is a tall, square building of stone, with wide open arches on the ground floor. Down below, it is the city's grain market, where the wheat is stored and sold. But it is also a church. Inside, among the sacks of grain, there is a beautiful painting of the Virgin Mary, which the people of Florence believe can work miracles.

[pause]

Just this year, a painter named Bernardo Daddi has finished a new painting of the Madonna to hang there, glowing with gold, and every evening, people come to light candles in front of it, and kneel and pray, and sing hymns together.

[long-pause]

In the late afternoon, when the work is done, the people of Florence come out into the streets and squares to walk and talk.

[pause]

{img:evening-piazza-strolling-families-lanterns}
The rich merchants walk in their long gowns of fine wool, red and blue and green, lined with fur in winter. Their wives walk beside them in their best dresses, with their hair braided and covered with a fine veil. The craftsmen and their families walk in their plainer clothes. Young men stand at the corners in groups, laughing and watching the girls go by.

[pause]

As the evening comes on, the bells of the churches ring for evening prayer, one after another, all over the city, and the lamps are lit in front of the little shrines on the street corners, and the shopkeepers fold up their counters and close their shutters, and go inside for supper.

[long-pause]

{img:merchant-house-courtyard-well-loggia}
A wealthy merchant's house in Florence is built around a small courtyard.

[pause]

From the street, it looks plain and strong, with thick stone walls, small barred windows on the ground floor, and a heavy wooden door studded with iron nails. But inside the door, there is a little open courtyard, paved with stone, with a well in the middle, and arches around the sides, and sometimes a lemon tree in a pot, or a climbing vine.

[long-pause]

On the ground floor, around the courtyard, there are storerooms, full of bales of wool and cloth, barrels of wine and jars of oil, and the merchant's office, where he keeps his accounts in great leather-bound books, writing down every sale and every purchase in careful columns of figures.

[pause]

Up a stone staircase, on the first floor, there is the main room of the house, the sala. It is a large, high room, with a great fireplace at one end, a long wooden table, benches and chests along the walls, and painted beams on the ceiling.

[pause]

The walls are often painted too, with patterns of flowers and leaves, or with coats of arms, or even with pictures of gardens and trees, so that the family can feel they are sitting outdoors, even in winter.

[long-pause]

Above the sala, there are the bedrooms.

[pause]

The beds are very large, big enough for several people, with a wooden frame, a mattress stuffed with wool or feathers, linen sheets, woollen blankets, and in winter, a coverlet of fur. Around the bed there are curtains, to keep out the draughts. At the foot of the bed, there is a great wooden chest, painted and carved, where the family keeps its clothes and linen.

[pause]

{img:canopy-bed-painted-chest-candle-bedroom}
At the very top of the house, under the roof, there is the kitchen, so that the smoke and the smell of cooking, and the danger of fire, are kept as far as possible from the rest of the house.

[long-pause]

The day begins early, at sunrise, with the ringing of the church bells.

[pause]

The family gets up, and washes in basins of cold water, and dresses. The father goes to his office, or to his shop, or to the guild hall. The mother runs the household, with the help of the servants: the cook, the maids, perhaps a nurse for the little children.

[pause]

{img:mother-servants-kitchen-top-floor-hearth}
In the kitchen at the top of the house, there is a great open hearth, with a fire burning all day, iron pots hanging from chains over it, and a spit for roasting meat. The cook bakes bread, or sends the dough to the baker's oven down the street. She makes soups and stews, of beans and cabbage and onions, with a little meat or salted pork.

[long-pause]

The main meal of the day is eaten in the late morning, after the morning's work.

[pause]

The family sits at the long table in the sala. There is bread, of course, and soup, and perhaps a roast of veal or kid, or chicken, or fish from the river, or eggs. There is cheese, and fruit, and nuts, and wine mixed with water, in earthenware jugs. On fast days, which are many, there is no meat at all, only fish and vegetables and bread.

[pause]

The Florentines are proud of their food. They like strong flavours: pepper and ginger, saffron and cinnamon, garlic and herbs, sour juices from unripe grapes. The rich use costly spices brought by ship from the East, and like to show them off to their guests.

[long-pause]

{img:mother-teaching-daughter-spinning-window}
The children of the merchants learn to read and write and do sums.

[pause]

The boys go to a school run by a teacher in the city, where they learn to read Latin, and then to a special school, called an abacus school, where they learn arithmetic and bookkeeping, so that one day they can join their father's business. By the age of fourteen or fifteen, many of them are already working as apprentices in the shops and offices of the merchants.

[pause]

The girls learn at home, from their mothers, to spin and weave and sew, to run a household, and sometimes to read their prayers. Most of them are married young, at fifteen or sixteen, to men who are often much older.

[long-pause]

Not every Florentine lives in such a house.

[pause]

The wool workers and the labourers and the poor live in small rented rooms in the crowded quarters near the walls, or across the river, in Oltrarno. A whole family might live in one or two rooms, with a fireplace for cooking, a bed for everyone to share, a chest, a table and a few stools.

[pause]

They eat bread, beans and vegetables, soup and a little cheese, and on feast days, a little meat. They fetch their water from the public fountains and wells, and buy their bread from the baker, and their wine from the tavern on the corner.

[pause]

Life is hard for them, especially in years when the harvest is poor and the price of bread goes up.

[long-pause]

But rich or poor, the evenings are much the same.

[pause]

After supper, when it grows dark, the family gathers around the fire, or in summer, on the stone bench outside the front door, or on the roof terrace, where it is cooler. They talk about the day, and the news, and the neighbours. The grandmother tells stories. The children play. Someone sings an old song, or plays a lute.

[pause]

{img:family-roof-terrace-evening-tuscan-sunset}
And then, as the bells ring out for the last prayers of the day, the candles are blown out, and the city goes to sleep under the stars, with the sound of the river flowing softly beneath the bridges, and the watchmen walking slowly through the dark streets with their lanterns.

[long-pause]

{img:wool-worker-family-small-room-dawn}
Across the river from the centre of Florence, in the quarter called Oltrarno, a family of wool workers is waking up before dawn.

[pause]

They live in two small rooms on the third floor of a narrow old house, up a steep, dark wooden staircase. The father, whom we might call Piero, is a wool comber. His wife, whom we might call Lisa, is a spinner. They have four children: two boys and two girls, the eldest twelve and the youngest four.

[long-pause]

There is no glass in the windows, only wooden shutters, and in the early morning, a thin grey light comes through the cracks.

[pause]

Lisa gets up first, and lights the fire in the little fireplace, and warms some leftover soup in an iron pot, and cuts slices from yesterday's loaf of bread. The children wake up slowly, rubbing their eyes, in the big bed that they all share, and climb out onto the cold floor.

[pause]

They eat their breakfast quickly, sitting on stools around the little table: a bowl of bean soup, a piece of bread, and a cup of watered wine.

[long-pause]

Then Piero goes off to work.

[pause]

He walks down the stairs, and through the narrow streets, still dark and quiet, and across the bridge to the workshop of the wool merchant who employs him. There, in a long room on the ground floor, he works with a dozen other men, combing the wool, all day long.

[pause]

{img:wool-combers-workshop-long-room-morning}
It is hard, hot, dusty work. The combs are heavy iron tools with long sharp teeth, which must be heated over a little stove so that they glide through the wool. The combers pull them through the greasy, tangled fleece again and again, until the fibres are straight and smooth and shining, ready to be spun.

[pause]

The workshop is noisy and full of fluff, which floats in the air and settles on everything. At midday, the men stop for a short meal, of bread and cheese and onions, sitting on the floor of the workshop or out in the street.

[long-pause]

At home, Lisa spins.

[pause]

The merchant's agent comes to the house every few days, bringing her a basket of combed wool, and taking away the yarn she has spun. She sits by the window, where the light is best, with her distaff tucked under her arm, holding the cloud of soft wool, and her spindle turning and turning beside her knee, drawing out the thread, fine and even.

[pause]

{img:woman-spinning-wool-window-distaff-spindle}
She has spun since she was a little girl, and her hands move without thinking. While she spins, she watches the children, and talks to the neighbours across the narrow street, whose windows are only a few feet away, and sings old songs under her breath.

[long-pause]

The children have their own work.

[pause]

The eldest boy, who is twelve, is already working as an errand boy in a wool workshop, running between the workshops with bundles of wool and cloth. The eldest girl, who is ten, helps her mother with the spinning, and the cooking, and the little ones. The younger children play in the street with the other children of the quarter, and are sent to fetch water from the fountain, or bread from the baker.

[pause]

{img:children-fetching-water-fountain-narrow-street}
In the afternoon, the eldest girl goes to the market with her mother, to buy beans and onions and a little cheese, and sometimes, on a lucky day, a little piece of meat for the soup.

[long-pause]

Life is not easy for the wool workers.

[pause]

They are paid by the piece, and not very much, and they have no guild of their own to speak for them. In a year of bad harvests, when the price of bread goes up, they often go hungry. Last year, the year of the famine, was very hard, and the family lived for weeks on the bread that the government gave out from the public ovens.

[pause]

But the wool workers are proud of their skill, and proud of the beautiful cloth that is made from their work, which is sold all over the world. And they have their own pleasures, too: the feasts of the saints, the processions and the games, the gossip in the street, the songs in the taverns, and the warmth of their families.

[long-pause]

In the evening, Piero comes home, tired and covered in fluff.

[pause]

Lisa has made a supper of soup, with beans and cabbage and a little bacon, and bread, and a jug of wine. They eat together around the table, by the light of the fire, and the children tell their father about their day.

[pause]

{img:family-supper-firelight-small-room-evening}
After supper, they sit for a while by the open window, in the cool of the evening, listening to the sounds of the street: the neighbours talking, a baby crying, a man singing in the tavern on the corner, the bells of Santo Spirito ringing for the evening prayer.

[pause]

Then they say their prayers, and blow out the candle, and lie down together in the big bed, under their woollen blankets, and fall asleep, while the city settles into the night around them.

[long-pause]

{img:cathedral-under-construction-scaffolding-1347}
In the heart of Florence, the people are building a cathedral.

[pause]

It was begun about fifty years ago, to replace an old, small church that stood on the same spot. The Florentines wanted it to be the largest and most beautiful church in all of Christendom, so that everyone who came to their city would be amazed.

[pause]

In 1347, it is still far from finished. And it will not be finished for nearly a hundred years more.

[long-pause]

The walls are slowly rising, of brown stone, covered with patterns of white and green and pink marble, in squares and stripes and stars. Inside, the great nave is open to the sky in places, and the floor is littered with blocks of stone and piles of sand and lime.

[pause]

All around it, the stonemasons are at work. In their sheds and yards, they cut and carve the blocks of marble, with their chisels and mallets, in a constant ringing and tapping. The carpenters build the wooden scaffolding and the great wooden cranes, worked by men walking inside huge wooden wheels, which lift the heavy stones up to the top of the walls.

[long-pause]

Beside the cathedral, a bell tower is rising.

[pause]

It was designed by the most famous painter in Italy, Giotto, who began it about thirteen years ago, when he was already an old man. He died only three years later, when only the first storey was finished. Now, another master, Andrea Pisano, is continuing the work.

[pause]

{img:bell-tower-rising-scaffold-marble-panels}
On the walls of the lowest storey, there are little carved panels of marble, showing the work of human beings through the ages: a man ploughing a field, a weaver at his loom, a shepherd with his sheep, a sailor in his boat, a builder, a doctor, a horse rider, an astronomer looking at the stars. People stop in the street to look at them, and point out their own trades.

[long-pause]

Across the square from the cathedral, there is a very old building, shaped like an octagon, covered with white and green marble.

[pause]

It is the Baptistery of Saint John, the patron saint of Florence. Every child born in the city is baptized here, in the great stone font in the middle. Inside, the domed ceiling is covered with mosaics of gold and colour, showing the story of the world from the Creation to the Last Judgment, glittering in the light of the candles.

[pause]

On its southern side, there is a pair of new bronze doors, made by Andrea Pisano just a few years ago, showing the life of Saint John, in little golden pictures.

[long-pause]

On the twenty-fourth of June, the feast of Saint John, the whole city celebrates.

[pause]

For days beforehand, the streets are decorated with banners and tapestries hanging from the windows. The shops display their finest goods. On the eve of the feast, there is a great procession, with the clergy and the guilds and the government, carrying candles and banners and relics, through the streets to the Baptistery.

[pause]

{img:feast-procession-banners-candles-street}
On the day itself, there are races, and games, and music and dancing in the squares. There is a horse race, called the palio, through the streets of the city, with the riders galloping wildly over the stones, and the crowds cheering. The prize is a length of costly red cloth.

[long-pause]

Florence has many other churches, too.

[pause]

In the west of the city, there is the great church of Santa Maria Novella, belonging to the Dominican friars, who wear black and white robes. In the east, there is the church of Santa Croce, belonging to the Franciscan friars, who wear plain brown robes tied with a rope.

[pause]

The friars preach in the squares in front of their churches, to great crowds of people. They visit the sick and the poor, and give food to the hungry. Inside their churches, the walls are covered with paintings by Giotto and his pupils, telling the stories of Saint Francis and the saints, in bright colours.

[long-pause]

There are also many small churches, chapels and monasteries all over the city, and the bells of each of them ring at the hours of prayer, from before dawn until late at night.

[pause]

The bells tell the time of day for everyone in the city: when to get up, when to start work, when to eat, when to stop work, when to go to bed. Most people do not have clocks. They listen to the bells.

[pause]

{img:bell-ringers-tower-ropes-evening-light}
In the evening, at sunset, all the bells of Florence ring together, for the evening prayer, the Ave Maria. And all over the city, people stop for a moment, wherever they are, in the street or in their shops or in their homes, and say a short prayer, before going on with their evening.

[long-pause]

{img:young-writer-desk-manuscripts-window-florence}
Giovanni Boccaccio is about thirty-four years old in the spring of 1347.

[pause]

He is a big, round-faced man, with dark eyes and a cheerful, friendly manner. He loves good food and good wine and good company, and he loves to laugh. Above all, he loves stories, and he loves to write.

[long-pause]

He was born in 1313, the son of a merchant of Florence who worked for the great Bardi bank. His father wanted him to become a merchant too, and when Giovanni was a boy, he sent him to learn the business.

[pause]

But young Giovanni was not interested in accounts and bales of wool. He wanted to read and write poetry. So his father sent him, instead, to study law. But Giovanni was not interested in law either.

[pause]

When he was about fourteen, his father took him to Naples, in the south of Italy, where the Bardi bank had an important office. And there, for many years, young Giovanni lived a happy life, in the beautiful, sunny city by the sea, under the great volcano of Vesuvius.

[long-pause]

Naples was the capital of a kingdom, with a king and a court, and Giovanni spent his time among the young nobles and the scholars and the poets of the court.

[pause]

He went to parties and dances and picnics by the sea. He read the old Latin poets, and the new poets of Italy, above all Dante, the great poet of Florence, who had died when Giovanni was a boy, and whose great poem, the Divine Comedy, he loved more than any other book.

[pause]

{img:young-man-reading-garden-bay-of-naples}
He fell in love, too, with a lady of the court, and wrote poems and stories about her, calling her by a secret name, Fiammetta, which means little flame. Nobody knows for certain who she really was.

[long-pause]

Then, in 1341, when he was about twenty-eight, his father's business ran into difficulties, and Giovanni had to come home to Florence.

[pause]

He was sad to leave Naples. Florence seemed grey and serious to him, after the sunshine and the sea and the court. He missed his friends, and his old life. He wrote that he was living in a gloomy house, with a gloomy old father.

[pause]

But he went on writing. He wrote long poems and romances, in Italian, about love and adventure, about knights and nymphs and shepherds, and about the lovers of ancient times. He read the old books in Latin and Greek. He began to make friends with the scholars of Florence.

[long-pause]

Boccaccio is a wonderful storyteller.

[pause]

He has an eye for the funny and the absurd, for the little foolishnesses of people, and also for their kindness and their courage. He listens to everyone: merchants and priests, peasants and nobles, servants and ladies. He remembers the stories he hears in the taverns and the markets and the houses of his friends, and he writes them down, and makes them better.

[pause]

{img:tavern-storyteller-listeners-candle-laughing}
He loves Florence, too, despite everything, its busy streets, its clever, quarrelsome people, its churches and towers and the hills around it. He knows it well, every street and square, every church and tavern.

[long-pause]

In the spring of 1347, he is living quietly in the city, working on his books, and visiting his friends.

[pause]

He has heard, perhaps, the first strange rumours from far away in the East, carried by the merchants and the sailors who come up the river from the port of Pisa. Rumours of a terrible sickness, in the lands beyond the sea, in Asia and around the Black Sea, killing great numbers of people.

[pause]

But everyone has heard such rumours before. The East is very far away. And the people of Florence have other things to think about: the harvest, the price of bread, the wool trade, and the politics of the city.

[long-pause]

Boccaccio is not yet famous.

[pause]

He is known in Florence as a clever young writer of love poems and romances, the son of a merchant who failed. He has a few friends who admire his work. But nobody, and certainly not Boccaccio himself, imagines that he will become one of the greatest writers in the history of Italy.

[pause]

{img:writer-walking-arno-bridge-sunset}
In the evenings, he likes to walk along the river, across the Ponte Vecchio, with its little shops, and watch the sun go down behind the hills, turning the water of the Arno to gold.

[pause]

He does not know that, in a year's time, the city he loves will be changed forever, and that he will be the one to tell the world what happened.

[long-pause]

{img:old-chronicler-writing-book-study-lamp}
In another house in Florence, an old man is writing a history of his city.

[pause]

His name is Giovanni Villani. He is about seventy years old, a grey-haired, thoughtful man, who has been a merchant and a banker, and has held many offices in the government of Florence. And for many years now, he has been writing a great book, a chronicle, which tells the whole history of Florence, year by year.

[long-pause]

Villani began his chronicle in the year 1300, which was a special year, a Jubilee year, when he went on a pilgrimage to Rome.

[pause]

There, he saw the great ruins of the ancient city, the Colosseum and the Forum and the aqueducts, and he thought about how the Romans had written down their history, so that people would remember them forever. And he thought that Florence, which he believed was the daughter of Rome, deserved a history too.

[pause]

So he went home, and began to write.

[long-pause]

His chronicle tells everything.

[pause]

It tells the legends of how Florence was founded, long ago, in the time of the Romans. It tells about the wars and quarrels of the city, between the great families, and between the parties, and with the neighbouring cities of Pisa and Siena and Lucca. It tells about floods and fires, famines and comets, the deaths of popes and kings.

[pause]

{img:chronicle-manuscript-pages-illuminated-letters}
But it also tells, in great detail, about the ordinary life of the city, as no other book of the time does.

[long-pause]

Villani loves numbers.

[pause]

He writes that Florence has about ninety thousand people living inside the walls, and that the city eats about a hundred and forty thousand bushels of grain a year. He writes that there are about two hundred workshops of the wool guild, making seventy or eighty thousand pieces of cloth a year, worth more than a million gold florins.

[pause]

He writes that there are about eight to ten thousand boys and girls learning to read in the city, and about a thousand or more learning arithmetic in the abacus schools, and five or six hundred learning Latin and logic in the grammar schools. He writes that there are a hundred and ten churches in the city, and thirty hospitals, with more than a thousand beds, for the sick and the poor.

[pause]

He counts the bakers, the doctors, the lawyers, the notaries. He counts the bridges, the gates, the towers. He wants future generations to know exactly what his city was like.

[long-pause]

Villani has had a difficult life lately.

[pause]

He was a partner in one of the banks that failed a few years ago, and he lost much of his fortune. For a while, he was even put in prison, because of the debts of the bank. Now he is an old man, living quietly, and the chronicle is his great comfort.

[pause]

{img:old-man-garden-bench-reading-afternoon}
Every day, he sits at his desk, in a quiet room of his house, with his books and his papers around him, and writes, in his clear, careful hand, about the events of the past year: the weather, the harvest, the prices in the market, the news from other cities, the doings of the government.

[long-pause]

In 1347, he writes about the famine of the year before, and about how the government of Florence bought grain from far away, and baked bread in public ovens, and gave it out to the hungry, so that many lives were saved.

[pause]

He writes about the strange events in the world. He writes that, according to reports from merchants coming from the East, there has been a great sickness in the lands of the Tartars and the Turks, and in Syria and the countries around the Black Sea, and that many people there have died.

[pause]

He writes it down calmly, as he writes everything, as a piece of news from far away.

[long-pause]

In the evening, Villani puts down his pen, and closes his book, and goes to sit in the garden behind his house, in the cool air, among the fig trees and the roses.

[pause]

{img:quiet-garden-twilight-fig-tree-roses}
He listens to the bells of the city ringing for the evening prayer, and the swallows calling as they swoop over the rooftops, and the voices of his neighbours in their gardens. He thinks about the history of Florence, and all the things that have happened in it, in his long life.

[pause]

He has seen floods, and famines, and wars, and fires. He thinks, perhaps, that he has seen everything.

[long-pause]

{img:tuscan-hills-olive-groves-cypress-farmhouse}
Outside the walls of Florence, the countryside rolls away in every direction, in soft green and golden hills.

[pause]

The Florentines call it the contado, the country that belongs to the city. It is a beautiful, gentle landscape: hills covered with olive groves, their leaves silver-green in the wind; vineyards climbing up the slopes in neat rows; fields of wheat and barley in the valleys; dark, slender cypress trees standing like sentinels along the roads and around the farmhouses; and little stone villages and churches perched on the hilltops.

[long-pause]

Most of the people of Tuscany live in the countryside, and work on the land.

[pause]

Many of them are sharecroppers. They live on a farm owned by a rich family in the city, and work the land, and at the end of each year, they give half of everything they have grown to the owner: half the wheat, half the wine, half the olive oil, half the eggs and the cheese and the chickens. The other half, they keep for themselves.

[pause]

The farmhouse is built of stone, with a red-tiled roof, two storeys high. On the ground floor, there are the stables for the oxen and the donkey, and storerooms for the tools and the grain. Upstairs, there is a big kitchen with an open hearth, where the family cooks and eats and spends the evenings, and a few small bedrooms.

[long-pause]

The year on the farm follows the seasons.

[pause]

In autumn, the farmers plough their fields with oxen, and sow the wheat. In winter, they prune the vines and the olive trees, mend their tools, and cut wood for the fire. In spring, they hoe the fields and plant the vegetables, and the sheep have their lambs. In early summer, they cut the hay. In midsummer, under the hot sun, they harvest the wheat with sickles, and thresh it on the stone floors in front of their houses.

[pause]

{img:wheat-harvest-sickles-tuscan-summer-field}
In late September, there is the grape harvest, the vendemmia, the happiest time of the year, when everyone, young and old, goes out into the vineyards to pick the grapes, and then the grapes are trodden in great wooden tubs to make the wine. And in November, the olives are picked, and taken to the press, and the new green oil flows out, and everyone eats bread soaked in it, warm from the fire.

[long-pause]

The rich families of Florence love the countryside too.

[pause]

Many of them own villas in the hills around the city: comfortable country houses, with gardens and orchards, fountains and fishponds, and views over the valley of the Arno. In the summer, when the city is hot and dusty and smelly, they leave Florence and go to their villas, for weeks or months at a time.

[pause]

{img:villa-garden-fountain-view-florence-hills}
There, they walk in their gardens, and ride in the hills, and hunt with hawks and dogs, and sit in the shade of the trees in the afternoons, reading and talking and playing music. In the evenings, they eat in the open air, under a pergola covered with vines, with the lights of Florence twinkling far away in the valley below.

[long-pause]

Above Florence, on a hill to the north, there is a little town called Fiesole.

[pause]

It is much older than Florence. It was built by the ancient Etruscans, long before the Romans came, and there are still the ruins of a Roman theatre there, and ancient walls. From the hill of Fiesole, there is a wonderful view of the whole of Florence, spread out in the valley below: the river, the walls, the towers, the roofs, the half-built cathedral.

[pause]

{img:fiesole-hill-view-florence-valley-evening}
On the slopes between Fiesole and the city, among the olive groves and the cypresses, there are many villas and gardens and monasteries, and little paths and lanes winding between stone walls covered with ivy and wild roses.

[long-pause]

Boccaccio knows these hills well.

[pause]

His family has a small property in the countryside, at Certaldo, a little hilltop town to the southwest of Florence, built of red brick. He has spent many summers there. And he loves to walk in the hills around Florence, among the villas and the gardens.

[pause]

One day, he will set his most famous book in these hills, in a beautiful villa with a garden full of flowers and fountains, where a group of young people will take refuge, and tell each other stories, to pass the time.

[long-pause]

On a summer evening in the countryside, the air is full of the scent of hay and wild thyme and the warm earth.

[pause]

The farmers come home from the fields, tired and sunburnt. The women cook supper over the fire. The children bring in the goats. The oxen are led into the stable. Then the family sits outside on the stone bench by the door, in the cooling air, and watches the sun go down behind the hills.

[pause]

{img:farm-family-stone-bench-sunset-fireflies}
The fireflies come out among the olive trees, and the crickets begin to sing, and the bells of the village church ring for the evening prayer. Far away, in the valley, the lights of the great city glow faintly in the dusk.

[pause]

It is a peaceful world, which has been much the same for hundreds of years.

[long-pause]

{img:summer-1347-wheat-harvest-golden-hills}
The summer of 1347 is a good summer in Tuscany.

[pause]

After the hunger of the year before, the harvest this year is rich. The wheat in the fields around Florence grows tall and golden. The farmers go out with their sickles in June and July, and bring in the sheaves, and thresh them on the stone floors in front of their houses, and the grain pours into the sacks.

[long-pause]

In the city, there is relief everywhere.

[pause]

The price of bread falls. The bakers' shelves are full again. The market is crowded with fresh fruit and vegetables: cherries and apricots, then peaches and figs and melons, beans and cucumbers and lettuces. People who went hungry last year can eat well again, and they are grateful.

[pause]

The friars hold services of thanksgiving in the churches. The guilds hold feasts in their halls. The government is pleased, and the people of Florence feel that perhaps the hard times are over.

[long-pause]

In the summer heat, the city slows down.

[pause]

By midday, the sun beats down on the stone streets and squares, and the air is hot and still. The shopkeepers pull down their awnings. People keep to the shade of the narrow streets and the loggias. In the afternoon, after the midday meal, many people rest, in the coolest rooms of their houses, behind closed shutters, while the city grows quiet, and only the cicadas sing in the gardens.

[pause]

{img:shuttered-street-afternoon-heat-cicadas}
In the evening, when the heat breaks, everyone comes out again.

[long-pause]

On summer evenings, the young people of Florence go down to the river.

[pause]

Below the city walls, the banks of the Arno are shady with poplars and willows, and the water is shallow and slow in summer. The boys swim and splash in the river, and lie on the sandy banks in the evening sun. Fishermen stand in the shallows with their nets. Washerwomen kneel on flat stones at the edge of the water, beating the clothes clean, and laughing and calling to one another.

[pause]

{img:arno-riverbank-summer-evening-swimmers-poplars}
Further along, there are mills on the river, with great wooden waterwheels turning slowly in the current, grinding the new wheat into flour.

[long-pause]

In the squares, there is music and dancing.

[pause]

Young men and women dance in rings, holding hands, to the music of pipes and drums and lutes, under the evening sky. Storytellers sit on the steps of the churches and tell old stories of knights and ladies, of Charlemagne and King Arthur, to little crowds of listeners. Children chase each other through the crowds, and dogs bark, and old men sit on stone benches by the walls of the palaces, talking about the old days.

[pause]

In the houses of the rich, there are dinners in the gardens and on the roof terraces, with lanterns hanging from the vines, and music, and long conversations late into the night.

[long-pause]

In the countryside, the grapes are ripening on the vines, and the olives are swelling on the trees.

[pause]

In the villas in the hills, the rich families are spending the summer away from the heat of the city. They walk in their gardens in the evening, and sit by their fountains, and watch the sun go down behind the hills, and the lights of Florence come on, far below in the valley.

[pause]

{img:villa-terrace-sunset-view-florence-lights}
In September, there is the grape harvest, and everyone, rich and poor, goes out into the vineyards, and the wine presses creak, and the new wine flows into the barrels. In November, the olives are picked, and pressed, and the new oil is poured over hot bread, and everyone says it is the best for years.

[long-pause]

Nobody knows that it is the last ordinary summer that many of them will see.

[pause]

The rumours from the East are still very far away. The ships from the Black Sea are still at sea. And in Florence, on a warm summer night in 1347, the people sleep peacefully, with their windows open to the night air, and the stars shining over the hills, and the river flowing softly under the bridges, and the bells of the churches ringing the hours, one after another, through the quiet dark.

[long-pause]

{img:genoese-galleys-harbour-sails-1347}
Far away from Florence, on the shores of the Black Sea, there is a city called Caffa.

[pause]

It is a busy trading port, on the coast of the Crimea, belonging to the merchants of Genoa, the great seafaring city on the northwest coast of Italy. Here, the Genoese buy silk and spices, furs and slaves and grain, brought from the lands of the East, along the great caravan roads across Asia, which people will one day call the Silk Road. And here, they load them onto their ships, and sail home to Italy.

[long-pause]

The ships of Genoa and Venice are the finest in the world.

[pause]

There are great galleys, long and narrow, with three masts and triangular sails, and banks of oars along each side, rowed by hundreds of men. There are round-bellied merchant ships, called cogs, with a single great square sail, which carry heavy cargoes of grain and wine and wool.

[pause]

They sail all over the Mediterranean, from Spain to Syria, from Egypt to the Black Sea, carrying goods and passengers and news. A ship from the Black Sea to Italy takes about a month or two, stopping at many ports along the way: Constantinople, the islands of Greece, Sicily.

[long-pause]

In 1346, a sickness came to Caffa.

[pause]

It had come from the East, along the caravan roads, from the lands of the Golden Horde, the great empire of the Mongols, which stretched across the steppes of Asia. Nobody knew exactly where it had begun. Somewhere far away, in central Asia, people said.

[pause]

{img:caravan-steppe-camels-dusk-distant}
The sickness came quickly, and it was very serious. Many people fell ill with a high fever, and with painful swellings in the neck and the arms and the legs, and a great many of them did not recover. It spread from person to person, and from town to town, along the roads and the rivers.

[long-pause]

Today, we know what it was.

[pause]

It was a disease caused by tiny living things, bacteria, far too small to be seen without a microscope. They lived in the fleas that lived on rats and other small animals, and when the fleas bit people, the people fell ill. Sometimes, it could also pass directly from person to person, through the air.

[pause]

But in the fourteenth century, nobody knew anything about bacteria, or about fleas. Nobody knew why people fell ill, or how the sickness spread. Some people thought it came from bad air, from poisonous mists rising out of the earth. Some thought it was caused by the stars and the planets, which had come together in an unlucky way. Many believed that it was a punishment sent by God.

[long-pause]

In the autumn of 1347, some Genoese ships sailed from the Black Sea, through Constantinople, toward Italy.

[pause]

Without knowing it, they carried the sickness with them, in the rats and the fleas hiding among the sacks of grain and the bales of cloth in their holds, and among the sailors themselves.

[pause]

{img:ships-arriving-sicily-harbour-autumn}
In October 1347, some of the ships arrived at the port of Messina, on the island of Sicily. And within a few days, the people of Messina began to fall ill.

[long-pause]

From Messina, the sickness spread slowly through Sicily, and across the narrow strait to the mainland of Italy.

[pause]

Other ships carried it to other ports. By the end of 1347, it had reached Genoa, and Venice, and Pisa, and Marseille in France. Then, from the ports, it began to spread inland, along the roads and the rivers, from town to town and village to village, carried by travellers and merchants and pilgrims, by carts and mules, and by the rats and the fleas that went everywhere with them.

[pause]

The cities began to take what precautions they could. Some of them closed their gates to travellers from places where the sickness was. Venice chose three wise men to look after the health of the city, and ordered that ships from the East should wait outside the harbour before anyone could come ashore. But nobody really knew what to do.

[long-pause]

In Florence, in the winter of 1347, the news came, from Pisa and from Genoa.

[pause]

Merchants arriving from the ports told of the sickness there. Letters came from friends and business partners in other cities. People talked about it in the markets and the taverns, and in the churches, and grew anxious.

[pause]

{img:florence-winter-street-worried-merchants}
The government of Florence did what it could. It ordered the streets to be cleaned, and the rubbish to be taken away. It forbade people from bringing in old clothes and cloth from places where the sickness was. It ordered prayers and processions in the churches, to ask God to spare the city.

[pause]

And the winter went by, cold and grey, and the people of Florence waited, and hoped.

[long-pause]

{img:avignon-papal-palace-rhone-bridge}
Far to the west, in the south of France, on the banks of the great river Rhône, there is the city of Avignon.

[pause]

In 1347, it is one of the most important cities in Europe, because the Pope lives here. For nearly forty years, the popes have lived in Avignon instead of Rome, and they have built themselves an enormous palace, high on a rock above the river, with great stone towers and walls like a fortress.

[long-pause]

The Pope in 1347 is Clement the Sixth.

[pause]

He is a Frenchman, about fifty-six years old, a learned, generous, cultured man, who loves books and music and art, and likes to live in splendour. He has made his palace one of the most magnificent in the world, with great halls painted with frescoes of hunting scenes and gardens and fishponds, and chapels glowing with blue and gold.

[pause]

The city around the palace is crowded with cardinals and bishops, priests and monks, lawyers and scholars, merchants and bankers, artists and musicians, from all over Europe, who have come to the papal court on business, or to seek their fortune.

[long-pause]

Among them is a man who will become one of the most famous poets in history.

[pause]

His name is Francesco Petrarca, whom we call Petrarch. He is forty-three years old in 1347, the son of a Florentine lawyer who was exiled from Florence, and he grew up in Avignon and the country around it. He is a tall, handsome, elegant man, a scholar who loves the ancient Latin writers more than anything, and searches the old libraries of Europe for their lost books.

[pause]

{img:poet-scholar-study-books-avignon-window}
He is already famous. A few years ago, he was crowned with a wreath of laurel on the Capitoline Hill in Rome, as the greatest poet of his age.

[long-pause]

Petrarch is famous above all for his poems about a lady named Laura.

[pause]

He first saw her, he wrote, on Good Friday, the sixth of April, 1327, in the church of Saint Claire in Avignon. She was young and beautiful, with golden hair, and he fell in love with her at once.

[pause]

But Laura was married to another man, and Petrarch could only love her from a distance. For twenty years, he wrote poems about her, hundreds of them, short, perfect poems called sonnets, about her beauty, and her goodness, and his love and his longing. They are some of the most beautiful love poems ever written, and poets all over Europe will imitate them for centuries.

[long-pause]

Petrarch does not live in the city. He finds Avignon noisy and crowded and corrupt, and he calls it, unkindly, the Babylon of the West.

[pause]

Instead, he has a little house in the countryside, in a quiet valley called Vaucluse, about twenty miles away, where a clear green river bursts out of a great cave at the foot of a cliff. There, he lives simply, with a few servants and a dog, among his books.

[pause]

{img:vaucluse-spring-cliff-river-green-valley}
He reads and writes, and walks in the hills, and fishes in the river, and tends his little garden. And he thinks about Laura.

[long-pause]

In the winter of 1347, the sickness reaches Marseille, and then, in January 1348, Avignon.

[pause]

The Pope's doctor, a wise and skilful man named Guy de Chauliac, does what he can. He visits the sick and studies the disease, and writes down what he sees, carefully and honestly. He advises the Pope to stay in his rooms, and to keep great fires burning on either side of him, day and night, to purify the air. The Pope does as he is told, and he stays well.

[pause]

{img:pope-seated-between-two-great-fires-calm}
Clement the Sixth does many good things during those terrible months. He pays doctors to care for the sick, and for the poor to be buried. He grants forgiveness to all who die of the sickness. And when frightened people in some towns blame the Jews for the disease, he issues letters saying that this is false, and that the Jews are suffering just as much as everyone else, and must be protected.

[long-pause]

Petrarch is away from Avignon that spring, travelling in Italy, in the city of Parma.

[pause]

One day in May, a letter arrives for him, from a friend in Avignon. It tells him that Laura has died, on the sixth of April, 1348, in the morning, exactly twenty-one years after the day he first saw her.

[long-pause]

Petrarch has a precious book: a manuscript of the poems of Virgil, the great Roman poet, which he loves and carries everywhere with him.

[pause]

On the first page of it, he writes a note in Latin, in his small, careful hand. He writes about the day he first saw Laura, and about the day she died, and about how he heard the news. He writes that he has written it in this book, which he looks at so often, so that he will always remember, and so that he will understand that there is nothing left for him in this life.

[pause]

{img:old-manuscript-virgil-handwritten-note}
The book still exists today, in a library in Milan. And the note is still there, on the first page, in Petrarch's own handwriting.

[long-pause]

In the years that follow, Petrarch will go on writing poems to Laura, but now they are poems of sorrow, and of memory, and of hope that he will see her again, in heaven.

[pause]

He will live for another twenty-six years, and become the most famous scholar in Europe, and be honoured by kings and popes. But he will never forget the year 1348. He will write that future generations will not believe it, and will think it a fable.

[pause]

And he will write: Oh happy people of the future, who have not known these miseries.

[long-pause]

{img:florence-spring-1348-almond-blossom-hills}
The spring of 1348 comes to Florence as it always does.

[pause]

The almond trees blossom pink and white on the hillsides. The days grow longer and warmer. The swallows come back from Africa and build their nests under the eaves of the houses. In the fields, the wheat is green and growing. In the vineyards, the first leaves are unfolding on the vines.

[long-pause]

In the city, people go about their business, as usual.

[pause]

The wool workers are at their looms, the merchants in their offices, the shopkeepers at their counters. The children go to school. The friars preach in the squares. The government meets in its palace. The masons go on building the cathedral, and the bell tower rises a little higher, stone by stone.

[pause]

But everyone is anxious. The news from Pisa, and from other cities, is not good. The sickness is there, and it is spreading. Every day, travellers come into Florence with new stories. And everyone wonders when it will reach their own city.

[long-pause]

The churches are full.

[pause]

People go to Mass every day, and to confession, and they pray more often and more earnestly than usual. The friars lead processions through the streets, carrying crosses and banners and holy relics, followed by long lines of people, singing hymns and praying for God to spare the city.

[pause]

{img:penitent-procession-candles-hymns-street}
At Orsanmichele, the people crowd in front of the painting of the Madonna, lighting candles, and leaving gifts of money, and asking her to protect them. So much money is given at Orsanmichele in these months that the brotherhood which looks after it becomes one of the richest in the city.

[long-pause]

The government of Florence does what it can.

[pause]

It orders the streets to be cleaned, the rubbish to be taken away, and the drains to be cleared. It forbids the sale of rotten meat and fish. It forbids travellers from Pisa and Genoa from entering the city. It appoints officials to make sure that its orders are carried out.

[pause]

The doctors of the city meet, and discuss the sickness, and write advice for the people. Eat simple food, they say. Avoid bad air. Burn sweet-smelling herbs and woods in your houses, juniper and rosemary and bay. Carry a little bunch of flowers or herbs to hold to your nose when you go out. Keep calm, and cheerful, and do not be afraid, for fear itself makes people ill.

[long-pause]

In his house, Giovanni Villani goes on writing his chronicle.

[pause]

He writes about the sickness in the East, and in Sicily, and in Pisa and Genoa. He writes, as he always does, carefully and calmly, setting down what he has heard, and what he has seen.

[pause]

{img:old-chronicler-desk-window-spring-light}
In Boccaccio's house, the young writer is working on his books, and visiting his friends, and listening to the news. He is worried, like everyone else.

[long-pause]

Some time in March or April 1348, the sickness reaches Florence.

[pause]

Nobody knows exactly how it came. Perhaps it came with a traveller from Pisa, or a merchant with a bale of cloth, or a carter with a load of grain. Perhaps it came hidden in the fleas of a rat in a cart. However it came, it came quietly, and at first, almost nobody noticed.

[pause]

A few people fell ill in one quarter of the city, and then a few more in another. The doctors were called, and shook their heads. The priests were called. The neighbours whispered.

[pause]

And then, quite suddenly, everyone knew that the sickness was in Florence.

[long-pause]

In the spring evenings, the people of Florence still come out into the streets and the squares, as they always have, as the light fades and the bells ring for evening prayer.

[pause]

But they walk more quietly now, and they do not stay out as long. They speak in low voices. They look at each other with worried eyes. Some of them hold little bunches of herbs or flowers to their faces as they pass.

[pause]

{img:lanterns-shrine-street-corner-dusk}
In front of the little shrines on the street corners, the lamps are burning, and there are more flowers than usual, and more candles. People stop, and kneel, and pray, and then hurry home, and close their doors behind them.

[pause]

The river flows on beneath the bridges, as it always has, and the swallows wheel in the twilight over the rooftops, and the stars come out over the hills.

[long-pause]

{img:florence-quiet-street-summer-1348-empty}
In the spring and summer of 1348, the great sickness spread through the city of Florence.

[pause]

Later, people would call it the Black Death, or the Great Mortality. In those days, people simply called it the pestilence, or the plague.

[pause]

We will not tell this part of the story in great detail tonight. It was very sad, and very hard, and much has been written about it. We will tell it gently, and from a little distance, as Boccaccio himself chose to tell it.

[long-pause]

The sickness came quickly.

[pause]

A person might feel well in the morning, and fall ill in the afternoon, with a fever and a headache and a great tiredness. Often there were painful swellings under the arms or in the neck. Some people recovered, after many days of illness. But many did not, and often they passed away within a few days.

[pause]

It seemed to strike everyone, rich and poor, young and old, good and bad, without any reason that anyone could see. In some houses, everyone fell ill. In others, nobody did. Nobody understood it, and nobody knew how to cure it.

[long-pause]

The doctors did their best.

[pause]

They visited the sick, and studied the sickness, and gave the medicines they knew: herbs and syrups and powders, and the letting of blood. Some of them worked tirelessly, day and night, going from house to house, and many of them fell ill themselves. Others were afraid, and stayed away.

[pause]

{img:doctor-long-robe-visiting-house-door}
The priests and the friars did their best too. They visited the sick, and heard their confessions, and gave them the last rites, and prayed with them. Many of them, too, fell ill and died, because they would not leave the people who needed them.

[long-pause]

In Florence, there was an old society of men, called the Misericordia, which means mercy.

[pause]

It had been founded about a hundred years before, by a group of porters in the wool trade, who had decided to help people in need. Its members wore long black robes, with black hoods over their faces, so that nobody would know who they were, and they would not be thanked or praised for their good works.

[pause]

In the summer of 1348, the brothers of the Misericordia went out into the streets, day after day, carrying the sick to the hospitals on stretchers, and caring for them, and helping to bury those who had died, with prayers and with dignity. Many of them, too, fell ill. But others took their places.

[long-pause]

The hospitals of Florence were full.

[pause]

The greatest of them was the hospital of Santa Maria Nuova, founded long ago by a rich man named Folco Portinari, the father of Beatrice, the lady whom Dante loved. It had long rooms full of beds, cared for by nuns and brothers and servants, who nursed the sick, washed them, fed them soup, and prayed with them.

[pause]

{img:hospital-long-hall-beds-nuns-candles}
In that terrible summer, they worked without rest. And the people of Florence, rich and poor, gave the hospitals so much money, and so many gifts and bequests, that after the plague was over, Santa Maria Nuova became one of the richest hospitals in Europe.

[long-pause]

Some time in the summer of 1348, the old chronicler Giovanni Villani fell ill.

[pause]

He had been writing about the sickness in his chronicle, calmly, as he wrote about everything. In his last chapter, he wrote that the pestilence would last until, and then he left a little space, so that he could fill in the date when it was over.

[pause]

{img:unfinished-chronicle-page-quill-resting}
But he never filled it in. He passed away in the summer of 1348, and the space in his book is still empty today.

[pause]

His brother, Matteo, took up the pen, and went on writing the chronicle, where Giovanni had left it.

[long-pause]

In Siena, a little way to the south of Florence, a shoemaker named Agnolo di Tura was also writing a chronicle of his city.

[pause]

He wrote about the sickness, too. He wrote that in Siena, people said, and believed, that it was the end of the world. And he wrote, simply and sadly, that he had lost his own children.

[pause]

In Siena, they had been building a new cathedral, even larger than the one in Florence. After the plague, there were not enough people left, and not enough money, to finish it. Its great unfinished walls still stand today, open to the sky, beside the old cathedral, with pigeons nesting in the arches.

[long-pause]

All through that summer, it seems, Boccaccio stayed in Florence, and saw what was happening around him.

[pause]

He saw the empty streets, and the closed shops, and the shuttered houses. He saw the people who had fled the city, and the people who had stayed. He saw great kindness, and great courage, and also great fear. He saw how some people helped their neighbours, and how others shut themselves away.

[pause]

{img:writer-watching-from-window-empty-square}
And he decided that one day, he would write it all down, so that people would know what it was like, and he would write something else too: a book of stories, full of life and laughter and love, as a kind of answer to the sorrow.

[long-pause]

{img:empty-piazza-noon-heat-pigeons}
By the middle of the summer of 1348, Florence had become strangely quiet.

[pause]

The markets were nearly empty. Many of the shops were closed. The looms of the wool workers were silent. The masons had stopped working on the cathedral, and the cranes stood still above the half-built walls. The streets, which had once been so crowded, were almost deserted, especially in the heat of the afternoon.

[long-pause]

Many people had left.

[pause]

Those who could afford it had gone to their villas and farms in the countryside, where the air was fresher, and where there were fewer people, hoping that the sickness would not follow them there. Others had gone to stay with relatives in other towns. The government of the city had almost stopped meeting, because so many of its members had gone away.

[pause]

Those who stayed tried to live as best they could.

[long-pause]

Some people shut themselves up in their houses, with a few friends and family, and lived very quietly, eating simple food, drinking a little good wine, listening to music, and trying not to think about what was happening outside.

[pause]

Others did the opposite. They decided that, since life might be short, they would enjoy it while they could, and went from tavern to tavern, eating and drinking and singing.

[pause]

{img:courtyard-lute-player-quiet-family-evening}
Most people simply went on, day by day, doing what needed to be done, looking after their families and their neighbours, going to church, and hoping.

[long-pause]

The sickness did not go on forever.

[pause]

In the heat of July and August, it was at its worst. Then, as autumn came, and the weather grew cooler, it began, slowly, to grow less. Fewer people fell ill. More of those who fell ill recovered. By the winter, it had almost gone.

[pause]

But when it was over, the city had changed.

[pause]

Nobody knows exactly how many people in Florence died that year. Boccaccio wrote that it was more than a hundred thousand, but he was probably exaggerating, in his grief. Today, historians think that perhaps half of the people of the city, or a little more, were lost. Some families lost everyone. Others lost no one at all.

[long-pause]

Florence was not alone.

[pause]

All over Italy, the same thing happened, in city after city, town after town: in Venice and Genoa, in Siena and Pisa, in Bologna and Naples, in little villages in the mountains, and in monasteries far from any town. Everywhere, perhaps a third or a half of the people were lost.

[pause]

{img:italian-hill-village-quiet-dusk-church}
And it did not stop at Italy.

[long-pause]

In the summer of 1348, the sickness crossed the Alps, and spread into France and Germany and Switzerland and Austria. In June, a ship brought it to a little port called Melcombe, on the south coast of England, and from there, it spread across England, reaching London in the autumn.

[pause]

In 1349, it reached Ireland and Scotland and Wales, and the Low Countries, and crossed the sea to Norway, on a ship from England. In 1350, it reached Sweden and Denmark, and the far north. And slowly, it moved eastward again, across Poland and into Russia, until, in the early 1350s, it had touched almost every corner of Europe.

[pause]

Today, historians believe that between a third and a half of all the people in Europe died, in those few years. It was the greatest disaster in the history of Europe.

[long-pause]

And yet, life went on.

[pause]

In every city and village, the people who survived picked themselves up, and began again. They buried their dead, with prayers and with tears. They went back to their fields and their workshops. They married, and had children. They planted crops, and harvested them, and baked bread, and brewed beer, and sang songs, and told stories.

[pause]

{img:farmer-ploughing-spring-field-after-loss}
And in the spring of 1349, in Florence, the almond trees blossomed on the hillsides, and the swallows came back from Africa, and the bells rang out across the city for the morning prayers, as they always had.

[long-pause]

{img:santa-maria-novella-church-morning-young-women}
A few years after the plague, Boccaccio began to write the book that would make him famous.

[pause]

He called it the Decameron, which means ten days, in Greek. It is a book of a hundred stories. And it begins, not with a story, but with Florence in the summer of 1348.

[long-pause]

In the opening pages, Boccaccio describes the plague in the city, honestly and simply, as he saw it.

[pause]

Then he tells how, one Tuesday morning, in the middle of that terrible summer, seven young women meet by chance in the great church of Santa Maria Novella, after Mass. They are friends, all from good families, between eighteen and twenty-eight years old. They are sad and lonely, because so many of their family and friends are gone, and the city has become so quiet and so sorrowful.

[pause]

The eldest of them, whom Boccaccio calls Pampinea, has an idea. Why should they stay in the city, she asks, among so much sadness? Why not go out into the countryside, to one of their families' villas, and live there quietly together for a while, among the gardens and the fields, with music and good food and good company, until the sickness has passed?

[long-pause]

The others agree. But they say that they cannot go alone, without some men to accompany them.

[pause]

Just at that moment, three young men come into the church. They are friends of the young women, gentle, well-mannered young men, who are also sad and lonely. The young women ask them to come too. And the three young men gladly agree.

[pause]

{img:three-young-men-entering-church-sunlight}
The next morning, at dawn, the ten young people leave Florence, with a few servants, and walk out through the gates, and up into the hills, to a beautiful villa, about two miles from the city.

[long-pause]

Boccaccio describes the villa with great love.

[pause]

It stands on a little hill, a little way from the road, surrounded by trees and green plants. It has a courtyard in the middle, with loggias and galleries and fine rooms, all decorated with cheerful paintings. Around it there are meadows and wonderful gardens, with wells of cool, fresh water, and cellars full of good wine.

[pause]

Everything has been swept and cleaned, and the beds are made, and there are flowers everywhere, of every kind of the season, and rushes strewn on the floors. The young people are delighted.

[long-pause]

They decide to live a simple, happy life together.

[pause]

Each day, one of them will be the king or queen of the day, and will decide what they all do. They get up early, and walk in the gardens, among the roses and the jasmine, singing and making garlands of flowers. They eat a fine meal at midday, at tables set with white linen, beside a fountain, with wine and sweetmeats.

[pause]

{img:young-people-garden-table-white-linen-fountain}
Then, in the heat of the afternoon, when it is too hot to walk, they gather in a shady meadow, in the green grass, under the trees, and sit in a circle.

[pause]

And each of them, in turn, tells a story.

[long-pause]

Every afternoon, each of the ten tells one story, so that there are ten stories each day.

[pause]

The king or queen of the day chooses a theme. One day, the stories are about people who were lucky, after many misfortunes. Another day, about people who got what they wanted through their own cleverness. Another, about lovers whose love ended happily. Another, about clever answers and quick wits.

[pause]

{img:young-people-sitting-circle-meadow-shade}
The stories are of every kind. Some are funny, some are sad, some are romantic, some are a little bit naughty. There are merchants and monks, kings and peasants, lovers and tricksters, sailors and pirates, princesses and cooks. They take place in Florence and Naples, in Paris and Egypt, in Sicily and on the sea.

[pause]

Many of them are stories that Boccaccio heard in the taverns and the markets and the houses of his friends, and made his own.

[long-pause]

In the evenings, after supper, the young people dance in the garden, and sing songs, and play music on the lute and the viol.

[pause]

At the end of each day, one of them sings a song, about love, or the spring, or the beauty of the evening. And then they go to bed, in their cool, quiet rooms, with the windows open to the night, and the sound of the fountain in the garden, and the nightingales singing in the trees.

[pause]

On Fridays and Saturdays, they do not tell stories, because Friday is the day of Christ's suffering, and on Saturday the ladies wash their hair.

[long-pause]

They stay for two weeks. In all, they tell one hundred stories.

[pause]

Then, at the end of the tenth day of storytelling, they decide that it is time to go home. They have rested, and laughed, and been happy together, and they feel stronger now. They walk back down the hill to Florence, and say goodbye to each other at the church of Santa Maria Novella, where they met, and go home to their families.

[pause]

{img:walking-down-hill-toward-florence-evening}
Boccaccio does not tell us what happened to them afterwards.

[long-pause]

The Decameron was finished a few years after the plague, perhaps around 1353.

[pause]

It quickly became one of the most popular books in Europe. People copied it out by hand, and read it aloud to each other, and laughed and cried over it. Later, writers all over Europe borrowed its stories: Chaucer in England, Shakespeare, and many others.

[pause]

Some people think of it as a strange book to write about a time of such great sorrow. But Boccaccio knew what he was doing. He wanted to give his readers, who had suffered so much, something to make them smile again. He wrote that his book was for those who were sad, and especially for ladies in love, to comfort them.

[pause]

And in a way, it is a book about how people, even in the darkest times, can still find beauty, and laughter, and friendship, and stories.

[long-pause]

{img:storyteller-young-woman-meadow-circle-afternoon}
Let us listen, for a little while, to two of the stories that the young people of the Decameron told each other, in the shady meadow, on those long summer afternoons.

[pause]

The first is told on the fifth day, by one of the young women, whose name is Fiammetta. It is the story of Federigo and his falcon.

[long-pause]

Once upon a time, in Florence, there lived a young nobleman named Federigo degli Alberighi.

[pause]

He was handsome and brave and courteous, and he fell deeply in love with a lady named Giovanna, who was the most beautiful and the most gracious lady in Florence. To win her love, Federigo held jousts and tournaments in her honour, and gave great feasts and presents, and spent all his money, without holding anything back.

[pause]

But Giovanna, who was married, and who was as virtuous as she was beautiful, paid no attention to him at all. And in the end, Federigo had spent everything he had, and he was poor.

[long-pause]

All that he had left was a little farm in the countryside, at Campi, outside Florence, and a falcon.

[pause]

It was one of the finest falcons in the world, a beautiful hunting bird, and Federigo loved it dearly. So he went to live on his little farm, quietly and simply, and went hunting with his falcon every day, and bore his poverty patiently, without complaining.

[pause]

{img:poor-nobleman-falcon-on-glove-country-farm}
Now it happened that Giovanna's husband died, and she was left a widow, with a young son. And that summer, she went to stay at her own country house, which was close to Federigo's little farm.

[long-pause]

Her son, who was a boy of about ten, became friends with Federigo, and often went to visit him, and to watch him fly his falcon.

[pause]

The boy grew to love the falcon more than anything, and longed to have it for his own. But he did not dare to ask Federigo for it, because he could see how much Federigo loved it.

[pause]

Then the boy fell ill. His mother sat by his bed, day and night, and asked him if there was anything in the world that he wanted, so that she could get it for him and make him well. And the boy said that if he could only have Federigo's falcon, he believed he would get better.

[long-pause]

Giovanna did not know what to do.

[pause]

She knew that the falcon was all that Federigo had left in the world, and that he loved it dearly. But she loved her son more than anything. And so, one morning, she went, with a lady companion, to visit Federigo at his little farm, to ask him for it.

[pause]

{img:lady-arriving-humble-farmhouse-garden-morning}
Federigo was astonished and overjoyed to see her. He welcomed her with great courtesy, and asked her to stay and eat with him. But then he realized that he had nothing at all in the house to give her to eat, and no money to buy anything.

[long-pause]

He looked around his little house, in despair. And then his eye fell on his falcon, sitting on its perch.

[pause]

Without a moment's hesitation, for the sake of his lady, he took the falcon, and had it prepared, and roasted, and served it to her at the table, on a white cloth, in his little garden, with great joy, as a meal worthy of her.

[pause]

After the meal, Giovanna, with great difficulty, told him why she had come, and asked him, for the sake of her son, to give her his falcon.

[long-pause]

When he heard this, Federigo began to weep.

[pause]

At first, Giovanna thought that he was weeping because he did not want to give up his falcon. But then he told her, sadly, what he had done, and showed her the feathers and the claws and the beak of the bird, as proof.

[pause]

Giovanna was deeply moved, that he had given up the thing he loved most in the world, to honour her. She thanked him, and went home, sorrowful.

[pause]

A few days later, her son passed away, and she grieved for a long time.

[pause]

But later, when her brothers urged her to marry again, she thought of Federigo, and of his falcon, and of his great heart. And she said that she would rather have a man who needed riches, than riches that needed a man. And so she married Federigo, and made him rich, and they lived happily together for the rest of their lives.

[long-pause]

The second story is a funny one. It is told on the sixth day, by a young woman named Neifile, and it is about a cook named Chichibio.

[pause]

{img:cook-roasting-crane-kitchen-fire-pretty-girl}
Chichibio worked for a rich gentleman of Florence named Currado, who loved hunting with falcons. One day, Currado's falcon caught a fine crane, a tall grey bird with long legs, and Currado gave it to Chichibio to roast for his supper.

[pause]

While the crane was roasting, filling the kitchen with a delicious smell, a pretty young girl from the village, whom Chichibio liked very much, came into the kitchen, and begged him to give her one of the legs. And Chichibio, to please her, cut off a leg and gave it to her.

[long-pause]

That evening, when the crane was served, with only one leg, Currado was surprised, and asked Chichibio where the other leg was.

[pause]

Chichibio, thinking quickly, said that cranes have only one leg. Currado was angry, and said that he had seen hundreds of cranes, and they all had two legs. But Chichibio insisted. Very well, said Currado. Tomorrow morning, we shall go and look at the cranes. And if they all have one leg, I will believe you. But if not, you will be sorry.

[pause]

The next morning, at dawn, the two of them rode out to the river, where the cranes lived. And there, by luck, they found a dozen cranes, all standing asleep in the shallows, on one leg, as cranes do.

[pause]

Look, sir, said Chichibio, delighted. You see, they have only one leg.

[long-pause]

Currado rode up close to the cranes, and clapped his hands, and shouted: Ho, ho! And the cranes woke up, and put down their other legs, and ran off, and flew away.

[pause]

Well, said Currado, turning to Chichibio. What do you say now? They all have two legs.

[pause]

And Chichibio, frightened, and not knowing where the answer came from, said: Yes, sir. But you did not shout ho, ho at the crane last night. If you had, it would have put down its other leg too.

[pause]

Currado laughed so much at this answer that he forgave Chichibio completely.

[long-pause]

{img:young-people-laughing-meadow-evening-light}
The young people in the meadow laughed too, and the afternoon went on, with story after story, until the sun began to go down behind the hills, and the shadows of the trees grew long across the grass, and it was time for supper, and music, and dancing in the garden, under the first stars.

[long-pause]

{img:english-port-ship-arriving-summer-1348}
In June 1348, a ship came into the little harbour of Melcombe, on the south coast of England, in the county of Dorset.

[pause]

It was summer. The fields around the harbour were green, and the sheep were grazing on the downs. The fishing boats were pulled up on the beach, and the fishermen were mending their nets in the sun. Nobody in Melcombe paid much attention to the ship. Ships came in every week.

[long-pause]

But the ship had come from France, where the sickness was. And within a few weeks, the people of Melcombe began to fall ill.

[pause]

From there, the plague spread slowly along the coast and inland, through the villages of the West Country, along the roads and the rivers, from village to village and town to town. By the autumn, it had reached Bristol, and Oxford, and London.

[pause]

England in 1348 was a land of villages. Most people lived in small communities of thatched cottages, gathered around a church and a manor house, surrounded by great open fields, divided into long strips, where the villagers grew their wheat and barley and oats and beans.

[long-pause]

In the villages, the sickness came quietly, and stayed for a few weeks or months, and then moved on.

[pause]

In some villages, almost everyone was lost. In others, very few. The priests did their best, and many of them, too, fell ill. In some places, there were not enough priests left, and the bishops allowed ordinary people to hear each other's confessions.

[pause]

{img:village-priest-walking-lane-evening-lantern}
In the manor records, which recorded the business of each village, the clerks wrote down, in Latin, the names of those who had died, and who had inherited their land, and their cows and their sheep. Many of these records survive today, and from them, we can see village by village what happened.

[long-pause]

In London, a great new cemetery was opened, outside the city walls, in a field called East Smithfield.

[pause]

The King, Edward the Third, lost his young daughter Joan, who was about fourteen years old. She was travelling to Spain to marry a prince, and on the way, in the city of Bordeaux, she fell ill and died. The King wrote a sad letter to the King of Spain, saying that no fellow human being could be surprised that he was inwardly desolated by the sting of this grief.

[pause]

At the King's palace, and in the great houses and the monasteries, and in the little cottages, everyone grieved.

[long-pause]

In Paris, the King of France asked the doctors of the great university of Paris to explain the sickness. They met, and wrote a long, learned report.

[pause]

They said that it had been caused by an unlucky meeting of three planets, Saturn, Jupiter and Mars, in the year 1345, which had poisoned the air. They advised people to avoid bad air, to burn fragrant woods, to eat light food, and to avoid hot baths and too much exercise. It was the best that anyone knew in those days.

[pause]

{img:paris-scholars-gowns-meeting-hall}
In Germany and the Low Countries, groups of people called flagellants walked from town to town, in long processions, singing hymns, and praying for forgiveness. In many places, frightened people looked for someone to blame.

[pause]

But in many other places, people simply helped each other, as people always have.

[long-pause]

In 1349, a ship from England sailed into the harbour of Bergen, in Norway, carrying wool and a few sailors.

[pause]

The sickness spread from there across Norway, through the fjords and the mountain valleys, to the farms and the fishing villages. Some of the most remote valleys of Norway were almost emptied. Stories were told for centuries afterward, of a little girl found alone in a valley, years later, living wild among the animals, or of a church found deep in the forest, overgrown with trees, with no one left to ring its bell.

[pause]

{img:norwegian-fjord-wooden-church-forest-mist}
From Norway, the sickness spread to Sweden, and to Denmark, and around the shores of the Baltic, and then eastward, into Poland and Lithuania and Russia, where it reached the great city of Moscow in 1353.

[long-pause]

By then, it had touched almost every part of Europe.

[pause]

A few places were spared, or almost spared, perhaps because they were remote, or because of good luck. Some say the city of Milan was one, because its ruler ordered that any house where the sickness appeared should be closed off at once from the rest of the city. Some small regions of Poland and the Pyrenees mountains seem to have escaped too.

[pause]

But almost everywhere else, from Sicily to Scotland, from Portugal to Russia, people had lost someone.

[long-pause]

And then, slowly, it was over, at least for a while.

[pause]

The sickness went away, as mysteriously as it had come. The people who had survived looked around them, at their emptied villages and towns, their silent workshops and their overgrown fields, and wondered what to do next.

[pause]

{img:spring-fields-village-church-bells-rising-sun}
And then, as people always do, they began to rebuild.

[long-pause]

{img:florence-spring-1349-workshops-reopening}
In the spring of 1349, Florence began to come back to life.

[pause]

The people who had fled to the countryside came home. The shops opened their shutters again. The looms began to clatter in the workshops. The market in the Mercato Vecchio filled up once more with farmers and their carts. The government met again in its palace. And up on the walls of the cathedral, the masons picked up their chisels, and went back to work.

[long-pause]

But the city was very different.

[pause]

There were empty houses on every street. There were workshops with no one to work in them. There were fields in the countryside with no one to plough them, and vineyards with no one to prune them. Many families had lost their parents, or their children, or their brothers and sisters. There were orphans to be cared for, and widows, and old people left alone.

[pause]

The people of Florence cared for them as best they could. The hospitals and the brotherhoods, which had been given so much money during the plague, used it to feed the poor, to look after the orphans, and to provide dowries for poor girls so that they could marry.

[long-pause]

There were weddings, many of them.

[pause]

In the year after the plague, people married in great numbers, young people who had lost their parents, widows and widowers who had lost their husbands and wives. The churches were full of wedding parties, and the streets were full of music and dancing.

[pause]

{img:medieval-wedding-procession-musicians-flowers}
And soon, there were babies too, many of them, and the sound of children crying and laughing came back into the empty houses.

[long-pause]

Because so many people had died, there were not enough workers.

[pause]

The wool merchants needed weavers and dyers and spinners. The farmers needed ploughmen and harvesters. The builders needed masons and carpenters. And so the workers who had survived found that they could ask for more money for their work, and get it.

[pause]

For the first time in many generations, ordinary working people, the labourers and the craftsmen and the peasants, found that their work was valued, and that they could earn a decent living. They ate better, and dressed better, and some of them were able to buy land of their own.

[long-pause]

In England, too, the same thing happened.

[pause]

There were not enough people to work the land. The lords of the manors, who owned the land, needed labourers, and the labourers began to ask for higher wages, or to move from one village to another, looking for a better master. The government tried to pass laws to stop them, and to keep wages low. But the laws did not work very well.

[pause]

{img:english-peasants-harvest-field-better-times}
Over the next hundred years, the old system, in which peasants were bound to their lord and his land and could not leave, slowly began to fade away. Many people believe that the Black Death, terrible as it was, helped to bring a new and fairer world.

[long-pause]

The plague also changed how people thought about medicine and health.

[pause]

Doctors began to study the sickness more carefully, and to write about it. Cities began to appoint health officers, to watch for signs of illness and to keep the streets clean.

[pause]

In 1377, the little city of Ragusa, on the coast of the Adriatic Sea, which today is called Dubrovnik, made a new law: that any ship or traveller coming from a place where there was sickness must wait for thirty days on a little island outside the city, before they could come in. Later, the time was made forty days, in Italian quaranta giorni. And that is where our word quarantine comes from.

[long-pause]

The plague also changed art.

[pause]

In the years after the Black Death, painters in Florence and Siena painted in a new, more serious way. They painted pictures of saints and of the Virgin Mary, and of heaven and the Last Judgment, and of the shortness of life and the hope of the world to come.

[pause]

{img:painter-fresco-scaffold-church-wall}
But the people of Florence also wanted beauty, and joy, and life. And they wanted to give thanks for their survival. So they gave money to build and decorate churches and chapels, and to commission paintings and statues and stained glass windows. In the years that followed, the city grew more beautiful than ever.

[long-pause]

The cathedral rose slowly, year by year.

[pause]

In 1359, Giotto's bell tower was finally finished, tall and slender and beautiful, covered in white and green and pink marble. Today, it is one of the most beautiful bell towers in the world.

[pause]

And many years later, in the 1420s and 1430s, a brilliant architect named Filippo Brunelleschi would build the great dome on top of the cathedral, the largest dome in the world, without any scaffolding from the ground, using a method that he invented himself. The people of Florence would call it simply the Cupola.

[long-pause]

The plague came back, more than once, in the years that followed.

[pause]

It came back in 1363, and again in 1374, and many times after that, over the next three hundred years. Each time, it was less terrible than the first time, but each time, people were afraid. And each time, they survived, and rebuilt, and went on.

[pause]

{img:tuscan-countryside-recovered-farms-harvest}
And in between, there were many good years. Years of good harvests, and peace, and prosperity, when the vines grew heavy with grapes on the hills of Tuscany, and the olive trees were laden with fruit, and the markets were full, and the children played in the streets, and the bells rang out over the city for weddings and christenings and feast days.

[long-pause]

{img:boccaccio-older-certaldo-study-books}
Giovanni Boccaccio lived for another twenty-seven years after the plague.

[pause]

He became a friend of Petrarch, whom he had admired for many years. They first met in 1350, when Petrarch passed through Florence on his way to Rome, and Boccaccio invited him to stay in his house. They became close friends, and wrote many letters to each other, and visited each other whenever they could, for the rest of their lives.

[long-pause]

Boccaccio became one of the leading men of Florence.

[pause]

The government sent him on missions to other cities, to the Pope in Avignon, and to the lords of the cities of northern Italy. He became a great scholar, studying the ancient Latin and Greek writers. He brought a Greek scholar to Florence to teach, and helped to make the first translation into Latin of the poems of Homer, the Iliad and the Odyssey, so that people in western Europe could read them again for the first time in a thousand years.

[pause]

He loved Dante above all other poets. He wrote a life of Dante, and, near the end of his own life, he was asked by the city of Florence to give public lectures on Dante's great poem, the Divine Comedy, in a church, to anyone who wanted to listen. He was the first person ever to do so.

[long-pause]

In his old age, Boccaccio grew more serious.

[pause]

He became a little ashamed of the Decameron, with its funny and naughty stories, and once told a friend not to let the ladies of his house read it. But he never destroyed it. He spent his last years in the little red brick town of Certaldo, among the hills, in his family's house, reading and writing and studying.

[pause]

{img:red-brick-town-certaldo-quiet-street-evening}
He died there in December 1375, at the age of sixty-two, a year and a half after his friend Petrarch. He is buried in a church in Certaldo, and his house is still there today, and visitors can climb up into its little tower and look out over the hills that he loved.

[long-pause]

Petrarch lived until 1374.

[pause]

He travelled all over Italy and France, and lived in many cities, honoured by everyone. In his last years, he lived in a little house in a village in the Euganean Hills, south of Padua, called Arquà, among vineyards and olive groves, with his daughter and her family.

[pause]

He died there in July 1374, at his desk, it is said, with his head resting on a manuscript of Virgil, as if he had fallen asleep while reading. His little house is still there too, with his chair and his desk, and in a glass case, the body of his cat, which was preserved after it died, and which people still come to see.

[long-pause]

Matteo Villani, the brother of Giovanni, carried on the family chronicle for another fifteen years, writing about the years after the plague, the recovery of Florence, the wars and the politics and the prices in the market, just as his brother had done.

[pause]

{img:chronicle-desk-two-quills-brothers-portrait}
In 1363, when the plague returned to Florence, Matteo too fell ill and died. His son, Filippo, took up the pen, and continued the chronicle for a little longer. Together, the three Villanis wrote one of the greatest histories of any city in the Middle Ages.

[long-pause]

Guy de Chauliac, the Pope's doctor in Avignon, recovered from the plague himself, after falling ill with it in 1348.

[pause]

He went on to write a great book about surgery and medicine, which he finished in 1363. It was used by doctors all over Europe for more than two hundred years. In it, he described the plague of 1348 carefully and honestly, and said that it was the most terrible illness he had ever seen, and that doctors had been helpless before it.

[pause]

But he also wrote that he had stayed with his patients, because he was afraid of being shamed if he ran away. And he encouraged other doctors to do the same.

[long-pause]

And the people of Florence, whose names we do not know, the wool workers and the merchants, the bakers and the shopkeepers, the mothers and the fathers and the children, went on with their lives.

[pause]

{img:ordinary-florentines-market-day-sunlight}
They rebuilt their city and their families. They worked and married and raised their children. They went to Mass, and to the market, and to the festival of Saint John every June. And their children and their grandchildren grew up to build the Florence of the Renaissance, the city of Brunelleschi and Donatello, Botticelli and Leonardo and Michelangelo, one of the most beautiful and creative cities that the world has ever known.

[long-pause]

{img:florence-today-dome-rooftops-morning}
Florence is still there today, in its valley among the hills of Tuscany, on the banks of the river Arno.

[pause]

From the hill of Fiesole, or from the terraces on the hills across the river, it looks very much as it did in the time of Boccaccio: a sea of red-tiled roofs, with towers and bell towers and church domes rising above them, and the river winding through the middle, crossed by its bridges, and the green hills all around, dotted with villas and cypress trees.

[long-pause]

In the middle of it all, there is the cathedral, finished at last.

[pause]

Its walls are covered with white and green and pink marble, in patterns of squares and stars and stripes, just as the builders of the fourteenth century planned them. And on top of it rises Brunelleschi's great dome, of red brick, with white ribs, so large that it seems to float above the city, and can be seen from everywhere in Florence, and from far out in the countryside.

[pause]

Beside it stands Giotto's bell tower, tall and slender, with the little marble panels on its lowest storey, showing the ploughman and the weaver, the shepherd and the sailor and the astronomer, just as they did in 1347. Visitors can climb the more than four hundred steps to the top, and look out over the whole city.

[long-pause]

The Baptistery of Saint John is still there, across the square, with Andrea Pisano's bronze doors, and the golden mosaics glittering on its domed ceiling.

[pause]

{img:baptistery-golden-mosaics-visitors-quiet}
Every year, on the twenty-fourth of June, the feast of Saint John, the city still celebrates, with a procession, and a parade in medieval costume, and fireworks over the river at night.

[long-pause]

The Ponte Vecchio still crosses the river, with its little shops hanging out over the water.

[pause]

But the butchers and the fishmongers and the tanners are gone. Long ago, in the sixteenth century, the ruler of Florence ordered that only goldsmiths and jewellers should have shops on the bridge, because the smell of the butchers offended him. And so today, the little shops on the bridge glitter with gold and silver and precious stones.

[pause]

In the evening, people stand on the bridge and watch the sun go down over the river, as Boccaccio once did, turning the water of the Arno to gold.

[long-pause]

Orsanmichele still stands in the centre of the city, the tall square building that was once the grain market and the church of the Madonna.

[pause]

Inside, in the dim light, Bernardo Daddi's painting of the Madonna, which the people of Florence prayed to in the summer of 1348, is still there, glowing with gold, in a beautiful marble shrine that was built around it in the years after the plague, with the money that the people gave.

[pause]

{img:orsanmichele-shrine-golden-madonna-candles}
Around the outside of the building, in niches in the walls, there are statues of the patron saints of the guilds, made by the greatest sculptors of the Renaissance.

[long-pause]

And the Misericordia is still there too.

[pause]

More than seven hundred and fifty years after it was founded, the brotherhood still has its headquarters on the cathedral square, in Florence. Its members, men and women, are volunteers, as they always have been. They no longer wear black robes and hoods. But they still drive ambulances, and care for the sick and the old and the poor, and help people in need, all over the city, every day and every night.

[pause]

{img:volunteer-ambulance-cathedral-square-today}
It is one of the oldest charities in the world, still doing the same work that its brothers did in the summer of 1348.

[long-pause]

The hospital of Santa Maria Nuova, founded by the father of Dante's Beatrice, is still a hospital, in the same place, still caring for the sick, more than seven hundred years later.

[pause]

In Certaldo, Boccaccio's little red brick house still stands, with its tower, and the town still holds a festival in his honour. And in libraries all over the world, people still read the Decameron, and laugh at its stories, and think about the ten young people in the villa on the hill, telling stories to each other in the summer of 1348.

[pause]

In the old Laurentian Library in Florence, and in other libraries, there are still old manuscripts of the Decameron, copied out by hand in those first years after Boccaccio wrote it.

[long-pause]

In the evening, Florence grows quiet.

[pause]

The tourists go back to their hotels. The shops close. The swallows wheel and cry over the rooftops in the last of the light. The dome of the cathedral glows red and gold in the sunset, and then grey, and then the lamps come on, and light it from below.

[pause]

{img:florence-dusk-dome-lit-swallows-sky}
Out in the hills, among the olive groves and the cypresses, the villas stand quietly in their gardens, as they did when the young people of the Decameron walked among the roses and the fountains, singing. The fireflies come out among the olive trees. The bells of the little churches ring for the evening prayer.

[long-pause]

So we leave Florence for tonight, the old city of stone in its valley among the hills, with its great dome glowing softly under the stars, and the river flowing quietly beneath its bridges, as it has done for a thousand years.
