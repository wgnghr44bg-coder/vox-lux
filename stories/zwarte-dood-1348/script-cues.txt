In the spring of 1347, Florence was one of the richest cities in Europe, a city of towers, wool workshops and busy markets on the river Arno. A year later, a great sickness would change it forever. Here is how its people lived, and how they came through.

[long-pause]

In 1347, Florence was one of the largest and richest cities in Europe. Florence lay on the river Arno, in Tuscany, in the middle of Italy. Around the year 1340, it had perhaps a hundred thousand people. That made it larger than London and one of the great cities of Christian Europe, alongside Paris, Venice and Milan. The city was surrounded by hills covered with olive trees, vineyards and small farms. Its wealth came from trade, banking and, above all, the making of fine woollen cloth. Historians know a great deal about Florence in this period, because its people wrote so much down. Merchants kept careful account books, the city kept tax records, and chroniclers described daily life. Many of these documents still survive in the city's archives. Even so, Florence had problems as well as riches. Its great banks had recently collapsed, and the harvest of 1346 had failed. Yet in the spring of 1347, the city was busy and confident, with no idea of what the coming year would bring.

[long-pause]

{img:02-city-walls-towers-gates-tuscan-hills}

Rich families in medieval Florence built tall stone towers beside their houses. In the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, powerful families in Florence fought one another for control of the city. To protect themselves, they built tall, narrow towers of stone next to their homes. Some were more than fifty metres high. From the top, family members could watch their rivals and defend themselves in times of street fighting. By the fourteenth century, the city government had ordered many towers to be cut down to a lower height, to reduce the power of the old noble families. Even so, dozens of them still stood, and some survive today. Around the city ran a new circuit of stone walls, finished in the 1330s, with tall gates and towers. It enclosed a much larger area than the old walls, including fields and gardens. Historians use old drawings, maps and surviving buildings to reconstruct the medieval city. Visitors to Florence can still see several tower houses in the old streets near the river.

[long-pause]

For anyone who likes to drift through the medieval world at night, a subscription keeps the next journey close.

[long-pause]

{img:03-ponte-vecchio-shops-river-1347}

The Ponte Vecchio, the old bridge of Florence, was rebuilt in 1345 with shops along both sides. Four stone bridges crossed the Arno in Florence in the fourteenth century. The oldest was the Ponte Vecchio, the old bridge. In 1333, a terrible flood swept it away, together with other bridges. The city rebuilt it in stone, and the new bridge was finished in 1345. It still stands today. Shops lined both sides of the bridge, built over the water. In the fourteenth century, they were rented to many kinds of traders, including butchers and fishmongers, who could throw their waste straight into the river below. Historians know about the flood from the chronicler Giovanni Villani, who described it in detail. Even so, the bridge looked different then. The famous jewellers and goldsmiths arrived only in 1593, when the ruler of Florence ordered the butchers to leave because of the smell. Today, the Ponte Vecchio is one of the most famous bridges in the world, still lined with small shops above the slow green river.

[long-pause]

{img:04-wool-merchants-counting-house-florins}

Florence grew rich from wool, and its gold coin, the florin, was trusted across Europe and beyond. The most important industry in Florence was the making of woollen cloth. Merchants imported fine wool from England, Spain and North Africa. In the city, thousands of workers washed, combed, spun, wove, dyed and finished it. The finished cloth was sold across Europe and around the Mediterranean. The wool guild, called the Arte della Lana, was one of the most powerful organisations in the city. Florence was also famous for banking. In 1252, the city began to mint a gold coin called the florin. It kept the same weight and purity for a very long time, so merchants everywhere trusted it. Historians call it one of the most important coins of medieval Europe. Even so, the 1340s were a difficult time. Two of the biggest banks, run by the Bardi and Peruzzi families, collapsed after lending huge sums to the king of England. Yet the wool trade continued, and the city remained wealthy and busy.

[long-pause]

{img:05-florence-rooftops-bell-towers-morning-light}

After a year of hunger, the spring of 1347 brought new hope to Florence. The harvest of 1346 had been very poor across Tuscany. Heavy rains damaged crops, and grain became scarce and expensive. In the winter and spring of 1347, many poor families in Florence went hungry. According to the chronicler Giovanni Villani, the city government bought grain from far away and baked bread to give to the poor. He recorded that thousands of people received this help each day. Historians use his figures carefully, but they show how serious the shortage was. When spring came, the fields turned green again, and the new crops grew well. The summer harvest of 1347 was good, and bread returned to the markets. Even so, many people had been weakened by months of poor food. Historians have wondered whether this made them more vulnerable to illness the following year. Yet in the spring of 1347, the bells rang over the red roofs, and the people of Florence looked forward to better times.

[long-pause]

{img:06-young-writer-desk-manuscripts-window-florence}

The young writer Giovanni Boccaccio lived in Florence in the 1340s. Giovanni Boccaccio was born in 1313, probably in Florence or in the nearby town of Certaldo. His father was a merchant who worked for the Bardi bank. As a young man, Boccaccio was sent to Naples to learn about business and law. He found both boring. Instead, he spent his time reading poetry, studying the classics and writing his own stories and poems. Around 1341, he returned to Florence when his father's fortunes declined. He continued to write. Historians know about his life from his own works, his letters and city records. In the years that followed, he would write the Decameron, a collection of one hundred stories that became one of the most famous books of the Middle Ages. Even so, in 1347 he was still a young writer, not yet famous. He walked the streets of Florence, listened to people talk and gathered the stories that would later fill his books.

[long-pause]

{img:07-narrow-florence-street-shops-awnings-medieval}

In medieval Florence, shops opened directly onto the street, and their wooden shutters folded down to make counters. The streets of Florence were narrow, and houses rose three, four or five storeys high. On the ground floor of many houses were shops and workshops. Each one had a wide opening onto the street, closed at night with wooden shutters. In the morning, the lower shutter was folded down to form a counter, where goods were displayed and sold. Above, a cloth awning gave shade from the sun. Shoemakers, tailors, bakers, spice sellers and many other traders worked side by side. Historians know about these shops from paintings, inventories and the remains of medieval buildings in the city. Some streets were named after the trades practised there. Even so, the streets were not only for buying and selling. They were places to meet neighbours and exchange news. Children played in them, and animals wandered through. The noise of hammers, voices and cart wheels filled the air from early morning until the evening bells rang.

[long-pause]

{img:08-guild-hall-meeting-merchants-banners}

Florence was governed by citizens whose names were drawn by lot every two months. Florence was a republic, ruled by its own citizens rather than by a king or prince. The government was led by a small group of officials called the priors. They served for only two months at a time. Their names were drawn from leather bags, which contained the names of eligible citizens. Only members of the city's guilds could hold office, so the merchants and master craftsmen had the most power. The guilds were associations of people in the same trade, such as wool merchants, bankers, doctors and spice sellers. Each guild had its own hall, its own rules and its own banner. Historians know about this system from the many government records that survive in Florence. Even so, the system was not as fair as it seemed. Powerful families often controlled who was placed in the bags. Ordinary workers, such as wool combers, had no guild of their own and no voice in the government.

[long-pause]

{img:09-wool-dyers-vats-river-coloured-cloth}

Along the Arno, dyers coloured wool and cloth in great vats, and cloth was hung to dry in the sun. Dyeing was one of the most important steps in making cloth. Dyers worked near the river, because they needed large amounts of water. They used natural dyes from plants and insects. Woad gave blue, madder gave red, and weld gave yellow. The most expensive red, called grain or kermes, came from a tiny insect. By combining dyes, dyers created many colours, from deep scarlet to violet and green. After dyeing, the cloth was rinsed in the river and stretched on wooden frames to dry. Historians know about these methods from guild rules and from merchants' manuals, which listed dyes and prices. One such manual was written by Francesco Pegolotti, a Florentine merchant of this period. Even so, dyeing was hard and dirty work. The dyers' hands were stained, and waste flowed into the Arno. Yet the bright cloth of Florence was admired across Europe and fetched high prices in distant markets.

[long-pause]

{img:10-mercato-vecchio-stalls-crowd-morning}

The Old Market was the busiest square in Florence, full of food stalls, shops and people. At the heart of the city lay the Mercato Vecchio, the Old Market. It stood on the site of the ancient Roman forum. Every morning, farmers from the countryside brought vegetables, fruit, eggs, cheese and chickens to sell. Butchers, fishmongers and bakers had stalls and shops around the square. There were also taverns, a well and small churches. People came to shop, to meet friends and to hear the latest news. Historians know about the market from chronicles, paintings and old maps. In the nineteenth century, the market area was demolished to build a modern square, now called the Piazza della Repubblica. Even so, some paintings and photographs show what it once looked like. The market was noisy and crowded. Children ran between the stalls, dogs looked for scraps, and traders called out their prices. For most people in Florence, a daily visit to the market was part of ordinary life.

[long-pause]

{img:11-orsanmichele-grain-market-madonna-candles}

At Orsanmichele in Florence, people bought grain on the ground floor and prayed before an image of the Madonna. Orsanmichele was an unusual building. It began as an open grain market, with an arched loggia on the ground floor where grain was sold. Above it, there were storerooms for grain. Inside the loggia hung an image of the Virgin Mary, believed to work miracles. Many people came to pray before it, light candles and leave gifts. A religious confraternity, a group of lay people, looked after the image and used the gifts to help the poor. In 1347, the painter Bernardo Daddi painted a new image of the Madonna for the building, which can still be seen there. Historians know about Orsanmichele from records of the confraternity and from chronicles. Even so, its role changed over time. Later in the century, the grain market moved elsewhere, and the arches were walled in. The building became a church, decorated with statues paid for by the guilds. It stands today as one of the most beautiful buildings in Florence.

[long-pause]

{img:12-evening-piazza-strolling-families-lanterns}

In the evening, church bells rang across Florence, and people paused to say a short prayer. Medieval Florence followed the rhythm of church bells. Bells rang at dawn, at midday and in the evening, marking the hours of prayer and work. In the evening, the bell for the Ave Maria rang across the city. Many people stopped what they were doing and said a short prayer. Then the shops closed, and the streets became quieter. Families walked in the squares, talked with neighbours and enjoyed the cool air after a warm day. Young people met and flirted. Historians know about these customs from chronicles, sermons and stories, including those of Boccaccio. Even so, the city was not entirely peaceful at night. A curfew bell warned people to go home, and the city gates were closed. Watchmen walked the streets with lanterns. Lighting was scarce, so most people went to bed early. Yet these evening hours, between work and sleep, were among the most pleasant times of the day in a busy medieval city.

[long-pause]

{img:13-merchant-house-courtyard-well-loggia}

A wealthy merchant's house in Florence looked like a fortress from the street, but had a courtyard inside. Rich families in Florence lived in large stone houses called palazzi. From the street, these houses looked strong and closed, with thick walls, heavy doors and small windows on the ground floor. This reflected the dangers of earlier times, when families fought in the streets. Inside, however, there was often a quiet courtyard open to the sky. It might have a well, a covered walkway and stairs leading to the upper floors. The ground floor was used for storage, business and shops. The family lived on the floors above. Historians know about these houses from surviving buildings, inventories and paintings. One of the best preserved is the Palazzo Davanzati, built in the fourteenth century, which is now a museum. Even so, not all merchants were rich enough for such houses. Many lived in simpler homes. Yet the merchant palazzo shows how wealthy Florentines combined safety, comfort and display in their daily lives.

[long-pause]

{img:14-sala-painted-walls-fireplace-long-table}

Some wealthy Florentine families had their walls painted with gardens, trees and birds. The main room of a merchant's house was the sala, a large hall used for meals, family gatherings and receiving guests. It often had a big fireplace for winter. Rich families decorated their walls with paintings. Some walls were painted to look like hanging cloth or tapestries. Others showed gardens, with trees, flowers and birds, so that people inside felt as if they were outdoors. In the Palazzo Davanzati, one room is painted with a garden of trees and parrots, and another tells a romantic story in pictures. Historians and art experts have studied these paintings as evidence of how people decorated their homes. Even so, furniture was quite simple by modern standards. Rooms contained tables, benches, chests and a few chairs. Most decoration came from the walls, cloth hangings and painted chests. These colourful rooms show the taste and comfort that wealthy families enjoyed in the fourteenth century.

[long-pause]

{img:15-canopy-bed-painted-chest-candle-bedroom}

Medieval beds in Florence were very large, often shared, and surrounded by curtains. In wealthy Florentine homes, the bed was one of the most valuable pieces of furniture. Beds were often very wide, large enough for several people, and stood on a raised wooden platform. Around them hung curtains, which kept out draughts and gave some privacy. Mattresses were filled with wool or feathers, and bedding included linen sheets and warm covers. Next to the bed stood a large wooden chest, called a cassone, used to store clothes and linen. Painted chests were often given at weddings. Historians know about bedrooms from household inventories, which listed every object in a house, and from paintings that show sleeping rooms. Even so, poorer families had much simpler beds. Several children often shared one bed, or slept on straw mattresses. Yet for everyone, the bed was an important place. People were born, rested, recovered from illness and died there. It was the centre of family life in many homes.

[long-pause]

{img:16-mother-servants-kitchen-top-floor-hearth}

In many Florentine houses, the kitchen was on the top floor, to keep smoke and fire away from the rest of the house. Fire was a great danger in medieval cities. To reduce the risk, many Florentine houses had their kitchens at the very top, under the roof. Smoke could escape more easily there, and if a fire broke out, it was less likely to spread to the whole building. The kitchen had a large hearth for cooking, with iron pots, pans and spits for roasting meat. Water and wood had to be carried up many stairs, usually by servants. In wealthy households, the mother of the family managed the kitchen and the servants. Historians know about kitchens from inventories and surviving buildings, including the Palazzo Davanzati, where the top-floor kitchen can still be seen. Even so, poorer families often had no kitchen at all. They cooked on small stoves or bought food from bakers and cookshops. Yet in every home, the preparation of food took up a large part of each day.

[long-pause]

{img:17-family-meal-long-table-bread-wine-jugs}

Wealthy Florentines enjoyed food flavoured with expensive spices, which showed their status. Meals in Florence were based on bread, wine and olive oil. Wealthy families also ate meat, fish, cheese, vegetables and fruit. Spices such as pepper, cinnamon, ginger, cloves and saffron were highly valued. They came from distant lands and were very expensive. Using them in cooking showed a family's wealth and good taste. Florentines liked strong flavours, mixing sweet and sour in sauces. Historians know about medieval Italian cooking from recipe collections written in the fourteenth century and from household accounts. Even so, ordinary families ate much more simply. Their meals consisted mostly of bread, soup made with beans or vegetables, and a little wine. Meat was eaten mainly on special days. The Church also set many fast days, when meat was forbidden and fish or eggs were eaten instead. For rich and poor alike, the family meal was an important moment of the day, shared at a long table or around the fire.

[long-pause]

{img:18-abacus-school-boys-counting-beads-teacher}

Sons of Florentine merchants went to special abacus schools to learn arithmetic for business. Florence was a city of merchants, and business required good arithmetic. Many boys first learned to read and write in basic schools. Then, at about the age of ten or eleven, many went on to an abacus school. There, they learned to calculate prices, profits, exchange rates and interest. They used Hindu-Arabic numerals, which were becoming common in Italian business, rather than Roman numerals. Teachers used textbooks full of practical problems. According to the chronicler Giovanni Villani, around a thousand to twelve hundred boys attended abacus schools in Florence in the 1330s. Historians use his figures with some caution, but they show how important practical education was. Even so, girls were rarely sent to these schools. Some daughters of rich families learned to read at home. After school, many boys became apprentices in shops or banks. The skills they learned helped make Florence one of the leading business centres of Europe.

[long-pause]

{img:19-wool-worker-family-small-room-dawn}

Many families of wool workers in Florence lived in one or two small rooms. Thousands of people in Florence worked in the wool industry, washing, combing, spinning and weaving. Most were poor. They lived in crowded neighbourhoods, often on the edges of the city, in small rented rooms. A family of five or six might share two rooms, with a single large bed. Furniture was simple: a table, a bench, a chest and a few pots. Historians know about these homes from tax records, rental agreements and inventories made after a death. Even so, these records describe only part of daily life. Families made the best of their small spaces. They cooked on small stoves or bought food from cookshops. Neighbours helped one another. Children played in the narrow streets. Historians note that wool workers had no guild of their own and could not take part in government. Their wages were low, and work depended on the demand for cloth. Yet they formed a large and important part of the city.

[long-pause]

{img:20-wool-combers-workshop-long-room-morning}

Wool combers worked with heavy iron combs to straighten the fibres before spinning. Before wool could be spun into thread, it had to be cleaned and prepared. After washing, the fibres were combed or carded to straighten them and remove tangles. Combers used large combs with long iron teeth. The work was hard and repetitive. Many combers worked in workshops owned by wool merchants, who paid them by the piece or by the day. Historians know about the stages of cloth making from the records of the wool guild and from merchants' account books. One account book from the 1330s lists more than a dozen different steps, each done by different workers. Even so, the workers themselves left few records. Their names appear mainly in payment lists and court records. Combers and other wool workers later became famous in Florentine history. In 1378, they led a rebellion known as the Revolt of the Ciompi, demanding the right to have their own guilds and a voice in government.

[long-pause]

{img:21-woman-spinning-wool-window-distaff-spindle}

Women spun wool into thread at home, often working by the window. Spinning was usually done by women, often in their own homes. Wool merchants gave them prepared wool and paid them for the thread they produced. This system is called putting out. Women used a distaff to hold the wool and a spindle to twist it into thread. Spinning wheels were becoming more common, but many still worked by hand. They often sat near windows for the light, and in narrow streets, they could talk with neighbours across the way. Historians know about this work from the records of wool merchants, which list payments to spinners, sometimes by name. Even so, spinners were among the lowest paid workers in the industry. Their work was essential, because weaving required huge amounts of thread. Many women combined spinning with caring for children and running the household. Spinning filled the quiet hours of the day, and the steady turning of the spindle was a familiar sight in every working neighbourhood of Florence.

[long-pause]

{img:22-children-fetching-water-fountain-narrow-street}

Children in working families in Florence helped with household tasks from an early age. In poor and working families, children began to help at a young age. Small children fetched water from public wells and fountains. They carried bread from the baker and ran errands. Older children looked after younger brothers and sisters, helped with spinning or other work and learned a trade from their parents. Many boys became apprentices at around ten or twelve years old. Historians know about children's lives from records of apprenticeships, from court cases and from stories of the period. Even so, children also played. They played with balls, tops and simple toys, and ran through the streets and squares. Religious festivals and processions were exciting events for them. Historians note that childhood was short by modern standards. Many children died young from illness. Yet families loved and cared for their children. Some parents wrote about them in their family books, recording births, names, illnesses and small events of daily life.

[long-pause]

{img:23-family-supper-firelight-small-room-evening}

In the evening, working families in Florence gathered to eat simple meals together. When the working day ended, families came home to their small rooms. Supper was usually simple: bread, a soup of beans or vegetables, perhaps a little cheese and some cheap wine mixed with water. Light came from a small oil lamp or the fire. Parents and children talked about the day, and neighbours sometimes visited. On Sundays and feast days, there might be a little meat. Historians know about the diet of ordinary Florentines from records of food prices, wages and charity, and from stories by writers such as Boccaccio and Franco Sacchetti. Even so, life was fragile. A bad harvest, illness or loss of work could bring hunger. Many families depended on charity at times. Confraternities and hospitals gave bread and help to the poor. Yet evenings together, however simple, gave comfort. They were times for rest, for family and for the small pleasures that made hard lives easier to bear.

[long-pause]

{img:24-cathedral-under-construction-scaffolding-1347}

In 1347, the great cathedral of Florence was still being built, and it would take more than a century to finish. In 1296, the people of Florence began building a new cathedral, dedicated to Santa Maria del Fiore, Saint Mary of the Flower. They wanted it to be one of the largest and most beautiful churches in Christendom. Work went on for many years. By 1347, the walls were rising, and the bell tower designed by the painter Giotto was under construction. Cranes, scaffolding and workshops stood around the building site. The guild of wool merchants was responsible for the work and paid for much of it. Historians know about the building from records of the cathedral works, which still survive in great detail. Even so, progress was slow, and the plans changed many times. The famous dome was completed only in 1436, designed by Filippo Brunelleschi. For nearly a century and a half, building the cathedral was part of daily life in Florence, a project that united generations of citizens in a shared goal.

[long-pause]

{img:25-florence-saint-john-prayer-candles}

The people of Florence believed their patron saint, John the Baptist, protected their city. Saint John the Baptist was the patron saint of Florence. His image appeared on the city's gold florin, and his feast on the twenty-fourth of June was the greatest celebration of the year. There were processions, horse races and games, and the whole city was decorated. The Baptistery of San Giovanni, an old octagonal church near the cathedral, was where nearly every child of Florence was baptised. In 1336, the sculptor Andrea Pisano completed a set of bronze doors for it, showing scenes from the life of the saint. Historians know about these celebrations from chronicles and city records. Even so, faith in Florence was not only about grand ceremonies. People prayed to many saints in their daily lives, asking for help with illness, travel and work. They lit candles in churches and gave money to religious causes. In difficult times, they turned especially to their patron saint and to the Virgin Mary for protection.

[long-pause]

{img:26-florence-procession-candles-relics}

In difficult times, the people of Florence held long religious processions through the streets. Processions were an important part of religious life in medieval Florence. Clergy, confraternities, guilds and ordinary citizens walked through the streets together, carrying candles, banners and holy images. They sang hymns and prayed aloud. Processions were held on great feast days, but also in times of danger, such as war, famine or illness. People believed that public prayer could win the help of God and the saints for the whole city. Historians know about processions from chronicles and from the records of confraternities, which describe the order of participants and the routes they took. In 1348, when the great sickness arrived, many processions were held in Florence. Even so, Boccaccio later noted that they did not stop the illness. Historians suggest that large gatherings may even have helped it spread. Yet at the time, processions gave people a sense of shared hope and of doing something together in the face of fear.

[long-pause]

{img:27-florence-herbs-rosemary-vinegar}

Many people in fourteenth century Florence carried fragrant herbs, believing they protected against bad air. Medieval doctors believed that many diseases were caused by bad air, which they called miasma. They thought that foul smells from rubbish, stagnant water or rotting matter could make people ill. To protect themselves, people carried pleasant smelling things, such as flowers, herbs and spices. Rosemary, juniper, rue and sage were common. Some soaked a cloth in vinegar and held it to their noses. Wealthy people carried small containers of perfume. In their homes, they burned aromatic woods and herbs to purify the air. Historians know about these practices from medical books and from Boccaccio, who described them in the opening of the Decameron. Even so, these methods did not protect against the plague. The real cause, a bacterium carried by fleas and spread between people, was discovered only in 1894. Yet the idea of cleaning the air and removing filth did lead to some useful measures, such as cleaning streets and removing rubbish.

[long-pause]

{img:28-friar-preaching-square-crowd-church}

Franciscan and Dominican friars preached to large crowds in the squares of Florence. In the thirteenth century, two new religious orders spread across Europe: the Franciscans and the Dominicans. Their members, called friars, lived among ordinary people in towns rather than in remote monasteries. In Florence, the Franciscans built the great church of Santa Croce, and the Dominicans built Santa Maria Novella. Both churches had large squares in front of them, where friars preached to crowds. Their sermons were in Italian, not Latin, so everyone could understand. They spoke about faith, morality and daily life, often using stories and examples. Historians know about their preaching from collections of sermons and from chronicles. Even so, the friars were not only preachers. They cared for the sick and the poor, and they ran schools. Some became famous scholars and teachers. Franciscans wore simple brown robes, and Dominicans wore white with black cloaks. Both orders played an important part in the life of the city, especially in times of trouble.

[long-pause]

{img:29-boccaccio-writing-desk-quiet-room}

Giovanni Boccaccio was in Florence in 1348, and he later wrote one of the most famous descriptions of that year. Most historians believe that Boccaccio was living in Florence when the great sickness arrived in 1348. A few years later, he began writing the Decameron, a book of one hundred stories. It opens with a long description of the plague in Florence: how it arrived, how people reacted and how the city changed. Historians consider this introduction one of the most important eyewitness accounts of the Black Death in Europe. Boccaccio described what he saw with care and honesty. He also explained that he would tell this sad part briefly, so that readers could move on to the happier stories that followed. Even so, historians read his account carefully. He was a storyteller, and some details may be shaped for effect. Other sources, such as city records and chronicles, help confirm what he wrote. Yet his words remain the clearest picture of how ordinary people in Florence experienced that year.

[long-pause]

{img:30-writer-walking-arno-bridge-sunset}

In the early 1340s, Boccaccio was called back from Naples to Florence by his father. Boccaccio spent his youth in Naples, a lively city ruled by a royal court. There, he enjoyed music, poetry and the company of educated friends. Around 1341, his father's business suffered from the collapse of the great Florentine banks, and Boccaccio had to return to Florence. In his writings, he expressed sadness at leaving Naples, which he remembered as a place of light and happiness. He found life in Florence more serious and less joyful. Historians know about these feelings from his poems and letters. Even so, Florence gave him much material for his stories. He walked its streets and bridges, met all kinds of people and observed their habits. He also studied the works of Dante, the great poet of Florence, whom he admired deeply. Over time, he came to love his city. His later writings show a deep knowledge of its people, places and customs, from rich merchants to humble craftsmen.

[long-pause]

{img:31-tavern-storyteller-listeners-candle-laughing}

Boccaccio collected stories from many sources, including traditional tales told across Europe and the East. The stories in the Decameron came from many places. Some were old folk tales, told and retold for generations. Others came from French, Latin and Eastern collections. Some were based on real events and people in Florence and Tuscany. Boccaccio reworked them all in his own lively Italian prose. Historians and literary scholars have traced the sources of many of his stories. They show that Boccaccio read widely and listened carefully to the world around him. Taverns, markets and family gatherings were places where stories were exchanged. Storytelling was a popular form of entertainment for all classes. Even so, Boccaccio added much of his own. His characters are vivid and realistic, and his stories are full of humour, cleverness and human feeling. Merchants, priests, craftsmen, servants and noblewomen all appear in his pages. The Decameron gives a rich picture of everyday life and attitudes in fourteenth century Italy.

[long-pause]

{img:32-red-brick-town-certaldo-quiet-street-evening}

Boccaccio's family came from Certaldo, a small hilltop town built of red brick. Certaldo is a small town in the Elsa valley, about thirty kilometres southwest of Florence. Its old upper part, called Certaldo Alto, sits on a hill and is built almost entirely of red brick. Boccaccio's family came from there, and he spent his final years in the town. He died there in 1375. His house, rebuilt after damage in the Second World War, is now a museum and library dedicated to him. Historians note that Certaldo, like many Tuscan towns, depended on agriculture and trade with Florence. Its streets are quiet, with views over vineyards and olive groves. Even so, the town was part of the wider world of Florence. Families moved between town and city, and news travelled quickly along the roads. Today, Certaldo Alto can be reached by a small funicular railway. Visitors walk its brick streets, see the palace of its former governors and visit the tomb of Boccaccio in the church of Saints Jacopo and Filippo.

[long-pause]

{img:33-old-chronicler-writing-book-study-lamp}

Giovanni Villani, a merchant of Florence, wrote a great history of his city in the fourteenth century. Giovanni Villani was born around 1276 into a family of Florentine merchants. He worked for some of the great trading and banking companies of the city and travelled widely. According to his own account, he decided to write a history of Florence after visiting Rome in 1300, during a great religious jubilee. Seeing the ruins of ancient Rome, he reflected that Florence, the daughter of Rome, was rising while Rome was declining. His work, called the Nuova Cronica, describes the history of Florence from its legendary origins to his own time. Historians value it as one of the most important sources for medieval Florence. Even so, Villani was not neutral. He wrote as a proud Florentine and a member of the merchant class, and his early chapters contain many legends. Yet for his own lifetime, he provides detailed and often reliable information. He also held public offices in the city and knew its affairs well.

[long-pause]

{img:34-chronicle-manuscript-pages-illuminated-letters}

Villani loved numbers and recorded many statistics about Florence in the 1330s. One of the most famous parts of Villani's chronicle describes Florence in the late 1330s, using numbers. He estimated the population, the number of men able to bear arms and the amount of grain and wine consumed. He counted the bakers, the wool workshops, the churches and hospitals, the schools and the children who attended them. He recorded the city's income and expenses. Historians consider this section one of the earliest statistical descriptions of a European city. They use his figures with care, since some are estimates or may be exaggerated. Even so, many details match other records. His description shows a city that was large, rich and well organised. Villani wanted readers to understand the greatness of Florence. His love of numbers reflected the culture of a merchant city, where people kept careful accounts. Today, his chronicle is still read and studied, and his figures appear in many books about medieval Florence.

[long-pause]

{img:35-quiet-garden-twilight-fig-tree-roses}

Villani continued writing his chronicle until 1348, when he died during the epidemic. Giovanni Villani wrote his chronicle over many years, adding new chapters as events happened. In the 1340s, he lived through the collapse of the great banks, which ruined many merchants. He himself was briefly imprisoned for debt. Even so, he kept writing. When the great sickness reached Florence in 1348, he described its arrival. He wrote that the epidemic lasted until, and then left a blank space, intending to add the date later. He died during the epidemic and never filled it in. Historians often mention this unfinished sentence as a reminder of how suddenly the plague struck. His brother Matteo continued the chronicle, and later Matteo's son Filippo continued it again. Historians value the work of the whole family, which covers much of the fourteenth century. Yet Giovanni's own writing remains the most famous. His chronicle preserves the voice of a merchant who loved his city and recorded its life with great care.

[long-pause]

{img:36-tuscan-hills-olive-groves-cypress-farmhouse}

Many farmers around Florence worked under a system called mezzadria, sharing their harvest with the landowner. The countryside around Florence was covered with small farms, vineyards and olive groves. Many farms were owned by families who lived in the city. They let the land to farming families under a system called mezzadria, meaning halving. The landowner provided the land, the house and part of the costs. The farming family did the work. At harvest, the produce was divided, usually half for each. Historians know about this system from thousands of contracts and account books that survive in Tuscan archives. It became especially common in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries and lasted until the twentieth century. Even so, the arrangement was not always equal. Farming families often fell into debt to their landlords. Yet the system linked city and countryside closely. City families received wine, oil, grain and fruit from their farms, and farming families had a secure place to live and work. The tidy Tuscan landscape of fields and farmhouses partly grew out of this system.

[long-pause]

{img:37-wheat-harvest-sickles-tuscan-summer-field}

The grape harvest in autumn was one of the happiest times of the farming year in Tuscany. The farming year in Tuscany followed the seasons. In early summer, wheat and barley were harvested with sickles and threshed on open floors. In autumn came the grape harvest, called the vendemmia. Whole families, including children and old people, went out to the vineyards to cut the grapes and carry them in baskets. The grapes were pressed to make wine, which was stored in barrels. Later in the autumn, olives were picked and pressed for oil. Historians know about these tasks from farm accounts, calendars and illustrations in manuscripts. Many medieval calendars showed the labours of the months, with harvesting and treading grapes in September and October. Even so, farm work was hard and depended on the weather. A storm or drought could ruin a harvest. Yet the grape harvest was also a time of celebration. Neighbours helped one another, and there was food, music and the new wine to enjoy when the work was done.

[long-pause]

{img:38-villa-garden-fountain-view-florence-hills}

Wealthy Florentines owned country houses in the hills, where they escaped the heat of the city in summer. Many rich families in Florence owned villas in the countryside around the city. These houses were surrounded by gardens, orchards and farms. In summer, when the city was hot, crowded and smelly, families moved to their villas to enjoy fresh air, shade and quiet. They supervised their farms, hunted, walked in the gardens and entertained guests. Historians know about villas from surviving buildings, letters and account books. In the fifteenth century, the Medici family built famous villas, but the custom was already well established in the fourteenth century. Even so, villas were not only for pleasure. They were also working farms that supplied the family with food and income. Boccaccio set the frame of the Decameron in such villas, where young people stayed away from the city during the epidemic. Today, many villas still stand on the hills around Florence, among cypress trees and olive groves, with views across the valley of the Arno.

[long-pause]

{img:39-fiesole-hill-view-florence-valley-evening}

The hill town of Fiesole, above Florence, is older than the city itself. Fiesole sits on a hill about eight kilometres northeast of Florence. It was founded by the Etruscans, an ancient people of central Italy, long before the Romans. The Romans later conquered it and built a theatre, baths and temples there, whose ruins can still be seen. Florence was founded later, as a Roman colony in the valley below, probably around 59 before Christ. In the Middle Ages, Florence grew larger and stronger, and in 1125 it conquered Fiesole. Historians know about Fiesole's history from archaeology and medieval chronicles. Villani described the legendary rivalry between the two towns. Even so, Fiesole remained a pleasant place, with monasteries, villas and wonderful views. Wealthy Florentines built country houses on its slopes. From the hill, the whole city of Florence can be seen spread out in the valley, with the cathedral dome rising above the red roofs. In the evening, the view of the city below is especially peaceful.

[long-pause]

{img:40-summer-1347-wheat-harvest-golden-hills}

After the hunger of 1346, the harvest of 1347 was good, and bread became plentiful again. The years 1346 and 1347 began badly in Tuscany. After heavy rains, the harvest of 1346 failed, and food prices rose sharply. Many poor families went hungry through the winter. The city government of Florence bought grain from distant places and distributed bread to the poor. According to Villani, the number of people receiving bread each day was very large. In the summer of 1347, however, the weather was better, and the harvest was good. Prices fell, and bread became plentiful in the markets once more. Historians know about these changes from Villani's chronicle and from records of grain prices. Even so, the effects of the hungry year lasted. Many people had become weaker, and some historians suggest that the famine may have made the population more vulnerable to disease in the following year. Yet in the summer of 1347, the people of Florence were relieved and hopeful as the golden fields were harvested.

[long-pause]

{img:41-arno-riverbank-summer-evening-swimmers-poplars}

On summer evenings in Florence, people gathered along the banks of the river Arno. The Arno was central to life in Florence. It provided water for the wool industry, power for mills and a route for transport. In summer, its banks were busy with people. Washerwomen cleaned clothes on the stones near the water. Fishermen cast their nets. Boys swam in the shallow parts of the river on hot evenings. Historians know about these scenes from stories, city regulations and paintings. Some city laws tried to control swimming and washing in certain places, which shows that these activities were common. Even so, the river could also be dangerous. Floods sometimes swept through the city, as in 1333, when bridges were destroyed and many people drowned. In dry summers, the river ran low and its waters became dirty from the dyeing and tanning workshops. Yet on calm evenings, the riverbanks were among the most pleasant places in the city, cool and lively, with the sun setting behind the hills.

[long-pause]

{img:42-genoese-galleys-harbour-sails-1347}

Italian merchant ships sailed as far as the Black Sea to trade for silk, spices and other goods from the East. In the fourteenth century, merchants from Genoa and Venice controlled much of the trade between Europe and the East. Their ships sailed through the Mediterranean to Constantinople and into the Black Sea. There, at trading posts such as Caffa in the Crimea and Tana near the mouth of the river Don, they bought silk, spices, furs, grain and slaves brought by caravans across Asia. Historians know about this trade from merchants' manuals, contracts and letters. A Florentine merchant named Francesco Pegolotti wrote a famous guide for traders, describing routes to China. Even so, these long-distance links had a hidden danger. The same trade routes that carried goods also carried disease. Historians believe that the plague spread along these routes from Central Asia to the Black Sea, and from there by ship to the Mediterranean. The busy harbours of Italy, so important for the wealth of its cities, became gateways for the sickness.

[long-pause]

{img:43-caravan-steppe-camels-dusk-distant}

People in the fourteenth century did not know what caused the plague. Today, scientists know that the Black Death was caused by a bacterium called Yersinia pestis. It was carried mainly by fleas living on rats and other animals, and in some forms it could spread directly between people. This was discovered only in 1894, by the scientist Alexandre Yersin. In the fourteenth century, people had very different explanations. Many doctors believed that disease came from bad air, poisoned by rotting matter or by unusual movements of the planets. Others saw the sickness as a punishment from God for human sins. Historians know about these ideas from medical treatises, chronicles and sermons of the time. In recent years, scientists have studied the DNA of plague bacteria from medieval graves. Their research suggests that the outbreak began in Central Asia in the 1330s or 1340s. Even so, many details remain uncertain. Yet these discoveries have greatly improved understanding of how the disease travelled and why it spread so fast.

[long-pause]

{img:44-ships-arriving-sicily-harbour-autumn}

In October 1347, merchant ships from the East brought the plague to Messina in Sicily. According to the chronicler Michele da Piazza, a Franciscan friar, twelve Genoese galleys arrived in the harbour of Messina, in Sicily, in October 1347. They had come from the Black Sea. Soon after their arrival, people in the city began to fall ill. The sickness spread quickly. Historians consider this one of the first recorded arrivals of the Black Death in Europe. From Sicily, ships carried the disease to other ports, including Genoa, Pisa, Venice and Marseille. Within a few months, it had reached many parts of Italy and France. Historians know about these events from chronicles and letters written at the time. Even so, details such as the exact number of ships may not be accurate. Some accounts were written years later. Yet the general picture is clear. Trade routes that had brought wealth to Italian cities now brought a terrible illness, and news of it travelled along the same roads and sea lanes.

[long-pause]

{img:45-venice-harbour-officials-ships-waiting-lagoon}

In 1348, Venice appointed special officials to protect the health of the city. When news of the sickness spread, some cities tried to protect themselves. In March 1348, the government of Venice appointed three officials to take charge of public health. They organised burials, controlled the arrival of ships and tried to keep the city clean. Historians see this as one of the earliest examples of a public health authority in Europe. Later, other cities created similar offices. In 1377, the city of Ragusa, now Dubrovnik, ordered ships and travellers from infected places to wait thirty days before entering. This was later extended to forty days, called quaranta giorni in Italian, from which the word quarantine comes. Historians know about these measures from city laws and records. Even so, they could not stop the epidemic of 1348, which spread too quickly. Yet the experience led to lasting changes. Over the following centuries, Italian cities developed health boards, isolation hospitals and rules that later influenced public health across Europe.

[long-pause]

{img:46-florence-winter-street-worried-merchants}

In the winter of 1347 and 1348, news of the sickness reached Florence through merchants' letters. Florence was a city of merchants, and merchants depended on news. Letters travelled constantly between Florence and its business partners in Pisa, Genoa, Venice, Avignon and beyond. In the winter of 1347, these letters began to mention a terrible sickness spreading in Sicily and the ports. Historians know about this from surviving merchant letters and from chronicles. Some letters described the illness and the fear it caused. Even so, people in Florence did not know how close the danger was or how to prepare. City officials discussed measures to keep the streets clean and to control travellers. Some families made plans to leave if the sickness came. Others hoped it would not reach them. Through the winter, life in Florence continued much as before. Shops opened, markets were busy and the bells rang. Yet many people followed the news anxiously, as reports came closer with each passing month.

[long-pause]

{img:47-avignon-papal-palace-rhone-bridge}

In the fourteenth century, the popes lived in Avignon, in southern France, instead of Rome. From 1309 to 1377, the popes lived in Avignon, a city on the river Rhône in Provence. This period is sometimes called the Avignon Papacy. The popes moved there partly because of violence and instability in Rome and partly because of the influence of the kings of France. In Avignon, they built an enormous Gothic palace, which looked like a fortress. Pope Benedict the Twelfth began it in the 1330s, and Pope Clement the Sixth enlarged it in the 1340s. Historians know about the papal court from its detailed accounts and records. Avignon became a busy international city, full of clergy, lawyers, merchants and artists. Many Italians lived there, including bankers from Florence. Even so, critics called the period a Babylonian captivity, because the popes were away from Rome. In 1348, the plague reached Avignon and caused great loss of life. Today, the Palace of the Popes still stands and is visited by many people.

[long-pause]

{img:48-poet-scholar-study-books-avignon-window}

The poet Francesco Petrarca, known as Petrarch, wrote hundreds of poems about a woman named Laura. Francesco Petrarca was born in Arezzo, in Tuscany, in 1304, to a Florentine family in exile. He grew up near Avignon, where his father worked at the papal court. According to Petrarch's own account, on the sixth of April 1327, he saw a young woman named Laura in a church in Avignon and fell in love with her. Her identity is uncertain, and she may have been married to someone else. For the rest of his life, he wrote poems about her, in Italian, which were collected in a book called the Canzoniere. Historians and literary scholars consider these poems among the most influential in European literature. Even so, much about Laura remains unknown. Some even wondered if she was only a poetic idea. Petrarch was also a great scholar who searched for ancient Latin texts and helped begin the movement later called humanism. He became friends with Boccaccio, and the two men exchanged many letters.

[long-pause]

{img:49-vaucluse-spring-cliff-river-green-valley}

Petrarch preferred to live in Vaucluse, a quiet valley where a river flows from a spring at the foot of a cliff. Petrarch disliked the noise and crowds of Avignon. He called it a place of corruption and wrote harsh words about the papal court. In 1337, he bought a small house in Vaucluse, a peaceful valley east of Avignon. There, the river Sorgue rises from a deep spring at the foot of a tall cliff. Petrarch lived there for long periods, reading, writing and walking in the countryside. He described the valley in many letters and poems. Historians know about his life there from his own writings, which are unusually personal. Even so, he also travelled widely and spent time in Italy. Vaucluse gave him the quiet he needed for study and reflection. Today, the village is called Fontaine-de-Vaucluse, and a small museum stands near the site of his house. The spring remains one of the largest in France, its clear water flowing out of the rock into a green and peaceful valley.

[long-pause]

{img:50-pope-seated-between-two-great-fires-calm}

During the plague, the doctor of Pope Clement the Sixth advised him to sit between two large fires. Pope Clement the Sixth lived in Avignon when the plague arrived in 1348. His personal doctor was Guy de Chauliac, one of the most respected surgeons of the time. Following the medical ideas of the period, Guy believed that disease spread through corrupted air. He advised the Pope to stay in his chambers and sit between two large fires, which were thought to purify the air. Historians note that this advice may have helped by chance, since fleas avoid heat. Clement survived the epidemic. He also took practical steps. He paid for the dead to be buried, bought a field for a new cemetery and protected the Jewish community of Avignon, who were being falsely blamed for the plague in some places. Historians know about these events from Guy de Chauliac's own medical writings and from papal records. Even so, the Pope could not stop the sickness, which killed a large part of the population of Avignon.

[long-pause]

{img:51-old-manuscript-virgil-handwritten-note}

Petrarch wrote a note about Laura's death in his copy of Virgil, which still survives in Milan. Petrarch owned a beautiful manuscript of the works of the Roman poet Virgil. It was decorated with a painting by the artist Simone Martini. Petrarch treasured this book and wrote notes in its margins. On one page, he recorded personal events. There he wrote that Laura had died on the sixth of April 1348, during the epidemic in Avignon, exactly twenty-one years after he first saw her. Historians and scholars consider this note very important, because it is one of the few pieces of evidence about Laura. Petrarch was in Italy at the time and learned of her death by letter. Even so, his feelings for her continued in his poetry for many years. The manuscript is now kept in the Biblioteca Ambrosiana, a famous library in Milan. Scholars still study it today. It is a personal link to a poet who lived through the year 1348 and recorded his grief in his favourite book.

[long-pause]

{img:52-florence-spring-1348-almond-blossom-hills}

The spring of 1348 began in Florence like any other, with blossom on the hills and swallows returning. In early 1348, the countryside around Florence turned green again. Almond and fruit trees blossomed on the hills, farmers prepared their fields and swallows returned to nest under the roofs. In the city, shops and workshops were busy, and the cathedral builders continued their work. Historians know from chronicles and letters that people in Florence had heard about the sickness in the ports. Yet daily life went on. Merchants sent their letters, children went to school and families prepared for Easter. Even so, there was unease. City officials were discussing how to protect Florence, and some wealthy families were making plans to leave if necessary. Historians note how ordinary the first weeks of spring must have seemed. No one could know what the coming months would bring. In the gentle weather of early spring, with the bells ringing over the valley, Florence looked much as it had for generations.

[long-pause]

{img:53-penitent-procession-candles-hymns-street}

In the spring of 1348, churches in Florence were full, and processions moved through the streets. As fear of the sickness grew, many people in Florence turned to prayer. Churches were crowded, and confraternities organised processions. Groups of citizens walked through the streets carrying candles, crosses and holy images, singing hymns and asking for protection. Priests and friars preached about repentance. Historians know about these events from chronicles and from Boccaccio, who described the many prayers and processions in the opening of the Decameron. He noted that they did not stop the sickness. Even so, for the people of the time, these acts of faith were natural and meaningful. They believed that God controlled all things and that sincere prayer could bring mercy. Processions also brought the community together, giving people strength and comfort. Historians suggest that the crowds may have helped the disease spread, but no one understood this then. In a frightening time, these gatherings were a way of facing the danger together.

[long-pause]

{img:54-apothecary-shop-herbs-jars-rosemary-bundles}

Doctors in Florence advised people to burn aromatic herbs, eat carefully and avoid fear. Medical advice in 1348 was based on ancient theories, especially those of the Greek doctor Galen. Doctors believed that health depended on a balance of fluids in the body, called humours, and that disease could be caused by corrupted air. To protect themselves, people were advised to burn fragrant woods and herbs such as rosemary, juniper and bay in their homes. They were told to eat light food, avoid hard exercise and keep calm. Some doctors believed that fear and sadness made people more likely to fall ill, so they recommended pleasant company, music and cheerful thoughts. Apothecaries sold herbs, spices and medicines. Historians know about this advice from plague treatises, short medical guides that were written in large numbers after 1348. Even so, none of these remedies could cure the plague. Yet some advice, such as avoiding crowds and keeping homes clean, may have helped a little, even though the reasons were not understood.

[long-pause]

{img:55-lanterns-shrine-street-corner-dusk}

The plague reached Florence in the spring of 1348, probably in March or April. Historians are not certain of the exact date when the plague reached Florence. Most sources suggest that it arrived in the early spring of 1348 and spread quickly in April and May. Boccaccio wrote that it began in the spring and grew worse through the summer. The city government had already taken some measures, such as cleaning the streets and controlling travellers from infected places. Even so, the illness spread through the crowded neighbourhoods. Historians know about the epidemic from chronicles, city records, letters and the registers of confraternities and hospitals. These sources show that the epidemic in Florence lasted until the autumn. Historians estimate that perhaps half or more of the population died during 1348, though exact numbers are impossible to know. These events are told here briefly and gently, from a distance, as Boccaccio himself chose to do. His aim, and the aim here, is to remember the people of the time with respect.

[long-pause]

{img:56-florence-quiet-street-summer-1348-empty}

In the summer of 1348, the streets of Florence became unusually quiet. As the epidemic continued, daily life in Florence changed. Many shops and workshops closed. Some wealthy families left for their country houses. Others stayed at home and avoided going out. Markets were quieter, and fewer people walked in the squares. Boccaccio described how the normal habits of the city were disrupted, and how people reacted in different ways. Some prayed, some withdrew from the world and some tried to enjoy life while they could. Historians know about these changes from his account and from other records, such as tax registers that show businesses closing. Even so, the city did not stop completely. City officials continued to govern, doctors and priests cared for the sick, and confraternities organised help. Bakers still baked bread, and food was brought in from the countryside. Historians note that many people showed great courage and kindness during these months. The quiet streets of that summer remained in the memory of the city for generations.

[long-pause]

{img:57-brotherhood-black-robes-carrying-stretcher-street}

The Misericordia, a confraternity founded in Florence in 1244, cared for the sick and buried the dead. The Confraternity of the Misericordia was founded in Florence in 1244. Its members were ordinary citizens who volunteered to help the sick and the poor. They carried sick people to hospitals and helped bury the dead with dignity. To show that they acted out of charity and not for praise, they wore black robes with hoods that covered their faces. In 1348, the Misericordia and other confraternities played an important role in caring for people during the epidemic. Historians know about their work from the confraternity's own records and from city documents. Many people left gifts to the Misericordia in their wills, which helped it continue its work. Even so, the volunteers faced great risks, and many of them died. Their service showed the strength of community life in Florence. The Misericordia still exists today, more than seven hundred and fifty years later, and its members still help people in need throughout the city.

[long-pause]

{img:58-hospital-long-hall-beds-nuns-candles}

The hospital of Santa Maria Nuova was founded in 1288 by Folco Portinari, the father of Dante's beloved Beatrice. Florence had many hospitals in the fourteenth century. Some cared for pilgrims and travellers, others for the poor, orphans or the sick. The largest was Santa Maria Nuova, founded in 1288 by Folco Portinari, a wealthy citizen. His daughter Beatrice was the woman whom the poet Dante loved and made famous in his poetry. The hospital had long halls with rows of beds, cared for by religious brothers and sisters. Doctors visited the patients, and medicines were prepared in its own pharmacy. Historians know about Santa Maria Nuova from its extensive records, which survive in the archives of Florence. In 1348, the hospital was overwhelmed with patients, but its staff continued their work. Even so, medieval hospitals could do little against the plague. Yet they provided care, food and comfort. Santa Maria Nuova continued to grow over the centuries and is still a working hospital in the centre of Florence today.

[long-pause]

{img:59-unfinished-chronicle-page-quill-resting}

The chronicler Giovanni Villani left a blank space in his book for the date when the epidemic would end. Giovanni Villani was writing his chronicle when the plague reached Florence. In one of his last passages, he described the epidemic and wrote that it would last until, followed by a blank space. He planned to add the date when the sickness ended. He died during the epidemic in 1348, and the space was never filled. Historians often mention this unfinished sentence as a quiet and moving reminder of the year. It shows how suddenly lives could be interrupted. His brother Matteo Villani continued the chronicle and described the plague in more detail. Matteo also wrote about how society changed afterwards. Historians value both brothers' work highly. Even so, they note that chronicles reflect the views of their authors, who were wealthy merchants. Other sources, such as tax records and wills, show the experiences of ordinary people. Together, these documents allow historians to understand how Florence lived through 1348 and the years that followed.

[long-pause]

{img:60-empty-piazza-noon-heat-pigeons}

During the summer of 1348, work stopped on many buildings and in many workshops in Florence. As the epidemic reached its height in the summer of 1348, much of the work in Florence came to a halt. Records of the cathedral building show that construction slowed or stopped for a time. Many wool workshops closed, because workers were ill or had left. The looms that normally clattered from morning until evening fell silent. Historians know about these interruptions from account books, which show gaps in payments and work. Even so, some essential activities continued. Food was brought into the city, and officials tried to maintain order. Historians note that, in the following years, labour became scarce because so many workers had died. This led to higher wages and changes in how work was organised. For those who lived through the summer of 1348, however, the silent workshops and empty squares were a strange and sad sight in a city that had always been so busy.

[long-pause]

{img:61-courtyard-lute-player-quiet-family-evening}

Some families in Florence shut themselves away and lived quietly, with simple food and music. Boccaccio described several ways in which people tried to protect themselves during the epidemic. Some families decided to live apart from others. They shut themselves in their houses, ate moderately, drank good wine and avoided news of the sickness. They spent their time with music and pleasant conversation. Others believed in enjoying life as much as possible. Many tried a middle way, going out but carrying flowers and herbs to protect against bad air. Historians use Boccaccio's description to understand the range of responses. Medical advice of the time also recommended a calm and cheerful mind. Even so, no method guaranteed safety. Historians note that isolation may sometimes have helped, by reducing contact with infected people or fleas. Yet families who stayed together also cared for each other. The image of a family gathered quietly in a courtyard, listening to music in the evening, reflects one way people found comfort and courage in a frightening time.

[long-pause]

{img:62-neighbour-leaving-bread-basket-doorstep}

Many people in Florence helped their neighbours during the epidemic, bringing food and caring for the sick. Boccaccio's account of the epidemic is often remembered for its descriptions of fear and abandonment. Yet other sources show that many people behaved with great kindness. Neighbours brought food to families who were ill. Relatives took in orphaned children. Priests, doctors, nurses and volunteers continued their work, often at great risk. Historians have found evidence of this help in wills, where people left money or property to those who had cared for them, and in the records of confraternities and hospitals. Some wills mention neighbours and friends by name, thanking them for their help. Even so, the epidemic placed enormous strain on families and communities. Historians emphasise that both fear and courage were part of the experience. In difficult times, ordinary people found ways to support one another. Small acts, such as leaving bread on a doorstep or watching over a neighbour's children, helped many families survive the hardest months of 1348.

[long-pause]

{img:63-writer-watching-from-window-empty-square}

Boccaccio decided to write about the epidemic, so that future generations would remember it. Boccaccio lived through the epidemic and saw how it changed his city. A few years later, around 1349 to 1353, he wrote the Decameron. In its introduction, he described the plague in Florence, explaining what he had seen and heard. He said that he wanted to record these events so that people would remember them. He also explained that the sad description would be followed by pleasant stories, like a traveller climbing a steep hill to reach a beautiful plain. Historians consider his account one of the most important descriptions of the Black Death in Europe. Literary scholars admire the way he combined sorrow and hope in one book. Even so, Boccaccio was not writing a scientific report. He was a storyteller, and his account was shaped to move readers. Yet it remains a powerful testimony. Through his words, the experience of ordinary people in Florence in 1348 has been remembered for nearly seven hundred years.

[long-pause]

{img:64-italian-hill-village-quiet-dusk-church}

By the autumn of 1348, the epidemic in Florence began to fade. The epidemic in Florence lasted through the spring and summer of 1348. With the cooler weather of autumn, it gradually declined. Historians believe that the plague often weakened in colder months, partly because fleas were less active. By the end of the year, the worst was over in Florence, though the sickness continued in other parts of Europe. People began to return to the city, and shops slowly reopened. Historians know about this recovery from records of taxes, rents and business activity, which began to increase again. Even so, the city had changed. Many families had lost members, and some had disappeared entirely. Houses stood empty, and property passed to new owners. Historians estimate that the population of Florence did not return to its earlier size for several centuries. Yet life continued. In the hill villages around the city, church bells rang again for weddings, harvests and feast days, as they had before.

[long-pause]

{img:65-english-village-thatched-cottages-church-summer}

In England, manor courts recorded who inherited land after the epidemic. The plague reached England in the summer of 1348, arriving first in the southwest, and spread across the country in the following year. Much of what historians know about its effects in England comes from manor court records. These were kept by the lords of rural estates and recorded the business of each village. When a tenant died, the court recorded who inherited his land, animals and goods, and what payments were made to the lord. After 1348, these records show large numbers of deaths and many changes in land ownership. Historians have studied thousands of such records, written in Latin by village clerks. They reveal the experiences of ordinary villagers in great detail. Even so, interpreting them requires care, since not everyone appears in the records. Yet they show that village life continued. Land was quickly taken over by heirs or new tenants, fields were farmed again and communities slowly recovered.

[long-pause]

{img:66-norwegian-fjord-wooden-church-forest-mist}

In 1349, the plague reached Norway, reportedly on a ship from England that arrived in Bergen. From England, the plague spread across the North Sea. According to Icelandic and Norwegian sources, a ship from England arrived in Bergen, on the west coast of Norway, in the summer of 1349. Soon afterwards, the sickness spread through the town and into the surrounding countryside. Norway was a land of scattered farms and small communities along fjords and valleys. Even so, the plague reached many of them, carried along trade and travel routes. Historians know about the epidemic in Norway from later chronicles and from records showing that many farms were abandoned. Some remained empty for a long time. Historians debate how many people died, since records are incomplete. Yet it is clear that the effects were serious and long-lasting. In the following centuries, Norway's population and economy recovered slowly. The quiet wooden churches and farms along its fjords became symbols of a land that rebuilt itself after hard times.

[long-pause]

{img:67-milan-city-walls-towers-quiet-morning}

A few places in Europe suffered much less from the plague than others. Not all regions were affected equally by the Black Death. Some cities and areas seem to have suffered fewer deaths. Milan, in northern Italy, is often mentioned as an example. According to some accounts, its ruler acted quickly when the sickness appeared, isolating infected houses. Historians debate how effective these measures were and whether the reports are accurate. Some regions may have escaped partly because of their isolation, or simply by chance. Parts of Poland and the Low Countries are also sometimes said to have suffered less, though recent research has questioned this. Historians use tax records, wills and archaeological evidence to compare different places. Even so, the evidence is often incomplete. Yet the uneven pattern of the epidemic shows that local conditions mattered. Trade routes, population density, climate and the actions of local authorities all played a role in how the sickness spread and how communities experienced it.

[long-pause]

{img:68-paris-scholars-gowns-meeting-hall}

In 1348, the medical faculty of Paris explained the plague as the result of a rare meeting of planets. In October 1348, the King of France asked the medical faculty of the University of Paris to explain the cause of the epidemic. The doctors wrote a report, which became widely known. They suggested that a conjunction of the planets Saturn, Jupiter and Mars, in 1345, had corrupted the air. They believed that this corrupted air spread disease. They also gave advice on diet, cleanliness and avoiding bad air. Historians consider this report an important document of medieval science. It shows how learned people tried to understand the disaster using the knowledge available to them. Even so, the explanation was wrong. The real cause, the plague bacterium, was not discovered until 1894. Yet the doctors' efforts reflected a serious attempt to find natural causes and practical remedies. Historians note that the experience of the plague led to many new medical writings and helped encourage the study of public health in the centuries that followed.

{img:69-santa-maria-novella-church-morning-young-women}

The Decameron begins with seven young women meeting in the church of Santa Maria Novella in Florence. In Boccaccio's book, the story begins on a Tuesday morning in the church of Santa Maria Novella, the great Dominican church of Florence. Seven young women, friends and relatives, meet there after attending Mass. They talk about the sad state of the city and decide to leave for the countryside for a while. They invite three young men to join them. The group of ten agrees to stay together in country villas, where they will pass the time pleasantly and honourably. Literary scholars note that Boccaccio gave each character a name with a meaning, and some may refer to real people he knew. Even so, the group is fictional. Boccaccio used them as a frame for his collection of stories. Santa Maria Novella still stands today near the main railway station of Florence. Its beautiful marble front was completed in the fifteenth century, and inside are famous paintings by Giotto, Masaccio and Ghirlandaio.

[long-pause]

{img:70-villa-hilltop-gardens-fountains-roses-morning}

Boccaccio described the country villas of his story as places of cool wells, beautiful rooms and flowers. The ten young people in the Decameron leave Florence and travel a short distance into the hills. They stay in beautiful villas surrounded by gardens. Boccaccio describes these places with great affection: wide courtyards, cool wells, fine rooms decorated with flowers, and gardens full of roses, jasmine and orange trees. There are lawns, fountains and views across the countryside. Literary scholars believe that Boccaccio may have had real villas near Florence in mind. Some local traditions name particular houses, such as the Villa Palmieri near Fiesole, but there is no certain evidence. Even so, his descriptions reflect the real country houses of wealthy Florentines. Historians use them as evidence of how such villas looked and how their owners enjoyed them. The gardens in the Decameron are also symbolic. They represent order, beauty and calm, in contrast to the troubled city. They offer the characters, and readers, a peaceful place to rest.

[long-pause]

{img:71-young-people-garden-table-white-linen-fountain}

In the Decameron, one of the young people is chosen each day to be king or queen and to organise the day. The ten young people in the Decameron agree on a simple set of rules for their stay in the countryside. Each day, one of them is chosen as king or queen. That person decides the plans for the day, including meals, walks and games. They also choose the theme for the day's stories. The days follow a peaceful pattern. In the morning, the group walks in the gardens. They eat together at tables set with white linen near fountains. In the hottest part of the day, they rest. Literary scholars note that this structure gives the book a sense of order and harmony. The young people treat each other with courtesy and respect. Even so, the stories they tell are often lively, funny and sometimes daring. Historians use the Decameron to understand ideas about manners, leisure and social life in fourteenth century Florence. The orderly days of the group stand in quiet contrast to the disorder of the city.

[long-pause]

{img:72-young-people-sitting-circle-meadow-shade}

Each afternoon in the Decameron, the ten young people each tell one story, making one hundred stories in ten days. In the afternoon, when the heat of the day begins to ease, the ten young people sit together in a shady meadow. Each of them tells one story on the theme chosen for the day. After ten days, there are one hundred stories in total. This gives the book its name, Decameron, from Greek words meaning ten days. The themes include stories of good fortune, of clever replies, of love with happy or sad endings, and of tricks played by wives and husbands. The stories are set in many places, from Florence and Naples to Paris, Egypt and the Holy Land. Literary scholars consider the Decameron one of the founding works of European prose. Its stories were translated and adapted by many later writers, including Geoffrey Chaucer and William Shakespeare. Even so, some stories were considered shocking in later centuries and were sometimes censored. Yet the book remained popular for its humour and its rich picture of human life.

[long-pause]

{img:73-poor-nobleman-falcon-on-glove-country-farm}

One of the most famous stories in the Decameron tells of a poor young man and his falcon. On the fifth day of the Decameron, one story tells of Federigo degli Alberighi, a young nobleman of Florence. He falls in love with a lady named Monna Giovanna and spends all his fortune trying to win her heart. She does not return his love. Federigo becomes poor and moves to a small farm in the countryside. All he has left is his beloved falcon. One day, the lady visits him unexpectedly. Federigo has nothing else to offer her for dinner, so he cooks his falcon to honour her. Then he learns that she had come to ask him for the falcon, for her sick son. Literary scholars consider this one of the finest stories in the book. It shows generosity, love and dignity in poverty. Even so, the story is gentle in tone and ends happily, when the lady later marries Federigo. Readers have admired it for centuries, and it has been retold in many forms.

[long-pause]

{img:74-cook-roasting-crane-kitchen-fire-pretty-girl}

Another story in the Decameron tells of a clever cook who claimed that cranes have only one leg. On the sixth day of the Decameron, the theme is clever replies that get people out of trouble. In one story, a Venetian cook named Chichibio works for a nobleman in Florence. His master has hunted a crane and asks him to roast it. A young woman Chichibio likes asks him for one of its legs, and he gives it to her. When the master asks where the other leg is, Chichibio claims that cranes have only one leg. The next morning, the master takes him to a river to see living cranes. They are standing on one leg, as cranes do. When the master shouts, the cranes put down their second legs and fly away. Chichibio quickly replies that the master did not shout at the roasted crane at dinner. The master laughs and forgives him. Literary scholars enjoy this story for its humour and quick wit. It shows the lighter side of the Decameron, which balances the sadness of its opening.

[long-pause]

{img:75-walking-down-hill-toward-florence-evening}

At the end of the Decameron, the ten young people return to Florence after two weeks away. After ten days of storytelling, and a few rest days, the young people decide to return to Florence. They have spent about two weeks in the countryside. They feel refreshed and stronger, and they worry that staying away too long might cause gossip. So they walk back to the city together and part at the church of Santa Maria Novella, where their journey began. Literary scholars see this ending as meaningful. The group leaves a troubled city, finds order and joy in storytelling and returns with renewed strength. The book suggests that stories, friendship and laughter can help people through hard times. Even so, Boccaccio does not pretend that the troubles have disappeared. The city they return to is still marked by the epidemic. Yet the Decameron ends on a note of hope and continuity. Many readers have found comfort in it, especially during difficult times, and it has never been out of print since printing began.

[long-pause]

{img:76-florence-spring-1349-workshops-reopening}

In the spring of 1349, workshops in Florence began to reopen, and life slowly returned to normal. After the epidemic ended, Florence began to recover. In 1349, shops and workshops reopened, and the sounds of work returned to the streets. The wool industry started again, though there were fewer workers than before. Building resumed on the cathedral and other projects. Historians know about this recovery from account books, tax records and the records of guilds. Matteo Villani, who continued his brother's chronicle, described how the city changed after the plague. He noted that people who had survived often had more money, because they had inherited property from relatives. Some spent freely on clothing, food and entertainment. Even so, the recovery was not simple. Prices rose, and there were shortages of workers in many trades. The city government passed new laws to control wages and prices. Yet Florence remained a great city. Within a few decades, it would become the centre of the Renaissance, one of the most creative periods in European history.

[long-pause]

{img:77-medieval-wedding-procession-musicians-flowers}

In the years after the plague, there were many weddings in Florence and across Europe. After the great epidemic, many people who had lost husbands or wives married again, and many young people married earlier than usual. Historians have found evidence of a sharp increase in marriages in many places in the years after 1348. Matteo Villani described how weddings became common in Florence. Weddings were celebrated with processions through the streets, music, flowers and feasts. The bride was accompanied to her new home by family and friends, and gifts, such as painted chests, were carried in the procession. Historians know about these customs from marriage contracts, account books and paintings. Even so, the number of births did not immediately replace the population that had been lost. The plague returned several times in the following decades. Yet the many weddings reflected a desire to rebuild families and look to the future. For many communities, they were a sign of hope and a way of beginning again after a time of sorrow.

[long-pause]

{img:78-english-peasants-harvest-field-better-times}

Because there were fewer workers after the plague, many ordinary people could ask for higher wages. The Black Death killed so many people that there were far fewer workers in towns and in the countryside. Landowners and employers needed labourers to farm their land, harvest crops and work in workshops. Workers were able to demand higher wages and better conditions. Historians know about this change from records of wages and prices across Europe. In England, the government tried to stop wages from rising by passing the Statute of Labourers in 1351. Similar laws were passed in other countries, including in Florence. Even so, these laws were difficult to enforce. Over time, many ordinary people saw their living standards improve. They ate better, with more meat and ale, and some acquired more land. Historians debate how widely these benefits were shared. Yet the period after the Black Death brought important changes in the relationship between workers and employers, which shaped European society for centuries.

[long-pause]

{img:79-painter-fresco-scaffold-church-wall}

After the plague, the people of Florence paid for paintings, chapels and religious works of art. In the years after the epidemic, many people in Florence gave money to churches, confraternities and religious orders. Some did this to give thanks for surviving. Others left money in their wills for prayers, chapels and paintings. As a result, artists received many commissions. Historians and art historians have studied the art of this period closely. Some scholars once argued that art after the Black Death became darker and more serious. Later research has questioned this idea, showing that styles varied and that many changes had begun earlier. Even so, the plague clearly affected how people thought about life, death and faith. In Florence, painters such as Andrea Orcagna and his brothers created important works in the following decades. The tabernacle Orcagna made for the Madonna of Orsanmichele, completed in 1359, was paid for partly by gifts made during and after the epidemic. It can still be seen in Orsanmichele today.

[long-pause]

{img:80-old-surgeon-writing-large-book-candle}

Guy de Chauliac, the surgeon to the Pope, wrote a great book of surgery that was used for centuries. Guy de Chauliac survived the epidemic of 1348 in Avignon, where he cared for the sick and treated the Pope. He also fell ill during a later outbreak and recovered. In 1363, he completed a large book on surgery, called the Chirurgia Magna, or Great Surgery. In it, he described medical knowledge from ancient and Arabic sources, as well as his own experience. He also wrote one of the most detailed descriptions of the plague, distinguishing between different forms of the disease. Historians consider his book one of the most important medical texts of the Middle Ages. It was copied, translated and printed many times, and doctors used it for more than two hundred years. Even so, his understanding of the plague was limited by the knowledge of his time. Yet his careful observation and honest reporting were valuable. He admitted that doctors could do little against the sickness and described his own fear with honesty.

[long-pause]

{img:81-tuscan-countryside-recovered-farms-harvest}

The plague returned several times in later centuries, but each time communities rebuilt. The epidemic of 1348 was not the last. The plague returned to Europe many times, in 1361, 1374 and in later decades and centuries. Each outbreak caused new deaths, though usually fewer than the first. Historians call this the second plague pandemic, which lasted until the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries in parts of Europe. Over time, cities developed better ways of responding, including health boards, quarantine and isolation hospitals. Historians know about these outbreaks from city records, medical writings and chronicles. Even so, between epidemics there were long periods of recovery and growth. In Tuscany, farms were cultivated again, villages repopulated and cities flourished. The fifteenth century in Florence was a time of great artistic and intellectual achievement. Historians note how remarkable it is that societies continued to rebuild despite repeated disasters. The calm Tuscan landscape of farms, vineyards and olive groves reflects centuries of patient work and renewal.

[long-pause]

{img:82-florence-hospital-courtyard-gifts}

After the plague, many Florentines left money in their wills to hospitals, confraternities and the poor. During and after the epidemic of 1348, many people in Florence made wills. Historians have studied thousands of these documents. They show that people left large sums of money to hospitals, confraternities, churches and the poor. The Confraternity of Orsanmichele, which cared for the image of the Madonna, received an enormous amount in gifts in 1348. It used the money to help people in need and to build the new tabernacle by Orcagna. Hospitals such as Santa Maria Nuova also received many bequests, which helped them grow. Historians see these gifts as evidence of both religious faith and concern for the community. Even so, managing such large sums caused some disputes and problems. Yet the generosity of ordinary citizens helped Florence care for its sick and poor in the following decades. Many of the charitable institutions that benefited from these gifts continued their work for centuries, and some still exist today.

{img:83-boccaccio-older-certaldo-study-books}

Boccaccio became a close friend of Petrarch and gave the first public lectures on Dante. In 1350, Boccaccio met Petrarch in Florence, and the two writers became close friends. They exchanged many letters and shared a love of ancient literature. Influenced by Petrarch, Boccaccio devoted much of his later life to scholarship. He wrote works in Latin about famous women, about the gods of ancient myth and about geography. He also helped bring knowledge of ancient Greek to Florence. Boccaccio greatly admired Dante. He wrote a biography of Dante and copied his works. In 1373, the city of Florence asked him to give public lectures on Dante's Divine Comedy, the first such lectures ever held. He gave them in the church of Santo Stefano di Badia until illness forced him to stop. Historians and literary scholars see Boccaccio as one of the three great writers of fourteenth century Italy, together with Dante and Petrarch. He died in Certaldo in 1375, a year after his friend Petrarch.

{img:84-florence-health-officials-clean-street}

In the spring of 1348, the government of Florence appointed eight citizens to protect the health of the city. In April 1348, as the epidemic began, the government of Florence appointed a special committee of eight citizens. Their task was to protect public health. They ordered the cleaning of streets and the removal of rubbish and dead animals. They controlled the sale of certain goods and limited the movement of people from infected places. They also organised burials. Historians know about these measures from the records of the city government. Similar committees were created in other Italian cities, including Venice. Even so, these measures could not stop the epidemic, because the true cause of the disease was unknown. Yet they were an important step in the history of public health. In the following decades, Florence and other cities created permanent health boards. Historians see these developments as the beginnings of modern public health systems. The idea that a city government should protect the health of its citizens grew partly from the experience of 1348.

{img:85-volunteer-ambulance-cathedral-square-today}

The Misericordia of Florence still helps people today, more than seven hundred and fifty years after it was founded. The Confraternity of the Misericordia, founded in 1244, is still active in Florence today. Its headquarters stand on the Piazza del Duomo, opposite the cathedral and the Baptistery. Its members are volunteers who provide ambulance services, help the elderly and the sick, and support people in need. For many centuries, they wore black robes and hoods, like their medieval predecessors. Today, they wear modern uniforms for most of their work, but traditions continue. Historians consider the Misericordia one of the oldest charitable organisations in the world still operating. Its archives contain records going back many centuries, including documents from the time of the Black Death. Even so, its work is not only historical. Every day, its volunteers answer calls for help across the city. Similar organisations exist in many other Italian towns. The continuity of the Misericordia shows how the charitable spirit of medieval Florence has survived into the present.

{img:86-florence-archive-old-records-reading-room}

The State Archives of Florence preserve thousands of documents that show how ordinary people lived through 1348. The Archivio di Stato di Firenze, the State Archives of Florence, is one of the richest historical archives in the world. It holds millions of documents from the Middle Ages onward, including government records, tax registers, court cases, wills, contracts and the papers of guilds and confraternities. Historians come from many countries to study them. For the year 1348, these records allow historians to trace the epidemic and its effects in remarkable detail. They show who paid taxes, who died, who inherited property and how businesses changed. Historians such as Samuel Cohn and Ann Carmichael have used them to study the plague in Florence. Even so, many questions remain. Records do not always say how people felt or what daily life was like. Yet they preserve the names and traces of thousands of ordinary men and women. In the quiet reading rooms of the archive, the people of medieval Florence are still remembered.
