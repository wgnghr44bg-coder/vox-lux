HEAD: In 1347, Florence had more people than London, and almost as many as Paris.
BODY: It's a spring morning in the year 1347. Picture yourself standing on a hill above the city, looking down at a sea of red roofs. The sun is rising over the hills around Florence, in Tuscany, in the north of Italy. The first light falls on red-tiled roofs and tall stone towers. It falls on the river Arno, which flows slowly through the middle of the city, green and brown between its stone banks. Mist lies in the valley. On the hills above, among olive groves and cypress trees, the bells of little churches are ringing for the first prayers of the day. Florence was one of the greatest cities in Europe. Perhaps a hundred thousand people lived inside its walls. That was more than in London, and almost as many as in Paris. Only a few cities in all of Christian Europe were larger. Imagine standing on a hill above it, as the bells ring out one after another. You'd hear the city long before you reached its gates. The whole valley seems to hum softly, like a great beehive waking up in the sun.

HEAD: Rich families in Florence once built tall stone towers beside their houses, just to defend themselves.
BODY: Florence was surrounded by a great wall of stone, only recently finished. You'd crane your neck to see the tops of them. It had tall towers and strong gates, with a moat in front. Inside, the streets were narrow and winding. The houses stood close together, three, four or five storeys high. They were built of brown and grey stone, with wooden balconies and shutters. Little gardens hid behind high walls. And over the rooftops rose the towers. Long ago, when Florence was smaller and rougher, every rich family built a tall tower beside its house. In times of trouble, the family could climb inside and defend itself against its rivals. There were once more than a hundred of these towers. They rose over the city like a forest of stone. By 1347, the government had ordered many of them cut down, to make the city more peaceful. But many still stood, looking down on the busy streets. You'd feel very small walking between them. Some of them are still standing today.

HEAD: On the Old Bridge, butchers and fishmongers sold their goods in little shops hanging over the river.
BODY: The river Arno was crossed by four stone bridges. You'd hear the river rushing under your feet as you shopped. The oldest and busiest was the Ponte Vecchio, the Old Bridge. It had just been rebuilt, after a terrible flood fourteen years earlier swept the old one away. On top of the new bridge stood little shops on both sides. They were built of wood and stone and hung out over the water. Butchers, fishmongers and tanners sold their goods there. They threw their scraps straight into the river below. It wasn't the sweetest-smelling place in Florence. Yet the view from the bridge was wonderful. Looking down the river, you could see houses rising along both banks. You could see towers, domes and bell towers. And beyond them were the hills, green and blue, dotted with villages and villas. That same bridge still stands today, more than six hundred and seventy years later. Can you smell the fish and the river? People still stop on it in the evening to watch the river turn gold.

HEAD: Florence grew rich from wool, and its little gold coin was trusted from England to Egypt.
BODY: Florence was rich, and most of its wealth came from wool. You'd hear looms clacking behind half the doors in town. Its merchants bought raw wool from England and Spain and brought it home. Then thousands of men and women in the city worked on it. They washed it, combed it, spun it and wove it. They dyed it in beautiful colours. Slowly, it became the finest cloth in Europe. The merchants sold that cloth all over the world, from London to Constantinople. Florence was also rich from banking. Its great banking families lent money to kings, popes and princes. Their gold coin was called the florin. It had a lily on one side and Saint John the Baptist on the other. People trusted it everywhere, from England to Egypt. A merchant in a faraway port would bite a florin, smile and accept it. Isn't it strange to think of it? You'd see the little gold coins changing hands at every stall. A little coin from one Italian city, travelling across the whole known world.

HEAD: After hungry years, the spring of 1347 made the people of Florence hopeful again.
BODY: Florence had lived through some hard years. You'd see blossom on the fruit trees and smiles in the market again. A few years before, two of its greatest banks had collapsed. Then came bad harvests, and food grew scarce and expensive. In 1346, many people in the city went hungry. The government baked bread in public ovens and gave it out, and many lives were saved. But the spring of 1347 felt different. The fields around the city were green. The vines were budding on the hills. The harvest looked good. In the city, the gates opened each morning, and carts and mules came in from the countryside. They brought vegetables and firewood, eggs, cheese and wine. Shopkeepers took down their shutters. Bakers pulled the first loaves from their ovens. And people began to feel that the hard times were over.

HEAD: In a house in Florence, a young writer named Boccaccio was dreaming about the stories he wanted to tell.
BODY: In a house somewhere in the city, a young writer was waking up too. You'd find him scribbling notes late into the night. His name was Giovanni Boccaccio. He was about thirty-four, a big, round-faced man with dark eyes and a cheerful, friendly manner. He loved good food, good wine and good company, and he loved to laugh. Above all, he loved stories. His father was a merchant who had worked for one of the great banks. He had wanted his son to become a merchant too. But young Giovanni wasn't interested in bales of wool or columns of numbers. So his father sent him to study law instead. Giovanni wasn't interested in law either. He wanted to write. In the spring of 1347, he wasn't yet famous. People knew him as a clever writer of love poems and romances. Nobody imagined he'd become one of the greatest writers in Italian history. And nobody, least of all Boccaccio, knew what he'd one day write about.

HEAD: In Florence, each shop's wooden shutter folded down to become its counter.
BODY: The streets of Florence were busy from dawn to dusk. You'd hear them banging down all along the street at dawn. They were narrow and paved with large flat stones. In many of them, two carts couldn't pass each other. The upper storeys of the houses leaned out over the street, held up on wooden beams. So the sky above was only a thin blue strip. Even in summer, the streets stayed cool and shady. On the ground floors were the shops. Each shop was a single room, open to the street through a wide arched doorway. In the morning, the shopkeeper took down his wooden shutter. He folded it out to make a counter and set out his goods on it. Behind him, in the dim room, his family and apprentices were at work. There were shops for everything. There were bakers and butchers, grocers selling spices and dried fruit. There were shoemakers, tailors and goldsmiths. There were barbers, who also pulled teeth. You'd lean on the counter and chat while you waited. You could buy almost anything in a single short walk.

HEAD: Every two months, the names of the city's new rulers were drawn out of leather bags.
BODY: The people of Florence belonged to guilds. You'd hold your breath with everyone else as the names were read out. A guild was a society of everyone who did the same kind of work. There were guilds of wool merchants, silk makers and bankers. There were guilds of doctors, lawyers, butchers, bakers and stonemasons. Each guild had its own rules and officers. It had its own coat of arms and its own patron saint. It had its own chapel, where members met and prayed together. The guilds were very powerful. Only guild members could be elected to govern the city. And the way they chose the government was unusual. Every two months, names were drawn from leather bags. The men whose names came out moved into the government palace. They lived there together for two months, eating, sleeping and working in the great stone building. Then they went home again, and new names were drawn. You might even find your own name in the bag one day. Imagine running a city for just eight weeks, and then going back to your shop.

HEAD: Along the river, newly dyed cloth dried in the sun like long flags of scarlet, blue and violet.
BODY: The greatest of all the guilds was the guild of the wool merchants. You'd see the colours flapping in the breeze like banners. About a third of all the people in Florence earned their living from wool, in one way or another. Most of them were poor. There were washers, who cleaned the raw wool in the river. There were carders and combers, who straightened its fibres. There were spinners, mostly women, working at home with their spindles. There were weavers at great wooden looms. And there were dyers, whose arms were stained blue, red and yellow from the great vats of dye. Along the banks of the Arno stood long rows of wooden frames. The newly dyed cloth was stretched out on them to dry in the sun. There were long bands of scarlet, blue, green and deep violet. They rippled in the wind like great flags. Can you picture it, on a bright morning? You'd want a cloak in every one of those colours. A whole riverbank shimmering with colour, and the smell of wet wool in the warm air.

There were butchers and poulterers, fishmongers and cheesemakers. There were sellers of bread, wine and oil. There were pedlars, jugglers and storytellers. Beggars sat on the church steps. Children ran between the stalls, chasing each other, the dogs and the chickens. Women stood and gossiped at the well. Men stood in tavern doorways, arguing about politics and prices. It was noisy, smelly and full of life. Every bit of news in Florence passed through this square. If something happened at dawn, the whole market knew by midday. You'd leave with a basket of figs and all the latest news. In a way, the Old Market was the beating heart of everyday Florence.

Every evening, people came to light candles in front of it. They knelt, prayed and sang hymns together. Imagine the scene. The smell of grain and dust, the flicker of candles, and soft voices singing among the sacks of wheat. Bread for the body and comfort for the soul, side by side. Farmers came in with their sacks in the morning. Mothers came back in the evening with a candle and a prayer. The same building served them all. HEAD: In Florentine houses, the kitchen was right at the top, to keep smoke and fire far from everything else.

That way, the smoke, the smell of cooking and the danger of fire were kept as far as possible from the rest of the house. In the kitchen was a great open hearth. A fire burned there all day. Iron pots hung from chains over it, and there was a spit for roasting meat. The cook baked bread, or sent the dough to the baker's oven down the street. She made soups and stews of beans, cabbage and onions, with a little meat or salted pork. The mother ran the household, with the help of the servants. There might be a cook, a couple of maids and a nurse for the little children. Every morning, she gave out the day's tasks and checked the stores. It was a big job, like running a small business. You'd be glad of the view from up there, though. And the merchant's wife was often very good at it.

HEAD: Merchants' sons went to a special abacus school, to learn the arithmetic they'd need in business.
BODY: The children of merchants learned to read, write and do sums. You'd hear them chanting sums in the classroom. The boys first went to a school run by a teacher in the city, where they learned to read. Then many went on to a special school called an abacus school. HEAD: The Florentines wanted the largest church in Christendom, and building it took more than a century.
BODY: In the heart of Florence, people were building a cathedral. You'd watch it grow, stone by stone, over your whole lifetime. It had been started about fifty years earlier, on the spot where an old, small church stood. The Florentines wanted it to be the largest and most beautiful church in all of Christian Europe. Everyone who came to their city should be amazed. In 1347, it was still far from finished. It wouldn't be finished for nearly another hundred years. The walls rose slowly, of brown stone covered with white, green and pink marble. The marble was laid in patterns of squares, stripes and stars. Inside, the great hall was open to the sky in places. The floor was littered with stone blocks and piles of sand. All around, the stonemasons worked in their sheds and yards. Their chisels rang and tapped all day. Great wooden cranes lifted the stones. You'd tell your grandchildren you saw it begin. They were worked by men walking inside huge wooden wheels, like hamsters in a giant wheel, step by patient step.

HEAD: The people of Florence believed their patron saint, John the Baptist, watched over their city in hard times.
BODY: Every city had its own protector in heaven, and Florence had Saint John. You'd see his image painted on walls, carved over doorways and stamped on the little gold coins. When times were difficult, people turned to him first. You'd see a mother lighting a candle in front of his picture, and a merchant leaving a coin in the box by the door. In the hard years before 1348, after floods and hungry winters, many Florentines prayed to him for protection. And when frightening news began to arrive from the East, they prayed even harder. You might think this was only fear. But it was also comfort. Kneeling quietly in a cool church, among neighbours and candles, people felt less alone. I find that rather gentle. Can you picture the little flames flickering in the dim church, while the bells ring the hour outside?

HEAD: In 1348, long processions moved slowly through Florence, with people carrying candles and holy images.
BODY: When people were afraid, they walked together. You'd see a long line of men, women and children moving slowly through the narrow streets, singing psalms, with candles in their hands. At the front, priests carried painted banners and precious boxes with holy relics. Shopkeepers came out of their doorways and joined in. The procession wound past the churches, the market and the river, and the whole city seemed to pray with one voice. People believed that this might soften God's anger and protect them from the sickness. We know now that gathering in crowds didn't help against an illness that spread from person to person. But they couldn't have known that. And there was something steadying about walking side by side. Would you have joined the line, with your little candle cupped in your hand against the wind?

HEAD: Many Florentines carried rosemary, juniper or a cloth soaked in vinegar, hoping to keep away the bad air.
BODY: Doctors in 1348 believed that sickness spread through foul, poisoned air. So people did their best to keep the air around them clean and sweet. You'd see a woman walking to market holding a little bunch of rosemary under her nose. A merchant might carry a small ball of sweet-smelling herbs and spices in his hand. Others pressed a cloth soaked in vinegar to their faces when they passed a crowded corner. In the houses, people burned juniper and other fragrant woods to purify the rooms. The smell of smoke and herbs drifted through the streets. You might think this was useless. It didn't stop the sickness. But a sweet smell can calm a frightened heart, and many of these herbs did help to keep houses fresher. Can you smell the rosemary and the woodsmoke on the warm spring air?

HEAD: Friars in brown and in black and white preached to great crowds in the squares, and cared for the poor.
BODY: Florence had many other churches too. You'd stop to listen to them preach in the square. In the west of the city stood the great church of Santa Maria Novella. It belonged to the Dominican friars, who wore black and white robes. In the east stood Santa Croce. It belonged to the Franciscan friars, who wore plain brown robes tied with a rope. The friars preached in the squares in front of their churches, to great crowds. People sat on the steps and listened. A good preacher could hold a crowd for hours, making them laugh and cry. The friars also visited the sick and the poor, and gave food to the hungry. Inside their churches, the walls were covered with paintings by Giotto and his pupils. They told the stories of Saint Francis and the saints, in bright colours. For people who couldn't read, these paintings were like a great picture book. You'd see them visiting the sick and the poor all day. You could stand before a wall and follow a whole story with your eyes.
