TOPIC: A city of towers on the Arno
===
IMG: florence-arno-river-dawn-1347 | a walled medieval city of red tiled roofs and tall stone towers beside a slow river at dawn, mist in the valley, olive groves and cypress trees on the hills, soft golden early light
HEAD: In 1347, Florence was one of the largest and richest cities in Europe.
BODY: Florence lay on the river Arno, in Tuscany, in the middle of Italy. Around the year 1340, it had perhaps a hundred thousand people. That made it larger than London and one of the great cities of Christian Europe, alongside Paris, Venice and Milan. The city was surrounded by hills covered with olive trees, vineyards and small farms. Its wealth came from trade, banking and, above all, the making of fine woollen cloth. Historians know a great deal about Florence in this period, because its people wrote so much down. Merchants kept careful account books, the city kept tax records, and chroniclers described daily life. Many of these documents still survive in the city's archives. Even so, Florence had problems as well as riches. Its great banks had recently collapsed, and the harvest of 1346 had failed. Yet in the spring of 1347, the city was busy and confident, with no idea of what the coming year would bring.
===
IMG: city-walls-towers-gates-tuscan-hills | a great stone city wall with tall towers and a fortified gate, carts and mules passing through, green Tuscan hills behind, soft morning light
HEAD: Rich families in medieval Florence built tall stone towers beside their houses.
BODY: In the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, powerful families in Florence fought one another for control of the city. To protect themselves, they built tall, narrow towers of stone next to their homes. Some were more than fifty metres high. From the top, family members could watch their rivals and defend themselves in times of street fighting. By the fourteenth century, the city government had ordered many towers to be cut down to a lower height, to reduce the power of the old noble families. Even so, dozens of them still stood, and some survive today. Around the city ran a new circuit of stone walls, finished in the 1330s, with tall gates and towers. It enclosed a much larger area than the old walls, including fields and gardens. Historians use old drawings, maps and surviving buildings to reconstruct the medieval city. Visitors to Florence can still see several tower houses in the old streets near the river.
===
IMG: ponte-vecchio-shops-river-1347 | an old stone bridge over a slow river with small wooden shops built along both sides hanging over the water, people crossing, warm golden evening light
HEAD: The Ponte Vecchio, the old bridge of Florence, was rebuilt in 1345 with shops along both sides.
BODY: Four stone bridges crossed the Arno in Florence in the fourteenth century. The oldest was the Ponte Vecchio, the old bridge. In 1333, a terrible flood swept it away, together with other bridges. The city rebuilt it in stone, and the new bridge was finished in 1345. It still stands today. Shops lined both sides of the bridge, built over the water. In the fourteenth century, they were rented to many kinds of traders, including butchers and fishmongers, who could throw their waste straight into the river below. Historians know about the flood from the chronicler Giovanni Villani, who described it in detail. Even so, the bridge looked different then. The famous jewellers and goldsmiths arrived only in 1593, when the ruler of Florence ordered the butchers to leave because of the smell. Today, the Ponte Vecchio is one of the most famous bridges in the world, still lined with small shops above the slow green river.
===
IMG: wool-merchants-counting-house-florins | a merchant's counting room with bales of fine wool cloth, a heavy ledger open on a wooden desk and small gold coins in neat stacks, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm lamplight
HEAD: Florence grew rich from wool, and its gold coin, the florin, was trusted across Europe and beyond.
BODY: The most important industry in Florence was the making of woollen cloth. Merchants imported fine wool from England, Spain and North Africa. In the city, thousands of workers washed, combed, spun, wove, dyed and finished it. The finished cloth was sold across Europe and around the Mediterranean. The wool guild, called the Arte della Lana, was one of the most powerful organisations in the city. Florence was also famous for banking. In 1252, the city began to mint a gold coin called the florin. It kept the same weight and purity for a very long time, so merchants everywhere trusted it. Historians call it one of the most important coins of medieval Europe. Even so, the 1340s were a difficult time. Two of the biggest banks, run by the Bardi and Peruzzi families, collapsed after lending huge sums to the king of England. Yet the wool trade continued, and the city remained wealthy and busy.
===
IMG: florence-rooftops-bell-towers-morning-light | a view over medieval rooftops with bell towers and a half-built cathedral wrapped in wooden scaffolding, green hills beyond, soft hopeful spring morning light
HEAD: After a year of hunger, the spring of 1347 brought new hope to Florence.
BODY: The harvest of 1346 had been very poor across Tuscany. Heavy rains damaged crops, and grain became scarce and expensive. In the winter and spring of 1347, many poor families in Florence went hungry. According to the chronicler Giovanni Villani, the city government bought grain from far away and baked bread to give to the poor. He recorded that thousands of people received this help each day. Historians use his figures carefully, but they show how serious the shortage was. When spring came, the fields turned green again, and the new crops grew well. The summer harvest of 1347 was good, and bread returned to the markets. Even so, many people had been weakened by months of poor food. Historians have wondered whether this made them more vulnerable to illness the following year. Yet in the spring of 1347, the bells rang over the red roofs, and the people of Florence looked forward to better times.
===
IMG: young-writer-desk-manuscripts-window-florence | a cheerful round-faced young man at a wooden desk covered with manuscripts beside an open window overlooking medieval rooftops, a quill in his hand, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft morning light
HEAD: The young writer Giovanni Boccaccio lived in Florence in the 1340s.
BODY: Giovanni Boccaccio was born in 1313, probably in Florence or in the nearby town of Certaldo. His father was a merchant who worked for the Bardi bank. As a young man, Boccaccio was sent to Naples to learn about business and law. He found both boring. Instead, he spent his time reading poetry, studying the classics and writing his own stories and poems. Around 1341, he returned to Florence when his father's fortunes declined. He continued to write. Historians know about his life from his own works, his letters and city records. In the years that followed, he would write the Decameron, a collection of one hundred stories that became one of the most famous books of the Middle Ages. Even so, in 1347 he was still a young writer, not yet famous. He walked the streets of Florence, listened to people talk and gathered the stories that would later fill his books.

TOPIC: Streets, guilds and markets
===
IMG: narrow-florence-street-shops-awnings-medieval | a narrow medieval street paved with large flat stones, upper storeys leaning out on wooden beams, small shops with folding wooden counters and cloth awnings, people shopping, soft morning light
HEAD: In medieval Florence, shops opened directly onto the street, and their wooden shutters folded down to make counters.
BODY: The streets of Florence were narrow, and houses rose three, four or five storeys high. On the ground floor of many houses were shops and workshops. Each one had a wide opening onto the street, closed at night with wooden shutters. In the morning, the lower shutter was folded down to form a counter, where goods were displayed and sold. Above, a cloth awning gave shade from the sun. Shoemakers, tailors, bakers, spice sellers and many other traders worked side by side. Historians know about these shops from paintings, inventories and the remains of medieval buildings in the city. Some streets were named after the trades practised there. Even so, the streets were not only for buying and selling. They were places to meet neighbours and exchange news. Children played in them, and animals wandered through. The noise of hammers, voices and cart wheels filled the air from early morning until the evening bells rang.
===
IMG: guild-hall-meeting-merchants-banners | merchants in long woollen gowns gathered in a stone hall hung with colourful guild banners, a carved wooden chest and candles on a table, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm light
HEAD: Florence was governed by citizens whose names were drawn by lot every two months.
BODY: Florence was a republic, ruled by its own citizens rather than by a king or prince. The government was led by a small group of officials called the priors. They served for only two months at a time. Their names were drawn from leather bags, which contained the names of eligible citizens. Only members of the city's guilds could hold office, so the merchants and master craftsmen had the most power. The guilds were associations of people in the same trade, such as wool merchants, bankers, doctors and spice sellers. Each guild had its own hall, its own rules and its own banner. Historians know about this system from the many government records that survive in Florence. Even so, the system was not as fair as it seemed. Powerful families often controlled who was placed in the bags. Ordinary workers, such as wool combers, had no guild of their own and no voice in the government.
===
IMG: wool-dyers-vats-river-coloured-cloth | long bands of freshly dyed cloth in scarlet, blue, green and violet stretched on wooden frames to dry along a riverbank, dyers with coloured arms beside great vats, bright sunlight
HEAD: Along the Arno, dyers coloured wool and cloth in great vats, and cloth was hung to dry in the sun.
BODY: Dyeing was one of the most important steps in making cloth. Dyers worked near the river, because they needed large amounts of water. They used natural dyes from plants and insects. Woad gave blue, madder gave red, and weld gave yellow. The most expensive red, called grain or kermes, came from a tiny insect. By combining dyes, dyers created many colours, from deep scarlet to violet and green. After dyeing, the cloth was rinsed in the river and stretched on wooden frames to dry. Historians know about these methods from guild rules and from merchants' manuals, which listed dyes and prices. One such manual was written by Francesco Pegolotti, a Florentine merchant of this period. Even so, dyeing was hard and dirty work. The dyers' hands were stained, and waste flowed into the Arno. Yet the bright cloth of Florence was admired across Europe and fetched high prices in distant markets.
===
IMG: mercato-vecchio-stalls-crowd-morning | a busy medieval market square with stalls of cabbages, onions, figs and cheeses, women gossiping by a stone well, children chasing chickens, warm morning light
HEAD: The Old Market was the busiest square in Florence, full of food stalls, shops and people.
BODY: At the heart of the city lay the Mercato Vecchio, the Old Market. It stood on the site of the ancient Roman forum. Every morning, farmers from the countryside brought vegetables, fruit, eggs, cheese and chickens to sell. Butchers, fishmongers and bakers had stalls and shops around the square. There were also taverns, a well and small churches. People came to shop, to meet friends and to hear the latest news. Historians know about the market from chronicles, paintings and old maps. In the nineteenth century, the market area was demolished to build a modern square, now called the Piazza della Repubblica. Even so, some paintings and photographs show what it once looked like. The market was noisy and crowded. Children ran between the stalls, dogs looked for scraps, and traders called out their prices. For most people in Florence, a daily visit to the market was part of ordinary life.
===
IMG: orsanmichele-grain-market-madonna-candles | a tall square stone building with wide open arches on the ground floor, sacks of grain stacked inside, and a golden painting of the Madonna glowing with candles at the back, warm evening light
HEAD: At Orsanmichele in Florence, people bought grain on the ground floor and prayed before an image of the Madonna.
BODY: Orsanmichele was an unusual building. It began as an open grain market, with an arched loggia on the ground floor where grain was sold. Above it, there were storerooms for grain. Inside the loggia hung an image of the Virgin Mary, believed to work miracles. Many people came to pray before it, light candles and leave gifts. A religious confraternity, a group of lay people, looked after the image and used the gifts to help the poor. In 1347, the painter Bernardo Daddi painted a new image of the Madonna for the building, which can still be seen there. Historians know about Orsanmichele from records of the confraternity and from chronicles. Even so, its role changed over time. Later in the century, the grain market moved elsewhere, and the arches were walled in. The building became a church, decorated with statues paid for by the guilds. It stands today as one of the most beautiful buildings in Florence.
===
IMG: evening-piazza-strolling-families-lanterns | families strolling across a medieval square in the evening, merchants in long coloured gowns and women in veils, small lamps glowing at a corner shrine, warm dusky light
HEAD: In the evening, church bells rang across Florence, and people paused to say a short prayer.
BODY: Medieval Florence followed the rhythm of church bells. Bells rang at dawn, at midday and in the evening, marking the hours of prayer and work. In the evening, the bell for the Ave Maria rang across the city. Many people stopped what they were doing and said a short prayer. Then the shops closed, and the streets became quieter. Families walked in the squares, talked with neighbours and enjoyed the cool air after a warm day. Young people met and flirted. Historians know about these customs from chronicles, sermons and stories, including those of Boccaccio. Even so, the city was not entirely peaceful at night. A curfew bell warned people to go home, and the city gates were closed. Watchmen walked the streets with lanterns. Lighting was scarce, so most people went to bed early. Yet these evening hours, between work and sleep, were among the most pleasant times of the day in a busy medieval city.

TOPIC: Inside a merchant's house
===
IMG: merchant-house-courtyard-well-loggia | a small stone courtyard inside a medieval house with a well in the middle, arches around the sides, a lemon tree in a pot and a climbing vine, soft afternoon light
HEAD: A wealthy merchant's house in Florence looked like a fortress from the street, but had a courtyard inside.
BODY: Rich families in Florence lived in large stone houses called palazzi. From the street, these houses looked strong and closed, with thick walls, heavy doors and small windows on the ground floor. This reflected the dangers of earlier times, when families fought in the streets. Inside, however, there was often a quiet courtyard open to the sky. It might have a well, a covered walkway and stairs leading to the upper floors. The ground floor was used for storage, business and shops. The family lived on the floors above. Historians know about these houses from surviving buildings, inventories and paintings. One of the best preserved is the Palazzo Davanzati, built in the fourteenth century, which is now a museum. Even so, not all merchants were rich enough for such houses. Many lived in simpler homes. Yet the merchant palazzo shows how wealthy Florentines combined safety, comfort and display in their daily lives.
===
IMG: sala-painted-walls-fireplace-long-table | a large high room with walls painted with flowers and trees, a great stone fireplace, a long wooden table and carved chests, painted ceiling beams, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm firelight
HEAD: Some wealthy Florentine families had their walls painted with gardens, trees and birds.
BODY: The main room of a merchant's house was the sala, a large hall used for meals, family gatherings and receiving guests. It often had a big fireplace for winter. Rich families decorated their walls with paintings. Some walls were painted to look like hanging cloth or tapestries. Others showed gardens, with trees, flowers and birds, so that people inside felt as if they were outdoors. In the Palazzo Davanzati, one room is painted with a garden of trees and parrots, and another tells a romantic story in pictures. Historians and art experts have studied these paintings as evidence of how people decorated their homes. Even so, furniture was quite simple by modern standards. Rooms contained tables, benches, chests and a few chairs. Most decoration came from the walls, cloth hangings and painted chests. These colourful rooms show the taste and comfort that wealthy families enjoyed in the fourteenth century.
===
IMG: canopy-bed-painted-chest-candle-bedroom | a large medieval bed with curtains, linen sheets and a fur coverlet, a painted wooden chest at its foot and a single candle burning, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft warm candlelight
HEAD: Medieval beds in Florence were very large, often shared, and surrounded by curtains.
BODY: In wealthy Florentine homes, the bed was one of the most valuable pieces of furniture. Beds were often very wide, large enough for several people, and stood on a raised wooden platform. Around them hung curtains, which kept out draughts and gave some privacy. Mattresses were filled with wool or feathers, and bedding included linen sheets and warm covers. Next to the bed stood a large wooden chest, called a cassone, used to store clothes and linen. Painted chests were often given at weddings. Historians know about bedrooms from household inventories, which listed every object in a house, and from paintings that show sleeping rooms. Even so, poorer families had much simpler beds. Several children often shared one bed, or slept on straw mattresses. Yet for everyone, the bed was an important place. People were born, rested, recovered from illness and died there. It was the centre of family life in many homes.
===
IMG: mother-servants-kitchen-top-floor-hearth | a kitchen at the top of a medieval house with a great open hearth, iron pots hanging on chains over the fire and a cook kneading dough at a wooden table, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm firelight
HEAD: In many Florentine houses, the kitchen was on the top floor, to keep smoke and fire away from the rest of the house.
BODY: Fire was a great danger in medieval cities. To reduce the risk, many Florentine houses had their kitchens at the very top, under the roof. Smoke could escape more easily there, and if a fire broke out, it was less likely to spread to the whole building. The kitchen had a large hearth for cooking, with iron pots, pans and spits for roasting meat. Water and wood had to be carried up many stairs, usually by servants. In wealthy households, the mother of the family managed the kitchen and the servants. Historians know about kitchens from inventories and surviving buildings, including the Palazzo Davanzati, where the top-floor kitchen can still be seen. Even so, poorer families often had no kitchen at all. They cooked on small stoves or bought food from bakers and cookshops. Yet in every home, the preparation of food took up a large part of each day.
===
IMG: family-meal-long-table-bread-wine-jugs | a medieval family eating together at a long wooden table with bread, a pot of soup, roast chicken, cheese and earthenware jugs of wine, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft daylight from a window
HEAD: Wealthy Florentines enjoyed food flavoured with expensive spices, which showed their status.
BODY: Meals in Florence were based on bread, wine and olive oil. Wealthy families also ate meat, fish, cheese, vegetables and fruit. Spices such as pepper, cinnamon, ginger, cloves and saffron were highly valued. They came from distant lands and were very expensive. Using them in cooking showed a family's wealth and good taste. Florentines liked strong flavours, mixing sweet and sour in sauces. Historians know about medieval Italian cooking from recipe collections written in the fourteenth century and from household accounts. Even so, ordinary families ate much more simply. Their meals consisted mostly of bread, soup made with beans or vegetables, and a little wine. Meat was eaten mainly on special days. The Church also set many fast days, when meat was forbidden and fish or eggs were eaten instead. For rich and poor alike, the family meal was an important moment of the day, shared at a long table or around the fire.
===
IMG: abacus-school-boys-counting-beads-teacher | boys sitting at wooden benches learning arithmetic with a counting frame and slates, a teacher in a long gown pointing at numbers, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft window light
HEAD: Sons of Florentine merchants went to special abacus schools to learn arithmetic for business.
BODY: Florence was a city of merchants, and business required good arithmetic. Many boys first learned to read and write in basic schools. Then, at about the age of ten or eleven, many went on to an abacus school. There, they learned to calculate prices, profits, exchange rates and interest. They used Hindu-Arabic numerals, which were becoming common in Italian business, rather than Roman numerals. Teachers used textbooks full of practical problems. According to the chronicler Giovanni Villani, around a thousand to twelve hundred boys attended abacus schools in Florence in the 1330s. Historians use his figures with some caution, but they show how important practical education was. Even so, girls were rarely sent to these schools. Some daughters of rich families learned to read at home. After school, many boys became apprentices in shops or banks. The skills they learned helped make Florence one of the leading business centres of Europe.

TOPIC: A family of wool workers
===
IMG: wool-worker-family-small-room-dawn | a small bare room in an old house with wooden shutters letting in thin grey dawn light, a mother lighting a fire in a little fireplace, children waking in one big shared bed, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling
HEAD: Many families of wool workers in Florence lived in one or two small rooms.
BODY: Thousands of people in Florence worked in the wool industry, washing, combing, spinning and weaving. Most were poor. They lived in crowded neighbourhoods, often on the edges of the city, in small rented rooms. A family of five or six might share two rooms, with a single large bed. Furniture was simple: a table, a bench, a chest and a few pots. Historians know about these homes from tax records, rental agreements and inventories made after a death. Even so, these records describe only part of daily life. Families made the best of their small spaces. They cooked on small stoves or bought food from cookshops. Neighbours helped one another. Children played in the narrow streets. Historians note that wool workers had no guild of their own and could not take part in government. Their wages were low, and work depended on the demand for cloth. Yet they formed a large and important part of the city.
===
IMG: wool-combers-workshop-long-room-morning | men combing wool with heavy iron combs in a long workshop room, soft wool fluff floating in shafts of morning light, a small stove heating the combs, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling
HEAD: Wool combers worked with heavy iron combs to straighten the fibres before spinning.
BODY: Before wool could be spun into thread, it had to be cleaned and prepared. After washing, the fibres were combed or carded to straighten them and remove tangles. Combers used large combs with long iron teeth. The work was hard and repetitive. Many combers worked in workshops owned by wool merchants, who paid them by the piece or by the day. Historians know about the stages of cloth making from the records of the wool guild and from merchants' account books. One account book from the 1330s lists more than a dozen different steps, each done by different workers. Even so, the workers themselves left few records. Their names appear mainly in payment lists and court records. Combers and other wool workers later became famous in Florentine history. In 1378, they led a rebellion known as the Revolt of the Ciompi, demanding the right to have their own guilds and a voice in government.
===
IMG: woman-spinning-wool-window-distaff-spindle | a woman sitting by an open window spinning wool with a distaff tucked under her arm and a spindle turning beside her knee, a neighbour's window very close across a narrow street, soft daylight
HEAD: Women spun wool into thread at home, often working by the window.
BODY: Spinning was usually done by women, often in their own homes. Wool merchants gave them prepared wool and paid them for the thread they produced. This system is called putting out. Women used a distaff to hold the wool and a spindle to twist it into thread. Spinning wheels were becoming more common, but many still worked by hand. They often sat near windows for the light, and in narrow streets, they could talk with neighbours across the way. Historians know about this work from the records of wool merchants, which list payments to spinners, sometimes by name. Even so, spinners were among the lowest paid workers in the industry. Their work was essential, because weaving required huge amounts of thread. Many women combined spinning with caring for children and running the household. Spinning filled the quiet hours of the day, and the steady turning of the spindle was a familiar sight in every working neighbourhood of Florence.
===
IMG: children-fetching-water-fountain-narrow-street | children carrying small clay jugs to a stone fountain in a narrow street, a little dog following them, washing hanging from windows above, warm afternoon light
HEAD: Children in working families in Florence helped with household tasks from an early age.
BODY: In poor and working families, children began to help at a young age. Small children fetched water from public wells and fountains. They carried bread from the baker and ran errands. Older children looked after younger brothers and sisters, helped with spinning or other work and learned a trade from their parents. Many boys became apprentices at around ten or twelve years old. Historians know about children's lives from records of apprenticeships, from court cases and from stories of the period. Even so, children also played. They played with balls, tops and simple toys, and ran through the streets and squares. Religious festivals and processions were exciting events for them. Historians note that childhood was short by modern standards. Many children died young from illness. Yet families loved and cared for their children. Some parents wrote about them in their family books, recording births, names, illnesses and small events of daily life.
===
IMG: family-supper-firelight-small-room-evening | a poor family eating soup and bread together by firelight at a small table in a simple room, the father dusted with wool fluff, children listening to him, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm firelight
HEAD: In the evening, working families in Florence gathered to eat simple meals together.
BODY: When the working day ended, families came home to their small rooms. Supper was usually simple: bread, a soup of beans or vegetables, perhaps a little cheese and some cheap wine mixed with water. Light came from a small oil lamp or the fire. Parents and children talked about the day, and neighbours sometimes visited. On Sundays and feast days, there might be a little meat. Historians know about the diet of ordinary Florentines from records of food prices, wages and charity, and from stories by writers such as Boccaccio and Franco Sacchetti. Even so, life was fragile. A bad harvest, illness or loss of work could bring hunger. Many families depended on charity at times. Confraternities and hospitals gave bread and help to the poor. Yet evenings together, however simple, gave comfort. They were times for rest, for family and for the small pleasures that made hard lives easier to bear.

TOPIC: Prayers, candles and processions
===
IMG: cathedral-under-construction-scaffolding-1347 | a vast half-built cathedral of stone covered in patterns of white, green and pink marble, wrapped in wooden scaffolding with a great wooden treadwheel crane, masons at work, soft morning light
HEAD: In 1347, the great cathedral of Florence was still being built, and it would take more than a century to finish.
BODY: In 1296, the people of Florence began building a new cathedral, dedicated to Santa Maria del Fiore, Saint Mary of the Flower. They wanted it to be one of the largest and most beautiful churches in Christendom. Work went on for many years. By 1347, the walls were rising, and the bell tower designed by the painter Giotto was under construction. Cranes, scaffolding and workshops stood around the building site. The guild of wool merchants was responsible for the work and paid for much of it. Historians know about the building from records of the cathedral works, which still survive in great detail. Even so, progress was slow, and the plans changed many times. The famous dome was completed only in 1436, designed by Filippo Brunelleschi. For nearly a century and a half, building the cathedral was part of daily life in Florence, a project that united generations of citizens in a shared goal.
===
IMG: florence-saint-john-prayer-candles | Florentines kneeling with small candles before a painted image of Saint John in a quiet stone church, soft candlelight
HEAD: The people of Florence believed their patron saint, John the Baptist, protected their city.
BODY: Saint John the Baptist was the patron saint of Florence. His image appeared on the city's gold florin, and his feast on the twenty-fourth of June was the greatest celebration of the year. There were processions, horse races and games, and the whole city was decorated. The Baptistery of San Giovanni, an old octagonal church near the cathedral, was where nearly every child of Florence was baptised. In 1336, the sculptor Andrea Pisano completed a set of bronze doors for it, showing scenes from the life of the saint. Historians know about these celebrations from chronicles and city records. Even so, faith in Florence was not only about grand ceremonies. People prayed to many saints in their daily lives, asking for help with illness, travel and work. They lit candles in churches and gave money to religious causes. In difficult times, they turned especially to their patron saint and to the Virgin Mary for protection.
===
IMG: florence-procession-candles-relics | a long quiet procession of Florentines carrying candles and a painted banner through a narrow stone street at dusk
HEAD: In difficult times, the people of Florence held long religious processions through the streets.
BODY: Processions were an important part of religious life in medieval Florence. Clergy, confraternities, guilds and ordinary citizens walked through the streets together, carrying candles, banners and holy images. They sang hymns and prayed aloud. Processions were held on great feast days, but also in times of danger, such as war, famine or illness. People believed that public prayer could win the help of God and the saints for the whole city. Historians know about processions from chronicles and from the records of confraternities, which describe the order of participants and the routes they took. In 1348, when the great sickness arrived, many processions were held in Florence. Even so, Boccaccio later noted that they did not stop the illness. Historians suggest that large gatherings may even have helped it spread. Yet at the time, processions gave people a sense of shared hope and of doing something together in the face of fear.
===
IMG: florence-herbs-rosemary-vinegar | a woman holding a small bunch of rosemary and a cloth soaked in vinegar to her face in a Florentine street, soft light
HEAD: Many people in fourteenth century Florence carried fragrant herbs, believing they protected against bad air.
BODY: Medieval doctors believed that many diseases were caused by bad air, which they called miasma. They thought that foul smells from rubbish, stagnant water or rotting matter could make people ill. To protect themselves, people carried pleasant smelling things, such as flowers, herbs and spices. Rosemary, juniper, rue and sage were common. Some soaked a cloth in vinegar and held it to their noses. Wealthy people carried small containers of perfume. In their homes, they burned aromatic woods and herbs to purify the air. Historians know about these practices from medical books and from Boccaccio, who described them in the opening of the Decameron. Even so, these methods did not protect against the plague. The real cause, a bacterium carried by fleas and spread between people, was discovered only in 1894. Yet the idea of cleaning the air and removing filth did lead to some useful measures, such as cleaning streets and removing rubbish.
===
IMG: friar-preaching-square-crowd-church | a friar in a plain brown robe tied with a rope preaching to a gathered crowd in front of a large church, people sitting on the steps listening, soft afternoon light
HEAD: Franciscan and Dominican friars preached to large crowds in the squares of Florence.
BODY: In the thirteenth century, two new religious orders spread across Europe: the Franciscans and the Dominicans. Their members, called friars, lived among ordinary people in towns rather than in remote monasteries. In Florence, the Franciscans built the great church of Santa Croce, and the Dominicans built Santa Maria Novella. Both churches had large squares in front of them, where friars preached to crowds. Their sermons were in Italian, not Latin, so everyone could understand. They spoke about faith, morality and daily life, often using stories and examples. Historians know about their preaching from collections of sermons and from chronicles. Even so, the friars were not only preachers. They cared for the sick and the poor, and they ran schools. Some became famous scholars and teachers. Franciscans wore simple brown robes, and Dominicans wore white with black cloaks. Both orders played an important part in the life of the city, especially in times of trouble.
