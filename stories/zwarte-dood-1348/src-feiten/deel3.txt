TOPIC: The spring of 1348
===
IMG: florence-spring-1348-almond-blossom-hills | pink and white almond blossom on trees covering green hills above a medieval city of towers and red roofs, swallows in a soft blue sky, gentle spring morning light
HEAD: The spring of 1348 began in Florence like any other, with blossom on the hills and swallows returning.
BODY: In early 1348, the countryside around Florence turned green again. Almond and fruit trees blossomed on the hills, farmers prepared their fields and swallows returned to nest under the roofs. In the city, shops and workshops were busy, and the cathedral builders continued their work. Historians know from chronicles and letters that people in Florence had heard about the sickness in the ports. Yet daily life went on. Merchants sent their letters, children went to school and families prepared for Easter. Even so, there was unease. City officials were discussing how to protect Florence, and some wealthy families were making plans to leave if necessary. Historians note how ordinary the first weeks of spring must have seemed. No one could know what the coming months would bring. In the gentle weather of early spring, with the bells ringing over the valley, Florence looked much as it had for generations.

IMG: penitent-procession-candles-hymns-street | a long quiet procession of townspeople carrying candles and banners through a narrow medieval street at dusk, singing hymns, people watching from doorways, soft warm candlelight
HEAD: In the spring of 1348, churches in Florence were full, and processions moved through the streets.
BODY: As fear of the sickness grew, many people in Florence turned to prayer. Churches were crowded, and confraternities organised processions. Groups of citizens walked through the streets carrying candles, crosses and holy images, singing hymns and asking for protection. Priests and friars preached about repentance. Historians know about these events from chronicles and from Boccaccio, who described the many prayers and processions in the opening of the Decameron. He noted that they did not stop the sickness. Even so, for the people of the time, these acts of faith were natural and meaningful. They believed that God controlled all things and that sincere prayer could bring mercy. Processions also brought the community together, giving people strength and comfort. Historians suggest that the crowds may have helped the disease spread, but no one understood this then. In a frightening time, these gatherings were a way of facing the danger together.

IMG: apothecary-shop-herbs-jars-rosemary-bundles | a small medieval apothecary shop with shelves of clay jars, bundles of rosemary, juniper and bay leaves hanging from the beams, a shopkeeper wrapping herbs, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm light
HEAD: Doctors in Florence advised people to burn aromatic herbs, eat carefully and avoid fear.
BODY: Medical advice in 1348 was based on ancient theories, especially those of the Greek doctor Galen. Doctors believed that health depended on a balance of fluids in the body, called humours, and that disease could be caused by corrupted air. To protect themselves, people were advised to burn fragrant woods and herbs such as rosemary, juniper and bay in their homes. They were told to eat light food, avoid hard exercise and keep calm. Some doctors believed that fear and sadness made people more likely to fall ill, so they recommended pleasant company, music and cheerful thoughts. Apothecaries sold herbs, spices and medicines. Historians know about this advice from plague treatises, short medical guides that were written in large numbers after 1348. Even so, none of these remedies could cure the plague. Yet some advice, such as avoiding crowds and keeping homes clean, may have helped a little, even though the reasons were not understood.

IMG: lanterns-shrine-street-corner-dusk | a small shrine on a street corner with a painted image of the Madonna, oil lamps glowing and fresh flowers laid below it, a woman kneeling briefly, soft blue dusk light
HEAD: The plague reached Florence in the spring of 1348, probably in March or April.
BODY: Historians are not certain of the exact date when the plague reached Florence. Most sources suggest that it arrived in the early spring of 1348 and spread quickly in April and May. Boccaccio wrote that it began in the spring and grew worse through the summer. The city government had already taken some measures, such as cleaning the streets and controlling travellers from infected places. Even so, the illness spread through the crowded neighbourhoods. Historians know about the epidemic from chronicles, city records, letters and the registers of confraternities and hospitals. These sources show that the epidemic in Florence lasted until the autumn. Historians estimate that perhaps half or more of the population died during 1348, though exact numbers are impossible to know. These events are told here briefly and gently, from a distance, as Boccaccio himself chose to do. His aim, and the aim here, is to remember the people of the time with respect.

IMG: florence-quiet-street-summer-1348-empty | a quiet empty medieval street in summer with closed wooden shutters and a single cat sleeping in a patch of sunlight, swallows overhead, soft hazy light
HEAD: In the summer of 1348, the streets of Florence became unusually quiet.
BODY: As the epidemic continued, daily life in Florence changed. Many shops and workshops closed. Some wealthy families left for their country houses. Others stayed at home and avoided going out. Markets were quieter, and fewer people walked in the squares. Boccaccio described how the normal habits of the city were disrupted, and how people reacted in different ways. Some prayed, some withdrew from the world and some tried to enjoy life while they could. Historians know about these changes from his account and from other records, such as tax registers that show businesses closing. Even so, the city did not stop completely. City officials continued to govern, doctors and priests cared for the sick, and confraternities organised help. Bakers still baked bread, and food was brought in from the countryside. Historians note that many people showed great courage and kindness during these months. The quiet streets of that summer remained in the memory of the city for generations.

IMG: brotherhood-black-robes-carrying-stretcher-street | men in long black robes with hoods walking calmly along a medieval street carrying a covered stretcher, a lantern lit, soft early evening light
HEAD: The Misericordia, a confraternity founded in Florence in 1244, cared for the sick and buried the dead.
BODY: The Confraternity of the Misericordia was founded in Florence in 1244. Its members were ordinary citizens who volunteered to help the sick and the poor. They carried sick people to hospitals and helped bury the dead with dignity. To show that they acted out of charity and not for praise, they wore black robes with hoods that covered their faces. In 1348, the Misericordia and other confraternities played an important role in caring for people during the epidemic. Historians know about their work from the confraternity's own records and from city documents. Many people left gifts to the Misericordia in their wills, which helped it continue its work. Even so, the volunteers faced great risks, and many of them died. Their service showed the strength of community life in Florence. The Misericordia still exists today, more than seven hundred and fifty years later, and its members still help people in need throughout the city.

IMG: hospital-long-hall-beds-nuns-candles | a long hospital hall with rows of simple beds, nuns in plain habits carrying bowls of soup and candles, arched windows and a quiet atmosphere, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft warm light
HEAD: The hospital of Santa Maria Nuova was founded in 1288 by Folco Portinari, the father of Dante's beloved Beatrice.
BODY: Florence had many hospitals in the fourteenth century. Some cared for pilgrims and travellers, others for the poor, orphans or the sick. The largest was Santa Maria Nuova, founded in 1288 by Folco Portinari, a wealthy citizen. His daughter Beatrice was the woman whom the poet Dante loved and made famous in his poetry. The hospital had long halls with rows of beds, cared for by religious brothers and sisters. Doctors visited the patients, and medicines were prepared in its own pharmacy. Historians know about Santa Maria Nuova from its extensive records, which survive in the archives of Florence. In 1348, the hospital was overwhelmed with patients, but its staff continued their work. Even so, medieval hospitals could do little against the plague. Yet they provided care, food and comfort. Santa Maria Nuova continued to grow over the centuries and is still a working hospital in the centre of Florence today.

IMG: unfinished-chronicle-page-quill-resting | an old handwritten book open on a wooden desk with a small empty space left at the end of a line of writing, a quill resting beside it, a window with soft summer light
HEAD: The chronicler Giovanni Villani left a blank space in his book for the date when the epidemic would end.
BODY: Giovanni Villani was writing his chronicle when the plague reached Florence. In one of his last passages, he described the epidemic and wrote that it would last until, followed by a blank space. He planned to add the date when the sickness ended. He died during the epidemic in 1348, and the space was never filled. Historians often mention this unfinished sentence as a quiet and moving reminder of the year. It shows how suddenly lives could be interrupted. His brother Matteo Villani continued the chronicle and described the plague in more detail. Matteo also wrote about how society changed afterwards. Historians value both brothers' work highly. Even so, they note that chronicles reflect the views of their authors, who were wealthy merchants. Other sources, such as tax records and wills, show the experiences of ordinary people. Together, these documents allow historians to understand how Florence lived through 1348 and the years that followed.

TOPIC: How people got through it
===
IMG: empty-piazza-noon-heat-pigeons | a large empty medieval square at noon in the summer heat, pigeons resting by a fountain, closed shutters and a half-built cathedral with still cranes, soft hazy light
HEAD: During the summer of 1348, work stopped on many buildings and in many workshops in Florence.
BODY: As the epidemic reached its height in the summer of 1348, much of the work in Florence came to a halt. Records of the cathedral building show that construction slowed or stopped for a time. Many wool workshops closed, because workers were ill or had left. The looms that normally clattered from morning until evening fell silent. Historians know about these interruptions from account books, which show gaps in payments and work. Even so, some essential activities continued. Food was brought into the city, and officials tried to maintain order. Historians note that, in the following years, labour became scarce because so many workers had died. This led to higher wages and changes in how work was organised. For those who lived through the summer of 1348, however, the silent workshops and empty squares were a strange and sad sight in a city that had always been so busy.

IMG: courtyard-lute-player-quiet-family-evening | a small family sitting quietly together in a house courtyard in the evening, an older man playing a lute, a woman sewing, a child listening, a lemon tree in a pot, soft warm light
HEAD: Some families in Florence shut themselves away and lived quietly, with simple food and music.
BODY: Boccaccio described several ways in which people tried to protect themselves during the epidemic. Some families decided to live apart from others. They shut themselves in their houses, ate moderately, drank good wine and avoided news of the sickness. They spent their time with music and pleasant conversation. Others believed in enjoying life as much as possible. Many tried a middle way, going out but carrying flowers and herbs to protect against bad air. Historians use Boccaccio's description to understand the range of responses. Medical advice of the time also recommended a calm and cheerful mind. Even so, no method guaranteed safety. Historians note that isolation may sometimes have helped, by reducing contact with infected people or fleas. Yet families who stayed together also cared for each other. The image of a family gathered quietly in a courtyard, listening to music in the evening, reflects one way people found comfort and courage in a frightening time.

IMG: neighbour-leaving-bread-basket-doorstep | a woman leaving a small basket of bread and vegetables on a neighbour's doorstep in a narrow medieval street, a window shutter half open above, soft morning light
HEAD: Many people in Florence helped their neighbours during the epidemic, bringing food and caring for the sick.
BODY: Boccaccio's account of the epidemic is often remembered for its descriptions of fear and abandonment. Yet other sources show that many people behaved with great kindness. Neighbours brought food to families who were ill. Relatives took in orphaned children. Priests, doctors, nurses and volunteers continued their work, often at great risk. Historians have found evidence of this help in wills, where people left money or property to those who had cared for them, and in the records of confraternities and hospitals. Some wills mention neighbours and friends by name, thanking them for their help. Even so, the epidemic placed enormous strain on families and communities. Historians emphasise that both fear and courage were part of the experience. In difficult times, ordinary people found ways to support one another. Small acts, such as leaving bread on a doorstep or watching over a neighbour's children, helped many families survive the hardest months of 1348.

IMG: writer-watching-from-window-empty-square | a big round-faced man standing quietly at an open window looking down at an empty medieval square, a notebook in his hand, soft late afternoon light
HEAD: Boccaccio decided to write about the epidemic, so that future generations would remember it.
BODY: Boccaccio lived through the epidemic and saw how it changed his city. A few years later, around 1349 to 1353, he wrote the Decameron. In its introduction, he described the plague in Florence, explaining what he had seen and heard. He said that he wanted to record these events so that people would remember them. He also explained that the sad description would be followed by pleasant stories, like a traveller climbing a steep hill to reach a beautiful plain. Historians consider his account one of the most important descriptions of the Black Death in Europe. Literary scholars admire the way he combined sorrow and hope in one book. Even so, Boccaccio was not writing a scientific report. He was a storyteller, and his account was shaped to move readers. Yet it remains a powerful testimony. Through his words, the experience of ordinary people in Florence in 1348 has been remembered for nearly seven hundred years.

IMG: italian-hill-village-quiet-dusk-church | a small stone hill village in Italy at dusk with a church bell tower, a few lit windows, olive trees on the slopes below, soft purple and gold light
HEAD: By the autumn of 1348, the epidemic in Florence began to fade.
BODY: The epidemic in Florence lasted through the spring and summer of 1348. With the cooler weather of autumn, it gradually declined. Historians believe that the plague often weakened in colder months, partly because fleas were less active. By the end of the year, the worst was over in Florence, though the sickness continued in other parts of Europe. People began to return to the city, and shops slowly reopened. Historians know about this recovery from records of taxes, rents and business activity, which began to increase again. Even so, the city had changed. Many families had lost members, and some had disappeared entirely. Houses stood empty, and property passed to new owners. Historians estimate that the population of Florence did not return to its earlier size for several centuries. Yet life continued. In the hill villages around the city, church bells rang again for weddings, harvests and feast days, as they had before.

IMG: english-village-thatched-cottages-church-summer | a small English village of thatched cottages around a stone church, green fields divided into long strips, sheep on the downs, soft summer evening light
HEAD: In England, manor courts recorded who inherited land after the epidemic.
BODY: The plague reached England in the summer of 1348, arriving first in the southwest, and spread across the country in the following year. Much of what historians know about its effects in England comes from manor court records. These were kept by the lords of rural estates and recorded the business of each village. When a tenant died, the court recorded who inherited his land, animals and goods, and what payments were made to the lord. After 1348, these records show large numbers of deaths and many changes in land ownership. Historians have studied thousands of such records, written in Latin by village clerks. They reveal the experiences of ordinary villagers in great detail. Even so, interpreting them requires care, since not everyone appears in the records. Yet they show that village life continued. Land was quickly taken over by heirs or new tenants, fields were farmed again and communities slowly recovered.

IMG: norwegian-fjord-wooden-church-forest-mist | a small dark wooden church among pine trees beside a calm misty fjord with steep green mountains, soft grey morning light
HEAD: In 1349, the plague reached Norway, reportedly on a ship from England that arrived in Bergen.
BODY: From England, the plague spread across the North Sea. According to Icelandic and Norwegian sources, a ship from England arrived in Bergen, on the west coast of Norway, in the summer of 1349. Soon afterwards, the sickness spread through the town and into the surrounding countryside. Norway was a land of scattered farms and small communities along fjords and valleys. Even so, the plague reached many of them, carried along trade and travel routes. Historians know about the epidemic in Norway from later chronicles and from records showing that many farms were abandoned. Some remained empty for a long time. Historians debate how many people died, since records are incomplete. Yet it is clear that the effects were serious and long-lasting. In the following centuries, Norway's population and economy recovered slowly. The quiet wooden churches and farms along its fjords became symbols of a land that rebuilt itself after hard times.

IMG: milan-city-walls-towers-quiet-morning | a great medieval city with tall brick walls, towers and a castle, quiet roads leading to closed gates, green plains around it, soft morning light
HEAD: A few places in Europe suffered much less from the plague than others.
BODY: Not all regions were affected equally by the Black Death. Some cities and areas seem to have suffered fewer deaths. Milan, in northern Italy, is often mentioned as an example. According to some accounts, its ruler acted quickly when the sickness appeared, isolating infected houses. Historians debate how effective these measures were and whether the reports are accurate. Some regions may have escaped partly because of their isolation, or simply by chance. Parts of Poland and the Low Countries are also sometimes said to have suffered less, though recent research has questioned this. Historians use tax records, wills and archaeological evidence to compare different places. Even so, the evidence is often incomplete. Yet the uneven pattern of the epidemic shows that local conditions mattered. Trade routes, population density, climate and the actions of local authorities all played a role in how the sickness spread and how communities experienced it.

IMG: paris-scholars-gowns-meeting-hall | learned doctors in long dark gowns and caps gathered around a table covered with books and star charts in a stone hall, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft window light
HEAD: In 1348, the medical faculty of Paris explained the plague as the result of a rare meeting of planets.
BODY: In October 1348, the King of France asked the medical faculty of the University of Paris to explain the cause of the epidemic. The doctors wrote a report, which became widely known. They suggested that a conjunction of the planets Saturn, Jupiter and Mars, in 1345, had corrupted the air. They believed that this corrupted air spread disease. They also gave advice on diet, cleanliness and avoiding bad air. Historians consider this report an important document of medieval science. It shows how learned people tried to understand the disaster using the knowledge available to them. Even so, the explanation was wrong. The real cause, the plague bacterium, was not discovered until 1894. Yet the doctors' efforts reflected a serious attempt to find natural causes and practical remedies. Historians note that the experience of the plague led to many new medical writings and helped encourage the study of public health in the centuries that followed.

TOPIC: Ten young people and a hundred stories
===
IMG: santa-maria-novella-church-morning-young-women | seven young women in long medieval dresses and veils talking quietly together inside a great church with tall striped arches, morning light falling through high windows, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls
HEAD: The Decameron begins with seven young women meeting in the church of Santa Maria Novella in Florence.
BODY: In Boccaccio's book, the story begins on a Tuesday morning in the church of Santa Maria Novella, the great Dominican church of Florence. Seven young women, friends and relatives, meet there after attending Mass. They talk about the sad state of the city and decide to leave for the countryside for a while. They invite three young men to join them. The group of ten agrees to stay together in country villas, where they will pass the time pleasantly and honourably. Literary scholars note that Boccaccio gave each character a name with a meaning, and some may refer to real people he knew. Even so, the group is fictional. Boccaccio used them as a frame for his collection of stories. Santa Maria Novella still stands today near the main railway station of Florence. Its beautiful marble front was completed in the fifteenth century, and inside are famous paintings by Giotto, Masaccio and Ghirlandaio.
===
IMG: villa-hilltop-gardens-fountains-roses-morning | a beautiful country villa on a small hill surrounded by green trees, flower gardens, fountains and meadows, rose bushes and jasmine, soft golden morning light
HEAD: Boccaccio described the country villas of his story as places of cool wells, beautiful rooms and flowers.
BODY: The ten young people in the Decameron leave Florence and travel a short distance into the hills. They stay in beautiful villas surrounded by gardens. Boccaccio describes these places with great affection: wide courtyards, cool wells, fine rooms decorated with flowers, and gardens full of roses, jasmine and orange trees. There are lawns, fountains and views across the countryside. Literary scholars believe that Boccaccio may have had real villas near Florence in mind. Some local traditions name particular houses, such as the Villa Palmieri near Fiesole, but there is no certain evidence. Even so, his descriptions reflect the real country houses of wealthy Florentines. Historians use them as evidence of how such villas looked and how their owners enjoyed them. The gardens in the Decameron are also symbolic. They represent order, beauty and calm, in contrast to the troubled city. They offer the characters, and readers, a peaceful place to rest.

IMG: young-people-garden-table-white-linen-fountain | young people in medieval dress eating together at a table set with white linen beside a stone fountain in a garden, wine jugs and bowls of fruit, roses climbing a wall, soft midday light
HEAD: In the Decameron, one of the young people is chosen each day to be king or queen and to organise the day.
BODY: The ten young people in the Decameron agree on a simple set of rules for their stay in the countryside. Each day, one of them is chosen as king or queen. That person decides the plans for the day, including meals, walks and games. They also choose the theme for the day's stories. The days follow a peaceful pattern. In the morning, the group walks in the gardens. They eat together at tables set with white linen near fountains. In the hottest part of the day, they rest. Literary scholars note that this structure gives the book a sense of order and harmony. The young people treat each other with courtesy and respect. Even so, the stories they tell are often lively, funny and sometimes daring. Historians use the Decameron to understand ideas about manners, leisure and social life in fourteenth century Florence. The orderly days of the group stand in quiet contrast to the disorder of the city.

IMG: young-people-sitting-circle-meadow-shade | ten young people in medieval clothes sitting in a circle on soft green grass in the shade of tall trees, one of them telling a story with a gentle gesture, dappled afternoon light
HEAD: Each afternoon in the Decameron, the ten young people each tell one story, making one hundred stories in ten days.
BODY: In the afternoon, when the heat of the day begins to ease, the ten young people sit together in a shady meadow. Each of them tells one story on the theme chosen for the day. After ten days, there are one hundred stories in total. This gives the book its name, Decameron, from Greek words meaning ten days. The themes include stories of good fortune, of clever replies, of love with happy or sad endings, and of tricks played by wives and husbands. The stories are set in many places, from Florence and Naples to Paris, Egypt and the Holy Land. Literary scholars consider the Decameron one of the founding works of European prose. Its stories were translated and adapted by many later writers, including Geoffrey Chaucer and William Shakespeare. Even so, some stories were considered shocking in later centuries and were sometimes censored. Yet the book remained popular for its humour and its rich picture of human life.

IMG: poor-nobleman-falcon-on-glove-country-farm | a young man in a simple worn tunic holding a beautiful hunting falcon on his gloved hand outside a small humble farmhouse in the countryside, soft golden morning light
HEAD: One of the most famous stories in the Decameron tells of a poor young man and his falcon.
BODY: On the fifth day of the Decameron, one story tells of Federigo degli Alberighi, a young nobleman of Florence. He falls in love with a lady named Monna Giovanna and spends all his fortune trying to win her heart. She does not return his love. Federigo becomes poor and moves to a small farm in the countryside. All he has left is his beloved falcon. One day, the lady visits him unexpectedly. Federigo has nothing else to offer her for dinner, so he cooks his falcon to honour her. Then he learns that she had come to ask him for the falcon, for her sick son. Literary scholars consider this one of the finest stories in the book. It shows generosity, love and dignity in poverty. Even so, the story is gentle in tone and ends happily, when the lady later marries Federigo. Readers have admired it for centuries, and it has been retold in many forms.

HEAD: Another story in the Decameron tells of a clever cook who claimed that cranes have only one leg.
BODY: On the sixth day of the Decameron, the theme is clever replies that get people out of trouble. In one story, a Venetian cook named Chichibio works for a nobleman in Florence. His master has hunted a crane and asks him to roast it. A young woman Chichibio likes asks him for one of its legs, and he gives it to her. When the master asks where the other leg is, Chichibio claims that cranes have only one leg. The next morning, the master takes him to a river to see living cranes. They are standing on one leg, as cranes do. When the master shouts, the cranes put down their second legs and fly away. Chichibio quickly replies that the master did not shout at the roasted crane at dinner. The master laughs and forgives him. Literary scholars enjoy this story for its humour and quick wit. It shows the lighter side of the Decameron, which balances the sadness of its opening.

IMG: walking-down-hill-toward-florence-evening | a small group of young people in medieval clothes walking down a country lane between cypress trees toward a city of towers in the valley, soft golden evening light
HEAD: At the end of the Decameron, the ten young people return to Florence after two weeks away.
BODY: After ten days of storytelling, and a few rest days, the young people decide to return to Florence. They have spent about two weeks in the countryside. They feel refreshed and stronger, and they worry that staying away too long might cause gossip. So they walk back to the city together and part at the church of Santa Maria Novella, where their journey began. Literary scholars see this ending as meaningful. The group leaves a troubled city, finds order and joy in storytelling and returns with renewed strength. The book suggests that stories, friendship and laughter can help people through hard times. Even so, Boccaccio does not pretend that the troubles have disappeared. The city they return to is still marked by the epidemic. Yet the Decameron ends on a note of hope and continuity. Many readers have found comfort in it, especially during difficult times, and it has never been out of print since printing began.

TOPIC: After the storm, and Florence today
===
IMG: florence-spring-1349-workshops-reopening | a medieval street in spring with shopkeepers opening their wooden shutters again, a weaver at his loom visible through a doorway, flowers in window boxes, soft hopeful morning light
HEAD: In the spring of 1349, workshops in Florence began to reopen, and life slowly returned to normal.
BODY: After the epidemic ended, Florence began to recover. In 1349, shops and workshops reopened, and the sounds of work returned to the streets. The wool industry started again, though there were fewer workers than before. Building resumed on the cathedral and other projects. Historians know about this recovery from account books, tax records and the records of guilds. Matteo Villani, who continued his brother's chronicle, described how the city changed after the plague. He noted that people who had survived often had more money, because they had inherited property from relatives. Some spent freely on clothing, food and entertainment. Even so, the recovery was not simple. Prices rose, and there were shortages of workers in many trades. The city government passed new laws to control wages and prices. Yet Florence remained a great city. Within a few decades, it would become the centre of the Renaissance, one of the most creative periods in European history.
===
IMG: medieval-wedding-procession-musicians-flowers | a joyful wedding procession through a medieval street with musicians playing pipes and drums, a bride in a long dress with flowers in her hair, neighbours smiling from windows, warm sunny light
HEAD: In the years after the plague, there were many weddings in Florence and across Europe.
BODY: After the great epidemic, many people who had lost husbands or wives married again, and many young people married earlier than usual. Historians have found evidence of a sharp increase in marriages in many places in the years after 1348. Matteo Villani described how weddings became common in Florence. Weddings were celebrated with processions through the streets, music, flowers and feasts. The bride was accompanied to her new home by family and friends, and gifts, such as painted chests, were carried in the procession. Historians know about these customs from marriage contracts, account books and paintings. Even so, the number of births did not immediately replace the population that had been lost. The plague returned several times in the following decades. Yet the many weddings reflected a desire to rebuild families and look to the future. For many communities, they were a sign of hope and a way of beginning again after a time of sorrow.
===
IMG: english-peasants-harvest-field-better-times | farm workers harvesting wheat in a sunny field and sharing bread and cheese at the edge of the field, a village and church in the distance, warm golden afternoon light
HEAD: Because there were fewer workers after the plague, many ordinary people could ask for higher wages.
BODY: The Black Death killed so many people that there were far fewer workers in towns and in the countryside. Landowners and employers needed labourers to farm their land, harvest crops and work in workshops. Workers were able to demand higher wages and better conditions. Historians know about this change from records of wages and prices across Europe. In England, the government tried to stop wages from rising by passing the Statute of Labourers in 1351. Similar laws were passed in other countries, including in Florence. Even so, these laws were difficult to enforce. Over time, many ordinary people saw their living standards improve. They ate better, with more meat and ale, and some acquired more land. Historians debate how widely these benefits were shared. Yet the period after the Black Death brought important changes in the relationship between workers and employers, which shaped European society for centuries.

IMG: painter-fresco-scaffold-church-wall | a painter on a wooden scaffold painting a large colourful fresco of saints and angels on a church wall, an assistant mixing paints below, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft window light
HEAD: After the plague, the people of Florence paid for paintings, chapels and religious works of art.
BODY: In the years after the epidemic, many people in Florence gave money to churches, confraternities and religious orders. Some did this to give thanks for surviving. Others left money in their wills for prayers, chapels and paintings. As a result, artists received many commissions. Historians and art historians have studied the art of this period closely. Some scholars once argued that art after the Black Death became darker and more serious. Later research has questioned this idea, showing that styles varied and that many changes had begun earlier. Even so, the plague clearly affected how people thought about life, death and faith. In Florence, painters such as Andrea Orcagna and his brothers created important works in the following decades. The tabernacle Orcagna made for the Madonna of Orsanmichele, completed in 1359, was paid for partly by gifts made during and after the epidemic. It can still be seen in Orsanmichele today.

HEAD: Guy de Chauliac, the surgeon to the Pope, wrote a great book of surgery that was used for centuries.
BODY: Guy de Chauliac survived the epidemic of 1348 in Avignon, where he cared for the sick and treated the Pope. He also fell ill during a later outbreak and recovered. In 1363, he completed a large book on surgery, called the Chirurgia Magna, or Great Surgery. In it, he described medical knowledge from ancient and Arabic sources, as well as his own experience. He also wrote one of the most detailed descriptions of the plague, distinguishing between different forms of the disease. Historians consider his book one of the most important medical texts of the Middle Ages. It was copied, translated and printed many times, and doctors used it for more than two hundred years. Even so, his understanding of the plague was limited by the knowledge of his time. Yet his careful observation and honest reporting were valuable. He admitted that doctors could do little against the sickness and described his own fear with honesty.

HEAD: The plague returned several times in later centuries, but each time communities rebuilt.
BODY: The epidemic of 1348 was not the last. The plague returned to Europe many times, in 1361, 1374 and in later decades and centuries. Each outbreak caused new deaths, though usually fewer than the first. Historians call this the second plague pandemic, which lasted until the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries in parts of Europe. Over time, cities developed better ways of responding, including health boards, quarantine and isolation hospitals. Historians know about these outbreaks from city records, medical writings and chronicles. Even so, between epidemics there were long periods of recovery and growth. In Tuscany, farms were cultivated again, villages repopulated and cities flourished. The fifteenth century in Florence was a time of great artistic and intellectual achievement. Historians note how remarkable it is that societies continued to rebuild despite repeated disasters. The calm Tuscan landscape of farms, vineyards and olive groves reflects centuries of patient work and renewal.

HEAD: After the plague, many Florentines left money in their wills to hospitals, confraternities and the poor.
BODY: During and after the epidemic of 1348, many people in Florence made wills. Historians have studied thousands of these documents. They show that people left large sums of money to hospitals, confraternities, churches and the poor. The Confraternity of Orsanmichele, which cared for the image of the Madonna, received an enormous amount in gifts in 1348. It used the money to help people in need and to build the new tabernacle by Orcagna. Hospitals such as Santa Maria Nuova also received many bequests, which helped them grow. Historians see these gifts as evidence of both religious faith and concern for the community. Even so, managing such large sums caused some disputes and problems. Yet the generosity of ordinary citizens helped Florence care for its sick and poor in the following decades. Many of the charitable institutions that benefited from these gifts continued their work for centuries, and some still exist today.

IMG: boccaccio-older-certaldo-study-books | an older heavy-set man with kind eyes reading at a desk in a small study with a window looking out over hills, books and manuscripts piled around him, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft afternoon light
HEAD: Boccaccio became a close friend of Petrarch and gave the first public lectures on Dante.
BODY: In 1350, Boccaccio met Petrarch in Florence, and the two writers became close friends. They exchanged many letters and shared a love of ancient literature. Influenced by Petrarch, Boccaccio devoted much of his later life to scholarship. He wrote works in Latin about famous women, about the gods of ancient myth and about geography. He also helped bring knowledge of ancient Greek to Florence. Boccaccio greatly admired Dante. He wrote a biography of Dante and copied his works. In 1373, the city of Florence asked him to give public lectures on Dante's Divine Comedy, the first such lectures ever held. He gave them in the church of Santo Stefano di Badia until illness forced him to stop. Historians and literary scholars see Boccaccio as one of the three great writers of fourteenth century Italy, together with Dante and Petrarch. He died in Certaldo in 1375, a year after his friend Petrarch.

IMG: florence-health-officials-clean-street | officials in long robes watching workers sweep and wash a narrow stone street in Florence, buckets and brooms, morning light
HEAD: In the spring of 1348, the government of Florence appointed eight citizens to protect the health of the city.
BODY: In April 1348, as the epidemic began, the government of Florence appointed a special committee of eight citizens. Their task was to protect public health. They ordered the cleaning of streets and the removal of rubbish and dead animals. They controlled the sale of certain goods and limited the movement of people from infected places. They also organised burials. Historians know about these measures from the records of the city government. Similar committees were created in other Italian cities, including Venice. Even so, these measures could not stop the epidemic, because the true cause of the disease was unknown. Yet they were an important step in the history of public health. In the following decades, Florence and other cities created permanent health boards. Historians see these developments as the beginnings of modern public health systems. The idea that a city government should protect the health of its citizens grew partly from the experience of 1348.

IMG: volunteer-ambulance-cathedral-square-today | the doorway of an old building on a cathedral square with volunteers in simple uniforms talking with passers-by, the great striped marble cathedral beside them, soft morning light
HEAD: The Misericordia of Florence still helps people today, more than seven hundred and fifty years after it was founded.
BODY: The Confraternity of the Misericordia, founded in 1244, is still active in Florence today. Its headquarters stand on the Piazza del Duomo, opposite the cathedral and the Baptistery. Its members are volunteers who provide ambulance services, help the elderly and the sick, and support people in need. For many centuries, they wore black robes and hoods, like their medieval predecessors. Today, they wear modern uniforms for most of their work, but traditions continue. Historians consider the Misericordia one of the oldest charitable organisations in the world still operating. Its archives contain records going back many centuries, including documents from the time of the Black Death. Even so, its work is not only historical. Every day, its volunteers answer calls for help across the city. Similar organisations exist in many other Italian towns. The continuity of the Misericordia shows how the charitable spirit of medieval Florence has survived into the present.

HEAD: The State Archives of Florence preserve thousands of documents that show how ordinary people lived through 1348.
BODY: The Archivio di Stato di Firenze, the State Archives of Florence, is one of the richest historical archives in the world. It holds millions of documents from the Middle Ages onward, including government records, tax registers, court cases, wills, contracts and the papers of guilds and confraternities. Historians come from many countries to study them. For the year 1348, these records allow historians to trace the epidemic and its effects in remarkable detail. They show who paid taxes, who died, who inherited property and how businesses changed. Historians such as Samuel Cohn and Ann Carmichael have used them to study the plague in Florence. Even so, many questions remain. Records do not always say how people felt or what daily life was like. Yet they preserve the names and traces of thousands of ordinary men and women. In the quiet reading rooms of the archive, the people of medieval Florence are still remembered.
