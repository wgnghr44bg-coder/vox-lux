BODY: The churches were full that spring. People went to Mass every day, and to confession. They prayed more often and more earnestly than ever before. The friars led processions through the streets. They carried crosses, banners and holy relics. Long lines of people followed them, singing hymns and praying for the city to be spared. At Orsanmichele, people crowded in front of the golden Madonna. They lit candles, left gifts of money and asked her to protect their families. So much money was given there in those months that the society which cared for the painting became one of the richest in Florence. Later, with that money, they built a beautiful marble shrine around the painting. It is still there today. Can you picture the evenings that spring? Thousands of small candle flames in the dusk, and quiet voices singing together, all over the city. Fear, yes. But also a great deal of hope, held together.

BODY: The government of Florence did what it could. It ordered the streets cleaned and the drains cleared. It forbade the sale of rotten meat and fish. It stopped travellers from Pisa and Genoa from entering the city. The doctors of the city met to discuss the sickness. They wrote advice for the people. Eat simple food, they said. Avoid bad air. Burn sweet-smelling herbs and woods in your houses, like juniper, rosemary and bay. Carry a little bunch of flowers or herbs to hold to your nose when you go out. And keep calm and cheerful, they said, because fear itself makes people ill. Much of their advice could not stop the sickness. They simply did not know what caused it. But some of it, like cleaning the streets, may have helped a little. And the advice to stay calm was kind. The apothecary shops did a busy trade that spring. The whole city smelled faintly of rosemary.

In front of the little shrines on the street corners, the lamps burned. There were more flowers than usual, and more candles. People stopped, knelt and prayed. Then they hurried home and closed their doors. And the river flowed on beneath the bridges, as it always had.

BODY: In the spring and summer of 1348, the great sickness spread through Florence. Later, people would call it the Black Death, or the Great Mortality. At the time, they simply called it the pestilence. We will not tell this part of the story in great detail. It was a very sad time, and much has been written about it. We will tell it gently, and from a little distance, as Boccaccio himself did. The sickness came quickly. A person might feel well in the morning and ill by the afternoon, with a fever and a great tiredness. Some people recovered, after many days. But many did not. It seemed to strike rich and poor, young and old, without any reason anyone could see. In some houses, everyone fell ill. In others, nobody did. Nobody understood it, and nobody knew how to cure it. And so the city grew quieter, and the long summer days went slowly by.

BODY: In Florence, there was an old society called the Misericordia, which means mercy. It had been founded about a hundred years earlier by a group of porters in the wool trade. They had decided to help anyone in need. Its members wore long black robes, with black hoods over their faces. That way, nobody would know who they were. They did not want to be thanked or praised for their good works. In the summer of 1348, the brothers went out into the streets, day after day. They carried the sick to the hospitals on stretchers and cared for them. They helped bury those who had died, with prayers and with dignity. Many of the brothers fell ill themselves. But others stepped forward and took their places. Think of that. Ordinary porters and craftsmen, choosing to walk toward the danger, hidden under hoods so that nobody would even know their names. The Misericordia is still working in Florence today.

BODY: The hospitals of Florence were full that summer. The greatest of them was the hospital of Santa Maria Nuova. It had been founded long before by a rich man named Folco Portinari. He was the father of Beatrice, the lady whom the poet Dante had loved. The hospital had long halls full of beds. Nuns, lay brothers and servants nursed the sick. They washed them and fed them soup. They sat with them and prayed with them. In that hard summer, they worked without rest. Doctors and priests and friars did their best too. Many of them stayed with the sick, even when they could have left. And the people of Florence, rich and poor, gave the hospitals money and gifts. After the sickness had passed, Santa Maria Nuova was one of the richest hospitals in Europe. And here is a lovely thing. Santa Maria Nuova is still a hospital today, in the same place, caring for the sick, nearly seven hundred years later.

He died in the summer of 1348, and the space in his book is still empty today. His brother Matteo picked up the pen. He went on writing the chronicle where Giovanni had left off. Later, Matteo's son carried it on too. Together, the three Villanis wrote one of the greatest histories of any medieval city. Isn't that a quiet, beautiful thing? A family passing a pen from hand to hand, so that the story of their city would not stop. That small blank space has become one of the most famous gaps in all of history.

BODY: By the middle of the summer of 1348, Florence had become strangely quiet. The markets were nearly empty. Many shops were closed. The looms of the wool workers were silent. The masons had stopped working on the cathedral, and the great wooden cranes stood still above the half-built walls. The streets, once so crowded, were almost deserted, especially in the heat of the afternoon. Pigeons dozed by the fountains. A dog trotted across an empty square. The bells still rang the hours, but fewer people were listening. Many people had left. Those who could afford it had gone to their villas and farms in the countryside. The air was fresher there, and there were fewer people. Others went to stay with relatives in other towns. The government had almost stopped meeting, because so many of its members had gone. Yet the city did not stop completely. Bakers still baked. Water still flowed from the fountains. Somehow, life went on.

BODY: Those who stayed in the city tried to live as best they could. Boccaccio later described how different people chose different ways. Some shut themselves up in their houses with a few friends and family. They lived very quietly. They ate simple food and drank a little good wine. They played music and talked about pleasant things, trying not to think about what was happening outside. Others did the opposite. They decided that, since life might be short, they would enjoy it while they could. They went from tavern to tavern, eating, drinking and singing. But most people simply went on, day by day. They did what needed to be done. They looked after their families and their neighbours. They went to church, and they hoped. That is often how it is, in hard times. The great stories are about heroes and villains. But most people are simply getting through each day, as kindly as they can.

BODY: In the old stories about the plague, people often remember the fear. But there was also a great deal of kindness. Neighbours left bread and soup on each other's doorsteps. They called up to each other's windows to ask how everyone was. They looked after each other's children when parents fell ill. Priests and friars visited the sick and sat with them. Women nursed strangers. Notaries went from house to house, writing down people's wills, so that their families would be looked after. Wills from that summer still survive in the archives of Florence. Many of them leave money to the poor, to hospitals and to orphans. In one way or another, many people were thinking of others, even in their own hardest hours. We should remember that, too. In the darkest times, there are always people who quietly keep the lamps lit for others.

BODY: All through that summer, it seems, Boccaccio stayed in Florence. He saw the empty streets, the closed shops and the shuttered houses. He saw who had fled the city and who had stayed. He saw great kindness and great courage, and he also saw great fear. He saw how some people helped their neighbours, while others shut themselves away. And he made a decision. One day, he would write it all down, honestly, so people would know what it had been like. But he would also write something else. He would write a book of stories, full of life, laughter and love. It would be a kind of answer to the sorrow. Isn't that a beautiful idea? When the world is very sad, you can describe the sadness. But you can also light a small fire of stories, for people to warm their hands by. Boccaccio chose to do both. And his book would be read for the next seven hundred years.

All over Italy, the same thing happened, in city after city and town after town. It happened in Venice and Genoa, Siena and Pisa, and in little villages high in the mountains. In Siena, people had been building a cathedral even larger than the one in Florence. Afterwards, there were not enough people or money to finish it. BODY: The sickness did not stop at Italy. In the summer of 1348, it crossed the Alps into France, Germany and Switzerland. In June, a ship brought it to a little port called Melcombe, on the south coast of England. Nobody in Melcombe paid much attention to the ship. Ships came in every week. From there, the sickness spread slowly along the coast and inland. By autumn, it had reached Bristol, Oxford and London. England in 1348 was a land of villages. Most people lived in small groups of thatched cottages, around a church and a manor house. Great open fields stretched around them, divided into long strips. Many of these records survive today. From them, historians can follow what happened, one village at a time. They also show something else: the fields were planted again the next spring.

BODY: In 1349, a ship from England sailed into the harbour of Bergen, in Norway. It carried wool, and a few sailors. The sickness spread from there across Norway. Historians believe that between a third and a half of all the people in Europe were lost in those few years. And yet, in every place, people picked themselves up and began again. They had to. There were fields to plant, and children to raise.

BODY: A few places in Europe were spared, or almost spared. Some were very remote, like small villages high in the mountains, far from any road. Others may simply have been lucky. One of them, people say, was the great city of Milan, in the north of Italy. Its ruler gave a very strict order. If the sickness appeared in a house, that house was to be closed off from the rest of the city at once. Historians still argue about how much this helped. But Milan suffered far less than most cities that year. Some small regions of Poland and the Pyrenees mountains also seem to have escaped. Nobody at the time knew why. It must have felt like a mystery, or a blessing. Today, we understand more. Distance helped, and luck helped, and so did keeping people apart. Those lessons, learned so painfully, would be remembered and used again for hundreds of years.

BODY: In Paris, the King of France asked the doctors of the great university to explain the sickness. They met in their gowns and caps and talked for many days. This, they said, had poisoned the air. They advised people to avoid bad air and burn fragrant woods. They said to eat light food and avoid hot baths and too much exercise. Today it sounds strange. Bit by bit, over hundreds of years, doctors learned more. Much later, in 1894, a scientist finally saw the tiny bacteria under a microscope. The mystery that had puzzled the doctors of Paris was solved at last.

TOPIC: Ten young people and a hundred stories
BRIDGE: Now let's return to Boccaccio, who has begun to write. His book opens in a church in Florence, on a Tuesday morning in that long summer.

IMG: santa-maria-novella-church-morning-young-women | seven young women in long medieval dresses and veils talking quietly together inside a great church with tall striped arches, morning light falling through high windows, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls
HEAD: Boccaccio's most famous book begins with seven young women meeting by chance in a church.
BODY: A few years after the plague, Boccaccio began the book that would make him famous. He called it the Decameron, which means ten days, in Greek. It is a book of a hundred stories. It begins with Florence in the summer of 1348. In the first pages, Boccaccio describes the sickness in the city, honestly and simply. Then he tells how, one Tuesday morning, seven young women met by chance in the great church of Santa Maria Novella. They were friends, all between eighteen and twenty-eight years old. They were sad and lonely, because so many of their family and friends were gone. The eldest, Pampinea, had an idea. Why stay in the city, among so much sadness? Why not go out to one of their families' villas in the countryside? They could live quietly there together for a while, with music, good food and good company. Just then, three young men they knew came into the church. They gladly agreed to come too.

BODY: The next morning at dawn, the ten young people left Florence with a few servants. They walked out through the gates and up into the hills, to a beautiful villa about two miles away. It had a courtyard with loggias, galleries and fine rooms, all decorated with cheerful paintings. Around it lay meadows and wonderful gardens. There were wells of cool, fresh water, and cellars full of good wine. Everything had been swept and cleaned. The beds were made. There were flowers everywhere, of every kind the season offered. Fresh rushes were strewn on the floors. The young people were delighted. Can you picture arriving there? After the quiet, sad streets of the city, suddenly you are standing in a garden full of roses, with fountains splashing and birds singing in the trees.

BODY: In the spring of 1349, Florence began to come back to life. People who had fled to the countryside came home. Shops opened their shutters again. The looms began to clatter in the workshops. Builders needed masons and carpenters. So the workers who had survived could ask for more money, and they often got it. For the first time in generations, ordinary working people found their work was truly valued. They ate better and dressed better. Some were able to buy land of their own. In England, the same thing happened. Labourers asked for higher wages, or moved to another village to find a better master. The government tried to pass laws to stop them and keep wages low. But the laws did not work very well. Over the next hundred years, the old system faded away. Under that system, many farm workers had been bound to their lord's land and could not leave. Many historians think the Black Death, sad as it was, helped bring about a fairer world for ordinary people.
