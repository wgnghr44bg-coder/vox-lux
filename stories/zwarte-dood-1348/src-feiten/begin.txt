In a library in Florence, there's an old handwritten book. It's a history of the city, written year by year, by a grey-haired merchant named Giovanni Villani. On one of its last pages, he began a sentence about a great sickness. He wrote that it would last until, and then he left a small empty space. He meant to fill in the date when it was over. That little space is still empty today, nearly seven hundred years later.

Villani lived in Florence in the 1340s, in a city of stone towers, wool workshops and church bells. Then, in the spring of 1348, a sickness came across the sea that changed all of Europe. People would later call it the Black Death. So how did ordinary people live through it, the weavers and the bakers, the writers and the children? And how did they find their way back to ordinary life afterwards? Let's go and see, gently, and from a kind distance.
