Tonight we travel back about three and a half thousand years, to the year 1500 before Christ. We go to the north of Europe, to the land that is now Denmark. It is a land of low green hills, wide heaths of heather, small woods of oak and birch, and long sandy beaches, where the grey sea washes up on the shore. And on this land, in a little village, people are waking up, in a long wooden house, with a roof of reeds.

[long-pause]

It is early morning, in late summer. A soft mist lies over the heath. On the low hills around the village, there are round mounds of earth, covered with grass. They are the graves of important people, from long ago. The sun is rising over the mounds, and lighting them up, gold and green. Larks are singing high above the heather. And from the roof of the longhouse, a thin line of smoke is rising, straight up into the still air.

[pause]

Inside the longhouse, a family is getting up. A fire is burning on the hearth. A woman is stirring a pot of porridge. A man is putting on his woollen cloak, fastened with a beautiful bronze pin. A girl of fourteen is combing her long hair. A little boy is still asleep, curled up under a sheepskin. And at the other end of the house, the cattle are stamping and lowing, waiting to be let out into the meadow.

[pause]

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

{img:bronze-age-family-longhouse-interior-hearth-porridge-pot-woollen-clothes}
This is the Bronze Age. A time when people had learned to make a wonderful new metal, called bronze. A shining, golden metal, harder than copper, which could be made into tools, weapons and beautiful jewellery. It changed the world. For the first time, people traded across all of Europe, over mountains and seas, to get the precious metals they needed. And the world became bigger, richer and more connected than ever before.

[pause]

We do not know the names of the people who lived in this village. They could not write. They left no books. But they left many other things. Their houses, their graves, their tools, their jewellery, their clothes, and even some of their bodies, preserved for thousands of years in the wet ground. And from all these things, we can learn a great deal about how they lived. So tonight, we will give them names, and imagine their lives.

[pause]

{img:girl-fourteen-bronze-age-long-hair-woollen-dress-bronze-disc-belt-heath}
We will follow a family in this village, through a year. Especially the girl, whom we will call Rana. We will see their longhouse, their fields and their animals. We will walk on the beach, looking for amber, the golden stone of the north. We will meet a trader from the far south, who brings bronze, and a smith who casts it into beautiful things. We will see how they honoured the sun, and their ancestors. And we will see how they lived, in a world without writing, but full of wonder.

[long-pause]

{img:sunrise-over-heath-burial-mounds-larks-heather-golden-light-calm}
The Bronze Age was a very long time ago. Even the Romans would have thought of it as ancient history. In many ways, life was very different from our own. But in other ways, it was not so different. People loved their children, cared for their old people, worked hard, celebrated, and wondered about the sun and the stars. So let us begin, on a misty morning, in a little village in the north, three and a half thousand years ago, as the sun rises over the heath.

[long-pause]

{img:copper-ore-green-stones-and-tin-ingots-bronze-age-smith-hands-close}
Before the Bronze Age, for hundreds of thousands of years, people made their tools of stone, wood and bone. They made axes of flint, a hard, sharp stone. Knives of flint. Arrowheads of flint. They were very skilled at it. But stone tools break, and wear out. And then, slowly, people discovered metals. First gold and copper, which can sometimes be found pure, in the ground. And then they discovered something much better.

[long-pause]

Copper is a soft, reddish metal. It can be hammered into shape, or melted in a hot fire, and poured into a mould. But it is too soft for good tools. A copper axe soon becomes blunt. Then, more than five thousand years ago, somewhere in the Middle East, people discovered that if they mixed copper with a little tin, about one part tin to nine parts copper, they got a new metal. Harder, stronger and more beautiful. Bronze.

[pause]

Bronze is a wonderful metal. When it is new and polished, it shines like gold. It can be melted and poured into moulds, to make almost any shape. Axes, knives, swords, spears, sickles, needles, pins, bracelets, necklaces, cups and statues. It can be sharpened to a fine edge. And if a bronze tool breaks, it can be melted down, and made into something new. Nothing is ever wasted.

[pause]

{img:bronze-objects-gleaming-axes-swords-spirals-pins-arranged-golden-light}
But bronze has one great problem. Copper and tin are not found in the same places. Copper is found in some mountains, in the Alps, in Spain, in Cyprus and in a few other places. Tin is even rarer. It is found in only a few places in Europe, like Cornwall, in the south-west of Britain, and the mountains of central Europe. So to make bronze, people must bring copper from one place, and tin from another, often from very far away.

[pause]

In the north, where our village is, there is no copper and no tin at all. Not a single grain. Every piece of bronze in the whole of Denmark has been brought from far away. From the Alps, from Germany, from Britain, from the lands around the Mediterranean. Over mountains, along rivers, across seas. Carried by traders, on foot, on horseback and in boats, for hundreds or thousands of kilometres.

[long-pause]

{img:bronze-age-trade-route-feeling-boats-river-traders-carrying-ingots-forest}
So the Bronze Age is a time of trade. Great trade routes stretch across all of Europe, from the Mediterranean to the Baltic, from Britain to the Black Sea. Along these routes, traders carry copper, tin and finished bronze. And in return, they take other precious things. Furs, salt, cloth, slaves, and, from the north, the golden stone that everyone wants. Amber.

[pause]

The Bronze Age began at different times, in different places. In the Middle East, it began more than five thousand years ago. In the north of Europe, it began a little later, about four thousand years ago. In our story, in 1500 before Christ, the Bronze Age in the north is at its height. It is a rich and confident time. And it will last for another thousand years, until people discover how to make iron.

[pause]

{img:bronze-age-village-chief-wearing-gleaming-bronze-collar-sword-proud-calm}
Bronze is precious. It is a sign of wealth and power. The most important people in the village wear beautiful bronze jewellery, and carry bronze weapons, as a sign of their rank. Ordinary people may have only a bronze knife, or a pin, or nothing at all. They still use stone tools, for many things. A bronze axe is worth as much as several cows. So people take great care of their bronze, and pass it down from parent to child, for generations.

[long-pause]

Rana has one piece of bronze of her own. A small pin, shaped like a spiral, which she uses to fasten her cloak. It was her grandmother's, and her grandmother's mother's before that. Nobody knows how old it is. It is worn smooth, from many years of use. When she holds it up to the light, it shines softly, like a little piece of the sun. She thinks it is the most beautiful thing she owns. And one day, she will give it to her own daughter.

[long-pause]

{img:bronze-age-world-1500bc-feeling-egypt-temples-mycenae-ships-mediterranean}
What was the world like, in 1500 before Christ? It was a world of great civilisations, in the south, and of farming villages, in the north. And all of them were connected, more than you might think, by the trade in bronze and amber.

[long-pause]

In Egypt, the pharaohs are building great temples at Thebes, on the river Nile. The pyramids are already a thousand years old. In a few years, a powerful queen called Hatshepsut will rule Egypt, as pharaoh, and send ships to a faraway land called Punt, to bring back incense trees and gold. Egypt is rich and mighty, the greatest kingdom in the world.

[pause]

In Greece, there is a warrior people called the Mycenaeans. They build great fortresses on hilltops, with walls of enormous stones. Their kings are buried in deep tombs, with gold masks, gold cups and bronze swords. And in the graves of Mycenae, archaeologists have found beads of amber. Amber from the Baltic Sea, in the north. Perhaps from the very beaches where Rana walks.

[pause]

{img:minoan-crete-palace-colourful-columns-frescoes-dolphins-sunny-sea}
On the island of Crete, there is a wonderful civilisation, called the Minoans. They live in great palaces, with many rooms, painted with beautiful pictures of dolphins, flowers, birds and young people leaping over bulls. They are sailors and traders, who sail all over the Mediterranean. Their palace at Knossos is like a maze, with hundreds of rooms. Perhaps it is the origin of the old Greek legend of the Labyrinth.

[pause]

In the Middle East, there are great kingdoms. Babylon, the Hittites, and others. They write on clay tablets, and trade with each other, and sometimes fight. Their merchants travel long distances, with caravans of donkeys, carrying tin, copper, cloth and other goods. In Mesopotamia and Egypt, people have been writing for more than fifteen hundred years. But in the north of Europe, nobody writes at all.

[long-pause]

{img:stonehenge-1500bc-in-use-people-gathering-summer-evening-chalk-plain}
In Britain, the great stone circle of Stonehenge is already about a thousand years old. People still gather there, at midsummer and midwinter, to watch the sun. In Cornwall, in the far south-west of Britain, people dig tin from the ground, and trade it across the sea. Without British tin, there would be much less bronze in Europe.

[pause]

And in central Europe, in the mountains of Austria and Germany, there are copper mines and salt mines, where people dig deep into the mountains. In the valley of a river called the Elbe, in Germany, there are rich farming villages, and powerful chiefs. Some of them are buried in great mounds, with gold, bronze and amber. They control the trade routes, between the south and the north.

[pause]

{img:central-europe-copper-mine-entrance-mountain-miners-torches-bronze-age}
In the north, where Rana lives, the people are farmers and herders. They live in small villages, scattered across the land. They have no towns, no palaces, no writing and no kings, as far as we know. But they have chiefs, important families, who lead the villages, and who control the trade in amber and bronze. And they have rich and beautiful traditions, of art, religion and craft.

[long-pause]

So the world of 1500 before Christ is a world of great differences. Pharaohs and farmers. Palaces and longhouses. Writing and memory. But it is also a world that is joined together, by the long threads of trade. A piece of amber, found on a beach in Denmark, might end up in the tomb of a king in Greece. And a bronze sword, made in the Alps, might end up in a mound on a heath in the north. And Rana, holding her old bronze pin, is connected, without knowing it, to the whole wide world.

[long-pause]

{img:bronze-age-longhouse-jutland-long-low-reed-roof-wattle-walls-cattle-yard}
The village where Rana lives is small. There are about five or six longhouses, scattered among the fields, a few hundred metres apart. Each longhouse is the home of one big family, with their animals. Around the houses, there are fields, meadows, small woods and the wide heath. And on the low hills nearby, there are the old burial mounds of the ancestors.

[long-pause]

The longhouse of Rana's family is about twenty-five metres long and six metres wide. It is a long, low building, with walls made of woven branches, covered with clay mixed with straw and cow dung. The roof is very steep, made of thick bundles of reeds, from the marsh. It reaches almost down to the ground. Inside, the roof is held up by two rows of strong wooden posts, made from oak trees, running down the middle of the house.

[pause]

The house is divided into two parts. At the western end, there is the living part, where the family lives. There is a hearth, in the middle of the floor, made of flat stones, where the fire burns. There is no chimney. The smoke rises up into the roof, and finds its way out through the reeds. There are benches along the walls, covered with sheepskins and furs, where the family sits and sleeps. And there are clay pots, baskets and wooden boxes, for storing food and things.

[pause]

{img:longhouse-interior-two-halves-family-living-end-cattle-stalls-other-end}
At the eastern end of the house, there is the byre, where the cattle live in winter. Each cow has its own stall, made of woven branches. In winter, the cattle stay inside, warm and dry, and their warmth helps to heat the whole house. It also smells of cows, of course. But the family is used to it. In summer, the cattle are out in the meadows, day and night, and the byre is cleaned out, and full of fresh straw.

[pause]

Around the house, there is a yard, with a fence of woven branches. There is a small storehouse, raised on posts, to keep the grain dry and safe from mice. There is a little hut, where Rana's mother weaves. There is a well. And there are the pens for the sheep, the pigs and the goats. A big dog lies by the door, guarding the house. And chickens? No. Chickens have not yet come to the north. They will come much later.

[long-pause]

{img:bronze-age-farmstead-fields-heath-small-woods-burial-mounds-on-horizon}
The landscape around the village is open and wide. Long ago, it was covered with thick forest. But over thousands of years, the farmers have cleared the trees, to make fields and pastures. Now, there are wide heaths of heather and grass, where the sheep and cattle graze. Small fields of barley and wheat, surrounded by low banks of earth. And small woods, of oak, birch and hazel, which the people use for timber and firewood.

[pause]

To the west, about a day's walk away, there is the sea. The North Sea, wild and grey, with long sandy beaches and high dunes. To the east, there are calmer waters, with many islands. The land is never far from the sea. The people of the north are sailors and fishermen, as well as farmers. And the sea brings them one of their greatest treasures. Amber.

[pause]

{img:dog-lying-by-longhouse-door-evening-sun-heather-hills-peaceful}
The village is quiet and peaceful. Life follows the seasons. Ploughing in the spring. Weeding and herding in the summer. Harvest in the late summer and autumn. Spinning, weaving and repairing tools in the winter. Every family works hard, from dawn until dark. But they also have time to celebrate, to tell stories, to visit their neighbours, and to honour their gods and their ancestors.

[long-pause]

In the evenings, Rana likes to climb the low hill behind the longhouse, where the old burial mound is. She sits on the grass, at the top, and looks out over the land. The fields, golden with barley. The heath, purple with heather. The smoke rising from the other longhouses. The cattle grazing in the meadows. And far away, on the western horizon, a thin grey line. The sea. She thinks that there is no more beautiful place in the whole world.

[long-pause]

{img:bronze-age-father-amber-trader-woollen-cloak-bronze-pin-beard-calm}
Rana's family is one of the important families of the village. Not the richest. But respected. Her father, whom we will call Torm, is about forty years old. He is a farmer, like everyone in the village. But he is also an amber collector, and a trader. He knows the beaches where the best amber is found. And he trades it with the merchants who come from the south, for bronze.

[long-pause]

Torm is a tall, strong man, with a long fair beard, and blue eyes, wrinkled from the wind and the sun. He wears a woollen tunic, a cloak fastened with a bronze pin, and a round woollen cap. At his belt, he carries a bronze knife, with a handle of horn. He is calm and wise, and the other people of the village often come to him for advice. He has travelled further than anyone in the village, along the coast, and once, as a young man, all the way south to the river Elbe.

[pause]

Rana's mother, whom we will call Ebba, is about thirty-five. She is a weaver, and a very good one. She weaves the cloth for the whole family, from the wool of their sheep. She also looks after the house, the fire, the food, the children, and the animals. She grinds the grain into flour, with a big stone quern. She makes cheese and butter, from the milk of the cows. And she knows all the plants of the heath and the woods, and their uses, for food and for healing.

[pause]

{img:bronze-age-mother-weaving-upright-loom-loom-weights-hut-light-wool}
Rana is fourteen. She has long, fair hair, which she wears loose, or tied with a woollen band. She is clever and curious, and loves to ask questions. Why does the sun rise every morning? Where does amber come from? What is beyond the sea? She helps her mother with the weaving, and the cooking, and the animals. And she helps her father on the beaches, looking for amber. She has very good eyes. She often finds pieces that her father has missed.

[pause]

Her little brother, whom we will call Ulf, is seven. He looks after the geese, and the goats, and is always getting into trouble. He climbs trees, falls into streams, and chases the sheep. He wants to be a great trader, like his father, and travel to faraway places. He has a little bronze ring, which his father gave him, and which he thinks is the most precious treasure in the world.

[long-pause]

{img:grandmother-bronze-age-telling-stories-children-hearth-firelight-sheepskins}
There is also a grandmother, Torm's mother. She is very old, about sixty, which is a great age in those days. She sits by the fire, spinning wool with a little spindle, and telling stories. Stories about the ancestors, who lie in the mounds on the hills. About the sun, which travels across the sky every day, in a golden chariot, and through the underworld every night, in a boat. About the spirits of the springs, the trees and the bogs. Rana knows all her stories by heart.

[pause]

And there are others who live in the longhouse. Torm's younger brother and his wife and their two small children. A young man, who helps with the farm work, and sleeps in the byre with the cattle. In all, about a dozen people live in the longhouse. They share the fire, the food, the work and everything else. It is crowded, but warm, and friendly. Everyone has their place, and their work.

[pause]

{img:extended-family-eating-together-longhouse-wooden-bowls-hearth-evening}
In the evenings, the whole family gathers around the hearth, to eat. Porridge of barley, with milk. Bread, baked on hot stones. Cheese and butter. Meat, sometimes. Beef, mutton or pork, or a deer from the woods. Fish from the sea. Berries and nuts, in season. And beer, made from barley, sometimes flavoured with honey, or with a bog plant called sweet gale. They eat from wooden bowls, with spoons of wood and horn.

[long-pause]

After the meal, they sit by the fire, and talk, and work. The women spin and sew. The men mend tools and make rope. The children play with little clay animals, or listen to the grandmother's stories. The fire crackles. The cattle shift in the byre. The wind blows over the heath. And Rana sits close to her grandmother, and asks her to tell, once more, the story of the sun, and her golden chariot, and her long journey through the night.

[long-pause]

{img:bronze-age-farmer-ard-plough-two-oxen-small-field-spring-earth}
Rana's family are farmers, first of all. They grow grain, and keep animals. Everything they eat, they grow, or gather, or catch themselves. Only a few things, like salt and bronze, come from far away. And the year follows the rhythm of the farm. Ploughing and sowing in the spring. Herding in the summer. Harvest in the late summer. And rest, and repairs, in the winter.

[long-pause]

In the spring, Torm ploughs the fields. He uses an ard, a simple plough made of wood. It is like a big wooden hook, pulled by two oxen, which scratches a furrow in the soil, but does not turn it over. So Torm ploughs each field twice, once along and once across, to break up the soil properly. On some of the old burial mounds, archaeologists have found the marks of these ancient ploughs, still in the soil, crossing each other like a net.

[pause]

After the ploughing, the family sows the seed, by hand, scattering it from a basket. They grow barley, which is the most important grain. Barley for porridge, for bread and for beer. They also grow emmer, an old kind of wheat, and millet. And they grow beans, peas and flax. Flax is a plant with blue flowers, whose stems can be made into linen thread, and whose seeds give oil.

[pause]

{img:emmer-wheat-barley-small-fields-earth-banks-summer-golden-heath-beyond}
The fields are small, square, and surrounded by low banks of earth and stones. Every year, some fields are used for grain, and others are left to rest, as pasture for the animals. Their dung makes the soil rich again, for the next year. The farmers know how to look after the soil, so that it does not become tired. But the soil of the north is sandy and poor. It needs much care.

[pause]

The animals are the family's wealth. They have cattle, about twenty of them. Small, brown, sturdy cows, with short horns. The cows give milk, for drinking, and for making cheese and butter. The oxen pull the plough and the cart. When an animal is killed, it gives meat, leather, bone and horn. Nothing is wasted. The family also has sheep, for wool and meat. Pigs, which root in the woods for acorns. And goats.

[long-pause]

{img:girl-herding-sheep-heath-heather-purple-summer-dog-crook-sky}
In the summer, the cattle and sheep graze on the heath and in the meadows. The children often look after them, with the dog. Rana spends many summer days on the heath, with the sheep, sitting in the heather, in the sun, spinning wool with her little spindle, and watching the animals. She sings to them, old songs that her grandmother taught her. The larks sing above her. The bees buzz in the heather. And the hours pass slowly.

[pause]

Horses are new and precious, in the north. Only the richest families have them. They are small, sturdy horses, with thick manes. They are used for riding, and for pulling light carts, and for showing off. Torm has one horse, a grey mare, which he uses when he travels to trade. The children love her. Rana is sometimes allowed to ride her, around the meadow, holding tightly to her mane.

[pause]

{img:small-bronze-age-horse-grey-mare-girl-riding-bareback-meadow-evening}
The family also gathers food from the wild. In the woods, there are hazelnuts, acorns, crab apples, blackberries, raspberries and mushrooms. In the meadows, there are wild herbs and greens. In the streams and the sea, there are fish, mussels and oysters. In the autumn, the birds fly south, over the land, in great flocks, and some are caught in nets. And in the woods, there are deer, wild boar and hares, which the men sometimes hunt.

[long-pause]

In the evening, Rana brings the sheep back from the heath. The sun is going down, red and gold, over the western horizon. The heather glows purple. The sheep trot along the path, bleating, toward the fold. Rana walks behind them, with the dog, spinning as she walks. And when she reaches the longhouse, she sees the smoke rising from the roof, and smells the porridge cooking, and hears her little brother shouting. And she is glad to be home.

[long-pause]

{img:amber-pieces-golden-on-sandy-beach-seaweed-morning-light-north-sea}
On the beaches of the north, after a storm, there is sometimes a wonderful treasure, lying among the seaweed. Little pieces of a golden stone, shining in the sun. Some are yellow, like honey. Some are orange, like fire. Some are brown, like dark beer. Some are almost white, like milk. And when you hold them up to the light, they glow, as if the sun were shining inside them. It is amber.

[long-pause]

Amber is not really a stone. It is the resin of ancient trees, which grew in great forests, millions of years ago, where the Baltic Sea is now. The resin dripped down the trees, sticky and golden, and slowly hardened, over millions of years, into amber. Sometimes, a little insect, or a leaf, or a seed, was caught in the sticky resin, long ago. And it is still there, inside the amber, perfectly preserved, after millions of years.

[pause]

Amber is very light. It floats in salt water. So after a storm, when the waves stir up the sea floor, pieces of amber are washed up onto the beaches, along the coasts of the North Sea and the Baltic. The people of the north collect them. They have collected them for thousands of years, since long before the Bronze Age. And amber has always been precious. People believe it has magic powers, to protect and to heal.

[pause]

{img:father-and-daughter-searching-beach-after-storm-baskets-amber-wind-dunes}
After a big autumn storm, Torm takes Rana to the beach, to look for amber. They walk for a day, westward, across the heath, to the coast. They sleep in the dunes, in a little shelter of driftwood. And at dawn, when the tide is going out, they walk along the beach, slowly, looking at the line of seaweed and shells and driftwood that the storm has left. Looking for the golden gleam.

[pause]

It is not easy. Amber looks a lot like ordinary pebbles, when it is wet and dull. You must look very carefully. Torm has a trick. He picks up a piece, and rubs it on his woollen sleeve, and then holds it near a little piece of straw. If it is amber, the straw jumps up, and sticks to it, as if by magic. Rana thinks it is the most wonderful thing she has ever seen. Today, we know that it is static electricity. In fact, the word electricity comes from the Greek word for amber.

[long-pause]

{img:girl-holding-amber-piece-up-to-sun-insect-inside-glowing-wonder}
Rana has very good eyes. By the end of the morning, she has found more amber than her father. Little pieces, mostly. But one piece is big, as big as a hen's egg, and clear and golden. And inside it, there is a tiny insect, a little fly, with its wings spread out, perfectly preserved, as if it had been caught yesterday. Rana holds it up to the sun, and stares at it, for a long time. Torm says that it is the best piece he has seen for years.

[pause]

They also find other things on the beach. Shells, of many kinds. Pieces of driftwood, worn smooth by the sea. Flint stones, with holes in them, which are said to be lucky. Seaweed, which they will take home to put on the fields. And a dead seal, which is very sad, but whose skin and fat are useful. They load everything into their baskets, and walk home, tired and happy, across the heath.

[pause]

{img:amber-beads-necklace-drilled-polished-craftsman-hands-workshop-firelight}
At home, the amber is sorted. Some pieces are kept, to be made into beads and pendants. Torm drills little holes through them, with a fine bronze drill, and polishes them with sand and leather, until they shine. Ebba makes them into necklaces, for the family. But most of the amber is kept for trading. When the trader from the south comes, Torm will trade it for bronze. Amber is the wealth of the north.

[long-pause]

Rana keeps the big piece with the insect. Her father lets her. She makes a little pouch of leather for it, and wears it around her neck, on a string. At night, she takes it out, and holds it up to the firelight, and looks at the little fly, caught in the golden light, millions of years ago. She wonders what the world was like, when it was alive. What the trees were like. Who lived then. And she thinks that the world must be much, much older than anyone knows.

[long-pause]

{img:bronze-age-trader-arriving-village-pack-horses-bundles-cloak-travel-worn}
At the end of the summer, a trader arrives in the village. He comes every year, at about this time, from the south. He is a man of about fifty, with a grey beard, a weathered face, and a strange accent. He has two pack horses, loaded with bundles. And he is welcomed in every village, because he brings the most precious thing of all. Bronze.

[long-pause]

The trader comes from far away. From the lands along the river Elbe, in what is now Germany, many days' journey to the south. He travels north every summer, along the old trade routes, through forests and over heaths, from village to village. He brings bronze, in the form of bars and rings, and finished things. Axes, knives, pins, bracelets and swords. And he takes back amber, furs, and other things from the north.

[pause]

He also brings news. News from the south, and from the other villages along the way. Who has married. Who has died. Which chief is powerful, and which is weak. What the harvest is like. And stories, from even further away. Of great kingdoms in the south, where there are cities of stone, and kings who live in palaces, and people who make marks on clay, which can speak. Rana listens to his stories, with her eyes wide.

[pause]

{img:trader-and-father-bargaining-hearth-amber-and-bronze-laid-out-on-hide}
The trading happens in the evening, by the fire, in the longhouse. The trader spreads out his goods on a cowhide, on the floor. The bronze shines in the firelight. Torm brings out his amber, in a leather bag, and pours it out onto another hide. The two men look at each other's goods, carefully, for a long time. They pick things up, and turn them over, and put them down again. Then they begin to bargain.

[pause]

Bargaining takes a long time. It is polite, and friendly, but serious. The trader praises the amber, but says that he has seen better. Torm praises the bronze, but says that it is too expensive. They drink beer, and talk about other things, and then come back to the bargaining. In the end, they agree. Torm gives the trader most of his amber, and two good cowhides. And the trader gives him a new bronze axe, a bronze sickle, several bars of bronze, and a beautiful bronze belt disc, for Ebba.

[long-pause]

{img:bronze-belt-disc-spiral-patterns-gleaming-woman-wearing-it-firelight}
Rana's big piece of amber, with the insect, is not traded. Rana keeps it in its pouch, around her neck. But the trader sees it, and asks to look at it. He holds it up to the firelight, and his eyes widen. He says that in the south, a piece like this would be worth a fine bronze sword. He offers Rana a beautiful bronze bracelet for it. Rana thinks about it, for a long time. The bracelet is very beautiful. But in the end, she shakes her head, and puts the amber back in its pouch. The trader laughs, and says that she is a wise girl.

[pause]

The trader stays for a few days, as a guest of the family. He sleeps by the fire, and eats with them, and tells stories in the evenings. Then he loads his horses with the amber and the hides, and sets off, to the next village, and the next. By the end of the summer, he will turn south again, and go home, with his packs full of amber. And from there, some of the amber will travel on, further and further south, from trader to trader, over the mountains, perhaps all the way to Greece.

[pause]

{img:trader-leaving-village-pack-horses-heath-path-family-waving-morning}
Rana stands at the edge of the village, and watches him go, with his horses, along the path across the heath, until he disappears. She thinks about the long road south, and the rivers and forests and mountains, and the cities of stone, and the kings in their palaces. She wonders if, one day, she will see them. Her grandmother says that girls do not travel. But Rana is not so sure.

[long-pause]

Long after, archaeologists found amber from the north in the royal graves of Mycenae, in Greece, and in other places all over Europe. And they found bronze from the south, from the Alps and beyond, in the graves of the north. The traders of the Bronze Age, with their pack horses and their bundles, had connected the whole continent. Slowly, patiently, from village to village, along the old roads. Without writing. Without maps. Just with their memories, their courage, and their knowledge of the way.

[long-pause]

{img:bronze-age-smith-casting-molten-metal-crucible-into-clay-mould-glow-workshop}
In the next village, about half a day's walk away, there lives a bronze smith. He is one of the most respected people in the whole region. Because he knows the secret of turning bars of bronze into beautiful things. Axes, swords, pins, bracelets, belt discs and spiral ornaments. People come from many villages to bring him their bronze, and to order new things from him.

[long-pause]

In the autumn, Torm takes Rana to visit the smith, with the bronze bars he got from the trader. He wants the smith to make a new pin for Ebba, and a bracelet for Rana. They walk across the heath, in the morning, and arrive at the smith's workshop in the afternoon. It is a small hut, a little apart from the village, with smoke rising from it, and the sound of a hammer.

[pause]

The smith is an old man, with a white beard, and arms like tree trunks. His hands and face are covered with little scars, from the sparks and the hot metal. His name, we will say, is Brand. He has been a smith for forty years. He learned from his father, who learned from his. The secrets of the craft are passed down, from father to son, and kept within the family. Brand welcomes Torm, and looks at the bronze, and nods.

[pause]

{img:old-smith-carving-wax-model-spiral-pattern-bone-tool-careful-workshop}
To make a beautiful bronze object, Brand uses a method called lost wax casting. It is very old, and very clever. First, he makes a model of the object, in beeswax. A pin, with a spiral head. A bracelet, with lines and patterns. He carves the wax very carefully, with little tools of bone and bronze, adding every detail. Wax is soft, and easy to carve. So the model can be very fine and beautiful.

[pause]

Then he covers the wax model with clay. Fine, wet clay first, which fills every little line of the pattern. Then thicker clay, on the outside, to make a strong mould. He leaves a little hole at the top. Then he puts the mould near the fire, to dry and harden. When the clay is hot, the wax melts, and runs out of the little hole. The wax is lost, which is why it is called lost wax casting. And inside the clay mould, there is a hollow, in the exact shape of the wax model.

[long-pause]

{img:bellows-fire-charcoal-furnace-crucible-glowing-orange-sparks-night}
Then comes the most exciting part. Brand puts the bronze bars into a crucible, a small clay pot, and puts it into the fire. The fire is made of charcoal, which burns very hot. Brand's son works the bellows, two bags of leather, pumping air into the fire, to make it hotter and hotter. The fire roars. The charcoal glows white. And slowly, the bronze in the crucible melts, and becomes a glowing, liquid metal, bright orange, like a little sun.

[pause]

With long tongs, Brand lifts the crucible out of the fire, and pours the liquid bronze, very carefully, into the hole in the clay mould. It hisses and smokes. The mould fills up. Then he leaves it to cool. Rana watches, holding her breath. It is like magic. The old smith works in silence, with total concentration. He does not make a single mistake.

[pause]

{img:smith-breaking-clay-mould-revealing-bronze-pin-spiral-polishing-pride}
When the mould is cool, Brand breaks it open, with a little hammer. And inside, there is the pin. Rough and dark, at first, with bits of clay sticking to it. Then Brand cleans it, and files it, and polishes it, with sand and leather, for a long time. And slowly, it begins to shine. Golden, bright and beautiful, with its spiral head, exactly like the wax model. He holds it up to the light, and nods, and gives it to Torm.

[long-pause]

Then he makes Rana's bracelet. A simple band of bronze, with lines and little circles, which curves around her wrist. When he gives it to her, she puts it on, and holds up her arm, and watches it shine in the firelight. Brand smiles, for the first time. He says that every piece of bronze has a little bit of the sun in it. Because it shines like the sun, and it is born in fire, like the sun. Rana thinks about that, on the long walk home. And she wears the bracelet every day, for the rest of her life.

[long-pause]

{img:egtved-girl-reconstruction-corded-string-skirt-short-wool-top-bronze-belt-disc}
We know a surprising amount about the clothes of the Bronze Age, in the north. Because in Denmark, some people were buried in oak coffins, in the great mounds. And in some of these mounds, the conditions were just right. Wet, and without air. So the wood, the cloth, the hair and even the skin of the people inside were preserved, for three and a half thousand years.

[long-pause]

One of the most famous of these burials is a young woman, found in 1921, in a place called Egtved, in Denmark. She was about sixteen to eighteen years old, when she passed away, around the year 1370 before Christ. A little later than our story. She was buried in a hollowed oak trunk, wrapped in a cowhide. Her clothes were perfectly preserved. And they tell us how the girls of the Bronze Age dressed.

[pause]

She wore a short top, of brown wool, with elbow-length sleeves. And around her waist, she wore a skirt made of woollen cords, hanging down to her knees, like a skirt of strings. Around her waist, there was a woollen belt. And on the belt, in front, there was a large round bronze disc, decorated with spirals, with a spike in the middle. It shone on her stomach, like a little sun. She had bronze bracelets on her arms, and a little bronze earring.

[pause]

{img:bronze-age-man-woollen-tunic-cloak-round-cap-leather-shoes-standing-heath}
Other women in the graves wore long skirts of wool, down to their ankles, with a belt and a bronze disc, and a woollen top, and a hairnet made of woollen threads. Their hair was long, and sometimes worn in elaborate styles, held in place with combs of horn. The men wore a woollen tunic, wrapped around the body, and held up with a leather strap over one shoulder. A long woollen cloak. Leather shoes. And a round woollen cap, a little like a beret, with a soft, thick pile.

[pause]

Rana wears clothes like these. Her mother made them, from the wool of their own sheep. First, the wool is sheared from the sheep in the spring. Then it is washed, combed and spun into thread, with a spindle. Then it is woven into cloth, on a big upright loom, in the weaving hut. It takes weeks to make a single piece of clothing. Clothes are very precious. They are mended again and again, and passed down from one person to another.

[long-pause]

{img:mother-and-daughter-spinning-wool-spindles-doorway-sunny-sheep-grazing}
Ebba is teaching Rana to weave. It is one of the most important skills a woman can have. Rana has been spinning since she was five, with a little spindle, a stick with a round weight of clay or stone at the bottom. Now, she is learning to weave on the big loom. The loom stands upright, against the wall of the hut. The long threads hang down from the top, held tight by heavy stones, called loom weights. Rana passes the other thread through them, back and forth.

[pause]

The wool is mostly the natural colours of the sheep. Brown, grey, white and black. But sometimes, it is dyed with plants. Yellow from weld. Red from madder roots. Brown from walnut shells. Blue from woad, a plant whose leaves give a beautiful deep blue. Rana loves the colours. She wants her new skirt to have a stripe of red and blue. Her mother says that is too much. But in the end, she gives in.

[pause]

{img:loom-weights-clay-rings-hanging-threads-upright-loom-girl-weaving-stripe}
For special occasions, like festivals and weddings, people wear their best clothes, and all their bronze. Their pins, bracelets, necklaces, belt discs and rings, all polished until they shine. And their amber beads. When the whole village gathers, for a festival, in their best clothes, with all their bronze glinting in the sun, it is a beautiful sight. Like a field of little suns.

[long-pause]

The Egtved girl is in the National Museum of Denmark, in Copenhagen, today. People come from all over the world to see her clothes, and her bronze disc, and her oak coffin. Scientists have studied her hair and her teeth, and found something amazing. She was not born in Denmark. She was born far to the south, probably in the Black Forest, in Germany. And she travelled north and south, more than once, in the last years of her life. Perhaps she married into a family in the north, to make an alliance. Like the amber and the bronze, she too travelled the great roads of the Bronze Age.

[long-pause]

{img:trundholm-sun-chariot-bronze-horse-gilded-disc-wheels-museum-glow}
For the people of the Bronze Age in the north, the most important thing in the world is the sun. The sun gives light and warmth. It makes the grain grow and ripen. It brings the spring after the long, dark winter. Without the sun, nothing could live. So the people honour the sun, above everything, and believe that it is alive, a great and powerful being, travelling across the sky.

[long-pause]

In the north, the winters are very long and dark. In December, the sun rises late, and sets early, and hardly climbs above the horizon. The days are short, grey and cold. And the people wonder if the sun will ever come back. Then, slowly, after midwinter, the days grow longer. The sun climbs higher. And in the summer, it hardly sets at all. The nights are short and light. The whole land is full of sunshine. It is a great joy.

[pause]

The people believe that the sun travels across the sky every day, from east to west, pulled by a horse, or carried in a chariot. And at night, when it disappears in the west, it travels back to the east, under the earth, or across the sea, in a boat, or carried by a fish or a snake. And every morning, it rises again, in the east, fresh and new. It is a never-ending journey, every day and every night, and every year.

[pause]

{img:sun-disc-gold-side-bright-dark-side-night-bronze-age-symbolism}
In 1902, a farmer in Denmark was ploughing a field, in a bog, at a place called Trundholm. And his plough hit something. When he dug it up, he found something extraordinary. A bronze horse, standing on wheels, pulling a large bronze disc, also on wheels. One side of the disc is covered with thin gold, decorated with beautiful spiral patterns, and shines like the sun. The other side is plain bronze, darker. It is called the Trundholm Sun Chariot.

[pause]

The sun chariot was made around the time of our story, about 1400 before Christ. It shows the sun, being pulled across the sky by a horse. The golden side is the day sun, shining and bright. The dark side is the night sun, travelling back under the earth. Perhaps the chariot was pulled along, in ceremonies, to show the sun's journey. And then, one day, it was placed in the bog, as a gift to the gods. It is one of the greatest treasures of the Bronze Age.

[long-pause]

{img:midsummer-celebration-bronze-age-hilltop-people-facing-rising-sun-dawn}
The sun appears everywhere, in the art of the Bronze Age. On the bronze belt discs that the women wear, which are round and shining, with spirals, like little suns. On the rock carvings, there are circles with crosses inside, which are the sun. There are ships carrying suns. There are horses pulling suns. And many of the beautiful spiral patterns on the bronze may be the sun too, turning, and moving, and shining.

[pause]

The most important festivals of the year are the festivals of the sun. Midwinter, when the sun is at its lowest, and the people light fires, to help it come back. And midsummer, when the sun is at its highest, and the people celebrate the long, light days. At midsummer, the whole village climbs to the top of the highest hill, before dawn, to watch the sun rise, in the north-east. And when it appears, they sing, and raise their arms, and welcome it.

[pause]

{img:women-wearing-bronze-belt-discs-shining-sunlight-festival-heath-gathering}
Rana loves the sun. She loves the long summer days, when she can stay out on the heath until late in the evening, with the sheep, and the light never really fades. She loves the warmth on her face, and the gold on the barley, and the glitter on the sea. Her grandmother says that the sun is a kind and generous being, who looks after the people, as long as they honour her. Rana believes it.

[long-pause]

In winter, when the days are short, Rana sometimes looks at her bronze bracelet, shining in the firelight, and remembers what the old smith said. That every piece of bronze has a little bit of the sun in it. And she holds the bracelet, in the dark winter evenings, and feels that she is holding a little piece of summer. Waiting, like the whole world, for the sun to come back.

[long-pause]

{img:nebra-sky-disc-bronze-green-gold-sun-moon-stars-pleiades-close-glow}
The people of the Bronze Age watched the sky very carefully. Not only the sun, but also the moon and the stars. They had no telescopes, no books and no writing. But they knew a great deal about the sky. And in 1999, in Germany, something was found that showed just how much they knew.

[long-pause]

It was found on a hilltop, called the Mittelberg, near a town called Nebra, in the middle of Germany. It is a round disc of bronze, about thirty centimetres across, now green with age. And on it, there are shapes of gold. A big round circle, which is the sun, or the full moon. A crescent, which is the moon. And thirty-two little stars, scattered across the disc. Among them, a little group of seven stars, close together. They are the Pleiades, the seven sisters, a famous group of stars.

[pause]

It is called the Nebra Sky Disc. It was made around 1600 before Christ, a little before our story. And it is the oldest known picture of the sky, in the whole world. Older than any star map from Egypt or Babylon. It shows the sun, the moon and the stars, as the people of the Bronze Age saw them, from their hilltops, at night.

[pause]

{img:bronze-age-people-on-hilltop-night-watching-pleiades-moon-stars-calm}
But it is more than a picture. It is also a kind of calendar. The Pleiades are very important for farmers. In the spring, they disappear from the evening sky. That was the time to sow the seeds. In the autumn, they reappear in the evening sky. That was the time to harvest. So by watching the Pleiades, and the moon, the farmers of the Bronze Age knew when to sow, and when to reap. The sky was their calendar.

[pause]

On the edges of the disc, there are two curved strips of gold. They show the places on the horizon where the sun rises and sets, at midsummer and midwinter. From the hilltop where the disc was found, the sun sets behind a mountain called the Brocken, on midsummer day. The people knew this. They watched the sun move along the horizon, through the year, from one edge to the other, and back again. And they marked it on the disc, in gold.

[long-pause]

{img:grandmother-teaching-girl-stars-night-heath-pointing-seven-sisters}
Rana's grandmother knows the stars too. On clear nights, in the autumn, she takes Rana and Ulf outside, onto the heath, and shows them. There, she says, pointing. The seven sisters. When they appear again in the evening, in the east, after the summer, it is time to bring in the harvest. And when they disappear again, in the spring, it is time to sow the barley. The stars tell us what to do.

[pause]

She shows them the moon, too. How it grows from a thin crescent to a full circle, and shrinks again, every month. The moon is the clock of the night, she says. The sun is the clock of the year. And the stars are the clock of the seasons. Rana looks up at the sky, full of stars, and thinks it is like a great book, which her grandmother can read, and which she is just beginning to learn.

[pause]

{img:shooting-star-over-heath-night-children-pointing-grandmother-smiling}
A shooting star flashes across the sky. Ulf shouts, and points. The grandmother says that it is a spark from the sun's fire, falling to earth. Or perhaps a soul, travelling to the land of the ancestors. Rana watches the place where it disappeared, for a long time. She wonders where it went. And she thinks that, one day, she would like to know all the stars, and all their stories, as well as her grandmother does.

[long-pause]

The Nebra Sky Disc was buried, about three hundred years after it was made, on the hilltop, with two beautiful bronze swords, two axes and some bracelets. Perhaps as an offering to the gods. It lay in the ground for more than three thousand years. Today, it is in a museum, in the town of Halle, in Germany. And it is listed by UNESCO, as one of the most important documents in the memory of the world. A document without a single word. Only gold, and the sky.

[long-pause]

{img:bronze-age-rock-carvings-tanum-ships-people-sun-symbols-smooth-granite}
To the north, across the sea, in the land that is now Sweden and Norway, there are great smooth rocks, along the coast. And on these rocks, the people of the Bronze Age carved pictures. Thousands and thousands of them. Ships, people, animals, suns, footprints, weapons and strange signs. They are called rock carvings. And they are like a great picture book, telling us about the lives and beliefs of the people of the north.

[long-pause]

The most famous rock carvings are at a place called Tanum, on the west coast of Sweden. There, on the smooth, grey granite, polished by the glaciers of the Ice Age, there are more than three thousand carved pictures. They were made over many hundreds of years, during the Bronze Age, by people tapping the rock with hard stones, slowly, patiently, until the shapes appeared.

[pause]

The most common picture is a ship. Long, narrow boats, with high, curved prows at both ends, often decorated with the heads of animals, like horses or snakes. On the ships, there are little lines, which are the people, rowing or standing. Some ships carry a sun, on a pole, or a disc. These are the ships that carry the sun across the sea at night, in the old stories. Or perhaps they are real ships, used for trade and travel along the coasts.

[pause]

{img:carved-rock-ship-many-rowers-high-prows-sun-disc-red-painted-lines}
There are also people. Men with bows, spears and axes. Men blowing great curved horns, called lurs. People dancing, in a line. People ploughing, with oxen and an ard. A man and a woman, together, perhaps a wedding. People holding up their arms, toward the sun, as if praying. And there are animals. Horses, deer, bulls, dogs, birds and snakes. And footprints. Pairs of bare feet, carved in the rock, as if someone had stood there, long ago.

[pause]

What do the carvings mean? Nobody knows for sure. Perhaps they tell stories, of gods and heroes. Perhaps they are prayers, for good harvests, good weather and safe voyages. Perhaps they mark important events, like weddings, festivals and journeys. Perhaps they are simply the work of people who wanted to leave a mark of their lives, on the hard grey rock, so that they would be remembered.

[long-pause]

{img:people-dancing-line-rock-carving-lur-players-sun-worship-figures}
In Denmark, there are fewer great rocks. But there are smaller carvings, on stones, and on bronze objects. And many stones have little round hollows carved into them, called cup marks. Nobody knows what they were for. Perhaps people poured offerings into them. Milk, or beer, or fat. Perhaps they were connected with the sun, or with the fertility of the land. There is a big stone with cup marks near Rana's village. The grandmother says that it is a very old and holy stone, and that you must never sit on it.

[pause]

Rana has never seen the great carvings of Tanum. But her father has. When he was young, he sailed north, with other traders, along the coast, and saw them. He tells her about them. The great smooth rocks, by the sea, covered with ships, people, animals and suns. He says that they seem to move, when the evening sun shines across them, and the shadows fill the carved lines. Rana wishes she could see them.

[pause]

{img:father-telling-daughter-about-rock-carvings-fireside-gestures-wonder}
Sometimes, Torm draws the pictures for her, with a stick, in the sand, on the beach. A ship, with high prows. A man with a horn. A sun on a pole. Rana copies them. She draws ships and suns and horses in the sand, along the beach, until the tide comes in and washes them away. She thinks that it would be wonderful to carve a picture in the rock, which the sea could not wash away, and which would last forever.

[long-pause]

Today, the rock carvings of Tanum are a World Heritage Site. Visitors come from all over the world to see them. In many places, the carved lines have been painted red, so that they can be seen more easily. On a summer evening, when the low sun shines across the rocks, the ships seem to sail, the people seem to dance, and the suns seem to shine, just as they did three thousand years ago, when the people of the Bronze Age carved them, and stood back, and looked.

[long-pause]

{img:great-burial-mound-heath-sunset-silhouette-grass-ancient-ancestor}
On the low hills around Rana's village, there are the burial mounds. Great round heaps of earth and turf, covered with grass and heather. Some of them are small, a few metres across. Others are huge, as high as a house, and fifty metres across. There are tens of thousands of them in Denmark. They are the graves of the ancestors. And they are the most sacred places in the landscape.

[long-pause]

The mounds are very old. Some were built by Rana's great-great-grandparents, or their parents, a hundred or two hundred years before. Others are older still. In each mound, there is the grave of an important person. A chief, or a chief's wife, or a respected elder. They were buried with their best clothes, their bronze, their amber, and gifts of food and drink, for their journey to the land of the dead.

[pause]

Building a mound is a great work. It needs hundreds of people, working together, for weeks. And it needs a great deal of turf. Strips of grass and earth, cut from the pastures, and piled up, layer by layer, into a great dome. Archaeologists have calculated that a big mound needed the turf from several hectares of pasture. Only an important family could organise such a work. The mounds show the power and the wealth of the people buried in them.

[pause]

{img:oak-log-coffin-hollowed-trunk-being-placed-burial-ceremony-people-gathered}
The most important people are buried in coffins made of oak trees. A great oak trunk is split in two, along its length, and each half is hollowed out, like a boat. The person is laid in one half, on a cowhide, dressed in their best clothes, with their bronze and their gifts. Then the other half is placed on top, like a lid. And the coffin is placed in the middle of the mound, and covered with earth and turf.

[pause]

In some mounds, a strange thing happened. Inside the mound, the turf, which was wet, formed a kind of hard iron crust, around the coffin, which kept out the air. Inside, everything stayed wet, cold and without air. And so, the wood, the wool, the hair, the skin and the leather were preserved, for thousands of years. That is how archaeologists found the Egtved girl, and others like her, with their clothes still perfect.

[long-pause]

{img:people-leaving-offerings-at-burial-mound-flowers-food-evening-respect}
For the people of the village, the mounds are not frightening. They are places of respect, and memory. The ancestors live there, in a way. They watch over their families, and over the land. The people visit the mounds, at festivals, and leave offerings. Food, beer, flowers, little pieces of bronze or amber. They tell the stories of the ancestors, buried there. And they ask them for help, and protection, and good harvests.

[pause]

The mounds also mark the land. They stand on the tops of the hills, where everyone can see them, from far away. They show who owns the land. They mark the old roads. Travellers use them to find their way, across the heath. And in the evenings, when the sun sets behind them, they stand dark against the golden sky, like great sleeping animals, watching over the land.

[pause]

{img:girl-sitting-on-top-of-burial-mound-evening-looking-at-landscape-calm}
Rana often sits on the mound behind the longhouse, in the evenings. It is the grave of her great-great-grandmother, a famous woman, a chief's wife, who was buried there with a bronze belt disc and many amber beads. Rana's grandmother says that she was a wise and powerful woman, who knew all the stars, and could heal the sick. Rana sits on the grass, at the top, and talks to her, quietly, telling her about her day. And she feels that her great-great-grandmother is listening.

[long-pause]

Many of the mounds still stand today, in the countryside of Denmark, and the north of Germany, and Sweden. They are protected, by law. Sheep graze on them. Heather grows on them. And people walk past them, every day, often without knowing what they are. But if you climb to the top of one, on a quiet evening, and look out over the land, you can feel, perhaps, a little of what the people of the Bronze Age felt. That the ancestors are still there, watching, from their green hills.

[long-pause]

{img:bronze-age-plank-boat-paddlers-calm-sea-coast-islands-summer-morning}
The people of the north are never far from the sea. Denmark is a land of coasts, islands and fjords. The sea is everywhere. It gives food, and amber, and a road to other lands. And the people of the Bronze Age are skilled sailors, in their long, narrow boats.

[long-pause]

Their boats are made of wooden planks, sewn together with cords of lime bark or roots, and made watertight with resin and moss. They are long and narrow, with high, curved ends, like the ships on the rock carvings. They have no sails. They are paddled, by a crew of men, sitting in pairs along the sides, with short wooden paddles. A big boat can carry twenty paddlers, and goods for trade. They are fast, and light, and can be carried over land when needed.

[pause]

In the summer, when the sea is calm, the boats travel along the coasts, from village to village, and across the narrow seas, between the islands, and over to Sweden and Norway. They carry amber, furs, cattle hides and people. And they bring back bronze, and news, and sometimes, a bride or a husband from another village. The sea is not a barrier. It is a road.

[pause]

{img:fishermen-small-boat-nets-calm-fjord-evening-fish-seagulls-low-sun}
The sea also gives food. The men of the village go fishing, in small boats, close to the shore. They catch cod, herring, flatfish and eels, with nets, hooks of bone and bronze, and traps of woven willow. The women and children gather mussels, oysters and cockles, on the beaches, at low tide. And sometimes, a seal is caught, which gives meat, fat for lamps, and a warm skin.

[pause]

In the summer, Torm takes Rana and Ulf to the coast, for a few days, to fish and to visit their relatives in a fishing village by the sea. They go out in a small boat, with their uncle, early in the morning, onto the calm, grey sea. The sun rises over the water, turning it gold. The seagulls follow the boat, crying. Seals pop their heads up out of the water, and look at them, with their big dark eyes.

[long-pause]

{img:children-on-beach-gathering-mussels-baskets-low-tide-dunes-sunny}
Ulf catches his first fish, a big cod, and shouts so loudly that the seals all disappear. Rana catches three herrings. Their uncle catches a basket full. In the afternoon, they come back to the shore, and clean the fish, and hang them up to dry in the wind, on wooden racks. In the evening, they cook some of them, over a fire on the beach, and eat them with bread and salt. Nothing has ever tasted so good.

[pause]

At night, they sleep in their uncle's house, by the sea, and listen to the waves. Rana lies awake, for a long time, listening. The waves come in, and go out, and come in again, like breathing. She thinks of the sun, travelling under the sea, at night, in its boat. And she wonders if the sound of the waves is the sound of the sun's boat, passing by, far away, on its long journey to the east.

[pause]

{img:moonlight-over-sea-beach-fishing-village-boats-pulled-up-quiet-night}
The next morning, her uncle shows her the boats, pulled up on the beach. He explains how they are made, plank by plank, sewn together. He shows her the paddles, and the carved heads on the prows. He tells her about his journeys, across the sea, to the islands and to the land of the great rock carvings. Rana listens, and imagines herself in one of the boats, paddling across the sea, toward the rising sun.

[long-pause]

In 1921, the same year the Egtved girl was found, archaeologists found a Bronze Age boat in a bog in Denmark, at a place called Hjortspring. Actually, it was from a little later, from the Iron Age. But it was made in the same way, as the boats of the Bronze Age. Of thin planks, sewn together, long and narrow, with high curved ends. Today, people have built copies of it, and paddled them on the sea. They are fast, and graceful, and beautiful. Just like the ships on the rocks.

[long-pause]

{img:bronze-age-wedding-procession-village-bride-bronze-jewellery-flowers-heath}
At midsummer, there is a wedding in the village. Rana's cousin, Torm's nephew, a young man of nineteen, is marrying a young woman from far away. From the south, from the lands along the river Elbe. The trader arranged it, between the families, the year before. It is an important wedding. It will make a friendship, an alliance, between the families of the north and the families of the south.

[long-pause]

The bride arrives with the trader, on his journey north, with her brother, and a few servants, and gifts. She is about seventeen. She has dark hair, braided around her head, and brown eyes, and she speaks with a strange accent. She has never been to the north before. She does not know anyone. She looks frightened, and a little lost. Rana goes to her at once, and takes her hand, and smiles.

[pause]

The bride's name, we will say, is Aldis. She has brought her dowry with her. Bronze bracelets, a bronze belt disc, beautiful woollen cloth, and a fine bronze dagger, from the workshops of the south, which are famous. These are gifts for her new family. And she has brought her own things. Her clothes, her spindle, her little comb of horn, and a small pot of seeds, from her mother's garden, which she wants to plant in the north.

[pause]

{img:two-young-women-becoming-friends-sitting-heath-talking-braiding-hair}
Rana and Aldis become friends. Rana shows her the village, the fields, the heath, the mounds and the beach. She teaches her the words of the north. Aldis teaches Rana the words of the south. They laugh at each other's accents. Aldis tells Rana about her home. The great river, wide and slow, with boats on it. The big villages, with many longhouses. The forests, much bigger and darker than the woods of the north. And the hills, in the distance, where the copper comes from.

[pause]

The wedding takes place at midsummer, on the longest day of the year. The whole village gathers, and people from other villages too. Everyone wears their best clothes, and all their bronze and amber. The bride wears a new dress, of fine wool, with a red stripe, which Ebba wove for her. And her bronze belt disc, polished until it shines like the sun. And a crown of summer flowers, in her dark hair.

[long-pause]

{img:wedding-feast-longhouse-bronze-age-people-eating-drinking-music-firelight}
The ceremony is simple. The bride and groom stand together, on the top of the great mound of the ancestors, in the evening sun. The oldest woman of the village, Rana's grandmother, ties their hands together, with a woollen cord, and says the old words, asking the sun and the ancestors to bless them. Then the cord is untied. And everyone cheers, and sings.

[pause]

Then there is a feast, which lasts for three days. A whole ox is roasted, over a great fire. There is bread, cheese, fish, berries and honey. And there is beer, and mead, in great bronze and clay vessels, which are passed around, from hand to hand. There is music, from horns and drums and pipes. There is dancing, in a long line, around the fire, as on the rock carvings. And there are games, and races, and stories, late into the light summer nights.

[pause]

{img:young-bride-planting-seeds-small-garden-longhouse-girl-helping-sun}
After the feast, the young couple moves into a part of the family longhouse. Aldis begins her new life, in the north. She is homesick, at first. She misses her mother, and the great river, and the forests. But Rana visits her every day. And together, they plant the seeds that Aldis brought from her mother's garden, in a little patch by the longhouse. Some of them grow. Little plants from the south, growing in the sandy soil of the north.

[long-pause]

Perhaps the Egtved girl was like Aldis. A young woman from the south, who married into a family in the north, to make an alliance. Who travelled the long roads, between her two homes. Who brought her bronze, her clothes, and her memories, from the south. And who was buried, at last, in a mound in the north, in her corded skirt, with her belt disc shining on her stomach, like a little sun. We will never know her name. But we know that she was loved. Because she was buried with great care.

[long-pause]

{img:bronze-age-harvest-barley-field-people-cutting-bronze-sickles-golden-autumn}
At the end of the summer, the barley is ripe. The fields around the village turn golden. The ears of grain hang heavy on their stalks, nodding in the wind. And the whole village comes out to the fields, to bring in the harvest. It is the most important work of the whole year. Because the grain will feed the people, and their animals, through the long, dark winter.

[long-pause]

The harvest begins when the grandmother says so. She walks through the fields, and picks an ear of barley, and rubs it between her hands, and bites a grain. If it is hard, and dry, the barley is ready. She looks at the sky, and feels the wind. And then she says, tomorrow. And the next morning, before sunrise, everyone goes out to the fields, with their sickles and their baskets.

[pause]

The sickles are new, this year, made of bronze, from the trader's metal, by the old smith Brand. Curved blades, sharp and shining, with handles of wood. They are much better than the old sickles of flint, which some of the poorer families still use. The reapers hold a bunch of barley stalks in one hand, and cut them with the sickle, just below the ears. Then they put the ears in their baskets, and leave the straw standing in the field, for the animals.

[pause]

{img:women-and-children-carrying-baskets-grain-to-longhouse-evening-sunlight}
Everyone works. Men, women and children. Rana cuts with a sickle, beside her mother. Ulf carries the full baskets to the longhouse, staggering under the weight. Aldis, the new bride, works beside Rana, and learns the songs of the harvest, which the women sing as they work, to keep the rhythm. The old grandmother sits at the edge of the field, in the shade, and watches, and tells everyone what they are doing wrong.

[pause]

The harvest takes about two weeks. Every day, from dawn until dusk, the people work in the fields. At midday, they rest in the shade, and eat bread and cheese, and drink beer. In the evening, they come home, tired and dusty and sunburnt, and eat a big meal, and sleep like stones. The weather is fine, and dry, and warm. The grandmother says that the sun is pleased with them, this year.

[long-pause]

{img:threshing-grain-on-floor-longhouse-flails-winnowing-basket-dust-light}
When the barley is cut, it must be dried, and threshed, and stored. The ears of grain are spread out in the sun, to dry. Then they are beaten, with sticks, on a hard clay floor, to knock the grains out of the husks. Then the grain is tossed in the air, in flat baskets, on a windy day, so that the light husks blow away, and the heavy grains fall back into the basket. This is called winnowing.

[pause]

The clean grain is stored in big clay pots, with lids, and in the little storehouse on posts, safe from damp and mice. It will be used all winter, for porridge, bread and beer. And some of it, the best grain, is kept separately, as seed, for the next spring. That grain must never be eaten, however hungry people get. Without seed, there will be no harvest next year.

[pause]

{img:harvest-festival-bronze-age-offerings-grain-at-sacred-stone-cup-marks-sunset}
At the end of the harvest, there is a feast, to thank the sun and the gods for the grain. The people go to the old stone with the cup marks, near the village, and pour beer and milk into the little hollows, and leave a sheaf of the first barley, as an offering. Then they go back to the village, and eat, and drink, and dance, around a great fire, until late in the night. The harvest is home. The winter can come.

[long-pause]

Rana loves the harvest, in spite of the hard work. She loves the golden fields, the warm sun, the songs, the laughter, and the feeling of everyone working together. And she loves the feast, at the end, when the barns are full, and everyone is happy, and the fire is warm. She dances with Aldis, around the fire, under the stars. And she thinks that this has been the best summer of her life.

[long-pause]

{img:bronze-age-longhouse-winter-snow-heath-smoke-rising-grey-sky-cattle-inside}
After the harvest, the year turns toward winter. The days grow shorter. The nights grow longer and colder. The leaves fall from the oaks and the birches. The heather fades to brown. Great flocks of geese fly south, high overhead, calling. And the cattle are brought in from the meadows, into the warm byre at the end of the longhouse.

[long-pause]

In the north, winter is long and dark. In December, the sun rises late in the morning, and sets in the middle of the afternoon. It hardly climbs above the trees. The sky is often grey. It rains, and it snows. The wind blows hard over the heath, from the sea. The people stay inside the longhouse, most of the day, around the fire. And they wait for the sun to come back.

[pause]

Inside the longhouse, it is warm and cosy, in spite of the cold outside. The fire burns on the hearth, day and night. The cattle in the byre give off warmth, and the soft sound of their breathing and chewing fills the house. The walls are thick, and the roof of reeds keeps out the rain and the snow. The family sits close together, wrapped in furs and woollen cloaks, by the fire.

[pause]

{img:longhouse-interior-winter-evening-family-around-fire-spinning-carving-cattle-breathing}
There is a lot of work to do, in the winter, inside the house. The women spin and weave, all day, making the cloth for the next year. The men make and mend tools, carve wooden bowls and spoons, make rope from bark and grass, and repair the harness for the oxen. The children help, and play, and listen to stories. And everyone takes turns to look after the animals, giving them hay and water, and cleaning the byre.

[pause]

The food is what was stored in the autumn. Barley, for porridge and bread. Dried peas and beans. Cheese and butter. Dried and smoked fish. Salted and smoked meat, from the animals killed in the autumn. Dried berries and nuts. Honey. And beer. The family must make the food last until spring. So they are careful. But in a good year, like this one, there is enough for everyone.

[long-pause]

{img:grandmother-telling-stories-winter-night-children-wrapped-furs-firelight}
In the long winter evenings, by the fire, the grandmother tells stories. The old stories of the north, which she learned from her grandmother, who learned them from hers. Stories of the sun and the moon, and how they came to be in the sky. Of the first people, and how they learned to farm. Of the great heroes, and their journeys across the sea. Of the spirits of the bogs and the woods, who must be treated with respect. And of the ancestors, who sleep in the mounds.

[pause]

Rana loves these stories. She listens to every word, and remembers them all. She knows that one day, when her grandmother is gone, she will be the one to tell them, to her own children and grandchildren, by the fire, in the long winter nights. That is how the stories survive, in a world without writing. Passed from grandmother to granddaughter, by the fire, generation after generation, for a thousand years.

[pause]

{img:snow-falling-heath-night-longhouse-glow-reed-roof-white-peaceful}
Sometimes it snows. The whole heath becomes white, and silent. The burial mounds are like great white loaves, on the hills. The children go out and play in the snow, throwing it at each other, and making tracks. The dog runs around, barking. And then they come back inside, cold and wet and laughing, and warm themselves by the fire, while Ebba gives them hot porridge with honey.

[long-pause]

In the depths of winter, the grandmother becomes ill. She has a cough, and a fever. Ebba gives her herbs and warm drinks, and keeps her warm by the fire. Rana sits beside her, every day, and holds her hand, and tells her the stories, back to her, to show that she remembers. The grandmother smiles, and nods, and corrects her when she gets a word wrong. And slowly, as the days begin to lengthen, she gets a little better. And Rana is very, very glad.

[long-pause]

{img:midwinter-bonfire-hilltop-bronze-age-people-gathered-dark-sky-sun-low}
In the middle of winter, on the shortest day of the year, there is a great festival. Midwinter. The sun is at its lowest. The days are at their shortest. The darkness is at its deepest. And the people of the north gather together, to help the sun come back. To light fires, in the darkness. And to celebrate, because from this day on, the days will grow longer again.

[long-pause]

On the morning of midwinter, before dawn, the whole village climbs to the top of the highest hill, near the great mound of the ancestors. It is bitterly cold. The ground is frozen. There is frost on the heather, and the stars are still shining. Everyone is wrapped in their warmest cloaks and furs. The children are carried on their parents' shoulders. And the grandmother, who has recovered from her illness, is carried on a little sledge, pulled by Torm.

[pause]

At the top of the hill, the people wait, in silence, facing the south-east, where the sun will rise. Slowly, the sky in the south-east begins to grow pale. Then pink. Then gold. And at last, the sun appears, a thin red line on the horizon, and then a half circle, and then the whole round sun, low and red, rising slowly above the land. The people raise their arms, and sing. The sun has come back.

[pause]

{img:people-raising-arms-to-low-red-winter-sunrise-frost-heath-mound}
Then, on the hilltop, the people light a great fire. A bonfire, of wood and branches, which they have been gathering for days. The flames leap up into the cold air, crackling and roaring. The people gather around it, warming their hands. It is a gift to the sun. A little sun on earth, to help the great sun in the sky to grow strong again. And the light of the fire can be seen from far away, from all the other villages, where other fires are burning on other hilltops.

[pause]

The people also make offerings. To the sun, and to the ancestors, and to the spirits of the land. Some offerings are placed on the great mound. Food, beer, amber beads. Some are taken to the bog, a wet, marshy place nearby, which is sacred. There, people sometimes put very precious things into the water. Bronze swords, axes, jewellery, and even, sometimes, beautiful objects like the sun chariot. As gifts to the gods. They believe that the bog is a door to the other world.

[long-pause]

{img:people-placing-bronze-offering-into-dark-bog-water-reeds-winter-respect}
This year, Torm makes a special offering, to thank the sun for the good harvest, and for the grandmother's recovery. He takes the old bronze axe, which he no longer needs, now that he has a new one. He walks to the edge of the bog, with the family, and holds the axe up to the sun. Then he throws it, gently, into the dark water. It sinks, with hardly a ripple. The grandmother nods. It is a good gift.

[pause]

Archaeologists have found thousands of bronze objects, in the bogs of Denmark and the north. Swords, axes, spears, shields, jewellery, horns, cauldrons and figures. Often placed there carefully, in good condition, sometimes in groups, as if in a ceremony. They were not lost. They were given. And they lay in the wet, dark peat, for three thousand years, until a farmer, or a peat cutter, found them, and brought them back into the light.

[pause]

{img:midwinter-feast-longhouse-roast-meat-mead-music-celebration-warmth}
After the ceremonies, there is a feast, in the longhouse. The best food that has been saved for this day. Roast pork. Fresh bread, made with the best barley. Cheese, honey and dried fruits. And mead, strong and sweet, made from the honey of the summer. There is music, from bone flutes and drums. And the grandmother tells the old story of the sun, and her journey, and how she always comes back, however dark the winter.

[long-pause]

That night, Rana sits by the fire, holding her bronze bracelet, and her piece of amber with the insect inside. The fire glows. The music plays. The cattle breathe in the byre. Outside, the frost sparkles on the heath, under the stars. And she thinks of the sun, who rose this morning, red and low, over the frozen land. Who will rise a little higher tomorrow. And a little higher the day after. Until, one day, it will be summer again. And she smiles, and closes her eyes, and listens to her grandmother's voice.

[long-pause]

{img:young-woman-bronze-age-standing-on-boat-prow-sea-journey-south-sunrise}
The years go by, in the little village on the heath. The seasons turn, spring, summer, autumn and winter. The barley grows and ripens. The cattle have their calves. The trader comes every summer, with his bronze. And Rana grows up.

[long-pause]

When Rana is seventeen, something wonderful happens. The trader, who has known her since she was a little girl, asks Torm if she may come with him, on his journey south, for one summer. His own daughter has married, and he needs someone to help him, someone clever, with good eyes, who knows amber. Torm and Ebba are worried. It is a long way. But the grandmother says that Rana has the spirit of a traveller, like her great-great-grandmother. And that she should go.

[pause]

So Rana goes south, with the trader, and his horses, along the old roads. Through the forests and across the rivers of the north. Past villages, and burial mounds, and sacred bogs. To the great river Elbe, wide and slow. She meets Aldis's family, who welcome her like a daughter. She sees the big villages of the south, with their many longhouses. She sees the hills where the copper comes from. And she sees bronze smiths at work, in great workshops, making swords and shields.

[pause]

{img:young-woman-trader-river-elbe-busy-bronze-age-village-boats-market}
She never sees the cities of stone, or the kings in their palaces, in the far south. Those are much too far away. But she meets traders who have been there. Who tell her about Greece, and the great fortresses on the hills, and the kings buried in gold. And she thinks of her amber, travelling south, from hand to hand, all the way to those kings. And she feels that she is a small part of something very big.

[pause]

When she comes home, in the autumn, she is full of stories. She tells them by the fire, all winter long. The children of the village gather around her, to listen. And she becomes, in time, the great storyteller of the village, as her grandmother was. And, later, she marries a young man from a neighbouring village, a fisherman and boat builder, who takes her across the sea, to see the great rock carvings of the north. At last.

[long-pause]

{img:old-grandmother-bronze-age-resting-by-hearth-family-around-calm-evening}
The grandmother passed away, peacefully, in her sleep, by the fire, two winters after the trader's journey. She was buried in a small mound, near the great mound of her ancestors, with her spindle, her bronze pin and a pot of honey. Rana sang the old songs for her, the ones she had taught her. And every summer, at the solstice, she left an offering of flowers on her grave, and told her the news of the village.

[pause]

Ulf grew up to be a trader, as he had always dreamed. He travelled all over the north, along the coasts, in boats, trading amber and furs for bronze. He became rich and respected. And he was buried, as an old man, in a great mound, with a bronze sword and a gold ring, on a hill overlooking the sea.

[pause]

{img:rana-old-woman-storyteller-children-gathered-firelight-amber-pendant}
Rana lived a long life. She had five children, and many grandchildren. She wove, and farmed, and told stories, and watched the sun and the stars. And she wore her bronze bracelet, and her amber pendant with the little fly inside, every day, until she was very old. When she passed away, they were buried with her, in her mound, on the heath, under the open sky.

[long-pause]

Perhaps, one day, an archaeologist will find her. A woman, buried in an oak coffin, in a mound in Denmark, with a bronze bracelet on her arm, and a piece of amber around her neck, with a little fly inside. And the archaeologist will hold the amber up to the light, and see the fly, caught in the golden glow, millions of years ago. And wonder, as Rana did, about the long, long story of the world.

[long-pause]

{img:iron-smelting-early-iron-age-bloomery-furnace-smith-new-metal-later}
The Bronze Age in the north lasted for about a thousand years, from about 1700 to about 500 before Christ. It was a rich and creative time. But in the end, it came to an end. Slowly, over many generations. And the reason was a new metal. Iron.

[long-pause]

Around 1200 before Christ, in the south, in the eastern Mediterranean, there was a great crisis. Many of the great kingdoms and cities collapsed, one after another, in a short time. The Mycenaean palaces in Greece were destroyed. The Hittite kingdom fell. Egypt was weakened. Nobody is quite sure why. Perhaps there were droughts, earthquakes, wars and migrations, all at once. Historians call it the Bronze Age Collapse. The old trade routes were broken. And the trade in copper and tin became difficult.

[pause]

At about the same time, people in the Middle East learned how to make iron, from iron ore. Iron is harder to make than bronze. It needs a hotter fire, and more skill. But iron ore is found almost everywhere. In the bogs and the hills of the north, too. So people did not need to trade with faraway lands to get it. Every village could make its own iron. And slowly, the knowledge of iron spread north, across Europe.

[pause]

{img:bog-iron-ore-dug-from-marsh-reddish-lumps-early-iron-age-villagers-later}
In Denmark, iron arrived around 500 before Christ. It was made from bog iron, a reddish ore found in the wet ground. Iron tools were cheaper and more common than bronze tools. Ordinary farmers could afford them. The power of the chiefs, who had controlled the bronze trade, became weaker. The great burial mounds stopped being built. And the beautiful bronze objects became rarer. The Bronze Age was over. The Iron Age had begun.

[pause]

But the people did not disappear. They were the same people, the same families, in the same villages, on the same land. They changed their tools, and some of their customs. But they kept many of their old ways, and their old beliefs. The sun was still important to them. The bogs were still sacred. And the old stories were still told, by the fire, in the long winter nights.

[long-pause]

{img:farmer-ploughing-field-finds-bronze-object-1800s-surprise-later-discovery}
For thousands of years, the Bronze Age was forgotten. The mounds stood on the hills, but nobody knew who had built them. The bronze objects lay in the bogs, and in the mounds, hidden. Then, in the eighteen hundreds, people began to study the past more carefully. And a Danish scholar, called Christian Jürgensen Thomsen, had a wonderful idea, which changed the study of history forever.

[pause]

Thomsen was the director of the national museum in Copenhagen. He was trying to organise the museum's collection of ancient objects. And he noticed that the objects could be sorted into three groups. Objects of stone. Objects of bronze. And objects of iron. And he realised that these groups came from three different ages, one after another. The Stone Age, the Bronze Age and the Iron Age. This idea is called the three-age system. It is still used by archaeologists all over the world, today.

[pause]

{img:archaeologists-excavating-burial-mound-denmark-oak-coffin-early-1900s-later}
After that, archaeologists began to dig into the mounds, carefully, and to study the bogs. They found the oak coffins, with the people inside, in their woollen clothes. They found the sun chariot, the Egtved girl, the lurs, the swords and the jewellery. And slowly, they pieced together the story of the people of the Bronze Age. How they lived, what they wore, what they believed. A whole world, rescued from the earth.

[long-pause]

The people of the Bronze Age left no writing. But they left so much else. Their houses, their graves, their tools, their art, and even their bodies and their clothes. And from all of these things, we can hear them, very faintly, across three and a half thousand years. Telling us that they lived, and worked, and loved their families, and wondered at the sun and the stars, just as we do.

[long-pause]

{img:national-museum-denmark-copenhagen-sun-chariot-display-visitors-glow-today}
Today, the treasures of the Bronze Age can be seen in museums all over Europe. And many of the places where the people of the Bronze Age lived, and worshipped, and buried their dead, can still be visited.

[long-pause]

In Copenhagen, the capital of Denmark, there is the National Museum. It has one of the greatest collections of Bronze Age objects in the world. The Trundholm Sun Chariot, shining gold, in its glass case. The Egtved girl, in her oak coffin, with her corded skirt and her bronze belt disc. Lurs, the great curved bronze horns, which can still be played. Swords, axes, shields, jewellery and amber. People stand in front of them, and stare, and wonder.

[pause]

The lurs are especially remarkable. They are long, curved bronze horns, about two metres long, made in pairs, from a little later in the Bronze Age. They were found in bogs, often in pairs, as if they had been played together. And they can still be played, today. They make a deep, rich, mournful sound, like a great animal calling. Sometimes, on special days, musicians play them at the museum. And people hear the music of the Bronze Age, for the first time in three thousand years.

[pause]

{img:lurs-bronze-horns-pair-curved-museum-musician-playing-today}
In the town of Halle, in Germany, there is the State Museum of Prehistory, where the Nebra Sky Disc is kept. It is displayed in a dark room, lit only by a single light, so that it glows. Green bronze, and gold. The sun, the moon and the stars. Visitors stand around it, quietly, and look at it, as if they were looking at the sky itself. And on the hill where it was found, near Nebra, there is a visitor centre, and a tall tower, from which you can see the Brocken, on the horizon.

[pause]

In Sweden, the rock carvings of Tanum are a World Heritage Site. Visitors walk among the smooth grey rocks, along wooden paths, and look at the ships, the people, the animals and the suns, painted red, so that they can be seen. On summer evenings, there are guided walks, when the low sun makes the carvings stand out. And there is a museum, which explains what they might mean.

[long-pause]

{img:tanum-rock-carvings-today-red-painted-ships-visitors-walkway-evening-sun}
In the countryside of Denmark, there are still tens of thousands of Bronze Age burial mounds. They stand on the hills, covered with grass and heather, as they have for three and a half thousand years. Some of them are huge. Some are small. They are protected by law. Walkers pass by them, and sheep graze on them, and children climb them. And on summer evenings, they stand dark against the golden sky, just as they did in Rana's time.

[pause]

In England, there is a wonderful Bronze Age site, called Must Farm, near Cambridge. A little village of round houses, built on stilts over a river, which burned down, about three thousand years ago, and fell into the mud. Everything was preserved. Pots with food still inside them. Clothes. Tools. Even the wooden wheels of a cart. It has been called the British Pompeii. It shows, in amazing detail, how ordinary people lived, in the Bronze Age.

[pause]

{img:reconstructed-bronze-age-longhouse-open-air-museum-denmark-visitors-sheep}
And in Denmark and other countries, there are open-air museums, where Bronze Age longhouses have been rebuilt, exactly as they were. Visitors can walk inside, and see the hearth, the benches, the byre for the cattle and the loom. In the summer, people in Bronze Age clothes show how the people lived. How they wove, cast bronze, made pots and ground grain. And children can try it themselves.

[long-pause]

On the beaches of Denmark, after a storm, people still look for amber. And they still find it. Little pieces of golden stone, among the seaweed, shining in the sun. Just as Rana did, three and a half thousand years ago. And some of those pieces, held up to the light, still have a tiny insect inside, caught in the golden resin, millions of years ago, waiting to be found.

[long-pause]

{img:bronze-age-village-evening-sunset-heath-longhouse-mounds-golden-calm}
So we leave the Bronze Age tonight, in the year 1500 before Christ, as the evening falls. The sun is setting over the heath, in the west, toward the sea. It is red and gold, and very beautiful. The burial mounds of the ancestors stand dark against the glowing sky. The larks have stopped singing. And the smoke is rising from the reed roof of the longhouse, straight up into the still air.

[long-pause]

In the meadow, the cattle are being brought in, for the night. The sheep are in their fold. The dog is lying by the door. Inside the longhouse, the fire is burning on the hearth. The porridge is bubbling in the pot. The grandmother is spinning, with her little spindle, by the fire. And the little boy is playing with his clay animals, on the sheepskin, by her feet.

[pause]

{img:girl-on-mound-evening-holding-amber-to-last-sunlight-heath-golden}
On the hill behind the longhouse, on top of the old mound, a girl of fourteen is sitting, with her arms around her knees. She is watching the sun go down, as she does every evening. She holds up her piece of amber, to the last light, and looks at the little fly inside, glowing gold. Then she puts it back in its pouch, and stands up, and says goodnight to her great-great-grandmother, under the grass. And she walks down the hill, toward the light of the longhouse.

[long-pause]

The night comes softly over the heath. The first stars come out, one by one. Then many. The seven sisters rise in the east. The moon, a thin crescent, hangs low in the west. An owl calls from the birch wood. The wind whispers in the heather. And somewhere, far away to the west, the sea is breaking softly on the long sandy beach, where the amber lies, waiting, among the seaweed.

[pause]

{img:longhouse-night-firelight-through-door-stars-heath-peaceful-bronze-age}
Inside the longhouse, the family is going to sleep, on their benches, under their furs and sheepskins, by the glowing fire. The cattle are breathing softly in the byre. The grandmother is telling one last story, very quietly, about the sun, and her golden chariot, and her long journey through the night, under the earth, in a boat, toward the east. Where she will rise again, tomorrow morning, fresh and new.

[long-pause]

The girl lies on her bench, listening, with her bronze bracelet on her wrist, and her amber around her neck. And slowly, her eyes close. And she dreams of the sun's boat, gliding through the dark sea, under the world, with its golden cargo, and its patient paddlers, carrying the light home again, through the long night, as it always has, and always will.
