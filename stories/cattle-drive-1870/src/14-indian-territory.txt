On the other side of the Red River lies the Indian Territory. Today, it is the state of Oklahoma. In 1870, it is the home of many Native American nations. Some of them have lived on the plains for centuries. Others were moved here by the American government, forty years before, from their homelands in the east, on long and terrible journeys, which are remembered as the Trail of Tears. We will remember them, but gently.

--

The nations of the Indian Territory include the Cherokee, the Chickasaw, the Choctaw, the Creek and the Seminole, who came from the east. And there are the peoples of the plains, like the Comanche, the Kiowa, the Wichita and the Caddo. Each nation has its own government, its own laws and its own land. The Chickasaw and the Cherokee have towns, schools, farms and newspapers, some printed in the Cherokee alphabet, which a Cherokee scholar called Sequoyah invented.

The cattle drives cross the lands of these nations, on the way to Kansas. The cattle eat their grass and drink their water. So the nations ask the drovers to pay a toll, a small payment for each herd that crosses their land, usually ten cents a head, or a few cattle. Most trail bosses pay it. It is fair, Mr Hardin says. It is their grass. And if you treat people fairly, they will treat you fairly too.

{img:native-american-riders-meeting-trail-boss-prairie-calm-respectful-talk}
One morning, a group of riders appears on a hill, near the herd. They are Chickasaw men, on horseback, in a mixture of traditional and American clothes. Their leader rides down to meet Mr Hardin. They talk, in English, for a while. The leader explains that the herd is crossing the land of his people, and asks for the toll. Mr Hardin agrees. He gives them some money, and ten steers, from his own cattle. The leader thanks him. And the riders turn, and ride away, driving the steers before them.

Eli watches from a distance. He has heard many stories about the Native Americans, some of them frightening. But these men are polite and dignified. Sam Walker tells him that most of the stories are exaggerated. That the Native American nations are people like anyone else. Farmers, ranchers, traders and families. Who have lost a great deal, and who are trying to keep what is left.

--

{img:trading-post-indian-territory-wooden-store-people-horses-wagons-summer}
In the Indian Territory, there are trading posts along the trail. Little stores, built of logs, where the cowboys can buy flour, coffee, tobacco and other supplies. They are run by traders, some white, some Native American, some of mixed family. Jesse Chisholm himself, the trader who gave his name to the trail, had a trading post here. He passed away a couple of years before, in 1868, but his name lives on.

The land of the Indian Territory is beautiful. Rolling hills, covered with tall grass and wild flowers. Creeks lined with cottonwood trees, and pecan and walnut trees. Herds of deer and antelope. And the skies, enormous and blue, with white clouds sailing across them. The cattle graze well here. They are getting fatter every day. Mr Hardin is pleased.

{img:prairie-flowers-indian-territory-spring-hills-herd-distance-blue-sky}
One evening, a family of Cherokee farmers comes to the camp, with a wagon full of vegetables and eggs, to sell to the cowboys. Fresh potatoes, onions, green beans and a basket of strawberries. The cowboys buy everything. After weeks of beans and beef, fresh vegetables are a great treat. Cookie makes a stew with them, and everyone says it is the best meal of the whole drive. The Cherokee farmer's son, a boy of Eli's age, shows Eli how to play a game with a stick and a ball. They play until it is dark.

--

Eli thinks about this evening, many times, in later years. About the Cherokee boy, and the game, and the strawberries. And about the Native American nations, who were already losing their land, and would lose much more, in the years to come. He did not understand it all, at fifteen. But he remembered the kindness of the family with the wagon. And he always said, afterwards, that the people of the Indian Territory had treated him better than many people in the towns.
