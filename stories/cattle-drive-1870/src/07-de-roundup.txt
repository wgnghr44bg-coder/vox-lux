Before the drive can begin, the cattle must be gathered. This is called the roundup. In the spring, the cowboys ride out over the open range, through the grassland and the brush, and find all the cattle, and bring them together, in one great herd. It takes several weeks. And it is hard, dusty, exciting work.

--

The cattle of Texas are not kept in fields with fences. In 1870, there are almost no fences in Texas. The cattle roam freely, over huge areas of open land, belonging to many different ranchers. The cattle of one ranch mix with the cattle of another. So every spring, all the ranchers of an area work together, to round up all the cattle, and sort them out, by their brands.

The cowboys ride out in pairs and small groups, in different directions. They search the creeks, the valleys, the thickets of mesquite and prickly pear. Some cattle are easy to find, grazing in the open. Others hide in the thick brush, where the thorns tear the cowboys' clothes and scratch their horses. The cowboys must ride into the thickets, and drive the cattle out, shouting and waving their hats.

{img:cowboys-sorting-cattle-by-brands-large-herd-holding-ground-dust}
Then the cattle are driven to a big open place, called the holding ground. There, the cowboys sort them. The cattle of each ranch are separated from the others, by their brands. The cattle that will go to Kansas are chosen. Mostly steers, young male cattle, three or four years old, which are big and strong, and will sell for a good price. The cows and the young calves are left at home, to grow the herd for next year.

The young calves that were born during the winter must be branded, with the brand of their mother's ranch. We will not look closely at that. It is quick, and the calves soon run back to their mothers. Eli helps his mother's neighbours with their calves, and keeps count of the Brooks cattle, carefully, in a little notebook. Two hundred steers, with the B and the line. All present.

--

{img:evening-holding-ground-herd-settling-cowboys-circling-sunset-campfires}
At the end of the roundup, Mr Hardin's herd is ready. Three thousand steers, from twenty ranches. Before they leave, each steer is given a road brand, a special small mark, which shows that it belongs to this particular herd, for this drive. That way, if the cattle get mixed with another herd on the trail, the cowboys can tell which are theirs. The road brand of Mr Hardin's herd is a small H on the left shoulder.

For a few days before the drive, the herd is held together on the holding ground, so that the cattle get used to each other, and to being a herd. The cowboys ride around them, day and night, keeping them together. The cattle are nervous at first. They have never been in such a big group. But slowly, they settle down. They begin to follow Old Ned, the lead steer. They begin to graze calmly. And they become a herd.

{img:boy-cowboy-counting-cattle-notebook-pencil-on-horseback-herd-passing}
Mr Hardin counts the cattle, as they pass between two riders, through a narrow gap. He counts them in groups of a hundred, tying a knot in a piece of string for every hundred. Three thousand and twelve. A good herd, he says. Strong and healthy. He is pleased. He looks at the sky, and at the grass, which is fresh and green after the spring rains. Tomorrow, he says. Tomorrow we start for Kansas.

--

That evening, the cowboys sit around the fire, cleaning their saddles, mending their clothes, and checking their equipment. Nobody says much. Everyone is thinking about the long journey ahead. Then Paddy, the Irishman, takes out a fiddle, and begins to play a lively tune. And Eli takes out his new harmonica, and tries to play along, very badly. Everyone laughs. And Paddy says that, in two months, by the time they reach Kansas, he will have taught him to play properly.
