Today, the great cattle drives are long gone. But in Texas and Kansas, they are still remembered, in many places. And some of the old traditions still live on.

--

In Fort Worth, in Texas, there is a part of the city called the Stockyards. It was once one of the biggest cattle markets in the world. Today, it is a historic district, with old brick buildings, wooden pens and cobbled streets. And twice every day, there is a little cattle drive, right down the main street. A small herd of real Texas longhorns, with their huge horns, is driven slowly along the street, by cowboys on horseback, in their hats and chaps. Visitors line the street, and watch them pass.

The Chisholm Trail is also remembered. Along its old route, through Texas, Oklahoma and Kansas, there are markers and signs, showing where the trail went. In some places, you can still see the old trail itself. Long, shallow depressions in the prairie, worn by millions of hooves, more than a hundred and fifty years ago. They are called swales. Grass grows in them now. But they are still there, if you know where to look.

{img:chisholm-trail-marker-stone-prairie-old-swales-grass-blue-sky-today}
In Oklahoma, there is a Chisholm Trail Heritage Center, in the town of Duncan, where visitors can learn about the trail. There is a great bronze sculpture outside, of cowboys driving longhorns across a river. And inside, you can see saddles, ropes, spurs and chuckwagons, and hear the stories of the cowboys. You can even sit on a saddle, and imagine riding drag, in the dust, for two months.

In Abilene, Kansas, there is a reconstruction of the old town, with wooden buildings, a boardwalk, and a museum about the cattle trade. Abilene is a quiet little town now. But in the summer, there is a festival, with a cattle drive, a rodeo, and people in old-fashioned clothes. And people remember the days, long ago, when this little town was the end of the trail, and thousands of cowboys rode into town, dusty and tired, with money in their pockets.

--

{img:longhorns-grazing-wildlife-refuge-oklahoma-wichita-mountains-today}
The Texas longhorns live on too. There are many herds of them, on ranches in Texas and other states. They are kept for their meat, which is lean and healthy, and for their beauty, and their history. At the Wichita Mountains Wildlife Refuge, in Oklahoma, where the longhorns were saved in 1927, there is still a herd, living wild, with buffalo, elk and prairie dogs. Visitors can drive through the refuge, and see them, grazing among the hills, just as they did on the trail.

And there are still cowboys. On ranches in Texas, Oklahoma, Montana, Wyoming and many other places, men and women still work cattle on horseback, as they did a hundred and fifty years ago. They still wear hats, boots and chaps. They still rope calves, and ride fences, and round up the herds in the spring. Their life is not easy. But many of them would not want any other.

{img:modern-cowboy-on-horse-ranch-sunset-cattle-open-land-today}
There are also rodeos, all over the West. Contests where cowboys and cowgirls show their skills. Roping, riding, racing around barrels. They began as friendly contests between cowboys, after the roundup, to see who was the best. Today, they are big events, with crowds of thousands. And at every rodeo, people remember the old days of the trail, and the young men who rode north, with the longhorns, to Kansas.

--

And sometimes, on a quiet evening, on a ranch in Texas, an old cowboy sits on his porch, and takes out a harmonica, and plays an old trail song, softly, as the sun goes down. The same song that the cowboys sang to the cattle, at night, under the stars, so long ago. And somewhere in the pasture, the cattle lift their heads, and listen. And then lie down again, in the grass, and settle down for the night.
