The Chisholm Trail crosses many rivers. The Colorado, the Brazos, the Red River, the Washita, the Canadian, the Cimarron, the Arkansas. They are wide, brown, and often fast. There are no bridges. The cattle must swim across. And crossing a river with three thousand cattle is the most difficult and the most exciting part of the whole drive.

--

Before each crossing, Mr Hardin rides ahead, to look at the river. He looks for a good place to cross. A place where the banks are low and firm, where the water is not too deep or too fast, and where there is no quicksand. Quicksand is wet, soft sand, in the riverbed, which looks firm, but sucks in anything that steps on it. The cattle must be kept away from it. Mr Hardin tests the river on his horse, riding in and out, until he is satisfied.

The best time to cross is in the morning, with the sun behind the cattle, so that it does not shine in their eyes and frighten them. And it is best if the cattle are thirsty, so that they want to go into the water. So the herd is not allowed to drink before the crossing. Then, when they come to the river, they are eager to go in. Old Ned leads the way, as always. He walks down the bank, into the water, and starts to swim. And the others follow him.

{img:longhorns-swimming-across-river-horns-and-heads-above-water-riders-alongside}
The cattle swim with only their heads and horns above the water. From the bank, it looks like a great field of horns, floating across the river. The cowboys swim their horses beside the cattle, on the downstream side, to keep them from being carried away by the current. Some cowboys cannot swim. So they hold on to their horses' manes or tails, and let the horses pull them across. The horses are good swimmers. Eli rides Smoke, his best swimmer, and holds on tightly.

The most dangerous river is the Red River, which is the border between Texas and the Indian Territory, which is now Oklahoma. In the spring, it is often in flood, wide, deep and fast, full of red mud, which gives it its name. When Mr Hardin's herd arrives at the Red River, it is high, after the spring rains. They wait for two days, camped on the bank, for the water to go down. Other herds arrive, and wait too. There are thousands of cattle, all waiting.

--

{img:cowboys-guiding-cattle-in-fast-river-current-tension-horses-swimming}
On the third morning, Mr Hardin decides that the river is low enough. The cattle go in, following Old Ned. Everything goes well, at first. The cattle swim steadily. The cowboys swim beside them. And then, in the middle of the river, something goes wrong. A floating tree, carried by the current, comes down the river, straight into the herd. The cattle in front of it panic. They turn back, and start to swim in a circle, round and round, in the middle of the river. This is called milling. If it goes on, they will tire, and some may be swept away.

Sam Walker sees it at once. He rides his horse right into the middle of the milling cattle, shouting and waving his hat, and pushes them, and turns them, toward the far bank. Mateo follows him. Eli, on the edge of the herd, holds his breath. And then, slowly, the cattle stop circling, and begin to swim again, toward the far bank. Old Ned is already there, climbing up out of the water, ringing his bell. And the others follow the sound.

{img:wet-cattle-climbing-out-far-bank-cowboys-relieved-shaking-water-off}
It takes all morning, but at last, all three thousand cattle are across. Wet, tired and muddy, they climb up the far bank, and shake themselves, and start to graze, as if nothing had happened. The cowboys count them. None are missing. Sam Walker is soaking wet, from head to toe, and smiling. Mr Hardin shakes his hand, and says that it was the finest piece of riding he has ever seen. Sam just shrugs, and says that it was nothing.

--

That evening, they camp on the far side of the Red River. They are out of Texas now, in the Indian Territory. The cowboys hang their wet clothes on the bushes to dry, and sit around the fire in their blankets. Cookie makes a peach cobbler, to celebrate. And Eli writes a letter to his mother, by the light of the fire, to send with the next herd going south. He tells her about the river crossing. But he leaves out the part about the floating tree. He does not want her to worry.
