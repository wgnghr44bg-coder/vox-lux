The great cattle drives lasted for only about twenty years. They began in 1866, and they were mostly over by about 1886. In that time, about five million cattle walked north from Texas, on the trails to Kansas and beyond. And then, quite suddenly, it all came to an end. Several things happened, one after another.

--

The first was the railways. Every year, the railways spread further and further, across the plains. In the 1870s and 1880s, they reached Texas itself. Now, the cattle could be put on trains in Texas, and did not need to walk all the way to Kansas. The long trails were no longer needed. The cattle towns of Kansas, like Abilene, became quiet. Abilene's cattle trade ended in 1872, only five years after it began. Other towns, further west, took its place, for a while. Then they too became quiet.

The second was barbed wire. It was invented in 1874. It was cheap, strong and easy to put up. And it changed the West forever. The farmers and the ranchers began to fence their land. The open range, where the cattle could roam freely, for hundreds of kilometres, disappeared. The trails were blocked by fences. The cowboys could no longer drive their herds across the open land.

{img:deep-snow-winter-plains-1886-cattle-sheltering-cold-grey-sky-later}
The third was the weather. In the winter of 1886 to 1887, there was a terrible winter on the northern plains. Blizzards, deep snow and freezing cold, for months. Many cattle on the open range could not survive it. It was called the Great Die-Up. After that, the ranchers changed their ways. They kept fewer cattle, on fenced land, with hay for the winter. The age of the open range was over.

The longhorns changed too. The ranchers began to breed other kinds of cattle, like Herefords and Angus, which gave more meat, and were calmer and easier to keep. Slowly, the longhorns disappeared from the ranches. By the early twentieth century, there were very few left. They almost died out completely. But in 1927, a small herd was saved, in a wildlife refuge in Oklahoma, by people who wanted to keep them alive. And today, there are again many longhorns in Texas, kept for their beauty and their history.

--

{img:wild-west-show-arena-cowboys-riding-crowds-grandstand-1890s-later}
The cowboys changed too. Some went on working on the ranches, as cowboys, but on fenced land, with less riding, and more mending fences. Some became farmers. Some went to the cities. Some went further west, to Montana, Wyoming and the Dakotas, where the open range lasted a little longer. And some joined the Wild West shows, which travelled around America and Europe, showing the cowboy life to crowds of people, who had never seen it.

And the cowboys became legends. In books, magazines, songs, and later in films and on television, the cowboy became the great hero of America. Brave, free, honest, and handy with a horse and a rope. Riding off into the sunset. The stories were often exaggerated. Full of gunfights and bandits and heroes in white hats. The real cowboys were mostly hard-working young men, who spent their days in the dust, with cattle. But the legend was bigger than the reality.

{img:old-cowboy-rocking-chair-porch-grandchildren-listening-harmonica-later}
In the legend, the cowboy was almost always shown as white. But as we have seen, many of the real cowboys were Black, Mexican, or Native American. About a quarter of all the cowboys, or more. For a long time, they were forgotten. But today, historians are telling their stories again. Men like Bose Ikard, a Black cowboy who rode with Charles Goodnight, and who Goodnight said was the most trusted man he ever knew. And the vaqueros, without whom there would have been no cowboys at all.

--

As for the men of our story, they were not real people. But they were like thousands of real cowboys who rode up the Chisholm Trail. Some of them lived to be very old, in the twentieth century. They saw motor cars and aeroplanes, and heard the radio. And some of them told their stories, to writers and historians, who wrote them down. So that today, we can still hear their voices, telling about the dust, the rivers, the stampedes, the singing to the cattle, and the stars over the endless prairie.
