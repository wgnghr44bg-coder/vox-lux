The cattle that the cowboys drive north are Texas longhorns. They are a special kind of cattle, which you do not see in many other places. They are descended from cattle brought to America by the Spanish, hundreds of years ago, which ran wild in Texas and Mexico, and became tough, hardy and independent. And they are famous for their horns.

--

The horns of a longhorn are enormous. They grow out to the sides, and curve upward at the tips, like the handlebars of a bicycle. On a big steer, they can be more than two metres from tip to tip. Some are even longer. The cowboys say that a longhorn can scratch its own back with its horns, and turn around in a narrow gully only by tilting its head sideways.

Longhorns come in all colours. Red, brown, black, white, yellow, grey and blue. Many of them are spotted, speckled or striped, in patterns of different colours. No two are the same. A herd of longhorns, moving across the prairie, looks like a great patchwork quilt, of every colour, with a forest of horns rising above it.

{img:longhorn-herd-many-colours-spotted-speckled-grazing-prairie-evening}
Longhorns are tough. Much tougher than the cattle of the eastern states. They can walk for weeks, eating only grass, and drinking water only once a day, or even less. They can survive heat and cold, drought and flood. They can find food where other cattle would starve. That is why they can make the long journey to Kansas, on foot, and arrive in good condition. Other cattle could not do it.

But longhorns are also wild. They are not like the gentle cows of a dairy farm. They are nervous and quick, and easily frightened. A sudden noise, a flash of lightning, a strange smell, can make them panic and run. And when one runs, they all run. A big longhorn steer can also be dangerous, if it is angry. The cowboys must always be careful, and watchful, and calm, when they are among them.

--

{img:lead-steer-old-longhorn-walking-front-herd-brass-bell-neck-dawn}
In every herd, there is a leader. Usually an old steer, big and wise, who walks at the front, and the others follow him. In Mr Hardin's herd, the leader is a huge old steer, with a pale brown coat and enormous horns, called Old Ned. Nobody knows who first gave him that name. He has made the trip to Kansas twice before, and come back to Texas. He knows the way. Mr Hardin says that Old Ned is worth any three cowboys.

Old Ned wears a brass bell around his neck. Every morning, when the herd sets out, he walks to the front, and the other cattle follow the sound of his bell. At night, when they bed down, he lies down first, and the others lie down around him. At river crossings, he walks into the water first, and the others follow him. He is calm and steady. When the other cattle are nervous, he is not. He is a little like a captain on a ship.

{img:boy-cowboy-patting-old-longhorn-steer-head-camp-evening-gentle}
Eli makes friends with Old Ned. Every evening, when the herd is resting, he goes to the old steer, and gives him a handful of corn, from the cook's supplies, when the cook is not looking. Old Ned lets Eli scratch his forehead, between his great horns. He closes his eyes, and grunts softly. The other cowboys laugh, and say that Eli has found himself a pet. But Eli does not care.

--

In the three thousand cattle of Mr Hardin's herd, there are cattle from twenty different ranches. Each ranch has its own brand, a mark burned into the hide of its cattle, so that everyone can tell whose they are. Eli's mother's two hundred cattle carry the brand of the Brooks ranch. A B with a line under it. Eli can pick them out of the herd, from far away, by their colours and their faces. He feels responsible for them. They are his family's future.
