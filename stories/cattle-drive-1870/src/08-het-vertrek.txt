Early the next morning, the drive begins. Mr Hardin rides out in front, on his big grey horse, and waves his hat. Old Ned, the lead steer, gets up from the grass, shakes his great horns, and starts to walk north, with his bell clanking. And slowly, one by one, then dozens at a time, then hundreds, the other cattle get up and follow him. The great herd is moving.

--

The cattle walk in a long line, stretching out across the prairie, sometimes for two or three kilometres. They do not walk in a tight group, but spread out, like a great river of horns and backs, flowing slowly north. The cowboys ride on both sides of the line, and at the front and the back, to keep the cattle together, and moving in the right direction. Each cowboy has his own position, which he keeps all the way to Kansas.

At the very front, on each side of the lead steers, ride the point riders. They are the most experienced cowboys, Sam Walker and Mateo Garza. They guide the herd, steering the leading cattle in the right direction, following Mr Hardin, who rides far ahead, choosing the way. It is the most important position, and the most honourable. And also the cleanest, because they are in front of the dust.

{img:point-rider-front-of-herd-guiding-lead-steer-open-prairie-morning-sun}
Behind them, along the sides of the herd, ride the swing riders and the flank riders. Their job is to keep the cattle from wandering off to the sides, and to keep the line moving smoothly. They ride back and forth, gently pushing the cattle back into line, when they stray. The brothers Jim and Tom ride swing. Paddy, Will and Slim ride flank.

And at the very back of the herd, ride the drag riders. Their job is to push along the slowest cattle, the tired ones, the lame ones, the lazy ones, and the young ones, who fall behind. It is the hardest and dirtiest job of all. Because at the back of the herd, there is a great cloud of dust, kicked up by three thousand cattle. The drag riders ride in that dust, all day long. And the new cowboys always start at drag. Including Eli.

--

{img:drag-riders-dust-cloud-back-of-herd-bandanas-over-faces-hot-afternoon}
By the end of the first day, Eli is covered in dust from head to toe. His face is brown, his hat is brown, his horse is brown. Dust in his eyes, in his nose, in his ears, between his teeth. He can hardly see. He keeps his bandana pulled up over his face, but the dust gets in anyway. The other drag rider, an older cowboy, laughs at him, and says that by the time they get to Kansas, Eli will have eaten a whole Texas ranch.

The herd moves slowly. About fifteen to twenty-five kilometres a day. That is the right speed. If the cattle go faster, they lose weight, and are worth less at the end. If they go slower, the journey takes too long. Mr Hardin wants the cattle to arrive in Kansas fat and healthy. So they walk slowly, and graze as they go, eating the fresh spring grass along the trail.

{img:herd-grazing-midday-rest-creek-cowboys-resting-horses-shade}
At midday, the herd stops for a rest, near a creek, so that the cattle can drink and graze. The cowboys take turns to ride to the chuckwagon, which has gone ahead, for a quick meal. Then the herd moves on again, in the afternoon, until the evening. And then, at the place that Mr Hardin has chosen, the cattle are bedded down for the night, on open ground, near water.

--

On the first evening, Eli climbs down from his horse, and his legs are so stiff that he can hardly stand. Every part of his body aches. He has ridden all day before, at home. But not like this. He drinks some water, washes the dust from his face in the creek, and goes to the chuckwagon, where Cookie gives him a plate of beans and beef and biscuits. It is the best meal he has ever eaten. And then, before he can even finish, he falls asleep, sitting by the fire, with the plate on his knees.
