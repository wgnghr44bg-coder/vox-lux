One evening, in the Indian Territory, the air becomes very still and heavy. It is hot and sticky, even after the sun goes down. There is no wind. The cattle are restless. They do not want to lie down. They stand around, lowing softly, and turning their heads this way and that. Mr Hardin looks at the sky in the west, where great dark clouds are piling up, higher and higher. He tells everyone to be ready. A storm is coming.

--

Storms on the prairie can be terrible. They come quickly, with great black clouds, strong winds, heavy rain, hail and lightning. The lightning is the worst. On the flat, open prairie, there is nothing taller than a man on a horse. And the sound of the thunder, and the flash of the lightning, frightens the cattle more than anything else. A storm at night is what every cowboy fears. Because a storm at night often means a stampede.

All the cowboys go out to the herd, that night. Nobody sleeps. They ride slowly around the cattle, singing, trying to keep them calm. The night is very dark. The clouds have covered the stars. Lightning flickers on the horizon, silent at first, then with a distant rumble of thunder. Eli rides Midnight, his black night horse, who is very calm. He sings, with a dry throat, every song he knows.

{img:lightning-flash-prairie-night-cattle-heads-raised-riders-silhouettes}
And then the storm arrives. The wind comes first, a sudden cold blast, that blows the cowboys' hats off. Then the rain, in great sheets. Then a flash of lightning, so bright that for a moment, the whole prairie is as bright as day, and every horn of every steer is lit up, with little blue flames dancing at their tips. The cowboys call it fox fire. And then, immediately, a crash of thunder, so loud that the ground shakes.

In that moment, the whole herd jumps to its feet, at once, as if it were one animal. And it runs. Three thousand cattle, running in the dark, through the rain, with a sound like thunder itself. That is a stampede. The ground trembles. The cowboys can hear nothing but the roar of hooves, the crash of the thunder, and the hiss of the rain.

--

{img:cowboys-riding-alongside-running-herd-night-rain-turning-leaders}
The cowboys know what to do. They gallop beside the running herd, in the darkness, trying to reach the front. They must turn the leaders. If they can make the front of the herd turn, the rest will follow, and the whole herd will start to run in a circle. And slowly, running in a circle, the cattle will tire, and slow down, and stop. It is called milling. It is the only way to stop a stampede.

Sam Walker and Mateo reach the front first. They ride alongside the leaders, shouting, waving their slickers, and firing their pistols in the air. Slowly, slowly, the leaders begin to turn. The herd bends, like a great river turning. Eli, on the edge of the herd, rides as hard as Midnight can go, in the dark, trusting the horse to see the ground. He cannot see anything. He just holds on, and rides, and shouts.

{img:herd-milling-in-circle-slowing-down-rain-easing-cowboys-circling-dawn-light}
And then, at last, the herd is circling. Running round and round, slower and slower. The storm is passing. The thunder is moving away to the east, grumbling. The rain is getting lighter. And slowly, the cattle stop running. They stand still, panting, steaming in the cool air, tired out. The cowboys ride slowly around them, singing again, softly, in hoarse voices. And the herd begins to calm down.

--

In the morning, the sky is clear and washed clean. The sun rises over a wet, shining prairie. The cowboys are soaked, exhausted and muddy. Some of the cattle have scattered, and the cowboys spend the whole day finding them, and bringing them back. At the end of the day, Mr Hardin counts them. Only twenty-three are missing, out of three thousand. And all of the men are safe. Mr Hardin says that they were lucky. And that they rode well. Eli, who rode in his first stampede, falls asleep at supper, again, with a biscuit in his hand.
