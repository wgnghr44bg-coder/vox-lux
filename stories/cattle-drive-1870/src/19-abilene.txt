In the morning, the herd is driven into Abilene. The cattle walk slowly down toward the great pens beside the railway, with Old Ned at the front, ringing his bell, as always. The people of the town come out to watch. Buyers from Chicago and the East, in their suits and hats. Railway men. Shopkeepers. Children. And other cowboys, from other herds, who have arrived before them.

--

Abilene in 1870 is a strange little town. Three years ago, it was just a few log cabins on the prairie. Now, because of the cattle trade, it is busy, noisy and growing fast. There is a main street, with wooden buildings on both sides, and a wooden boardwalk to walk on, because the street is mud when it rains and dust when it does not. There are hotels, stores, banks, saloons, a blacksmith, a barber, and the cattle pens and the railway station.

The most famous building is the Drovers Cottage, a big hotel built by Joseph McCoy, the man who started the cattle trade in Abilene. It has a hundred rooms, and a long porch, where the cattle buyers and the trail bosses sit, and do business. Mr Hardin goes there, in his best coat, to meet the buyers, and to sell the herd. The cowboys wait outside with the cattle, in the pens.

{img:cattle-pens-railroad-stockyards-abilene-cattle-being-loaded-onto-train-cars}
The buyers look at the cattle. They walk along the pens, looking at the steers, checking how fat and healthy they are. They talk with Mr Hardin for a long time, about prices. Mr Hardin is a good bargainer. In the end, he sells the herd for a good price. About twenty-five dollars a head. Six times more than they were worth in Texas. It is a very good result. Mr Hardin comes out of the hotel, smiling, and shakes hands with every cowboy.

Then the cattle are loaded onto the train. It is an enormous job. The cattle are driven up wooden ramps, into the cattle cars, about twenty in each car. The cowboys use long poles to push them along. Some of the cattle do not want to go. They have never seen a train before, and they are frightened by its noise and its smoke. But slowly, all three thousand are loaded. And the long train, with its dozens of cars, puffs away, toward Chicago, and the cities of the East.

--

{img:cowboy-saying-goodbye-to-old-longhorn-steer-pen-gentle-moment}
But not Old Ned. Mr Hardin never sells his lead steer. He is too valuable. Old Ned will go back to Texas with the cowboys, and lead another herd north next year. Eli is very glad. He goes to the pen where Old Ned is waiting, and gives him a handful of corn, and scratches his forehead. The old steer closes his eyes, and grunts. He has done it again. Three trips to Kansas. Perhaps four, next year.

Then the cowboys are paid. Mr Hardin pays each man his wages, in silver dollars and paper money, at a table on the porch of the hotel. About sixty or ninety dollars each, for two or three months of work. It is more money than many of them have ever had at once. Eli is paid fifty dollars, for his first drive. He holds it in his hands, and can hardly believe it. And Mr Hardin gives him an envelope for his mother, with the money for her two hundred cattle. Five thousand dollars. A fortune.

{img:cowboys-new-clothes-store-buying-boots-hats-shirts-mirror-laughing}
The cowboys go into the town, to spend their money. First, to the barber, for a proper shave and a haircut, and a hot bath, in a big tin tub, the first hot bath in two months. Then to the stores, to buy new clothes. New shirts, trousers, boots and hats. Some of them buy presents for their families at home. Some of them go to the saloons, to drink and play cards. Mr Hardin warns them to be careful with their money, and not to get into trouble.

--

Eli is careful. He buys a new hat, a real Stetson, which he has always wanted. And presents for his family. A warm shawl for his mother, of soft blue wool. A book for Rose, with pictures of animals from all over the world. And a little silver locket, with a picture of a horse on it, also for Rose. He puts the rest of his money, and his mother's envelope, in a leather pouch, inside his shirt, and keeps his hand on it, all day. In the evening, he sits on the porch of the hotel, with his new hat on, and watches the sun go down over the prairie, feeling very proud.
