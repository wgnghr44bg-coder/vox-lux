Why did the cowboys drive cattle all the way from Texas to Kansas? The answer begins with a war. From 1861 to 1865, America fought a long and terrible civil war, between the states of the North and the states of the South. Texas was on the side of the South. Many of its men went away to fight. And while they were gone, something happened to the cattle of Texas.

--

Before the war, there were many cattle in Texas. But with the men away, nobody looked after them. They wandered off into the brush, the wild country of thorny bushes and mesquite trees, and lived there, wild and free. They had calves, and the calves had calves. And by the end of the war, there were millions of wild cattle in Texas. Perhaps five million. More cattle than people.

But in Texas, these cattle were almost worthless. Everybody had cattle. Nobody needed to buy them. A cow might be sold for four dollars, if you could find a buyer. But in the cities of the North, like Chicago and New York, it was different. The cities were growing fast, with factories and workers, and they needed meat. There, a cow could be sold for forty dollars. Ten times as much.

{img:railroad-tracks-prairie-kansas-1867-steam-train-cattle-cars-loading-pens}
The problem was getting the cattle from Texas to the North. There were no railways in Texas yet. But the railways were growing quickly, pushing west across the prairies. And in 1867, a railway reached a little town in Kansas, called Abilene. A cattle dealer, called Joseph McCoy, had an idea. He built big pens for cattle in Abilene, beside the railway. And he sent word to Texas that cattle brought to Abilene could be sold, and put on trains, and sent to Chicago.

The cattlemen of Texas heard the news. And they began to round up their wild cattle, and drive them north, on foot, to Abilene. The journey was long. About a thousand kilometres, across rivers, prairies, and the lands of the Native American nations. But the price at the end made it worth it. And so began the great cattle drives.

--

{img:jesse-chisholm-trader-wagon-trail-ruts-prairie-1860s-old-route}
The cattle followed a route that became known as the Chisholm Trail. It was named after a trader, called Jesse Chisholm, whose father was Scottish and whose mother was Cherokee. Before the cattle drives, he had made a trail with his wagons, from his trading post in what is now Oklahoma, north to Kansas. The cattlemen followed his wagon tracks. And soon, the trail was a great wide road of trampled grass and dust, hundreds of metres wide, from Texas to Kansas.

In 1870, the cattle drives are at their peak. Every spring, hundreds of herds set out from Texas, one after another, heading north. Each herd has two or three thousand cattle. In this one year, about three hundred thousand cattle go up the trail to Kansas. In the twenty years of the cattle drives, about five million cattle will make the journey.

{img:many-herds-on-trail-distance-dust-clouds-prairie-spring-north}
Each herd needs men to drive it. Cowboys. Young men, mostly, between sixteen and thirty, who are good with horses and cattle, and willing to work hard, for long hours, in all weather, for very little pay. They are paid about thirty dollars a month. Some of them are Texans, sons of farmers and ranchers. Some are former soldiers, from both sides of the war. Some are Mexican vaqueros. Some are formerly enslaved men, newly free after the war.

--

And some are boys of fifteen, like Eli, who have never been anywhere, and want to see the world. To the cowboys, the trail is hard, dusty and tiring. But it is also an adventure. And for many of them, it will be the greatest adventure of their lives. Something to tell their children and grandchildren about, many years later, by the fire, when they are old men.
