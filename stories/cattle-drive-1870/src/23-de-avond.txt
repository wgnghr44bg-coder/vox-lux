So we leave the Chisholm Trail tonight, in the year 1870, as the evening falls. The sun is going down over the endless prairie. The sky is red and gold and purple. The grass is glowing. And the great herd is bedding down for the night, three thousand longhorns, lying down one by one, around an old steer with a brass bell.

--

At the camp, by a little creek, the chuckwagon stands with its white canvas cover glowing in the last light. Cookie is scraping the last of the beans from the pot. The coffee is on the coals. The cowboys are sitting around the fire, with their plates on their knees, tired and dusty, talking softly about the day. The horses of the remuda are grazing nearby. And the tongue of the wagon is pointing north, toward the star that will show the way to Kansas.

{img:boy-cowboy-harmonica-campfire-friends-listening-stars-appearing-prairie}
A boy of fifteen takes out a harmonica, and begins to play an old song, very softly. One of his mother's songs. The fiddler joins in. Then the vaquero, with his little guitar. The other cowboys listen, or hum along. The fire crackles. The sparks fly up into the darkening sky. And for a while, nobody thinks about the dust, or the rivers, or the long way still to go.

--

Out on the prairie, two night herders are riding slowly around the sleeping herd, in opposite directions, singing softly. Their voices drift across the grass, low and gentle. The cattle lie still, chewing their cud, breathing slowly. A coyote calls, far away. Another answers. The wind moves through the grass, with a sound like the sea. And the first stars are coming out, one by one, over the edge of the world.

{img:night-herder-silhouette-horse-moonlit-prairie-sleeping-herd-milky-way}
The moon rises, huge and golden, over the eastern horizon, and climbs slowly into the sky, turning silver. It shines on the sleeping herd, on the white canvas of the chuckwagon, on the horses, and on the cowboys, rolled up in their blankets around the dying fire, with their saddles for pillows and their hats over their faces. The Big Dipper turns slowly around the North Star, marking the hours of the night.

--

The boy lies on his back, in his bedroll, looking up at the stars. He can hear the singing of the night herders, far away. The soft breathing of the cattle. The jingle of a spur. The crackle of the last embers. He thinks of his mother and his sister, far away in Texas, under the same stars. And he thinks of Kansas, somewhere ahead, in the dark. And slowly, with the harmonica still in his hand, he falls asleep, under the great sky of the prairie.
