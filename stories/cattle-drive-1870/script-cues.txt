{img:texas-prairie-dawn-1870-cattle-herd-longhorns-chuckwagon-campfire-smoke}
Tonight we travel back about a hundred and fifty years, to the year 1870. We go to the wide, open plains of Texas, in the south of the United States. The land is flat and endless, covered with grass, stretching away to the horizon in every direction. And on this land, slowly moving north, there is a great herd of cattle. Thousands of them. With long, curving horns. And with them ride a small group of men on horseback. Cowboys.

[long-pause]

It is early morning, in spring. The sky in the east is turning pale gold. The stars are fading. A cool wind moves across the grass, making it ripple like water. In a little hollow, by a creek, there is a camp. A wagon, with a white canvas cover. A small fire, with a coffee pot on it, sending up a thin line of smoke. And around the fire, men are sleeping on the ground, wrapped in blankets, with their saddles for pillows.

[pause]

The cook is already up. He is an old man, with a grey beard and a battered hat, stirring the coals, and putting more wood on the fire. He bangs a spoon on a tin pan, and shouts. Roll out! Come and get it! And one by one, the cowboys wake up, stretch, pull on their boots and their hats, and come to the fire, for coffee and breakfast. Out on the prairie, the cattle are beginning to stir too, getting up from the grass, lowing softly.

[pause]

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

{img:young-cowboy-fifteen-1870-hat-bandana-standing-by-horse-sunrise-prairie}
Among the cowboys, there is a boy of fifteen, called Eli. It is his first cattle drive. He has never been more than a day's ride from home. And now he is on his way to Kansas, almost a thousand kilometres to the north, with three thousand cattle, eleven other men, and about a hundred horses. The journey will take more than two months. And it will change his life.

[pause]

Tonight, we will ride with Eli on the long cattle drive, up the famous Chisholm Trail, from Texas to Kansas. We will meet the cowboys, and learn how they worked and lived. We will sit by the campfire, and eat beans and biscuits from the chuckwagon. We will ride night watch, singing softly to the cattle under the stars. We will cross great rivers, and wide prairies, and see herds of buffalo, and prairie dogs, and wild flowers.

[pause]

{img:longhorn-cattle-herd-moving-across-prairie-dust-riders-on-flanks}
There were some dangerous moments on a cattle drive. Rivers in flood. Storms. And sometimes a stampede, when the whole herd suddenly ran, in a panic. We will see these, but gently, as they were told afterwards around the campfire. Because most of the time, a cattle drive was slow, quiet, and very hard work. Riding all day, in the sun and the dust. Watching the cattle. Finding grass and water. And sleeping under the stars.

[long-pause]

{img:cowboys-riding-alongside-herd-wide-grassland-blue-sky-spring-flowers}
The cattle drives lasted only about twenty years. But they became one of the most famous parts of American history. They gave the world the cowboy, the hat, the boots, the songs, and the dream of the open range. So let us begin, on a spring morning in 1870, on the wide plains of Texas. The coffee is boiling. The cattle are waking. And a boy of fifteen is pulling on his boots, for the first day of the longest journey of his life.

[long-pause]

{img:wild-longhorns-texas-brush-country-mesquite-trees-1866-roaming-free}
Why did the cowboys drive cattle all the way from Texas to Kansas? The answer begins with a war. From 1861 to 1865, America fought a long and terrible civil war, between the states of the North and the states of the South. Texas was on the side of the South. Many of its men went away to fight. And while they were gone, something happened to the cattle of Texas.

[long-pause]

Before the war, there were many cattle in Texas. But with the men away, nobody looked after them. They wandered off into the brush, the wild country of thorny bushes and mesquite trees, and lived there, wild and free. They had calves, and the calves had calves. And by the end of the war, there were millions of wild cattle in Texas. Perhaps five million. More cattle than people.

[pause]

But in Texas, these cattle were almost worthless. Everybody had cattle. Nobody needed to buy them. A cow might be sold for four dollars, if you could find a buyer. But in the cities of the North, like Chicago and New York, it was different. The cities were growing fast, with factories and workers, and they needed meat. There, a cow could be sold for forty dollars. Ten times as much.

[pause]

{img:railroad-tracks-prairie-kansas-1867-steam-train-cattle-cars-loading-pens}
The problem was getting the cattle from Texas to the North. There were no railways in Texas yet. But the railways were growing quickly, pushing west across the prairies. And in 1867, a railway reached a little town in Kansas, called Abilene. A cattle dealer, called Joseph McCoy, had an idea. He built big pens for cattle in Abilene, beside the railway. And he sent word to Texas that cattle brought to Abilene could be sold, and put on trains, and sent to Chicago.

[pause]

The cattlemen of Texas heard the news. And they began to round up their wild cattle, and drive them north, on foot, to Abilene. The journey was long. About a thousand kilometres, across rivers, prairies, and the lands of the Native American nations. But the price at the end made it worth it. And so began the great cattle drives.

[long-pause]

{img:jesse-chisholm-trader-wagon-trail-ruts-prairie-1860s-old-route}
The cattle followed a route that became known as the Chisholm Trail. It was named after a trader, called Jesse Chisholm, whose father was Scottish and whose mother was Cherokee. Before the cattle drives, he had made a trail with his wagons, from his trading post in what is now Oklahoma, north to Kansas. The cattlemen followed his wagon tracks. And soon, the trail was a great wide road of trampled grass and dust, hundreds of metres wide, from Texas to Kansas.

[pause]

In 1870, the cattle drives are at their peak. Every spring, hundreds of herds set out from Texas, one after another, heading north. Each herd has two or three thousand cattle. In this one year, about three hundred thousand cattle go up the trail to Kansas. In the twenty years of the cattle drives, about five million cattle will make the journey.

[pause]

{img:many-herds-on-trail-distance-dust-clouds-prairie-spring-north}
Each herd needs men to drive it. Cowboys. Young men, mostly, between sixteen and thirty, who are good with horses and cattle, and willing to work hard, for long hours, in all weather, for very little pay. They are paid about thirty dollars a month. Some of them are Texans, sons of farmers and ranchers. Some are former soldiers, from both sides of the war. Some are Mexican vaqueros. Some are formerly enslaved men, newly free after the war.

[long-pause]

And some are boys of fifteen, like Eli, who have never been anywhere, and want to see the world. To the cowboys, the trail is hard, dusty and tiring. But it is also an adventure. And for many of them, it will be the greatest adventure of their lives. Something to tell their children and grandchildren about, many years later, by the fire, when they are old men.

[long-pause]

{img:small-texas-ranch-house-1870-wooden-porch-corral-windmill-oak-trees}
Eli Brooks was born on a small ranch, in the hill country of central Texas, near a little town called Lockhart. His family's house is made of wood, with a long porch in front, shaded by big old oak trees. There is a corral for the horses, a barn, a garden, and a few fields of corn. And all around, there is open grassland, where the family's cattle graze, with their long horns, among the trees.

[long-pause]

Eli's father fought in the Civil War. He came home in 1865, thin and tired, and was never quite well again. Two winters ago, he passed away, of a fever. Since then, Eli's mother has run the ranch, with Eli and his younger sister, Rose, who is eleven. It has been hard. There is not much money. The cattle are worth almost nothing in Texas. And there is always work to do, from dawn until dark.

[pause]

Eli's mother is called Martha. She is a small, strong, quiet woman, with grey eyes and grey streaks in her brown hair. She can ride, rope a calf, mend a fence, shoot a rifle, cook, sew and keep accounts. She is the bravest person Eli knows. But she worries about money, and about the future. And when she heard that a neighbour was gathering a herd to drive to Kansas, she had an idea.

[pause]

{img:mother-and-son-on-ranch-porch-evening-talking-serious-oak-shade}
The neighbour is a big rancher, called Walt Hardin. He is putting together a herd of three thousand cattle, from his own ranch and from the small ranchers around, to drive to Abilene. Martha offered to send two hundred of her cattle with his herd. He would sell them in Kansas, and bring her the money, minus a share for his costs. And she asked him to take Eli with him, as a cowboy, to learn the trade, and to earn some wages.

[pause]

Walt Hardin looked at Eli for a long time. Fifteen is young for the trail. But Eli is tall for his age, and strong, and he has been riding horses since he was four. He can rope and herd cattle as well as many grown men. And he is a quiet, steady boy, who does what he is told. So Mr Hardin said yes. Eli could come, as the youngest hand, and ride drag, at the back of the herd, which is the job for beginners.

[long-pause]

{img:sister-giving-brother-small-gift-ranch-porch-goodbye-dawn-horse-saddled}
On the night before he leaves, Eli cannot sleep. He lies in bed, listening to the crickets outside, and the wind in the oak trees, and thinking about Kansas. He has never been further than San Antonio. He has never seen a railway. He has never seen a town with more than a few hundred people. And now he will ride for two months, across the whole of Texas, through the Indian Territory, to Kansas.

[pause]

In the morning, his mother gives him a bag of things for the trail. A spare shirt, two pairs of socks she knitted, a warm jacket, a slicker, which is a long raincoat made of oiled canvas, and a little Bible, which belonged to his father. Rose gives him a present too. A harmonica, which she bought with her own savings, from a pedlar. She says that cowboys need music. Eli puts it in his shirt pocket, and promises to learn to play it.

[pause]

{img:boy-riding-away-from-ranch-sunrise-looking-back-mother-sister-waving}
Then he saddles his horse, a strong, reliable bay mare called Dolly, which he has ridden for three years. He hugs his mother, who holds him tightly, and tells him to be careful, and to listen to Mr Hardin, and to write if he can. He hugs Rose, who is trying not to cry. And then he rides away, down the lane, toward the Hardin ranch, where the herd is gathering.

[long-pause]

At the bend in the lane, he turns around in the saddle, and looks back. His mother and Rose are standing on the porch, under the oak trees, waving. He waves back, with his hat. Then he turns, and rides on, into the morning sun. He does not look back again. Because if he did, he thinks, he might turn around, and go home. And he does not want to do that. He wants to see Kansas.

[long-pause]

{img:texas-longhorn-steer-close-huge-curving-horns-patterned-coat-grass}
The cattle that the cowboys drive north are Texas longhorns. They are a special kind of cattle, which you do not see in many other places. They are descended from cattle brought to America by the Spanish, hundreds of years ago, which ran wild in Texas and Mexico, and became tough, hardy and independent. And they are famous for their horns.

[long-pause]

The horns of a longhorn are enormous. They grow out to the sides, and curve upward at the tips, like the handlebars of a bicycle. On a big steer, they can be more than two metres from tip to tip. Some are even longer. The cowboys say that a longhorn can scratch its own back with its horns, and turn around in a narrow gully only by tilting its head sideways.

[pause]

Longhorns come in all colours. Red, brown, black, white, yellow, grey and blue. Many of them are spotted, speckled or striped, in patterns of different colours. No two are the same. A herd of longhorns, moving across the prairie, looks like a great patchwork quilt, of every colour, with a forest of horns rising above it.

[pause]

{img:longhorn-herd-many-colours-spotted-speckled-grazing-prairie-evening}
Longhorns are tough. Much tougher than the cattle of the eastern states. They can walk for weeks, eating only grass, and drinking water only once a day, or even less. They can survive heat and cold, drought and flood. They can find food where other cattle would starve. That is why they can make the long journey to Kansas, on foot, and arrive in good condition. Other cattle could not do it.

[pause]

But longhorns are also wild. They are not like the gentle cows of a dairy farm. They are nervous and quick, and easily frightened. A sudden noise, a flash of lightning, a strange smell, can make them panic and run. And when one runs, they all run. A big longhorn steer can also be dangerous, if it is angry. The cowboys must always be careful, and watchful, and calm, when they are among them.

[long-pause]

{img:lead-steer-old-longhorn-walking-front-herd-brass-bell-neck-dawn}
In every herd, there is a leader. Usually an old steer, big and wise, who walks at the front, and the others follow him. In Mr Hardin's herd, the leader is a huge old steer, with a pale brown coat and enormous horns, called Old Ned. Nobody knows who first gave him that name. He has made the trip to Kansas twice before, and come back to Texas. He knows the way. Mr Hardin says that Old Ned is worth any three cowboys.

[pause]

Old Ned wears a brass bell around his neck. Every morning, when the herd sets out, he walks to the front, and the other cattle follow the sound of his bell. At night, when they bed down, he lies down first, and the others lie down around him. At river crossings, he walks into the water first, and the others follow him. He is calm and steady. When the other cattle are nervous, he is not. He is a little like a captain on a ship.

[pause]

{img:boy-cowboy-patting-old-longhorn-steer-head-camp-evening-gentle}
Eli makes friends with Old Ned. Every evening, when the herd is resting, he goes to the old steer, and gives him a handful of corn, from the cook's supplies, when the cook is not looking. Old Ned lets Eli scratch his forehead, between his great horns. He closes his eyes, and grunts softly. The other cowboys laugh, and say that Eli has found himself a pet. But Eli does not care.

[long-pause]

In the three thousand cattle of Mr Hardin's herd, there are cattle from twenty different ranches. Each ranch has its own brand, a mark burned into the hide of its cattle, so that everyone can tell whose they are. Eli's mother's two hundred cattle carry the brand of the Brooks ranch. A B with a line under it. Eli can pick them out of the herd, from far away, by their colours and their faces. He feels responsible for them. They are his family's future.

[long-pause]

{img:trail-crew-1870-cowboys-gathered-chuckwagon-diverse-men-hats-saddles}
A cattle drive of three thousand cattle needs about twelve men. That is not very many, for so many animals. Each man is responsible for about two hundred and fifty cattle. And each man has his own job, and his own place around the herd. Let us meet the men of Mr Hardin's crew, as they sit around the fire, on the first evening.

[long-pause]

At the head of the crew is the trail boss, Walt Hardin himself. He is about fifty years old, a tall, lean man with a grey moustache, a deeply lined face, and pale eyes that seem to see everything. He has been driving cattle for twenty years. He is in charge of everything. He decides the route, where to camp, where to cross the rivers. He rides ahead of the herd, every day, looking for grass and water. And his word is law.

[pause]

His second in command is Sam Walker. He is about thirty-five, a Black cowboy, born enslaved on a ranch near the coast. He learned to ride and rope as a boy, and became one of the best cowboys in Texas. After the war, when he was free, he went to work for Mr Hardin. He has been up the trail three times. He is calm, wise and very skilled. Everyone respects him. He is the one who will teach Eli the most.

[pause]

{img:black-cowboy-1870-skilled-rider-roping-steer-prairie-calm-confident}
There is Mateo Garza, a Mexican vaquero, about forty years old, from the border country near the Rio Grande. The vaqueros are the original cowboys. They were herding cattle on horseback in Mexico long before the Americans came to Texas. Many of the things the cowboys use come from them. The saddle, the lariat, the chaps, the spurs. Even many of the words. Mateo is the best roper in the crew. He can rope a running steer by its horns, or its feet, from far away, with a single throw.

[pause]

There are the other cowboys. Young men from the farms and ranches of Texas. Jim and Tom, two brothers from Gonzales. A quiet young man called Will, who fought in the war, and does not talk about it. A cheerful Irishman called Paddy, who sings all day. A tall, thin cowboy called Slim, who is always hungry. And Pete, a boy of sixteen, who is the wrangler, the one who looks after the horses.

[long-pause]

{img:mexican-vaquero-sombrero-lariat-saddle-horse-skilled-texas-1870}
About a quarter of all the cowboys on the trail were Black or Mexican. Some historians think even more. In the cities and the towns, life could be hard for them, because of prejudice. On the trail, it was often a little different. What mattered most was whether you could do your job. Whether you could ride, rope, and handle cattle. Whether you could be trusted. Sam and Mateo are respected because they are the best.

[pause]

And there is the cook. His name is Henry Pruitt, but everyone calls him Cookie. He is the oldest man on the drive, about sixty, with a white beard and a bad leg, from falling off a horse many years ago. He cannot ride all day any more. So he drives the chuckwagon, the wagon with the food and the cooking things, and cooks for the whole crew. He is grumpy, bossy and very important. Everyone knows that you never, ever, argue with the cook.

[pause]

{img:old-trail-cook-white-beard-apron-chuckwagon-dutch-oven-grumpy-kind}
The cowboys come from many different backgrounds. But on the trail, they become a team. They eat together, sleep together and work together, for months. They depend on each other. If one man does his job badly, everyone suffers. If one man is in trouble, everyone helps. By the end of the drive, they will be like brothers. Some of them will stay friends for the rest of their lives.

[long-pause]

On the first evening, sitting around the fire, the men look at Eli, the new boy. Some of them tease him a little. They tell him stories about rattlesnakes in his boots and wolves in the night, to see if he is scared. Eli pretends not to be. Then Sam Walker tells them to leave the boy alone. He sits down next to Eli, and says quietly that everybody is new once. Even him. And that the best thing a new hand can do is keep his eyes open, and his mouth shut, and learn.

[long-pause]

{img:cowboy-gear-laid-out-saddle-lariat-chaps-boots-spurs-hat-bandana}
A cowboy's clothes and equipment are simple, practical and tough. Everything has a reason. Everything is made to last through months of riding, in sun, rain, wind and dust. And most of it comes from the Mexican vaqueros, who invented the cowboy's way of life long before.

[long-pause]

The most important thing is the hat. A cowboy's hat has a wide brim, to keep off the sun and the rain. A high crown, to keep his head cool. It is made of thick felt, which lasts for years. It can be used as a bucket, to carry water for his horse. As a fan, to light a fire. As a pillow at night. In 1865, a hatmaker called John Stetson made a famous hat for the West, called the Boss of the Plains. Soon every cowboy wanted one. But they were expensive. Eli's hat is an old one of his father's.

[pause]

Around his neck, a cowboy wears a bandana, a big square scarf of cotton, often red. It has a hundred uses. Pulled up over the nose and mouth, it keeps out the dust, when riding behind the herd. It can be a towel, a bandage, a sling, a hat on a hot day, or a strainer for muddy water. Eli wears a blue one, which Rose made for him.

[pause]

{img:cowboy-pulling-bandana-over-face-riding-drag-dust-cloud-behind-herd}
He wears a cotton shirt, a vest with pockets, and trousers of strong wool or canvas. Over his trousers, he wears chaps. Leather leggings, from the Spanish word chaparreras. They protect his legs from the thorny bushes of the brush country, and from rubbing against the saddle, and from the horns of the cattle. In the rain, he wears his slicker, the long oiled raincoat, which also covers his saddle.

[pause]

On his feet, he wears tall leather boots, with high heels and pointed toes. They are not made for walking. They are made for riding. The pointed toe slips easily into the stirrup. The high heel stops the foot from sliding through the stirrup, which could be dangerous, if he fell from his horse. Cowboys hate walking. They say that a cowboy would rather ride a mile to catch a horse than walk a hundred metres to the corral.

[long-pause]

{img:western-saddle-horn-leather-tooled-rope-coiled-hanging-close}
The saddle is the cowboy's most precious possession. It is heavy, made of wood covered with leather, with a high back and a horn at the front. The horn is for the rope. When a cowboy ropes a steer, he wraps the rope around the horn, so that the horse can hold the steer, not the man. A good saddle can cost a month's wages. Cowboys say that a man can sell his horse, but never his saddle. Eli's saddle is his father's. It is old and worn and comfortable.

[pause]

The rope, or lariat, from the Spanish la reata, is about twenty metres long, made of braided rawhide or hemp. It has a loop at the end, called a noose. A good cowboy can throw it, and catch a running steer by the horns or the feet, at full gallop. It takes years of practice. Mateo, the vaquero, can make the rope do almost anything. He can make the loop dance in the air, and jump through it. Eli practises every evening. He is getting better.

[pause]

{img:vaquero-teaching-boy-roping-loop-campfire-evening-practice-fence-post}
There are also spurs, little metal wheels on the heels of the boots, which jingle as the cowboy walks. A good cowboy hardly uses them. Just a touch, to tell the horse what to do. And there is a pistol, which most cowboys carry. Not for fighting, but for emergencies. For rattlesnakes, or for a horse that has broken its leg. Or to turn a stampede, by firing in the air. Mr Hardin does not allow pistols to be worn in camp. They are kept in the wagon.

[long-pause]

Each cowboy also has a bedroll. A canvas sheet, with blankets inside, rolled up and tied, and carried in the chuckwagon during the day. At night, he unrolls it on the ground, wherever he is, and sleeps in it, under the stars. His saddle is his pillow. His slicker covers him when it rains. And his hat goes over his face, to keep off the morning sun. It is not very comfortable. But after a long day in the saddle, the cowboys sleep like babies.

[long-pause]

{img:spring-roundup-texas-cowboys-gathering-cattle-from-brush-dust-riders}
Before the drive can begin, the cattle must be gathered. This is called the roundup. In the spring, the cowboys ride out over the open range, through the grassland and the brush, and find all the cattle, and bring them together, in one great herd. It takes several weeks. And it is hard, dusty, exciting work.

[long-pause]

The cattle of Texas are not kept in fields with fences. In 1870, there are almost no fences in Texas. The cattle roam freely, over huge areas of open land, belonging to many different ranchers. The cattle of one ranch mix with the cattle of another. So every spring, all the ranchers of an area work together, to round up all the cattle, and sort them out, by their brands.

[pause]

The cowboys ride out in pairs and small groups, in different directions. They search the creeks, the valleys, the thickets of mesquite and prickly pear. Some cattle are easy to find, grazing in the open. Others hide in the thick brush, where the thorns tear the cowboys' clothes and scratch their horses. The cowboys must ride into the thickets, and drive the cattle out, shouting and waving their hats.

[pause]

{img:cowboys-sorting-cattle-by-brands-large-herd-holding-ground-dust}
Then the cattle are driven to a big open place, called the holding ground. There, the cowboys sort them. The cattle of each ranch are separated from the others, by their brands. The cattle that will go to Kansas are chosen. Mostly steers, young male cattle, three or four years old, which are big and strong, and will sell for a good price. The cows and the young calves are left at home, to grow the herd for next year.

[pause]

The young calves that were born during the winter must be branded, with the brand of their mother's ranch. We will not look closely at that. It is quick, and the calves soon run back to their mothers. Eli helps his mother's neighbours with their calves, and keeps count of the Brooks cattle, carefully, in a little notebook. Two hundred steers, with the B and the line. All present.

[long-pause]

{img:evening-holding-ground-herd-settling-cowboys-circling-sunset-campfires}
At the end of the roundup, Mr Hardin's herd is ready. Three thousand steers, from twenty ranches. Before they leave, each steer is given a road brand, a special small mark, which shows that it belongs to this particular herd, for this drive. That way, if the cattle get mixed with another herd on the trail, the cowboys can tell which are theirs. The road brand of Mr Hardin's herd is a small H on the left shoulder.

[pause]

For a few days before the drive, the herd is held together on the holding ground, so that the cattle get used to each other, and to being a herd. The cowboys ride around them, day and night, keeping them together. The cattle are nervous at first. They have never been in such a big group. But slowly, they settle down. They begin to follow Old Ned, the lead steer. They begin to graze calmly. And they become a herd.

[pause]

{img:boy-cowboy-counting-cattle-notebook-pencil-on-horseback-herd-passing}
Mr Hardin counts the cattle, as they pass between two riders, through a narrow gap. He counts them in groups of a hundred, tying a knot in a piece of string for every hundred. Three thousand and twelve. A good herd, he says. Strong and healthy. He is pleased. He looks at the sky, and at the grass, which is fresh and green after the spring rains. Tomorrow, he says. Tomorrow we start for Kansas.

[long-pause]

That evening, the cowboys sit around the fire, cleaning their saddles, mending their clothes, and checking their equipment. Nobody says much. Everyone is thinking about the long journey ahead. Then Paddy, the Irishman, takes out a fiddle, and begins to play a lively tune. And Eli takes out his new harmonica, and tries to play along, very badly. Everyone laughs. And Paddy says that, in two months, by the time they reach Kansas, he will have taught him to play properly.

[long-pause]

{img:herd-starting-north-long-line-cattle-riders-positions-point-swing-flank-drag}
Early the next morning, the drive begins. Mr Hardin rides out in front, on his big grey horse, and waves his hat. Old Ned, the lead steer, gets up from the grass, shakes his great horns, and starts to walk north, with his bell clanking. And slowly, one by one, then dozens at a time, then hundreds, the other cattle get up and follow him. The great herd is moving.

[long-pause]

The cattle walk in a long line, stretching out across the prairie, sometimes for two or three kilometres. They do not walk in a tight group, but spread out, like a great river of horns and backs, flowing slowly north. The cowboys ride on both sides of the line, and at the front and the back, to keep the cattle together, and moving in the right direction. Each cowboy has his own position, which he keeps all the way to Kansas.

[pause]

At the very front, on each side of the lead steers, ride the point riders. They are the most experienced cowboys, Sam Walker and Mateo Garza. They guide the herd, steering the leading cattle in the right direction, following Mr Hardin, who rides far ahead, choosing the way. It is the most important position, and the most honourable. And also the cleanest, because they are in front of the dust.

[pause]

{img:point-rider-front-of-herd-guiding-lead-steer-open-prairie-morning-sun}
Behind them, along the sides of the herd, ride the swing riders and the flank riders. Their job is to keep the cattle from wandering off to the sides, and to keep the line moving smoothly. They ride back and forth, gently pushing the cattle back into line, when they stray. The brothers Jim and Tom ride swing. Paddy, Will and Slim ride flank.

[pause]

And at the very back of the herd, ride the drag riders. Their job is to push along the slowest cattle, the tired ones, the lame ones, the lazy ones, and the young ones, who fall behind. It is the hardest and dirtiest job of all. Because at the back of the herd, there is a great cloud of dust, kicked up by three thousand cattle. The drag riders ride in that dust, all day long. And the new cowboys always start at drag. Including Eli.

[long-pause]

{img:drag-riders-dust-cloud-back-of-herd-bandanas-over-faces-hot-afternoon}
By the end of the first day, Eli is covered in dust from head to toe. His face is brown, his hat is brown, his horse is brown. Dust in his eyes, in his nose, in his ears, between his teeth. He can hardly see. He keeps his bandana pulled up over his face, but the dust gets in anyway. The other drag rider, an older cowboy, laughs at him, and says that by the time they get to Kansas, Eli will have eaten a whole Texas ranch.

[pause]

The herd moves slowly. About fifteen to twenty-five kilometres a day. That is the right speed. If the cattle go faster, they lose weight, and are worth less at the end. If they go slower, the journey takes too long. Mr Hardin wants the cattle to arrive in Kansas fat and healthy. So they walk slowly, and graze as they go, eating the fresh spring grass along the trail.

[pause]

{img:herd-grazing-midday-rest-creek-cowboys-resting-horses-shade}
At midday, the herd stops for a rest, near a creek, so that the cattle can drink and graze. The cowboys take turns to ride to the chuckwagon, which has gone ahead, for a quick meal. Then the herd moves on again, in the afternoon, until the evening. And then, at the place that Mr Hardin has chosen, the cattle are bedded down for the night, on open ground, near water.

[long-pause]

On the first evening, Eli climbs down from his horse, and his legs are so stiff that he can hardly stand. Every part of his body aches. He has ridden all day before, at home. But not like this. He drinks some water, washes the dust from his face in the creek, and goes to the chuckwagon, where Cookie gives him a plate of beans and beef and biscuits. It is the best meal he has ever eaten. And then, before he can even finish, he falls asleep, sitting by the fire, with the plate on his knees.

[long-pause]

{img:cowboys-breakfast-before-dawn-campfire-coffee-tin-cups-stars-fading}
A day on the trail begins before dawn. At about four o'clock in the morning, while it is still dark, Cookie gets up, lights the fire, and starts making breakfast. Then he wakes the cowboys, by banging on a pan, and shouting. The cowboys crawl out of their bedrolls, cold and stiff, pull on their boots, and stumble to the fire, for coffee.

[long-pause]

Breakfast is quick. Coffee, black and strong, in tin cups. Sourdough biscuits, hot from the Dutch oven. Bacon, or beef, fried in a pan. And sometimes beans, left over from the night before. The cowboys eat standing up, or sitting on their heels, by the fire. Nobody talks much, in the morning. They are too tired, and too busy. When they have eaten, they put their dirty plates and cups in a big tub, which Cookie calls the wreck pan.

[pause]

Then the wrangler, Pete, brings in the remuda. The remuda is the herd of spare horses, which travels with the drive. Each cowboy has five to eight horses, which he rides in turn, so that they do not get too tired. The cowboys choose their horse for the morning, rope it, saddle it, and mount up. Then they ride out to the herd, which is beginning to get up from the ground, in the grey light of dawn.

[pause]

{img:remuda-spare-horses-herd-wrangler-boy-morning-cowboys-roping-mounts}
The herd sets out at sunrise. Old Ned leads the way, with his bell clanking. The cowboys take their positions. And the long line of cattle begins to move north, across the prairie, as the sun rises in the east, red and gold, and lights up the grass, and the dew, and the horns of three thousand cattle. It is a beautiful sight. Even after many days on the trail, Eli still looks at it with wonder.

[pause]

The morning is cool and fresh. The cattle walk steadily. The cowboys ride beside them, watching. There is not much to do, most of the time. Just riding, and watching, and keeping the cattle in line. Some cowboys sing, to pass the time. Some talk to their horses. Some just think. Eli thinks about home, about his mother and Rose, and about what Kansas will be like. And he eats dust.

[long-pause]

{img:chuckwagon-going-ahead-on-prairie-cook-driving-mules-dust-trail}
Meanwhile, Cookie packs up the camp, harnesses the mules to the chuckwagon, and drives ahead of the herd, to the place where they will stop at midday. The wrangler drives the remuda along behind him. When the herd arrives at noon, Cookie has a meal ready. The cowboys take turns to come in and eat, while the others watch the herd, which is grazing and resting.

[pause]

The afternoon is the hardest part of the day. The sun is high and hot. The dust is thick. The cattle are tired, and slow. The cowboys are tired too. Their eyes sting, their backs ache, their lips crack. They change horses, to give their morning horses a rest. And they keep going, mile after mile, across the endless grass, until the sun begins to sink in the west.

[pause]

{img:herd-bedding-down-evening-golden-light-cowboys-riding-slowly-around-cattle}
In the evening, Mr Hardin chooses a place for the night, with good grass and water. The cattle are allowed to drink, and graze for a while. Then the cowboys slowly bring them together, in a big loose circle, and the cattle lie down, one by one, to chew their cud and sleep. This is called bedding down the herd. Old Ned lies down first. And then all the others lie down around him, like a great family.

[long-pause]

Then the cowboys go to the chuckwagon, for supper. Beans, beef, biscuits, and coffee. Sometimes dried apples, stewed with sugar, for dessert. They eat by the fire, and talk about the day. Then some of them go to sleep, and some go out to ride the first watch, around the sleeping herd. And the camp grows quiet, under the stars. And tomorrow, it will all begin again. Every day, for two months, until Kansas.

[long-pause]

{img:chuckwagon-chuck-box-open-drawers-shelves-cook-preparing-food-prairie}
The most important man on a cattle drive, some cowboys say, is not the trail boss. It is the cook. A good cook can make a crew happy. A bad cook can make them miserable. And Cookie, the old man with the white beard and the bad leg, is one of the best cooks on the trail. The cowboys respect him, and are a little afraid of him.

[long-pause]

The cook's kitchen is the chuckwagon. Chuck is cowboy slang for food. The chuckwagon was invented a few years before, in 1866, by a famous cattleman called Charles Goodnight. He took an old army wagon, and built a big wooden box at the back of it, called the chuck box, with shelves and drawers, for the food and the cooking things. The back of the box folds down, on a hinge, to make a table, where the cook works.

[pause]

In the chuck box, there are drawers for flour, sugar, coffee, salt and spices. For beans, dried apples, rice and dried peaches. For tin plates, cups, knives and forks. For medicines, needles and thread, and a bottle of whisky, for emergencies only. Under the wagon, there is a cowhide, stretched like a hammock, where Cookie collects wood and dried cow dung, called cow chips, for the fire, as they go along. On the prairie, there are few trees, and cow chips make a good, hot fire.

[pause]

{img:dutch-ovens-coals-campfire-sourdough-biscuits-rising-cook-lifting-lid}
Cookie cooks in Dutch ovens. Big, heavy iron pots, with lids, which are put in the coals of the fire, with more coals on the lid. In them, he makes everything. Biscuits, stews, beans, cobblers. His sourdough biscuits are famous. He keeps a pot of sourdough starter, a bubbling mixture of flour, water and wild yeast, which he looks after like a baby. On cold nights, he even takes it into his bedroll with him, to keep it warm.

[pause]

The food on the trail is simple, and the same almost every day. Beef, from a steer of the herd, which is slaughtered every few days. Beans, cooked for hours with salt pork. Biscuits. Coffee, strong and black, and lots of it. Sometimes, dried fruit, stewed with sugar. And on special days, a cobbler, a sweet pie of dried peaches or apples, baked in the Dutch oven. When Cookie makes a cobbler, the whole crew cheers.

[long-pause]

{img:cowboys-eating-plates-on-knees-around-fire-cook-pouring-coffee-evening}
Cookie has strict rules, and everyone must obey them. Nobody touches the chuck box without permission. Nobody rides his horse near the cooking fire, because it stirs up dust into the food. Nobody complains about the food. If somebody complains, he has to be the cook the next day. So nobody ever complains. And when Cookie calls them to eat, they must come at once, or go without.

[pause]

But Cookie is also kind, in his gruff way. He is a doctor, a barber, a dentist and a banker, as well as a cook. He mends the cowboys' clothes, and cuts their hair. He puts medicine on their cuts and blisters. He holds their money for them, in a tin box. And he gives advice, whether they want it or not. When Eli has a terrible blister, from his new boots, Cookie puts some ointment on it, and wraps it up, and grumbles about young fools who do not break in their boots properly.

[pause]

{img:old-cook-and-boy-cowboy-talking-chuckwagon-tailgate-stars-lantern}
Late at night, when the others are asleep, Eli sometimes sits with Cookie, by the dying fire, and listens to his stories. Cookie has been everywhere. He was a sailor, a soldier, a gold miner in California, and a cook on a steamboat on the Mississippi. He has been up the trail four times. He tells Eli about all of it, in his slow, rough voice, while he smokes his pipe and watches the stars.

[long-pause]

On the trail, the chuckwagon is home. It is where the cowboys eat, sleep, keep their things, and gather in the evenings. At night, Cookie always points the tongue of the wagon, the long pole at the front, toward the North Star. So that in the morning, Mr Hardin knows exactly which way is north, even on a cloudy day. It is an old cowboy trick. And every morning, the tongue of the wagon points the way to Kansas.

[long-pause]

{img:remuda-horses-grazing-prairie-wrangler-boy-watching-sunset-many-colours}
A cowboy without a horse is not a cowboy. Horses are everything on the trail. The cowboys ride them all day, and often half the night. They depend on them completely. And a good cowboy loves his horses, and looks after them, sometimes better than he looks after himself.

[long-pause]

On Mr Hardin's drive, there are about a hundred horses, for twelve men. This herd of horses is called the remuda, from a Spanish word meaning change, or exchange. Each cowboy has his own string of six or seven horses, which nobody else may ride. He changes horses two or three times a day, so that none of them gets too tired. A tired horse is a dangerous horse, slow and clumsy, at the moment when you need it most.

[pause]

The remuda is looked after by the wrangler, Pete. He is sixteen, the youngest after Eli. It is his job to look after the horses, day and night. To drive them along behind the chuckwagon. To bring them in, when the cowboys need fresh horses. To watch them at night, so that they do not wander away. To check their hooves and their backs, for sores and stones. Pete knows every horse by name, and by its character.

[pause]

{img:cowboy-saddling-horse-morning-gentle-hands-horse-nuzzling-shoulder}
Each horse is different, and has its own job. Some horses are good for the morning, calm and steady. Some are good for the afternoon, strong and patient. Some are good for river crossings, because they are strong swimmers and not afraid of water. And the most precious of all is the night horse. A calm, sure-footed horse, with good eyes, who can find his way in the dark, and stay calm in a storm. A good night horse can save a cowboy's life.

[pause]

The best horses of all are the cutting horses. These are horses trained to work with cattle. A good cutting horse can pick out a single steer from the herd, and separate it from the others, all by itself, almost without the rider doing anything. It watches the steer, and moves when the steer moves, quick as a cat, turning and stopping, until the steer gives up and goes where the horse wants. It is wonderful to watch. Mateo's horse, a little brown mare called Paloma, is the best cutting horse on the drive.

[long-pause]

{img:cutting-horse-separating-steer-from-herd-quick-turn-dust-vaquero-riding}
Eli has his mare Dolly, from home, and six other horses from Mr Hardin's remuda. There is a big bay called Sandy, for the mornings. A grey called Smoke, who is a good swimmer. A spotted pony called Patches, who is clever but lazy. And for the night, a quiet black horse called Midnight, who has very good eyes, and never gets frightened. Eli learns all their characters. Which one likes to have his ears scratched. Which one bites. Which one hates rain.

[pause]

But Dolly is his favourite. She is not the fastest horse in the remuda, or the strongest. But she is the most loyal. She comes when he whistles. She follows him around the camp like a dog. When he is sad, and thinking of home, he goes to her, and puts his face against her warm neck, and tells her about it. And she stands very still, and listens, and blows softly through her nose.

[pause]

{img:boy-face-against-horse-neck-evening-remuda-quiet-moment-sunset}
At night, the horses are kept near the camp, in a rope corral, or watched by Pete, as they graze. The cowboys keep their night horse saddled and tied near their bedroll, in case they are needed suddenly, in the night. On the open prairie, there are dangers for horses too. Rattlesnakes in the grass. Prairie dog holes, which can break a horse's leg. Wolves, though they rarely attack. And thieves, who sometimes try to steal horses at night.

[long-pause]

Sam Walker says that a cowboy should always look after his horse before himself. Water the horse before you drink. Feed the horse before you eat. Rub down the horse before you rest. Because without your horse, you are nothing, out here on the prairie. Eli takes this advice very seriously. Every evening, before he eats his supper, he rubs Dolly down with a handful of dry grass, and checks her hooves, and gives her a little corn. And only then does he go to the fire.

[long-pause]

{img:cowboy-night-herding-riding-slowly-around-sleeping-cattle-stars-moon}
The herd must be watched all night. Even when the cattle are lying down, sleeping, two cowboys ride slowly around them, in opposite directions, all night long. This is called night herding. The night is divided into watches of about two hours. Every cowboy takes his turn, one watch every night. So every cowboy loses two hours of sleep, every night, for two months.

[long-pause]

Why must the cattle be watched at night? Because longhorns are nervous animals. A sudden noise, a coyote howling, a flash of lightning, a horse sneezing, can startle them. And if they are startled, they might get up and run. And three thousand running cattle, in the dark, is the most dangerous thing on the trail. So the night herders ride quietly around the herd, keeping them calm, and making sure that they stay together.

[pause]

And they sing. That is the most famous thing about night herding. The cowboys sing to the cattle, softly, as they ride around them in the dark. It is not because the cattle love music. It is because the cattle need to know where the riders are. If a rider suddenly appears out of the darkness, without warning, the cattle might be frightened. But if they hear him singing, softly, as he comes closer, they know that he is there, and they stay calm.

[pause]

{img:cowboy-singing-softly-on-horseback-night-sleeping-herd-starry-sky}
The cowboys sing all kinds of songs. Old hymns, from church. Old ballads, from Ireland and Scotland. Sad songs, about home and mothers and lost loves. Funny songs, which they make up themselves. Songs about the trail, and the cattle, and the long ride to Kansas. Some of these cowboy songs are still sung today, all over the world. Their tunes are slow and gentle, like lullabies. Because that is exactly what they are. Lullabies for cattle.

[pause]

Eli's first night watch is from midnight to two in the morning. Sam Walker rides it with him, to show him how. They ride slowly around the sleeping herd, in opposite directions, meeting every half hour or so, on the far side. Sam sings an old hymn, in his deep, soft voice. Eli is too shy to sing, at first. So he plays his harmonica, very softly. The cattle do not seem to mind. Sam says that it is the first time he has heard cattle being played to sleep with a harmonica.

[long-pause]

{img:milky-way-prairie-night-sky-cattle-silhouettes-lone-rider-quiet}
The night on the prairie is very beautiful. The sky is enormous, and full of stars. More stars than Eli has ever seen, even at home. The Milky Way stretches across the sky, like a river of light. The moon rises over the edge of the prairie, huge and golden, and climbs slowly into the sky, turning silver. The grass is silver in the moonlight. And the herd lies quietly, three thousand dark shapes, breathing softly.

[pause]

The cowboys tell the time at night by the stars. They watch the Big Dipper, the group of seven stars that the English call the Plough, as it turns slowly around the North Star, during the night, like the hand of a giant clock. When the Big Dipper has turned a certain distance, the watch is over. Sam teaches Eli how to read it. Eli is amazed. A clock, in the sky, that never stops, and never needs winding.

[pause]

{img:big-dipper-north-star-night-sky-cowboy-pointing-teaching-boy}
Sometimes, at night, there are other sounds. A coyote howling, far away, on the prairie. Then another, answering. An owl. The wind in the grass. The soft lowing of a cow, dreaming. The jingle of a spur. The creak of a saddle. And always, the soft singing of the night herders, drifting across the sleeping herd. Eli thinks that he has never heard anything so peaceful.

[long-pause]

At two o'clock, the next watch comes out from the camp. Two sleepy cowboys, yawning, pulling on their coats. Eli and Sam ride back to the camp, unsaddle their horses, and crawl into their bedrolls. Eli lies on his back, looking up at the stars, listening to the singing of the next watch, far away. And he falls asleep, thinking that, despite the dust and the tiredness and the sore legs, he would not want to be anywhere else in the world.

[long-pause]

{img:red-river-wide-brown-water-cattle-herd-waiting-bank-cowboys-looking}
The Chisholm Trail crosses many rivers. The Colorado, the Brazos, the Red River, the Washita, the Canadian, the Cimarron, the Arkansas. They are wide, brown, and often fast. There are no bridges. The cattle must swim across. And crossing a river with three thousand cattle is the most difficult and the most exciting part of the whole drive.

[long-pause]

Before each crossing, Mr Hardin rides ahead, to look at the river. He looks for a good place to cross. A place where the banks are low and firm, where the water is not too deep or too fast, and where there is no quicksand. Quicksand is wet, soft sand, in the riverbed, which looks firm, but sucks in anything that steps on it. The cattle must be kept away from it. Mr Hardin tests the river on his horse, riding in and out, until he is satisfied.

[pause]

The best time to cross is in the morning, with the sun behind the cattle, so that it does not shine in their eyes and frighten them. And it is best if the cattle are thirsty, so that they want to go into the water. So the herd is not allowed to drink before the crossing. Then, when they come to the river, they are eager to go in. Old Ned leads the way, as always. He walks down the bank, into the water, and starts to swim. And the others follow him.

[pause]

{img:longhorns-swimming-across-river-horns-and-heads-above-water-riders-alongside}
The cattle swim with only their heads and horns above the water. From the bank, it looks like a great field of horns, floating across the river. The cowboys swim their horses beside the cattle, on the downstream side, to keep them from being carried away by the current. Some cowboys cannot swim. So they hold on to their horses' manes or tails, and let the horses pull them across. The horses are good swimmers. Eli rides Smoke, his best swimmer, and holds on tightly.

[pause]

The most dangerous river is the Red River, which is the border between Texas and the Indian Territory, which is now Oklahoma. In the spring, it is often in flood, wide, deep and fast, full of red mud, which gives it its name. When Mr Hardin's herd arrives at the Red River, it is high, after the spring rains. They wait for two days, camped on the bank, for the water to go down. Other herds arrive, and wait too. There are thousands of cattle, all waiting.

[long-pause]

{img:cowboys-guiding-cattle-in-fast-river-current-tension-horses-swimming}
On the third morning, Mr Hardin decides that the river is low enough. The cattle go in, following Old Ned. Everything goes well, at first. The cattle swim steadily. The cowboys swim beside them. And then, in the middle of the river, something goes wrong. A floating tree, carried by the current, comes down the river, straight into the herd. The cattle in front of it panic. They turn back, and start to swim in a circle, round and round, in the middle of the river. This is called milling. If it goes on, they will tire, and some may be swept away.

[pause]

Sam Walker sees it at once. He rides his horse right into the middle of the milling cattle, shouting and waving his hat, and pushes them, and turns them, toward the far bank. Mateo follows him. Eli, on the edge of the herd, holds his breath. And then, slowly, the cattle stop circling, and begin to swim again, toward the far bank. Old Ned is already there, climbing up out of the water, ringing his bell. And the others follow the sound.

[pause]

{img:wet-cattle-climbing-out-far-bank-cowboys-relieved-shaking-water-off}
It takes all morning, but at last, all three thousand cattle are across. Wet, tired and muddy, they climb up the far bank, and shake themselves, and start to graze, as if nothing had happened. The cowboys count them. None are missing. Sam Walker is soaking wet, from head to toe, and smiling. Mr Hardin shakes his hand, and says that it was the finest piece of riding he has ever seen. Sam just shrugs, and says that it was nothing.

[long-pause]

That evening, they camp on the far side of the Red River. They are out of Texas now, in the Indian Territory. The cowboys hang their wet clothes on the bushes to dry, and sit around the fire in their blankets. Cookie makes a peach cobbler, to celebrate. And Eli writes a letter to his mother, by the light of the fire, to send with the next herd going south. He tells her about the river crossing. But he leaves out the part about the floating tree. He does not want her to worry.

[long-pause]

{img:indian-territory-1870-rolling-green-hills-creeks-trees-cherokee-farmsteads}
On the other side of the Red River lies the Indian Territory. Today, it is the state of Oklahoma. In 1870, it is the home of many Native American nations. Some of them have lived on the plains for centuries. Others were moved here by the American government, forty years before, from their homelands in the east, on long and terrible journeys, which are remembered as the Trail of Tears. We will remember them, but gently.

[long-pause]

The nations of the Indian Territory include the Cherokee, the Chickasaw, the Choctaw, the Creek and the Seminole, who came from the east. And there are the peoples of the plains, like the Comanche, the Kiowa, the Wichita and the Caddo. Each nation has its own government, its own laws and its own land. The Chickasaw and the Cherokee have towns, schools, farms and newspapers, some printed in the Cherokee alphabet, which a Cherokee scholar called Sequoyah invented.

[pause]

The cattle drives cross the lands of these nations, on the way to Kansas. The cattle eat their grass and drink their water. So the nations ask the drovers to pay a toll, a small payment for each herd that crosses their land, usually ten cents a head, or a few cattle. Most trail bosses pay it. It is fair, Mr Hardin says. It is their grass. And if you treat people fairly, they will treat you fairly too.

[pause]

{img:native-american-riders-meeting-trail-boss-prairie-calm-respectful-talk}
One morning, a group of riders appears on a hill, near the herd. They are Chickasaw men, on horseback, in a mixture of traditional and American clothes. Their leader rides down to meet Mr Hardin. They talk, in English, for a while. The leader explains that the herd is crossing the land of his people, and asks for the toll. Mr Hardin agrees. He gives them some money, and ten steers, from his own cattle. The leader thanks him. And the riders turn, and ride away, driving the steers before them.

[pause]

Eli watches from a distance. He has heard many stories about the Native Americans, some of them frightening. But these men are polite and dignified. Sam Walker tells him that most of the stories are exaggerated. That the Native American nations are people like anyone else. Farmers, ranchers, traders and families. Who have lost a great deal, and who are trying to keep what is left.

[long-pause]

{img:trading-post-indian-territory-wooden-store-people-horses-wagons-summer}
In the Indian Territory, there are trading posts along the trail. Little stores, built of logs, where the cowboys can buy flour, coffee, tobacco and other supplies. They are run by traders, some white, some Native American, some of mixed family. Jesse Chisholm himself, the trader who gave his name to the trail, had a trading post here. He passed away a couple of years before, in 1868, but his name lives on.

[pause]

The land of the Indian Territory is beautiful. Rolling hills, covered with tall grass and wild flowers. Creeks lined with cottonwood trees, and pecan and walnut trees. Herds of deer and antelope. And the skies, enormous and blue, with white clouds sailing across them. The cattle graze well here. They are getting fatter every day. Mr Hardin is pleased.

[pause]

{img:prairie-flowers-indian-territory-spring-hills-herd-distance-blue-sky}
One evening, a family of Cherokee farmers comes to the camp, with a wagon full of vegetables and eggs, to sell to the cowboys. Fresh potatoes, onions, green beans and a basket of strawberries. The cowboys buy everything. After weeks of beans and beef, fresh vegetables are a great treat. Cookie makes a stew with them, and everyone says it is the best meal of the whole drive. The Cherokee farmer's son, a boy of Eli's age, shows Eli how to play a game with a stick and a ball. They play until it is dark.

[long-pause]

Eli thinks about this evening, many times, in later years. About the Cherokee boy, and the game, and the strawberries. And about the Native American nations, who were already losing their land, and would lose much more, in the years to come. He did not understand it all, at fifteen. But he remembered the kindness of the family with the wagon. And he always said, afterwards, that the people of the Indian Territory had treated him better than many people in the towns.

[long-pause]

{img:thunderstorm-building-over-prairie-evening-dark-clouds-herd-uneasy-cowboys}
One evening, in the Indian Territory, the air becomes very still and heavy. It is hot and sticky, even after the sun goes down. There is no wind. The cattle are restless. They do not want to lie down. They stand around, lowing softly, and turning their heads this way and that. Mr Hardin looks at the sky in the west, where great dark clouds are piling up, higher and higher. He tells everyone to be ready. A storm is coming.

[long-pause]

Storms on the prairie can be terrible. They come quickly, with great black clouds, strong winds, heavy rain, hail and lightning. The lightning is the worst. On the flat, open prairie, there is nothing taller than a man on a horse. And the sound of the thunder, and the flash of the lightning, frightens the cattle more than anything else. A storm at night is what every cowboy fears. Because a storm at night often means a stampede.

[pause]

All the cowboys go out to the herd, that night. Nobody sleeps. They ride slowly around the cattle, singing, trying to keep them calm. The night is very dark. The clouds have covered the stars. Lightning flickers on the horizon, silent at first, then with a distant rumble of thunder. Eli rides Midnight, his black night horse, who is very calm. He sings, with a dry throat, every song he knows.

[pause]

{img:lightning-flash-prairie-night-cattle-heads-raised-riders-silhouettes}
And then the storm arrives. The wind comes first, a sudden cold blast, that blows the cowboys' hats off. Then the rain, in great sheets. Then a flash of lightning, so bright that for a moment, the whole prairie is as bright as day, and every horn of every steer is lit up, with little blue flames dancing at their tips. The cowboys call it fox fire. And then, immediately, a crash of thunder, so loud that the ground shakes.

[pause]

In that moment, the whole herd jumps to its feet, at once, as if it were one animal. And it runs. Three thousand cattle, running in the dark, through the rain, with a sound like thunder itself. That is a stampede. The ground trembles. The cowboys can hear nothing but the roar of hooves, the crash of the thunder, and the hiss of the rain.

[long-pause]

{img:cowboys-riding-alongside-running-herd-night-rain-turning-leaders}
The cowboys know what to do. They gallop beside the running herd, in the darkness, trying to reach the front. They must turn the leaders. If they can make the front of the herd turn, the rest will follow, and the whole herd will start to run in a circle. And slowly, running in a circle, the cattle will tire, and slow down, and stop. It is called milling. It is the only way to stop a stampede.

[pause]

Sam Walker and Mateo reach the front first. They ride alongside the leaders, shouting, waving their slickers, and firing their pistols in the air. Slowly, slowly, the leaders begin to turn. The herd bends, like a great river turning. Eli, on the edge of the herd, rides as hard as Midnight can go, in the dark, trusting the horse to see the ground. He cannot see anything. He just holds on, and rides, and shouts.

[pause]

{img:herd-milling-in-circle-slowing-down-rain-easing-cowboys-circling-dawn-light}
And then, at last, the herd is circling. Running round and round, slower and slower. The storm is passing. The thunder is moving away to the east, grumbling. The rain is getting lighter. And slowly, the cattle stop running. They stand still, panting, steaming in the cool air, tired out. The cowboys ride slowly around them, singing again, softly, in hoarse voices. And the herd begins to calm down.

[long-pause]

In the morning, the sky is clear and washed clean. The sun rises over a wet, shining prairie. The cowboys are soaked, exhausted and muddy. Some of the cattle have scattered, and the cowboys spend the whole day finding them, and bringing them back. At the end of the day, Mr Hardin counts them. Only twenty-three are missing, out of three thousand. And all of the men are safe. Mr Hardin says that they were lucky. And that they rode well. Eli, who rode in his first stampede, falls asleep at supper, again, with a biscuit in his hand.

[long-pause]

{img:buffalo-herd-grazing-great-plains-kansas-1870-vast-numbers-horizon}
After the Indian Territory, the trail climbs slowly onto the great plains. The land becomes higher, flatter and wider. The trees disappear, except along the creeks. And there is grass. Nothing but grass, in every direction, as far as the eye can see, waving in the wind like the sea. It is called the prairie. And it is one of the most beautiful and lonely landscapes in the world.

[long-pause]

The grass of the prairie is tall in some places, up to a horse's belly, and short in others. In the spring and early summer, it is full of wild flowers. Purple coneflowers, yellow sunflowers, red Indian paintbrush, blue lupins, white daisies. The prairie is like a great carpet of colour, rolling away to the horizon. And the wind is always blowing, making the grass and the flowers ripple and sway.

[pause]

The prairie is full of animals. Antelope, the fastest animals in America, with white rumps that flash as they run. Deer in the creek bottoms. Coyotes, which howl at night. Jackrabbits, with enormous ears. Prairie chickens, which dance in the spring. Hawks circling high overhead. And prairie dogs. Little brown animals, a little like squirrels, which live in great towns of burrows, underground, with thousands of them, popping up out of their holes, and chattering.

[pause]

{img:prairie-dog-town-little-animals-standing-on-mounds-watching-grassland}
Eli loves the prairie dogs. They stand on top of their little mounds, on their back legs, looking around, and calling to each other, with a high, chirping bark. When they see danger, they all disappear, at once, into their holes. And then, after a while, they pop up again, one by one, to see if it is safe. But the cowboys must be careful. The holes of the prairie dogs can trip a horse, and break its leg. So they ride around the prairie dog towns.

[pause]

And there are the buffalo. In 1870, there are still millions of American bison, which everyone calls buffalo, on the great plains. They live in enormous herds. One day, the cowboys see a herd of buffalo, far away, moving slowly across the prairie. It is so big that it takes all day to pass. The ground seems to be moving. Thousands and thousands of great brown shaggy animals, with their huge heads and humped shoulders, grazing as they go.

[long-pause]

{img:cowboys-on-horseback-watching-buffalo-herd-pass-awe-sunset-dust}
Mr Hardin stops the herd, to let the buffalo pass. If the cattle get mixed with the buffalo, it could be a disaster. So the cowboys sit on their horses, on a low hill, and watch. Eli has never seen anything so amazing. Sam Walker tells him to remember it. He says that the buffalo are being killed, in great numbers, by hunters, for their hides. And that one day, perhaps, they will be gone. Eli does not believe him. There are so many of them. How could they ever be gone?

[pause]

But Sam was right. In the next fifteen years, the great herds of buffalo were almost completely destroyed, by hunters with rifles, who killed them in their millions, for their hides and their tongues. By 1890, there were only a few hundred left, out of tens of millions. It was one of the great tragedies of American history. For the Native American peoples of the plains, who depended on the buffalo, it was a catastrophe. Today, thanks to the work of a few people who saved them, there are again about half a million buffalo in America.

[pause]

{img:sunset-over-endless-prairie-golden-grass-lone-tree-herd-bedding-down}
In the evenings, on the prairie, the sunsets are magnificent. The sky turns gold, then orange, then red, then purple, across the whole enormous horizon. The grass glows. The clouds are on fire. And the herd, bedding down for the night, is a great dark shape against the burning sky. The cowboys sit on their horses and watch, in silence. Some of them take off their hats.

[long-pause]

Eli writes in his little notebook, that evening, by the light of the fire. He tries to describe the prairie, the flowers, the prairie dogs, the buffalo, and the sunset. But the words are not good enough. Nothing he writes is as big, or as beautiful, as the real thing. So in the end, he just writes, today we saw the buffalo, and the sky was on fire. And then he closes the book, and lies back, and looks at the stars, which are coming out, one by one, over the endless grass.

[long-pause]

{img:cowboys-around-campfire-night-fiddle-harmonica-singing-stories-chuckwagon}
In the evenings, after supper, when the herd is bedded down, and the first night watch has ridden out, the other cowboys sit around the campfire, for an hour or so, before they go to sleep. It is the best time of the day. The work is done. The fire is warm. The coffee pot is on the coals. And the stars are coming out over the prairie.

[long-pause]

The cowboys talk. About the day. About the weather. About the cattle and the horses. About the places they have been, and the people they have known. About their homes, and their families, and the girls they left behind. About what they will do with their wages, when they get to Kansas. Some want to buy a new saddle. Some want to buy a ranch of their own, one day. Some just want to have a good time in town.

[pause]

They tell stories. Tall tales, which nobody believes, but everybody enjoys. Paddy tells about a cowboy in Texas who was so tall that he had to climb a ladder to shave himself. Slim tells about a horse so fast that it could outrun its own shadow. Jim tells about a winter so cold that the words froze in the air, and the cowboys had to wait until spring to hear what they had said. Everyone laughs, and tries to tell a bigger story.

[pause]

{img:old-cook-telling-tall-tale-cowboys-laughing-firelight-faces-glow}
Cookie tells the best stories. About his years at sea, when he sailed around Cape Horn. About the gold fields of California, where men found nuggets as big as eggs. About the Mississippi steamboats, with their grand saloons. And about a famous bear in the mountains of New Mexico, which stole his breakfast every morning, for a whole week, until he left a pot of beans for it, and they became friends. Nobody knows if any of it is true.

[pause]

And there is music. Paddy plays his fiddle, old Irish jigs and reels, and cowboy songs. Mateo plays a little Spanish guitar, which he keeps wrapped in a blanket in the chuckwagon, and sings sad, beautiful songs from Mexico, about love and the moon. And Eli plays his harmonica. By now, after weeks of practice, and Paddy's lessons, he can play quite well. He plays the songs his mother used to sing. And the cowboys go quiet, and listen.

[long-pause]

{img:vaquero-playing-guitar-singing-campfire-cowboys-listening-quiet-night}
Some of the songs are about the trail itself. About the dust, and the rivers, and the long ride to Kansas. About the cattle, and the night herding, and the stars. About the cowboy's life, lonely and hard, but free. One song tells of a young cowboy who rode up the trail, and never came home. It is a sad song. The cowboys sing it softly, and some of them look into the fire, and think of their own homes.

[pause]

Other songs are funny. About a cowboy who tried to ride a horse that could not be ridden, and was thrown so high that he came down in Kansas. About a cook whose biscuits were so hard that the cowboys used them as horseshoes. Paddy makes up new verses every night, about the men of the crew. Slim's hunger. Pete's horses. Eli's harmonica. Everyone laughs at their own verse, and tries to make one about Paddy in return.

[pause]

{img:cowboy-writing-letter-by-firelight-pencil-paper-on-saddle-knee}
Some cowboys write letters, by the light of the fire, to send home with the next herd going south, or to post in Kansas. Some read, if they have a book. There are not many books on the trail, but they are passed around, until they fall apart. Will, the quiet one who fought in the war, has a book of poems, which he reads over and over. One evening, he reads one aloud, about the stars and the sea. Nobody says anything. But everyone listens.

[long-pause]

Then, one by one, the cowboys go to their bedrolls, spread on the ground around the fire. They take off their boots and their hats, and lie down, with their saddles for pillows. The fire burns low. The coffee pot is empty. The fiddle is put away. And from out on the prairie, the soft singing of the night herders drifts across the darkness, to the sleeping camp, like a lullaby, for cattle and cowboys alike.

[long-pause]

{img:crossing-into-kansas-prairie-flat-wheat-fields-first-farms-herd-moving}
After about seven weeks on the trail, the herd crosses the border into Kansas. It is a great moment. The cowboys cheer and throw their hats in the air. They have come almost a thousand kilometres, from the hill country of Texas, across the rivers, the Indian Territory and the prairie. And now, there is only a little way left to go. About a hundred and fifty kilometres to Abilene.

[long-pause]

Kansas is different from Texas. The land is flat and open, like the prairie. But here, there are farms. Homesteaders have come from the east, and from Europe, to farm the land. They have built little houses of sod, which is blocks of grass and earth, cut from the prairie, because there are no trees for wood. They plough the prairie, and plant wheat and corn. And they have fences, to keep the cattle out of their crops.

[pause]

The farmers do not like the cattle drives. The longhorns trample their crops. And the longhorns carry a disease, called Texas fever, spread by ticks. The longhorns themselves are not affected by it. But it kills the cattle of the farmers. So the farmers of Kansas have made laws, to keep the Texas cattle out of the settled parts of the state. Every year, the line moves further west. And the trail must move with it.

[pause]

{img:sod-house-homestead-kansas-family-standing-outside-wheat-field-wind}
Mr Hardin is careful. He keeps the herd away from the farms, on the open prairie. He pays the farmers for any damage. And when a farmer comes to complain, he listens politely, and gives him a steer, to make up for it. Some of the farmers become friendly, after that. They sell the cowboys eggs, milk, butter and fresh bread. One farmer's wife, from Sweden, gives Eli a whole loaf of rye bread, warm from the oven, because he reminds her of her son.

[pause]

Eli is fascinated by the sod houses. They look like little hills, with doors and windows. Inside, they are cool in summer, and warm in winter, but dark, and a little dusty. Snakes and mice sometimes come through the walls. The farmers are poor, and work very hard. Their children run out to look at the herd, barefoot, in the grass. Eli waves to them. He thinks of Rose, at home in Texas, and wonders if she is all right.

[long-pause]

{img:cowboys-washing-in-creek-shaving-before-town-laughing-splashing-summer}
As they get closer to Abilene, the cowboys start to get excited. They have been on the trail for two months. They are dirty, tired, sunburnt and dusty. Their clothes are in rags. Their hair is long, and their beards are wild. So, on the last day before Abilene, Mr Hardin lets the herd graze in a quiet valley, with a creek. And the cowboys wash. They wash their clothes, their bodies and their hair, in the creek, laughing and splashing like boys.

[pause]

Cookie cuts everyone's hair, with his big scissors, sitting on a bucket by the chuckwagon. The cowboys shave, with their razors, looking into a little mirror, hanging on the wagon. They put on the clean shirts they have saved, at the bottom of their bags, for this day. Eli does not need to shave yet, much to the amusement of the other cowboys. But he washes his face and his hair, and puts on his clean blue shirt, and his bandana, and feels very grown up.

[pause]

{img:distant-town-abilene-kansas-1870-railroad-water-tower-smoke-horizon}
In the evening, from the top of a low hill, the cowboys can see Abilene. A little town, on the flat prairie, beside a river. Wooden buildings. A water tower. The smoke of a train, far away, along the railway. And the great cattle pens, beside the tracks, already full of cattle from other herds. After two months of grass and sky, it looks like a city to Eli.

[long-pause]

That night, the cowboys sit around the campfire, for the last time on the trail. They are quiet. Excited about tomorrow. But also a little sad. The trail has been hard. But it has also been their home, for two months. They have become friends. Tomorrow, they will sell the cattle, and get their wages, and go their separate ways. Paddy plays a slow tune on his fiddle. Eli plays along on his harmonica. And Old Ned, the lead steer, lies by the fire, at the edge of the light, as if he were listening too.

[long-pause]

{img:abilene-kansas-1870-main-street-wooden-buildings-cowboys-horses-boardwalk}
In the morning, the herd is driven into Abilene. The cattle walk slowly down toward the great pens beside the railway, with Old Ned at the front, ringing his bell, as always. The people of the town come out to watch. Buyers from Chicago and the East, in their suits and hats. Railway men. Shopkeepers. Children. And other cowboys, from other herds, who have arrived before them.

[long-pause]

Abilene in 1870 is a strange little town. Three years ago, it was just a few log cabins on the prairie. Now, because of the cattle trade, it is busy, noisy and growing fast. There is a main street, with wooden buildings on both sides, and a wooden boardwalk to walk on, because the street is mud when it rains and dust when it does not. There are hotels, stores, banks, saloons, a blacksmith, a barber, and the cattle pens and the railway station.

[pause]

The most famous building is the Drovers Cottage, a big hotel built by Joseph McCoy, the man who started the cattle trade in Abilene. It has a hundred rooms, and a long porch, where the cattle buyers and the trail bosses sit, and do business. Mr Hardin goes there, in his best coat, to meet the buyers, and to sell the herd. The cowboys wait outside with the cattle, in the pens.

[pause]

{img:cattle-pens-railroad-stockyards-abilene-cattle-being-loaded-onto-train-cars}
The buyers look at the cattle. They walk along the pens, looking at the steers, checking how fat and healthy they are. They talk with Mr Hardin for a long time, about prices. Mr Hardin is a good bargainer. In the end, he sells the herd for a good price. About twenty-five dollars a head. Six times more than they were worth in Texas. It is a very good result. Mr Hardin comes out of the hotel, smiling, and shakes hands with every cowboy.

[pause]

Then the cattle are loaded onto the train. It is an enormous job. The cattle are driven up wooden ramps, into the cattle cars, about twenty in each car. The cowboys use long poles to push them along. Some of the cattle do not want to go. They have never seen a train before, and they are frightened by its noise and its smoke. But slowly, all three thousand are loaded. And the long train, with its dozens of cars, puffs away, toward Chicago, and the cities of the East.

[long-pause]

{img:cowboy-saying-goodbye-to-old-longhorn-steer-pen-gentle-moment}
But not Old Ned. Mr Hardin never sells his lead steer. He is too valuable. Old Ned will go back to Texas with the cowboys, and lead another herd north next year. Eli is very glad. He goes to the pen where Old Ned is waiting, and gives him a handful of corn, and scratches his forehead. The old steer closes his eyes, and grunts. He has done it again. Three trips to Kansas. Perhaps four, next year.

[pause]

Then the cowboys are paid. Mr Hardin pays each man his wages, in silver dollars and paper money, at a table on the porch of the hotel. About sixty or ninety dollars each, for two or three months of work. It is more money than many of them have ever had at once. Eli is paid fifty dollars, for his first drive. He holds it in his hands, and can hardly believe it. And Mr Hardin gives him an envelope for his mother, with the money for her two hundred cattle. Five thousand dollars. A fortune.

[pause]

{img:cowboys-new-clothes-store-buying-boots-hats-shirts-mirror-laughing}
The cowboys go into the town, to spend their money. First, to the barber, for a proper shave and a haircut, and a hot bath, in a big tin tub, the first hot bath in two months. Then to the stores, to buy new clothes. New shirts, trousers, boots and hats. Some of them buy presents for their families at home. Some of them go to the saloons, to drink and play cards. Mr Hardin warns them to be careful with their money, and not to get into trouble.

[long-pause]

Eli is careful. He buys a new hat, a real Stetson, which he has always wanted. And presents for his family. A warm shawl for his mother, of soft blue wool. A book for Rose, with pictures of animals from all over the world. And a little silver locket, with a picture of a horse on it, also for Rose. He puts the rest of his money, and his mother's envelope, in a leather pouch, inside his shirt, and keeps his hand on it, all day. In the evening, he sits on the porch of the hotel, with his new hat on, and watches the sun go down over the prairie, feeling very proud.

[long-pause]

{img:cowboys-riding-south-small-group-prairie-going-home-remuda-chuckwagon}
After a few days in Abilene, the cowboys go home. Some stay in Kansas, looking for work on the ranches there. Some take the train east, to see the cities. But most of them ride back to Texas, with Mr Hardin, the chuckwagon and the remuda. Without the cattle, it is much faster. They ride about fifty kilometres a day. The journey that took two months going north takes only three or four weeks going south.

[long-pause]

It is strange, riding without the herd. No dust. No night herding. No cattle to watch. Just the cowboys, riding along together, across the prairie, talking and singing. They feel light and free, with money in their pockets, and new hats on their heads. They stop at the same places they passed on the way north. The rivers, the trading posts, the Cherokee farm. Eli waves to the Cherokee boy, who remembers him, and runs out to say hello.

[pause]

At the Red River, they part. Some cowboys turn west, to their homes in other parts of Texas. Jim and Tom, the brothers, turn east, toward Gonzales. Paddy says he is going to Fort Worth, to see a girl. Will, the quiet one, does not say where he is going. They shake hands, and promise to meet again, next year, on the trail. Some of them do. Some of them never see each other again. That is the way of the trail.

[pause]

{img:cowboys-parting-ways-shaking-hands-river-crossing-going-different-directions}
Sam Walker and Mateo ride with Eli, most of the way home, because their homes are in the same direction. Sam says that Eli has done well, for his first drive. That he has learned to ride drag, and swing, and night herd, and swim the rivers, and turn a stampede. That he is a real cowboy now. Mateo gives Eli his old lariat, which he has used for twenty years. Eli is so proud, he does not know what to say.

[pause]

At last, one evening in late summer, Eli rides over a hill, and sees the little ranch below him, in the valley. The house with the long porch. The big old oak trees. The corral. The windmill. Smoke rising from the chimney. And on the porch, a woman and a girl, looking up the lane, shading their eyes against the setting sun. They have heard that the herds are coming back. They have been watching for him for days.

[long-pause]

{img:mother-and-sister-running-down-lane-to-meet-returning-boy-on-horse-evening}
Rose sees him first. She screams, and runs down the lane, as fast as she can. Eli jumps off Dolly, and catches her, and swings her around. And then his mother is there, walking quickly, and then running too, and she holds him, and holds him, and does not say anything at all. And Eli finds that he cannot say anything either. Dolly stands beside them, and blows softly through her nose, as if she understands.

[pause]

That evening, at supper, Eli gives his mother the envelope from Mr Hardin. She opens it, and counts the money, and sits very still for a long time, with her hand over her mouth. Five thousand dollars. Enough to pay off all the debts of the ranch. Enough to buy more cattle, and a new roof for the barn, and a good horse for Rose. Enough to make the future safe. She looks at her son, and says that his father would have been very proud of him.

[pause]

{img:family-supper-ranch-kitchen-lamp-presents-shawl-book-locket-joy}
Then Eli gives them their presents. His mother puts the blue shawl around her shoulders, and says that it is the most beautiful thing she has ever owned. Rose opens her book of animals, and the silver locket, and cries with happiness. And Eli takes out his harmonica, and plays the songs he learned on the trail, by the fire, while his mother and sister listen, and the crickets sing outside, under the big old oak trees.

[long-pause]

Eli went up the trail six more times, in the following years. He became a point rider, and then a trail boss himself. And then, with the money he earned, he bought more land, and more cattle, and built up the little ranch into a big one. He married, and had children. And on winter evenings, by the fire, he told them about his first drive, at fifteen. About Old Ned, and the river, and the stampede, and the buffalo, and the sky on fire. And he played them the songs on his harmonica, which he kept all his life.

[long-pause]

{img:barbed-wire-fence-prairie-1880s-posts-stretching-horizon-cattle-later}
The great cattle drives lasted for only about twenty years. They began in 1866, and they were mostly over by about 1886. In that time, about five million cattle walked north from Texas, on the trails to Kansas and beyond. And then, quite suddenly, it all came to an end. Several things happened, one after another.

[long-pause]

The first was the railways. Every year, the railways spread further and further, across the plains. In the 1870s and 1880s, they reached Texas itself. Now, the cattle could be put on trains in Texas, and did not need to walk all the way to Kansas. The long trails were no longer needed. The cattle towns of Kansas, like Abilene, became quiet. Abilene's cattle trade ended in 1872, only five years after it began. Other towns, further west, took its place, for a while. Then they too became quiet.

[pause]

The second was barbed wire. It was invented in 1874. It was cheap, strong and easy to put up. And it changed the West forever. The farmers and the ranchers began to fence their land. The open range, where the cattle could roam freely, for hundreds of kilometres, disappeared. The trails were blocked by fences. The cowboys could no longer drive their herds across the open land.

[pause]

{img:deep-snow-winter-plains-1886-cattle-sheltering-cold-grey-sky-later}
The third was the weather. In the winter of 1886 to 1887, there was a terrible winter on the northern plains. Blizzards, deep snow and freezing cold, for months. Many cattle on the open range could not survive it. It was called the Great Die-Up. After that, the ranchers changed their ways. They kept fewer cattle, on fenced land, with hay for the winter. The age of the open range was over.

[pause]

The longhorns changed too. The ranchers began to breed other kinds of cattle, like Herefords and Angus, which gave more meat, and were calmer and easier to keep. Slowly, the longhorns disappeared from the ranches. By the early twentieth century, there were very few left. They almost died out completely. But in 1927, a small herd was saved, in a wildlife refuge in Oklahoma, by people who wanted to keep them alive. And today, there are again many longhorns in Texas, kept for their beauty and their history.

[long-pause]

{img:wild-west-show-arena-cowboys-riding-crowds-grandstand-1890s-later}
The cowboys changed too. Some went on working on the ranches, as cowboys, but on fenced land, with less riding, and more mending fences. Some became farmers. Some went to the cities. Some went further west, to Montana, Wyoming and the Dakotas, where the open range lasted a little longer. And some joined the Wild West shows, which travelled around America and Europe, showing the cowboy life to crowds of people, who had never seen it.

[pause]

And the cowboys became legends. In books, magazines, songs, and later in films and on television, the cowboy became the great hero of America. Brave, free, honest, and handy with a horse and a rope. Riding off into the sunset. The stories were often exaggerated. Full of gunfights and bandits and heroes in white hats. The real cowboys were mostly hard-working young men, who spent their days in the dust, with cattle. But the legend was bigger than the reality.

[pause]

{img:old-cowboy-rocking-chair-porch-grandchildren-listening-harmonica-later}
In the legend, the cowboy was almost always shown as white. But as we have seen, many of the real cowboys were Black, Mexican, or Native American. About a quarter of all the cowboys, or more. For a long time, they were forgotten. But today, historians are telling their stories again. Men like Bose Ikard, a Black cowboy who rode with Charles Goodnight, and who Goodnight said was the most trusted man he ever knew. And the vaqueros, without whom there would have been no cowboys at all.

[long-pause]

As for the men of our story, they were not real people. But they were like thousands of real cowboys who rode up the Chisholm Trail. Some of them lived to be very old, in the twentieth century. They saw motor cars and aeroplanes, and heard the radio. And some of them told their stories, to writers and historians, who wrote them down. So that today, we can still hear their voices, telling about the dust, the rivers, the stampedes, the singing to the cattle, and the stars over the endless prairie.

[long-pause]

{img:fort-worth-stockyards-today-longhorn-cattle-drive-street-tourists-watching}
Today, the great cattle drives are long gone. But in Texas and Kansas, they are still remembered, in many places. And some of the old traditions still live on.

[long-pause]

In Fort Worth, in Texas, there is a part of the city called the Stockyards. It was once one of the biggest cattle markets in the world. Today, it is a historic district, with old brick buildings, wooden pens and cobbled streets. And twice every day, there is a little cattle drive, right down the main street. A small herd of real Texas longhorns, with their huge horns, is driven slowly along the street, by cowboys on horseback, in their hats and chaps. Visitors line the street, and watch them pass.

[pause]

The Chisholm Trail is also remembered. Along its old route, through Texas, Oklahoma and Kansas, there are markers and signs, showing where the trail went. In some places, you can still see the old trail itself. Long, shallow depressions in the prairie, worn by millions of hooves, more than a hundred and fifty years ago. They are called swales. Grass grows in them now. But they are still there, if you know where to look.

[pause]

{img:chisholm-trail-marker-stone-prairie-old-swales-grass-blue-sky-today}
In Oklahoma, there is a Chisholm Trail Heritage Center, in the town of Duncan, where visitors can learn about the trail. There is a great bronze sculpture outside, of cowboys driving longhorns across a river. And inside, you can see saddles, ropes, spurs and chuckwagons, and hear the stories of the cowboys. You can even sit on a saddle, and imagine riding drag, in the dust, for two months.

[pause]

In Abilene, Kansas, there is a reconstruction of the old town, with wooden buildings, a boardwalk, and a museum about the cattle trade. Abilene is a quiet little town now. But in the summer, there is a festival, with a cattle drive, a rodeo, and people in old-fashioned clothes. And people remember the days, long ago, when this little town was the end of the trail, and thousands of cowboys rode into town, dusty and tired, with money in their pockets.

[long-pause]

{img:longhorns-grazing-wildlife-refuge-oklahoma-wichita-mountains-today}
The Texas longhorns live on too. There are many herds of them, on ranches in Texas and other states. They are kept for their meat, which is lean and healthy, and for their beauty, and their history. At the Wichita Mountains Wildlife Refuge, in Oklahoma, where the longhorns were saved in 1927, there is still a herd, living wild, with buffalo, elk and prairie dogs. Visitors can drive through the refuge, and see them, grazing among the hills, just as they did on the trail.

[pause]

And there are still cowboys. On ranches in Texas, Oklahoma, Montana, Wyoming and many other places, men and women still work cattle on horseback, as they did a hundred and fifty years ago. They still wear hats, boots and chaps. They still rope calves, and ride fences, and round up the herds in the spring. Their life is not easy. But many of them would not want any other.

[pause]

{img:modern-cowboy-on-horse-ranch-sunset-cattle-open-land-today}
There are also rodeos, all over the West. Contests where cowboys and cowgirls show their skills. Roping, riding, racing around barrels. They began as friendly contests between cowboys, after the roundup, to see who was the best. Today, they are big events, with crowds of thousands. And at every rodeo, people remember the old days of the trail, and the young men who rode north, with the longhorns, to Kansas.

[long-pause]

And sometimes, on a quiet evening, on a ranch in Texas, an old cowboy sits on his porch, and takes out a harmonica, and plays an old trail song, softly, as the sun goes down. The same song that the cowboys sang to the cattle, at night, under the stars, so long ago. And somewhere in the pasture, the cattle lift their heads, and listen. And then lie down again, in the grass, and settle down for the night.

[long-pause]

{img:cattle-drive-camp-evening-1870-prairie-sunset-herd-bedded-chuckwagon-fire}
So we leave the Chisholm Trail tonight, in the year 1870, as the evening falls. The sun is going down over the endless prairie. The sky is red and gold and purple. The grass is glowing. And the great herd is bedding down for the night, three thousand longhorns, lying down one by one, around an old steer with a brass bell.

[long-pause]

At the camp, by a little creek, the chuckwagon stands with its white canvas cover glowing in the last light. Cookie is scraping the last of the beans from the pot. The coffee is on the coals. The cowboys are sitting around the fire, with their plates on their knees, tired and dusty, talking softly about the day. The horses of the remuda are grazing nearby. And the tongue of the wagon is pointing north, toward the star that will show the way to Kansas.

[pause]

{img:boy-cowboy-harmonica-campfire-friends-listening-stars-appearing-prairie}
A boy of fifteen takes out a harmonica, and begins to play an old song, very softly. One of his mother's songs. The fiddler joins in. Then the vaquero, with his little guitar. The other cowboys listen, or hum along. The fire crackles. The sparks fly up into the darkening sky. And for a while, nobody thinks about the dust, or the rivers, or the long way still to go.

[long-pause]

Out on the prairie, two night herders are riding slowly around the sleeping herd, in opposite directions, singing softly. Their voices drift across the grass, low and gentle. The cattle lie still, chewing their cud, breathing slowly. A coyote calls, far away. Another answers. The wind moves through the grass, with a sound like the sea. And the first stars are coming out, one by one, over the edge of the world.

[pause]

{img:night-herder-silhouette-horse-moonlit-prairie-sleeping-herd-milky-way}
The moon rises, huge and golden, over the eastern horizon, and climbs slowly into the sky, turning silver. It shines on the sleeping herd, on the white canvas of the chuckwagon, on the horses, and on the cowboys, rolled up in their blankets around the dying fire, with their saddles for pillows and their hats over their faces. The Big Dipper turns slowly around the North Star, marking the hours of the night.

[long-pause]

The boy lies on his back, in his bedroll, looking up at the stars. He can hear the singing of the night herders, far away. The soft breathing of the cattle. The jingle of a spur. The crackle of the last embers. He thinks of his mother and his sister, far away in Texas, under the same stars. And he thinks of Kansas, somewhere ahead, in the dark. And slowly, with the harmonica still in his hand, he falls asleep, under the great sky of the prairie.
