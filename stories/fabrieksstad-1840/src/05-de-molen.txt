The Kelsall mill stands beside the river, at the bottom of the valley. It is a huge building, of dark red brick, five storeys high, and nearly a hundred yards long. It has rows and rows of tall windows, to let in as much light as possible. Beside it stands the tall, square chimney, taller than any church tower in the town, pouring out black smoke. And at one end, there is a smaller building, with tall arched windows. This is the engine house.

The engine house is the heart of the mill. Inside, there is a great steam engine, painted green and red, with polished brass and shining steel. It is as big as a house. It has a huge iron beam, which rocks up and down, slowly, like a giant seesaw. And a great flywheel, taller than three men, which turns round and round, steadily, all day long.

--

{img:mill-steam-beam-engine-house-polished-brass-flywheel-engineer-oil-can}
The engine is looked after by the engine man, Mr Ogden. He is a serious, careful man, who loves his engine like a child. He polishes the brass every day, and oils every moving part, and listens carefully to the sound of it, for any sign of trouble. Next door, in the boiler house, the stokers shovel coal into the furnaces, all day long, to heat the water in the great boilers, and make the steam. They are black with coal dust, and sweat from the heat.

From the flywheel, the power goes up through the whole mill. A great upright iron shaft, turning inside the building, from the bottom to the top. On every floor, it turns long horizontal shafts, which run along the ceiling, from one end of the room to the other. And from those shafts, wide leather belts come down to every machine, turning their wheels, and driving them. Thousands of belts, spinning and flapping, in every room.

On each floor, there is a different stage of the work. On the ground floor, the raw cotton arrives, in big bales, from Liverpool. It is pressed tight, and dirty, full of seeds and leaves and dust. First, it is opened and cleaned, by machines that beat it and blow it, until it is fluffy and loose. This is the dustiest work in the mill. The air is thick with cotton fluff, which the workers call fly.

{img:carding-room-1840-cotton-fluff-air-machines-workers-dusty-light}
On the next floor, the cotton is carded. Big machines, covered with thousands of tiny wire teeth, comb the cotton fibres, until they all lie in the same direction. The cotton comes out of the carding machine as a soft, thick rope, called a sliver. Then the sliver is stretched and twisted, again and again, on other machines, until it becomes thinner, and stronger. Now it is ready for spinning.

--

The spinning rooms are on the upper floors. Long, wide rooms, filled with spinning mules. And at the top of the building, or in a separate shed beside the mill, is the weaving shed, where the thread is woven into cloth, on power looms. In some mills, the spinning and weaving are done in different buildings, or even in different mills. In the Kelsall mill, they are all together, on the same site.

{img:mill-yard-carts-cotton-bales-unloading-horses-workers-mill-wall}
Altogether, about six hundred people work at the Kelsall mill. Men, women, and children. The overlookers, who are in charge of each room. The spinners, the piecers, the carders, the weavers, the mechanics, the engine men, the stokers, the warehousemen. The bookkeepers, in the office. And the manager, Mr Firth, who walks through the mill every day, with his hands behind his back, watching everything.

The mill is warm, and damp, inside. The cotton needs warm, damp air, or the thread breaks. So the windows are kept closed, and the air is kept moist, with steam. In the summer, it is very hot and stuffy. In the winter, it is warmer inside than out. But the air is always full of the fine white fly, which settles on everything. On the machines, on the floor, on the workers' hair and clothes. And on the windows, so that the light comes through soft and white, like snow.
