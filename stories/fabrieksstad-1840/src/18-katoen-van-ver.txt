The cotton that the Hartleys spin and weave comes from very far away. Most of it comes from the southern states of America. From Mississippi, Alabama, Georgia, Louisiana, and the Carolinas. There, on great plantations, the cotton grows in the hot sun, in long rows of green bushes, with white, fluffy bolls. And there, in 1840, it is picked by enslaved people.

This is a sad part of the story, but an important one. In 1840, about two and a half million people are held in slavery in the United States. Many of them work on the cotton plantations. Men, women, and children. They are not paid. They cannot leave. They can be bought and sold, and their families can be separated. They work from dawn until dark, picking cotton, in the heat. And the cotton they pick is sent across the sea, to Lancashire.

--

{img:cotton-plantation-field-1840-rows-white-bolls-distant-workers-southern-sky}
Britain itself had abolished slavery in its own colonies, just a few years before, in 1833. Many people in Britain were proud of this. But the cotton mills of Lancashire still depended on cotton grown by enslaved people, in America. Some people in Britain saw this clearly, and spoke out about it. Others preferred not to think about it. For most mill workers, the cotton just arrived, in bales, at the mill. They did not know where it came from.

The cotton travels down the great rivers of the American South, on steamboats, to the port of New Orleans, or other ports. There, it is loaded onto sailing ships, and carried across the Atlantic Ocean. The voyage takes about four or five weeks. The ships arrive at Liverpool, which is the busiest port in the world for cotton. The docks are crowded with ships, from every country, and the quaysides are piled high with bales.

In Liverpool, the cotton is bought and sold by merchants, who test its quality, by pulling out a few fibres, and looking at them carefully. The longer and finer the fibres, the better the cotton, and the higher the price. Then it is sent by canal, or by the new railway, to Manchester, and then on to the mill towns. A bale of cotton that left a plantation in Mississippi in the spring might reach the Kelsall mill in Rochdale in the summer.

Some years later, in the 1860s, something remarkable happened. In 1861, the American Civil War began, between the northern states and the southern states. The North blockaded the ports of the South, and the cotton stopped coming. In Lancashire, the mills ran out of cotton, and closed. Hundreds of thousands of mill workers lost their work. It was called the Cotton Famine. It was a terrible time of hunger and hardship.

--

{img:free-trade-hall-manchester-1862-working-people-meeting-letter-lincoln}
But in 1862, at a great meeting in Manchester, the cotton workers did something very brave. Even though they were hungry, and out of work, they voted to support President Abraham Lincoln, and the North, and the end of slavery. They sent him a letter. And Lincoln wrote back, thanking them. He called their support an act of sublime Christian heroism. Today, there is a statue of Lincoln in Manchester, to remember it.

Of course, in 1840, all of that is still far in the future. For now, the cotton keeps coming, in great bales, to Liverpool, and to Rochdale. And the Hartleys spin it and weave it, in the mill, without thinking much about where it comes from. But Ellen has read about slavery, in a book from the Sunday school library. And one evening, she asks her father about it.

{img:father-and-daughter-talking-by-fire-serious-gentle-evening-kitchen}
Tom is quiet for a long time. Then he says that it is a wicked thing, to own another human being. He says that he has heard men speak about it, at the chapel, and at the meetings. And that one day, he hopes, it will end everywhere in the world. Ellen nods. She thinks about the people, far away, who picked the cotton that she weaves. And she says a little prayer for them, that night, before she goes to sleep.
