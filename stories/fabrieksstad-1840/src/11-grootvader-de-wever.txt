Grandad Joseph was born in 1775, in a stone cottage, high on the hillside above Rochdale, in a little village on the edge of the moors. His father was a hand-loom weaver, and his grandfather too. In those days, almost every family in the hills around Rochdale made cloth at home. At first it was woollen cloth, called flannel, for which Rochdale was famous. Later, more and more of them wove cotton.

The weavers' cottages had long rows of windows on the top floor, to let in as much light as possible. That was where the looms stood, in the weaving room, upstairs. The whole family worked together. Joseph's mother and sisters spun the thread, and wound it onto bobbins. His father wove it into cloth, on the big wooden loom. And when Joseph was old enough, he learned to weave too, sitting beside his father, at the loom.

{img:father-and-son-at-wooden-handloom-upstairs-weaving-room-light-windows}
Joseph remembers those days as a golden time. In the 1790s, when he was a young man, hand-loom weavers were well paid. There was more thread than ever before, from the new spinning mills, and not enough weavers to weave it all. A good weaver could earn as much as a skilled craftsman. They wore good clothes, and kept a cow, and a few hens, and a little garden. They could work when they liked, and rest when they liked.

On fine days, Joseph and his friends would stop work in the afternoon, and go out onto the moors, or play games, or go to the alehouse. Then they would work late into the night, to make up for it. On Monday, many weavers did not work at all. They called it Saint Monday. And at the end of the week, Joseph would carry his cloth down the hill to the town, on his back, and sell it to the merchant, and come home with his money.

{img:weavers-carrying-cloth-pieces-down-hill-path-to-town-market-morning}
But then the power looms came. At first, there were not many. But each year, there were more and more. The power looms could weave cloth much faster and cheaper than the hand-loom weavers. So the price of hand-woven cloth went down, and down, and down. The weavers had to work longer and longer hours, for less and less money. By the 1820s, a hand-loom weaver could hardly earn enough to feed his family, even working fourteen hours a day.

It was a terrible time for the weavers. Many of them were very poor, and hungry. In 1826, in desperation, some of them broke into the mills, and smashed the power looms. But it did not help. The power looms were there to stay. One by one, the hand-loom weavers gave up. Their sons and daughters went to work in the mills. And the old weavers stayed at home, at their looms, weaving for a few pennies, until they could not weave any more.

Joseph kept weaving until 1835, when his wife passed away. Then he sold his loom, for a few shillings, and came down the hill, to live with his son, in Spring Street. He has never worked in the mill. He says he is too old, and he would not want to. He does not like the noise, and the smoke, and the rules, and the bell. He misses his cottage, and the moors, and the quiet, and the freedom.

But he does not complain much. He is glad to be with his family. He helps Sarah with the house, and the cooking, and the fire. He minds the house, while the others are at work. He mends the clogs, and the chairs, and anything that is broken. And in the evenings, he tells the children stories, of the old days, when he was young. Of the moors, and the weavers, and the fairs, and the songs.

Jack loves his grandad's stories. Especially the ones about the boggarts, the strange little spirits that lived on the moors, and in old houses, and played tricks on people. Joseph tells them in a low voice, by the fire, in the dark. Jack listens, wide-eyed. Then he goes to bed, and pulls the blanket over his head, and listens for boggarts. But he only hears the rain, and the wind, and the distant hum of the town.
