On a fine morning in the Wakes week, the Hartleys set off early, for their walk to the moors. They take a basket, with bread, and cheese, and cold bacon, and a bottle of cold tea, and some oatcakes. Grandad Joseph walks with a stick, but he is determined to come. He says he wants to see the old places, one more time. Patch, the dog from next door, comes too, because he always follows Jack everywhere.

They walk out of the town, along the road beside the Rochdale Canal. The canal was built about forty years ago, to carry goods across the Pennines, from Lancashire to Yorkshire. Long, narrow boats, pulled by horses, move slowly along the still water, loaded with coal, and cotton, and stone. The boatmen wave to them. The horses plod along the towpath, with their heads down.

--

{img:rochdale-canal-narrowboat-horse-towpath-lock-summer-hills-ahead}
Then they leave the canal, and start to climb. Up a steep, stony lane, between stone walls, past little farms, and fields of sheep. The town falls away behind them, and below them. When they stop to rest, and look back, they can see it all, spread out in the valley. The chimneys, the mills, the rows of houses, and the smoke, hanging over everything, like a grey cloud. And above them, the sky is blue, and clear.

The higher they climb, the cleaner the air becomes. It smells of grass, and heather, and sheep. Skylarks sing high above them, too high to see. The wind blows gently, and the children run ahead, laughing. Sarah takes off her shawl, and lets the wind blow through her hair. Tom takes deep breaths, and says it is like drinking cold water. After a week in the mill, the fresh air feels wonderful.

At last, they reach the top, at Blackstone Edge. A long ridge of dark, rough rocks, standing up from the moor, like the back of a sleeping giant. From here, they can see for miles. Back toward Rochdale, and Manchester beyond, in the haze. And the other way, over the hills, into Yorkshire. Green valleys, and brown moors, and little villages, and far away, more mill chimneys, in other towns.

{img:blackstone-edge-gritstone-rocks-ridge-wide-view-pennine-moors-summer}
Near the top, there is an old stone road, crossing the moor. It is very old. Some people say it was built by the Romans, almost two thousand years ago. It is made of big flat stones, with a groove down the middle. Grandad Joseph says that when he was a boy, the packhorses still came over this road, carrying wool and cloth, in long lines, with bells on their harness. Now, the canal and the railway carry everything, and the old road is quiet.

--

They have their picnic among the rocks, out of the wind. The bread and cheese and bacon taste better than anything they have eaten all year. Jack and Patch explore the rocks, and find a hare, which runs away, very fast, with Patch barking behind it. Ellen sits on a rock, and reads a book of poems, by William Wordsworth, which she has borrowed from the Sunday school. Tom lies on his back in the heather, with his cap over his eyes, and falls asleep.

In the afternoon, they walk down the other side of the hill, to the little village where Grandad Joseph was born. The stone cottages are still there, with their long rows of upstairs windows. But the looms are gone, and the cottages are quiet. Joseph stops in front of one of them, and looks at it for a long time. Then he says, that was my house. And he tells them about his mother, and his father, and his brothers and sisters, and the sound of the loom, in the upstairs room.

An old woman comes out of the cottage next door, and looks at him. Then she says his name. She was a girl in the village, when he was a boy. They talk for a long time, about the old days, and the people they knew. Most of them have passed away now. When they say goodbye, both of them have tears in their eyes.

{img:sunset-over-moors-family-walking-home-down-lane-town-lights-valley}
They walk home in the evening, slowly, as the sun goes down behind the hills. The sky turns gold, and then pink, and then purple. The town below them begins to light up, with gas lamps, and candles in the windows. Jack is so tired that Tom carries him on his back, for the last mile. And Grandad Joseph walks in silence, smiling, thinking of the old days, and the old places, and the people he once knew.
