Every year, in August, Rochdale has a holiday, called the Wakes. For a few days, the mills close, and everyone has a rest from work. It is the only real holiday of the year, apart from Christmas Day and Good Friday. And it begins with an old and colourful custom, called the Rushbearing.

The Rushbearing is very old. It goes back to the days when the floors of the churches were made of earth, and covered with rushes, to keep them warm and dry. Every year, the people cut fresh rushes from the moors, and carried them to the church, to spread on the floor. Over time, it became a great festival. The church floors now have stone and wood. But the Rushbearing goes on, every August, just for the joy of it.

--

{img:morris-dancers-clogs-ribbons-garlands-dancing-street-crowd-mill-town}
In the days before, the young men from each village around the town go up onto the moors, to cut the rushes. They pile them high on a big wooden cart, in the shape of a tall haystack, and tie them down neatly. Then they decorate the cart, with ribbons, and flowers, and garlands, and shining things. Silver cups, and plates, and teapots, borrowed from the houses, hang from the sides, and flash in the sun. On top of the cart, a man sits on the rushes, waving a flag.

On the day of the Rushbearing, the carts are pulled through the streets of Rochdale, by teams of young men, in white shirts, with ribbons on their hats. In front of each cart, there is a group of morris dancers, dancing in their clogs, with bells on their legs, and handkerchiefs in their hands. And there is music, from fiddles, and drums, and pipes. The whole town turns out to watch, and cheer.

{img:wakes-fair-1840-stalls-swing-boats-gingerbread-crowds-sunny-field}
Behind the carts comes the fair. Stalls selling gingerbread, and brandy snaps, and toffee, and oranges, and nuts. Swing boats, and roundabouts, pushed by hand, and coconut shies. Jugglers, and acrobats, and a man with a dancing bear. Fortune tellers, and peep shows, and a travelling theatre, in a tent. And the noise, and the music, and the laughter, of thousands of people, enjoying themselves.

Jack has saved his pennies for weeks, for the fair. He spends them all in the first hour. Gingerbread, and a ride on the swing boats, and a go at the coconut shy, where he wins nothing. Then he wanders around, with his friends, watching everything, with his mouth open. The acrobats, the jugglers, and the strong man, who lifts an anvil above his head. Ellen gives him one of her pennies, so that he can buy a toffee apple.

--

The grown-ups enjoy the fair too. Tom and Sarah walk around arm in arm, in their best clothes, greeting their friends. They watch the morris dancers, and listen to the music, and buy a little bag of brandy snaps, to share. Sarah has a go on the swing boats, and screams, and laughs, and holds her bonnet on. Tom says she is like a girl again. And Grandad Joseph sits on a bench in the sun, with his old friends, and watches it all, and remembers the Rushbearings of his youth.

{img:old-men-sitting-bench-sunshine-watching-fair-pipes-smiling-memories}
In the evening, there is dancing, in the inns, and in the streets. And there is drinking too, rather too much of it, sometimes. The town is noisy until late at night. The Hartleys go home before it gets too wild. They sit by the open door, in the warm summer evening, and listen to the distant music, and the laughter. Jack falls asleep on the settle, with his face sticky from toffee.

For the rest of the Wakes week, the mill stays closed. Some families go to visit relatives in the country. A few lucky ones take the train to Liverpool, to see the great ships and the sea. But most families just rest, and go for walks, and enjoy the summer. The Hartleys plan a long walk, up onto the moors, to Blackstone Edge, near the village where Grandad Joseph was born.
