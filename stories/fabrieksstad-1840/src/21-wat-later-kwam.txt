Four years after our story, in 1844, something happened in Rochdale that would become famous all over the world. A group of twenty-eight working men, most of them weavers and mill workers, opened a small shop in Toad Lane, a narrow street in the centre of the town. They called themselves the Rochdale Society of Equitable Pioneers. And their little shop was the beginning of the modern cooperative movement.

The idea was simple. The members each paid a small amount of money, to buy the first stock. The shop sold good, honest food, with fair weights and fair prices. No cheating, no watered milk, no sand in the sugar. And at the end of each year, the profits were shared among the members, according to how much they had bought. Anyone could join. Men and women alike. And every member had one vote, in how the shop was run.

--

At first, the shop was tiny. It sold only butter, sugar, flour, oatmeal, and candles. It was open only two evenings a week. People laughed at the pioneers, and said it would never last. But it did. It grew, and grew. Soon, it had hundreds of members, and then thousands. It opened more shops, and a library, and a newsroom, and classes for the members. The idea spread all over Britain, and then all over the world.

{img:later-cooperative-store-victorian-large-shop-window-co-op-sign-crowd}
Today, there are cooperatives in almost every country in the world, with hundreds of millions of members. Shops, banks, farms, and housing. And they all follow the principles written down by those twenty-eight pioneers in Rochdale, in 1844. The little shop in Toad Lane is still there today, as a museum. In our story, perhaps the Hartleys would have joined. Tom would have liked the idea very much.

The mill towns changed slowly, over the years that followed. In 1847, Parliament passed the Ten Hours Act, which limited the working day for women and young people in the mills to ten hours. It was a great victory for the reformers, after many years of campaigning. Later, the half-day on Saturday was introduced, and the Wakes holidays grew longer. And in the 1850s and 1860s, the new railways began to take the mill workers to the seaside, to Blackpool, for their holidays.

{img:victorian-blackpool-beach-mill-workers-holiday-crowds-sea-promenade-1880s}
The towns themselves slowly became healthier. New laws were passed, about drains, and water, and housing. Clean water was piped into the houses. Proper sewers were built. Parks and public baths and libraries were opened. The worst slums were pulled down. People began to live longer, and fewer children died young. It took a long time. But by the end of the century, the mill towns were much better places to live.

--

The cotton industry in Lancashire grew bigger and bigger, until about 1913, when it was at its greatest. Then, after the First World War, it slowly began to decline. Other countries, like India and Japan, built their own mills, and made their own cloth, more cheaply. One by one, the Lancashire mills closed. By the 1960s and 1970s, most of them had gone. The tall chimneys were pulled down, and the great mills were demolished, or turned into warehouses, or flats.

{img:later-empty-old-cotton-mill-weeds-broken-windows-chimney-quiet-afternoon}
And what about the Hartleys? Ellen did become a teacher, as she had dreamed. She went to a training college, in her twenties, with the help of the minister at the chapel. And she taught in a school in Rochdale for forty years, and taught hundreds of mill children to read. Jack became a spinner, like his father, and later an overlooker. He married a weaver called Martha, and they had six children.

Grandad Joseph passed away peacefully in 1846, at the age of seventy-one, in his chair by the fire. Tom and Sarah worked in the mill until they were old, and then lived with Ellen, in a little house on the edge of the town, with a garden, and a view of the moors. On fine evenings, they sat outside, and looked at the hills. And Tom said that he had seen more change in his life than his grandfather could ever have imagined.
