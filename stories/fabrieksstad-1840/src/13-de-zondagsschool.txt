On Sunday, the mill is silent. The great engine is still. The chimneys are cold, and for once, the smoke clears a little, over the town. The bell does not ring, and the knocker-up does not come. The families sleep a little longer. And the streets are quiet, except for the sound of church and chapel bells, calling people to worship.

The Hartleys go to the Methodist chapel, on Union Street. Many working families in the mill towns are Methodists, or Baptists, or belong to other chapels. They are called Nonconformists, because they do not belong to the Church of England. The chapel is a plain, square building of stone, with tall windows, and rows of wooden pews, and a gallery. There are no statues, no pictures, no candles. Just the pulpit, and the organ, and the people.

The service is lively. The minister preaches with great passion, about God's love, and sin, and forgiveness, and how to live a good life. And the people sing. Methodists love to sing. They sing the hymns of Charles Wesley, loudly and joyfully, with all their hearts. Tom has a deep bass voice. Sarah sings the melody, clear and true. And Ellen sings in the chapel choir. The sound fills the chapel, and spills out into the street.

But the most important thing on Sunday, for the children, is the Sunday school. After the morning service, and again in the afternoon, hundreds of children gather in the big schoolroom, beneath the chapel. They are divided into classes, by age, with a teacher for each class. The teachers are volunteers, from the chapel. Many of them are mill workers themselves.

The Sunday schools are very important in the mill towns. For many children, they are the only place where they can learn to read and write. The children learn their letters, and read from the Bible, and learn hymns and prayers by heart. Some Sunday schools also teach writing and arithmetic. Thousands of working people in Lancashire learned to read in a Sunday school. And reading changed their lives.

Ellen is now a teacher herself, in the Sunday school. She teaches a class of little girls, aged six and seven. She is very proud of this. She sits with them, on a bench, in the corner of the schoolroom, and helps them sound out their letters. B, A, T, bat. C, A, T, cat. The little girls look up at her, with shining eyes. And when one of them reads a whole sentence for the first time, Ellen is happier than at any other moment in her week.

The Sunday school also has a library, of a few hundred books. The children and the young people can borrow them, for a week at a time. Ellen has read almost all of them. History books, travel books, poetry, and stories. She has read about the pyramids of Egypt, and the voyages of Captain Cook, and the life of John Wesley. She reads late at night, by the light of a candle, until her mother tells her to blow it out.

Once a year, in the summer, the Sunday school has a great procession, called the Whit Walk. All the children, in their best clothes, walk through the streets of the town, behind a big banner, and a brass band. The girls wear white dresses, and carry flowers. The boys wear clean collars, and their hair is brushed flat. Their families line the streets, and cheer. Then they all go to a field outside the town, for games, and races, and buns and tea.

{img:whit-walk-procession-children-white-dresses-banner-brass-band-mill-town}
On Sunday afternoon, if the weather is fine, the families go for a walk. Out of the town, along the river, or up onto the hills, where the air is clean. They walk slowly, in their Sunday clothes, and greet their neighbours, and enjoy the fresh air, and the green fields. It is the one afternoon in the week when they can see the sky properly, without the smoke.

In the evening, there is another service, at the chapel. Then home, for a light supper, and early to bed. Because tomorrow, the bell will ring again, and the engine will start again, and the week of work will begin again. But for now, the town is quiet, and peaceful, under the stars.
