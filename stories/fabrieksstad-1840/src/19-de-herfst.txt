After the Wakes, the mill opens again, and the long weeks of work begin once more. The summer slowly turns to autumn. The days grow shorter, and the mornings darker. When the family walks to the mill, before six o'clock, it is still night. And when they walk home, at the end of the day, it is already getting dark. The gas lamps are lit in the streets, glowing yellow in the mist.

In the autumn, the weather in Lancashire is often damp and grey. A fine, steady rain falls, day after day. People say that the damp air is the reason the cotton industry came to Lancashire. The cotton thread breaks less easily in damp air. So the rain, which everyone grumbles about, is good for the mills. Tom says that Lancashire was made for cotton, and cotton was made for Lancashire.

--

On still autumn days, the smoke from the chimneys does not rise. It hangs low over the town, mixing with the mist, into a thick, yellow fog. You can hardly see across the street. The gas lamps are like little moons, in the gloom. People walk carefully, with their shawls over their mouths. The fog smells of coal, and it makes everyone cough. Grandad Joseph stays inside, by the fire, on those days.

In the evenings, after work and after tea, the family gathers around the fire. Sarah sews, and mends the clothes, and knits socks. Ellen reads, or writes letters to Aunt Anne, or prepares her lessons for the Sunday school. Jack does his homework from the mill school, with Ellen's help, or plays with a wooden top that his grandad carved for him. Tom reads the newspaper, slowly, and sometimes reads bits aloud.

{img:family-evening-by-range-fire-mother-sewing-boy-top-grandfather-pipe}
Some evenings, Tom goes to the Mechanics' Institute, in the town. It is a place where working men can go, to learn. There are lectures, about science, and history, and geography. There is a library, with hundreds of books. And there are classes, in reading, writing, arithmetic, and drawing. Tom goes to a lecture about the stars, and comes home full of wonder. He tells the children that the stars are suns, like ours, very far away.

The autumn brings its own small pleasures. Chestnuts, roasted on the fire, and shared between everyone. Apples from the market, cheap and crisp. And on the fifth of November, Bonfire Night. The children of Spring Street collect old wood, and boxes, and rubbish, for weeks, and build a big bonfire on a piece of waste ground, at the end of the street. They make a guy, a figure of old clothes stuffed with straw, and put it on top.

--

{img:bonfire-night-1840-children-waste-ground-fire-sparks-terraced-houses}
On Bonfire Night, the whole street comes out to watch the bonfire burn. The flames leap up, high into the dark sky, and the sparks fly. The children dance around it, and shout, and sing. The grown-ups stand back, with their hands held out to the warmth. Someone brings potatoes, to bake in the ashes. And Mr Ashworth, next door, lets off a few small fireworks, which fizz and bang, and make the children squeal.

Then, slowly, the bonfire burns down, to a glowing heap of embers. The families go home, smelling of smoke, with warm faces, and cold backs. Jack has burnt his fingers on a hot potato, but he does not care. He says it was the best bonfire ever. He says that every year. And Sarah puts him to bed, and tucks him in, and he falls asleep in a moment.

{img:quiet-terraced-street-late-night-embers-glow-one-lamp-mist-rising}
Outside, the fog is coming down again, soft and grey, over the town. The embers of the bonfire glow faintly, at the end of the street. The mill chimneys are dark shapes, against the sky. And the town is quiet, at last, except for the distant sound of the night watchman, calling out the hour, as he walks through the streets.
