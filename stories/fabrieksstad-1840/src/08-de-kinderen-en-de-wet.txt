In 1840, there are many children working in the mills of Lancashire. Thousands of them. Boys and girls, some of them only nine years old. They work as piecers, and scavengers, and doffers, and helpers. Their small fingers are quick, and they can crawl under the machines. And they are cheap to employ. For the families, the children's wages are an important part of the weekly income.

Some years before, the conditions for children in the mills were very bad. Children as young as five or six worked in the mills, for twelve or fourteen hours a day, or even more. Some of them were orphans, sent from the workhouses, far away. They were often tired, ill, and unhappy. Some were treated cruelly by the overlookers. And people began to speak out, and to say that it was wrong.

--

Reformers like Richard Oastler, and Lord Ashley, and many working people themselves, campaigned for a law to protect the children. They held meetings, and marches, and wrote letters and pamphlets. They wanted a limit of ten hours a day for all mill workers. This was called the Ten Hours Movement. And in 1833, Parliament passed a new law, called the Factory Act. It did not give them everything they wanted. But it was a beginning.

The Factory Act said that no child under nine could work in a textile mill. Children between nine and thirteen could work no more than nine hours a day, and must go to school for two hours a day. Young people between thirteen and eighteen could work no more than twelve hours a day. And nobody under eighteen could work at night. For the first time, the government sent inspectors to the mills, to make sure that the law was obeyed.

{img:factory-inspector-top-hat-checking-register-mill-office-children-lined-up}
The inspectors were few, and they had many mills to visit. So the law was not always obeyed. Some mill owners cheated. They let children work longer hours, or lied about their ages. In 1840, there are no birth certificates yet, for most children, so it is hard to know how old a child really is. Instead, a doctor looks at the child, and checks their teeth and their height, and gives them a certificate, to say they look old enough.

Jack has such a certificate. It says that he has the ordinary strength and appearance of a child of at least nine years. He is ten, really. So he works in the mill for half the day, and goes to the mill school for the other half. He is one of the half-timers. He does not mind the mill too much. He likes being with his father, and he likes the money he earns. He gives it to his mother, every Saturday, and she gives him a penny back, for himself.

--

{img:mill-school-room-1840-children-slates-teacher-small-windows-benches}
The mill school is in a small room, beside the mill. It has rows of wooden benches, and a blackboard, and a few books. The teacher is an old man, who was once a soldier, and he is not a very good teacher. Sometimes he falls asleep in the afternoons. The children learn to read a little, and write a little, and count. They write on slates, with slate pencils, which squeak. It is not much of an education. But it is better than nothing.

Jack likes the school less than the mill. He finds it hard to sit still, after the busy morning. He would rather be outside, playing in the street, or by the river. But Ellen helps him with his reading, in the evenings, at home. She is very patient with him. And slowly, he is learning. Ellen says that reading is the most important thing in the world, because it opens doors. Jack is not sure about that. But he likes the stories she reads him.

{img:sister-teaching-little-brother-to-read-by-candle-kitchen-table-evening}
Over the years that followed, the laws became better and better. More inspectors were appointed. The hours for women and young people were limited further. And in the end, school became compulsory for all children, and child labour in the mills came to an end. But in 1840, that is still far in the future. And for now, Jack goes to the mill in the morning, and to school in the afternoon, and plays in the street in the evening, until it gets dark.
