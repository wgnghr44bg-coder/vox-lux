Sarah and Ellen work in the weaving shed. It is a long, low building, beside the main mill, with a roof shaped like the teeth of a saw. Each part of the roof has a window, facing north, so that the light is soft and even, all day long, without any harsh sunlight. Beneath the roof, in long rows, stand the power looms. Hundreds of them, close together, with narrow passages between them.

A power loom is a machine that weaves cloth automatically, driven by the steam engine. In the old days, a hand-loom weaver threw the shuttle across the loom by hand, back and forth, thousands of times a day. Now, the machine does it. The shuttle flies from side to side, faster than the eye can follow, carrying the thread across. And the cloth grows slowly, inch by inch, and winds onto a roller at the front.

--

{img:weaver-woman-tending-two-power-looms-shuttle-flying-cloth-growing}
Sarah looks after two looms. She walks between them, watching the cloth, and the threads, and the shuttle. When a thread breaks, she stops the loom, and mends it, quickly. When the shuttle runs out of thread, she puts in a new bobbin, and starts the loom again. She must keep the looms running as much as possible, because she is paid by the piece, for each length of cloth she weaves. The more cloth, the more money.

The weaving shed is the noisiest place in the whole mill. The noise of hundreds of looms, all clattering together, is enormous. A great, crashing, rattling roar, that never stops. It is so loud that the weavers cannot hear each other speak, even if they shout. So they have learned to talk without sound. They move their lips very clearly, and slowly, and read each other's lips. This is called mee-mawing. The mill girls are very good at it.

Across the shed, Sarah can have a whole conversation with her friend Mary, by mee-mawing. They talk about their children, and their husbands, and the price of bread, and the gossip of the street. They laugh, silently, at the overlooker's new hat. And sometimes they tell jokes that would make the men blush, without anyone else knowing. Even years later, when they leave the mill, many weavers still move their lips clearly when they talk, out of habit.

{img:two-mill-women-lip-reading-across-looms-laughing-silently-noisy-shed}
Ellen is still learning. She works beside her mother, on her own loom, while Sarah watches her, and helps her. Ellen is quick, and careful, and she learns fast. Soon, she will have two looms of her own. But she does not want to be a weaver all her life. She wants to be a teacher. She thinks about it often, as she watches the shuttle fly back and forth. She dreams of a schoolroom, quiet and peaceful, with children reading.

--

The overlooker in the weaving shed is a man called Mr Butterworth. He is in charge of the looms, and the weavers. He walks up and down the rows, watching the work, and mending the looms when they go wrong. He is strict, but fair. He fines the weavers if their cloth has faults in it, or if they are late, or if they talk too much. The weavers do not like the fines. But they like Mr Butterworth well enough.

At half past twelve, the engine stops again, for an hour. It is dinner time. Some of the workers go home, if they live close by. Others eat in the mill, or in the yard, in the fresh air, if it is fine. Sarah and Ellen go home, quickly, through the streets, to eat with Grandad Joseph, who has made a pot of potato stew, with a little bacon. Then they hurry back, before the bell rings again.

The afternoon is long. The looms clatter on, hour after hour, and the light from the north windows slowly changes. The air is warm and damp, and full of fly. The weavers grow tired. Their feet ache, from standing all day, and their ears ring with the noise. But they keep going, watching the threads, mending the breaks, changing the shuttles, until the evening comes.

{img:finished-cotton-cloth-rolls-stacked-warehouse-calico-white-light}
The cloth they weave is a plain cotton cloth, called calico. It is white, or light grey, and soft. It will be taken to Manchester, and sold, and then printed with colourful patterns, or dyed, or bleached. It will be made into shirts, dresses, sheets, curtains, and handkerchiefs. And it will be sent all over the world. Somewhere, far away, a woman in India, or Africa, or South America, will wear a dress made from cloth woven by Sarah Hartley, in Rochdale.
