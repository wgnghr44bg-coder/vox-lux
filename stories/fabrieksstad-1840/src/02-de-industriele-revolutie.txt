To understand the mill towns, we must go back a little. About eighty years before 1840, in the middle of the eighteenth century, almost all cloth in Britain was made by hand. In the villages of Lancashire and Yorkshire, families spun and wove at home, in their cottages. The women and children spun the thread, on spinning wheels. The men wove the thread into cloth, on wooden looms, in the upstairs rooms of their houses.

It was slow work. A spinner could make only one thread at a time. A weaver could weave only a few yards of cloth in a day. But it was a life that many people liked. They worked at home, with their families. They could choose their own hours. They could stop, and go out into the fields, or rest on a sunny afternoon. And they were proud of their skill.

--

Then, from the 1760s onward, a series of inventions changed everything. Clever men, many of them from Lancashire, invented machines that could spin thread much faster than any person. First the spinning jenny, invented by James Hargreaves, which could spin eight threads at once, and later many more. Then the water frame, by Richard Arkwright, driven by a water wheel. And then the spinning mule, by Samuel Crompton, from Bolton, which combined the best of both.

{img:early-water-powered-mill-river-valley-1790s-stone-building-wheel}
These machines were too big, and too expensive, to go in a cottage. They needed a special building, with power to drive them. At first, the power came from water. The first mills were built beside fast-flowing rivers, in the valleys of the Pennines, where water wheels could turn the machines. Then, at the end of the eighteenth century, the steam engine arrived, improved by James Watt. Now mills could be built anywhere, near coal, and near towns, and near the workers.

The cotton itself came from far away. From the southern states of America, mostly, where it was grown on great plantations, and picked by enslaved people. The raw cotton was carried across the Atlantic, in sailing ships, to the port of Liverpool. From there, it came by canal, and later by railway, to Manchester, and then to the mill towns. And the finished cloth went back out, through Liverpool, to the whole world.

--

{img:manchester-1840-cottonopolis-skyline-chimneys-canal-warehouses}
Manchester became the centre of it all. People called it Cottonopolis, the city of cotton. In 1830, the first passenger railway in the world opened, between Liverpool and Manchester. Now people and goods could travel quickly between the port and the city. The railway was a sensation. Within a few years, railways were being built all over the country. In 1839, a railway line reached Rochdale too.

With the mills came the people. From the countryside, from the hills, from Ireland, families came to the towns, to find work in the mills. The towns grew faster than anyone could manage. Houses were built quickly and cheaply, in long rows, close together, with no proper drains or clean water. The towns became crowded, dirty and smoky. But there was work, and wages, every week, which many people had never had before.

{img:mill-owner-victorian-top-hat-standing-before-his-mill-proud-1840}
Some people became very rich from the cotton mills. The mill owners built big houses, outside the towns, away from the smoke. Some of them were hard and greedy. Others were kind, and built schools and chapels and better houses for their workers. In the mill towns, the gap between rich and poor was very great. And many people began to ask questions, about fairness, and about the rights of working people.

This was the Industrial Revolution. It began in Britain, and especially in Lancashire, and it spread around the world. It changed how people lived and worked, more than anything since the beginning of farming, thousands of years ago. And in 1840, it was in full swing.
