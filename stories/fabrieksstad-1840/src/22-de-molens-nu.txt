Today, Rochdale is a busy town, of more than a hundred thousand people. Most of the old mills have gone. The great chimneys that once filled the sky have almost all been pulled down. But if you look carefully, you can still see the traces of the cotton age, everywhere. In the rows of brick terraced houses, in the canal, and in the grand old buildings in the centre of the town.

The most beautiful of these is the Town Hall, built in the 1860s and 1870s, when the town was rich from cotton. It is a magnificent building, in the Gothic style, with a tall clock tower, and beautiful stained glass windows, and a great hall with a painted ceiling. It shows how proud the people of the mill towns were of their towns, and their work, and their success.

{img:today-toad-lane-rochdale-pioneers-museum-small-shop-front-visitors}
In Toad Lane, the little shop of the Rochdale Pioneers is still there, as a museum. Visitors from all over the world come to see it. Many of them belong to cooperatives in their own countries. They walk through the little shop, with its old wooden counter, and its scales, and its barrels of flour and oatmeal. And they read about the twenty-eight pioneers, and how their small idea changed the world.

Across Lancashire, some of the old mills have been saved, and turned into museums. At Queen Street Mill, near Burnley, there is a weaving shed, with hundreds of old looms, which still work. When the steam engine is running, and the looms start up, the noise is overwhelming, just as it was in the old days. Visitors can see the weavers at work, and try to mee-maw, like the mill girls once did.

At Quarry Bank Mill, at Styal, near Manchester, there is a great mill, from the early days of the Industrial Revolution, beside a river, with a huge water wheel. There are the houses of the workers, and the house where the child apprentices lived, and the mill owner's garden. And in Manchester itself, the Science and Industry Museum has spinning mules, and steam engines, and the old railway station from 1830, the oldest in the world.

The Rochdale Canal is still there too. It was closed for many years, and became choked with weeds and rubbish. But it has been restored, and reopened, and boats can travel along it again, across the Pennines, from Manchester to Yorkshire. People walk and cycle along the towpath, where the horses once pulled the boats. And the water is clean again, with ducks, and swans, and fish.

Up on the moors, at Blackstone Edge, the rocks are still there, as they always have been. And the old stone road still crosses the moor. Walkers come up from the town, as the Hartleys did, on summer days. They look out over the valley, at the town below. There is much less smoke now, and the air is clean. But it is the same view, of hills, and valleys, and towns, and the wide sky.

The Rushbearing faded away, in the late nineteenth century. But in recent years, the people of the area have brought it back, in some places, with carts, and rushes, and morris dancers, and music. Just as in the old days. And in many Lancashire towns, the old brass bands still play, and the Whit Walks still march through the streets, behind their banners.

{img:today-brass-band-playing-park-bandstand-lancashire-town-families-listening}
The cotton mills of Lancashire changed the world. They were the beginning of the modern industrial age, of factories, and machines, and cities, and mass production. They brought great wealth, and great hardship. But they also brought new ideas, about fairness, and the rights of working people, and how people can work together. And those ideas came from ordinary people, like the Hartleys, in their little houses, in the shadow of the mill.
