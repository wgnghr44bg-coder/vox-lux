Let us meet the Hartleys properly. Tom Hartley is thirty-eight years old. He is a mule spinner, at a big cotton mill near the river, owned by a family called the Kelsalls. Spinning is the best paid job in the mill, for a working man. It needs skill and strength, and many years of experience. Tom is proud of his trade. He is a tall, quiet man, with big hands, and a dry sense of humour.

Sarah Hartley is thirty-six. She is a weaver, at the same mill, in the weaving shed, where she looks after two power looms. She has worked in the mill since she was a girl of nine. She is small and quick, and talks a lot, and laughs easily. She is the one who manages the family's money, and makes sure there is always food on the table, and the rent is always paid.

--

{img:teenage-mill-girl-1840-shawl-apron-hair-tied-back-cheerful-cobbled-lane}
Ellen is fourteen. She works in the mill full time now, in the weaving shed with her mother, learning to be a weaver. Before that, she was a piecer, in the spinning room, helping her father. She is clever and quick, and loves to read, whenever she has the chance. She goes to the Sunday school every week, and reads every book she can find. She dreams of being a teacher one day.

Jack is ten. He is a half-timer. By the new law, children his age can only work in the mill for part of the day. So Jack works in the mill in the morning, as a little piecer and helper in his father's spinning room, and goes to the mill school in the afternoon. Or the other way round, depending on the week. He is cheeky, and lively, and always hungry. He wants to be a spinner, like his father, when he grows up.

And there is Grandad Joseph, who is sixty-five. He lives with the family, in the back room downstairs. He was a hand-loom weaver all his life, working at home, in his cottage, up on the hillside above the town. But when the power looms came, he could not earn a living any more. Now, he is too old to work in the mill. So he stays at home, and helps with the house, and minds the fire, and tells stories of the old days.

{img:old-handloom-weaver-sitting-by-hearth-clay-pipe-grandson-listening}
The family speaks in the broad Lancashire way, with its own words and its own sounds. They say thee and thou, and aye for yes, and summat for something. They call each other love, and lass, and lad. They are warm and plain-spoken, and have no time for airs and graces. And they have a great sense of humour, often about the hardships of their own lives.

--

Altogether, the family earns about thirty-five shillings a week. Tom earns the most, about twenty-two shillings. Sarah earns about nine, and Ellen about four. Jack earns a little more than a shilling, for his half days. It is a good income, for a working family, in those days. Better than many. But the rent, the food, the coal, the clothes and the candles take almost all of it. There is very little left over, at the end of the week.

{img:mother-counting-coins-kitchen-table-candle-ration-of-shillings-careful}
On Saturday evening, when the wages are paid, Sarah sits at the kitchen table, and divides the money into little piles. The rent, the coal, the bread, the meat, the tea, the sugar, the boots, the burial club. And a few pennies saved, in a tin on the shelf, in case of illness or hard times. She is very careful with money. She has to be. In the mill towns, hard times are never far away.
