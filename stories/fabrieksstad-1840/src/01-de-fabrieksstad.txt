Tonight, we travel back to the year 1840, to a town in the north of England. A town called Rochdale, in Lancashire, on the edge of the moors, about ten miles north of Manchester. It lies in a valley, beside a small river called the Roch. All around it rise the green and brown hills of the Pennines, with their sheep, and their stone walls, and their wide open sky.

But the town itself is not green. It is grey and brown and black. From the valley rise dozens of tall chimneys, pouring out smoke, day and night. Beneath them stand the great cotton mills, huge buildings of brick and stone, five or six floors high, with hundreds of windows. And around the mills, row after row of small brick houses, crowded together along narrow streets, where the people who work in the mills live.

--

Rochdale is a mill town. One of dozens of mill towns in Lancashire, in those years. Oldham, Bolton, Bury, Blackburn, Preston, Stockport. Each one with its chimneys, its mills, and its rows of houses. Together, they make up the greatest cotton industry the world has ever seen. Lancashire spins and weaves more cotton than the rest of the world put together. The cloth goes everywhere. To India, to Africa, to America, to China.

{img:cotton-mill-interior-1840-rows-spinning-mules-workers-light-through-windows}
Fifty years ago, Rochdale was a small market town, where people spun wool and wove flannel by hand, in their own cottages. Now, it is a busy industrial town, of more than twenty thousand people. Most of them work in the mills, or in the trades that serve the mills. The machines have changed everything. The way people work, the way they live, and the way they think about the world.

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

In this story, we will follow one family through a year in Rochdale. The Hartleys. They live in a small brick house in a narrow street, a few minutes' walk from the mill where they work. There is Tom, the father, who is a spinner. Sarah, the mother, who is a weaver. Ellen, their daughter, who is fourteen, and works in the mill too. Jack, their son, who is ten, and works half the day, and goes to school the other half. And Grandad Joseph, Tom's father, who was once a hand-loom weaver, in the old days.

We will see how they live. Their work in the mill, among the noisy machines. Their little house, and their meals, and their streets. Their Sundays, and their holidays, and their worries, and their small pleasures. We will see the knocker-up, waking the town before dawn, and the great steam engine that drives the mill. We will go to the market, and the Sunday school, and the fair. And we will walk up onto the moors, on a summer holiday, where the air is fresh and clean.

{img:lancashire-moors-above-mill-town-family-walking-heather-summer-sky}
It was a hard life, in many ways. The hours were long, the work was tiring, and the towns were crowded and smoky. Many people were poor, and many were often ill. But it was also a time of great change, of new ideas, and of hope. The people of the mill towns were tough, and proud, and they looked after each other. They had their own songs, their own humour, their own way of speaking. And from their towns, in those years, came ideas that changed the world.

So let us go to Rochdale, in the year 1840. Let us walk down the cobbled streets, in the early morning, and hear the clatter of clogs on the stones. And let us meet the Hartleys, and their neighbours, in their little house in the shadow of the mill.
