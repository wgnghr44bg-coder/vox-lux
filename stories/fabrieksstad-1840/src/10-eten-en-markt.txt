On Saturday evening, after the mill closes and the wages are paid, the whole town goes to the market. The market place, in the centre of Rochdale, is full of stalls, lit by flaring gas lamps and oil lamps. There are stalls selling meat, fish, cheese, butter, eggs, vegetables, fruit, bread, cakes, sweets, pots and pans, clogs, cloth, and second-hand clothes. And everywhere, the shouting of the stallholders, calling out their prices.

Saturday night is the best time to shop. The stallholders want to sell everything before Sunday, when the market is closed. So late in the evening, the prices go down, and the bargains come out. Sarah knows this. She waits until about nine o'clock, and then goes round the stalls, with Ellen, and her big basket. She looks carefully at everything, and argues about the price, and buys only what she needs.

--

The family eats simply. For breakfast, porridge, made with oatmeal and water, and sometimes a little milk. Or bread and butter, or bread and dripping, which is the fat left over from roasting meat. With tea, which everyone drinks, many times a day, with a little sugar. For dinner, at midday, potatoes, with a little bacon or fat, or a stew, or a pie. For tea, in the evening, bread and butter, and perhaps cheese, or a herring, or some cold meat.

Oatcakes are very popular in Lancashire. They are made from oatmeal, and are soft and flat, like pancakes. They are hung up to dry on a wooden rack, above the fire, called a creel. And there is a dish that everyone in Lancashire loves, called hotpot. It is a stew of mutton, onions, and potatoes, cooked slowly in a deep pot, in the oven, all day. The potatoes on top go crisp and brown. It is the perfect meal, after a long, cold day.

{img:lancashire-hotpot-earthenware-dish-crisp-potatoes-steam-family-table}
On Sundays, the Hartleys have a proper dinner. A small piece of beef, or mutton, roasted in the oven, with potatoes and cabbage, and a pudding. Sarah buys the meat on Saturday night, at the market, when it is cheap. The family looks forward to Sunday dinner all week. It is the best meal of the week, and they eat it slowly, and enjoy every bite.

Food is the biggest part of the family's spending. About half of all their money goes on food. And the price of food changes, from year to year, depending on the harvest. In 1840, bread is expensive. There is a law, called the Corn Law, which keeps the price of wheat high, to protect the farmers and the landowners. Many people in the mill towns are angry about it. They say it makes the rich richer, and the poor hungrier.

--

{img:anti-corn-law-meeting-hall-crowd-1840-speaker-banner-free-trade}
A man from Rochdale, called John Bright, is one of the leaders of the campaign against the Corn Laws. His family owns a cotton mill in the town. He is a Quaker, and a brilliant speaker. Together with Richard Cobden, from Manchester, he travels all over the country, speaking at great meetings, against the Corn Laws. Tom has heard him speak, at a meeting in Rochdale. He says he has never heard anyone speak so well. In 1846, the Corn Laws were finally repealed, and the price of bread came down.

After the market, the family goes home, with the basket full. There are potatoes, and onions, and a piece of mutton, and some bacon, and cheese, and butter, and a big loaf. And a little bag of humbugs, for the children, as a treat. Jack eats his straight away. Ellen saves hers, for later.

{img:children-sharing-sweets-by-fire-saturday-night-basket-on-table-happy}
At home, Grandad Joseph has kept the fire going, and the kettle boiling. They sit by the fire, and drink their tea, and talk about the week. Tom smokes his pipe. Sarah puts her feet up, on the fender, for the first time all week. And outside, the market noise slowly dies away, and the streets grow quiet, and the gas lamps glow in the dark.
