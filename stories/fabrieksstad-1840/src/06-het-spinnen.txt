Tom Hartley works on the fourth floor of the mill, in the mule room. He is in charge of a pair of spinning mules. Each mule is a long machine, more than forty yards long, with hundreds of spindles in a row. Altogether, Tom's pair of mules has more than a thousand spindles, spinning a thousand threads at once. In the old days, a thousand spinners would have been needed, each with a spinning wheel, to do the same work.

The mule works in a strange, beautiful way. One part of it, called the carriage, moves slowly out, across the floor, for about five feet, on little wheels, pulling the threads and stretching them, and twisting them, as it goes. Then it stops, and rolls slowly back, winding the finished thread onto the spindles. Then out again, and back again, every few seconds, all day long. Out and back, out and back, like the waves of the sea.

--

The mule is partly driven by the steam engine, and partly by the spinner. Tom has to guide the carriage back, with his hands and his knee, at just the right speed, so the threads wind evenly onto the spindles. It takes great skill, and strength. He walks many miles a day, backward and forward, beside his mules. People say that a spinner walks twenty miles a day, without leaving the room. And he does it barefoot, because the floor is slippery with oil.

{img:young-boy-piecer-mending-broken-thread-mule-spindles-quick-fingers}
Tom has helpers, called piecers. Usually two or three. They are young people, mostly boys and girls, and some young men. Their job is to watch the threads, and when one breaks, which happens often, to join the two ends together again, quickly, while the mule is moving. They lick their fingers, and twist the ends together, in a moment. They must be very quick, and very nimble. A good piecer can mend a thread in two seconds.

Jack is one of Tom's piecers, in the mornings. He is still learning, and he is not very fast yet. But his father is patient with him, and shows him how to do it. Jack walks up and down beside the mule, watching the threads, and mending the broken ones. Sometimes he has to crawl underneath the moving machine, to clean up the fly and the waste cotton. This is called scavenging. It can be dangerous, and he must be very careful.

--

The mule room is very noisy. The clatter and whirr of the mules, the flapping of the belts, the hum of the spindles. The workers must shout to be heard. But they get used to it. They hardly notice it after a while. And there are moments of calm, too, when the mule is stopped, for the full spindles to be taken off, and replaced with empty ones. This is called doffing. Then the room is quieter, for a few minutes, and people can talk.

{img:doffing-break-spinners-chatting-mule-room-sunlight-through-windows-fly}
At half past eight, the engine stops, for half an hour. It is breakfast time. The workers sit on the floor, or on the window sills, and eat what they have brought from home. Usually porridge, kept warm in a can, or bread and dripping, and tea. Some of the women have brought their children's breakfast too. Sarah has made a can of porridge for Jack, and a slice of bread with a scrape of butter.

The spinners are the aristocrats of the mill. They are the best paid workers, and the most respected. They have their own trade union, to protect their wages and their rights, and they are proud of their skill. Some of them are leaders in their chapels, and their communities. Many of them have strong opinions, about politics, and about the rights of working men. They read the newspapers, and argue, during the breaks.

{img:spinners-reading-newspaper-aloud-breakfast-break-mill-floor-workers-listening}
After breakfast, the engine starts again, and the mules begin to move, out and back, out and back. Tom takes his place beside them again. His hands guide the carriage, and his eyes watch the threads. It is hard, tiring work, hour after hour. But there is something steady and calm about it, too. The rhythm of the machine, like breathing. And the white thread, winding slowly onto the spindles, in the soft light from the windows.
