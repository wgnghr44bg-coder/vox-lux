Tonight, we travel back to the year 1840, to a town in the north of England. A town called Rochdale, in Lancashire, on the edge of the moors, about ten miles north of Manchester. It lies in a valley, beside a small river called the Roch. All around it rise the green and brown hills of the Pennines, with their sheep, and their stone walls, and their wide open sky.

[pause]

But the town itself is not green. It is grey and brown and black. From the valley rise dozens of tall chimneys, pouring out smoke, day and night. Beneath them stand the great cotton mills, huge buildings of brick and stone, five or six floors high, with hundreds of windows. And around the mills, row after row of small brick houses, crowded together along narrow streets, where the people who work in the mills live.

[long-pause]

Rochdale is a mill town. One of dozens of mill towns in Lancashire, in those years. Oldham, Bolton, Bury, Blackburn, Preston, Stockport. Each one with its chimneys, its mills, and its rows of houses. Together, they make up the greatest cotton industry the world has ever seen. Lancashire spins and weaves more cotton than the rest of the world put together. The cloth goes everywhere. To India, to Africa, to America, to China.

[pause]

{img:cotton-mill-interior-1840-rows-spinning-mules-workers-light-through-windows}
Fifty years ago, Rochdale was a small market town, where people spun wool and wove flannel by hand, in their own cottages. Now, it is a busy industrial town, of more than twenty thousand people. Most of them work in the mills, or in the trades that serve the mills. The machines have changed everything. The way people work, the way they live, and the way they think about the world.

[pause]

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

{img:mill-workers-walking-to-work-dawn-clogs-shawls-cobbled-street-gaslight}
In this story, we will follow one family through a year in Rochdale. The Hartleys. They live in a small brick house in a narrow street, a few minutes' walk from the mill where they work. There is Tom, the father, who is a spinner. Sarah, the mother, who is a weaver. Ellen, their daughter, who is fourteen, and works in the mill too. Jack, their son, who is ten, and works half the day, and goes to school the other half. And Grandad Joseph, Tom's father, who was once a hand-loom weaver, in the old days.

[pause]

We will see how they live. Their work in the mill, among the noisy machines. Their little house, and their meals, and their streets. Their Sundays, and their holidays, and their worries, and their small pleasures. We will see the knocker-up, waking the town before dawn, and the great steam engine that drives the mill. We will go to the market, and the Sunday school, and the fair. And we will walk up onto the moors, on a summer holiday, where the air is fresh and clean.

[pause]

{img:lancashire-moors-above-mill-town-family-walking-heather-summer-sky}
It was a hard life, in many ways. The hours were long, the work was tiring, and the towns were crowded and smoky. Many people were poor, and many were often ill. But it was also a time of great change, of new ideas, and of hope. The people of the mill towns were tough, and proud, and they looked after each other. They had their own songs, their own humour, their own way of speaking. And from their towns, in those years, came ideas that changed the world.

[pause]

So let us go to Rochdale, in the year 1840. Let us walk down the cobbled streets, in the early morning, and hear the clatter of clogs on the stones. And let us meet the Hartleys, and their neighbours, in their little house in the shadow of the mill.

[long-pause]

{img:hand-spinning-wheel-cottage-1760s-woman-spinning-wool-hearth-old-way}
To understand the mill towns, we must go back a little. About eighty years before 1840, in the middle of the eighteenth century, almost all cloth in Britain was made by hand. In the villages of Lancashire and Yorkshire, families spun and wove at home, in their cottages. The women and children spun the thread, on spinning wheels. The men wove the thread into cloth, on wooden looms, in the upstairs rooms of their houses.

[pause]

It was slow work. A spinner could make only one thread at a time. A weaver could weave only a few yards of cloth in a day. But it was a life that many people liked. They worked at home, with their families. They could choose their own hours. They could stop, and go out into the fields, or rest on a sunny afternoon. And they were proud of their skill.

[long-pause]

Then, from the 1760s onward, a series of inventions changed everything. Clever men, many of them from Lancashire, invented machines that could spin thread much faster than any person. First the spinning jenny, invented by James Hargreaves, which could spin eight threads at once, and later many more. Then the water frame, by Richard Arkwright, driven by a water wheel. And then the spinning mule, by Samuel Crompton, from Bolton, which combined the best of both.

[pause]

{img:early-water-powered-mill-river-valley-1790s-stone-building-wheel}
These machines were too big, and too expensive, to go in a cottage. They needed a special building, with power to drive them. At first, the power came from water. The first mills were built beside fast-flowing rivers, in the valleys of the Pennines, where water wheels could turn the machines. Then, at the end of the eighteenth century, the steam engine arrived, improved by James Watt. Now mills could be built anywhere, near coal, and near towns, and near the workers.

[pause]

The cotton itself came from far away. From the southern states of America, mostly, where it was grown on great plantations, and picked by enslaved people. The raw cotton was carried across the Atlantic, in sailing ships, to the port of Liverpool. From there, it came by canal, and later by railway, to Manchester, and then to the mill towns. And the finished cloth went back out, through Liverpool, to the whole world.

[long-pause]

{img:manchester-1840-cottonopolis-skyline-chimneys-canal-warehouses}
Manchester became the centre of it all. People called it Cottonopolis, the city of cotton. In 1830, the first passenger railway in the world opened, between Liverpool and Manchester. Now people and goods could travel quickly between the port and the city. The railway was a sensation. Within a few years, railways were being built all over the country. In 1839, a railway line reached Rochdale too.

[pause]

With the mills came the people. From the countryside, from the hills, from Ireland, families came to the towns, to find work in the mills. The towns grew faster than anyone could manage. Houses were built quickly and cheaply, in long rows, close together, with no proper drains or clean water. The towns became crowded, dirty and smoky. But there was work, and wages, every week, which many people had never had before.

[pause]

{img:mill-owner-victorian-top-hat-standing-before-his-mill-proud-1840}
Some people became very rich from the cotton mills. The mill owners built big houses, outside the towns, away from the smoke. Some of them were hard and greedy. Others were kind, and built schools and chapels and better houses for their workers. In the mill towns, the gap between rich and poor was very great. And many people began to ask questions, about fairness, and about the rights of working people.

[pause]

This was the Industrial Revolution. It began in Britain, and especially in Lancashire, and it spread around the world. It changed how people lived and worked, more than anything since the beginning of farming, thousands of years ago. And in 1840, it was in full swing.

[long-pause]

{img:victorian-working-family-1840-terraced-house-doorstep-father-mother-children-grandfather}
Let us meet the Hartleys properly. Tom Hartley is thirty-eight years old. He is a mule spinner, at a big cotton mill near the river, owned by a family called the Kelsalls. Spinning is the best paid job in the mill, for a working man. It needs skill and strength, and many years of experience. Tom is proud of his trade. He is a tall, quiet man, with big hands, and a dry sense of humour.

[pause]

Sarah Hartley is thirty-six. She is a weaver, at the same mill, in the weaving shed, where she looks after two power looms. She has worked in the mill since she was a girl of nine. She is small and quick, and talks a lot, and laughs easily. She is the one who manages the family's money, and makes sure there is always food on the table, and the rent is always paid.

[long-pause]

{img:teenage-mill-girl-1840-shawl-apron-hair-tied-back-cheerful-cobbled-lane}
Ellen is fourteen. She works in the mill full time now, in the weaving shed with her mother, learning to be a weaver. Before that, she was a piecer, in the spinning room, helping her father. She is clever and quick, and loves to read, whenever she has the chance. She goes to the Sunday school every week, and reads every book she can find. She dreams of being a teacher one day.

[pause]

Jack is ten. He is a half-timer. By the new law, children his age can only work in the mill for part of the day. So Jack works in the mill in the morning, as a little piecer and helper in his father's spinning room, and goes to the mill school in the afternoon. Or the other way round, depending on the week. He is cheeky, and lively, and always hungry. He wants to be a spinner, like his father, when he grows up.

[pause]

And there is Grandad Joseph, who is sixty-five. He lives with the family, in the back room downstairs. He was a hand-loom weaver all his life, working at home, in his cottage, up on the hillside above the town. But when the power looms came, he could not earn a living any more. Now, he is too old to work in the mill. So he stays at home, and helps with the house, and minds the fire, and tells stories of the old days.

[pause]

{img:old-handloom-weaver-sitting-by-hearth-clay-pipe-grandson-listening}
The family speaks in the broad Lancashire way, with its own words and its own sounds. They say thee and thou, and aye for yes, and summat for something. They call each other love, and lass, and lad. They are warm and plain-spoken, and have no time for airs and graces. And they have a great sense of humour, often about the hardships of their own lives.

[long-pause]

Altogether, the family earns about thirty-five shillings a week. Tom earns the most, about twenty-two shillings. Sarah earns about nine, and Ellen about four. Jack earns a little more than a shilling, for his half days. It is a good income, for a working family, in those days. Better than many. But the rent, the food, the coal, the clothes and the candles take almost all of it. There is very little left over, at the end of the week.

[pause]

{img:mother-counting-coins-kitchen-table-candle-ration-of-shillings-careful}
On Saturday evening, when the wages are paid, Sarah sits at the kitchen table, and divides the money into little piles. The rent, the coal, the bread, the meat, the tea, the sugar, the boots, the burial club. And a few pennies saved, in a tin on the shelf, in case of illness or hard times. She is very careful with money. She has to be. In the mill towns, hard times are never far away.

[long-pause]

{img:knocker-up-long-pole-tapping-bedroom-window-dark-terraced-street-dawn}
The day begins very early in Rochdale. Long before dawn, at about half past four in the morning, a strange figure walks through the dark, silent streets. It is the knocker-up. An old man, in a heavy coat and a cap, carrying a long wooden pole, with a little piece of wire on the end. He stops beneath each bedroom window, and taps on the glass, with his pole. Tap, tap, tap.

[pause]

In 1840, very few working people have clocks. And nobody has an alarm clock. So the knocker-up is paid a few pennies a week, to wake people up for work. He knows exactly which houses to visit, and at what time. He keeps tapping until someone comes to the window, and calls out that they are awake. Then he moves on, to the next house. Some knocker-ups use a pea shooter instead, blowing dried peas at the windows.

[long-pause]

In the Hartleys' house, the tapping comes at a quarter to five. Tom wakes first, and calls out that he is up. Then he lights a candle, and wakes Sarah. It is cold, and dark. The fire went out in the night. Sarah gets up quickly, and puts on her clothes. A long dark skirt, a blouse, an apron, and a thick woollen shawl. Then she wakes the children. Ellen gets up without a word. Jack takes longer, and has to be shaken twice.

[pause]

{img:dark-kitchen-before-dawn-candle-mother-lighting-fire-kettle-children-sleepy}
Downstairs, Grandad Joseph has already raked out the ashes, and lit the fire. He always wakes early, out of old habit. The kettle is on, and soon there is hot tea, with a little sugar and milk. There is no time for a proper breakfast now. That comes later, at the mill. Just a cup of tea, and a piece of bread. Then the family puts on their clogs, and their shawls and caps, and goes out into the dark.

[pause]

Outside, the streets are full of people. Hundreds of them, coming out of every door, walking toward the mills. Men, women and children. They walk quickly, in the cold, with their shawls pulled over their heads, and their hands in their pockets. They hardly speak. The only sound is the clatter of thousands of wooden clogs, on the cobblestones. It echoes between the houses, like the sound of a great, rushing river.

[long-pause]

{img:crowd-of-mill-workers-clogs-walking-toward-mill-gate-lamps-dark-morning}
Clogs are the shoes of the mill towns. They have wooden soles, and leather tops, and little iron strips on the bottom, called irons, to make them last longer. They are cheap, warm, and strong, and they keep the feet dry, on the wet streets and the wet mill floors. Everyone wears them. And the clatter of clogs, in the early morning, is the sound that everyone in a mill town remembers, all their lives.

[pause]

At a quarter to six, the mill bell rings. A loud, clanging bell, in a little tower on the roof of the mill. It means that there are fifteen minutes left before the gates close. The workers walk faster. Some of them run. If they are late, even by a few minutes, they will be locked out, and lose a quarter of a day's wages. Or they will be fined. So nobody wants to be late.

[pause]

{img:mill-gate-closing-gatekeeper-pocket-watch-last-workers-hurrying-in-dawn}
At six o'clock exactly, the gatekeeper closes the big iron gates. And a moment later, deep inside the mill, the great steam engine starts to turn. Slowly at first, then faster and faster. The long iron shafts begin to spin, all through the building, and the thousands of machines begin to move, on every floor. And the mill comes to life, with a great roar of noise, that will not stop until the evening.

[pause]

The working day has begun. It will last for twelve hours, with breaks for breakfast and dinner. And outside, the sky slowly turns grey, and then pale, over the smoky town, as the dawn comes up over the moors.

[long-pause]

{img:large-five-storey-brick-cotton-mill-1840-chimney-river-roch-many-windows}
The Kelsall mill stands beside the river, at the bottom of the valley. It is a huge building, of dark red brick, five storeys high, and nearly a hundred yards long. It has rows and rows of tall windows, to let in as much light as possible. Beside it stands the tall, square chimney, taller than any church tower in the town, pouring out black smoke. And at one end, there is a smaller building, with tall arched windows. This is the engine house.

[pause]

The engine house is the heart of the mill. Inside, there is a great steam engine, painted green and red, with polished brass and shining steel. It is as big as a house. It has a huge iron beam, which rocks up and down, slowly, like a giant seesaw. And a great flywheel, taller than three men, which turns round and round, steadily, all day long.

[long-pause]

{img:mill-steam-beam-engine-house-polished-brass-flywheel-engineer-oil-can}
The engine is looked after by the engine man, Mr Ogden. He is a serious, careful man, who loves his engine like a child. He polishes the brass every day, and oils every moving part, and listens carefully to the sound of it, for any sign of trouble. Next door, in the boiler house, the stokers shovel coal into the furnaces, all day long, to heat the water in the great boilers, and make the steam. They are black with coal dust, and sweat from the heat.

[pause]

From the flywheel, the power goes up through the whole mill. A great upright iron shaft, turning inside the building, from the bottom to the top. On every floor, it turns long horizontal shafts, which run along the ceiling, from one end of the room to the other. And from those shafts, wide leather belts come down to every machine, turning their wheels, and driving them. Thousands of belts, spinning and flapping, in every room.

[pause]

On each floor, there is a different stage of the work. On the ground floor, the raw cotton arrives, in big bales, from Liverpool. It is pressed tight, and dirty, full of seeds and leaves and dust. First, it is opened and cleaned, by machines that beat it and blow it, until it is fluffy and loose. This is the dustiest work in the mill. The air is thick with cotton fluff, which the workers call fly.

[pause]

{img:carding-room-1840-cotton-fluff-air-machines-workers-dusty-light}
On the next floor, the cotton is carded. Big machines, covered with thousands of tiny wire teeth, comb the cotton fibres, until they all lie in the same direction. The cotton comes out of the carding machine as a soft, thick rope, called a sliver. Then the sliver is stretched and twisted, again and again, on other machines, until it becomes thinner, and stronger. Now it is ready for spinning.

[long-pause]

The spinning rooms are on the upper floors. Long, wide rooms, filled with spinning mules. And at the top of the building, or in a separate shed beside the mill, is the weaving shed, where the thread is woven into cloth, on power looms. In some mills, the spinning and weaving are done in different buildings, or even in different mills. In the Kelsall mill, they are all together, on the same site.

[pause]

{img:mill-yard-carts-cotton-bales-unloading-horses-workers-mill-wall}
Altogether, about six hundred people work at the Kelsall mill. Men, women, and children. The overlookers, who are in charge of each room. The spinners, the piecers, the carders, the weavers, the mechanics, the engine men, the stokers, the warehousemen. The bookkeepers, in the office. And the manager, Mr Firth, who walks through the mill every day, with his hands behind his back, watching everything.

[pause]

The mill is warm, and damp, inside. The cotton needs warm, damp air, or the thread breaks. So the windows are kept closed, and the air is kept moist, with steam. In the summer, it is very hot and stuffy. In the winter, it is warmer inside than out. But the air is always full of the fine white fly, which settles on everything. On the machines, on the floor, on the workers' hair and clothes. And on the windows, so that the light comes through soft and white, like snow.

[long-pause]

{img:spinning-mule-carriage-moving-out-hundreds-spindles-spinner-watching-1840}
Tom Hartley works on the fourth floor of the mill, in the mule room. He is in charge of a pair of spinning mules. Each mule is a long machine, more than forty yards long, with hundreds of spindles in a row. Altogether, Tom's pair of mules has more than a thousand spindles, spinning a thousand threads at once. In the old days, a thousand spinners would have been needed, each with a spinning wheel, to do the same work.

[pause]

The mule works in a strange, beautiful way. One part of it, called the carriage, moves slowly out, across the floor, for about five feet, on little wheels, pulling the threads and stretching them, and twisting them, as it goes. Then it stops, and rolls slowly back, winding the finished thread onto the spindles. Then out again, and back again, every few seconds, all day long. Out and back, out and back, like the waves of the sea.

[long-pause]

The mule is partly driven by the steam engine, and partly by the spinner. Tom has to guide the carriage back, with his hands and his knee, at just the right speed, so the threads wind evenly onto the spindles. It takes great skill, and strength. He walks many miles a day, backward and forward, beside his mules. People say that a spinner walks twenty miles a day, without leaving the room. And he does it barefoot, because the floor is slippery with oil.

[pause]

{img:young-boy-piecer-mending-broken-thread-mule-spindles-quick-fingers}
Tom has helpers, called piecers. Usually two or three. They are young people, mostly boys and girls, and some young men. Their job is to watch the threads, and when one breaks, which happens often, to join the two ends together again, quickly, while the mule is moving. They lick their fingers, and twist the ends together, in a moment. They must be very quick, and very nimble. A good piecer can mend a thread in two seconds.

[pause]

Jack is one of Tom's piecers, in the mornings. He is still learning, and he is not very fast yet. But his father is patient with him, and shows him how to do it. Jack walks up and down beside the mule, watching the threads, and mending the broken ones. Sometimes he has to crawl underneath the moving machine, to clean up the fly and the waste cotton. This is called scavenging. It can be dangerous, and he must be very careful.

[long-pause]

The mule room is very noisy. The clatter and whirr of the mules, the flapping of the belts, the hum of the spindles. The workers must shout to be heard. But they get used to it. They hardly notice it after a while. And there are moments of calm, too, when the mule is stopped, for the full spindles to be taken off, and replaced with empty ones. This is called doffing. Then the room is quieter, for a few minutes, and people can talk.

[pause]

{img:doffing-break-spinners-chatting-mule-room-sunlight-through-windows-fly}
At half past eight, the engine stops, for half an hour. It is breakfast time. The workers sit on the floor, or on the window sills, and eat what they have brought from home. Usually porridge, kept warm in a can, or bread and dripping, and tea. Some of the women have brought their children's breakfast too. Sarah has made a can of porridge for Jack, and a slice of bread with a scrape of butter.

[pause]

The spinners are the aristocrats of the mill. They are the best paid workers, and the most respected. They have their own trade union, to protect their wages and their rights, and they are proud of their skill. Some of them are leaders in their chapels, and their communities. Many of them have strong opinions, about politics, and about the rights of working men. They read the newspapers, and argue, during the breaks.

[pause]

{img:spinners-reading-newspaper-aloud-breakfast-break-mill-floor-workers-listening}
After breakfast, the engine starts again, and the mules begin to move, out and back, out and back. Tom takes his place beside them again. His hands guide the carriage, and his eyes watch the threads. It is hard, tiring work, hour after hour. But there is something steady and calm about it, too. The rhythm of the machine, like breathing. And the white thread, winding slowly onto the spindles, in the soft light from the windows.

[long-pause]

{img:weaving-shed-1840-rows-of-power-looms-north-light-roof-women-weavers}
Sarah and Ellen work in the weaving shed. It is a long, low building, beside the main mill, with a roof shaped like the teeth of a saw. Each part of the roof has a window, facing north, so that the light is soft and even, all day long, without any harsh sunlight. Beneath the roof, in long rows, stand the power looms. Hundreds of them, close together, with narrow passages between them.

[pause]

A power loom is a machine that weaves cloth automatically, driven by the steam engine. In the old days, a hand-loom weaver threw the shuttle across the loom by hand, back and forth, thousands of times a day. Now, the machine does it. The shuttle flies from side to side, faster than the eye can follow, carrying the thread across. And the cloth grows slowly, inch by inch, and winds onto a roller at the front.

[long-pause]

{img:weaver-woman-tending-two-power-looms-shuttle-flying-cloth-growing}
Sarah looks after two looms. She walks between them, watching the cloth, and the threads, and the shuttle. When a thread breaks, she stops the loom, and mends it, quickly. When the shuttle runs out of thread, she puts in a new bobbin, and starts the loom again. She must keep the looms running as much as possible, because she is paid by the piece, for each length of cloth she weaves. The more cloth, the more money.

[pause]

The weaving shed is the noisiest place in the whole mill. The noise of hundreds of looms, all clattering together, is enormous. A great, crashing, rattling roar, that never stops. It is so loud that the weavers cannot hear each other speak, even if they shout. So they have learned to talk without sound. They move their lips very clearly, and slowly, and read each other's lips. This is called mee-mawing. The mill girls are very good at it.

[pause]

Across the shed, Sarah can have a whole conversation with her friend Mary, by mee-mawing. They talk about their children, and their husbands, and the price of bread, and the gossip of the street. They laugh, silently, at the overlooker's new hat. And sometimes they tell jokes that would make the men blush, without anyone else knowing. Even years later, when they leave the mill, many weavers still move their lips clearly when they talk, out of habit.

[pause]

{img:two-mill-women-lip-reading-across-looms-laughing-silently-noisy-shed}
Ellen is still learning. She works beside her mother, on her own loom, while Sarah watches her, and helps her. Ellen is quick, and careful, and she learns fast. Soon, she will have two looms of her own. But she does not want to be a weaver all her life. She wants to be a teacher. She thinks about it often, as she watches the shuttle fly back and forth. She dreams of a schoolroom, quiet and peaceful, with children reading.

[long-pause]

The overlooker in the weaving shed is a man called Mr Butterworth. He is in charge of the looms, and the weavers. He walks up and down the rows, watching the work, and mending the looms when they go wrong. He is strict, but fair. He fines the weavers if their cloth has faults in it, or if they are late, or if they talk too much. The weavers do not like the fines. But they like Mr Butterworth well enough.

[pause]

At half past twelve, the engine stops again, for an hour. It is dinner time. Some of the workers go home, if they live close by. Others eat in the mill, or in the yard, in the fresh air, if it is fine. Sarah and Ellen go home, quickly, through the streets, to eat with Grandad Joseph, who has made a pot of potato stew, with a little bacon. Then they hurry back, before the bell rings again.

[pause]

The afternoon is long. The looms clatter on, hour after hour, and the light from the north windows slowly changes. The air is warm and damp, and full of fly. The weavers grow tired. Their feet ache, from standing all day, and their ears ring with the noise. But they keep going, watching the threads, mending the breaks, changing the shuttles, until the evening comes.

[pause]

{img:finished-cotton-cloth-rolls-stacked-warehouse-calico-white-light}
The cloth they weave is a plain cotton cloth, called calico. It is white, or light grey, and soft. It will be taken to Manchester, and sold, and then printed with colourful patterns, or dyed, or bleached. It will be made into shirts, dresses, sheets, curtains, and handkerchiefs. And it will be sent all over the world. Somewhere, far away, a woman in India, or Africa, or South America, will wear a dress made from cloth woven by Sarah Hartley, in Rochdale.

[long-pause]

{img:child-workers-1840-mill-floor-boys-girls-small-quiet-gentle-scene}
In 1840, there are many children working in the mills of Lancashire. Thousands of them. Boys and girls, some of them only nine years old. They work as piecers, and scavengers, and doffers, and helpers. Their small fingers are quick, and they can crawl under the machines. And they are cheap to employ. For the families, the children's wages are an important part of the weekly income.

[pause]

Some years before, the conditions for children in the mills were very bad. Children as young as five or six worked in the mills, for twelve or fourteen hours a day, or even more. Some of them were orphans, sent from the workhouses, far away. They were often tired, ill, and unhappy. Some were treated cruelly by the overlookers. And people began to speak out, and to say that it was wrong.

[long-pause]

Reformers like Richard Oastler, and Lord Ashley, and many working people themselves, campaigned for a law to protect the children. They held meetings, and marches, and wrote letters and pamphlets. They wanted a limit of ten hours a day for all mill workers. This was called the Ten Hours Movement. And in 1833, Parliament passed a new law, called the Factory Act. It did not give them everything they wanted. But it was a beginning.

[pause]

The Factory Act said that no child under nine could work in a textile mill. Children between nine and thirteen could work no more than nine hours a day, and must go to school for two hours a day. Young people between thirteen and eighteen could work no more than twelve hours a day. And nobody under eighteen could work at night. For the first time, the government sent inspectors to the mills, to make sure that the law was obeyed.

[pause]

{img:factory-inspector-top-hat-checking-register-mill-office-children-lined-up}
The inspectors were few, and they had many mills to visit. So the law was not always obeyed. Some mill owners cheated. They let children work longer hours, or lied about their ages. In 1840, there are no birth certificates yet, for most children, so it is hard to know how old a child really is. Instead, a doctor looks at the child, and checks their teeth and their height, and gives them a certificate, to say they look old enough.

[pause]

Jack has such a certificate. It says that he has the ordinary strength and appearance of a child of at least nine years. He is ten, really. So he works in the mill for half the day, and goes to the mill school for the other half. He is one of the half-timers. He does not mind the mill too much. He likes being with his father, and he likes the money he earns. He gives it to his mother, every Saturday, and she gives him a penny back, for himself.

[long-pause]

{img:mill-school-room-1840-children-slates-teacher-small-windows-benches}
The mill school is in a small room, beside the mill. It has rows of wooden benches, and a blackboard, and a few books. The teacher is an old man, who was once a soldier, and he is not a very good teacher. Sometimes he falls asleep in the afternoons. The children learn to read a little, and write a little, and count. They write on slates, with slate pencils, which squeak. It is not much of an education. But it is better than nothing.

[pause]

Jack likes the school less than the mill. He finds it hard to sit still, after the busy morning. He would rather be outside, playing in the street, or by the river. But Ellen helps him with his reading, in the evenings, at home. She is very patient with him. And slowly, he is learning. Ellen says that reading is the most important thing in the world, because it opens doors. Jack is not sure about that. But he likes the stories she reads him.

[pause]

{img:sister-teaching-little-brother-to-read-by-candle-kitchen-table-evening}
Over the years that followed, the laws became better and better. More inspectors were appointed. The hours for women and young people were limited further. And in the end, school became compulsory for all children, and child labour in the mills came to an end. But in 1840, that is still far in the future. And for now, Jack goes to the mill in the morning, and to school in the afternoon, and plays in the street in the evening, until it gets dark.

[long-pause]

{img:row-of-two-up-two-down-brick-terraced-houses-1840-cobbled-street-washing-lines}
The Hartleys live in Spring Street, a narrow cobbled street, about five minutes' walk from the mill. On each side of the street, there is a long row of small brick houses, all joined together, all exactly the same. Each one has a front door, and a window downstairs, and a window upstairs. They were built by the mill owner, about fifteen years ago, for his workers. The Hartleys pay three shillings a week in rent.

[pause]

The house is what people call a two up, two down. Two small rooms downstairs, and two small rooms upstairs. Downstairs, at the front, is the main room, called the house, or the living room. This is where the family cooks, eats, washes, sits, and spends their evenings. At the back is a smaller room, the back kitchen, where Grandad Joseph sleeps, and where the dishes are washed, and the coal is kept.

[long-pause]

{img:victorian-working-class-kitchen-range-fire-oven-kettle-rag-rug-dresser}
The living room is the heart of the house. Against one wall is the fireplace, with a black iron range. It has an open fire in the middle, and a small oven on one side, and a boiler for hot water on the other. A kettle always sits on the fire, singing quietly. Sarah blacks the range with black lead every week, until it shines. On the mantelpiece above, there are two china dogs, a clock that does not work, and a little tin box, for the savings.

[pause]

There is a wooden table, scrubbed white, and four chairs, and a wooden settle by the fire, with a cushion. On the floor, there is a rag rug, which Sarah made herself, from old scraps of cloth. There is a dresser against the wall, with the plates and cups. And in the corner, a small shelf, with the family's few books. The Bible, a hymn book, an old almanac, and a copy of The Pilgrim's Progress, which Ellen has read three times.

[pause]

{img:small-bedroom-terraced-house-iron-bed-patchwork-quilt-sloping-ceiling-candle}
Upstairs, there are two bedrooms. Tom and Sarah sleep in the front one, in a big iron bed. Ellen and Jack sleep in the back one, in two small beds, with a curtain between them, for Ellen's privacy. The beds have straw mattresses, and patchwork quilts, made by Sarah's mother. There is no heating upstairs. In the winter, the bedrooms are very cold, and sometimes there is frost on the inside of the windows.

[long-pause]

There is no running water in the house. The water comes from a pump, at the end of the street, which all the families share. Every morning and evening, someone must carry buckets of water home, for cooking and washing. This is usually Jack's job. He grumbles about it, but he does it. And there is no toilet in the house either. Behind the row of houses, there is a narrow yard, with a few privies, shared by several families.

[pause]

The streets are not very clean. There are no proper drains, and the rubbish piles up in the yards and the alleys. In wet weather, the unpaved alleys turn to mud. And the smoke from the mill chimneys, and from the hundreds of house chimneys, hangs over the town, especially on still days. Everything is covered with a fine layer of soot. The washing on the lines turns grey, if it is left out too long.

[pause]

{img:mother-scrubbing-front-doorstep-white-stone-neighbours-chatting-street}
But Sarah, like most of the women in the street, keeps her house as clean as she can. She scrubs the floor, and polishes the range, and washes the windows. And every week, she scrubs the front doorstep, and rubs it with a soft white stone, until it is bright and clean. All the women in the street do this. It is a matter of pride. A clean doorstep shows that the family is respectable and hard-working.

[pause]

On Monday, which is washing day, the whole street is full of steam, and soap, and wet washing. The women boil the clothes in big tubs, and pound them with a wooden dolly, and squeeze them through a mangle, and hang them out to dry. And they talk, and laugh, and share the news, across the backyards, as they work. Washing day is hard work. But it is also one of the most sociable days of the week.

[long-pause]

{img:rochdale-market-1840-stalls-saturday-night-gas-lamps-crowds-baskets}
On Saturday evening, after the mill closes and the wages are paid, the whole town goes to the market. The market place, in the centre of Rochdale, is full of stalls, lit by flaring gas lamps and oil lamps. There are stalls selling meat, fish, cheese, butter, eggs, vegetables, fruit, bread, cakes, sweets, pots and pans, clogs, cloth, and second-hand clothes. And everywhere, the shouting of the stallholders, calling out their prices.

[pause]

Saturday night is the best time to shop. The stallholders want to sell everything before Sunday, when the market is closed. So late in the evening, the prices go down, and the bargains come out. Sarah knows this. She waits until about nine o'clock, and then goes round the stalls, with Ellen, and her big basket. She looks carefully at everything, and argues about the price, and buys only what she needs.

[long-pause]

The family eats simply. For breakfast, porridge, made with oatmeal and water, and sometimes a little milk. Or bread and butter, or bread and dripping, which is the fat left over from roasting meat. With tea, which everyone drinks, many times a day, with a little sugar. For dinner, at midday, potatoes, with a little bacon or fat, or a stew, or a pie. For tea, in the evening, bread and butter, and perhaps cheese, or a herring, or some cold meat.

[pause]

Oatcakes are very popular in Lancashire. They are made from oatmeal, and are soft and flat, like pancakes. They are hung up to dry on a wooden rack, above the fire, called a creel. And there is a dish that everyone in Lancashire loves, called hotpot. It is a stew of mutton, onions, and potatoes, cooked slowly in a deep pot, in the oven, all day. The potatoes on top go crisp and brown. It is the perfect meal, after a long, cold day.

[pause]

{img:lancashire-hotpot-earthenware-dish-crisp-potatoes-steam-family-table}
On Sundays, the Hartleys have a proper dinner. A small piece of beef, or mutton, roasted in the oven, with potatoes and cabbage, and a pudding. Sarah buys the meat on Saturday night, at the market, when it is cheap. The family looks forward to Sunday dinner all week. It is the best meal of the week, and they eat it slowly, and enjoy every bite.

[pause]

Food is the biggest part of the family's spending. About half of all their money goes on food. And the price of food changes, from year to year, depending on the harvest. In 1840, bread is expensive. There is a law, called the Corn Law, which keeps the price of wheat high, to protect the farmers and the landowners. Many people in the mill towns are angry about it. They say it makes the rich richer, and the poor hungrier.

[long-pause]

{img:anti-corn-law-meeting-hall-crowd-1840-speaker-banner-free-trade}
A man from Rochdale, called John Bright, is one of the leaders of the campaign against the Corn Laws. His family owns a cotton mill in the town. He is a Quaker, and a brilliant speaker. Together with Richard Cobden, from Manchester, he travels all over the country, speaking at great meetings, against the Corn Laws. Tom has heard him speak, at a meeting in Rochdale. He says he has never heard anyone speak so well. In 1846, the Corn Laws were finally repealed, and the price of bread came down.

[pause]

After the market, the family goes home, with the basket full. There are potatoes, and onions, and a piece of mutton, and some bacon, and cheese, and butter, and a big loaf. And a little bag of humbugs, for the children, as a treat. Jack eats his straight away. Ellen saves hers, for later.

[pause]

{img:children-sharing-sweets-by-fire-saturday-night-basket-on-table-happy}
At home, Grandad Joseph has kept the fire going, and the kettle boiling. They sit by the fire, and drink their tea, and talk about the week. Tom smokes his pipe. Sarah puts her feet up, on the fender, for the first time all week. And outside, the market noise slowly dies away, and the streets grow quiet, and the gas lamps glow in the dark.

[long-pause]

{img:handloom-weaver-cottage-hillside-pennines-long-upper-windows-stone-1800}
Grandad Joseph was born in 1775, in a stone cottage, high on the hillside above Rochdale, in a little village on the edge of the moors. His father was a hand-loom weaver, and his grandfather too. In those days, almost every family in the hills around Rochdale made cloth at home. At first it was woollen cloth, called flannel, for which Rochdale was famous. Later, more and more of them wove cotton.

[pause]

The weavers' cottages had long rows of windows on the top floor, to let in as much light as possible. That was where the looms stood, in the weaving room, upstairs. The whole family worked together. Joseph's mother and sisters spun the thread, and wound it onto bobbins. His father wove it into cloth, on the big wooden loom. And when Joseph was old enough, he learned to weave too, sitting beside his father, at the loom.

[long-pause]

{img:father-and-son-at-wooden-handloom-upstairs-weaving-room-light-windows}
Joseph remembers those days as a golden time. In the 1790s, when he was a young man, hand-loom weavers were well paid. There was more thread than ever before, from the new spinning mills, and not enough weavers to weave it all. A good weaver could earn as much as a skilled craftsman. They wore good clothes, and kept a cow, and a few hens, and a little garden. They could work when they liked, and rest when they liked.

[pause]

On fine days, Joseph and his friends would stop work in the afternoon, and go out onto the moors, or play games, or go to the alehouse. Then they would work late into the night, to make up for it. On Monday, many weavers did not work at all. They called it Saint Monday. And at the end of the week, Joseph would carry his cloth down the hill to the town, on his back, and sell it to the merchant, and come home with his money.

[pause]

{img:weavers-carrying-cloth-pieces-down-hill-path-to-town-market-morning}
But then the power looms came. At first, there were not many. But each year, there were more and more. The power looms could weave cloth much faster and cheaper than the hand-loom weavers. So the price of hand-woven cloth went down, and down, and down. The weavers had to work longer and longer hours, for less and less money. By the 1820s, a hand-loom weaver could hardly earn enough to feed his family, even working fourteen hours a day.

[pause]

It was a terrible time for the weavers. Many of them were very poor, and hungry. In 1826, in desperation, some of them broke into the mills, and smashed the power looms. But it did not help. The power looms were there to stay. One by one, the hand-loom weavers gave up. Their sons and daughters went to work in the mills. And the old weavers stayed at home, at their looms, weaving for a few pennies, until they could not weave any more.

[long-pause]

{img:old-weaver-looking-out-window-mill-chimneys-below-thoughtful-evening}
Joseph kept weaving until 1835, when his wife passed away. Then he sold his loom, for a few shillings, and came down the hill, to live with his son, in Spring Street. He has never worked in the mill. He says he is too old, and he would not want to. He does not like the noise, and the smoke, and the rules, and the bell. He misses his cottage, and the moors, and the quiet, and the freedom.

[pause]

But he does not complain much. He is glad to be with his family. He helps Sarah with the house, and the cooking, and the fire. He minds the house, while the others are at work. He mends the clogs, and the chairs, and anything that is broken. And in the evenings, he tells the children stories, of the old days, when he was young. Of the moors, and the weavers, and the fairs, and the songs.

[pause]

Jack loves his grandad's stories. Especially the ones about the boggarts, the strange little spirits that lived on the moors, and in old houses, and played tricks on people. Joseph tells them in a low voice, by the fire, in the dark. Jack listens, wide-eyed. Then he goes to bed, and pulls the blanket over his head, and listens for boggarts. But he only hears the rain, and the wind, and the distant hum of the town.

[long-pause]

{img:spring-street-evening-1840-neighbours-on-doorsteps-children-playing-cobbles}
Spring Street is a small world of its own. There are thirty houses, fifteen on each side, and about a hundred and fifty people living in them. Everyone knows everyone else. They work in the same mill, they go to the same chapel, they shop at the same market, and they share the same pump and the same privies. They know each other's business, and each other's troubles. And when something goes wrong, they help each other.

[pause]

On summer evenings, after the mill closes, the street comes to life. The women sit on their doorsteps, on little stools, knitting and talking. The men lean against the walls, smoking their pipes, and talking about work, and wages, and politics. The children play in the street, running, shouting, laughing. The street is the children's playground. There is nowhere else to play.

[long-pause]

{img:victorian-children-street-games-hopscotch-marbles-whip-and-top-cobbles}
The children play all kinds of games. Hopscotch, with the squares drawn in chalk on the stones. Marbles, in the gutter. Whip and top, spinning the wooden top along the street, with a little whip. Skipping, with an old rope, while the girls sing skipping songs. Tig, which is a chasing game. And games of soldiers, and highwaymen, and hide and seek. Jack is always in the middle of it, running, and shouting, and getting dirty.

[pause]

Next door to the Hartleys live the Ashworths. Mr Ashworth is a carder, and Mrs Ashworth used to be a weaver, until she had too many children. They have seven children, and the house is always full, and noisy. On the other side live old Mrs Kershaw, who is a widow, and her grown-up son, who is a mechanic in the mill. Mrs Kershaw is the street's unofficial nurse. Whenever anyone is ill, or a baby is born, they call for Mrs Kershaw.

[pause]

{img:old-woman-neighbour-nurse-bringing-broth-to-sick-house-shawl-doorway}
In the mill towns, the neighbours are very important. There is no government help for the poor, except the workhouse, which everyone fears. So people help each other. When someone is ill, the neighbours bring food, and look after the children. When someone passes away, they collect money for the funeral. When someone loses their job, they share what they have. It is not charity. It is just how things are done.

[pause]

Many families also belong to a friendly society, or a burial club. They pay a few pennies every week, into a fund. And when they are ill, or injured, or when someone passes away, the society pays them some money. The Hartleys pay into a burial club, so that there will be money for a decent funeral, when the time comes. For working people, a pauper's funeral, paid for by the parish, is a great shame.

[long-pause]

At the end of Spring Street, there is a small shop, run by a Mr Holt. It sells everything. Tea, sugar, flour, candles, soap, tobacco, matches, string, and sweets for the children. Many families buy their food there, on credit, during the week, and pay at the weekend. Mr Holt writes it all down in a little book. Sometimes his prices are high, and his weights are not quite right. People grumble about it. But they have no choice.

[pause]

{img:small-corner-shop-1840-shelves-jars-shopkeeper-scales-child-buying-candle}
On the corner, there is a beerhouse, the Weavers' Arms. It is a small, smoky room, with a few benches, and a fire, and barrels of beer. The men go there in the evenings, to drink a pint, and talk, and play dominoes, and sing. Tom goes there sometimes, on a Saturday night, but not often. Sarah does not approve of drinking. She has seen too many families ruined by it.

[pause]

As the evening goes on, the light fades, and the street grows quiet. The children are called in, one by one. The women take their stools inside. The lamplighter comes along, and lights the one gas lamp, at the end of the street. And one by one, the windows go dark, as the families go to bed. Tomorrow, the knocker-up will come again, before dawn.

[long-pause]

{img:methodist-chapel-1840-mill-town-stone-front-families-arriving-sunday}
On Sunday, the mill is silent. The great engine is still. The chimneys are cold, and for once, the smoke clears a little, over the town. The bell does not ring, and the knocker-up does not come. The families sleep a little longer. And the streets are quiet, except for the sound of church and chapel bells, calling people to worship.

[pause]

The Hartleys go to the Methodist chapel, on Union Street. Many working families in the mill towns are Methodists, or Baptists, or belong to other chapels. They are called Nonconformists, because they do not belong to the Church of England. The chapel is a plain, square building of stone, with tall windows, and rows of wooden pews, and a gallery. There are no statues, no pictures, no candles. Just the pulpit, and the organ, and the people.

[long-pause]

{img:chapel-congregation-singing-hymns-gallery-full-mill-families-sunday-best}
The service is lively. The minister preaches with great passion, about God's love, and sin, and forgiveness, and how to live a good life. And the people sing. Methodists love to sing. They sing the hymns of Charles Wesley, loudly and joyfully, with all their hearts. Tom has a deep bass voice. Sarah sings the melody, clear and true. And Ellen sings in the chapel choir. The sound fills the chapel, and spills out into the street.

[pause]

But the most important thing on Sunday, for the children, is the Sunday school. After the morning service, and again in the afternoon, hundreds of children gather in the big schoolroom, beneath the chapel. They are divided into classes, by age, with a teacher for each class. The teachers are volunteers, from the chapel. Many of them are mill workers themselves.

[pause]

The Sunday schools are very important in the mill towns. For many children, they are the only place where they can learn to read and write. The children learn their letters, and read from the Bible, and learn hymns and prayers by heart. Some Sunday schools also teach writing and arithmetic. Thousands of working people in Lancashire learned to read in a Sunday school. And reading changed their lives.

[pause]

Ellen is now a teacher herself, in the Sunday school. She teaches a class of little girls, aged six and seven. She is very proud of this. She sits with them, on a bench, in the corner of the schoolroom, and helps them sound out their letters. B, A, T, bat. C, A, T, cat. The little girls look up at her, with shining eyes. And when one of them reads a whole sentence for the first time, Ellen is happier than at any other moment in her week.

[long-pause]

{img:teenage-girl-teaching-little-girls-alphabet-sunday-school-corner-warm-light}
The Sunday school also has a library, of a few hundred books. The children and the young people can borrow them, for a week at a time. Ellen has read almost all of them. History books, travel books, poetry, and stories. She has read about the pyramids of Egypt, and the voyages of Captain Cook, and the life of John Wesley. She reads late at night, by the light of a candle, until her mother tells her to blow it out.

[pause]

Once a year, in the summer, the Sunday school has a great procession, called the Whit Walk. All the children, in their best clothes, walk through the streets of the town, behind a big banner, and a brass band. The girls wear white dresses, and carry flowers. The boys wear clean collars, and their hair is brushed flat. Their families line the streets, and cheer. Then they all go to a field outside the town, for games, and races, and buns and tea.

[pause]

{img:whit-walk-procession-children-white-dresses-banner-brass-band-mill-town}
On Sunday afternoon, if the weather is fine, the families go for a walk. Out of the town, along the river, or up onto the hills, where the air is clean. They walk slowly, in their Sunday clothes, and greet their neighbours, and enjoy the fresh air, and the green fields. It is the one afternoon in the week when they can see the sky properly, without the smoke.

[pause]

In the evening, there is another service, at the chapel. Then home, for a light supper, and early to bed. Because tomorrow, the bell will ring again, and the engine will start again, and the week of work will begin again. But for now, the town is quiet, and peaceful, under the stars.

[long-pause]

{img:penny-black-stamp-1840-letter-envelope-victorian-writing-desk-quill}
In 1840, something new happened, which changed life for ordinary people all over Britain. On the tenth of January, 1840, the Penny Post began. And in May, the first postage stamp in the world went on sale. It was a small black stamp, with a picture of the young Queen Victoria on it. It cost one penny. It was called the Penny Black.

[pause]

Before 1840, sending a letter was very expensive. The cost depended on the distance, and on the number of sheets of paper. And it was paid by the person who received the letter, not the person who sent it. A letter from Rochdale to London might cost a shilling or more. That was more than a day's wages for many people. So working people hardly ever wrote or received letters.

[long-pause]

Some people found clever ways to avoid paying. They would send a letter with a secret code on the outside. The person receiving it would look at the outside, read the code, and then refuse to accept the letter, so they did not have to pay. The code might say, all is well, or come home, or the baby is born. It was a kind of cheating. But it was the only way many poor families could keep in touch.

[pause]

A man called Rowland Hill had a better idea. He said that every letter, to anywhere in Britain, should cost the same small amount, one penny, and it should be paid in advance, by the sender. He invented the postage stamp, to show that the letter had been paid for. Many people said it would never work. But it did. In the first year, the number of letters in Britain more than doubled.

[pause]

{img:young-woman-writing-letter-by-candle-kitchen-table-ink-pot-paper}
For the Hartleys, the Penny Post is a wonderful thing. Sarah has a sister, Anne, who married a farmer and lives far away, near Lancaster, in the north of the county. Before, they almost never heard from each other. Now, they can write to each other every month. Ellen writes the letters for her mother, because Sarah never learned to write very well. Sarah tells her what to say, and Ellen writes it down, in her best handwriting.

[pause]

The letters are full of news. About the children, and the work, and the weather, and the neighbours. About illnesses, and recoveries, and births, and weddings. Anne writes about the farm, and the cows, and the harvest, and the long walk to market. Sarah writes about the mill, and the town, and the price of bread. When a letter comes, the whole family gathers round, and Ellen reads it aloud, by the fire.

[long-pause]

{img:postman-red-coat-delivering-letter-terraced-house-door-woman-smiling}
The postman comes along Spring Street once a day, in his uniform, with his bag. In the old days, he hardly ever stopped there. Now, he stops at many doors. The families wait for him eagerly. Letters from relatives far away. From sons who have gone to work in other towns. From daughters in service, in big houses. From brothers who have emigrated to America. The world is getting smaller.

[pause]

Ellen loves letters. She keeps every one that Aunt Anne sends, tied together with a ribbon, in a little box under her bed. She reads them again and again. And sometimes she writes a letter of her own, to her cousin Mary, who is the same age as her, and lives on the farm. They tell each other their secrets, and their dreams. Mary wants to marry a farmer. Ellen wants to be a teacher. They promise to visit each other one day.

[pause]

{img:girl-reading-old-letters-tied-with-ribbon-bedroom-candle-dreaming}
The Penny Post was copied all over the world. Soon, every country had postage stamps. And the Penny Black became the most famous stamp in history. Today, there are still many Penny Blacks in existence, in collections and museums. They are not very rare, because so many were printed. But they are treasured, as the first stamp ever made. And they show the face of a young queen, at the beginning of her long reign.

[long-pause]

{img:chartist-meeting-on-moor-crowd-listening-speaker-cart-1839-evening-calm}
In the mill towns, in these years, many working people are talking about politics. In the beerhouses, in the mills during the breaks, on the doorsteps, and in the chapels. They talk about wages, and hours, and the Corn Laws, and the Poor Law, and the workhouse. But most of all, they talk about the vote. Because in 1840, most working men cannot vote. And no women can vote at all.

[pause]

Only men who own or rent property of a certain value can vote in elections for Parliament. In a town like Rochdale, that means only a small part of the men. The rest have no say in the laws that rule their lives. The laws about the hours they work, the food they eat, and the help they get when they are poor. Many working people think this is deeply unfair.

[long-pause]

In 1838, a group of working men in London wrote a document, called the People's Charter. It asked for six things. A vote for every man over twenty-one. Secret voting, so that nobody could be bullied into voting for the wrong person. No need to own property to become a member of Parliament. Payment for members of Parliament, so that working men could afford to be one. Constituencies of equal size. And an election every year.

[pause]

{img:people-s-charter-document-printed-1838-six-points-table-candle}
The people who supported the Charter were called Chartists. The movement spread very quickly, all over the country, and especially in the mill towns of the north. In 1839, they collected more than a million signatures on a great petition, and took it to Parliament, in London. It was rolled up into a huge ball of paper, and carried on a cart. But Parliament refused to even discuss it. Many Chartists were very disappointed, and very angry.

[pause]

Some of them wanted to use force. There were riots, in some places, and some Chartists were sent to prison. But most of them wanted to win by peaceful means. By meetings, and petitions, and newspapers, and education. They believed that if they were patient, and organised, and reasonable, they would win in the end. Tom Hartley is one of these. He is a Chartist, but a peaceful one.

[pause]

{img:working-men-reading-room-1840-newspaper-northern-star-pipes-lamplight}
In the evenings, Tom sometimes goes to the Chartist reading room, above a shop in the town. It is a small room, with a fire, and tables, and newspapers. The men sit and read, or listen to someone reading aloud, for those who cannot read. They read the Northern Star, which is the Chartist newspaper. And they talk, and argue, and plan. Some of them write poems and songs, about the rights of working people.

[pause]

Sometimes, there are big meetings, on the moors above the town. Thousands of people walk up the hill, in the evening, with their families. The speakers stand on a cart, and talk about freedom, and justice, and the rights of man. The crowds listen, and cheer, and sing. Then everyone walks home again, peacefully, in the dark. Sarah worries about these meetings. She is afraid that Tom will get into trouble. But he always comes home safely.

[long-pause]

{img:family-walking-home-from-moor-meeting-dusk-lanterns-town-lights-below}
The Chartists did not win in their own time. In 1842, and again in 1848, they sent more great petitions to Parliament. Each time, Parliament refused them. After 1848, the movement slowly faded away. Many people thought it had failed. But the ideas did not die. Over the following eighty years, one by one, almost all of the six points of the Charter became law.

[pause]

In 1867, many working men in the towns got the vote, for the first time. In 1872, secret voting was introduced. In 1884, more men got the vote, in the countryside too. And in 1918, all men over twenty-one, and many women over thirty, could vote at last. In 1928, women got the vote on the same terms as men. Only the yearly election never happened. Today, it seems hard to believe that it was ever any other way.

[pause]

Tom does not live to see all of this. But he lives long enough to vote, once, in 1868, as an old man. He walks to the polling place in his best coat, slowly, with Ellen beside him. And when he comes out, he is smiling. He says that it took a long time. But it was worth the wait.

[long-pause]

{img:rushbearing-cart-piled-high-rushes-decorated-ribbons-men-pulling-rochdale}
Every year, in August, Rochdale has a holiday, called the Wakes. For a few days, the mills close, and everyone has a rest from work. It is the only real holiday of the year, apart from Christmas Day and Good Friday. And it begins with an old and colourful custom, called the Rushbearing.

[pause]

The Rushbearing is very old. It goes back to the days when the floors of the churches were made of earth, and covered with rushes, to keep them warm and dry. Every year, the people cut fresh rushes from the moors, and carried them to the church, to spread on the floor. Over time, it became a great festival. The church floors now have stone and wood. But the Rushbearing goes on, every August, just for the joy of it.

[long-pause]

{img:morris-dancers-clogs-ribbons-garlands-dancing-street-crowd-mill-town}
In the days before, the young men from each village around the town go up onto the moors, to cut the rushes. They pile them high on a big wooden cart, in the shape of a tall haystack, and tie them down neatly. Then they decorate the cart, with ribbons, and flowers, and garlands, and shining things. Silver cups, and plates, and teapots, borrowed from the houses, hang from the sides, and flash in the sun. On top of the cart, a man sits on the rushes, waving a flag.

[pause]

On the day of the Rushbearing, the carts are pulled through the streets of Rochdale, by teams of young men, in white shirts, with ribbons on their hats. In front of each cart, there is a group of morris dancers, dancing in their clogs, with bells on their legs, and handkerchiefs in their hands. And there is music, from fiddles, and drums, and pipes. The whole town turns out to watch, and cheer.

[pause]

{img:wakes-fair-1840-stalls-swing-boats-gingerbread-crowds-sunny-field}
Behind the carts comes the fair. Stalls selling gingerbread, and brandy snaps, and toffee, and oranges, and nuts. Swing boats, and roundabouts, pushed by hand, and coconut shies. Jugglers, and acrobats, and a man with a dancing bear. Fortune tellers, and peep shows, and a travelling theatre, in a tent. And the noise, and the music, and the laughter, of thousands of people, enjoying themselves.

[pause]

Jack has saved his pennies for weeks, for the fair. He spends them all in the first hour. Gingerbread, and a ride on the swing boats, and a go at the coconut shy, where he wins nothing. Then he wanders around, with his friends, watching everything, with his mouth open. The acrobats, the jugglers, and the strong man, who lifts an anvil above his head. Ellen gives him one of her pennies, so that he can buy a toffee apple.

[long-pause]

The grown-ups enjoy the fair too. Tom and Sarah walk around arm in arm, in their best clothes, greeting their friends. They watch the morris dancers, and listen to the music, and buy a little bag of brandy snaps, to share. Sarah has a go on the swing boats, and screams, and laughs, and holds her bonnet on. Tom says she is like a girl again. And Grandad Joseph sits on a bench in the sun, with his old friends, and watches it all, and remembers the Rushbearings of his youth.

[pause]

{img:old-men-sitting-bench-sunshine-watching-fair-pipes-smiling-memories}
In the evening, there is dancing, in the inns, and in the streets. And there is drinking too, rather too much of it, sometimes. The town is noisy until late at night. The Hartleys go home before it gets too wild. They sit by the open door, in the warm summer evening, and listen to the distant music, and the laughter. Jack falls asleep on the settle, with his face sticky from toffee.

[pause]

For the rest of the Wakes week, the mill stays closed. Some families go to visit relatives in the country. A few lucky ones take the train to Liverpool, to see the great ships and the sea. But most families just rest, and go for walks, and enjoy the summer. The Hartleys plan a long walk, up onto the moors, to Blackstone Edge, near the village where Grandad Joseph was born.

[long-pause]

{img:family-climbing-moor-path-above-rochdale-summer-morning-town-smoke-below}
On a fine morning in the Wakes week, the Hartleys set off early, for their walk to the moors. They take a basket, with bread, and cheese, and cold bacon, and a bottle of cold tea, and some oatcakes. Grandad Joseph walks with a stick, but he is determined to come. He says he wants to see the old places, one more time. Patch, the dog from next door, comes too, because he always follows Jack everywhere.

[pause]

They walk out of the town, along the road beside the Rochdale Canal. The canal was built about forty years ago, to carry goods across the Pennines, from Lancashire to Yorkshire. Long, narrow boats, pulled by horses, move slowly along the still water, loaded with coal, and cotton, and stone. The boatmen wave to them. The horses plod along the towpath, with their heads down.

[long-pause]

{img:rochdale-canal-narrowboat-horse-towpath-lock-summer-hills-ahead}
Then they leave the canal, and start to climb. Up a steep, stony lane, between stone walls, past little farms, and fields of sheep. The town falls away behind them, and below them. When they stop to rest, and look back, they can see it all, spread out in the valley. The chimneys, the mills, the rows of houses, and the smoke, hanging over everything, like a grey cloud. And above them, the sky is blue, and clear.

[pause]

The higher they climb, the cleaner the air becomes. It smells of grass, and heather, and sheep. Skylarks sing high above them, too high to see. The wind blows gently, and the children run ahead, laughing. Sarah takes off her shawl, and lets the wind blow through her hair. Tom takes deep breaths, and says it is like drinking cold water. After a week in the mill, the fresh air feels wonderful.

[pause]

At last, they reach the top, at Blackstone Edge. A long ridge of dark, rough rocks, standing up from the moor, like the back of a sleeping giant. From here, they can see for miles. Back toward Rochdale, and Manchester beyond, in the haze. And the other way, over the hills, into Yorkshire. Green valleys, and brown moors, and little villages, and far away, more mill chimneys, in other towns.

[pause]

{img:blackstone-edge-gritstone-rocks-ridge-wide-view-pennine-moors-summer}
Near the top, there is an old stone road, crossing the moor. It is very old. Some people say it was built by the Romans, almost two thousand years ago. It is made of big flat stones, with a groove down the middle. Grandad Joseph says that when he was a boy, the packhorses still came over this road, carrying wool and cloth, in long lines, with bells on their harness. Now, the canal and the railway carry everything, and the old road is quiet.

[long-pause]

They have their picnic among the rocks, out of the wind. The bread and cheese and bacon taste better than anything they have eaten all year. Jack and Patch explore the rocks, and find a hare, which runs away, very fast, with Patch barking behind it. Ellen sits on a rock, and reads a book of poems, by William Wordsworth, which she has borrowed from the Sunday school. Tom lies on his back in the heather, with his cap over his eyes, and falls asleep.

[pause]

In the afternoon, they walk down the other side of the hill, to the little village where Grandad Joseph was born. The stone cottages are still there, with their long rows of upstairs windows. But the looms are gone, and the cottages are quiet. Joseph stops in front of one of them, and looks at it for a long time. Then he says, that was my house. And he tells them about his mother, and his father, and his brothers and sisters, and the sound of the loom, in the upstairs room.

[pause]

An old woman comes out of the cottage next door, and looks at him. Then she says his name. She was a girl in the village, when he was a boy. They talk for a long time, about the old days, and the people they knew. Most of them have passed away now. When they say goodbye, both of them have tears in their eyes.

[pause]

{img:sunset-over-moors-family-walking-home-down-lane-town-lights-valley}
They walk home in the evening, slowly, as the sun goes down behind the hills. The sky turns gold, and then pink, and then purple. The town below them begins to light up, with gas lamps, and candles in the windows. Jack is so tired that Tom carries him on his back, for the last mile. And Grandad Joseph walks in silence, smiling, thinking of the old days, and the old places, and the people he once knew.

[long-pause]

{img:liverpool-docks-1840-sailing-ships-unloading-cotton-bales-warehouses}
The cotton that the Hartleys spin and weave comes from very far away. Most of it comes from the southern states of America. From Mississippi, Alabama, Georgia, Louisiana, and the Carolinas. There, on great plantations, the cotton grows in the hot sun, in long rows of green bushes, with white, fluffy bolls. And there, in 1840, it is picked by enslaved people.

[pause]

This is a sad part of the story, but an important one. In 1840, about two and a half million people are held in slavery in the United States. Many of them work on the cotton plantations. Men, women, and children. They are not paid. They cannot leave. They can be bought and sold, and their families can be separated. They work from dawn until dark, picking cotton, in the heat. And the cotton they pick is sent across the sea, to Lancashire.

[long-pause]

{img:cotton-plantation-field-1840-rows-white-bolls-distant-workers-southern-sky}
Britain itself had abolished slavery in its own colonies, just a few years before, in 1833. Many people in Britain were proud of this. But the cotton mills of Lancashire still depended on cotton grown by enslaved people, in America. Some people in Britain saw this clearly, and spoke out about it. Others preferred not to think about it. For most mill workers, the cotton just arrived, in bales, at the mill. They did not know where it came from.

[pause]

The cotton travels down the great rivers of the American South, on steamboats, to the port of New Orleans, or other ports. There, it is loaded onto sailing ships, and carried across the Atlantic Ocean. The voyage takes about four or five weeks. The ships arrive at Liverpool, which is the busiest port in the world for cotton. The docks are crowded with ships, from every country, and the quaysides are piled high with bales.

[pause]

In Liverpool, the cotton is bought and sold by merchants, who test its quality, by pulling out a few fibres, and looking at them carefully. The longer and finer the fibres, the better the cotton, and the higher the price. Then it is sent by canal, or by the new railway, to Manchester, and then on to the mill towns. A bale of cotton that left a plantation in Mississippi in the spring might reach the Kelsall mill in Rochdale in the summer.

[pause]

Some years later, in the 1860s, something remarkable happened. In 1861, the American Civil War began, between the northern states and the southern states. The North blockaded the ports of the South, and the cotton stopped coming. In Lancashire, the mills ran out of cotton, and closed. Hundreds of thousands of mill workers lost their work. It was called the Cotton Famine. It was a terrible time of hunger and hardship.

[long-pause]

{img:free-trade-hall-manchester-1862-working-people-meeting-letter-lincoln}
But in 1862, at a great meeting in Manchester, the cotton workers did something very brave. Even though they were hungry, and out of work, they voted to support President Abraham Lincoln, and the North, and the end of slavery. They sent him a letter. And Lincoln wrote back, thanking them. He called their support an act of sublime Christian heroism. Today, there is a statue of Lincoln in Manchester, to remember it.

[pause]

Of course, in 1840, all of that is still far in the future. For now, the cotton keeps coming, in great bales, to Liverpool, and to Rochdale. And the Hartleys spin it and weave it, in the mill, without thinking much about where it comes from. But Ellen has read about slavery, in a book from the Sunday school library. And one evening, she asks her father about it.

[pause]

{img:father-and-daughter-talking-by-fire-serious-gentle-evening-kitchen}
Tom is quiet for a long time. Then he says that it is a wicked thing, to own another human being. He says that he has heard men speak about it, at the chapel, and at the meetings. And that one day, he hopes, it will end everywhere in the world. Ellen nods. She thinks about the people, far away, who picked the cotton that she weaves. And she says a little prayer for them, that night, before she goes to sleep.

[long-pause]

{img:mill-town-autumn-evening-1840-gas-lamps-fog-workers-going-home}
After the Wakes, the mill opens again, and the long weeks of work begin once more. The summer slowly turns to autumn. The days grow shorter, and the mornings darker. When the family walks to the mill, before six o'clock, it is still night. And when they walk home, at the end of the day, it is already getting dark. The gas lamps are lit in the streets, glowing yellow in the mist.

[pause]

In the autumn, the weather in Lancashire is often damp and grey. A fine, steady rain falls, day after day. People say that the damp air is the reason the cotton industry came to Lancashire. The cotton thread breaks less easily in damp air. So the rain, which everyone grumbles about, is good for the mills. Tom says that Lancashire was made for cotton, and cotton was made for Lancashire.

[long-pause]

On still autumn days, the smoke from the chimneys does not rise. It hangs low over the town, mixing with the mist, into a thick, yellow fog. You can hardly see across the street. The gas lamps are like little moons, in the gloom. People walk carefully, with their shawls over their mouths. The fog smells of coal, and it makes everyone cough. Grandad Joseph stays inside, by the fire, on those days.

[pause]

In the evenings, after work and after tea, the family gathers around the fire. Sarah sews, and mends the clothes, and knits socks. Ellen reads, or writes letters to Aunt Anne, or prepares her lessons for the Sunday school. Jack does his homework from the mill school, with Ellen's help, or plays with a wooden top that his grandad carved for him. Tom reads the newspaper, slowly, and sometimes reads bits aloud.

[pause]

{img:family-evening-by-range-fire-mother-sewing-boy-top-grandfather-pipe}
Some evenings, Tom goes to the Mechanics' Institute, in the town. It is a place where working men can go, to learn. There are lectures, about science, and history, and geography. There is a library, with hundreds of books. And there are classes, in reading, writing, arithmetic, and drawing. Tom goes to a lecture about the stars, and comes home full of wonder. He tells the children that the stars are suns, like ours, very far away.

[pause]

The autumn brings its own small pleasures. Chestnuts, roasted on the fire, and shared between everyone. Apples from the market, cheap and crisp. And on the fifth of November, Bonfire Night. The children of Spring Street collect old wood, and boxes, and rubbish, for weeks, and build a big bonfire on a piece of waste ground, at the end of the street. They make a guy, a figure of old clothes stuffed with straw, and put it on top.

[long-pause]

{img:bonfire-night-1840-children-waste-ground-fire-sparks-terraced-houses}
On Bonfire Night, the whole street comes out to watch the bonfire burn. The flames leap up, high into the dark sky, and the sparks fly. The children dance around it, and shout, and sing. The grown-ups stand back, with their hands held out to the warmth. Someone brings potatoes, to bake in the ashes. And Mr Ashworth, next door, lets off a few small fireworks, which fizz and bang, and make the children squeal.

[pause]

Then, slowly, the bonfire burns down, to a glowing heap of embers. The families go home, smelling of smoke, with warm faces, and cold backs. Jack has burnt his fingers on a hot potato, but he does not care. He says it was the best bonfire ever. He says that every year. And Sarah puts him to bed, and tucks him in, and he falls asleep in a moment.

[pause]

{img:quiet-terraced-street-late-night-embers-glow-one-lamp-mist-rising}
Outside, the fog is coming down again, soft and grey, over the town. The embers of the bonfire glow faintly, at the end of the street. The mill chimneys are dark shapes, against the sky. And the town is quiet, at last, except for the distant sound of the night watchman, calling out the hour, as he walks through the streets.

[long-pause]

{img:snow-on-terraced-rooftops-mill-chimneys-winter-morning-1840-rochdale}
Winter comes to Rochdale in December. The rain turns to sleet, and then to snow. The moors above the town turn white. The streets are slippery with ice, and the clogs slip and clatter on the frozen cobbles. The children slide along the icy pavements, and throw snowballs, and build snowmen, in the street. The grown-ups grumble about the cold. But the children love it.

[pause]

In the winter, the mill is a warm place. Warmer than the houses, and much warmer than the streets. The workers are almost glad to go to work, in the cold mornings. But the walk there and back is hard, in the dark, in the snow and ice. Sarah wraps the children up in every piece of warm clothing they have. Shawls, scarves, and mittens, knitted by Grandad Joseph, who has learned to knit, to keep his hands busy.

[long-pause]

Coal is the most important thing in winter. Without coal, there is no fire, no cooking, no hot water, and no warmth. The coalman comes along the street with his horse and cart, and his sacks of coal, shouting out his price. Coal is cheap in Lancashire, because it is mined nearby. But in the winter, the families need a lot of it. Sarah buys a sack a week, and it is never quite enough.

[pause]

Winter is also a time of illness. Coughs, colds, and fevers spread through the crowded streets. The damp, the cold, the smoke, and the fly from the mill make many people ill. Some people have weak chests, from years of breathing in cotton dust. There are doctors in the town, but they cost money. So most people make their own remedies, or ask Mrs Kershaw, next door, for help.

[pause]

{img:neighbour-woman-making-herbal-remedy-kitchen-steam-bottles-shawl}
In January, Jack catches a bad cold, which turns into a fever. For three days, he lies in bed, hot and restless, and cannot eat. Sarah is very worried. She stays at home from the mill, which means losing her wages. Mrs Kershaw comes, and makes him a hot drink of honey, lemon, and herbs. She puts a warm poultice on his chest. And she sits with him, while Sarah sleeps for a few hours.

[pause]

On the fourth day, the fever breaks. Jack wakes up, and says he is hungry. Sarah cries with relief, and makes him some broth. Within a week, he is back to normal, running in the street, and throwing snowballs, as if nothing had happened. But Sarah does not forget those three days. Many families in the street have lost children, to illness. She knows how lucky they are.

[long-pause]

{img:christmas-1840-working-family-small-table-goose-candles-holly-simple-joy}
Christmas is a quiet affair in the mill towns, in 1840. The mills close for just one day, on Christmas Day. There are no Christmas cards yet, and few Christmas trees. But the families go to chapel in the morning, and sing carols. And they have the best dinner of the year. The Hartleys have a goose, which Sarah has been saving for since the autumn, with potatoes, and a plum pudding.

[pause]

After dinner, they sit around the fire, and sing carols, and old songs. Grandad Joseph sings a song from the old days, about a weaver who fell in love with a farmer's daughter. Tom sings a Chartist song, very quietly, so the neighbours do not hear. Ellen and Jack sing a hymn they learned at Sunday school. And Sarah gives everyone a little present. A new pair of mittens for Jack, a ribbon for Ellen, tobacco for Tom and Grandad.

[pause]

{img:candlelit-window-terraced-house-snow-falling-christmas-night-street}
Outside, the snow is falling softly, on the quiet streets. The mill is silent, and the chimneys are cold. For one day, the town is peaceful. The windows glow with candlelight, all along the street. And from some of the houses, there is singing, drifting out into the cold night air. And then, one by one, the candles go out, and the town sleeps.

[long-pause]

{img:toad-lane-rochdale-1844-small-cooperative-shop-pioneers-opening-evening}
Four years after our story, in 1844, something happened in Rochdale that would become famous all over the world. A group of twenty-eight working men, most of them weavers and mill workers, opened a small shop in Toad Lane, a narrow street in the centre of the town. They called themselves the Rochdale Society of Equitable Pioneers. And their little shop was the beginning of the modern cooperative movement.

[pause]

The idea was simple. The members each paid a small amount of money, to buy the first stock. The shop sold good, honest food, with fair weights and fair prices. No cheating, no watered milk, no sand in the sugar. And at the end of each year, the profits were shared among the members, according to how much they had bought. Anyone could join. Men and women alike. And every member had one vote, in how the shop was run.

[long-pause]

At first, the shop was tiny. It sold only butter, sugar, flour, oatmeal, and candles. It was open only two evenings a week. People laughed at the pioneers, and said it would never last. But it did. It grew, and grew. Soon, it had hundreds of members, and then thousands. It opened more shops, and a library, and a newsroom, and classes for the members. The idea spread all over Britain, and then all over the world.

[pause]

{img:later-cooperative-store-victorian-large-shop-window-co-op-sign-crowd}
Today, there are cooperatives in almost every country in the world, with hundreds of millions of members. Shops, banks, farms, and housing. And they all follow the principles written down by those twenty-eight pioneers in Rochdale, in 1844. The little shop in Toad Lane is still there today, as a museum. In our story, perhaps the Hartleys would have joined. Tom would have liked the idea very much.

[pause]

The mill towns changed slowly, over the years that followed. In 1847, Parliament passed the Ten Hours Act, which limited the working day for women and young people in the mills to ten hours. It was a great victory for the reformers, after many years of campaigning. Later, the half-day on Saturday was introduced, and the Wakes holidays grew longer. And in the 1850s and 1860s, the new railways began to take the mill workers to the seaside, to Blackpool, for their holidays.

[pause]

{img:victorian-blackpool-beach-mill-workers-holiday-crowds-sea-promenade-1880s}
The towns themselves slowly became healthier. New laws were passed, about drains, and water, and housing. Clean water was piped into the houses. Proper sewers were built. Parks and public baths and libraries were opened. The worst slums were pulled down. People began to live longer, and fewer children died young. It took a long time. But by the end of the century, the mill towns were much better places to live.

[long-pause]

The cotton industry in Lancashire grew bigger and bigger, until about 1913, when it was at its greatest. Then, after the First World War, it slowly began to decline. Other countries, like India and Japan, built their own mills, and made their own cloth, more cheaply. One by one, the Lancashire mills closed. By the 1960s and 1970s, most of them had gone. The tall chimneys were pulled down, and the great mills were demolished, or turned into warehouses, or flats.

[pause]

{img:later-empty-old-cotton-mill-weeds-broken-windows-chimney-quiet-afternoon}
And what about the Hartleys? Ellen did become a teacher, as she had dreamed. She went to a training college, in her twenties, with the help of the minister at the chapel. And she taught in a school in Rochdale for forty years, and taught hundreds of mill children to read. Jack became a spinner, like his father, and later an overlooker. He married a weaver called Martha, and they had six children.

[pause]

Grandad Joseph passed away peacefully in 1846, at the age of seventy-one, in his chair by the fire. Tom and Sarah worked in the mill until they were old, and then lived with Ellen, in a little house on the edge of the town, with a garden, and a view of the moors. On fine evenings, they sat outside, and looked at the hills. And Tom said that he had seen more change in his life than his grandfather could ever have imagined.

[long-pause]

{img:today-rochdale-town-hall-gothic-victorian-sunny-day-people}
Today, Rochdale is a busy town, of more than a hundred thousand people. Most of the old mills have gone. The great chimneys that once filled the sky have almost all been pulled down. But if you look carefully, you can still see the traces of the cotton age, everywhere. In the rows of brick terraced houses, in the canal, and in the grand old buildings in the centre of the town.

[pause]

The most beautiful of these is the Town Hall, built in the 1860s and 1870s, when the town was rich from cotton. It is a magnificent building, in the Gothic style, with a tall clock tower, and beautiful stained glass windows, and a great hall with a painted ceiling. It shows how proud the people of the mill towns were of their towns, and their work, and their success.

[long-pause]

{img:today-toad-lane-rochdale-pioneers-museum-small-shop-front-visitors}
In Toad Lane, the little shop of the Rochdale Pioneers is still there, as a museum. Visitors from all over the world come to see it. Many of them belong to cooperatives in their own countries. They walk through the little shop, with its old wooden counter, and its scales, and its barrels of flour and oatmeal. And they read about the twenty-eight pioneers, and how their small idea changed the world.

[pause]

Across Lancashire, some of the old mills have been saved, and turned into museums. At Queen Street Mill, near Burnley, there is a weaving shed, with hundreds of old looms, which still work. When the steam engine is running, and the looms start up, the noise is overwhelming, just as it was in the old days. Visitors can see the weavers at work, and try to mee-maw, like the mill girls once did.

[pause]

At Quarry Bank Mill, at Styal, near Manchester, there is a great mill, from the early days of the Industrial Revolution, beside a river, with a huge water wheel. There are the houses of the workers, and the house where the child apprentices lived, and the mill owner's garden. And in Manchester itself, the Science and Industry Museum has spinning mules, and steam engines, and the old railway station from 1830, the oldest in the world.

[pause]

The Rochdale Canal is still there too. It was closed for many years, and became choked with weeds and rubbish. But it has been restored, and reopened, and boats can travel along it again, across the Pennines, from Manchester to Yorkshire. People walk and cycle along the towpath, where the horses once pulled the boats. And the water is clean again, with ducks, and swans, and fish.

[long-pause]

{img:today-rochdale-canal-towpath-walkers-narrowboat-hills-autumn-sun}
Up on the moors, at Blackstone Edge, the rocks are still there, as they always have been. And the old stone road still crosses the moor. Walkers come up from the town, as the Hartleys did, on summer days. They look out over the valley, at the town below. There is much less smoke now, and the air is clean. But it is the same view, of hills, and valleys, and towns, and the wide sky.

[pause]

The Rushbearing faded away, in the late nineteenth century. But in recent years, the people of the area have brought it back, in some places, with carts, and rushes, and morris dancers, and music. Just as in the old days. And in many Lancashire towns, the old brass bands still play, and the Whit Walks still march through the streets, behind their banners.

[pause]

{img:today-brass-band-playing-park-bandstand-lancashire-town-families-listening}
The cotton mills of Lancashire changed the world. They were the beginning of the modern industrial age, of factories, and machines, and cities, and mass production. They brought great wealth, and great hardship. But they also brought new ideas, about fairness, and the rights of working people, and how people can work together. And those ideas came from ordinary people, like the Hartleys, in their little houses, in the shadow of the mill.

[long-pause]

{img:rochdale-1840-dusk-mill-lights-going-out-chimneys-pink-sky-valley}
So we leave Rochdale tonight, in the year 1840, as the evening falls. The mill bell has rung for the last time today. The great steam engine has slowed, and stopped. The thousands of machines are still. And the workers pour out of the mill gates, into the streets, tired, but glad that the day is over. The clatter of their clogs echoes between the houses, as they walk home.

[pause]

The sky above the town is turning pink and gold, behind the chimneys. The smoke is thinning, now that the boilers are cooling. The first stars are coming out, over the moors. And one by one, the gas lamps are being lit, along the streets, by the lamplighter, with his long pole, going from lamp to lamp.

[long-pause]

{img:spring-street-evening-windows-glowing-family-entering-house-clogs}
In Spring Street, the Hartleys come home. Grandad Joseph has the fire burning, and the kettle boiling, and a pot of stew bubbling on the range. The family takes off their clogs and shawls, and washes their hands and faces in a bowl of warm water. And they sit down together, at the scrubbed table, for their tea. Bread, and stew, and hot tea with sugar. It tastes wonderful, after the long day.

[pause]

After tea, they sit by the fire. Tom smokes his pipe, and reads the newspaper. Sarah mends a shirt, by the light of a candle. Ellen reads, quietly, a book of poems. Jack sits on the rag rug, by Grandad Joseph's feet, and listens to one of his stories, about the old days, and the moors, and the weavers, and the boggarts. The fire crackles, and the kettle sings, and the clock on the mantelpiece, which does not work, says ten past three, as always.

[long-pause]

{img:family-by-firelight-terraced-house-kitchen-grandfather-story-boy-listening}
Then it is time for bed. The children go up first, with a candle, up the narrow stairs. Ellen helps Jack into bed, and tucks him in, and blows out the candle. Then Tom and Sarah go up too, and Grandad Joseph settles down in his bed, in the back room, by the warm wall of the range. And the house is quiet.

[pause]

Outside, the town is quiet too. The streets are empty. The gas lamps glow in the mist. The mill stands dark and silent, by the river, waiting for the morning. The river runs quietly past it, under the stars. Up on the moors, the wind blows softly over the heather. And far away, an owl calls, from the old woods above the town.
