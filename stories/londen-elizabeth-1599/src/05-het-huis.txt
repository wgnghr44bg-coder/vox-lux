The house of the Wood family is typical of London in 1599. It is tall and narrow, squeezed in between its neighbours. It is made of a wooden frame, filled in with plaster, and painted. It has four floors, and each floor sticks out a little further over the street than the one below. This is called jettying. It gives more room upstairs. But it also means that, in narrow streets, the houses on both sides almost touch at the top, and the street below is dark.

--

On the ground floor, at the front, there is the shop. It has a wide window, with wooden shutters. In the daytime, the lower shutter is folded down to make a counter, on which the gloves are displayed. Customers stand in the street and look at the goods, and talk to the shopkeeper. At night, the shutters are closed and locked. Behind the shop, there is the workshop, where Thomas and Kit cut and sew the leather.

On the first floor, there is the hall, the main room of the family. It is the best room in the house. It has a big fireplace, a table with benches and stools, and a carved wooden chest. On the walls, there are painted cloths, showing scenes from the Bible, and a little mirror. The windows have small panes of glass, set in lead. Glass windows are still quite new for ordinary people. The family is proud of them.

{img:tudor-hall-room-fireplace-painted-cloths-table-benches-leaded-windows}
Above the hall, there is the bedroom of Thomas and Margaret. It has a big bed with four posts and curtains, which keep out the draughts at night. Their best possessions are kept here. A chest of linen. Margaret's few pieces of jewellery. A little silver cup, which was a wedding present. And the family Bible. Above that, under the roof, there are small rooms for the children, Kit and Joan.

At the back of the house, there is a tiny yard. In it, there is a little kitchen, separate from the house, because of the danger of fire. There is a privy, a little shed with a wooden seat over a pit. There is a pump, which the family shares with the neighbours. And there are a few pots of herbs, growing in the sun. Rosemary, thyme, sage, mint and lavender. Margaret uses them for cooking, medicine and to make the house smell sweet.

--

{img:kitchen-tudor-fire-spit-pots-maid-cooking-herbs-hanging-beams}
The floors of the house are made of wooden boards upstairs, and stone or beaten earth downstairs. They are covered with rushes, long dry stems of reeds, mixed with sweet-smelling herbs. The rushes keep the floor warm and soft. Every few weeks, Joan sweeps out the old rushes and puts down fresh ones. Some richer people have rush mats, woven together. And the very rich have carpets, from Turkey. But carpets are too precious to walk on. They are put on tables.

At night, the house is lit by candles. Cheap candles made of tallow, which is animal fat, for everyday. They smoke and smell a little. Expensive candles made of beeswax, for special occasions. The family goes to bed early, because candles cost money. In winter, when the nights are long, they sit close to the fire, and use the light of the flames to sew and talk.

{img:bedroom-four-poster-bed-curtains-candle-night-tudor-chest-quilt}
Fire is the great danger in London. All the houses are made of wood, with straw or tile roofs, close together. A single spark can set a whole street on fire. So there are strict rules. Every householder must keep a bucket of water by the door, and a ladder. Fires must be covered at night. And a bell, called the curfew bell, rings in the evening, to remind everyone to put out their fires. Margaret checks the fire every night, before she goes to bed, twice.

--

Bess loves the house. She knows every creaking board, every crack in the plaster, every corner where the cat likes to sleep. Her room is small, under the roof, with a tiny window. From it, she can see over the rooftops of Cheapside, all the way to the tall tower of St Paul's. On clear nights, she can see the stars above the chimneys. And she can hear the bells of a dozen churches, ringing the hours through the night.
