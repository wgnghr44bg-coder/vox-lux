In the middle of the City, not far from Cheapside, there is a grand building, built about thirty years before. It is called the Royal Exchange. It was built by a rich merchant called Thomas Gresham, as a meeting place for merchants. And the queen herself opened it, and gave it its royal name. It is one of the most important places in London.

--

The Royal Exchange is built around a big square courtyard, surrounded by covered walkways with stone columns. On top of the building, there is a tall bell tower, with a golden grasshopper on top, which was the symbol of the Gresham family. Twice a day, at noon and in the evening, the merchants gather in the courtyard, to do business. They buy and sell goods, ships, shares in voyages, and money from other countries.

The merchants come from everywhere. English merchants, of course. But also Dutch, Flemish, French, Italian, Spanish, German and Portuguese merchants. Each group stands in its own part of the courtyard. They speak many languages. They talk about prices, ships, cargoes and the news from abroad. Which ships have arrived from the Baltic. What the price of pepper is in Antwerp. Whether there is war or peace in the Netherlands.

{img:upper-gallery-shops-royal-exchange-ladies-buying-lace-gloves-perfume}
Upstairs, around the courtyard, there is a long gallery of small shops. More than a hundred of them. They sell all kinds of luxury goods. Fine lace, silk ribbons, gloves, perfumes, jewellery, hats, mirrors and fans. It is the most fashionable place to shop in London. Elegant ladies and gentlemen walk up and down the gallery, looking at the goods, and at each other. It is a little like a shopping centre of today.

Thomas Wood dreams of having a shop in the Royal Exchange one day. It would be a great step up, from Cheapside. But the rents are very high. So for now, he sells some of his finest gloves to a shopkeeper there, a friend of his, who sells them on to the rich ladies. Margaret's embroidered gloves sell especially well. They are among the finest in the gallery.

--

{img:merchant-ship-departing-thames-voyage-east-indies-sails-crowd-watching}
In 1599, the merchants of London are talking about something very exciting. The Dutch have recently sent ships all the way to the East Indies, to the islands where spices grow. Pepper, cloves, nutmeg and cinnamon. And their ships have come back loaded with spices, and made their owners very rich. Now the English merchants want to do the same. In September 1599, a group of them meet, and decide to form a company to trade with the East Indies.

The next year, the queen will give them a charter, a special permission, to trade with all the lands east of the Cape of Good Hope. It will be called the East India Company. Nobody in 1599 can imagine how big and powerful it will one day become. For now, it is just a group of merchants, in a room near the Exchange, dreaming of spices and distant islands.

{img:old-merchant-with-globe-maps-telling-stories-young-girl-listening}
Thomas's friend at the Exchange is an old Dutch merchant, called Master Pieter. He has lived in London for thirty years. He came from Antwerp, during the wars in the Netherlands, like many others. He has a shop full of maps, globes and instruments for sailors. When Bess comes with her father to deliver the gloves, Master Pieter shows her his globe. He spins it, and shows her England, small and green, and then the whole wide world around it.

--

He shows her the Americas, where the Spanish have found gold and silver, and the English are trying to start colonies. He shows her Africa, and the long way around it to India. He shows her China and Japan, and the islands of spices. He tells her that Sir Francis Drake sailed all the way around this globe, twenty years before. Bess spins the globe slowly with her finger, and feels very small, and very excited, all at once. The world is so big. And London, it seems, is right at the centre of it.
