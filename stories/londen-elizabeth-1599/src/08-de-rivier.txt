The river Thames is the heart of London. It is wide, deep and busy, full of boats of every kind. And for most Londoners, it is the main road. Because the streets are narrow, crowded and muddy, it is often much quicker and more pleasant to travel by water. And there are thousands of boatmen, waiting to row you wherever you want to go.

--

The boatmen are called watermen. There are about three thousand of them in London. They row small wooden boats, called wherries, which can carry a few passengers. They wait at the river stairs, the stone steps that lead down to the water from the streets. When someone comes down the stairs, the watermen shout, all at once, eastward ho! westward ho! Oars! Oars! Each one hoping to get the passenger for his boat.

The watermen are a lively, cheeky group of men. They know every bend and every current of the river. They argue about prices, and tell jokes, and sing as they row. Some of them are good company. Others are rude. Passengers sit on cushions at the back of the boat, and enjoy the view. A trip from the City to Westminster takes about half an hour, if the tide is right.

{img:swans-on-thames-river-palaces-strand-gardens-summer-afternoon}
The Thames is also very beautiful. Along its banks, to the west of the City, there are the palaces of the great nobles, with their gardens and orchards running down to the water. Each palace has its own water gate, with steps, and its own boats. On the river, there are hundreds of white swans, gliding gracefully among the boats. Foreign visitors always wrote about them. The swans belong to the queen, and it is forbidden to harm them.

The queen herself often travels by river. She has a beautiful royal barge, painted and gilded, with a cabin covered in rich cloth, and a team of oarsmen in her colours. When she travels from one palace to another, from Greenwich to Whitehall, or from Whitehall to Richmond, she goes by barge. And when the royal barge passes, all the boats on the river stop, and the people on the banks take off their hats and cheer.

--

{img:royal-barge-gilded-queen-elizabeth-oarsmen-river-crowds-banks-cheer}
One day, in the summer, Thomas takes his family on a trip on the river. It is a holiday, and the shop is closed. They walk down to the river stairs, and Thomas hires a waterman. They climb into the boat, and the waterman rows them upstream, past the palaces of the Strand, past the gardens and the orchards, toward Westminster. The water sparkles in the sun. The swans swim alongside. Bess trails her fingers in the cool water.

And then, the waterman points ahead, and says, look, the queen's barge. Coming down the river toward them is a magnificent golden boat, with flags flying, and oarsmen in red and gold, rowing in perfect time. Music is playing on board. And in the cabin, under a canopy, there is a figure in a white dress, glittering with jewels. The waterman stops rowing, and takes off his cap. Everyone in the boat stands up, as well as they can, and bows.

{img:family-in-small-boat-bowing-as-royal-barge-passes-sparkling-water}
The royal barge passes quite close to them. For a moment, Bess sees the queen's face. Old and white, with a red wig and a great ruff of lace. And the queen turns her head, and looks at the little boat, with the glover's family standing in it, bowing. And she raises her hand, just a little, in greeting. Then the barge is past, and gone down the river.

--

Bess cannot speak for the rest of the trip. She has seen the queen. Up close. And the queen raised her hand to them. Her mother squeezes her arm and says, I told you that you might, one day. That evening, Bess tells the story to Joan, and to Kit, and to the neighbours, and to the cat. And every time she tells it, the queen comes a little closer, and smiles a little more.
