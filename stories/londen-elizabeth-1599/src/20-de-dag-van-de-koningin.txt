The year goes on. The summer ends, and the autumn comes. The leaves fall in the fields outside the city. The days get shorter. And on the seventeenth of November 1599, there is a great celebration. It is Accession Day, the day on which Queen Elizabeth became queen, forty-one years before. All over England, the bells ring, and people celebrate.

--

In London, the biggest event of the day is the tournament, called the Accession Day Tilt. It is held at the palace of Whitehall, in Westminster, in a long open yard called the tiltyard. The queen watches from a window of the palace, with her ladies. And thousands of people come to watch, paying a few pennies to stand or sit in the stands around the yard.

The Wood family goes too. It is a holiday, and the shops are closed. They walk all the way to Westminster, along the Strand, in the crowd. Everyone is in their best clothes. There are flags and banners everywhere. Music is playing. And when they reach the tiltyard, they find a place on the wooden stand, high enough to see everything. Bess is shivering, from the cold and from excitement.

{img:knight-in-fantastical-costume-pageant-servants-dressed-as-characters}
Before the tournament begins, each knight arrives in a splendid procession, called a pageant. Each one has a theme, like a little play. One knight comes as a wild man of the woods, with his servants dressed as trees. Another comes as a knight from an old story, with a poet who reads a speech to the queen, explaining the meaning of his costume. Another arrives in a carriage shaped like a ship. The costumes are magnificent, covered in gold, silver, feathers and jewels.

Each knight has a painted shield, with a picture and a motto, a short saying, which is a riddle about himself. After the tournament, these shields are hung up in a gallery of the palace, for everyone to see. People spend hours trying to understand the meanings of the pictures and mottos. Some years later, Shakespeare himself was paid to design a motto for a knight's shield, for one of these tournaments.

--

{img:two-knights-riding-at-each-other-tilt-barrier-lances-crowd-cheering}
Then the tilting begins. Two knights ride toward each other, on either side of a long wooden barrier, holding long lances. They try to break their lances against each other's armour. It is not a real fight. The lances are made to break easily. And the knights are protected by heavy armour. But it is still very exciting. The horses thunder along the barrier. The lances crack and splinter. And the crowd roars.

Bess does not watch the knights very much. She watches the window of the palace. And there, behind the glass, she sees a figure in white and silver, glittering with jewels. The queen. She is watching the tournament, and sometimes she claps, or waves her handkerchief. Bess cannot see her face, from so far away. But she knows that it is her. The same queen who raised her hand to them from her barge, on the river, in the summer.

{img:winter-evening-bonfires-london-streets-bells-celebration-accession-day}
In the evening, there are bonfires in the streets of London. People dance around them, and drink to the health of the queen. The bells ring until late. And the Wood family walks home through the streets, past the bonfires, tired and happy. Ned is talking about the knights. Bess is talking about the queen. Margaret is complaining about her cold feet. And Thomas is carrying a sleepy Ned on his back, for the last part of the way.

--

Nobody knows it yet. But this will be one of the last Accession Day Tilts of Elizabeth's reign. She will see only three more. In March 1603, she will pass away, peacefully, at her palace of Richmond, at the age of sixty-nine. And with her, the age that bears her name, the Elizabethan age, will come to an end. But tonight, in November 1599, the bells are ringing for her all over London, and the bonfires are burning bright.
