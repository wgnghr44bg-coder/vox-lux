On a sunny afternoon in September 1599, the whole Wood family goes to the Globe. Thomas has closed the shop early. It is a great treat. Even Margaret, who usually says that theatres are noisy, crowded places, has agreed to come. They walk across London Bridge, with crowds of other people, all going the same way. Ned runs ahead, and then runs back, and then runs ahead again.

--

At the door of the Globe, there is a man with a box, collecting the money. The money is dropped into the box, through a slot. Some people say that is where the words box office come from. Thomas pays for the whole family to sit in the lower gallery, on a bench, two pennies each. It is a big expense. But Ned has been begging for weeks. And Thomas thinks that it is worth it, to see the boy's face.

The theatre is filling up. The yard is crowded with groundlings, standing around the stage. Apprentices, servants, sailors, soldiers and porters. Men and women, young and old. They are noisy and cheerful, eating nuts and apples, drinking beer, and calling out to their friends. Sellers walk through the crowd, selling food and drinks. In the galleries, people are finding their seats. Rich gentlemen in silk. Merchants with their wives. Young law students from the Inns of Court.

{img:actors-in-roman-togas-stage-globe-julius-caesar-crowd-listening-sunlight}
Then a trumpet sounds, three times. The noise slowly dies down. And actors come out onto the stage. The play is new. It is called Julius Caesar. It is by William Shakespeare. It tells the story of the great Roman general, Caesar, who wanted to become king of Rome, and of the men who plotted against him. It is a story that everyone in the audience knows a little, from school.

The actors wear rich costumes. Not Roman togas, exactly, but a mixture of old and new clothes, with cloaks and swords. There is no scenery, only the stage and the painted pillars. The actors must make the audience imagine everything with their words. A street in Rome. A storm at night. A great crowd in the forum. And they do it so well that the audience forgets that they are in Southwark, and believes that they are in Rome.

--

{img:actor-delivering-speech-friends-romans-countrymen-crowd-spellbound}
All the women's parts are played by boys. In 1599, women are not allowed to act on the stage. So boys with high, clear voices play the queens, the ladies and the wives. They wear gowns and wigs, and speak in soft voices. Some of them are very good. Ned watches the boy who plays Caesar's wife, Calpurnia, very closely. He thinks that he could do that too.

There is one moment that everyone remembers. The great speech of Mark Antony, Caesar's friend, to the people of Rome. Friends, Romans, countrymen, lend me your ears, he begins. The whole theatre goes silent. Even the noisiest groundlings stop talking. The actor speaks so beautifully that some people in the audience have tears in their eyes. Margaret, who did not want to come, is holding her handkerchief tightly.

{img:jig-dance-end-of-play-actors-dancing-stage-audience-clapping-laughing}
We will not follow the sad parts of the story. They are told with great feeling, but we will let them pass. At the end of the play, something surprising happens. The actors come back onto the stage, and dance. A lively, funny dance, called a jig, with music and jokes and singing. It is the custom at the end of every play, even a sad one. The audience claps and laughs and cheers. And then it is over, and everyone pours out of the theatre, talking about what they have seen.

--

A traveller from Switzerland, called Thomas Platter, was in London in September 1599. He wrote in his diary that on the twenty-first of September, after lunch, he crossed the river and saw a tragedy about the first emperor Julius Caesar, in a house with a thatched roof, very well acted, with about fifteen characters. And that at the end, two of the actors in men's clothes and two in women's clothes danced together very gracefully. Perhaps, on that same afternoon, the Wood family was sitting in the gallery, just a few seats away from him.
