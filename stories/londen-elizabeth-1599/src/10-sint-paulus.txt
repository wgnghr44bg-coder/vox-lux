On the highest hill of the City, there stands the great old cathedral of St Paul's. It is huge, one of the biggest churches in Europe, built of grey stone, in the Gothic style, more than three hundred years before. It once had a tall spire, one of the highest in the world. But about forty years ago, the spire was struck by lightning and burned down. It was never rebuilt. So now the tower stands without it, square and grey.

--

St Paul's is not only a church. It is also the busiest meeting place in London. Every day, the long middle aisle of the cathedral, called the nave, is full of people. Not praying, but walking, talking, doing business and gossiping. This is called Paul's Walk. Merchants make deals. Lawyers meet their clients. Servants look for work. Young gentlemen show off their new clothes. And everybody exchanges news.

If you want to know what is happening in London, you go to Paul's Walk. Who is in favour with the queen. Who has been arrested. Which ships have arrived. What is happening in the war in Ireland. What the latest fashion is. People say that if you walk up and down the nave for an hour, you will hear all the news of England. The noise of so many voices fills the great building like the buzzing of bees.

{img:pauls-walk-nave-crowded-merchants-gentlemen-gossip-light-through-windows}
Some people think that this is not a proper way to behave in a church. And preachers often complain about it. But nobody can stop it. People even carry baskets and beer through the church, using it as a shortcut. Children play in the aisles. And sometimes, someone leads a horse right through the nave. In the side chapels, the priests say their prayers, while all around them, London talks.

Around the cathedral, there is a big churchyard. And in the churchyard, there are the shops of the booksellers. Dozens of little shops and stalls, built against the walls of the cathedral, selling books, pamphlets and ballads. This is the centre of the book trade in England. Each shop has its own sign. The Green Dragon. The Rose. The Angel. The Bible. Customers browse the books on the stalls, and buy the latest news, poems and plays.

--

{img:booksellers-shops-pauls-churchyard-books-pamphlets-ballads-customers}
Printed books are becoming more and more common. There are Bibles and prayer books, of course. But also books of history, poetry, travel, medicine, cooking and gardening. There are little books of jokes and riddles. There are songs, printed on single sheets, called ballads, which people buy for a penny and sing in the streets. And there are the printed texts of plays, so that people can read the plays they have seen in the theatre.

Ned loves the booksellers. Whenever he has a penny, he goes to Paul's Churchyard, and looks at the plays. Most of them are too expensive for him. But sometimes, a kind bookseller lets him read for a while, standing at the stall. And once, with a whole sixpence that he saved for months, he buys a little printed play. It is called Romeo and Juliet. Ned knows that it was written by a player called William Shakespeare. He reads it so many times that the pages fall out.

{img:boy-reading-small-printed-playbook-stall-churchyard-afternoon-light}
Next to the cathedral, in the churchyard, there is an outdoor pulpit, called Paul's Cross. Every Sunday, a famous preacher gives a sermon there, in the open air, to a huge crowd. Thousands of people stand and listen. Important announcements are made there too. News of victories, royal proclamations, and warnings. It is like the voice of London, speaking to its people.

--

Bess prefers the cathedral when it is quiet, early in the morning, before the crowds come. Sometimes she goes there with her mother, to say a prayer. Then the great nave is almost empty, and the light comes down through the tall windows, in long beams, full of dust. The sound of their footsteps echoes softly. And it is easy to imagine how the cathedral must have felt, long ago, when it was new, and the spire still rose to the sky.
