Today, London is one of the biggest cities in the world, with almost nine million people. Most of Elizabethan London is gone, burned in the Great Fire, or pulled down and rebuilt over the centuries. But if you know where to look, you can still find traces of the city of 1599. And in one place, you can even step back into it.

--

On Bankside, by the Thames, very close to where the original stood, there is a new Globe Theatre. It was built in the 1990s, as an exact copy of the old one, as far as historians could work it out. It was the dream of an American actor and director, called Sam Wanamaker, who spent more than twenty years raising money for it. Sadly, he passed away a few years before it opened, in 1997.

The new Globe is built of oak, with wooden pegs, not nails, just like the original. It has white plaster walls and a thatched roof, the first thatched roof allowed in London since the Great Fire. It has three galleries, an open yard, and a stage with two painted pillars and a painted heaven. And every summer, Shakespeare's plays are performed there, in the afternoon and the evening, in the open air, just as they were in 1599.

{img:groundlings-standing-yard-modern-globe-play-performance-summer-evening}
You can stand in the yard, as a groundling, for only a few pounds. You can sit in the galleries, on wooden benches. If it rains, the groundlings get wet. The actors speak directly to the audience, and the audience reacts, laughing, gasping and sometimes calling out. And at the end of every play, even the saddest, the actors dance a jig. It is the closest you can get to the theatre of Shakespeare's time.

Not far from the Globe, the remains of the Rose Theatre, an older playhouse from Shakespeare's time, were discovered in 1989, under an office building. You can visit them, in a dark basement, with lights marking out the shape of the old theatre. And in 2008, archaeologists found the remains of The Theatre, the first playhouse, whose timber was used to build the Globe, under a street in the north of London.

--

{img:tower-of-london-today-white-tower-visitors-beefeaters-ravens-sunny}
The Tower of London still stands by the river, as it did in 1599. Its great White Tower rises above the walls. Visitors from all over the world come to see the crown jewels, the old armour and the ravens, which live there. The guards, called Yeomen Warders or Beefeaters, still wear red and black uniforms in the Tudor style. And they tell the stories of the Tower, every day, to crowds of visitors.

In Cheapside, the old street is now full of modern offices and shops. But the church of St Mary-le-Bow is still there, rebuilt by Wren after the Great Fire, and its bells still ring. And under the modern streets, archaeologists have found many traces of the old city. In 1912, workmen found a great hoard of jewellery under a house in Cheapside. Hundreds of pieces of gold, pearls and precious stones, from the time of Elizabeth and James. It is called the Cheapside Hoard. Nobody knows who hid it, or why.

{img:cheapside-hoard-jewels-museum-london-gold-pearls-gems-display-today}
In the Museum of London, you can see the Cheapside Hoard. Rings, necklaces, brooches and pendants, glittering in their glass cases. And you can also see ordinary things from the time of Elizabeth. Shoes, gloves, combs, toys, pots and pans. A child's leather shoe. A pair of fine embroidered gloves, perhaps made by a glover in Cheapside, long ago.

--

And in Westminster Abbey, Queen Elizabeth lies in her tomb, under a beautiful white marble statue of herself, with her crown and her ruff. Visitors stand beside it, and look at her calm stone face. On the tomb, there is a Latin inscription. And nearby, there is another, in English, from later times. It says, simply, that she and her sister Mary, who were rivals in life, lie here together, in the hope of the resurrection. It is a peaceful place, after a long and eventful reign.
