Dinner is the main meal of the day in 1599. It is eaten at about eleven o'clock in the morning, or at noon. Supper is a smaller meal, eaten in the evening, at about six. And breakfast, for most people, is just a quick snack. The family of the glover eats well. They are not rich. But they are not poor either. And Margaret is a good cook.

--

On an ordinary day, dinner might be a pottage, which is a thick soup of vegetables, beans and a little meat, cooked slowly in a pot over the fire. With it, there is bread, cheese and ale. On meat days, there might be boiled beef or mutton, or a roast chicken. On fish days, there might be herrings, or eels, or stockfish soaked and boiled. There are always plenty of vegetables, especially cabbages, onions, leeks and turnips.

The family eats at the table in the hall. They sit on benches and stools. Thomas, as the head of the house, sits in the one chair, with arms. Everyone has a plate, made of pewter, a dull grey metal, or of wood. Everyone has a spoon and a knife, which they carry with them. Forks are still very rare in England, a foreign fashion. People eat with their fingers, their knives and their spoons. And they wipe their hands on a napkin, or on the tablecloth.

{img:sunday-roast-beef-spit-fire-pies-tudor-kitchen-feast-day}
On Sundays and holidays, there is a feast. Roast beef, turned on a spit in front of the fire, by Joan or by Ned. Meat pies, with thick golden crusts. A pudding, boiled in a cloth. And sweet things, which the English love. Tarts of apples and plums. Gingerbread. Marchpane, which is a sweet made of almonds and sugar, shaped into little figures and painted. And everything is spiced with cinnamon, ginger, cloves and lots of sugar.

The English are famous for their love of sugar. They put it in their wine, their meat, their sauces and their vegetables. Rich people eat so much of it that their teeth turn black. Some people even blacken their teeth on purpose, to look rich. The queen herself has bad teeth, from too many sweets. Margaret does not let her children eat too much sugar. But on feast days, they have a little marchpane, shaped like a rose.

--

{img:new-foods-1599-turkey-potato-tomato-curiosity-kitchen-table-woman}
There are new foods in 1599, from the Americas. The turkey, a big bird from Mexico, has become popular at feasts, especially at Christmas. Some people have tried potatoes, which are very new and strange. They are usually sweet potatoes, from the Caribbean, cooked with sugar and spices. Tomatoes are known, but most people think they are poisonous, and only grow them as pretty plants. Margaret has never tasted any of these. But she has heard about them at the market.

People drink ale, and beer. Beer is made with hops, a plant that gives it a bitter taste, and helps it keep longer. It was brought to England by the Dutch, and many people still prefer the old English ale, without hops. Rich people drink wine, from France, Spain and the Canary Islands. A sweet Spanish wine, called sack, is very fashionable. And there is a new drink, from the Netherlands, made from juniper. But it will be a while yet before it becomes popular in London.

{img:tavern-london-1599-men-drinking-talking-pipes-wooden-tables-fire}
In the evenings, many men go to the taverns and alehouses, to drink, talk, smoke their pipes and play games. There are hundreds of them in London. Some are rough places. Others are quite respectable, where merchants and gentlemen meet. The most famous have names like the Mermaid, the Boar's Head and the Mitre. Writers and actors meet in some of them, and talk late into the night.

--

Thomas sometimes goes to the tavern on Saturday evenings, with his friends from the Company of Glovers. But never for long. Margaret does not approve of taverns. She says that a man's best tavern is his own fireside. And on most evenings, Thomas agrees with her. He sits by the fire, with a mug of Margaret's spiced ale, and listens to Ned reciting speeches from plays, and Bess telling about the customers in the shop, and he thinks that he is a lucky man.
