On Saturday mornings, Margaret goes to the markets, to buy food for the week. And this Saturday, she takes Bess with her. They put on their aprons, take two big baskets, and set off through the crowded streets. London has many markets, each selling different things. And Margaret knows exactly where to find the best of everything, at the best price.

--

First, they go to the market on Cheapside itself, just outside their door. Here, farmers from the countryside sell fruit and vegetables, butter, eggs and cheese. The farmers' wives sit on stools behind their baskets, calling out to the passers-by. Margaret buys cabbages, onions, carrots, and a big bunch of herbs. Then she buys some early apples and some plums, which are ripe in late summer. And a pot of fresh butter, wrapped in cabbage leaves.

Then they walk to Leadenhall, a big covered market in a stone building, where meat, poultry and cheese are sold. There are chickens, geese, ducks, rabbits and pigeons, hanging in rows. There are great cheeses from Cheshire and Suffolk. There are sides of beef and mutton. Margaret buys a piece of beef for Sunday dinner, and a chicken. She bargains with the butcher for a long time, until he lowers his price. Bess watches, and learns.

{img:billingsgate-fish-market-riverside-fishwives-baskets-oysters-boats}
Then they go down to Billingsgate, by the river, where the fish market is. It is very noisy and very smelly. Fishing boats unload their catch right onto the quay. There are herrings, mackerel, cod, eels, salmon from the Thames, and huge baskets of oysters. The fishwives are famous for their loud voices and their rude language. Bess giggles at some of the things they shout. Margaret pretends not to hear, and buys some fresh herrings and a dozen oysters.

On Fridays, and during the forty days of Lent, people are not supposed to eat meat. So fish is very important. In fact, the government has made more fish days, to help the fishermen and the sailors of England. On those days, there is a great demand for fish. Salted herring, dried cod, which is called stockfish, and fresh fish from the river and the sea.

--

{img:street-sellers-london-1599-crying-wares-pies-brooms-lavender-milk}
On the way home, they pass many street sellers, each crying out their goods with a special call. Hot codlings, which are baked apples. Hot mutton pies. Fine Seville oranges. Ripe strawberries, though it is too late for those now. New milk. Mussels and cockles. Brooms, old shoes, mended pots. Each seller has a different song, and Londoners can recognise them from far away, without even looking.

There is also a man selling something new and strange. Little rolls of dried brown leaves. It is tobacco, which comes from America. It was brought to England a few years before. And now it is very fashionable. Gentlemen smoke it in long clay pipes, in the taverns and on the street. Some people think it is good for the health. Others think it is a filthy habit. Margaret thinks it smells terrible, and walks past quickly.

{img:spice-shop-grocers-sugar-cones-pepper-cinnamon-london-1599-counter}
They stop at a grocer's shop, which sells spices and other luxuries from abroad. Pepper, cinnamon, cloves, ginger, nutmeg and mace. Dried fruits, like raisins, currants, figs and dates. Almonds. And sugar, in big hard cones, wrapped in blue paper. Sugar is expensive, but the English love it. They put it in everything. Margaret buys a small piece of sugar, cut off from the cone with special clippers, and a little packet of cinnamon. It is for a special pie.

--

By the time they get home, their baskets are heavy and their arms are aching. But they have bought everything for the week. Margaret is pleased. And Bess has learned a lot about prices, and bargaining, and how to tell a fresh fish from a stale one. She has also had a good look at all the people, and all the things, in the great markets of London. She tells Ned all about the fishwives, at supper. Ned laughs so much that he gets the hiccups.
