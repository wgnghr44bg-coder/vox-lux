On the south bank of the Thames, in Southwark, there is something very special. The theatres. Big round buildings, made of wood, with roofs of straw, and flags flying on top. When a play is going to be performed, a flag is raised, and a trumpet is blown. And crowds of people cross the river, by boat or over the bridge, to see the play.

--

Theatres are quite new in London. Before, actors performed in the courtyards of inns, or in the halls of great houses, or in the open air. But about twenty-five years before, in 1576, an actor called James Burbage built the first proper playhouse in London. It was called simply The Theatre. It stood in a field to the north of the City. And it was a huge success. Soon, other playhouses were built. The Curtain, The Rose, The Swan.

In 1599, something remarkable happens. The company of actors to which William Shakespeare belongs, called the Lord Chamberlain's Men, has a problem. The land where their old playhouse, The Theatre, stood, belongs to a landlord who will not renew their lease. So one cold night, just after Christmas, the actors and their friends take The Theatre apart, piece by piece, beam by beam. They carry the timber across the frozen city, and across the river, to Southwark.

{img:carpenters-building-globe-theatre-timber-frame-round-1599-spring}
In the spring and summer of 1599, they use the old timber to build a new playhouse, on Bankside, near the river. It is round, or rather, it has many sides, almost twenty, so that it looks round. It has three storeys of galleries, where people can sit, around an open yard in the middle. And at one side, there is a big stage, sticking out into the yard. They call it the Globe. Its sign shows the hero Hercules, carrying the world on his shoulders.

Ned has watched the building of the Globe all summer. Every time he crosses to Southwark, to visit his aunt, he goes to look at it. He watches the carpenters raise the great timber frame, and put on the roof of thatch. He watches the painters decorate the stage. And he talks to the workers, and asks a thousand questions. One of the carpenters, a kind old man, lets him climb up into the top gallery, to look down at the stage. Ned thinks it is the most wonderful place in the world.

--

{img:inside-globe-theatre-galleries-yard-stage-painted-heavens-pillars-empty}
Inside the Globe, the stage is covered by a roof, held up by two big painted pillars. The underside of the roof is painted blue, with golden stars, and the signs of the zodiac. It is called the heavens. Under the stage, there is a space called hell, with a trapdoor, through which ghosts and devils can appear. At the back of the stage, there are doors for the actors to come in and go out, and a balcony above, where musicians play, or actors appear, as if at a window.

The Globe can hold about three thousand people. The poorest people stand in the open yard, around the stage, for just one penny. They are called groundlings. If it rains, they get wet. For two pennies, you can sit on a bench in the galleries, under the roof. For three pennies, you can have a cushion, and a better seat. And the richest people sit in special rooms, close to the stage, called the lords' rooms, where everyone can see them.

{img:flag-raised-on-globe-roof-trumpet-afternoon-crowds-gathering-river}
Plays are performed in the afternoon, by daylight, at about two o'clock. There is no electric light, of course. So the plays need the light of the sun. They last about two or three hours. Performances happen almost every day, except on Sundays, and during Lent, and when there is plague in the city. A new play might be performed a few times, and then replaced by another. The actors must learn many plays at once.

--

At last, in the late summer of 1599, the Globe is finished. The flag is raised on its roof for the first time. A trumpet sounds over Bankside. And Ned, who has been waiting all summer for this moment, runs home to Cheapside as fast as he can, to beg his father to let him go and see the first play.
