When Queen Elizabeth passed away in 1603, the people of London mourned. Her funeral procession through the streets was enormous. Thousands of people lined the way, dressed in black, many of them weeping. A writer said that there was such a sighing and groaning and weeping, as had never been known. Her body was buried in Westminster Abbey, where her tomb can still be seen today.

--

She had no children. So the crown went to her cousin, King James the Sixth of Scotland, who became King James the First of England. For the first time, England and Scotland had the same king. James was very interested in the theatre. He became the patron of Shakespeare's company, which was renamed the King's Men. They performed at court many times, and Shakespeare wrote some of his greatest plays for the new king.

Shakespeare went on writing until about 1613. In those years, he wrote Hamlet, Othello, King Lear, Macbeth, The Tempest and many more. Then he went home to Stratford, to his family and his big house, New Place. He passed away in 1616, at the age of fifty-two. Seven years later, two of his actor friends collected his plays and printed them in one big book, called the First Folio. Without it, half of his plays would have been lost forever.

{img:globe-theatre-fire-1613-thatched-roof-smoke-crowd-safely-outside-later}
In 1613, during a performance of a play about Henry the Eighth, a small cannon was fired on stage, for a special effect. A spark landed on the thatched roof of the Globe. And within an hour, the whole theatre had burned down. Nobody was hurt, except one man whose trousers caught fire. Someone put out the flames with a bottle of ale. The Globe was rebuilt the next year, with a tiled roof this time.

In 1642, the Puritans, who believed that plays were sinful, closed all the theatres in England. Two years later, the Globe was pulled down, and houses were built where it had stood. For almost twenty years, there were no plays in London. When the theatres opened again in 1660, women were allowed to act for the first time. And the boy actors, like the one Ned had watched, playing Calpurnia, were no longer needed.

--

{img:great-fire-of-london-1666-st-pauls-burning-distance-night-sky-later}
London went on growing. By 1700, it had more than half a million people. It suffered terrible disasters. In 1665, a great plague. And in 1666, the Great Fire of London, which burned for four days and destroyed most of the old City. Cheapside burned. St Paul's Cathedral burned. Almost all the old timber houses, with their jetties and their signs, were lost.

After the fire, London was rebuilt in brick and stone. The great architect Christopher Wren built a new St Paul's Cathedral, with a huge dome, which still stands on the hill today. He also built more than fifty new churches, including a new St Mary-le-Bow, whose bells still ring in Cheapside. Old London Bridge survived the fire, and stood, with its houses, until 1831, when it was replaced by a new bridge.

{img:wren-st-pauls-dome-rebuilt-london-skyline-1700s-river-later}
The East India Company, which the merchants had begun to plan in September 1599, grew into one of the most powerful companies the world has ever seen. It traded spices, tea, silk and cotton, and later ruled large parts of India. It changed the history of Britain and of Asia, for good and for bad. And it all began with a meeting of merchants, near the Royal Exchange, in the autumn of 1599.

--

As for Bess and Ned, they are not in the history books. But we can imagine. Perhaps Bess became a famous embroiderer, with her own shop in the Royal Exchange, and married Kit, the shy apprentice. Perhaps Ned became a boy actor, playing queens and ladies at the Globe, and later an actor in the King's Men. Perhaps he even acted in a play by Shakespeare. And perhaps, as an old man, he told his grandchildren about that afternoon in September 1599, when the greatest writer in the world asked him if he liked the play.
