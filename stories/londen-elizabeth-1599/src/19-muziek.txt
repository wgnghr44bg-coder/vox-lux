The English in 1599 love music. Everyone sings. In the streets, in the taverns, in the churches, in the fields and at home. Foreign visitors said that England was a land of music, where even the servants and the farmers could sing. And in the evenings, many families make music together, at home, by candlelight.

--

The most popular instrument is the lute. It is a beautiful stringed instrument, with a round, pear-shaped body, like a half melon, and a long neck. It is played by plucking the strings with the fingers. Its sound is soft and sweet. Rich people play the lute, and so do many ordinary people. In the barbers' shops, there is often a lute hanging on the wall, for customers to play while they wait their turn.

There are also viols, which are stringed instruments played with a bow, a little like the violins and cellos of today. There are recorders, flutes and pipes. There are keyboards, called virginals, which are like small harpsichords, in a box, played with the fingers. The queen herself plays the virginals very well. She is said to practise often, alone, in her private rooms.

{img:madrigal-singers-sitting-around-table-part-books-tudor-hall-evening}
The most fashionable music in 1599 is the madrigal. It is a song for several voices, usually four or five, singing different parts at the same time, that weave together into beautiful harmonies. The words are usually about love, nature, spring, shepherds and nymphs. After supper, friends and family sit around a table, each with a little printed book of their part, and sing madrigals together. It is a lovely way to spend an evening.

There are many great English composers in 1599. William Byrd, who writes beautiful church music, and music for the virginals. John Dowland, who writes sad and lovely songs for the voice and the lute. One of them is called Flow, my tears, and it is so famous that everyone in London knows it. Thomas Morley, who writes cheerful madrigals. Their music is still sung and played today, all over the world.

--

{img:ballad-seller-street-corner-singing-printed-sheets-crowd-listening}
In the streets, there is a different kind of music. Ballads. Simple songs, with many verses, telling a story. About love, about a murder, about a disaster, about a famous person, or about something funny. Ballad sellers stand on the street corners, singing their ballads loudly, and selling the printed sheets for a penny. People buy them, learn the tunes, and sing them at home and in the taverns. It is like the popular music of the day.

Margaret has a beautiful voice. She sings while she embroiders the gloves, old songs that she learned from her mother. Greensleeves, which is said to have been written by the queen's father, King Henry the Eighth, though probably it was not. And songs about the seasons, the harvest, the green fields and lost loves. Bess has learned all of them. When they sing together, the customers in the shop stop and listen.

{img:tudor-dance-circle-holding-hands-village-green-london-fields-music}
Dancing is also very popular. At court, the queen loves to dance. Even in her old age, she still dances. People say that she dances a lively dance, called the galliard, with great leaps and kicks, every morning, for exercise. Ordinary people dance too, at weddings, on holidays and at fairs. Country dances, in lines and circles, holding hands, skipping and turning. Everyone joins in, young and old.

--

On the evening of Margaret's birthday, the Dutch family from next door comes to visit. Master Jacob brings his recorder. Kit plays a simple tune on a borrowed lute, which he has been secretly practising for weeks. Margaret and Bess sing. Griet and Bess dance, in the small space between the table and the fireplace. Ned recites a speech from Julius Caesar, very dramatically. Everyone claps. And Thomas sits by the fire, smiling, with the cat on his lap, thinking that there is no better music in the world than the sound of a happy home.
