Tonight we travel back more than four hundred years, to the year 1599. We go to London, the capital of England, on the banks of the river Thames. It is the time of Queen Elizabeth the First. She has ruled England for more than forty years. And London is busy, crowded, noisy, smelly and full of life. One of the most exciting cities in the world.

[long-pause]

It is early morning in late summer. A soft mist lies over the river. Out of the mist rise the church towers and spires of the city, more than a hundred of them. The great old cathedral of St Paul's stands on its hill, above all the rest. Seagulls cry over the water. And across the river stretches London Bridge, with houses and shops built right on top of it, so many that it looks like a street floating over the water.

[pause]

On the river, the first boats are already moving. Little rowing boats, carrying passengers from one bank to the other. Barges loaded with wood, hay and coal. Fishing boats coming back with their catch. The boatmen call out to each other. And from the city, the bells begin to ring. One church, then another, then dozens, all over London, calling people to wake up and begin the day.

[pause]

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

{img:cheapside-street-1599-timber-houses-shop-signs-crowds-carts-morning}
In the streets, people are waking up. Shopkeepers are opening their shutters. Apprentices are sweeping the doorsteps. Water carriers are walking from house to house with big wooden tankards of water on their shoulders. Carts are rattling over the cobbles, bringing food into the city from the countryside. And in a narrow street called Cheapside, in the heart of the city, a family of glovers is getting ready for the day.

[pause]

Tonight, we will spend some time with this family. The father is a glover, who makes and sells fine leather gloves. His wife helps him in the shop. They have a daughter of thirteen, called Bess, and a son of eleven, called Ned. We will see their house and their shop, their food and their friends. We will walk with them through the markets and along the river. And we will go with them to church on Sunday.

[pause]

{img:globe-theatre-1599-new-round-wooden-theatre-bankside-flag-flying}
We will also visit a brand-new building, on the other side of the river. It is a round, wooden theatre, built just this year, with a flag flying from its roof. It is called the Globe. And in it, the plays of a man called William Shakespeare are being performed for the first time. We will stand in the crowd, and watch one of them, in the afternoon sun.

[long-pause]

And we will catch a glimpse of the queen herself. She is sixty-five years old, and has ruled England since 1558. She has never married. She has seen her country through many dangers. And her people love her, and call her Good Queen Bess, and Gloriana, and the Virgin Queen. Her reign is often called the golden age of England.

[pause]

{img:queen-elizabeth-i-1599-white-face-red-hair-pearls-ruff-dignified-portrait}
London in 1599 was not always pleasant. It was dirty and crowded, and there were many poor people. Diseases spread easily. But it was also a city of energy, of new ideas, of trade with the whole world, of music and poetry and theatre. So let us begin, on a late summer morning in 1599, as the bells ring out over the Thames, and the great city wakes up.

[long-pause]

{img:map-view-london-1599-city-walls-gates-river-southwark-fields-around}
London in 1599 is the biggest city in England, by far. About two hundred thousand people live here. That is more than ten times as many as in any other English town. And it is growing fast. Every year, thousands of people come from the countryside, looking for work, for adventure, or for a new life.

[long-pause]

The old heart of London is called the City. It is surrounded by an old stone wall, built by the Romans more than fourteen hundred years before, and repaired many times since. There are seven great gates in the wall, with names like Aldgate, Bishopsgate, Moorgate and Ludgate. At night, the gates are closed. Inside the walls, the streets are narrow and crowded, and the houses are tall, with upper floors that hang out over the street.

[pause]

But London has long since grown beyond its walls. Outside the gates, there are new streets and suburbs, spreading out into the fields. To the west, along the river, there is the Strand, where the great nobles have their palaces, with gardens running down to the water. And further west, there is the city of Westminster, with its old abbey, its royal palace, and the halls where Parliament meets.

[pause]

{img:westminster-abbey-1599-palace-river-thames-barges-strand-mansions}
On the south side of the river, across London Bridge, there is a district called Southwark. It is outside the control of the City. So all kinds of things that are not allowed in the City happen there. There are inns, taverns, gardens and fairs. There are rings for bear-baiting, a cruel sport which we will not visit. And there are the theatres, where crowds come every afternoon to see plays.

[pause]

The river Thames is the great highway of London. There are very few bridges. In fact, there is only one, London Bridge. So everyone crosses the river by boat. There are thousands of small boats, rowed by boatmen called watermen, waiting at the stairs along the river. If you want to go somewhere, you go down to the water and call out, and a waterman rows you there.

[long-pause]

{img:ships-tower-of-london-pool-of-london-merchant-ships-masts-sails-quays}
Below London Bridge, the river is full of ships. Big sailing ships, from all over the world. From the Netherlands, France and Spain. From the cities of the Baltic and Russia. From Italy and the eastern Mediterranean. Some English ships have even sailed all the way around the world, like the famous Golden Hind of Sir Francis Drake. They bring wine, spices, silk, sugar, timber and many other goods. And they take away English wool and cloth.

[pause]

At the eastern edge of the City, by the river, there stands the Tower of London. It is an old castle, built more than five hundred years before by William the Conqueror. Its great White Tower rises above the walls. It is a royal fortress, a prison, an armoury, a treasury and a mint, where coins are made. And there is even a little zoo, with lions, which visitors can pay to see.

[pause]

{img:tower-of-london-white-tower-walls-moat-river-ravens-afternoon-1599}
The streets of London are noisy all day long. Street sellers call out their goods, each with their own song. Hot pies! Fresh oysters! Ripe cherries! New brooms! Who will buy my lavender? Bells ring. Dogs bark. Carts rattle. Hammers bang in workshops. And people talk, shout, laugh and argue, in English, and in Dutch, French, Italian and other languages, because many foreigners live here too.

[long-pause]

The streets are also dirty. There are no proper drains. Rubbish and dirty water are thrown into the street, and run down a gutter in the middle. People walk close to the walls, to avoid being splashed. The richest people ride in coaches, or are carried in chairs. But most people walk. And they wear thick shoes, or wooden overshoes called pattens, to keep their feet out of the mud. London is not a clean city. But it is never, ever boring.

[long-pause]

{img:elizabeth-i-elderly-queen-gown-pearls-ruff-palace-window-greenwich}
Queen Elizabeth the First is sixty-five years old in the summer of 1599. She has been queen for more than forty years, longer than most of her subjects have been alive. For most people in London, she is the only ruler they have ever known. She is like the sun in the sky. Always there, shining over everything.

[long-pause]

Elizabeth had a difficult start in life. She was the daughter of King Henry the Eighth and his second wife, Anne Boleyn. When Elizabeth was only two years old, her mother fell from the king's favour, and lost her life. Elizabeth grew up without a mother, and was not even sure, for many years, if she would ever be a princess again. During the reign of her sister Mary, she was even kept a prisoner for a while, in the Tower of London, and then in a country house.

[pause]

But Elizabeth was clever, careful and very well educated. She spoke Latin, Greek, French, Italian and Spanish. She played music and danced beautifully. She read history and philosophy. And she learned, from her difficult youth, to be patient, to watch people closely, and to keep her thoughts to herself. When her sister Mary passed away in 1558, Elizabeth became queen, at the age of twenty-five. The people of London cheered in the streets.

[pause]

{img:young-elizabeth-coronation-1559-procession-london-crowds-cheering}
Elizabeth never married. Many princes and noblemen wanted to marry her, from England and from foreign countries. She considered many of them, sometimes for years. But in the end, she married none of them. She said that she was married to her kingdom, and that the English people were her children. So she became known as the Virgin Queen. Some of the new lands in America were named after her. Virginia, the land of the Virgin Queen.

[pause]

During her long reign, England changed a great deal. There were dangers. Plots against her life. Quarrels about religion. And in 1588, the great Spanish Armada sailed against England, a huge fleet of warships. But the Armada was scattered by English ships and by storms. And Elizabeth gave a famous speech to her soldiers, saying that she had the body of a weak and feeble woman, but the heart and stomach of a king.

[long-pause]

{img:elizabeth-i-portrait-jewels-elaborate-gown-ermine-sieve-symbols}
In 1599, Elizabeth is old. But she still looks magnificent, at least from a distance. She wears a red wig, curled and decorated with pearls and jewels. Her face is painted white, as was the fashion, with a powder made of white lead. Her dresses are enormous, with wide skirts, puffed sleeves, and huge stiff lace collars, called ruffs. They are covered with embroidery, pearls and precious stones. She is like a living jewel box.

[pause]

A German traveller saw her the year before, in 1598, at her palace at Greenwich. He wrote that her face was oblong, fair, but wrinkled, her eyes small, yet black and pleasant, her nose a little hooked, and her lips narrow. He wrote that her teeth were black, which he thought came from eating too much sugar, which the English loved. She wore a necklace of very fine jewels, and her manner was mild and obliging.

[pause]

{img:queen-walking-palace-gallery-courtiers-kneeling-as-she-passes}
As she walked through the rooms of her palace, the traveller wrote, people fell to their knees on either side as she passed. She spoke to some of them kindly, in English, French or Italian. And wherever she turned her face, everyone knelt. He also saw her dinner being served, with great ceremony, by noble ladies, with trumpets and drums, even though she was not there, and ate in her private room.

[long-pause]

The people of London love their old queen. Every year, on the seventeenth of November, the day she became queen, there are celebrations all over England. Church bells ring. Bonfires are lit. People eat and drink and dance in the streets. And in London, there are great tournaments, where knights ride against each other in her honour. The glover and his family always go to see them. Bess says that one day, she hopes to see the queen up close, just once. Her mother says that she might.

[long-pause]

{img:glovers-shop-cheapside-1599-leather-gloves-hanging-father-at-counter}
In Cheapside, the busiest street of London, there is a narrow timber house, with a shop on the ground floor. Above the door hangs a painted wooden sign, in the shape of a glove. There are no house numbers in London in 1599. So every shop has a sign, to show what it sells, and people find their way by the signs. This one is the shop of Thomas Wood, glover.

[long-pause]

Thomas is about forty years old. He is a tall, thin man, with a short brown beard and careful hands. He makes gloves of soft leather. Plain gloves for workers, warm gloves for winter, and fine gloves for ladies and gentlemen. The finest ones are made of thin, soft kidskin, embroidered with silk and gold thread, and perfumed with sweet scents. Gloves are very fashionable. Rich people give them as presents, and carry them in their hands to show off.

[pause]

Thomas is a member of the Company of Glovers. In London, every trade has its own company, or guild. The bakers, the butchers, the goldsmiths, the drapers, the fishmongers and all the others. The company makes rules about the trade, about prices, quality and training. It looks after its members, and helps their widows and children if they are in need. Every year, it holds a great feast, in its hall.

[pause]

{img:mother-sewing-embroidery-gloves-window-light-daughter-watching}
His wife is called Margaret. She is a small, lively woman, with quick blue eyes and a ready laugh. She works in the shop too. She does the fine embroidery on the gloves, in silk and gold thread, with patterns of flowers, birds and leaves. She is the best embroiderer in the street. She also runs the house, does the cooking, keeps the accounts, and knows everybody's business in Cheapside.

[pause]

Their daughter, Bess, is thirteen. She helps her mother with the embroidery, and is already very skilled. She also helps in the shop, serving customers, and she can read and write a little, which her father taught her. She is curious about everything. She loves to stand at the shop window and watch the people go by in the busy street. And she has a secret wish. She would love, one day, to see the queen up close.

[long-pause]

{img:boy-eleven-tudor-clothes-reciting-lines-pretending-actor-attic}
Their son, Ned, is eleven. He goes to a small school in the mornings, where he learns to read, write and do sums, and a little Latin. In the afternoons, he is supposed to help his father in the workshop. But Ned has another passion. He loves plays. Whenever he can, he slips away to watch the actors, in the inn yards and the theatres. He knows long speeches by heart. And he recites them in the attic, to the cat.

[pause]

There is also an apprentice in the house, a young man of seventeen called Kit, from a village in Kent. He is learning the trade of the glover from Thomas. He will live with the family for seven years, working for them, and in return, they will feed him, house him and teach him. At the end, he will become a glover himself. Kit is shy and hardworking. And he is a little bit in love with Bess, though he would never say so.

[pause]

{img:family-supper-table-tudor-house-candles-pewter-plates-evening}
And there is a maid, a cheerful country girl called Joan, who helps with the cooking and the cleaning. She sleeps in a little room under the roof. Like many maids in London, she came from the countryside, to earn some money before she marries. She sends most of her wages home to her mother. She sings while she works, old country songs, which Bess loves to hear.

[long-pause]

The family is not rich, but they are comfortable. They have a good house, enough food, and some savings. They have fine clothes for Sundays. They are respected in their street. Thomas is a member of his company, and hopes one day to be one of its masters. And in the evenings, when the shop is closed, they sit together by the fire, and talk about the day, and the news, and the queen, and whatever else is happening in the great city outside.

[long-pause]

{img:tudor-timber-framed-house-jettied-upper-floors-narrow-street-london}
The house of the Wood family is typical of London in 1599. It is tall and narrow, squeezed in between its neighbours. It is made of a wooden frame, filled in with plaster, and painted. It has four floors, and each floor sticks out a little further over the street than the one below. This is called jettying. It gives more room upstairs. But it also means that, in narrow streets, the houses on both sides almost touch at the top, and the street below is dark.

[long-pause]

On the ground floor, at the front, there is the shop. It has a wide window, with wooden shutters. In the daytime, the lower shutter is folded down to make a counter, on which the gloves are displayed. Customers stand in the street and look at the goods, and talk to the shopkeeper. At night, the shutters are closed and locked. Behind the shop, there is the workshop, where Thomas and Kit cut and sew the leather.

[pause]

On the first floor, there is the hall, the main room of the family. It is the best room in the house. It has a big fireplace, a table with benches and stools, and a carved wooden chest. On the walls, there are painted cloths, showing scenes from the Bible, and a little mirror. The windows have small panes of glass, set in lead. Glass windows are still quite new for ordinary people. The family is proud of them.

[pause]

{img:tudor-hall-room-fireplace-painted-cloths-table-benches-leaded-windows}
Above the hall, there is the bedroom of Thomas and Margaret. It has a big bed with four posts and curtains, which keep out the draughts at night. Their best possessions are kept here. A chest of linen. Margaret's few pieces of jewellery. A little silver cup, which was a wedding present. And the family Bible. Above that, under the roof, there are small rooms for the children, Kit and Joan.

[pause]

At the back of the house, there is a tiny yard. In it, there is a little kitchen, separate from the house, because of the danger of fire. There is a privy, a little shed with a wooden seat over a pit. There is a pump, which the family shares with the neighbours. And there are a few pots of herbs, growing in the sun. Rosemary, thyme, sage, mint and lavender. Margaret uses them for cooking, medicine and to make the house smell sweet.

[long-pause]

{img:kitchen-tudor-fire-spit-pots-maid-cooking-herbs-hanging-beams}
The floors of the house are made of wooden boards upstairs, and stone or beaten earth downstairs. They are covered with rushes, long dry stems of reeds, mixed with sweet-smelling herbs. The rushes keep the floor warm and soft. Every few weeks, Joan sweeps out the old rushes and puts down fresh ones. Some richer people have rush mats, woven together. And the very rich have carpets, from Turkey. But carpets are too precious to walk on. They are put on tables.

[pause]

At night, the house is lit by candles. Cheap candles made of tallow, which is animal fat, for everyday. They smoke and smell a little. Expensive candles made of beeswax, for special occasions. The family goes to bed early, because candles cost money. In winter, when the nights are long, they sit close to the fire, and use the light of the flames to sew and talk.

[pause]

{img:bedroom-four-poster-bed-curtains-candle-night-tudor-chest-quilt}
Fire is the great danger in London. All the houses are made of wood, with straw or tile roofs, close together. A single spark can set a whole street on fire. So there are strict rules. Every householder must keep a bucket of water by the door, and a ladder. Fires must be covered at night. And a bell, called the curfew bell, rings in the evening, to remind everyone to put out their fires. Margaret checks the fire every night, before she goes to bed, twice.

[long-pause]

Bess loves the house. She knows every creaking board, every crack in the plaster, every corner where the cat likes to sleep. Her room is small, under the roof, with a tiny window. From it, she can see over the rooftops of Cheapside, all the way to the tall tower of St Paul's. On clear nights, she can see the stars above the chimneys. And she can hear the bells of a dozen churches, ringing the hours through the night.

[long-pause]

{img:early-morning-london-street-apprentices-opening-shutters-water-carrier}
The day begins early in London. At about four or five in the morning, the bells of the churches ring. Thomas and Margaret get up first. Then Joan, the maid, who lights the fire and fetches water. Then Kit, the apprentice, who must open the shop and sweep the doorstep. And last of all, the children, who must be called three times before they come down.

[long-pause]

People in 1599 do not wash very much. They think that too much water is bad for the health, because it opens the skin to diseases. Instead, they wash their hands and faces in a bowl of cold water, and rub their bodies with a clean linen cloth. They change their linen shirts often, because they believe that clean linen draws the dirt out of the skin. Rich people use perfumes, to cover the smells. Everyone else simply gets used to them.

[pause]

Bess puts on her clothes. First, a long linen shift, which is like a long shirt, and which she also sleeps in. Then a bodice, laced up the front, and a skirt of wool. Over that, an apron, and a white linen cap on her head, because women and girls always cover their hair. And on her feet, leather shoes. Ned puts on a linen shirt, a doublet, which is like a short jacket, and woollen breeches and stockings. Then he tries to escape without combing his hair.

[pause]

{img:tudor-breakfast-bread-ale-cheese-family-quick-meal-kitchen-dawn}
Breakfast is simple and quick. Bread, a piece of cheese, perhaps some leftover meat from the day before, and a mug of ale. Ale is drunk by everyone, even children, because the water in London is often not safe to drink. It is a weak, slightly sweet ale, made by brewers in the city. Then everyone goes to work. Thomas and Kit in the workshop. Margaret and Bess at the embroidery. Joan at the housework. And Ned to school.

[pause]

Outside, Cheapside is already busy. It is the widest and grandest street in the City. On both sides, there are shops, many of them goldsmiths, with their windows full of gold and silver plates, cups and jewellery. Other shops sell cloth, silk, lace, books, spices and every kind of luxury. In the middle of the street, there are market stalls, selling food. And there are great stone crosses and fountains, called conduits, where people come for water.

[long-pause]

{img:conduit-fountain-cheapside-water-carriers-women-buckets-gossip}
The conduits are very important. They bring fresh water from springs outside the city, through lead pipes, into the middle of London. People come with buckets and jugs to fetch water. Or they pay water carriers, called cobs, to bring it to their houses. The water carriers walk all day, with huge wooden tankards on their shoulders, crying out, fresh water, fresh water. The conduit is also a great place for gossip. All the news of the street is passed on there.

[pause]

At eleven o'clock, or a little before, it is time for dinner, the main meal of the day. The shop is closed for an hour. The whole family, with Kit and Joan, sits around the table in the hall. Thomas says a grace. And then they eat. After dinner, everyone goes back to work, until the evening. In summer, when the days are long, work goes on until seven or eight o'clock.

[pause]

{img:apprentices-playing-football-street-evening-shops-closing-laughter}
In the evening, when the shops are closed, the streets are full of people again. Apprentices play football in the streets, with a leather ball stuffed with straw, which is not allowed, but they do it anyway. Old men sit on benches outside their doors. Women talk at their windows. Children play games. And the smell of supper drifts out from the kitchens.

[long-pause]

Then the curfew bell rings, at about nine o'clock. The apprentices must go home. The gates of the City are closed. The watchmen begin to walk the streets with their lanterns and their bells, calling out the hour and the weather. Nine o'clock, and a fine summer night. And the family goes up to bed, by the light of a single candle, as the great city slowly falls asleep.

[pause]

Bess likes the mornings best. When she opens the shutters of the shop, the street is still cool and grey, and the first sellers are arriving with their baskets. A milkmaid comes by with two pails on a yoke, calling out. A baker's boy runs past with a tray of fresh bread on his head. And the sparrows hop along the gutter, looking for crumbs. Then the sun comes up over the rooftops, and Cheapside fills with light.

[long-pause]

{img:leadenhall-market-1599-stalls-meat-poultry-cheese-crowds-stone-hall}
On Saturday mornings, Margaret goes to the markets, to buy food for the week. And this Saturday, she takes Bess with her. They put on their aprons, take two big baskets, and set off through the crowded streets. London has many markets, each selling different things. And Margaret knows exactly where to find the best of everything, at the best price.

[long-pause]

First, they go to the market on Cheapside itself, just outside their door. Here, farmers from the countryside sell fruit and vegetables, butter, eggs and cheese. The farmers' wives sit on stools behind their baskets, calling out to the passers-by. Margaret buys cabbages, onions, carrots, and a big bunch of herbs. Then she buys some early apples and some plums, which are ripe in late summer. And a pot of fresh butter, wrapped in cabbage leaves.

[pause]

Then they walk to Leadenhall, a big covered market in a stone building, where meat, poultry and cheese are sold. There are chickens, geese, ducks, rabbits and pigeons, hanging in rows. There are great cheeses from Cheshire and Suffolk. There are sides of beef and mutton. Margaret buys a piece of beef for Sunday dinner, and a chicken. She bargains with the butcher for a long time, until he lowers his price. Bess watches, and learns.

[pause]

{img:billingsgate-fish-market-riverside-fishwives-baskets-oysters-boats}
Then they go down to Billingsgate, by the river, where the fish market is. It is very noisy and very smelly. Fishing boats unload their catch right onto the quay. There are herrings, mackerel, cod, eels, salmon from the Thames, and huge baskets of oysters. The fishwives are famous for their loud voices and their rude language. Bess giggles at some of the things they shout. Margaret pretends not to hear, and buys some fresh herrings and a dozen oysters.

[pause]

On Fridays, and during the forty days of Lent, people are not supposed to eat meat. So fish is very important. In fact, the government has made more fish days, to help the fishermen and the sailors of England. On those days, there is a great demand for fish. Salted herring, dried cod, which is called stockfish, and fresh fish from the river and the sea.

[long-pause]

{img:street-sellers-london-1599-crying-wares-pies-brooms-lavender-milk}
On the way home, they pass many street sellers, each crying out their goods with a special call. Hot codlings, which are baked apples. Hot mutton pies. Fine Seville oranges. Ripe strawberries, though it is too late for those now. New milk. Mussels and cockles. Brooms, old shoes, mended pots. Each seller has a different song, and Londoners can recognise them from far away, without even looking.

[pause]

There is also a man selling something new and strange. Little rolls of dried brown leaves. It is tobacco, which comes from America. It was brought to England a few years before. And now it is very fashionable. Gentlemen smoke it in long clay pipes, in the taverns and on the street. Some people think it is good for the health. Others think it is a filthy habit. Margaret thinks it smells terrible, and walks past quickly.

[pause]

{img:spice-shop-grocers-sugar-cones-pepper-cinnamon-london-1599-counter}
They stop at a grocer's shop, which sells spices and other luxuries from abroad. Pepper, cinnamon, cloves, ginger, nutmeg and mace. Dried fruits, like raisins, currants, figs and dates. Almonds. And sugar, in big hard cones, wrapped in blue paper. Sugar is expensive, but the English love it. They put it in everything. Margaret buys a small piece of sugar, cut off from the cone with special clippers, and a little packet of cinnamon. It is for a special pie.

[long-pause]

By the time they get home, their baskets are heavy and their arms are aching. But they have bought everything for the week. Margaret is pleased. And Bess has learned a lot about prices, and bargaining, and how to tell a fresh fish from a stale one. She has also had a good look at all the people, and all the things, in the great markets of London. She tells Ned all about the fishwives, at supper. Ned laughs so much that he gets the hiccups.

[long-pause]

{img:thames-watermen-rowing-boats-passengers-river-stairs-busy-1599}
The river Thames is the heart of London. It is wide, deep and busy, full of boats of every kind. And for most Londoners, it is the main road. Because the streets are narrow, crowded and muddy, it is often much quicker and more pleasant to travel by water. And there are thousands of boatmen, waiting to row you wherever you want to go.

[long-pause]

The boatmen are called watermen. There are about three thousand of them in London. They row small wooden boats, called wherries, which can carry a few passengers. They wait at the river stairs, the stone steps that lead down to the water from the streets. When someone comes down the stairs, the watermen shout, all at once, eastward ho! westward ho! Oars! Oars! Each one hoping to get the passenger for his boat.

[pause]

The watermen are a lively, cheeky group of men. They know every bend and every current of the river. They argue about prices, and tell jokes, and sing as they row. Some of them are good company. Others are rude. Passengers sit on cushions at the back of the boat, and enjoy the view. A trip from the City to Westminster takes about half an hour, if the tide is right.

[pause]

{img:swans-on-thames-river-palaces-strand-gardens-summer-afternoon}
The Thames is also very beautiful. Along its banks, to the west of the City, there are the palaces of the great nobles, with their gardens and orchards running down to the water. Each palace has its own water gate, with steps, and its own boats. On the river, there are hundreds of white swans, gliding gracefully among the boats. Foreign visitors always wrote about them. The swans belong to the queen, and it is forbidden to harm them.

[pause]

The queen herself often travels by river. She has a beautiful royal barge, painted and gilded, with a cabin covered in rich cloth, and a team of oarsmen in her colours. When she travels from one palace to another, from Greenwich to Whitehall, or from Whitehall to Richmond, she goes by barge. And when the royal barge passes, all the boats on the river stop, and the people on the banks take off their hats and cheer.

[long-pause]

{img:royal-barge-gilded-queen-elizabeth-oarsmen-river-crowds-banks-cheer}
One day, in the summer, Thomas takes his family on a trip on the river. It is a holiday, and the shop is closed. They walk down to the river stairs, and Thomas hires a waterman. They climb into the boat, and the waterman rows them upstream, past the palaces of the Strand, past the gardens and the orchards, toward Westminster. The water sparkles in the sun. The swans swim alongside. Bess trails her fingers in the cool water.

[pause]

And then, the waterman points ahead, and says, look, the queen's barge. Coming down the river toward them is a magnificent golden boat, with flags flying, and oarsmen in red and gold, rowing in perfect time. Music is playing on board. And in the cabin, under a canopy, there is a figure in a white dress, glittering with jewels. The waterman stops rowing, and takes off his cap. Everyone in the boat stands up, as well as they can, and bows.

[pause]

{img:family-in-small-boat-bowing-as-royal-barge-passes-sparkling-water}
The royal barge passes quite close to them. For a moment, Bess sees the queen's face. Old and white, with a red wig and a great ruff of lace. And the queen turns her head, and looks at the little boat, with the glover's family standing in it, bowing. And she raises her hand, just a little, in greeting. Then the barge is past, and gone down the river.

[long-pause]

Bess cannot speak for the rest of the trip. She has seen the queen. Up close. And the queen raised her hand to them. Her mother squeezes her arm and says, I told you that you might, one day. That evening, Bess tells the story to Joan, and to Kit, and to the neighbours, and to the cat. And every time she tells it, the queen comes a little closer, and smiles a little more.

[long-pause]

{img:old-london-bridge-1599-houses-shops-on-bridge-arches-river-rushing}
London Bridge is one of the wonders of England. It is the only bridge over the Thames in London. It is more than three hundred and fifty years old in 1599, built of stone, with nineteen arches. And on top of it, there is a whole street of houses and shops. Some of them are four or five storeys high, hanging out over the river on both sides.

[long-pause]

Walking across London Bridge is like walking down a busy, narrow street. There are shops on both sides, selling everything. Gloves, hats, needles, pins, cloth, silk and haberdashery. The shopkeepers live above their shops. In some places, the houses are built across the street, like a tunnel. In others, there are gaps, where you can look out over the river. It is so crowded that it can take an hour to walk across.

[pause]

In the middle of the bridge, there is a little chapel, and a big, beautiful house called Nonsuch House. It was built about twenty years before, in pieces, in the Netherlands, and brought to London by ship, and put together on the bridge, with wooden pegs, without a single iron nail. It is painted in bright colours, with towers and onion domes, and many windows. It is one of the most admired buildings in London.

[pause]

{img:nonsuch-house-colourful-towers-onion-domes-on-bridge-sunlit}
The arches of the bridge are narrow, and the stone piers are very wide, standing on big wooden platforms called starlings. So the river is squeezed through the arches. When the tide is going out, the water rushes through the arches like a waterfall, foaming and roaring. The difference in the height of the water on the two sides of the bridge can be more than a metre. Going through the arches in a boat is called shooting the bridge. It is very exciting, and a little dangerous.

[pause]

Many passengers get out of their boats before the bridge, and walk around it, and get back in on the other side. The watermen say that London Bridge was made for wise men to go over, and fools to go under. But some young men love to shoot the bridge, for the thrill. Ned dreams of doing it one day. His mother says that he certainly will not.

[long-pause]

{img:waterwheels-under-london-bridge-arches-river-foam-pumping-water}
Under some of the arches, there are big waterwheels, turned by the rushing water. They work pumps, which push water from the river up through pipes into the houses of the city. The water is not very clean, of course. But it is useful for washing and cleaning. The wheels make a great creaking and splashing noise, day and night. People who live on the bridge get used to it, and cannot sleep without it.

[pause]

At the southern end of the bridge, there is a great stone gateway. And on top of the gateway, sadly, there is something that every visitor to London remembers. But we will not look at that. We will look instead at the view from the bridge, over the river. Upstream, the river curves away to the west, past the palaces and gardens. Downstream, there is the Pool of London, crowded with ships, and the grey walls of the Tower.

[pause]

{img:view-from-london-bridge-ships-pool-tower-sunset-gulls-river}
Bess and Ned often walk across the bridge, to visit their aunt, who lives in Southwark, on the other side. They love to stop at the gaps between the houses, and lean over the wall, and look down at the rushing water below, and the boats shooting through the arches. They watch the gulls and the swans, and the ships coming in from the sea. And they count the boats, and argue about whose boat is the biggest.

[long-pause]

There is a famous old song about the bridge. London Bridge is falling down, falling down, falling down. Children sing it, holding hands, in a circle. It is probably very old already in 1599. The bridge has been damaged many times, by floods, fires and ice. But it has always been repaired. And it will stand, with its houses and its shops, for more than two hundred years more, until a new bridge replaces it.

[long-pause]

{img:old-st-pauls-cathedral-1599-gothic-huge-nave-no-spire-churchyard-booksellers}
On the highest hill of the City, there stands the great old cathedral of St Paul's. It is huge, one of the biggest churches in Europe, built of grey stone, in the Gothic style, more than three hundred years before. It once had a tall spire, one of the highest in the world. But about forty years ago, the spire was struck by lightning and burned down. It was never rebuilt. So now the tower stands without it, square and grey.

[long-pause]

St Paul's is not only a church. It is also the busiest meeting place in London. Every day, the long middle aisle of the cathedral, called the nave, is full of people. Not praying, but walking, talking, doing business and gossiping. This is called Paul's Walk. Merchants make deals. Lawyers meet their clients. Servants look for work. Young gentlemen show off their new clothes. And everybody exchanges news.

[pause]

If you want to know what is happening in London, you go to Paul's Walk. Who is in favour with the queen. Who has been arrested. Which ships have arrived. What is happening in the war in Ireland. What the latest fashion is. People say that if you walk up and down the nave for an hour, you will hear all the news of England. The noise of so many voices fills the great building like the buzzing of bees.

[pause]

{img:pauls-walk-nave-crowded-merchants-gentlemen-gossip-light-through-windows}
Some people think that this is not a proper way to behave in a church. And preachers often complain about it. But nobody can stop it. People even carry baskets and beer through the church, using it as a shortcut. Children play in the aisles. And sometimes, someone leads a horse right through the nave. In the side chapels, the priests say their prayers, while all around them, London talks.

[pause]

Around the cathedral, there is a big churchyard. And in the churchyard, there are the shops of the booksellers. Dozens of little shops and stalls, built against the walls of the cathedral, selling books, pamphlets and ballads. This is the centre of the book trade in England. Each shop has its own sign. The Green Dragon. The Rose. The Angel. The Bible. Customers browse the books on the stalls, and buy the latest news, poems and plays.

[long-pause]

{img:booksellers-shops-pauls-churchyard-books-pamphlets-ballads-customers}
Printed books are becoming more and more common. There are Bibles and prayer books, of course. But also books of history, poetry, travel, medicine, cooking and gardening. There are little books of jokes and riddles. There are songs, printed on single sheets, called ballads, which people buy for a penny and sing in the streets. And there are the printed texts of plays, so that people can read the plays they have seen in the theatre.

[pause]

Ned loves the booksellers. Whenever he has a penny, he goes to Paul's Churchyard, and looks at the plays. Most of them are too expensive for him. But sometimes, a kind bookseller lets him read for a while, standing at the stall. And once, with a whole sixpence that he saved for months, he buys a little printed play. It is called Romeo and Juliet. Ned knows that it was written by a player called William Shakespeare. He reads it so many times that the pages fall out.

[pause]

{img:boy-reading-small-printed-playbook-stall-churchyard-afternoon-light}
Next to the cathedral, in the churchyard, there is an outdoor pulpit, called Paul's Cross. Every Sunday, a famous preacher gives a sermon there, in the open air, to a huge crowd. Thousands of people stand and listen. Important announcements are made there too. News of victories, royal proclamations, and warnings. It is like the voice of London, speaking to its people.

[long-pause]

Bess prefers the cathedral when it is quiet, early in the morning, before the crowds come. Sometimes she goes there with her mother, to say a prayer. Then the great nave is almost empty, and the light comes down through the tall windows, in long beams, full of dust. The sound of their footsteps echoes softly. And it is easy to imagine how the cathedral must have felt, long ago, when it was new, and the spire still rose to the sky.

[long-pause]

{img:royal-exchange-london-1599-courtyard-arcades-merchants-many-nations}
In the middle of the City, not far from Cheapside, there is a grand building, built about thirty years before. It is called the Royal Exchange. It was built by a rich merchant called Thomas Gresham, as a meeting place for merchants. And the queen herself opened it, and gave it its royal name. It is one of the most important places in London.

[long-pause]

The Royal Exchange is built around a big square courtyard, surrounded by covered walkways with stone columns. On top of the building, there is a tall bell tower, with a golden grasshopper on top, which was the symbol of the Gresham family. Twice a day, at noon and in the evening, the merchants gather in the courtyard, to do business. They buy and sell goods, ships, shares in voyages, and money from other countries.

[pause]

The merchants come from everywhere. English merchants, of course. But also Dutch, Flemish, French, Italian, Spanish, German and Portuguese merchants. Each group stands in its own part of the courtyard. They speak many languages. They talk about prices, ships, cargoes and the news from abroad. Which ships have arrived from the Baltic. What the price of pepper is in Antwerp. Whether there is war or peace in the Netherlands.

[pause]

{img:upper-gallery-shops-royal-exchange-ladies-buying-lace-gloves-perfume}
Upstairs, around the courtyard, there is a long gallery of small shops. More than a hundred of them. They sell all kinds of luxury goods. Fine lace, silk ribbons, gloves, perfumes, jewellery, hats, mirrors and fans. It is the most fashionable place to shop in London. Elegant ladies and gentlemen walk up and down the gallery, looking at the goods, and at each other. It is a little like a shopping centre of today.

[pause]

Thomas Wood dreams of having a shop in the Royal Exchange one day. It would be a great step up, from Cheapside. But the rents are very high. So for now, he sells some of his finest gloves to a shopkeeper there, a friend of his, who sells them on to the rich ladies. Margaret's embroidered gloves sell especially well. They are among the finest in the gallery.

[long-pause]

{img:merchant-ship-departing-thames-voyage-east-indies-sails-crowd-watching}
In 1599, the merchants of London are talking about something very exciting. The Dutch have recently sent ships all the way to the East Indies, to the islands where spices grow. Pepper, cloves, nutmeg and cinnamon. And their ships have come back loaded with spices, and made their owners very rich. Now the English merchants want to do the same. In September 1599, a group of them meet, and decide to form a company to trade with the East Indies.

[pause]

The next year, the queen will give them a charter, a special permission, to trade with all the lands east of the Cape of Good Hope. It will be called the East India Company. Nobody in 1599 can imagine how big and powerful it will one day become. For now, it is just a group of merchants, in a room near the Exchange, dreaming of spices and distant islands.

[pause]

{img:old-merchant-with-globe-maps-telling-stories-young-girl-listening}
Thomas's friend at the Exchange is an old Dutch merchant, called Master Pieter. He has lived in London for thirty years. He came from Antwerp, during the wars in the Netherlands, like many others. He has a shop full of maps, globes and instruments for sailors. When Bess comes with her father to deliver the gloves, Master Pieter shows her his globe. He spins it, and shows her England, small and green, and then the whole wide world around it.

[long-pause]

He shows her the Americas, where the Spanish have found gold and silver, and the English are trying to start colonies. He shows her Africa, and the long way around it to India. He shows her China and Japan, and the islands of spices. He tells her that Sir Francis Drake sailed all the way around this globe, twenty years before. Bess spins the globe slowly with her finger, and feels very small, and very excited, all at once. The world is so big. And London, it seems, is right at the centre of it.

[long-pause]

{img:tudor-family-dinner-hall-table-pewter-plates-roast-meat-bread-ale}
Dinner is the main meal of the day in 1599. It is eaten at about eleven o'clock in the morning, or at noon. Supper is a smaller meal, eaten in the evening, at about six. And breakfast, for most people, is just a quick snack. The family of the glover eats well. They are not rich. But they are not poor either. And Margaret is a good cook.

[long-pause]

On an ordinary day, dinner might be a pottage, which is a thick soup of vegetables, beans and a little meat, cooked slowly in a pot over the fire. With it, there is bread, cheese and ale. On meat days, there might be boiled beef or mutton, or a roast chicken. On fish days, there might be herrings, or eels, or stockfish soaked and boiled. There are always plenty of vegetables, especially cabbages, onions, leeks and turnips.

[pause]

The family eats at the table in the hall. They sit on benches and stools. Thomas, as the head of the house, sits in the one chair, with arms. Everyone has a plate, made of pewter, a dull grey metal, or of wood. Everyone has a spoon and a knife, which they carry with them. Forks are still very rare in England, a foreign fashion. People eat with their fingers, their knives and their spoons. And they wipe their hands on a napkin, or on the tablecloth.

[pause]

{img:sunday-roast-beef-spit-fire-pies-tudor-kitchen-feast-day}
On Sundays and holidays, there is a feast. Roast beef, turned on a spit in front of the fire, by Joan or by Ned. Meat pies, with thick golden crusts. A pudding, boiled in a cloth. And sweet things, which the English love. Tarts of apples and plums. Gingerbread. Marchpane, which is a sweet made of almonds and sugar, shaped into little figures and painted. And everything is spiced with cinnamon, ginger, cloves and lots of sugar.

[pause]

The English are famous for their love of sugar. They put it in their wine, their meat, their sauces and their vegetables. Rich people eat so much of it that their teeth turn black. Some people even blacken their teeth on purpose, to look rich. The queen herself has bad teeth, from too many sweets. Margaret does not let her children eat too much sugar. But on feast days, they have a little marchpane, shaped like a rose.

[long-pause]

{img:new-foods-1599-turkey-potato-tomato-curiosity-kitchen-table-woman}
There are new foods in 1599, from the Americas. The turkey, a big bird from Mexico, has become popular at feasts, especially at Christmas. Some people have tried potatoes, which are very new and strange. They are usually sweet potatoes, from the Caribbean, cooked with sugar and spices. Tomatoes are known, but most people think they are poisonous, and only grow them as pretty plants. Margaret has never tasted any of these. But she has heard about them at the market.

[pause]

People drink ale, and beer. Beer is made with hops, a plant that gives it a bitter taste, and helps it keep longer. It was brought to England by the Dutch, and many people still prefer the old English ale, without hops. Rich people drink wine, from France, Spain and the Canary Islands. A sweet Spanish wine, called sack, is very fashionable. And there is a new drink, from the Netherlands, made from juniper. But it will be a while yet before it becomes popular in London.

[pause]

{img:tavern-london-1599-men-drinking-talking-pipes-wooden-tables-fire}
In the evenings, many men go to the taverns and alehouses, to drink, talk, smoke their pipes and play games. There are hundreds of them in London. Some are rough places. Others are quite respectable, where merchants and gentlemen meet. The most famous have names like the Mermaid, the Boar's Head and the Mitre. Writers and actors meet in some of them, and talk late into the night.

[long-pause]

Thomas sometimes goes to the tavern on Saturday evenings, with his friends from the Company of Glovers. But never for long. Margaret does not approve of taverns. She says that a man's best tavern is his own fireside. And on most evenings, Thomas agrees with her. He sits by the fire, with a mug of Margaret's spiced ale, and listens to Ned reciting speeches from plays, and Bess telling about the customers in the shop, and he thinks that he is a lucky man.

[long-pause]

{img:petty-school-tudor-children-benches-hornbook-schoolmaster-classroom}
Every morning, Ned goes to school. It is a small school, in a room above a shop, a few streets away. It is called a petty school, where young children learn to read, write and count. The schoolmaster is an old man with spectacles and a long black gown. He is strict, but not unkind. About twenty boys sit on long benches, with their books on their knees.

[long-pause]

The youngest children learn to read with a hornbook. It is a little wooden board, with a handle, like a small paddle. On it, there is a sheet of paper, with the alphabet and the Lord's Prayer printed on it. And over the paper, there is a thin, clear sheet of cow's horn, to protect it from dirty fingers. The children point to the letters and say them aloud, again and again, until they know them by heart.

[pause]

The older boys, like Ned, learn to write with a quill pen and ink, on paper. They learn sums. And they begin to learn a little Latin, which is the language of scholars, lawyers and churchmen. Clever boys may go on to a grammar school, where they will learn much more Latin, and Greek, and read the old Roman poets and writers. William Shakespeare went to a grammar school, in the town of Stratford, where he learned to love the old stories.

[pause]

{img:schoolboys-reciting-latin-schoolmaster-pointing-book-morning-light}
Ned is not a very good pupil. He is bored by sums, and finds Latin grammar terribly dull. But he loves one thing about school. Sometimes, the schoolmaster lets the boys act out little scenes from old Latin plays, to help them learn the language. Then Ned comes alive. He acts every part, with all his heart, and makes the other boys laugh. The schoolmaster shakes his head. But secretly, he smiles.

[pause]

Most girls in 1599 do not go to school. Some girls from rich families have tutors at home, and learn to read, write, sing, play music, speak French and dance. The queen herself was one of the most educated women in Europe. But most girls learn at home, from their mothers. They learn to cook, sew, embroider, keep house and look after children. Some learn to read a little, from the Bible. Bess learned to read from her father, in the evenings, by the fire.

[long-pause]

{img:tudor-children-playing-street-games-hoop-spinning-top-hopscotch-sunny}
When they are not at school or at work, the children of London play. In the streets, in the courtyards and in the fields outside the walls. They play with hoops and sticks, spinning tops and marbles. They play hide and seek, blind man's buff, and leapfrog. They play a game called hopscotch, which was already old in 1599. They fly kites. They play football, a rough game with few rules, which often ends with bruises.

[pause]

Girls play with dolls, made of wood or cloth, which they dress in little gowns. They play skipping games and clapping games, with songs. They play cat's cradle, with a loop of string. And they play at being grown-ups. Running a shop, cooking a meal, holding a wedding. Bess is too old for most of these games now. But sometimes she still plays cat's cradle with the little girl next door.

[pause]

{img:fields-outside-london-walls-children-flying-kites-windmills-summer}
On holidays, the people of London go out into the fields outside the walls, to the north and east of the city. There, there are green meadows, archery grounds, windmills and little ponds. People walk, have picnics, play games, practise archery and dance. In winter, when the ponds freeze, they go skating, with skates made of animal bones tied to their shoes. The children love it.

[long-pause]

One summer holiday, Thomas takes the whole family out to the fields at Moorfields, just north of the City wall. They bring a basket of bread, cheese, cold chicken and plums. They sit on the grass and eat. Ned flies a kite, which Kit made for him. Bess makes a chain of daisies. Margaret and Thomas sit together in the sun, and do nothing at all, for once. And the windmills turn slowly on the hill, in the warm summer breeze.

[pause]

On the way home, as the sun sets, the windmills stop turning, one by one. The family walks back through Moorgate, into the city, with the crowds of other people coming home from the fields. Ned carries his kite. Bess wears her daisy chain. And they all agree that it was the best day of the summer.

[long-pause]

{img:bankside-southwark-1599-theatres-round-buildings-flags-river-boats-crowds}
On the south bank of the Thames, in Southwark, there is something very special. The theatres. Big round buildings, made of wood, with roofs of straw, and flags flying on top. When a play is going to be performed, a flag is raised, and a trumpet is blown. And crowds of people cross the river, by boat or over the bridge, to see the play.

[long-pause]

Theatres are quite new in London. Before, actors performed in the courtyards of inns, or in the halls of great houses, or in the open air. But about twenty-five years before, in 1576, an actor called James Burbage built the first proper playhouse in London. It was called simply The Theatre. It stood in a field to the north of the City. And it was a huge success. Soon, other playhouses were built. The Curtain, The Rose, The Swan.

[pause]

In 1599, something remarkable happens. The company of actors to which William Shakespeare belongs, called the Lord Chamberlain's Men, has a problem. The land where their old playhouse, The Theatre, stood, belongs to a landlord who will not renew their lease. So one cold night, just after Christmas, the actors and their friends take The Theatre apart, piece by piece, beam by beam. They carry the timber across the frozen city, and across the river, to Southwark.

[pause]

{img:carpenters-building-globe-theatre-timber-frame-round-1599-spring}
In the spring and summer of 1599, they use the old timber to build a new playhouse, on Bankside, near the river. It is round, or rather, it has many sides, almost twenty, so that it looks round. It has three storeys of galleries, where people can sit, around an open yard in the middle. And at one side, there is a big stage, sticking out into the yard. They call it the Globe. Its sign shows the hero Hercules, carrying the world on his shoulders.

[pause]

Ned has watched the building of the Globe all summer. Every time he crosses to Southwark, to visit his aunt, he goes to look at it. He watches the carpenters raise the great timber frame, and put on the roof of thatch. He watches the painters decorate the stage. And he talks to the workers, and asks a thousand questions. One of the carpenters, a kind old man, lets him climb up into the top gallery, to look down at the stage. Ned thinks it is the most wonderful place in the world.

[long-pause]

{img:inside-globe-theatre-galleries-yard-stage-painted-heavens-pillars-empty}
Inside the Globe, the stage is covered by a roof, held up by two big painted pillars. The underside of the roof is painted blue, with golden stars, and the signs of the zodiac. It is called the heavens. Under the stage, there is a space called hell, with a trapdoor, through which ghosts and devils can appear. At the back of the stage, there are doors for the actors to come in and go out, and a balcony above, where musicians play, or actors appear, as if at a window.

[pause]

The Globe can hold about three thousand people. The poorest people stand in the open yard, around the stage, for just one penny. They are called groundlings. If it rains, they get wet. For two pennies, you can sit on a bench in the galleries, under the roof. For three pennies, you can have a cushion, and a better seat. And the richest people sit in special rooms, close to the stage, called the lords' rooms, where everyone can see them.

[pause]

{img:flag-raised-on-globe-roof-trumpet-afternoon-crowds-gathering-river}
Plays are performed in the afternoon, by daylight, at about two o'clock. There is no electric light, of course. So the plays need the light of the sun. They last about two or three hours. Performances happen almost every day, except on Sundays, and during Lent, and when there is plague in the city. A new play might be performed a few times, and then replaced by another. The actors must learn many plays at once.

[long-pause]

At last, in the late summer of 1599, the Globe is finished. The flag is raised on its roof for the first time. A trumpet sounds over Bankside. And Ned, who has been waiting all summer for this moment, runs home to Cheapside as fast as he can, to beg his father to let him go and see the first play.

[long-pause]

{img:crowd-entering-globe-theatre-afternoon-paying-penny-box-gatherer}
On a sunny afternoon in September 1599, the whole Wood family goes to the Globe. Thomas has closed the shop early. It is a great treat. Even Margaret, who usually says that theatres are noisy, crowded places, has agreed to come. They walk across London Bridge, with crowds of other people, all going the same way. Ned runs ahead, and then runs back, and then runs ahead again.

[long-pause]

At the door of the Globe, there is a man with a box, collecting the money. The money is dropped into the box, through a slot. Some people say that is where the words box office come from. Thomas pays for the whole family to sit in the lower gallery, on a bench, two pennies each. It is a big expense. But Ned has been begging for weeks. And Thomas thinks that it is worth it, to see the boy's face.

[pause]

The theatre is filling up. The yard is crowded with groundlings, standing around the stage. Apprentices, servants, sailors, soldiers and porters. Men and women, young and old. They are noisy and cheerful, eating nuts and apples, drinking beer, and calling out to their friends. Sellers walk through the crowd, selling food and drinks. In the galleries, people are finding their seats. Rich gentlemen in silk. Merchants with their wives. Young law students from the Inns of Court.

[pause]

{img:actors-in-roman-togas-stage-globe-julius-caesar-crowd-listening-sunlight}
Then a trumpet sounds, three times. The noise slowly dies down. And actors come out onto the stage. The play is new. It is called Julius Caesar. It is by William Shakespeare. It tells the story of the great Roman general, Caesar, who wanted to become king of Rome, and of the men who plotted against him. It is a story that everyone in the audience knows a little, from school.

[pause]

The actors wear rich costumes. Not Roman togas, exactly, but a mixture of old and new clothes, with cloaks and swords. There is no scenery, only the stage and the painted pillars. The actors must make the audience imagine everything with their words. A street in Rome. A storm at night. A great crowd in the forum. And they do it so well that the audience forgets that they are in Southwark, and believes that they are in Rome.

[long-pause]

{img:actor-delivering-speech-friends-romans-countrymen-crowd-spellbound}
All the women's parts are played by boys. In 1599, women are not allowed to act on the stage. So boys with high, clear voices play the queens, the ladies and the wives. They wear gowns and wigs, and speak in soft voices. Some of them are very good. Ned watches the boy who plays Caesar's wife, Calpurnia, very closely. He thinks that he could do that too.

[pause]

There is one moment that everyone remembers. The great speech of Mark Antony, Caesar's friend, to the people of Rome. Friends, Romans, countrymen, lend me your ears, he begins. The whole theatre goes silent. Even the noisiest groundlings stop talking. The actor speaks so beautifully that some people in the audience have tears in their eyes. Margaret, who did not want to come, is holding her handkerchief tightly.

[pause]

{img:jig-dance-end-of-play-actors-dancing-stage-audience-clapping-laughing}
We will not follow the sad parts of the story. They are told with great feeling, but we will let them pass. At the end of the play, something surprising happens. The actors come back onto the stage, and dance. A lively, funny dance, called a jig, with music and jokes and singing. It is the custom at the end of every play, even a sad one. The audience claps and laughs and cheers. And then it is over, and everyone pours out of the theatre, talking about what they have seen.

[long-pause]

A traveller from Switzerland, called Thomas Platter, was in London in September 1599. He wrote in his diary that on the twenty-first of September, after lunch, he crossed the river and saw a tragedy about the first emperor Julius Caesar, in a house with a thatched roof, very well acted, with about fifteen characters. And that at the end, two of the actors in men's clothes and two in women's clothes danced together very gracefully. Perhaps, on that same afternoon, the Wood family was sitting in the gallery, just a few seats away from him.

[long-pause]

{img:william-shakespeare-1599-age-35-writing-quill-room-candle-thoughtful}
In 1599, William Shakespeare is thirty-five years old. He is an actor, a playwright and a shareholder in the Globe. He is already well known in London. His plays are popular. But he is not yet the most famous writer in the world, as he will become. He is simply a successful man of the theatre, working hard, writing new plays, and acting in them.

[long-pause]

Shakespeare was born in 1564, in Stratford-upon-Avon, a small market town in the middle of England, by a river. His father, John Shakespeare, was a glover, just like Thomas Wood. He made gloves, and sold them in a shop on Henley Street. He was also an important man in the town, and was even mayor for a while. William grew up among the leather and the gloves, just like Bess and Ned.

[pause]

William went to the grammar school in Stratford, where he learned Latin, and read the old Roman poets. At eighteen, he married a woman called Anne Hathaway, who was eight years older than him. They had three children, a daughter called Susanna, and twins, called Hamnet and Judith. Then, some time in the next few years, William left Stratford and came to London, to work in the theatre. Nobody knows exactly when or why.

[pause]

{img:stratford-upon-avon-glovers-shop-henley-street-timber-house-river}
In London, he became an actor, and then began to write plays. At first, plays about English history, about kings and wars. Then comedies, full of love, mistaken identities and funny servants. Then tragedies, like Romeo and Juliet. His plays were full of wonderful language, unforgettable characters and new words. Many words and phrases we still use today were first written by Shakespeare. Or at least, he was the first to write them down.

[pause]

Shakespeare's family stayed in Stratford. He visited them when he could, perhaps once a year, a journey of several days on horseback. In 1596, his only son, Hamnet, passed away, at the age of eleven. It was a great sorrow. Some people think that it can be felt in the plays he wrote afterwards. Shakespeare used his money to buy the second-biggest house in Stratford, called New Place, for his family.

[long-pause]

{img:shakespeare-walking-bankside-river-evening-thinking-globe-in-background}
In 1599, Shakespeare is working on several plays. Julius Caesar has just been performed at the Globe. He is writing a comedy called As You Like It, set in a magical forest, with the famous lines, all the world's a stage, and all the men and women merely players. And he may be starting to think about a new tragedy, about a young prince of Denmark, called Hamlet. Some people think that its title is a little like the name of his lost son.

[pause]

He lives in rented rooms, near the theatres. He is a quiet man, people say. Friendly, gentle and witty. He does not drink too much, or fight, or make a fuss. He works. In the mornings, perhaps, he writes. In the afternoons, he acts, or watches the rehearsals and the plays. In the evenings, perhaps, he sits in a tavern with the other actors, and talks about the plays, the actors, the audience, and the news of the day.

[pause]

{img:boy-actor-meeting-playwright-stage-door-globe-shy-hopeful-afternoon}
After the play, Ned waits by the back door of the Globe, where the actors come out. He wants to see them up close. One by one, they come out, in their ordinary clothes, laughing and talking. And among them, there is a man of medium height, with a high forehead, a small beard and kind, tired eyes. One of the other actors calls him Will. Ned is too shy to speak. But the man notices him, and smiles, and asks if he liked the play. Ned says yes. Very much. The man nods, and says that's good, and walks on.

[long-pause]

Ned never knows for sure if that was really Shakespeare. But he believes it was. And for the rest of his life, he tells the story. How, one afternoon in September 1599, outside the Globe, the greatest writer in the world asked him if he liked the play. And how he said yes. And how the great man smiled. It is a small story. But it is Ned's story. And he never gets tired of telling it.

[long-pause]

{img:strangers-in-london-1599-dutch-french-huguenot-weavers-workshop-street}
London in 1599 is not only English. It is full of people from other countries. Londoners call them strangers. There are perhaps five or ten thousand of them. Some are merchants, who come and go. Others have settled in London for good, with their families, their churches and their own ways. And they bring new skills, new foods and new ideas to the city.

[long-pause]

The biggest group are the Dutch and the Flemish, from the Low Countries, across the North Sea. Many of them came to England as refugees, fleeing from the wars and the religious persecution in their own lands. They are Protestants, like the English, and they were welcomed. They are very skilled craftsmen. Weavers of fine cloth, brewers of beer, makers of glass, printers and engravers. They have their own church in London, called the Dutch Church, at Austin Friars.

[pause]

There are also French Protestants, called Huguenots, who fled from the wars of religion in France. Many of them are silk weavers, goldsmiths and makers of fine things. There are Italians, who are bankers, merchants, musicians and fencing masters. There are a few Spanish and Portuguese, including some Jewish families, who have to keep their faith quietly. And there are Germans, Danes and many others.

[pause]

{img:black-tudor-londoners-1599-trumpeter-seamstress-street-life-church}
And there are Africans. In 1599, there are perhaps a few hundred Black people living in London. Some came from Africa, some from Spain and Portugal, some from the Americas. Their names appear in the records of the London churches, where they were baptised, married and buried. They worked as servants, sailors, seamstresses, musicians and craftsmen. Some were free, and some were not. One of them, a man called John Blanke, had been a royal trumpeter, for the queen's grandfather and father, many years before.

[pause]

Not everyone in London is friendly to the strangers. Some English workers are jealous of them, because they are so skilled, and fear that they will take their work. Sometimes, there is trouble in the streets. But many Londoners are glad to have them. They buy their goods, learn from their skills, and become their friends. And slowly, the strangers become Londoners too.

[long-pause]

{img:dutch-church-austin-friars-london-congregation-sunday-singing}
In Cheapside, next door to the Wood family, there is a Dutch family, who make spectacles and fine glass. The father, Master Jacob, came from Antwerp twenty years ago. His wife, Anneke, makes the best pancakes in London. Their daughter, Griet, is the same age as Bess, and the two girls are best friends. They talk to each other in a mixture of English and Dutch. Bess has learned quite a few Dutch words. Griet has learned many English ones.

[pause]

On Sundays, the Dutch family goes to the Dutch Church, where the services are in Dutch. Sometimes, Bess goes with them, for a visit. She does not understand much of the sermon. But she loves the singing. The Dutch sing their psalms slowly and loudly, all together, in a way that makes the whole church vibrate. Afterwards, Anneke makes pancakes, with butter and sugar. Bess eats so many that she cannot eat her own dinner at home.

[pause]

{img:two-girls-friends-english-dutch-laughing-window-pancakes-sunday}
London is also connected to the world through its ships. Sailors come home from voyages to faraway places, with stories, parrots, monkeys, shells and strange objects. In the taverns by the river, they tell about Africa and the Americas, about Russia and the islands of spices. Some of them have sailed with Drake, or Raleigh, or Frobisher. Ned loves to listen to them. He sits in the corner, quietly, with a mug of small ale, and hears about whales and storms, about icebergs and tropical islands.

[long-pause]

All these people, and all these stories, make London a very exciting place to live. It is the meeting place of England and the world. Bess sometimes thinks about Master Pieter's globe, at the Royal Exchange, and how it spun under her finger. And she thinks that perhaps the whole world, spinning round, comes to London in the end. Through its river, its ships, its markets, and its people.

[long-pause]

{img:london-parish-church-sunday-morning-families-in-best-clothes-bells}
On Sundays, the shops of London are closed. Nobody works. And everyone goes to church. In fact, it is the law. Everyone must go to church on Sunday, to their own parish church. If they do not go, they must pay a fine. The bells of more than a hundred churches ring out over the city, calling the people to prayer.

[long-pause]

The Wood family puts on their best clothes. Thomas wears his good black doublet and a starched white ruff around his neck. Margaret wears her best gown, of dark blue wool, with a white lace collar, and a hat. Bess has a new bodice, embroidered by her mother, and a clean white cap. Ned's hair has been combed, by force. And even Kit and Joan are dressed in their Sunday best.

[pause]

Their parish church is St Mary-le-Bow, in Cheapside. It is an old church, with a tall tower, and famous bells. People say that a true Londoner, a Cockney, is someone born within the sound of Bow Bells. The bells ring every evening too, at nine o'clock, as the curfew, to tell the apprentices to stop work and go home.

[pause]

{img:interior-church-1599-whitewashed-walls-pulpit-pews-congregation-sermon}
Inside, the church is plain and simple. The walls are whitewashed. Most of the old paintings and statues have been removed, because of the changes in religion in England, earlier in the century. There is a pulpit, from which the minister preaches. There are wooden benches and pews, where the families sit. The important families sit at the front. The poor sit at the back, or stand. And on the walls, there are boards painted with the Ten Commandments.

[pause]

The service is in English, from the Book of Common Prayer. There are prayers, readings from the Bible, and psalms, which everyone sings together. And there is a sermon. Sometimes the sermon is very long, an hour or even two hours. The minister speaks about God, about sin and goodness, about how people should live. Some ministers are wonderful speakers. Others are very dull. Ned sometimes falls asleep, and Margaret pokes him with her elbow.

[long-pause]

{img:family-walking-home-after-church-cheapside-sunday-afternoon-sunny}
After church, there is Sunday dinner. It is the best meal of the week. Roast beef, if they can afford it, with a pudding and vegetables, and a pie. Sometimes they invite friends or neighbours. The Dutch family from next door. Or Margaret's sister, the aunt from Southwark, with her husband and her children. The table is crowded and noisy. Everyone talks at once. And the cat sits under the table, waiting for scraps.

[pause]

In the afternoon, people rest, or go for walks. In summer, they walk in the fields outside the city walls, or along the river. Young men and women walk together, and flirt a little, under the eyes of their parents. Old people sit in the sun. Children play quietly, because it is Sunday, and loud games are not allowed. Some people read the Bible. Some people sing psalms. Some people simply sleep.

[pause]

{img:father-reading-bible-aloud-fireside-family-listening-sunday-evening}
In the evening, Thomas reads from the big family Bible, by the fire. He reads slowly, following the words with his finger. The Bible is in English, which is still quite new. For centuries, the Bible was only in Latin, and ordinary people could not understand it. Now, anyone who can read can read it for themselves. The family listens. Bess follows the words over her father's shoulder. Ned looks at the pictures.

[long-pause]

Sunday is a quiet day, and a good one. After a week of hard work in the shop, and the noise of Cheapside, it is a day of rest, of family, of prayer and of peace. And when the bells of Bow ring out at nine o'clock, and the family goes up to bed, everyone feels calm and rested, ready for another week.

[long-pause]

{img:tudor-family-music-evening-lute-viol-singing-around-table-candles}
The English in 1599 love music. Everyone sings. In the streets, in the taverns, in the churches, in the fields and at home. Foreign visitors said that England was a land of music, where even the servants and the farmers could sing. And in the evenings, many families make music together, at home, by candlelight.

[long-pause]

The most popular instrument is the lute. It is a beautiful stringed instrument, with a round, pear-shaped body, like a half melon, and a long neck. It is played by plucking the strings with the fingers. Its sound is soft and sweet. Rich people play the lute, and so do many ordinary people. In the barbers' shops, there is often a lute hanging on the wall, for customers to play while they wait their turn.

[pause]

There are also viols, which are stringed instruments played with a bow, a little like the violins and cellos of today. There are recorders, flutes and pipes. There are keyboards, called virginals, which are like small harpsichords, in a box, played with the fingers. The queen herself plays the virginals very well. She is said to practise often, alone, in her private rooms.

[pause]

{img:madrigal-singers-sitting-around-table-part-books-tudor-hall-evening}
The most fashionable music in 1599 is the madrigal. It is a song for several voices, usually four or five, singing different parts at the same time, that weave together into beautiful harmonies. The words are usually about love, nature, spring, shepherds and nymphs. After supper, friends and family sit around a table, each with a little printed book of their part, and sing madrigals together. It is a lovely way to spend an evening.

[pause]

There are many great English composers in 1599. William Byrd, who writes beautiful church music, and music for the virginals. John Dowland, who writes sad and lovely songs for the voice and the lute. One of them is called Flow, my tears, and it is so famous that everyone in London knows it. Thomas Morley, who writes cheerful madrigals. Their music is still sung and played today, all over the world.

[long-pause]

{img:ballad-seller-street-corner-singing-printed-sheets-crowd-listening}
In the streets, there is a different kind of music. Ballads. Simple songs, with many verses, telling a story. About love, about a murder, about a disaster, about a famous person, or about something funny. Ballad sellers stand on the street corners, singing their ballads loudly, and selling the printed sheets for a penny. People buy them, learn the tunes, and sing them at home and in the taverns. It is like the popular music of the day.

[pause]

Margaret has a beautiful voice. She sings while she embroiders the gloves, old songs that she learned from her mother. Greensleeves, which is said to have been written by the queen's father, King Henry the Eighth, though probably it was not. And songs about the seasons, the harvest, the green fields and lost loves. Bess has learned all of them. When they sing together, the customers in the shop stop and listen.

[pause]

{img:tudor-dance-circle-holding-hands-village-green-london-fields-music}
Dancing is also very popular. At court, the queen loves to dance. Even in her old age, she still dances. People say that she dances a lively dance, called the galliard, with great leaps and kicks, every morning, for exercise. Ordinary people dance too, at weddings, on holidays and at fairs. Country dances, in lines and circles, holding hands, skipping and turning. Everyone joins in, young and old.

[long-pause]

On the evening of Margaret's birthday, the Dutch family from next door comes to visit. Master Jacob brings his recorder. Kit plays a simple tune on a borrowed lute, which he has been secretly practising for weeks. Margaret and Bess sing. Griet and Bess dance, in the small space between the table and the fireplace. Ned recites a speech from Julius Caesar, very dramatically. Everyone claps. And Thomas sits by the fire, smiling, with the cat on his lap, thinking that there is no better music in the world than the sound of a happy home.

[long-pause]

{img:accession-day-tilt-whitehall-tiltyard-knights-pageant-crowds-november}
The year goes on. The summer ends, and the autumn comes. The leaves fall in the fields outside the city. The days get shorter. And on the seventeenth of November 1599, there is a great celebration. It is Accession Day, the day on which Queen Elizabeth became queen, forty-one years before. All over England, the bells ring, and people celebrate.

[long-pause]

In London, the biggest event of the day is the tournament, called the Accession Day Tilt. It is held at the palace of Whitehall, in Westminster, in a long open yard called the tiltyard. The queen watches from a window of the palace, with her ladies. And thousands of people come to watch, paying a few pennies to stand or sit in the stands around the yard.

[pause]

The Wood family goes too. It is a holiday, and the shops are closed. They walk all the way to Westminster, along the Strand, in the crowd. Everyone is in their best clothes. There are flags and banners everywhere. Music is playing. And when they reach the tiltyard, they find a place on the wooden stand, high enough to see everything. Bess is shivering, from the cold and from excitement.

[pause]

{img:knight-in-fantastical-costume-pageant-servants-dressed-as-characters}
Before the tournament begins, each knight arrives in a splendid procession, called a pageant. Each one has a theme, like a little play. One knight comes as a wild man of the woods, with his servants dressed as trees. Another comes as a knight from an old story, with a poet who reads a speech to the queen, explaining the meaning of his costume. Another arrives in a carriage shaped like a ship. The costumes are magnificent, covered in gold, silver, feathers and jewels.

[pause]

Each knight has a painted shield, with a picture and a motto, a short saying, which is a riddle about himself. After the tournament, these shields are hung up in a gallery of the palace, for everyone to see. People spend hours trying to understand the meanings of the pictures and mottos. Some years later, Shakespeare himself was paid to design a motto for a knight's shield, for one of these tournaments.

[long-pause]

{img:two-knights-riding-at-each-other-tilt-barrier-lances-crowd-cheering}
Then the tilting begins. Two knights ride toward each other, on either side of a long wooden barrier, holding long lances. They try to break their lances against each other's armour. It is not a real fight. The lances are made to break easily. And the knights are protected by heavy armour. But it is still very exciting. The horses thunder along the barrier. The lances crack and splinter. And the crowd roars.

[pause]

Bess does not watch the knights very much. She watches the window of the palace. And there, behind the glass, she sees a figure in white and silver, glittering with jewels. The queen. She is watching the tournament, and sometimes she claps, or waves her handkerchief. Bess cannot see her face, from so far away. But she knows that it is her. The same queen who raised her hand to them from her barge, on the river, in the summer.

[pause]

{img:winter-evening-bonfires-london-streets-bells-celebration-accession-day}
In the evening, there are bonfires in the streets of London. People dance around them, and drink to the health of the queen. The bells ring until late. And the Wood family walks home through the streets, past the bonfires, tired and happy. Ned is talking about the knights. Bess is talking about the queen. Margaret is complaining about her cold feet. And Thomas is carrying a sleepy Ned on his back, for the last part of the way.

[long-pause]

Nobody knows it yet. But this will be one of the last Accession Day Tilts of Elizabeth's reign. She will see only three more. In March 1603, she will pass away, peacefully, at her palace of Richmond, at the age of sixty-nine. And with her, the age that bears her name, the Elizabethan age, will come to an end. But tonight, in November 1599, the bells are ringing for her all over London, and the bonfires are burning bright.

[long-pause]

{img:elizabeth-funeral-procession-1603-london-crowds-mourning-later}
When Queen Elizabeth passed away in 1603, the people of London mourned. Her funeral procession through the streets was enormous. Thousands of people lined the way, dressed in black, many of them weeping. A writer said that there was such a sighing and groaning and weeping, as had never been known. Her body was buried in Westminster Abbey, where her tomb can still be seen today.

[long-pause]

She had no children. So the crown went to her cousin, King James the Sixth of Scotland, who became King James the First of England. For the first time, England and Scotland had the same king. James was very interested in the theatre. He became the patron of Shakespeare's company, which was renamed the King's Men. They performed at court many times, and Shakespeare wrote some of his greatest plays for the new king.

[pause]

Shakespeare went on writing until about 1613. In those years, he wrote Hamlet, Othello, King Lear, Macbeth, The Tempest and many more. Then he went home to Stratford, to his family and his big house, New Place. He passed away in 1616, at the age of fifty-two. Seven years later, two of his actor friends collected his plays and printed them in one big book, called the First Folio. Without it, half of his plays would have been lost forever.

[pause]

{img:globe-theatre-fire-1613-thatched-roof-smoke-crowd-safely-outside-later}
In 1613, during a performance of a play about Henry the Eighth, a small cannon was fired on stage, for a special effect. A spark landed on the thatched roof of the Globe. And within an hour, the whole theatre had burned down. Nobody was hurt, except one man whose trousers caught fire. Someone put out the flames with a bottle of ale. The Globe was rebuilt the next year, with a tiled roof this time.

[pause]

In 1642, the Puritans, who believed that plays were sinful, closed all the theatres in England. Two years later, the Globe was pulled down, and houses were built where it had stood. For almost twenty years, there were no plays in London. When the theatres opened again in 1660, women were allowed to act for the first time. And the boy actors, like the one Ned had watched, playing Calpurnia, were no longer needed.

[long-pause]

{img:great-fire-of-london-1666-st-pauls-burning-distance-night-sky-later}
London went on growing. By 1700, it had more than half a million people. It suffered terrible disasters. In 1665, a great plague. And in 1666, the Great Fire of London, which burned for four days and destroyed most of the old City. Cheapside burned. St Paul's Cathedral burned. Almost all the old timber houses, with their jetties and their signs, were lost.

[pause]

After the fire, London was rebuilt in brick and stone. The great architect Christopher Wren built a new St Paul's Cathedral, with a huge dome, which still stands on the hill today. He also built more than fifty new churches, including a new St Mary-le-Bow, whose bells still ring in Cheapside. Old London Bridge survived the fire, and stood, with its houses, until 1831, when it was replaced by a new bridge.

[pause]

{img:wren-st-pauls-dome-rebuilt-london-skyline-1700s-river-later}
The East India Company, which the merchants had begun to plan in September 1599, grew into one of the most powerful companies the world has ever seen. It traded spices, tea, silk and cotton, and later ruled large parts of India. It changed the history of Britain and of Asia, for good and for bad. And it all began with a meeting of merchants, near the Royal Exchange, in the autumn of 1599.

[long-pause]

As for Bess and Ned, they are not in the history books. But we can imagine. Perhaps Bess became a famous embroiderer, with her own shop in the Royal Exchange, and married Kit, the shy apprentice. Perhaps Ned became a boy actor, playing queens and ladies at the Globe, and later an actor in the King's Men. Perhaps he even acted in a play by Shakespeare. And perhaps, as an old man, he told his grandchildren about that afternoon in September 1599, when the greatest writer in the world asked him if he liked the play.

[long-pause]

{img:shakespeares-globe-reconstruction-bankside-today-white-walls-thatched-roof}
Today, London is one of the biggest cities in the world, with almost nine million people. Most of Elizabethan London is gone, burned in the Great Fire, or pulled down and rebuilt over the centuries. But if you know where to look, you can still find traces of the city of 1599. And in one place, you can even step back into it.

[long-pause]

On Bankside, by the Thames, very close to where the original stood, there is a new Globe Theatre. It was built in the 1990s, as an exact copy of the old one, as far as historians could work it out. It was the dream of an American actor and director, called Sam Wanamaker, who spent more than twenty years raising money for it. Sadly, he passed away a few years before it opened, in 1997.

[pause]

The new Globe is built of oak, with wooden pegs, not nails, just like the original. It has white plaster walls and a thatched roof, the first thatched roof allowed in London since the Great Fire. It has three galleries, an open yard, and a stage with two painted pillars and a painted heaven. And every summer, Shakespeare's plays are performed there, in the afternoon and the evening, in the open air, just as they were in 1599.

[pause]

{img:groundlings-standing-yard-modern-globe-play-performance-summer-evening}
You can stand in the yard, as a groundling, for only a few pounds. You can sit in the galleries, on wooden benches. If it rains, the groundlings get wet. The actors speak directly to the audience, and the audience reacts, laughing, gasping and sometimes calling out. And at the end of every play, even the saddest, the actors dance a jig. It is the closest you can get to the theatre of Shakespeare's time.

[pause]

Not far from the Globe, the remains of the Rose Theatre, an older playhouse from Shakespeare's time, were discovered in 1989, under an office building. You can visit them, in a dark basement, with lights marking out the shape of the old theatre. And in 2008, archaeologists found the remains of The Theatre, the first playhouse, whose timber was used to build the Globe, under a street in the north of London.

[long-pause]

{img:tower-of-london-today-white-tower-visitors-beefeaters-ravens-sunny}
The Tower of London still stands by the river, as it did in 1599. Its great White Tower rises above the walls. Visitors from all over the world come to see the crown jewels, the old armour and the ravens, which live there. The guards, called Yeomen Warders or Beefeaters, still wear red and black uniforms in the Tudor style. And they tell the stories of the Tower, every day, to crowds of visitors.

[pause]

In Cheapside, the old street is now full of modern offices and shops. But the church of St Mary-le-Bow is still there, rebuilt by Wren after the Great Fire, and its bells still ring. And under the modern streets, archaeologists have found many traces of the old city. In 1912, workmen found a great hoard of jewellery under a house in Cheapside. Hundreds of pieces of gold, pearls and precious stones, from the time of Elizabeth and James. It is called the Cheapside Hoard. Nobody knows who hid it, or why.

[pause]

{img:cheapside-hoard-jewels-museum-london-gold-pearls-gems-display-today}
In the Museum of London, you can see the Cheapside Hoard. Rings, necklaces, brooches and pendants, glittering in their glass cases. And you can also see ordinary things from the time of Elizabeth. Shoes, gloves, combs, toys, pots and pans. A child's leather shoe. A pair of fine embroidered gloves, perhaps made by a glover in Cheapside, long ago.

[long-pause]

And in Westminster Abbey, Queen Elizabeth lies in her tomb, under a beautiful white marble statue of herself, with her crown and her ruff. Visitors stand beside it, and look at her calm stone face. On the tomb, there is a Latin inscription. And nearby, there is another, in English, from later times. It says, simply, that she and her sister Mary, who were rivals in life, lie here together, in the hope of the resurrection. It is a peaceful place, after a long and eventful reign.

[long-pause]

{img:london-1599-evening-thames-sunset-london-bridge-spires-boats-golden}
So we leave London tonight, in the year 1599, as the evening falls. The sun is setting over the river, in the west, behind the palaces of the Strand. The water of the Thames turns red and gold. The last boats are crossing to Southwark. And the swans are gliding slowly toward the reeds, to sleep.

[long-pause]

On Bankside, the flag on the roof of the Globe has been taken down. The play is over. The crowds have gone home, across the bridge and in the boats. The actors are taking off their costumes and their paint. And the empty theatre stands quietly by the river, its round shape dark against the sky, waiting for tomorrow's play.

[pause]

{img:cheapside-dusk-shops-closing-shutters-lantern-glow-quiet-street}
In Cheapside, the shops are closing. The shutters are being pulled up and locked. The apprentices are sweeping the doorsteps one last time. In the glover's house, Margaret is putting away her embroidery, and Thomas is counting the money from the day. Joan is in the kitchen, stirring a pot of soup. And the smell of supper drifts out into the evening street.

[long-pause]

The bells of Bow ring out for the curfew. Nine o'clock. The gates of the City close. The watchmen begin their rounds, with their lanterns and their bells, calling out softly. Nine o'clock, and a fair night, and all is well. In the houses, the fires are covered. The candles are lit, one by one, in the upstairs windows. And then, one by one, they go out again.

High up under the roof, in a small room, a girl of thirteen is looking out of her little window. Over the rooftops of Cheapside, she can see the dark tower of St Paul's against the stars. She can hear the river, far away, and the soft calls of the watchmen. She is thinking about the queen in her golden barge, and the play at the Globe, and the globe of Master Pieter, spinning under her finger. And she is smiling.

In the next room, her brother is already asleep, with a little printed play under his pillow. The cat is curled up at the foot of his bed. Downstairs, the fire glows softly under its cover of ashes. The great city is quiet now. And over all of London, over the river and the bridge, the churches and the theatres, the palaces and the little timber houses, the moon is rising, calm and silver, in the autumn sky.
