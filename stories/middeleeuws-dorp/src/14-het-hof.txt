Several times a year, in Elton, there is the manor court: the hallmote.

It is held in the great hall of the manor house, or sometimes, in summer, outside, under a great tree, on the green. And every villager must come. The beadle goes round the village, the day before, and tells everyone, and those who do not come, without a good reason, are fined.

--

The court is led by the steward, the abbot's man, who rides over from Ramsey, with a clerk, who writes everything down, on the parchment roll. But the judgements are made by the villagers themselves: a group of twelve, or so, of the older, and more respected men, called the jurors, who know the customs of the village, and what is fair, and they decide.

The court deals with everything that happens, in the village: with land, and inheritance, and marriage, and quarrels, and debts, and damage, and trespass, and the breaking of the village rules.

{img:clerk-writing-court-roll-quill-parchment-table-steward-listening}
When a villager dies, his son, or his widow, comes to the court, to take over his land, and pays a fine, to the lord, and the clerk writes it down. When a young man wants to marry, and needs land, the court arranges it. When a villein's daughter marries, her father pays the merchet. When someone wants to leave the village, to live somewhere else, they must ask permission, and pay a little, every year.

--

And then there are the offences, the little crimes of village life, which fill the court rolls of Elton, year after year.

Someone's cow got into the lord's wheat, and ate it. Someone's pigs got into the meadow. Someone ploughed a furrow too far, into their neighbour's strip. Someone cut wood, in the lord's wood, without permission. Someone did not come to work, on the lord's land, when they should have. Someone's ale was too weak, or was sold in a short measure. Someone left a dungheap, in the lane, or did not mend their fence. Someone called their neighbour a thief, in a quarrel, at the well.

{img:villagers-arguing-politely-court-witnesses-jurors-medieval-hall}
For each of these things, the court gives a small fine, a few pennies, or sixpence, or a shilling, or orders that the damage be paid for, or the fence be mended, by a certain day. And the clerk writes it all down, in his neat Latin: John, son of Richard, for his cows in the lord's corn, sixpence.

--

The court also makes the village rules, the bylaws: when the harvest will begin, and nobody may start before, and who may glean, in the fields, after the harvest, and when the animals may go onto the stubble, and how many each family may have, on the common, and who must keep the ditches clean.

And thanks to the court rolls, of Elton, and many other villages, we know about these little things, the cows, and the fences, and the quarrels, and the ale, of ordinary people, seven hundred years ago, which, without them, would have been forgotten, long ago.

{img:medieval-village-ale-tasters-testing-ale-cup-doorway-bush-sign}
There are also ale-tasters, two of them, chosen every year, from the villagers, whose job is to go round, and taste the ale, of every ale-wife, when she has brewed, and make sure that it is good, and sold at the right price, and in honest measures. It sounds like a pleasant job. But the ale-tasters must report their neighbours, and their friends, to the court, if the ale is bad, and that is not always easy.
