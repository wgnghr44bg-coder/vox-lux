Around Elton lie the great open fields.

There are two or three of them. Each is huge, hundreds of acres. There are no hedges or fences or walls. They stretch away from the village across the gently rolling land, all the way to the woods.

--

Each big field is divided into sections. Each section is divided into long narrow strips, side by side. Each strip is about two hundred metres long and only a few metres wide. A strip is about as much land as a team of oxen can plough in one day.

The strips are ploughed the same way year after year. Slowly each strip becomes a long low ridge, with a ditch on each side for the water to drain away. This makes a pattern like long gentle waves across the fields. In many places in England, you can still see it today. It shows in the grass when the sun is low in the evening. Or after a light fall of snow. More than seven hundred years later.

{img:ridge-and-furrow-pasture-evening-low-sun-long-shadows-sheep-today}
Each family's land is not in one piece. It is spread out in strips all over the fields. A strip here and two strips there and another far away. That way everyone gets a share of the good land and the poor land. The land by the river and the land on the hill.

--

The whole village farms the fields together. One year, one field is sown with wheat in the autumn. Another field is sown with barley and oats and beans in the spring. And the third field rests for a year. The animals graze on it and leave their dung, to make it rich again. The next year they change round.

So everyone must plough and sow and harvest at the same time. And everyone must agree on the rules. When can the animals go onto the fields after harvest? Who can cut the hay in the meadows, and how much? Agreeing is not always easy.

{img:medieval-ploughman-heavy-plough-eight-oxen-ploughboy-goad-furrow}
The ploughing is done with a heavy wooden plough with an iron blade. A team of oxen pulls it, sometimes eight of them, in pairs. A ploughman holds the handles. A boy walks beside the oxen with a long stick. He sings and calls to them by their names, to keep them walking straight.

--

It is slow work. The oxen walk slowly and steadily down the long strip. They turn at the end and come back. Behind them the earth turns over, dark and rich, smelling of soil. Black birds and gulls follow behind, picking up worms.

Few families have a whole team of oxen. So neighbours join their oxen together. And they plough each other's strips in turn.

{img:medieval-sower-scattering-seed-basket-field-birds-boy-sling}
Then the sower walks along the ploughed strips. A bag of seed hangs from his shoulder. He throws the seed by hand in great sweeps, from side to side. Behind him a horse pulls a heavy frame with teeth. It covers the seed with earth. And children run up and down the fields with slings and clappers. They chase away the crows and pigeons that want to eat the seed.

--

And then everyone waits. They watch the sky. They hope for gentle rain and warm sun. And they pray for a good harvest.
