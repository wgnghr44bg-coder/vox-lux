Today, Elton is still there.

It is still a small village, in the countryside of Cambridgeshire, beside the river Nene, with about seven hundred people. Its houses are built, now, of the pale, golden-grey local stone, with roofs of stone slates, and thatch, and gardens full of flowers, along the winding lanes, and around the little green.

--

The church of All Saints still stands, in the middle of the village, larger than in 1300, with a tall tower, added in later centuries, and in the churchyard, the two old Anglo-Saxon crosses still stand, weathered, and worn, among the gravestones, and the yew trees, as they stood when the villagers of 1300 passed them, on their way to mass.

Down by the river, there is still a mill, at Elton, a later one, built of stone, beside the weir, where the water still pours over, and the river still winds, slowly, through the green meadows, with their willows, and their reeds, and the cows still graze, in the mist, on summer mornings.

Near the village stands Elton Hall, a great country house, with towers, and gardens, built over many centuries, partly on the site of an old manor house, and still lived in, today, by the same family, who have owned it for more than three hundred and fifty years.

--

And in the fields, and the meadows, around Elton, and all over the English Midlands, if you know how to look, you can still see the old ridge and furrow, the long, gentle waves, in the grass, left by the ploughs of the medieval villagers, in their strips, in the great open fields, seven hundred years ago.

The court rolls, and the accounts, of Elton, are kept, today, in the archives, carefully stored, in boxes, in the dry, and the dark. Historians have read them, and translated them, from their Latin, and written books about them, so that the people of Elton, in the year 1300, the reeves, and the ale-wives, and the millers, and the ploughmen, and their quarrels, and their cows, and their marriages, are not forgotten.

So we leave Elton, tonight, in the year 1300, as the evening falls, at the end of a summer day.

--

The ploughmen are coming home, from the fields, with their oxen, slowly, along the lane, and the smoke is rising, from the thatched roofs, and the women are standing in the doorways, calling the children in. The geese are being driven home, from the green, hissing, and grumbling. The church bell is ringing, for vespers, over the fields. Down by the river, the mill wheel has stopped, and the water is pouring, softly, over the weir, and the mist is rising, from the meadows, as the light fades, and the first stars come out, over the quiet village, beside the river.
