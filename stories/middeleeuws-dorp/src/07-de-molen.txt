Down by the river Nene, at Elton, there is the mill.

It is a wooden building, with a thatched roof, standing beside a channel, cut from the river, called the mill race, where the water is held back, by a dam, and a sluice gate, and then let through, to turn the great wooden water wheel, on the side of the mill.

--

The wheel turns, slowly, steadily, creaking, and splashing, and dripping, and inside the mill, its turning is passed, by wooden gears, with wooden teeth, to the millstones: two great round stones, one on top of the other, the bottom one still, and the top one turning, grinding the grain, between them, into flour.

The grain is poured into a wooden hopper, above the stones, and trickles down, through a hole, in the middle of the top stone, and is ground, and the flour comes out, at the edges, soft, and white, and warm, and falls into a wooden trough, and into the sacks. And the whole mill is filled with a soft, rumbling, humming sound, and the air is full of fine white dust, which settles on everything, and on the miller, so that he is white, all over, from his hair to his boots.

{img:miller-floury-clothes-taking-toll-grain-bowl-villager-sack}
The mill belongs to the lord. And the villagers must bring their grain here, to be ground, and nowhere else. And they must pay the miller, by letting him keep a part of their grain: one sixteenth, or one twentieth, measured out with a special bowl, called the toll dish.

--

The villagers are not allowed to grind their own grain, at home, with hand mills, called querns. If they are caught doing it, they are fined, at the manor court. And many of them do it anyway, secretly, because it is cheaper.

The miller is not always popular. People suspect that he takes more than his share, that his toll dish is a little too big, or that he mixes a little sand into the flour, or keeps back some of the good flour, for himself. There are many old stories, and jokes, and songs, about dishonest millers. In the Canterbury Tales, written by Geoffrey Chaucer, about ninety years later, there is a miller, who has a thumb of gold: that is, he knows exactly how to cheat his customers, by pressing his thumb on the scales.

{img:river-nene-mill-pond-reflections-ducks-evening-calm-willows}
But the mill is also a meeting place. While they wait for their grain to be ground, the villagers sit on the bank, outside, in the sunshine, and talk, and share the news, and the children play, by the water, and watch the great wheel turn, and fish in the mill pond, for eels, and roach, and pike.

--

The mill pond is full of fish, and the lord takes his eels, as part of the rent: hundreds, and thousands of eels, every year, caught in wicker traps, set in the mill race, and sent to the abbey, for the monks to eat, on the days when they may not eat meat.

And in the evening, when the work is done, the miller closes the sluice gate, and the great wheel slows down, and stops, and the mill falls silent, and the only sound is the water, running over the dam, and the frogs, and the moorhens, calling, in the reeds.
