In August, it is harvest time, the most important time of the whole year.

The corn in the fields has turned from green, to gold. The wheat, and the rye, and the barley, and the oats, stand tall, and heavy, and ripe, rustling, in the warm wind, under a blue sky. The whole year depends on the next few weeks. If the harvest is good, there will be bread, all winter. If it is bad, there will be hunger.

--

On the first of August, there is Lammas, loaf mass, when the first loaf, made from the new wheat, is brought to the church, and blessed, at the mass, to give thanks for the harvest that is coming.

And then the harvest begins. The court has decided the day, and nobody may start before it. And then everyone, every man, and woman, and child, who can work, goes out, into the fields, from dawn until dusk, for weeks.

{img:reapers-row-sickles-cutting-wheat-binders-following-stooks-sun}
The corn is cut with sickles: small, curved blades, with a wooden handle, held in one hand, while the other hand grasps a bunch of stalks, and the reaper cuts them, low, near the ground, and lays them down, in a row. It is slow, back-breaking work, bending over, all day long, in the heat, with the sweat running down, and the dust, and the scratching straw.

--

Behind the reapers come the binders, often the women, who gather up the cut corn, and tie it, into sheaves, with a twist of straw, and then stand the sheaves up, together, in little groups, called stooks, leaning against each other, with the ears of corn at the top, so that they can dry, in the sun, and the wind.

The lord's corn must be harvested first. And for this, there are special days, called boon works, when every household in the village must send everyone who can work, to reap the lord's corn, all together, sometimes hundreds of people, in the lord's fields, at once.

{img:boon-work-harvest-lunch-field-bread-cheese-ale-herrings-workers-resting}
On the boon days, the lord gives the workers food, and drink, in the fields: bread, and cheese, and herrings, and ale, and sometimes meat, and soup. On the great boon days, at Elton, there is a feast, at midday, in the field, with the workers sitting down, in the stubble, in the shade of the stooks, and eating, and drinking, and resting, together, in the heat.

--

And then, when the corn has dried, it is loaded onto the carts, and carried, home, to the barns, and the stack yards, and piled up, in great stacks, thatched, against the rain, until it can be threshed, in the winter.

And after the harvest, the poor of the village, the old, and the widows, and the children, are allowed to go into the fields, and glean: to pick up the ears of corn that have been left behind, on the ground, one by one, and take them home, in their aprons, and their baskets. It is a precious right, and the court makes sure that it is respected.

{img:gleaners-women-children-stubble-field-evening-picking-ears-corn}
When the last sheaf is cut, in the last field, there is a great shout, and the last sheaf is decorated, with flowers, and ribbons, and carried home, in triumph, on the last cart, with the reapers riding on top, singing.

--

And then, there is the harvest supper, the greatest feast of the year, with meat, and pies, and bread, and cheese, and ale, and music, and dancing, in the lord's barn, or on the green, until late into the night, under the harvest moon, rising, huge, and golden, over the stubble fields.
