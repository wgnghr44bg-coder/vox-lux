On the twenty-ninth of September, it is Michaelmas, the feast of Saint Michael the Archangel, and the end of the farming year.

The harvest is in. The corn is in the barns, and the stack yards. The apples, and the pears, are being picked, in the orchards, and stored, in lofts, on straw. The nuts are falling, in the woods. And the swallows are gathering, on the church roof, getting ready to fly away, for the winter.

--

At Michaelmas, the reeve gives his accounts, to the clerk, from the abbey, and a new reeve is chosen, for the new year, and the rents are paid, and the servants are hired, for the next year. And the villagers eat a goose, at Michaelmas, fattened on the stubble, in the fields, after the harvest, for luck, and for the feast.

And in the barns, the threshing begins.

{img:two-men-threshing-flails-barn-floor-rhythm-grain-chaff}
All through the autumn, and the winter, the men thresh the corn, on the floor of the barns, with flails: two sticks, joined together, with a leather strap, one long, held in the hands, and one short, which swings round, and beats the ears of corn, on the floor, to knock out the grain.

--

Two or three men stand in a circle, and beat, in turn, in a steady rhythm, thump, thump, thump, all day long, so that, all through the autumn, and winter, you can hear the sound of the flails, from the barns, all over the village.

Then, the grain is winnowed: on a windy day, with the barn doors open, at both ends, so that the wind blows through, the grain is tossed up, into the air, from a wide, shallow basket, and the wind blows away the chaff, the light husks, and the dust, and the heavy grain falls back, clean, into the basket.

{img:winnowing-grain-basket-barn-doors-open-wind-chaff-flying-light}
In the autumn, too, the pigs are taken into the woods, to feed on the acorns, and the beechnuts, which fall, from the trees, in October, and November, and grow fat, for the winter. This is called pannage, and the villagers pay the lord a few pennies, for each pig.

--

And the fields are ploughed again, and the wheat, and the rye, are sown, for the next year's harvest, and the cycle begins again.

So the year turns, in Elton, as it has turned for centuries: from the ploughing, to the sowing, to the hay, to the harvest, to the threshing, and back to the ploughing. And the villagers know it, and feel it, in their bones, and their hands, and their hearts, so that every season has its work, and its feasts, and its songs, and its own particular light, and smell, and sound.
