Tonight we are going more than seven hundred years back, to a small village in the middle of England, in the year 1300.

[long-pause]

It is early morning, in spring. The sun is rising over a wide, green valley, where a slow river winds, between meadows, and willows, and reeds. Mist lies on the water, and over the low fields, and the cows stand in it, up to their knees, quietly grazing. A heron flies slowly over the river, and the cuckoo is calling, from the woods, on the hill.

[pause]

And on a gentle rise, above the river, there is a village: a cluster of houses, with thatched roofs, and gardens, and fences, along a winding lane, with a stone church, with a little tower, in the middle, and smoke rising, from the roofs, into the still morning air.

[pause]

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can easily find the next one. And now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

The village is called Elton. It lies in Huntingdonshire, in the east of England, beside the river Nene, about ten miles from the town of Peterborough, with its great abbey church.

[pause]

{img:medieval-cottages-lane-thatched-roofs-crofts-gardens-dawn-smoke}
It is not a famous place. Nothing very important ever happened here. It is simply a village, like thousands of other villages, all over England, in the year 1300, where people were born, and grew up, and worked in the fields, and married, and had children, and grew old.

[pause]

But we know a great deal about Elton. Because the village belonged to a great monastery, Ramsey Abbey, and the monks of Ramsey kept careful records, written in Latin, on long rolls of parchment: records of the manor court, where the villagers' quarrels, and fines, and agreements, were written down, and the accounts of the farm, every year, telling how much was sown, and harvested, and sold, and spent.

[long-pause]

Many of these rolls survive, today, in archives, and libraries. And in them, we can still read the names of the people of Elton, more than seven hundred years ago, and the little stories of their lives: who brewed ale, and who let their cows stray into the lord's corn, who married, and who built a new house, and who quarrelled with their neighbour, over a fence.

[pause]

{img:monk-scribe-writing-parchment-roll-court-record-candle-abbey}
Tonight, we are going to spend a whole year in Elton, from spring to spring, from the ploughing to the harvest. We will walk through the open fields, and the meadows, and visit the mill, and the church, and the manor house. We will meet the reeve, and the miller, and the ale-wife, and the priest. We will sit in the manor court, and dance on Midsummer's Eve, and bring in the harvest, and ride to the great fair, at Saint Ives.

[long-pause]

So let us walk down the lane, into the village, in the early morning, with the dew on the grass, and the smell of woodsmoke, and the sound of a cock crowing, and the church bell ringing, softly, for the morning prayers.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-village-plan-lane-tofts-crofts-houses-gardens-church-green}
Let us walk through Elton, in the year 1300.

[pause]

The village is built along a winding lane, which runs up from the river, past the church, and on toward the fields, and the woods. On each side of the lane, there are the houses of the villagers, about a hundred and twenty households, perhaps five hundred, or six hundred people, in all.

[long-pause]

Each house stands on its own little plot of land, called a toft, with a fence, or a hedge, or a low wall, around it, and a gate onto the lane. Behind the house, there is a long, narrow garden, called a croft, stretching back, where the family grows vegetables, and herbs, and keeps a few fruit trees, and hens, and geese, and perhaps a pig, and a cow.

[pause]

The houses are long, and low, built of a frame of timber, with walls of wattle, and daub: woven hazel branches, covered with a mixture of clay, and straw, and dung, and plastered, and whitewashed. The roofs are thatched, with straw, or reeds, from the river, thick, and steep, coming down low, over the walls.

[pause]

{img:medieval-cruck-frame-house-wattle-daub-thatch-garden-hens}
In the middle of the village, there is the church, built of the pale grey stone of the district, with a little tower, and a churchyard, around it, with old yew trees, and the graves of the villagers, with no gravestones, only little mounds, in the grass.

[long-pause]

Near the church, by the river, there is the manor house, the lord's house: a bigger building, of stone, and timber, with a great hall, and barns, and stables, and a dovecote, and a courtyard, and a garden, surrounded by a wall. But the lord himself, the Abbot of Ramsey, does not live here. He lives at his great abbey, at Ramsey, about fifteen miles away, across the fens. The manor is run, for him, by his officials.

[pause]

And down by the river, there is the mill, with its great wooden water wheel, turning slowly, in the stream, and the miller's house, beside it.

[pause]

{img:medieval-watermill-wooden-wheel-river-nene-willows-morning}
Around the village, in every direction, there are the fields: great, wide, open fields, with no hedges, divided into long, narrow strips, where the villagers grow their corn. And beyond the fields, there are the meadows, by the river, and the common pasture, where the animals graze, and the woods, on the higher ground, where the pigs root for acorns, and the villagers gather firewood.

[long-pause]

The village is busy, from dawn to dusk, with people, and animals: children driving geese, along the lane, and women carrying water, from the well, and men going out to the fields, with their oxen, and ploughs, and dogs barking, and pigs rooting in the mud, and the smith hammering, at his forge, and the bell of the church, ringing the hours.

[pause]

It is a small world, but a complete one. Most of the people of Elton will live their whole lives here, and never travel more than a day's walk away. Everything they need is here, or close by: their food, and their work, and their families, and their friends, and their church, and their graves.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-village-smithy-forge-glowing-anvil-smith-hammer-ploughshare}
Not everyone in Elton is a farmer. There are craftsmen, too, and the most important of them is the smith.

[pause]

The smithy stands at the edge of the village, by the lane, a low building, of stone, and timber, open at the front, with a great hearth, of stone, inside, where the fire glows, red, and orange, and the smith's boy pumps the bellows, a great bag of leather, which breathes air into the fire, with a wheeze, and a roar, so that the charcoal glows, white hot.

[long-pause]

The smith is a big man, with strong arms, and a leather apron, burnt with little holes, by the sparks. He takes the iron, from the fire, with his long tongs, glowing, yellow, and red, and lays it on the anvil, and strikes it, with his hammer, ringing, again, and again, so that the sparks fly, and the iron bends, and flattens, and takes shape, under his blows. Then he plunges it into a tub of water, where it hisses, and steams.

[pause]

He makes, and mends, everything of iron that the village needs: the shares, and the coulters, of the ploughs, which wear down, every season, and must be sharpened, and remade, and the sickles, and the scythes, and the spades, and the forks, and the axes, and the knives, and the nails, and the hinges, and the locks, and the keys, and the pots, and the chains, and the horseshoes.

[pause]

{img:smith-shoeing-horse-smithy-doorway-villagers-waiting-chatting}
And he shoes the horses, of the manor, and the villagers, lifting their great hooves, between his knees, and fitting the hot shoe, so that it smokes, and smells, and nailing it on.

[long-pause]

The smithy, like the mill, is a meeting place. On a cold morning, people come, and stand around the warm hearth, and talk, while they wait for their tools to be mended, and the children come, to watch the sparks fly, and the iron glow.

[pause]

Then there is the carpenter. Almost everything, in Elton, is made of wood: the houses, and the barns, and the carts, and the ploughs, and the harrows, and the furniture, and the bowls, and the spoons, and the buckets, and the barrels, and the mill wheel, and its gears.

[pause]

{img:medieval-carpenter-shaping-timber-adze-house-frame-workshop-shavings}
The carpenter works with axes, and adzes, and saws, and chisels, and augers, which bore holes, and he knows every kind of wood, and what it is good for: oak, for the beams of the houses, and the barns, strong, and lasting for centuries; ash, for the handles of tools, and the shafts of carts, springy, and tough; elm, for the hubs of wheels, and the bottoms of carts, because it does not split; willow, and hazel, for baskets, and wattle, and hurdles.

[long-pause]

When a villager builds a new house, the carpenter cuts the great timbers, in his yard, and fits them together, with joints, and wooden pegs, without nails, and then, on one day, all the neighbours come, and help to raise the frame, pulling on ropes, and pushing with poles, until it stands upright, and then they have a little feast, to celebrate.

[pause]

There are other craftsmen too, in Elton, and the villages around: the thatcher, who climbs the roofs, with his bundles of straw, and reed, and his wooden mallet, and his hazel spars; and the wheelwright; and the cooper, who makes barrels; and the potter; and the weaver; and the tailor; and the shoemaker. And many of the villagers have a little craft, of their own, as well as their land.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-villagers-gathered-church-porch-sunday-men-women-children}
Who are the people of Elton?

[pause]

Most of them are peasants: farmers, who work the land, and grow their own food. But they are not all the same. Some are richer, and some are poorer, and some are free, and some are not.

[long-pause]

The most important difference, in a village like Elton, is between the free, and the unfree. About half of the families of Elton are villeins, or serfs. They are not slaves. They have their own houses, and their own land, and their own animals, and their own money, and they can buy, and sell, and marry, and leave their land to their children. But they are bound to the manor, and to the lord.

[pause]

They must work for the lord, on his own land, for a certain number of days, every week, and more at harvest time. They must pay him rents, in money, and in kind, in hens, at Christmas, and eggs, at Easter. They must grind their corn at his mill, and pay for it. They must have his permission to leave the village, or to marry their daughters, and pay a fine for it, called merchet. And when they die, their family must give the lord their best animal, called a heriot.

[pause]

{img:medieval-peasant-family-doorway-cottage-father-mother-children-ox}
The free tenants are different. They pay rent, for their land, but they do not have to work on the lord's land, or ask his permission to marry, and they can leave, when they like.

[long-pause]

And among the villagers, there are richer, and poorer families. The richest villeins hold a whole virgate of land: about thirty acres, in strips, scattered across the fields, enough to feed a large family, and to sell some corn, at the market, and to keep a team of oxen, or share one with their neighbours. Others hold half a virgate, or less. And the poorest, the cottars, have only a cottage, and a garden, and a few acres, or none at all, and must work for others, for wages, to live.

[pause]

So, in Elton, there are some families who are quite comfortable, with big houses, and barns, and cattle, and servants, and others who are very poor, and live from day to day.

[pause]

{img:medieval-village-woman-spinning-distaff-doorway-chatting-neighbour}
The names of the people of Elton, in the court rolls, sound strange, and familiar, at the same time. There are Johns, and Williams, and Richards, and Roberts, and Henrys, and Agneses, and Alices, and Emmas, and Matildas, and Christinas. And their surnames, names like these, tell us about them: John Carpenter, and Richard Smith, and Robert Miller, who have a trade. Or Henry atte Brook, who lives by the brook, and Alice atte Green, by the green. Or Reginald Child, and Gilbert Dunning, and Nicholas Blaccalf.

[long-pause]

Many of the families have lived in Elton for generations, and are related to each other, by marriage, and by blood, and everybody knows everybody, and everybody knows everybody's business. The same family names appear, again and again, in the rolls, for a hundred years: fathers, and sons, and grandsons, working the same strips of land, in the same fields, and living in the same houses, along the same lane, beside the same river.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-open-field-strips-ridge-and-furrow-ploughmen-oxen-wide-sky}
Around Elton, there are the great open fields.

[pause]

There are two of them, or three, huge fields, each of hundreds of acres, without any hedges, or fences, or walls, stretching away, from the village, across the gently rolling land, to the woods, and the edges of the parish.

[long-pause]

Each great field is divided into smaller sections, called furlongs, and each furlong is divided into long, narrow strips, side by side, each about a furlong long, two hundred metres, and only a few metres wide. A strip is about as much land as a team of oxen can plough, in one day.

[pause]

The strips are ploughed, always in the same way, year after year, so that the soil is turned toward the middle, and each strip becomes a long, low ridge, with a furrow, on each side, for the water to drain away. This makes a pattern, called ridge and furrow, like long, gentle waves, across the fields. And in many places, in the Midlands of England, you can still see it, today, in the grass of the meadows, when the sun is low, in the evening, or after a light fall of snow, more than seven hundred years later.

[pause]

{img:ridge-and-furrow-pasture-evening-low-sun-long-shadows-sheep-today}
Each family's land is not in one piece, but scattered, in strips, all over the fields: a strip here, and two strips there, and another, far away, on the other side of the village. This way, everyone has a share of the good land, and the poor land, the land by the river, and the land on the hill.

[long-pause]

The fields are farmed together, by the whole village. One year, one field is sown with wheat, and rye, in the autumn. Another field is sown with barley, and oats, and peas, and beans, in the spring. And the third field is left fallow, to rest, for a year, and the animals graze on it, and leave their dung, to make it fertile again. And the next year, they change round.

[pause]

So everyone must plough, and sow, and harvest, at the same time, and everyone must agree on the rules: when the animals can go onto the stubble, after the harvest, and when they must be taken off, and who can cut the hay, in the meadows, and how much.

[pause]

{img:medieval-ploughman-heavy-plough-eight-oxen-ploughboy-goad-furrow}
The ploughing is done with a heavy wooden plough, with an iron blade, and an iron share, pulled by a team of oxen, sometimes eight of them, in pairs, yoked together, with a ploughman, holding the handles, and a ploughboy, walking beside the oxen, with a long stick, called a goad, singing, and calling to them, by their names, to keep them walking straight.

[long-pause]

It is slow work. The oxen walk slowly, steadily, down the long strip, and turn, at the end, on a piece of land called the headland, and come back. And behind them, the earth turns over, dark, and rich, and smelling of the soil, and the rooks, and the gulls, follow behind, picking up the worms.

[pause]

Few families have a whole team of oxen. So the villagers share them, joining their oxen together, with their neighbours, to make a team, and ploughing each other's strips, in turn.

[pause]

{img:medieval-sower-scattering-seed-basket-field-birds-boy-sling}
Then, the sower walks along the ploughed strips, with a basket, or a bag of seed, hanging from his shoulder, and scatters the seed, by hand, in great sweeps, from side to side, as he walks. And behind him, the harrow, a heavy frame of wood, with wooden, or iron teeth, pulled by a horse, covers the seed with earth. And the children run up and down the fields, with slings, and clappers, and stones, to scare away the crows, and the pigeons, which would eat the seed.

[long-pause]

And then, everyone waits, and watches the sky, and hopes for gentle rain, and warm sun, and prays for a good harvest.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-spring-blossom-village-lambs-meadow-children-primroses}
In Elton, the year begins in the spring.

[pause]

In March, the days grow longer, and the earth begins to warm. The first lambs are born, in the sheepfolds, and stagger about, on their long legs, and the primroses, and the violets, flower, along the banks, and the hedges, and under the trees, in the woods. The rooks are building their nests, in the tall elms, around the church, cawing, and quarrelling, all day long.

[long-pause]

On the twenty-fifth of March, there is Lady Day, the feast of the Annunciation, when the angel came to Mary. In the year 1300, in England, this is the first day of the new year. Rents are paid, on Lady Day, and new agreements made.

[pause]

In the fields, the ploughing, and sowing, of the spring crops go on: barley, and oats, and peas, and beans, and vetch, for the animals. The men are out in the fields, from dawn till dusk, with the oxen, and the ploughs, and the harrows.

[pause]

{img:medieval-women-planting-garden-croft-beans-leeks-cabbages-spring}
In the gardens, the crofts, behind the houses, the women plant the vegetables: onions, and leeks, and garlic, and cabbages, and beans, and peas, and parsley, and herbs, for cooking, and for medicine, and flowers, for the church: lilies, and roses, and marigolds.

[long-pause]

The animals, which have spent the winter in the barns, and the yards, eating hay, and straw, are let out, onto the pastures, and the meadows, thin, and stiff, and they kick up their heels, and run, in the fresh, green grass.

[pause]

The hens begin to lay again, and the geese sit on their nests, and soon there are goslings, and chicks, and ducklings, running about, in the yards, and the lanes, and on the green, and the children are given the job of looking after them, and keeping them away from the foxes, and the crows.

[pause]

{img:easter-morning-medieval-church-villagers-candles-flowers-sunrise}
Then comes Easter, the greatest feast of the year.

[long-pause]

During Lent, the forty days before Easter, the villagers have eaten no meat, and no eggs, and no butter, and no cheese, only bread, and fish, and vegetables, and beans. It is a long, hungry time, at the end of the winter, when the stores are running low.

[pause]

And on Easter Sunday, everyone goes to church, in their best clothes, early in the morning, and the church is full of candles, and flowers, and the bells ring, and the priest sings the Easter mass. And afterwards, at home, there is a feast, with meat, and eggs, the first for weeks, and the villagers bring eggs, to the lord's manor, as part of their rent, and to the priest, as a gift.

[long-pause]

And the children play games with the eggs, rolling them down the hills, and the grass banks, to see whose egg goes furthest, without breaking, as children in some parts of England still do, on Easter Monday, today.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-children-playing-village-green-hoops-ball-hobby-horse}
Let us think about the children of Elton.

[pause]

There are a great many of them. Families have many children, and the lanes, and the green, and the riverbank, are full of them, playing, and running, and shouting, and working.

[long-pause]

When a baby is born, at home, with the help of the midwife, and the women of the family, and the neighbours, it is washed, and rubbed with salt, and wrapped, tightly, in strips of linen, swaddling bands, and carried to the church, the same day, or the next, to be baptised. Its godparents choose its name, often their own name, so that there are many Johns, and many Agneses, in Elton.

[pause]

The babies are carried everywhere, by their mothers, and their sisters, and lie in wooden cradles, by the fire, rocked by the older children.

[pause]

{img:medieval-mother-cradle-baby-cottage-fire-older-sister-rocking}
And as soon as they can walk, the children play. They play with balls, made of leather, stuffed with rags, or with pigs' bladders, blown up. They play with tops, and hoops, and hobby horses, sticks with a carved horse's head, and with dolls, of wood, and cloth. They play hide and seek, and blind man's buff, and leapfrog, and tag, and fight, with wooden swords, and play at being knights, and ladies.

[long-pause]

They climb the trees, and swim in the river, in summer, and fish for minnows, and catch frogs, and look for birds' nests, in the hedges. They make whistles, from willow, and boats, from bark, and sail them, on the mill stream. In winter, they slide on the frozen ponds.

[pause]

But they also work, from an early age. By the time they are five, or six, they are helping their parents: looking after the geese, and the hens, and the younger children, and fetching water, and gathering firewood, and scaring the birds, from the fields, and picking up stones, from the ploughed land.

[pause]

{img:medieval-boy-scaring-crows-field-clapper-sling-spring-morning}
By the time they are ten, or twelve, the boys are helping in the fields, leading the oxen, at the plough, and carrying, and reaping, and the girls are helping their mothers, spinning, and brewing, and cooking, and milking, and working in the garden, and the fields, at harvest time.

[long-pause]

Few of the children of Elton go to school. There is no village school. A few boys may learn their letters, from the priest. And a very few, the cleverest, or the luckiest, may go away, to a grammar school, in a town, or to the abbey, and become priests, or monks, or clerks.

[pause]

At about fourteen, the children are thought to be almost grown up. Some of them go to work, as servants, in other households, in the village, or on the manor farm, or in the town, to earn some money, and learn the skills of farming, and housekeeping, before they marry, and have households of their own.

[long-pause]

And in the court rolls, we sometimes see them, the children of Elton, growing up, from one year to the next: first as the son, or daughter, of their father, and then, years later, with land of their own, and children of their own, and then, at last, their own children, taking their place.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-reeve-tally-sticks-manor-barn-grain-sacks-checking}
The most important man in Elton, in the everyday life of the village, is the reeve.

[pause]

He is not a lord, or a knight, or a monk. He is one of the villagers, a villein, like most of his neighbours, a solid, sensible, experienced farmer, with a good holding of land, chosen by the other villagers, every year, at Michaelmas, to run the lord's farm, for the coming year.

[long-pause]

The lord's own land, the demesne, is not separate from the villagers' land, but lies in strips, and fields, among theirs, and is worked by them, as part of their duties. And the reeve must organise it all: who must come to plough, and when, and who must sow, and harrow, and weed, and mow, and reap, and carry. He must make sure that the work is done, and done well, and that nobody is lazy, or late.

[pause]

He must look after the lord's animals: the oxen, and the horses, and the cows, and the sheep, and the pigs, and the geese, and the doves. He must sell the lord's corn, and wool, and cheese, at the market, and buy what is needed: iron, for the ploughs, and tar, for the sheep, and salt, and nails, and cartwheels.

[pause]

{img:reeve-and-hayward-walking-fields-dawn-discussing-crops-staff}
He cannot read, or write, most likely. So he keeps his accounts on tally sticks: little sticks of hazel wood, with notches cut into them, for every bushel of corn, and every shilling. A big notch for a pound. A smaller one for a shilling. A tiny one for a penny. And then the stick is split in two, down the middle, so that each side has a half, with the same notches, which must fit together exactly, so that nobody can cheat.

[long-pause]

And at Michaelmas, at the end of the farming year, a clerk, from the abbey, comes, and the reeve sits down with him, and goes through everything, with his tally sticks, and his memory, and the clerk writes it all down, in Latin, on the great parchment roll: the account of the manor of Elton, for the year.

[pause]

For his work, the reeve is excused his own labour services, for the year, and he may eat at the lord's table, at harvest time, and keep a horse, in the lord's pasture. But it is hard, and thankless work, and he is caught between the lord, who wants as much as possible, and his neighbours, who want to do as little as possible, for the lord.

[pause]

{img:beadle-hayward-medieval-village-officials-horn-field-boundary}
He has helpers. There is the beadle, who calls the villagers to their work, and to the court, and collects the fines. There is the hayward, who looks after the hedges, and the fences, and the meadows, and makes sure that the animals do not stray into the corn, and blows his horn, to warn people, if they do, and impounds the animals that have strayed, in the village pound, a little stone enclosure, until their owners pay a fine, to get them back.

[long-pause]

And above them all, there is the steward, or the bailiff, a man appointed by the abbot, who rides from manor to manor, on a good horse, checking on everything, and holding the manor court.

[pause]

But it is the reeve, every morning, before dawn, walking through the village, and out to the fields, who keeps the whole great, slow machine of the manor turning, through the year.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-manor-house-elton-stone-hall-barns-dovecote-courtyard-river}
Down by the river, beside the church, there is the manor house of Elton.

[pause]

It belongs to the Abbot of Ramsey, but he rarely comes here. It is the centre of his farm, at Elton, and the place where his officials stay, when they visit, and where the manor court is held, and where the lord's corn, and hay, and animals, are kept.

[long-pause]

It stands in a courtyard, surrounded by a wall, with a gatehouse, and inside, there is the hall: a long, tall building, of stone, and timber, with a high roof, of thatch, or stone slates, and a great hearth, in the middle of the floor, and a raised platform, at one end, called the dais, with a table, for the steward, and his guests.

[pause]

Around the courtyard, there are the other buildings: the kitchen, separate from the hall, in case of fire, and the bakehouse, and the brewhouse, and the dairy, and the granary, raised on stone pillars, to keep the rats out, and the great barns, for the corn, and the hay, and the stables, and the cowsheds, and the pigsties, and the sheepfolds, and the hen house, and the dovecote.

[pause]

{img:medieval-dovecote-round-stone-doves-flying-courtyard-morning}
The dovecote is a round tower, of stone, with hundreds of little nesting holes, inside, in the walls, from the floor to the roof, where hundreds of pigeons live, and fly in, and out, through a hole, in the roof. Only the lord may keep a dovecote. The young pigeons are eaten, at the lord's table, and the doves fly out, every day, over the fields, and eat the villagers' corn, which the villagers do not like at all.

[long-pause]

There are servants, who live, and work, at the manor all year: the ploughmen, and the carters, and the shepherd, and the cowherd, and the swineherd, and the dairymaid, who makes the lord's butter, and cheese. They are paid, partly in money, and partly in corn, and they eat together, in the hall, or the kitchen.

[pause]

And sometimes, a few times a year, there is great excitement, at the manor: the steward is coming, or the abbot himself, with his monks, and his servants, and his horses.

[pause]

{img:abbot-arriving-manor-horseback-monks-servants-villagers-bowing}
Then, everything must be cleaned, and swept, and the rushes on the floor of the hall changed, and fresh herbs strewn, and the best food prepared: capons, and pigeons, and pike, and eels, and white bread, and wine, brought from the town. The villagers come, to see the abbot ride in, on his fine horse, in his black robes, with his monks, and his men, and they bow, as he passes.

[long-pause]

The abbot stays a night, or two, and looks at the fields, and the barns, and the animals, and the accounts, and hears the reeve, and perhaps the villagers, and gives his orders, and then rides on, to his next manor, and the village breathes again, and goes back, to its everyday life.

[pause]

And in the evenings, when the officials have gone, the manor house is quiet, and the servants sit, in the hall, around the fire, and eat their supper, and talk, and the dogs sleep, by the hearth, and the doves coo, softly, in the dovecote, as the night falls, over the river.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-watermill-interior-millstones-hopper-flour-dust-light-beams}
Down by the river Nene, at Elton, there is the mill.

[pause]

It is a wooden building, with a thatched roof, standing beside a channel, cut from the river, called the mill race, where the water is held back, by a dam, and a sluice gate, and then let through, to turn the great wooden water wheel, on the side of the mill.

[long-pause]

The wheel turns, slowly, steadily, creaking, and splashing, and dripping, and inside the mill, its turning is passed, by wooden gears, with wooden teeth, to the millstones: two great round stones, one on top of the other, the bottom one still, and the top one turning, grinding the grain, between them, into flour.

[pause]

The grain is poured into a wooden hopper, above the stones, and trickles down, through a hole, in the middle of the top stone, and is ground, and the flour comes out, at the edges, soft, and white, and warm, and falls into a wooden trough, and into the sacks. And the whole mill is filled with a soft, rumbling, humming sound, and the air is full of fine white dust, which settles on everything, and on the miller, so that he is white, all over, from his hair to his boots.

[pause]

{img:miller-floury-clothes-taking-toll-grain-bowl-villager-sack}
The mill belongs to the lord. And the villagers must bring their grain here, to be ground, and nowhere else. And they must pay the miller, by letting him keep a part of their grain: one sixteenth, or one twentieth, measured out with a special bowl, called the toll dish.

[long-pause]

The villagers are not allowed to grind their own grain, at home, with hand mills, called querns. If they are caught doing it, they are fined, at the manor court. And many of them do it anyway, secretly, because it is cheaper.

[pause]

The miller is not always popular. People suspect that he takes more than his share, that his toll dish is a little too big, or that he mixes a little sand into the flour, or keeps back some of the good flour, for himself. There are many old stories, and jokes, and songs, about dishonest millers. In the Canterbury Tales, written by Geoffrey Chaucer, about ninety years later, there is a miller, who has a thumb of gold: that is, he knows exactly how to cheat his customers, by pressing his thumb on the scales.

[pause]

{img:river-nene-mill-pond-reflections-ducks-evening-calm-willows}
But the mill is also a meeting place. While they wait for their grain to be ground, the villagers sit on the bank, outside, in the sunshine, and talk, and share the news, and the children play, by the water, and watch the great wheel turn, and fish in the mill pond, for eels, and roach, and pike.

[long-pause]

The mill pond is full of fish, and the lord takes his eels, as part of the rent: hundreds, and thousands of eels, every year, caught in wicker traps, set in the mill race, and sent to the abbey, for the monks to eat, on the days when they may not eat meat.

[pause]

And in the evening, when the work is done, the miller closes the sluice gate, and the great wheel slows down, and stops, and the mill falls silent, and the only sound is the water, running over the dam, and the frogs, and the moorhens, calling, in the reeds.

[long-pause]

{img:river-nene-medieval-flat-boat-stone-cargo-willows-summer-afternoon}
The river Nene flows past Elton, slowly, and quietly, between its meadows, on its way from the hills of Northamptonshire, in the west, to Peterborough, and the fens, and the sea, at the Wash, in the east.

[pause]

It is a gentle river, wide, and slow, and full of life, with reeds, and rushes, and yellow water lilies, along its edges, and willows, and alders, leaning over it, and little islands, and backwaters, where the water is still, and dark, and deep.

[long-pause]

For the people of Elton, the river is a source of food, and work, and pleasure. In it, there are fish: eels, above all, and pike, and perch, and roach, and bream, and tench. The lord has the right to the fishing, and he lets it, to some of the villagers, who pay their rent in eels, and fish, for the monks of Ramsey. Others fish, quietly, on summer evenings, with a line, and a hook, and a worm, or with a little net, and sometimes, are caught, and fined, at the court.

[pause]

In the reed beds, the villagers cut reeds, for thatching their roofs, and rushes, for the floors of their houses, and the church, and for making baskets, and mats, and rushlights, which are rushes, peeled, and dipped in fat, and burned, as a cheap, little light, in the evenings.

[pause]

{img:villagers-cutting-reeds-riverbank-bundles-boat-heron-morning}
And the river is a road. In the year 1300, it is easier to carry heavy things by water, than by land, along the muddy roads. And on the Nene, there are boats: flat-bottomed boats, and barges, pushed with poles, or pulled, by men, along the bank, carrying corn, and timber, and wool, and salt, and peat, and stone.

[long-pause]

Not far from Elton, at Barnack, there are famous quarries, of fine, pale limestone, Barnack stone, which has been used, for centuries, to build the great churches, and abbeys, of the east of England: Peterborough, and Ely, and Ramsey, and many others. Some of the stone is carried, by boat, along the rivers, and the fens, to the building sites, far away.

[pause]

And the boatmen, and the traders, stop at Elton, sometimes, and buy ale, and bread, and tell the news, of the towns, and the abbeys, and the world beyond.

[pause]

{img:river-nene-evening-children-swimming-fishing-meadow-sunset}
In the summer, the children of Elton swim in the river, and splash, and play, in the shallows, by the meadows, and the young men swim across, and back, to show how strong they are. And in the evenings, people walk along the banks, and watch the fish rising, and the swallows skimming over the water, and the kingfisher, darting, like a little blue flame, from the willows.

[long-pause]

But the river can also be dangerous. In winter, and in wet springs, it floods, and spreads out, over the meadows, in a great sheet of grey water, and sometimes, into the lower parts of the village. And the court rolls, of many villages, record, sadly, the children, and the grown-ups, who fell into the river, and were not saved. We will leave them there, gently, with a quiet thought.

[pause]

And then the waters go down, and the meadows are green again, and richer, because of the mud the flood has left behind, and the river flows on, gently, past the village, as it always has.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-family-cottage-dawn-may-mother-fire-pottage-children-waking}
Let us spend a day in May, with a family of Elton.

[pause]

They are a family of villeins, with half a virgate of land, about fifteen acres, in strips, across the fields: a father, called Robert, and a mother, called Agnes, and four children, two boys, and two girls, and Robert's old mother, who lives with them, in a little room, at the end of the house.

[long-pause]

They wake at dawn, at about four o'clock, in May, when the birds begin to sing, and the light comes in, through the doorway, and the little window, with its wooden shutter. Agnes gets up first, and blows on the ashes of the fire, in the middle of the floor, and puts on some sticks, and the fire flares up, and she hangs the pot over it, and warms the pottage, left over from yesterday.

[pause]

They eat breakfast quickly: a piece of bread, dark, and heavy, made of rye, and barley, and a drink of ale, weak, and thin, which everyone drinks, even the children, because the water is not always safe.

[pause]

{img:medieval-father-son-walking-to-fields-hoe-scythe-dawn-mist}
Then Robert goes out, to the fields, with his eldest son, who is fourteen. Today, it is his turn to work on the lord's land, weeding the barley, with a long-handled hook, and a forked stick, pulling out the thistles, and the docks, and the weeds, that would choke the crop.

[long-pause]

At home, Agnes milks the cow, and feeds the hens, and the pig, and lets the geese out, onto the green, with her youngest daughter, who is seven, to look after them. Then she fetches water, from the well, in a wooden bucket, and goes into the garden, and weeds the beans, and the onions, and picks some herbs, and some young nettles, for the pottage.

[pause]

Her elder daughter, who is twelve, sits at the door, in the sunshine, spinning wool, with a spindle, and a distaff, as she minds the baby, a little boy, who sits, in the dust, playing with a wooden toy, and a puppy.

[pause]

{img:medieval-woman-brewing-ale-vat-cottage-yard-barley-malt}
And then Agnes brews. She is one of the ale-wives of Elton. Several times a month, she brews a batch of ale, in a big wooden vat, from barley malt, and water, and when it is ready, she puts a branch, or a bush, on a pole, outside her door, as a sign, and her neighbours come, with their jugs, to buy it.

[long-pause]

At midday, the old grandmother takes a basket of bread, and cheese, and onions, and a jug of ale, out to the fields, for Robert, and the boy, and they sit down, at the edge of the strip, in the shade of a bush, with the other men, and eat, and rest, and talk.

[pause]

In the afternoon, they work again, until the evening, when the church bell rings, for vespers, and they walk home, through the fields, tired, and dusty, with the sun low, behind them, and the swallows swooping, over the corn.

[pause]

{img:medieval-family-supper-cottage-evening-bench-bowls-door-open-sunset}
At home, Agnes has made the supper: pottage, a thick soup, of peas, and beans, and oats, and onions, and herbs, and a little bacon, cooked all afternoon, in the pot, over the fire, with bread, and cheese, and ale. They eat it together, sitting on benches, and stools, around the table, with wooden bowls, and wooden spoons, with the door open, to the long, golden May evening.

[long-pause]

And then, as the light fades, they sit outside, on the bench, by the door, and talk with the neighbours, over the fence, and the children play, in the lane, until it is dark. And then, they go in, and bar the door, and cover the fire, and lie down, on their straw mattresses, under their blankets, and sleep, while the nightingale sings, in the bushes, by the river.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-village-women-washing-linen-river-bank-beating-paddles-chatting}
The women of Elton work as hard as the men, and often harder.

[pause]

They run the house, and the garden, and the yard. They cook, and bake, and brew, and churn the butter, and make the cheese. They spin the wool, and the flax, and weave, and sew, and mend the clothes, for the whole family. They look after the hens, and the geese, and the pigs, and the cow. They fetch the water, and the firewood. They look after the children, and the old, and the sick.

[long-pause]

And in the busy seasons, at haymaking, and harvest, they work in the fields, too, beside the men, raking, and binding, and gleaning. Some of them work for wages, on the manor farm, or for richer neighbours, and are paid, though usually a little less than the men.

[pause]

Down by the river, on washing days, the women gather, with their baskets of linen, and wash it, in the shallows, beating it with wooden paddles, on the flat stones, and spreading it out, to dry, on the grass, and the bushes, in the sun. And they talk, and laugh, and share the news, and the gossip, of the village.

[pause]

{img:medieval-woman-making-cheese-dairy-wooden-press-churn-cottage}
Many of the women of Elton brew ale, to sell, as well as for their families. In the court rolls, the names of the ale-wives appear, again and again, fined a few pence, for breaking the rules about ale, which is really a kind of licence, a tax, that every brewer pays. It is one of the few ways a woman can earn her own money.

[long-pause]

And some women hold land of their own. When a villager dies, his widow usually keeps his land, for the rest of her life, or until she marries again, and she must manage it, and organise the ploughing, and the harvest, and pay the rents, and do the services, or pay someone else to do them. There are many widows in the court rolls of Elton, managing their holdings, and their families, and coming to the court, to defend their rights.

[pause]

A widow with land is a good match, and often, she marries again, quite soon, and her new husband takes over the land, with her.

[pause]

{img:medieval-wife-husband-marketing-eggs-cheese-basket-lane-morning}
Marriages, in Elton, are arranged by the families, often, with the help of the court, and the lord, because land, and animals, and money, are involved. But the young people usually choose each other, too, and many marriages are happy ones.

[long-pause]

The wedding takes place at the church door, in the porch, where the priest asks the couple if they take each other, freely, and the groom gives the bride a ring, and some coins, and then they go into the church, for the mass. And afterwards, there is a feast, at the bride's house, with ale, and food, and music, and dancing, and the whole village comes.

[pause]

And then the bride goes to her new home, and her new life, as a wife, and soon a mother, and the mistress of her own house, and garden, and hearth, in the village where she has always lived.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-may-day-young-people-gathering-hawthorn-blossom-dawn-woods}
On the first of May, the young people of Elton go a-Maying.

[pause]

Before dawn, while the stars are still in the sky, they get up, quietly, and go out, together, into the woods, and the fields, to gather the May: branches of hawthorn, covered with white blossom, and flowers, and green leaves, and they wash their faces, in the dew, which is said to make them beautiful, for the whole year.

[long-pause]

And then, as the sun rises, they come back into the village, singing, with their arms full of blossom, and green branches, and decorate the doors, and the windows, of the houses, and the church, so that the whole village smells of may blossom, sweet, and heavy, in the morning air.

[pause]

On the green, they put up a tall pole, a maypole, decorated with flowers, and ribbons, and greenery, and dance around it, holding hands, in a ring, singing the old songs, to the music of a pipe, and a drum, and a fiddle.

[pause]

{img:villagers-dancing-ring-around-maypole-green-flowers-pipe-drum}
A girl is chosen, to be the Queen of the May, and is crowned with flowers, and sits, on a seat, decorated with greenery, and watches the dancing. And there are games, and races, and wrestling, and archery, and the ale-wives bring out their ale, and everyone eats, and drinks, and dances, all day long.

[long-pause]

The church does not always approve. Some of the priests think that the May games are too wild, and too old, coming from the time before Christianity. But the villagers love them, and they go on, year after year, century after century.

[pause]

Later in May, or in June, there is Whitsun, Pentecost, the feast of the Holy Spirit, seven weeks after Easter, and there is another holiday, and often, a church ale: a great feast, held by the parish, to raise money for the church.

[pause]

{img:church-ale-feast-trestle-tables-churchyard-villagers-barrels}
The church wardens brew a great quantity of ale, from malt, given by the villagers, and set up tables, in the churchyard, or in a barn, and everyone comes, and pays a little, to eat, and drink, and the money is used, to mend the church roof, or buy a new bell, or candles, or a new book, for the priest.

[long-pause]

There is music, and dancing, and games, and sometimes, a minstrel, passing through, who sings old songs, and tells stories, of knights, and outlaws, and kings, in the churchyard, or on the green.

[pause]

And in the long, light evenings of late spring, the young men, and the young women, of Elton, walk together, along the river, and through the fields, among the flowering hedges, and the green corn, and many of the marriages of Elton begin, on these May evenings.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-haymaking-meadow-river-men-scythes-row-june-morning}
In June, it is time to make the hay.

[pause]

The meadows, beside the river Nene, are some of the most valuable land in Elton. All through the spring, the grass has been growing, thick, and tall, and full of flowers: buttercups, and clover, and ox-eye daisies, and meadowsweet, and sorrel, and cuckooflowers, and the meadows are fenced off, so that no animals can graze on them.

[long-pause]

The hay is precious, because it is the only food, for the oxen, and the horses, and the cows, during the long winter. Without enough hay, the animals would starve, and without the oxen, there could be no ploughing, and without ploughing, no bread.

[pause]

The meadows are divided into strips, or doles, like the fields, marked out with little posts, or stones, and each family has its share, decided by lot, sometimes, every year.

[pause]

Early in the morning, when the dew is still on the grass, the mowers go out, into the meadows, in a long line, one behind the other, with their scythes: long, curved blades, on long, crooked wooden handles. And they swing them, slowly, steadily, from right to left, all together, in a rhythm, and the tall grass falls, in long rows, called swaths, behind them, with a soft, swishing sound.

[long-pause]

Every now and then, they stop, and sharpen their scythes, with a whetstone, which rings, against the blade, across the meadow. And the skylarks rise up, singing, from the grass, and the corncrakes, hidden in the meadow, call, with their strange, rasping cry.

[pause]

Behind the mowers come the women, and the children, with wooden rakes, and forks, and turn the grass, so that it dries, in the sun, and then rake it up, into rows, and then into little heaps, called haycocks, all across the meadow.

[pause]

{img:women-children-raking-hay-haycocks-meadow-afternoon-sunshine}
Everyone hopes for dry weather. If it rains, the hay will rot, and must be spread out, and dried again. So they watch the sky, anxiously, and work as fast as they can, from dawn till dusk, while the sun shines.

[long-pause]

The lord's hay is mown first, by the villeins, as part of their work. And at the end of the mowing, there is a custom: the mowers are given a sheep, or some money, for a feast, and sometimes, they are allowed to take as much hay as each man can lift, on the handle of his scythe, without letting it touch the ground. If the handle breaks, or the hay falls, he gets nothing, and everyone laughs.

[pause]

And then, the hay is loaded onto the carts, piled high, and carried home, to the barns, and the haystacks, and the meadows lie bare, and green, and short, and the animals are let back onto them, to graze.

[pause]

{img:haycart-piled-high-oxen-lane-village-evening-children-riding-top}
And the children ride home, on the top of the last cart, high up, in the sweet-smelling hay, through the evening light, singing, and the whole village smells of hay, for weeks.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-shepherd-crook-dog-flock-sheep-common-pasture-hillside}
On the common pasture, and the fallow field, and the heath, above Elton, there are the sheep.

[pause]

The sheep of medieval England are smaller than the sheep of today, with thinner bodies, and longer legs, and many of them have horns. But their wool is the great treasure of England. English wool is the best in Europe, and the merchants of Flanders, and Italy, pay high prices for it, to weave into fine cloth. Much of the wealth of England, in the year 1300, comes from the backs of sheep.

[long-pause]

The lord has a great flock, on the manor, and the villagers have their own sheep, too, a few each, which graze together, on the common land, all looked after by the shepherd.

[pause]

The shepherd is a quiet, patient man, who spends his days, and often his nights, with the sheep, out on the pasture, in all weathers, with his crook, and his dog, and his bag, with his bread, and his cheese, and his little pot of tar, for the sheep's wounds, and his pipe, of wood, or bone, on which he plays, to pass the time.

[pause]

{img:shepherd-hut-lambing-fold-hurdles-night-lantern-ewes-lambs}
In the spring, at lambing time, he hardly sleeps at all. He stays with the ewes, in the fold, a shelter of hurdles, woven of hazel, day, and night, with a lantern, helping the lambs to be born, and keeping them warm, and safe, from the cold, and the foxes. For this, he is given a lamb, or two, of his own, every year, and some of the milk, and the wool.

[long-pause]

In the summer, the sheep are washed, in the river, or a pond, to clean their wool, and then, a few days later, they are sheared.

[pause]

Sheep shearing is a great event. The shearers come, and sit, or kneel, on the grass, or the barn floor, and catch the sheep, one by one, and hold them, firmly, between their knees, and cut off the fleece, with great iron shears, like big scissors, close to the skin, quickly, and carefully, so that the whole fleece comes off, in one piece, like a soft, thick blanket.

[pause]

{img:sheep-shearing-day-barn-shearers-iron-shears-fleeces-rolled}
The fleeces are rolled up, and tied, and stacked, and weighed, and sold, to the wool merchants, who come round, from the towns, or taken to the fairs, at Saint Ives, and Stamford, and Boston, and from there, by ship, across the sea, to Flanders.

[long-pause]

And at the end of the shearing, there is a feast, with food, and ale, for the shearers, and the shepherd, and the helpers, and singing, and dancing, on the grass.

[pause]

The sheep also give milk, which is made into cheese, and their dung, which is precious, for the fields. Sometimes, the lord's sheep are folded, at night, on the villagers' fallow strips, in pens, of hurdles, moved each day, so that the dung falls on the land, and makes it rich. The villagers value this very much, and pay for it.

[long-pause]

And on summer evenings, the shepherd sits on the hillside, above the village, and watches the sun go down, over the valley, and the river, and the sheep grazing, around him, and the lights coming on, in the village, below, and plays a little tune, on his pipe, that drifts down, softly, over the fields.

[long-pause]

{img:midsummer-eve-bonfire-medieval-village-hill-dancing-silhouettes-twilight}
On the twenty-third of June, it is Midsummer Eve, the eve of the feast of Saint John the Baptist.

[pause]

It is the longest day, or close to it, and the shortest night, when the sun sets late, after nine o'clock, and the twilight lasts, almost until midnight, and the sky in the north never quite grows dark.

[long-pause]

On Midsummer Eve, in Elton, as in villages all over England, and Europe, the villagers light bonfires: great fires, of wood, and bones, and brushwood, on the green, and on the hill, above the village. And they dance around them, and sing, and leap over the flames, for luck, the young men, and the girls, holding hands.

[pause]

The doors of the houses are decorated with green birch branches, and fennel, and Saint John's wort, a little yellow flower, which is said to keep away evil spirits, and lilies, and the lamps are left burning, all night long.

[pause]

{img:midsummer-doorway-decorated-birch-st-johns-wort-flowers-lamp}
Some people say that, on Midsummer Eve, the fairies are about, and that strange things can happen, in the woods, and the fields, and that, if a girl looks into a well, or a pool, at midnight, she will see the face of the man she will marry. The old women tell stories, by the fire, of the things that happened, on Midsummer nights, long ago.

[long-pause]

The children are allowed to stay up late, to see the fires, and they run about, in the half-light, waving burning sticks, and making circles of light, in the air, and then fall asleep, on the grass, wrapped in their mothers' cloaks.

[pause]

And on the next day, the feast of Saint John, there is a holiday, and mass, in the church, and the rents are paid, on Midsummer Day, one of the four quarter days of the year.

[pause]

{img:short-summer-night-village-fields-glowing-north-sky-embers}
And then, as the fires die down, to glowing embers, in the middle of the night, and the people go home, to bed, the sky, in the north, is still glowing, faintly, with the light of the sun, below the horizon, and the birds begin to sing again, only a few hours later, in the dawn, which comes so early, on midsummer morning.

[long-pause]

After Midsummer, there is a little quiet time, in the village, between the haymaking, and the harvest. The corn is growing, in the fields, green, and then golden, and the villagers mend their tools, and their carts, and their barns, and weed the fields, and wait, and watch the sky, for the harvest.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-village-church-interior-painted-walls-rood-screen-candles-light}
In the middle of Elton stands the church, the church of All Saints.

[pause]

It is the biggest building in the village, and the oldest, and the most beautiful. It is built of stone, with thick walls, and a roof of lead, and timber, and a little tower, with bells, and it can be seen, from everywhere, across the fields, and the meadows, and heard, from everywhere, when the bells ring.

[long-pause]

Inside, it is cool, and dim, and quiet, and smells of stone, and candle wax, and incense. There are no pews, or seats. The people stand, or kneel, on the stone floor, which is covered with rushes, and only the old, and the sick, may sit, on a stone bench, along the wall. Some say that this is where the old saying comes from: the weakest go to the wall.

[pause]

The walls are covered with paintings, in red, and yellow, and black, and white: pictures of the saints, and of the life of Jesus, and of the Last Judgement, over the chancel arch, with the good souls, going up to heaven, on one side, and the wicked, going down, on the other. Most of the villagers cannot read. So the paintings are their book, which tells them the stories of the Bible.

[pause]

{img:rood-screen-carved-wood-crucifix-chancel-medieval-candles-altar}
Across the church, between the nave, where the people stand, and the chancel, where the altar is, there is a carved wooden screen, called the rood screen, with a great crucifix, the rood, above it, with the figures of Mary, and Saint John, on each side, lit by candles.

[long-pause]

In the chancel, beyond the screen, the priest says the mass, every day, at the altar, in Latin, which the villagers do not understand, but they know the words, and the rhythm, and the bells, and they know when to kneel, and when to cross themselves, and when to look up, as the priest lifts the bread, above his head, and the little bell rings.

[pause]

The church is the centre of village life. Every child of Elton is baptised here, in the great stone font, by the door, a few days after it is born. Every couple of Elton is married here, at the church door, in the porch, and then goes inside, for the mass. And every villager of Elton is buried here, in the churchyard, in the shadow of the tower.

[pause]

{img:medieval-wedding-church-porch-couple-priest-villagers-ring-spring}
And on Sundays, and the great feast days, everyone comes, in their best clothes, to the mass, and the village gathers, afterwards, in the churchyard, and talks, and arranges business, and hears the news, and the children play among the graves.

[long-pause]

In Elton's churchyard, today, there are still two old stone crosses, carved more than a thousand years ago, in Anglo-Saxon times, before the Normans came, which were already very old, in 1300, and which the villagers of 1300 would have known, and touched, and passed by, every Sunday.

[pause]

The church year gives the village its rhythm: Christmas, and Lent, and Easter, and Whitsun, and the feasts of the saints, scattered through the year, each one a holy day, a holiday, when nobody works, and there is mass, and feasting, and games. There are about forty or fifty of them, every year, as well as the Sundays.

[pause]

{img:medieval-church-tower-bells-evening-swifts-fields-village-roofs}
And the bells of the church ring, every day, to mark the hours, and the prayers, at dawn, and noon, and dusk, so that the people, out in the fields, far from the village, can hear them, and know the time, and stop, for a moment, and say a prayer, and then go on with their work.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-pilgrims-walking-road-staffs-scallop-badges-spring-fields}
Once in their lives, perhaps, some of the people of Elton go on a pilgrimage.

[pause]

A pilgrimage is a journey, to a holy place, a shrine, where there are the relics of a saint, or a holy image, to pray, and give thanks, and ask for help: for healing, from an illness, or for a child, or for forgiveness, or simply, out of love, and devotion.

[long-pause]

The greatest pilgrimages, to Rome, or Jerusalem, or Santiago de Compostela, in Spain, are far too long, and expensive, for most villagers. But there are shrines in England, too. There is the shrine of Saint Thomas Becket, at Canterbury, the most famous of all. And closer to Elton, in Norfolk, there is the shrine of Our Lady, at Walsingham, where a little wooden house stands, said to be a copy of the house of the Virgin Mary, in Nazareth, where pilgrims come, from all over England, and kings, and queens, too.

[pause]

And there are smaller shrines, close by, which can be reached in a day or two: the relics of saints, in the great abbeys, at Peterborough, and Ramsey, and Ely, and Crowland, in the fens.

[pause]

The pilgrims set out, in the spring, or the summer, when the roads are dry, and the days are long, in a little group, from the village, or the neighbouring villages, with their staffs, and their bags, and their cloaks, and a little money, sewn into their clothes. The priest blesses them, before they go, at the church.

[long-pause]

They walk, all day, along the roads, and the tracks, through the fields, and the woods, and the villages, and the towns, singing hymns, and telling stories, to pass the time, and they stay at night, in inns, or in the guesthouses, of the monasteries, which welcome pilgrims, or in barns, and churches.

[pause]

They meet other pilgrims, on the road, from other places, and share their stories, and their food, and their company. And they see things they have never seen before: new villages, and towns, and churches, and the great cathedrals, and the sea.

[pause]

{img:candlelit-shrine-medieval-pilgrims-kneeling-offerings-candles-gold}
And at last, they arrive, at the shrine, and go in, into the great church, full of candles, and gold, and the smell of incense, and the murmur of prayers, and kneel, before the shrine, and pray, and leave their offering, a coin, or a candle, or a little wax model, of what they want to be healed: an arm, or a leg, or an eye.

[long-pause]

Then, they buy a little badge, of lead, and tin, made at the shrine, showing the saint, or the holy image: a scallop shell, for Santiago, an ampulla, a little flask, of holy water, for Canterbury, or a badge, of the Virgin, for Walsingham. And they pin it to their hat, or their cloak, so that everyone they meet, on the way home, knows where they have been.

[pause]

Many of these little pilgrim badges have been found, by archaeologists, and by people searching the riverbanks, especially of the Thames, in London, lost, or thrown in, by pilgrims, long ago, for luck.

[pause]

{img:returning-pilgrim-village-lane-family-welcome-badge-hat-evening}
And when the pilgrims come home, to Elton, after a week, or two, or more, tired, and footsore, and happy, the whole village comes out to meet them, and they tell their stories, again, and again, around the fire, for the rest of their lives: of the long road, and the great church, and the candles, and the crowds, and the sea.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-parish-priest-simple-robes-walking-lane-greeting-villagers}
The priest of Elton lives beside the church, in a house, a little bigger than the others, with a garden, and a barn, and some land of his own, in the fields, called the glebe, which he farms, like the villagers.

[pause]

He is the rector, or perhaps a vicar, sent by the rector, who lives far away. He is not a monk, like the monks of Ramsey. He is a parish priest, who lives in the village, among his people, and knows them all, from their baptism, to their burial.

[long-pause]

He is supported by the tithe: one tenth of everything that the villagers grow, and produce, every year. A tenth of the corn, and the hay, and the lambs, and the calves, and the wool, and the eggs, and the cheese. At harvest time, every tenth sheaf of corn, in every field, is left aside, for the priest, and carried to his tithe barn.

[pause]

The villagers do not always like paying the tithe, and there are arguments, and complaints. But they also know that the priest uses some of it, to help the poor, and the sick, and to look after the church.

[pause]

{img:priest-visiting-sick-villager-cottage-bed-candle-comfort}
The priest is often a local man, the son of a villager, or a craftsman, who has learned his letters, and some Latin, from another priest, and has been ordained, by the bishop. He can read, and write, a little, and he knows the mass, and the prayers, and the stories of the saints, by heart. Some priests are learned, and some are not. Some are very good men, and some are not so good. But most of them are simply ordinary men, doing their best.

[long-pause]

He says mass, every morning. He baptises the babies, and marries the young couples, and buries the dead. He hears the villagers' confessions, once a year, at least, in Lent, and gives them their penance. He visits the sick, and the dying, carrying the holy oil, and the bread, through the village, with a little bell, and a candle, so that the people, as he passes, kneel down, in the lane.

[pause]

He teaches the children their prayers: the Our Father, and the Hail Mary, and the Creed, in Latin, which they learn by heart, without understanding all the words. And sometimes, he teaches a clever boy to read, and write, so that one day, he too may become a priest, or a clerk.

[pause]

{img:priest-teaching-boy-letters-wax-tablet-church-porch-sunlight}
He also helps the villagers in other ways: he writes letters for them, and reads documents, and witnesses their agreements, and gives them advice, and settles their quarrels, and speaks for them, sometimes, to the lord's officials.

[long-pause]

And in the evening, when his work is done, the priest sits in his garden, among his beehives, and his apple trees, and reads his breviary, his book of prayers, in the last of the light, while the bees hum, and the swallows fly in, and out, of the church tower, beside him.

[long-pause]

{img:manor-court-hallmote-great-hall-steward-clerk-villagers-standing}
Several times a year, in Elton, there is the manor court: the hallmote.

[pause]

It is held in the great hall of the manor house, or sometimes, in summer, outside, under a great tree, on the green. And every villager must come. The beadle goes round the village, the day before, and tells everyone, and those who do not come, without a good reason, are fined.

[long-pause]

The court is led by the steward, the abbot's man, who rides over from Ramsey, with a clerk, who writes everything down, on the parchment roll. But the judgements are made by the villagers themselves: a group of twelve, or so, of the older, and more respected men, called the jurors, who know the customs of the village, and what is fair, and they decide.

[pause]

The court deals with everything that happens, in the village: with land, and inheritance, and marriage, and quarrels, and debts, and damage, and trespass, and the breaking of the village rules.

[pause]

{img:clerk-writing-court-roll-quill-parchment-table-steward-listening}
When a villager dies, his son, or his widow, comes to the court, to take over his land, and pays a fine, to the lord, and the clerk writes it down. When a young man wants to marry, and needs land, the court arranges it. When a villein's daughter marries, her father pays the merchet. When someone wants to leave the village, to live somewhere else, they must ask permission, and pay a little, every year.

[long-pause]

And then there are the offences, the little crimes of village life, which fill the court rolls of Elton, year after year.

[pause]

Someone's cow got into the lord's wheat, and ate it. Someone's pigs got into the meadow. Someone ploughed a furrow too far, into their neighbour's strip. Someone cut wood, in the lord's wood, without permission. Someone did not come to work, on the lord's land, when they should have. Someone's ale was too weak, or was sold in a short measure. Someone left a dungheap, in the lane, or did not mend their fence. Someone called their neighbour a thief, in a quarrel, at the well.

[pause]

{img:villagers-arguing-politely-court-witnesses-jurors-medieval-hall}
For each of these things, the court gives a small fine, a few pennies, or sixpence, or a shilling, or orders that the damage be paid for, or the fence be mended, by a certain day. And the clerk writes it all down, in his neat Latin: John, son of Richard, for his cows in the lord's corn, sixpence.

[long-pause]

The court also makes the village rules, the bylaws: when the harvest will begin, and nobody may start before, and who may glean, in the fields, after the harvest, and when the animals may go onto the stubble, and how many each family may have, on the common, and who must keep the ditches clean.

[pause]

And thanks to the court rolls, of Elton, and many other villages, we know about these little things, the cows, and the fences, and the quarrels, and the ale, of ordinary people, seven hundred years ago, which, without them, would have been forgotten, long ago.

[pause]

{img:medieval-village-ale-tasters-testing-ale-cup-doorway-bush-sign}
There are also ale-tasters, two of them, chosen every year, from the villagers, whose job is to go round, and taste the ale, of every ale-wife, when she has brewed, and make sure that it is good, and sold at the right price, and in honest measures. It sounds like a pleasant job. But the ale-tasters must report their neighbours, and their friends, to the court, if the ale is bad, and that is not always easy.

[long-pause]

{img:view-of-frankpledge-villagers-gathered-tithing-groups-steward-green}
In Elton, as in many villages of England, every man, over the age of twelve, belongs to a tithing.

[pause]

A tithing is a group of about ten men, neighbours, who are responsible for each other. If one of them does something wrong, the others must bring him to the court. And if they do not, the whole tithing is fined. It is an old system, from Anglo-Saxon times, and it keeps the peace, in the village, by making everyone look after everyone else.

[long-pause]

Each tithing has a leader, called the tithingman, or the chief pledge. And once or twice a year, at the view of frankpledge, a special session of the manor court, all the tithings must come, and report anything that has happened, in the village, that the court should know about.

[pause]

So, at the view of frankpledge, the court hears about the things that keep the peace: the broken fences, and the blocked ditches, and the stray animals, and the quarrels, and the weak ale, and the new people, who have come to live in the village, and must join a tithing.

[pause]

{img:village-watch-night-lantern-men-walking-lane-quiet-houses}
There is also the watch. In the summer months, some of the men of the village take turns, to walk through the village, at night, with a lantern, and a staff, watching for fire, and for strangers, and keeping the village safe, while the others sleep.

[long-pause]

And if something goes wrong, there is the hue and cry. Anyone who sees something wrong must shout, and call out, loudly, and everyone who hears must come running, and help, and follow, from village to village, if necessary, until the matter is settled. If someone raises the hue and cry for nothing, he is fined. And if someone does not come, when he should, he is fined too.

[pause]

Most of what happens, in Elton, is small, and ordinary, and settled peacefully, among neighbours. People quarrel, and make it up. They borrow, and lend, and forget to pay back, and are reminded, at the court. They help each other, at the harvest, and in sickness, and in trouble.

[pause]

{img:neighbours-helping-rebuild-fence-lane-handshake-medieval-village}
And the village is held together, not only by the lord, and the court, and the rules, but by the people themselves, who have to live together, all their lives, in the same small place, and depend on each other, for everything: for the ploughing, and the harvest, and the oxen, and the hay, and the help, when it is needed.

[long-pause]

They know each other's families, and histories, and quarrels, and kindnesses, going back for generations. They stand beside each other, at baptisms, and weddings, and funerals. They pray together, and work together, and feast together. And when the court needs twelve men to judge a quarrel, it is to their neighbours, the old, wise men, of the village, that they turn.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-harvest-golden-wheat-reapers-sickles-women-binding-sheaves-august}
In August, it is harvest time, the most important time of the whole year.

[pause]

The corn in the fields has turned from green, to gold. The wheat, and the rye, and the barley, and the oats, stand tall, and heavy, and ripe, rustling, in the warm wind, under a blue sky. The whole year depends on the next few weeks. If the harvest is good, there will be bread, all winter. If it is bad, there will be hunger.

[long-pause]

On the first of August, there is Lammas, loaf mass, when the first loaf, made from the new wheat, is brought to the church, and blessed, at the mass, to give thanks for the harvest that is coming.

[pause]

And then the harvest begins. The court has decided the day, and nobody may start before it. And then everyone, every man, and woman, and child, who can work, goes out, into the fields, from dawn until dusk, for weeks.

[pause]

{img:reapers-row-sickles-cutting-wheat-binders-following-stooks-sun}
The corn is cut with sickles: small, curved blades, with a wooden handle, held in one hand, while the other hand grasps a bunch of stalks, and the reaper cuts them, low, near the ground, and lays them down, in a row. It is slow, back-breaking work, bending over, all day long, in the heat, with the sweat running down, and the dust, and the scratching straw.

[long-pause]

Behind the reapers come the binders, often the women, who gather up the cut corn, and tie it, into sheaves, with a twist of straw, and then stand the sheaves up, together, in little groups, called stooks, leaning against each other, with the ears of corn at the top, so that they can dry, in the sun, and the wind.

[pause]

The lord's corn must be harvested first. And for this, there are special days, called boon works, when every household in the village must send everyone who can work, to reap the lord's corn, all together, sometimes hundreds of people, in the lord's fields, at once.

[pause]

{img:boon-work-harvest-lunch-field-bread-cheese-ale-herrings-workers-resting}
On the boon days, the lord gives the workers food, and drink, in the fields: bread, and cheese, and herrings, and ale, and sometimes meat, and soup. On the great boon days, at Elton, there is a feast, at midday, in the field, with the workers sitting down, in the stubble, in the shade of the stooks, and eating, and drinking, and resting, together, in the heat.

[long-pause]

And then, when the corn has dried, it is loaded onto the carts, and carried, home, to the barns, and the stack yards, and piled up, in great stacks, thatched, against the rain, until it can be threshed, in the winter.

[pause]

And after the harvest, the poor of the village, the old, and the widows, and the children, are allowed to go into the fields, and glean: to pick up the ears of corn that have been left behind, on the ground, one by one, and take them home, in their aprons, and their baskets. It is a precious right, and the court makes sure that it is respected.

[pause]

{img:gleaners-women-children-stubble-field-evening-picking-ears-corn}
When the last sheaf is cut, in the last field, there is a great shout, and the last sheaf is decorated, with flowers, and ribbons, and carried home, in triumph, on the last cart, with the reapers riding on top, singing.

[long-pause]

And then, there is the harvest supper, the greatest feast of the year, with meat, and pies, and bread, and cheese, and ale, and music, and dancing, in the lord's barn, or on the green, until late into the night, under the harvest moon, rising, huge, and golden, over the stubble fields.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-st-ives-fair-river-ouse-stalls-booths-merchants-crowds-spring}
Once a year, at Easter, some of the villagers of Elton go to the greatest fair in England: the fair at Saint Ives.

[pause]

Saint Ives is a little town, on the river Ouse, in Huntingdonshire, about twenty-five miles from Elton, and, like Elton, it belongs to the Abbot of Ramsey. And every year, from Easter, for several weeks, there is a great fair, which brings merchants, and traders, from all over England, and from across the sea.

[long-pause]

The fair fills the whole town. The main street, and the fields, around it, are covered with booths, and stalls, and tents, of wood, and canvas, in long rows, where the merchants set out their goods. And the river is crowded with boats, and barges, bringing goods, from the coast, and from the great port of King's Lynn.

[pause]

There are merchants from Flanders, and Brabant, with fine cloth, woven in their great cloth towns, and merchants from France, and Gascony, with wine, and from Italy, with silks, and spices, and from Germany, and Scandinavia, with furs, and timber, and iron. And English merchants, from London, and York, and Lincoln, and Norwich, buying wool, the great wealth of England, to send across the sea.

[pause]

{img:medieval-fair-cloth-merchant-booth-bolts-colourful-cloth-buyers}
And there is everything to buy: cloth, of every colour, and leather, and furs, and hides, and wool, and linen, and hemp, and rope, and iron, and steel, and pots, and pans, and knives, and tools, and horses, and cattle, and sheep, and salt, and fish, salted, and dried, and wax, and spices, pepper, and ginger, and cinnamon, and saffron, and almonds, and figs, and raisins, and sugar, which is very rare, and very expensive.

[long-pause]

For the villagers of Elton, it is a great adventure. They walk, or ride in a cart, along the roads, through the fens, for a day, and arrive in the crowded town, and wander, wide-eyed, among the stalls, among the crowds, and the noise, and the smells, and the strange languages, and the people from far away.

[pause]

They come to sell: a few fleeces, or some cheeses, or a calf, or some eggs. And to buy: a new knife, or an iron pot, or a pair of shoes, or some cloth, for a new tunic, or a few spices, for a special feast, or a little present, a ribbon, or a brooch, for a wife, or a daughter.

[pause]

{img:medieval-fair-entertainers-jugglers-musicians-dancing-bear-crowd}
And there are entertainments too: jugglers, and acrobats, and musicians, and minstrels, singing, and storytellers, and puppet shows, and wrestling matches, and cockfights, and a man with a dancing bear, on a chain.

[long-pause]

The Abbot of Ramsey runs the fair, and collects rents, for the booths, and tolls, on the goods, and holds a special court, every day, for the merchants, to settle their quarrels, and debts, quickly, before they leave, called the court of piepowder, from the French words for dusty feet, because the merchants come in, straight from the dusty roads.

[pause]

And at the end of the day, the villagers of Elton find a place to sleep, in a barn, or an inn, or under their cart, and the next morning, they go home, along the roads, with their purchases, and their stories, to tell to their neighbours, around the fire, for the rest of the year.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-road-near-village-travellers-cart-friar-pedlar-horseman}
Not far from Elton, a few miles away, there runs a great road: the old Roman road, called Ermine Street, which runs, straight, from London, to Lincoln, and York, and the north. It was built by the Romans, more than a thousand years before, and in the year 1300, it is still one of the main roads of England, the Great North Road.

[pause]

Along it, and along the smaller roads, and tracks, that join it, there come travellers, and some of them pass through Elton.

[long-pause]

There are pedlars, walking from village to village, with packs, on their backs, full of little things to sell: needles, and pins, and thread, and ribbons, and buttons, and buckles, and combs, and little mirrors, and knives, and spoons, and spices, in little bags. The women of the village gather round, when a pedlar comes, to look, and buy, and hear the news.

[pause]

There are friars: the grey friars, the Franciscans, and the black friars, the Dominicans, in their long robes, with ropes around their waists, walking, barefoot, or in sandals, from place to place, preaching, in the villages, on the green, or in the churchyard, simple, lively sermons, with stories, which the villagers love, and begging for their food, and their lodging.

[pause]

{img:friar-preaching-village-green-villagers-listening-sitting-grass}
There are messengers, riding fast, on horses, with letters, for the abbot, and the bishop, and the king's officials. There are drovers, driving cattle, and sheep, along the roads, to the markets, in the towns, slowly, with their dogs, and their sticks.

[long-pause]

There are merchants, with their packhorses, loaded with wool, and cloth, and salt, and wine. There are carters, with their carts, pulled by horses, or oxen, creaking, along the rutted roads. There are wandering craftsmen, masons, and carpenters, and glaziers, going to work, on a church, or a castle, far away.

[pause]

And sometimes, there are great people: a lord, or a bishop, or an abbot, with his whole household, riding by, with his servants, and his knights, and his packhorses, and his carts, with all his furniture, and his tapestries, and his kitchen pots, travelling from one of his manors to another, in a long, slow, colourful procession, which the villagers stop, and stare at, and talk about, for weeks.

[pause]

{img:noble-procession-riders-banners-carts-medieval-road-villagers-watching}
In the year 1290, only ten years before our story, a very sad procession passed along this road, not far from Elton. Queen Eleanor, the beloved wife of King Edward the First, died, at Harby, near Lincoln, and her body was carried, slowly, all the way to London, to be buried, in Westminster Abbey. And at every place where the procession stopped, for the night, the king had a tall, beautiful stone cross built, in her memory: the Eleanor crosses. One of them was built at Geddington, in Northamptonshire, not far away, and it still stands, today.

[long-pause]

So, though most of the people of Elton never travel far, the world comes to them, along the roads, and the river, with its news, and its goods, and its stories, and its strangers, and then goes on, down the road, and over the hill, and is gone.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-autumn-village-threshing-barn-flails-dust-light-doorway}
On the twenty-ninth of September, it is Michaelmas, the feast of Saint Michael the Archangel, and the end of the farming year.

[pause]

The harvest is in. The corn is in the barns, and the stack yards. The apples, and the pears, are being picked, in the orchards, and stored, in lofts, on straw. The nuts are falling, in the woods. And the swallows are gathering, on the church roof, getting ready to fly away, for the winter.

[long-pause]

At Michaelmas, the reeve gives his accounts, to the clerk, from the abbey, and a new reeve is chosen, for the new year, and the rents are paid, and the servants are hired, for the next year. And the villagers eat a goose, at Michaelmas, fattened on the stubble, in the fields, after the harvest, for luck, and for the feast.

[pause]

And in the barns, the threshing begins.

[pause]

{img:two-men-threshing-flails-barn-floor-rhythm-grain-chaff}
All through the autumn, and the winter, the men thresh the corn, on the floor of the barns, with flails: two sticks, joined together, with a leather strap, one long, held in the hands, and one short, which swings round, and beats the ears of corn, on the floor, to knock out the grain.

[long-pause]

Two or three men stand in a circle, and beat, in turn, in a steady rhythm, thump, thump, thump, all day long, so that, all through the autumn, and winter, you can hear the sound of the flails, from the barns, all over the village.

[pause]

Then, the grain is winnowed: on a windy day, with the barn doors open, at both ends, so that the wind blows through, the grain is tossed up, into the air, from a wide, shallow basket, and the wind blows away the chaff, the light husks, and the dust, and the heavy grain falls back, clean, into the basket.

[pause]

{img:winnowing-grain-basket-barn-doors-open-wind-chaff-flying-light}
In the autumn, too, the pigs are taken into the woods, to feed on the acorns, and the beechnuts, which fall, from the trees, in October, and November, and grow fat, for the winter. This is called pannage, and the villagers pay the lord a few pennies, for each pig.

[long-pause]

And the fields are ploughed again, and the wheat, and the rye, are sown, for the next year's harvest, and the cycle begins again.

[pause]

So the year turns, in Elton, as it has turned for centuries: from the ploughing, to the sowing, to the hay, to the harvest, to the threshing, and back to the ploughing. And the villagers know it, and feel it, in their bones, and their hands, and their hearts, so that every season has its work, and its feasts, and its songs, and its own particular light, and smell, and sound.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-orchard-autumn-apples-pears-ladder-baskets-family-picking}
In the autumn, the orchards, and the gardens, of Elton are full of fruit.

[pause]

Behind the houses, in the crofts, and around the manor, there are fruit trees: apples, and pears, and plums, and cherries, and walnuts, and medlars, a strange, brown fruit, which can only be eaten when it is soft, and nearly rotten. And in the hedges, and the woods, there are wild fruits: blackberries, and sloes, and crab apples, and elderberries, and hazelnuts.

[long-pause]

In September, and October, the families pick the fruit, climbing the trees, on wooden ladders, and filling their baskets, and their aprons. Some of the fruit is eaten, fresh. Some is stewed, or baked, with honey. The apples, and the pears, are laid out, carefully, on straw, in the lofts, so that they do not touch, and keep, through the winter. And some are crushed, and pressed, to make cider, and perry.

[pause]

And the children go out, into the woods, and the lanes, to pick blackberries, and gather nuts, and come home with purple fingers, and mouths.

[pause]

{img:beehives-skeps-straw-garden-bench-bees-flowers-sunny-afternoon}
In the gardens, there are beehives, called skeps: round, domed baskets, woven of straw, standing on little wooden benches, or in niches, in the walls, out of the rain.

[long-pause]

Bees are very important, in the year 1300, because honey is the only sweetness most people know. Sugar is rare, and expensive, and comes from far away. So honey is used, in cooking, and baking, and medicine, and to make mead, a sweet, strong drink, of honey, and water, fermented. And the wax is precious too, for candles, for the church, which needs a great deal of it, because only beeswax candles may be burned, on the altar.

[pause]

The villagers keep bees, in their gardens, and the lord keeps bees, at the manor. And in the summer, when a swarm of bees leaves the hive, and flies away, the owner runs after it, banging a pan, or a bell, to make the bees settle, so that he can catch them, in an empty skep, and bring them home. If the swarm settles on a neighbour's land, there may be a quarrel, and the court must decide whose bees they are.

[pause]

{img:herb-garden-medieval-cottage-lavender-sage-rosemary-woman-picking}
And in the gardens, too, there are the herbs, which the women grow, for cooking, and for medicine: sage, and rosemary, and thyme, and parsley, and mint, and fennel, and dill, and lavender, and rue, and tansy, and chamomile, and feverfew, and marigolds.

[long-pause]

The women know the herbs, and what they are good for, from their mothers, and grandmothers: chamomile, for sleep, and calm, and mint, for the stomach, and sage, for sore throats, and feverfew, for headaches, and marigold, for wounds, and lavender, for a sweet smell, in the linen chests, and to keep away the moths.

[pause]

And in the evenings, in the autumn, the women hang bunches of herbs, to dry, from the beams of the houses, so that the whole house smells, sweetly, of sage, and thyme, and lavender, all through the winter, when the fire is burning, and the rain is falling, on the thatch, and the family is sleeping, warm, and safe, inside.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-village-rainy-summer-flooded-fields-1315-grey-sky-quiet}
The year 1300 is a good time, in Elton, and in England. The population has grown, for two hundred years, and the villages are full, and the fields stretch out, further, and further, into the woods, and the heaths, and the fens. There are more people in England than there will be again, for three hundred years.

[pause]

But in the years that follow, hard times come.

[long-pause]

In 1315, and 1316, and 1317, the summers are cold, and wet, and the rain falls, and falls, and the corn rots, in the fields, and the harvests fail, all over northern Europe, three years in a row. It is called the Great Famine. In the villages, people go hungry, and many of the old, and the poor, and the very young, do not survive. Then, the cattle, and the sheep, fall sick, too.

[pause]

And in 1348, and 1349, there comes something even worse: the Black Death, a great plague, which sweeps across Europe, from the east. In England, perhaps a third, or even half, of all the people die, in little more than a year. In Elton, too, many houses stand empty, and many strips, in the fields, have nobody to work them.

[pause]

{img:empty-cottage-overgrown-garden-medieval-village-1350-quiet-sorrow}
We will leave those sad years there, gently, in the past.

[long-pause]

But afterwards, the world changes. With so few people left, the workers are precious, and they know it. They ask for higher wages, and better terms. They refuse to do their labour services, for the lord. They leave their villages, and go to other places, where the pay is better. And the lords cannot stop them.

[pause]

In 1381, there is a great rising, the Peasants' Revolt, when the villagers of the south, and the east, of England, march on London, demanding to be free. The rising fails. But slowly, over the next hundred years, the old bonds of serfdom fade away. The villeins become free tenants, paying rent, in money, for their land, instead of working for the lord. By 1500, there are very few serfs left, in England.

[pause]

{img:tudor-village-elton-farmhouse-stone-1500s-fields-hedges-sheep}
And the open fields, too, slowly change. In many places, over the following centuries, the strips are joined together, and fenced, and hedged, into the fields we know today, with hedges, and walls, and gates. This is called enclosure. At Elton, the old open fields lasted for centuries more.

[long-pause]

The Abbey of Ramsey, which owned Elton, for six hundred years, is closed, in 1539, by King Henry the Eighth, when he closes all the monasteries of England. The monks are sent away, and the abbey is pulled down, and its stones are used for other buildings, and its lands are sold. The manor of Elton passes into other hands.

[pause]

But the village goes on. The people of Elton go on living, and working, and marrying, and raising their children, beside the river Nene, as they always have, generation after generation, and century after century, through all the changes, of the world beyond.

[long-pause]

{img:elton-village-today-stone-cottages-thatch-lane-church-tower-summer}
Today, Elton is still there.

[pause]

It is still a small village, in the countryside of Cambridgeshire, beside the river Nene, with about seven hundred people. Its houses are built, now, of the pale, golden-grey local stone, with roofs of stone slates, and thatch, and gardens full of flowers, along the winding lanes, and around the little green.

[long-pause]

The church of All Saints still stands, in the middle of the village, larger than in 1300, with a tall tower, added in later centuries, and in the churchyard, the two old Anglo-Saxon crosses still stand, weathered, and worn, among the gravestones, and the yew trees, as they stood when the villagers of 1300 passed them, on their way to mass.

[pause]

Down by the river, there is still a mill, at Elton, a later one, built of stone, beside the weir, where the water still pours over, and the river still winds, slowly, through the green meadows, with their willows, and their reeds, and the cows still graze, in the mist, on summer mornings.

[pause]

Near the village stands Elton Hall, a great country house, with towers, and gardens, built over many centuries, partly on the site of an old manor house, and still lived in, today, by the same family, who have owned it for more than three hundred and fifty years.

[long-pause]

And in the fields, and the meadows, around Elton, and all over the English Midlands, if you know how to look, you can still see the old ridge and furrow, the long, gentle waves, in the grass, left by the ploughs of the medieval villagers, in their strips, in the great open fields, seven hundred years ago.

[pause]

The court rolls, and the accounts, of Elton, are kept, today, in the archives, carefully stored, in boxes, in the dry, and the dark. Historians have read them, and translated them, from their Latin, and written books about them, so that the people of Elton, in the year 1300, the reeves, and the ale-wives, and the millers, and the ploughmen, and their quarrels, and their cows, and their marriages, are not forgotten.

[pause]

{img:english-countryside-ridge-and-furrow-meadow-church-tower-distance-golden-evening}
So we leave Elton, tonight, in the year 1300, as the evening falls, at the end of a summer day.

[long-pause]

The ploughmen are coming home, from the fields, with their oxen, slowly, along the lane, and the smoke is rising, from the thatched roofs, and the women are standing in the doorways, calling the children in. The geese are being driven home, from the green, hissing, and grumbling. The church bell is ringing, for vespers, over the fields. Down by the river, the mill wheel has stopped, and the water is pouring, softly, over the weir, and the mist is rising, from the meadows, as the light fades, and the first stars come out, over the quiet village, beside the river.
