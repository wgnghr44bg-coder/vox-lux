Tonight we go more than seven hundred years back. We go to a small village in the middle of England, in the year 1300.

[long-pause]

It is early morning, in spring. The sun is rising over a wide green valley. A slow river winds between meadows and willows and reeds. Mist lies on the water and over the low fields. Cows stand in it up to their knees, quietly eating. A heron flies slowly over the river. And a cuckoo is calling from the woods on the hill.

[pause]

On a gentle rise above the river there is a village. It is a cluster of houses with straw roofs, gardens and fences. They stand along a winding lane. In the middle is a stone church with a little tower. Smoke rises from the roofs into the still morning air.

[pause]

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can easily find the next one. And now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

The village is called Elton. It lies in the east of England, beside the river Nene. A great abbey church stands about ten miles away, in the town of Peterborough.

[pause]

{img:medieval-cottages-lane-thatched-roofs-crofts-gardens-dawn-smoke}
It is not a famous place. Nothing very important ever happened here. It is simply a village, like thousands of other villages in England in the year 1300. People were born here. They grew up and worked in the fields. They married and had children and grew old.

[pause]

But we know a lot about Elton. The village belonged to a great monastery, Ramsey Abbey. And the monks of Ramsey kept careful records. They wrote them in Latin on long rolls of parchment, a kind of paper made from animal skin. They wrote down the village quarrels and fines and agreements. And every year they wrote down how much was sown and harvested and sold.

[long-pause]

Many of these rolls still exist today. In them we can still read the names of the people of Elton, more than seven hundred years ago. And we can read the little stories of their lives. Who brewed ale. Whose cows got into the lord's corn. Who married. Who built a new house. Who quarrelled with a neighbour over a fence.

[pause]

{img:monk-scribe-writing-parchment-roll-court-record-candle-abbey}
Tonight we will spend a whole year in Elton, from spring to spring. We will walk through the fields and meadows. We will visit the mill and the church and the lord's house. We will meet the village leader and the miller and the woman who brews the ale. We will sit in the village court. We will dance on Midsummer's Eve. We will bring in the harvest. And we will ride to a great fair. And all year long, there is one big question. Will the harvest be good enough to get them through the winter?

[long-pause]

So let us walk down the lane into the village in the early morning. There is dew on the grass and the smell of wood smoke. A rooster crows. And the church bell rings softly for the morning prayers.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-village-plan-lane-tofts-crofts-houses-gardens-church-green}
Let us walk through Elton in the year 1300.

[pause]

The village is built along a winding lane. It runs up from the river, past the church, and on toward the fields and the woods. On each side of the lane stand the houses. There are about a hundred and twenty families. Perhaps five or six hundred people in all.

[long-pause]

Each house stands on its own little piece of land, with a fence or a hedge around it. A gate opens onto the lane. Behind the house is a long narrow garden. There the family grows vegetables and herbs. They keep a few fruit trees and hens and geese. Perhaps a pig and a cow.

[pause]

The houses are long and low. They have a frame of wood. The walls are woven branches covered with clay and straw and painted white. The roofs are thick straw or reeds from the river. They are steep and come down low over the walls.

[pause]

{img:medieval-cruck-frame-house-wattle-daub-thatch-garden-hens}
In the middle of the village stands the church. It is built of pale grey stone, with a little tower. Around it is the churchyard with old yew trees. The graves have no stones. They are just little mounds in the grass.

[long-pause]

Near the church, by the river, is the lord's house. It is bigger, of stone and wood. It has a great hall and barns and stables and a tower for doves. A wall goes all around it. But the lord himself does not live here. The lord is the head of the abbey, the abbot. He lives at his great abbey, about fifteen miles away across the wet lands. His helpers run the village for him.

[pause]

Down by the river is the mill. Its great wooden water wheel turns slowly in the stream. The miller's house stands beside it.

[pause]

{img:medieval-watermill-wooden-wheel-river-nene-willows-morning}
All around the village are the fields. They are great wide open fields with no hedges. They are divided into long narrow strips where the villagers grow their grain. Beyond the fields are meadows by the river. There is common land where all the animals graze. And on the higher ground there are woods. The pigs look for acorns there. And the villagers gather firewood.

[long-pause]

The village is busy from dawn to dusk. Children drive geese along the lane. Women carry water from the well. Men go out to the fields with their oxen and ploughs. Dogs bark and pigs root in the mud. The blacksmith hammers at his fire. And the church bell rings the hours.

[pause]

It is a small world, but a complete one. Most people of Elton will live their whole lives here. They will never travel more than a day's walk away. Everything they need is here or close by. Their food and their work. Their families and friends. Their church. And their graves.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-village-smithy-forge-glowing-anvil-smith-hammer-ploughshare}
Not everyone in Elton is a farmer. There are craftsmen too. The most important of them is the smith, the man who works iron.

[pause]

The smithy stands at the edge of the village by the lane. It is a low building of stone and wood, open at the front. Inside is a big stone fireplace where the fire glows red and orange. The smith's boy pumps a big leather bag that blows air into the fire. It wheezes and roars. And the fire glows white hot.

[long-pause]

The smith is a big man with strong arms. He wears a leather apron full of little holes burnt by sparks. He takes the glowing iron from the fire with long tongs. He lays it on a heavy iron block. And he strikes it with his hammer, again and again. Sparks fly. The iron bends and flattens and takes shape. Then he plunges it into a tub of water, where it hisses and steams.

[pause]

He makes and mends everything of iron the village needs. The blades of the ploughs wear down every season, so he sharpens them. He makes curved knives for cutting grain. He makes spades and axes and nails and hinges. He makes locks and keys and pots and chains.

[pause]

{img:smith-shoeing-horse-smithy-doorway-villagers-waiting-chatting}
And he puts shoes on the horses. He lifts their great hooves between his knees. He fits the hot iron shoe. It smokes and smells. And he nails it on.

[long-pause]

The smithy is a meeting place. On a cold morning, people stand around the warm fire and talk while they wait for their tools. Children come to watch the sparks fly.

[pause]

Then there is the carpenter. Almost everything in Elton is made of wood. The houses and barns. The carts and ploughs. The furniture and bowls and spoons. The buckets and barrels. Even the mill wheel.

[pause]

{img:medieval-carpenter-shaping-timber-adze-house-frame-workshop-shavings}
The carpenter works with axes and saws and chisels. He knows every kind of wood and what it is good for. Oak is for the beams of houses. It is strong and lasts for hundreds of years. Ash is for tool handles, because it is springy and tough. Elm is for the middle of cart wheels, because it does not split. Willow and hazel are for baskets and fences.

[long-pause]

When someone builds a new house, the carpenter cuts the big wooden beams in his yard. He fits them together with wooden pegs, without nails. Then on one day, all the neighbours come to help raise the frame. They pull on ropes and push with poles. Will it go up straight? Slowly it rises. And when it stands upright, they have a little feast to celebrate.

[pause]

There are other craftsmen too. A man who makes straw roofs. A man who makes wheels. A man who makes barrels. A potter, a weaver, a tailor and a shoemaker. And many villagers have a little craft of their own, as well as their land.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-villagers-gathered-church-porch-sunday-men-women-children}
Who are the people of Elton?

[pause]

Most of them are peasants. These are farmers who work the land and grow their own food. But they are not all the same. Some are richer and some are poorer. Some are free and some are not.

[long-pause]

The biggest difference is between the free and the unfree. About half of the families in Elton are not fully free. They are not slaves. They have their own houses and land and animals and money. They can buy and sell and marry. They can leave their land to their children. But they are bound to the lord.

[pause]

They must work on the lord's land for a few days every week. At harvest time they must work even more. They must pay him rent, in money and in hens and eggs. They must grind their grain at his mill, and pay for it. They need his permission to leave the village. They even need it to let their daughter marry. And they must pay a fee for it.

[pause]

{img:medieval-peasant-family-doorway-cottage-father-mother-children-ox}
The free families are different. They pay rent for their land. But they do not have to work for the lord or ask his permission. They can leave when they like.

[long-pause]

Among the villagers there are richer and poorer families. The richest have about thirty acres of land, in strips all over the fields. That is enough to feed a big family and sell some grain at the market. They may own oxen or share them with neighbours. Others have half as much, or less. And the poorest have only a cottage and a garden. They must work for others for wages, just to live. In a bad year, they are the first to go hungry.

[pause]

So in Elton, some families are quite comfortable, with big houses and barns and cattle. Others are very poor and live from day to day.

[pause]

{img:medieval-village-woman-spinning-distaff-doorway-chatting-neighbour}
The names in the old rolls sound strange and familiar at the same time. There are Johns and Williams and Richards. There are Agnes and Alice and Emma. And the last names tell us about them. John Carpenter and Richard Smith and Robert Miller had a trade. Henry atte Brook lived by the brook. Alice atte Green lived by the green.

[long-pause]

Many families have lived in Elton for generations. They are related to each other by marriage and by blood. Everybody knows everybody. And everybody knows everybody's business. The same family names appear again and again in the rolls for a hundred years. Fathers and sons and grandsons work the same strips of land in the same fields. And they live in the same houses, along the same lane, beside the same river.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-open-field-strips-ridge-and-furrow-ploughmen-oxen-wide-sky}
Around Elton lie the great open fields.

[pause]

There are two or three of them. Each is huge, hundreds of acres. There are no hedges or fences or walls. They stretch away from the village across the gently rolling land, all the way to the woods.

[long-pause]

Each big field is divided into sections. Each section is divided into long narrow strips, side by side. Each strip is about two hundred metres long and only a few metres wide. A strip is about as much land as a team of oxen can plough in one day.

[pause]

The strips are ploughed the same way year after year. Slowly each strip becomes a long low ridge, with a ditch on each side for the water to drain away. This makes a pattern like long gentle waves across the fields. In many places in England, you can still see it today. It shows in the grass when the sun is low in the evening. Or after a light fall of snow. More than seven hundred years later.

[pause]

{img:ridge-and-furrow-pasture-evening-low-sun-long-shadows-sheep-today}
Each family's land is not in one piece. It is spread out in strips all over the fields. A strip here and two strips there and another far away. That way everyone gets a share of the good land and the poor land. The land by the river and the land on the hill.

[long-pause]

The whole village farms the fields together. One year, one field is sown with wheat in the autumn. Another field is sown with barley and oats and beans in the spring. And the third field rests for a year. The animals graze on it and leave their dung, to make it rich again. The next year they change round.

[pause]

So everyone must plough and sow and harvest at the same time. And everyone must agree on the rules. When can the animals go onto the fields after harvest? Who can cut the hay in the meadows, and how much? Agreeing is not always easy.

[pause]

{img:medieval-ploughman-heavy-plough-eight-oxen-ploughboy-goad-furrow}
The ploughing is done with a heavy wooden plough with an iron blade. A team of oxen pulls it, sometimes eight of them, in pairs. A ploughman holds the handles. A boy walks beside the oxen with a long stick. He sings and calls to them by their names, to keep them walking straight.

[long-pause]

It is slow work. The oxen walk slowly and steadily down the long strip. They turn at the end and come back. Behind them the earth turns over, dark and rich, smelling of soil. Black birds and gulls follow behind, picking up worms.

[pause]

Few families have a whole team of oxen. So neighbours join their oxen together. And they plough each other's strips in turn.

[pause]

{img:medieval-sower-scattering-seed-basket-field-birds-boy-sling}
Then the sower walks along the ploughed strips. A bag of seed hangs from his shoulder. He throws the seed by hand in great sweeps, from side to side. Behind him a horse pulls a heavy frame with teeth. It covers the seed with earth. And children run up and down the fields with slings and clappers. They chase away the crows and pigeons that want to eat the seed.

[long-pause]

And then everyone waits. They watch the sky. They hope for gentle rain and warm sun. And they pray for a good harvest.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-spring-blossom-village-lambs-meadow-children-primroses}
In Elton, the year begins in the spring.

[pause]

In March the days grow longer and the earth starts to warm. The first lambs are born. They wobble about on their long legs. Primroses and violets flower along the banks and under the trees. The rooks build their nests in the tall trees around the church. They caw and quarrel all day long.

[long-pause]

On the twenty-fifth of March there is a church feast called Lady Day. In England in the year 1300, this is the first day of the new year. Rents are paid on Lady Day and new deals are made.

[pause]

In the fields, the spring ploughing and sowing goes on. Barley and oats and peas and beans. The men are out from dawn till dusk with the oxen and the ploughs.

[pause]

{img:medieval-women-planting-garden-croft-beans-leeks-cabbages-spring}
In the gardens behind the houses, the women plant the vegetables. Onions and leeks and garlic. Cabbages and beans and peas. Herbs for cooking and for medicine. And flowers for the church. Lilies and roses and marigolds.

[long-pause]

The animals have spent the winter in the barns, eating hay. Now they are let out onto the meadows. They are thin and stiff. But they kick up their heels and run in the fresh green grass.

[pause]

The hens start laying again. The geese sit on their nests. Soon there are goslings and chicks and ducklings running about the yards and the lanes. The children must look after them. They must keep them safe from the foxes and the crows. A fox can take a whole family of goslings in one night.

[pause]

{img:easter-morning-medieval-church-villagers-candles-flowers-sunrise}
Then comes Easter, the greatest feast of the year.

[long-pause]

For forty days before Easter, the villagers eat no meat and no eggs and no butter and no cheese. Only bread and fish and vegetables and beans. It is a long hungry time at the end of winter. The stores are running low. Everyone looks at the sacks in the barn and wonders. Will there be enough until the new harvest?

[pause]

On Easter Sunday everyone goes to church early in the morning in their best clothes. The church is full of candles and flowers. The bells ring. The priest sings the Easter service. Afterwards, at home, there is a feast with meat and eggs, the first for weeks. The villagers bring eggs to the lord's house, as part of their rent. And they bring some to the priest, as a gift.

[long-pause]

The children play games with the eggs. They roll them down the grassy hills to see whose egg goes furthest without breaking. In some parts of England, children still do this on the Monday after Easter, today.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-children-playing-village-green-hoops-ball-hobby-horse}
Let us think about the children of Elton.

[pause]

There are a great many of them. Families have many children. The lanes and the green and the river bank are full of them. They play and run and shout and work.

[long-pause]

When a baby is born at home, the midwife and the women of the family help. The baby is washed and wrapped tightly in strips of linen. Then it is carried to the church, the same day or the next, to be baptised. Its godparents choose its name. Often they give it their own name. So there are many Johns and many Agneses in Elton.

[pause]

Babies are carried everywhere by their mothers and sisters. They lie in wooden cradles by the fire. The older children rock them.

[pause]

{img:medieval-mother-cradle-baby-cottage-fire-older-sister-rocking}
As soon as they can walk, the children play. They play with balls made of leather stuffed with rags. They play with spinning tops and hoops and hobby horses. They have dolls of wood and cloth. They play hide and seek and tag and leapfrog. They fight with wooden swords and play at being knights and ladies.

[long-pause]

They climb trees and swim in the river in summer. They catch little fish and frogs. They look for birds' nests in the hedges. They make whistles from willow wood and little boats from bark. They sail the boats on the mill stream. In winter they slide on the frozen ponds.

[pause]

But they also work from a young age. At five or six they already help their parents. They look after the geese and the hens and the younger children. They fetch water and gather firewood. They chase birds from the fields. And they pick up stones from the ploughed land.

[pause]

{img:medieval-boy-scaring-crows-field-clapper-sling-spring-morning}
By ten or twelve, the boys help in the fields. They lead the oxen at the plough. They carry and they cut grain. The girls help their mothers spin and brew and cook and milk. And at harvest time, everyone works in the fields.

[long-pause]

Few children in Elton go to school. There is no village school. A few boys may learn their letters from the priest. And a very few, the cleverest or the luckiest, may go away to a school in town or to the abbey. They may become priests or monks or clerks.

[pause]

At about fourteen, children count as almost grown up. Some go to work as servants in other houses, in the village or in town. They earn a little money. And they learn the skills of farming and running a house, before they marry and have homes of their own.

[long-pause]

In the old rolls we sometimes see the children of Elton growing up, from one year to the next. First as the son or daughter of their father. Then years later with land of their own and children of their own. And then at last, their own children taking their place.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-reeve-tally-sticks-manor-barn-grain-sacks-checking}
The most important man in the everyday life of Elton is the reeve.

[pause]

He is not a lord or a knight or a monk. He is one of the villagers, like most of his neighbours. He is a solid sensible farmer with a good piece of land. Every year in autumn, the villagers choose him to run the lord's farm for the coming year.

[long-pause]

The lord's own land lies in strips among the villagers' land. The villagers must work it as part of their duties. And the reeve must organise it all. Who must come to plough, and when. Who must sow and weed and cut and carry. He must make sure the work is done, and done well. And that nobody is lazy or late.

[pause]

He must look after the lord's animals. The oxen and horses and cows. The sheep and pigs and geese. He must sell the lord's grain and wool and cheese at the market. And he must buy what is needed. Iron for the ploughs, salt, nails and cart wheels.

[pause]

{img:reeve-and-hayward-walking-fields-dawn-discussing-crops-staff}
He most likely cannot read or write. So he keeps his accounts on little sticks of hazel wood. He cuts notches in them. A big notch for a pound. A smaller one for a shilling. A tiny one for a penny. Then the stick is split in two down the middle. Each side keeps one half. The two halves must fit together exactly. That way nobody can cheat.

[long-pause]

At the end of the farming year, a clerk comes from the abbey. The reeve sits down with him and goes through everything. He uses his sticks and his memory. The clerk writes it all down in Latin on the great roll. Every sack of grain must be explained. If something is missing, the reeve must pay for it himself. So he worries about it all year.

[pause]

For his work, the reeve does not have to work his own days on the lord's land that year. At harvest he may eat at the lord's table. But it is hard and thankless work. He is caught in the middle. The lord wants as much as possible. And his neighbours want to do as little as possible for the lord.

[pause]

{img:beadle-hayward-medieval-village-officials-horn-field-boundary}
He has helpers. One man calls the villagers to their work and to the court. Another looks after the hedges and fences and meadows. He makes sure the animals do not get into the grain. If they do, he blows his horn. He shuts them in a little stone pen in the village. The owner must pay a fine to get them back.

[long-pause]

And above them all is the steward. The abbot chooses him. He rides from village to village on a good horse. He checks on everything and runs the village court.

[pause]

But it is the reeve who keeps everything turning, through the whole year. Every morning before dawn, he walks through the village and out to the fields.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-manor-house-elton-stone-hall-barns-dovecote-courtyard-river}
Down by the river, beside the church, stands the lord's house of Elton. It is called the manor house.

[pause]

It belongs to the abbot of Ramsey. But he rarely comes here. It is the centre of his farm at Elton. His helpers stay here when they visit. The village court meets here. And the lord's grain and hay and animals are kept here.

[long-pause]

It stands in a courtyard with a wall around it and a gate. Inside is the hall. It is a long tall building of stone and wood with a high roof. A great fire burns in the middle of the floor. At one end there is a raised platform with a table for the steward and his guests.

[pause]

Around the courtyard are the other buildings. The kitchen stands on its own, in case of fire. There is a bakehouse and a brewhouse and a dairy. There is a store for grain, raised on stone pillars to keep the rats out. There are great barns and stables and sheds for cows and pigs and sheep. And there is a round tower for doves.

[pause]

{img:medieval-dovecote-round-stone-doves-flying-courtyard-morning}
The dove tower is made of stone. Inside are hundreds of little nesting holes in the walls, from floor to roof. Hundreds of pigeons live there. They fly in and out through a hole in the roof. Only the lord may keep a dove tower. The young pigeons are eaten at his table. But every day the doves fly out over the fields. And they eat the villagers' grain. The villagers do not like that at all.

[long-pause]

Some servants live and work here all year. There are ploughmen and carters. There is the shepherd and the cowherd and the swineherd. And there is the dairymaid, who makes the lord's butter and cheese. They are paid partly in money and partly in grain. They eat together in the hall or the kitchen.

[pause]

And a few times a year, there is great excitement. The steward is coming. Or even the abbot himself, with his monks and servants and horses.

[pause]

{img:abbot-arriving-manor-horseback-monks-servants-villagers-bowing}
Then everything must be cleaned and swept. The rushes on the hall floor are changed. Fresh herbs are spread. The best food is made ready. Chickens and pigeons and fish and white bread. Wine is brought from town. Everyone is nervous. Will the abbot be pleased? The villagers come to see him ride in on his fine horse, in his black robes. And they bow as he passes.

[long-pause]

The abbot stays a night or two. He looks at the fields and barns and animals and accounts. He listens to the reeve and gives his orders. Then he rides on to his next village. And Elton breathes again and goes back to its everyday life.

[pause]

In the evenings, when the visitors have gone, the manor house is quiet. The servants sit around the fire in the hall. They eat their supper and talk. The dogs sleep by the fire. And the doves coo softly in their tower, as night falls over the river.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-watermill-interior-millstones-hopper-flour-dust-light-beams}
Down by the river at Elton is the mill.

[pause]

It is a wooden building with a straw roof. It stands beside a channel dug from the river. A small dam and a wooden gate hold the water back. When the gate opens, the water rushes through. It turns the great wooden water wheel on the side of the mill.

[long-pause]

The wheel turns slowly and steadily. It creaks and splashes and drips. Inside the mill, wooden gears pass the turning to the millstones. There are two great round stones, one on top of the other. The bottom one stays still. The top one turns. The grain is ground between them into flour.

[pause]

The grain is poured into a wooden funnel above the stones. It trickles down through a hole in the top stone. The flour comes out at the edges, soft and white and warm. It falls into a trough and into the sacks. The whole mill hums and rumbles. The air is full of fine white dust. It settles on everything. And it settles on the miller, so he is white all over, from his hair to his boots.

[pause]

{img:miller-floury-clothes-taking-toll-grain-bowl-villager-sack}
The mill belongs to the lord. The villagers must bring their grain here and nowhere else. And they must pay the miller. He keeps a small part of their grain, measured with a special bowl.

[long-pause]

The villagers are not allowed to grind their own grain at home with small hand stones. If they are caught, they are fined at the village court. Many do it anyway, secretly, because it is cheaper. And they listen for footsteps at the door.

[pause]

The miller is not always popular. People suspect he takes more than his share. Maybe his bowl is a little too big. Maybe he mixes a little sand into the flour. There are many old jokes and songs about millers who cheat. In a famous old book of stories, there is a miller with a thumb of gold. That means he knows just how to press his thumb on the scales.

[pause]

{img:river-nene-mill-pond-reflections-ducks-evening-calm-willows}
But the mill is also a meeting place. While they wait, the villagers sit on the bank outside in the sunshine. They talk and share the news. The children play by the water. They watch the great wheel turn. And they fish in the mill pond.

[long-pause]

The mill pond is full of fish. The lord takes eels as part of the rent. Thousands of eels every year. They are caught in woven traps in the mill stream. They are sent to the abbey for the monks to eat on days when they may not eat meat.

[pause]

In the evening, when the work is done, the miller closes the gate. The great wheel slows down and stops. The mill falls silent. The only sound is the water running over the dam. And the frogs and water birds calling in the reeds.

[long-pause]

{img:river-nene-medieval-flat-boat-stone-cargo-willows-summer-afternoon}
The river Nene flows past Elton, slow and quiet, between its meadows. It comes from the hills in the west. It flows east toward the wet lands and the sea.

[pause]

It is a gentle river, wide and slow and full of life. Reeds and yellow water lilies grow along its edges. Willows lean over it. There are little islands and quiet backwaters where the water is still and dark and deep.

[long-pause]

For the people of Elton, the river gives food and work and pleasure. It is full of fish. Eels most of all. And pike and perch and other fish. The fishing belongs to the lord. Some villagers rent it and pay with eels for the monks. Others fish quietly on summer evenings with a line and a worm. Sometimes they are caught and fined at the court.

[pause]

In the reed beds, the villagers cut reeds for their roofs. They cut rushes for the floors of their houses and the church. They make baskets and mats. And they make small cheap lights. They peel a rush and dip it in fat and burn it in the evening.

[pause]

{img:villagers-cutting-reeds-riverbank-bundles-boat-heron-morning}
The river is also a road. In the year 1300 it is easier to carry heavy things by water than along muddy roads. Flat boats move along the river. Men push them with poles or pull them from the bank. They carry grain and wood and wool and salt and stone.

[long-pause]

Not far from Elton there are famous quarries of fine pale stone. For hundreds of years it has been used to build the great churches and abbeys of the east of England. Some of the stone travels by boat along the rivers to building sites far away.

[pause]

The boatmen and traders sometimes stop at Elton. They buy ale and bread. And they tell the news of the towns and the abbeys and the world beyond.

[pause]

{img:river-nene-evening-children-swimming-fishing-meadow-sunset}
In summer, the children of Elton swim and splash in the shallows by the meadows. The young men swim across and back to show how strong they are. In the evenings, people walk along the banks. They watch the fish rise and the swallows skim over the water. And the kingfisher darts from the willows, like a little blue flame.

[long-pause]

But the river can also be dangerous. In winter and in wet springs it floods. It spreads out over the meadows in a great sheet of grey water. Sometimes it creeps into the lower part of the village. People move their animals to higher ground and watch the water rise. The old rolls sadly record people who fell into the river and could not be saved. We will leave them there, gently, with a quiet thought.

[pause]

Then the waters go down. The meadows are green again, and even richer, because of the mud the flood left behind. And the river flows on gently past the village, as it always has.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-family-cottage-dawn-may-mother-fire-pottage-children-waking}
Let us spend a day in May with a family of Elton.

[pause]

They have about fifteen acres of land in strips across the fields. There is the father, Robert, and the mother, Agnes. There are four children, two boys and two girls. And Robert's old mother lives with them, in a little room at the end of the house.

[long-pause]

They wake at dawn, at about four o'clock, when the birds start to sing. Light comes in through the doorway and the little window. Agnes gets up first. She blows on the ashes of the fire in the middle of the floor. She adds some sticks and the fire flares up. She hangs the pot over it and warms yesterday's thick soup.

[pause]

They eat breakfast quickly. A piece of dark heavy bread and a drink of weak ale. Everyone drinks ale, even the children. The water is not always safe.

[pause]

{img:medieval-father-son-walking-to-fields-hoe-scythe-dawn-mist}
Then Robert goes out to the fields with his eldest son, who is fourteen. Today it is his turn to work on the lord's land. He must weed the barley. He pulls out the thistles and the weeds that would choke the crop.

[long-pause]

At home, Agnes milks the cow and feeds the hens and the pig. She lets the geese out onto the green. Her youngest daughter, who is seven, must watch them. Then Agnes fetches water from the well in a wooden bucket. She weeds the beans and onions in the garden. She picks herbs and young nettles for the soup.

[pause]

Her older daughter, who is twelve, sits at the door in the sun, spinning wool into thread. She minds the baby. He sits in the dust playing with a wooden toy and a puppy.

[pause]

{img:medieval-woman-brewing-ale-vat-cottage-yard-barley-malt}
And then Agnes brews. She is one of the ale-wives of Elton. Several times a month she makes ale in a big wooden tub. When it is ready, she puts a leafy branch on a pole outside her door. That is the sign. Her neighbours come with their jugs to buy it.

[long-pause]

At midday, the old grandmother takes a basket out to the fields. There is bread and cheese and onions and a jug of ale. Robert and the boy sit at the edge of the strip in the shade of a bush, with the other men. They eat and rest and talk. They look at the young barley. It looks good this year. But it is still a long way to harvest.

[pause]

In the afternoon they work again until evening. Then the church bell rings, and they walk home through the fields. They are tired and dusty. The sun is low behind them. And the swallows swoop over the grain.

[pause]

{img:medieval-family-supper-cottage-evening-bench-bowls-door-open-sunset}
At home, Agnes has made supper. It is thick soup of peas and beans and oats and onions and herbs. There is a little bacon in it. It has cooked all afternoon over the fire. There is bread and cheese and ale too. They eat together on benches around the table, with wooden bowls and spoons. The door is open to the long golden May evening.

[long-pause]

As the light fades, they sit on the bench by the door. They talk with the neighbours over the fence. The children play in the lane until it is dark. Then they go in and bar the door. They cover the fire. They lie down on their straw mattresses under their blankets. And they sleep, while the nightingale sings in the bushes by the river.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-village-women-washing-linen-river-bank-beating-paddles-chatting}
The women of Elton work as hard as the men. Often harder.

[pause]

They run the house and the garden and the yard. They cook and bake and brew. They make butter and cheese. They spin and weave and sew and mend clothes for the whole family. They look after the hens and the geese and the pigs and the cow. They fetch the water and the firewood. And they care for the children and the old and the sick.

[long-pause]

In the busy seasons, at haymaking and harvest, they work in the fields too. They rake and tie bundles beside the men. Some work for wages on the lord's farm. They are usually paid a little less than the men.

[pause]

On washing days, the women gather by the river with baskets of linen. They wash it in the shallows. They beat it with wooden paddles on flat stones. They spread it out to dry on the grass and the bushes. And they talk and laugh and share the news of the village.

[pause]

{img:medieval-woman-making-cheese-dairy-wooden-press-churn-cottage}
Many women of Elton brew ale to sell. In the old rolls, the names of the ale-wives appear again and again. Each one pays a few pennies at the court. It is really a kind of fee that every brewer pays. Brewing is one of the few ways a woman can earn her own money.

[long-pause]

And some women have land of their own. When a man dies, his widow usually keeps his land. She must manage it. She must organise the ploughing and the harvest. She must pay the rent and do the work, or pay someone else to do it. Many widows appear in the old rolls. They come to the court to stand up for their rights.

[pause]

A widow with land is a good match. Often she marries again quite soon. And her new husband works the land with her.

[pause]

{img:medieval-wife-husband-marketing-eggs-cheese-basket-lane-morning}
Marriages in Elton are often arranged by the families. Land and animals and money are involved. The lord must give permission too. But the young people usually choose each other as well. And many marriages are happy ones.

[long-pause]

The wedding takes place at the church door, in the porch. The priest asks the couple if they take each other freely. The groom gives the bride a ring and some coins. Then they go into the church for the service. Afterwards there is a feast at the bride's house. There is ale and food and music and dancing. And the whole village comes.

[pause]

Then the bride goes to her new home and her new life. She is a wife now, and soon a mother. She has her own house and garden and fire, in the village where she has always lived.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-may-day-young-people-gathering-hawthorn-blossom-dawn-woods}
On the first of May, the young people of Elton go out to gather the May.

[pause]

Before dawn, while the stars are still out, they get up quietly. They go out together into the woods and fields. They gather branches of hawthorn covered with white blossom. They gather flowers and green leaves. And they wash their faces in the dew. People say it makes you beautiful for the whole year.

[long-pause]

As the sun rises, they come back into the village singing. Their arms are full of blossom and green branches. They decorate the doors and windows of the houses and the church. The whole village smells of blossom, sweet and heavy, in the morning air.

[pause]

On the green they put up a tall pole decorated with flowers and ribbons. It is the maypole. They dance around it in a ring, holding hands. They sing the old songs to the music of a pipe and a drum and a fiddle.

[pause]

{img:villagers-dancing-ring-around-maypole-green-flowers-pipe-drum}
A girl is chosen to be the Queen of the May. She is crowned with flowers. She sits on a seat covered with greenery and watches the dancing. There are games and races and wrestling and archery. The ale-wives bring out their ale. And everyone eats and drinks and dances all day long.

[long-pause]

The church does not always approve. Some priests think the May games are too wild. They come from very old times, before Christianity. But the villagers love them. And they go on year after year, for hundreds of years.

[pause]

Later in spring there is another holiday. And often there is a church ale. That is a big feast held by the village to raise money for the church.

[pause]

{img:church-ale-feast-trestle-tables-churchyard-villagers-barrels}
The church helpers brew a lot of ale, from grain the villagers have given. They set up tables in the churchyard or in a barn. Everyone comes and pays a little to eat and drink. The money is used to mend the church roof, or buy a new bell, or candles.

[long-pause]

There is music and dancing and games. Sometimes a travelling singer comes by. He sings old songs and tells stories of knights and outlaws and kings, in the churchyard or on the green.

[pause]

And in the long light evenings of late spring, the young men and women of Elton walk together. They walk along the river and through the fields, among the flowering hedges and the green grain. And many of the marriages of Elton begin on these May evenings.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-haymaking-meadow-river-men-scythes-row-june-morning}
In June it is time to make the hay.

[pause]

The meadows by the river are some of the most valuable land in Elton. All spring, the grass has been growing thick and tall. It is full of flowers. Buttercups and clover and daisies. The meadows are fenced off so no animals can eat the grass.

[long-pause]

The hay is precious. It is the only food for the oxen and horses and cows all winter. Without enough hay, the animals would starve. Without oxen there could be no ploughing. And without ploughing, there would be no bread.

[pause]

The meadows are divided into strips, like the fields, marked with little posts or stones. Each family has its share.

[pause]

Early in the morning, while the dew is still on the grass, the mowers go out. They walk into the meadow in a long line, one behind the other. Each man has a scythe. That is a long curved blade on a long wooden handle. They swing them slowly and steadily, all together, in a rhythm. The tall grass falls in long rows behind them, with a soft swishing sound.

[long-pause]

Now and then they stop to sharpen their blades with a stone. The sound rings across the meadow. Skylarks rise singing from the grass. And a hidden bird in the meadow calls with a strange rasping cry.

[pause]

Behind the mowers come the women and children with wooden rakes and forks. They turn the grass so it dries in the sun. Then they rake it into rows. And then into little heaps all across the meadow.

[pause]

{img:women-children-raking-hay-haycocks-meadow-afternoon-sunshine}
Everyone hopes for dry weather. If it rains, the hay will rot. It must be spread out and dried all over again. So they watch the sky anxiously. Is that a dark cloud in the west? They work as fast as they can, from dawn till dusk, while the sun shines.

[long-pause]

The lord's hay is cut first. And at the end of the mowing there is an old custom. The mowers are given a sheep, or some money, for a feast. Sometimes each man may take as much hay as he can lift on the handle of his scythe. But it must not touch the ground. If the handle breaks or the hay falls, he gets nothing. And everyone laughs.

[pause]

Then the hay is loaded onto carts, piled high, and carried home to the barns. The meadows lie bare and green and short. And the animals are let back onto them to graze.

[pause]

{img:haycart-piled-high-oxen-lane-village-evening-children-riding-top}
The children ride home on top of the last cart. They sit high up in the sweet-smelling hay, in the evening light, singing. And the whole village smells of hay for weeks.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-shepherd-crook-dog-flock-sheep-common-pasture-hillside}
On the common land above Elton, there are the sheep.

[pause]

The sheep of this time are smaller than sheep today. They have thinner bodies and longer legs, and many have horns. But their wool is the great treasure of England. English wool is the best in Europe. Traders from across the sea pay high prices for it. Much of England's wealth comes from the backs of sheep.

[long-pause]

The lord has a big flock. The villagers have a few sheep each too. They all graze together on the common land. And the shepherd looks after them all.

[pause]

The shepherd is a quiet patient man. He spends his days and often his nights with the sheep, in all weathers. He has his crook and his dog and a bag with bread and cheese. He carries a little pot of tar for the sheep's cuts. And he has a pipe made of wood or bone. He plays it to pass the time.

[pause]

{img:shepherd-hut-lambing-fold-hurdles-night-lantern-ewes-lambs}
In spring, when the lambs are born, he hardly sleeps at all. He stays with the mother sheep day and night, in a shelter of woven fences. He holds up his lantern. He helps the lambs to be born. He keeps them warm and safe from the cold and the foxes. On cold nights, he does not close his eyes. For this he is given a lamb or two of his own every year.

[long-pause]

In summer the sheep are washed in the river to clean their wool. A few days later their wool is cut off.

[pause]

Shearing is a big event. The shearers sit or kneel on the grass or the barn floor. They catch the sheep one by one and hold them firmly between their knees. With big iron shears, like large scissors, they cut off the wool close to the skin. They work quickly and carefully. The whole fleece comes off in one piece, like a soft thick blanket.

[pause]

{img:sheep-shearing-day-barn-shearers-iron-shears-fleeces-rolled}
The fleeces are rolled up and tied and weighed. Wool traders come from the towns to buy them. Or they are taken to the great fairs. From there they go by ship across the sea.

[long-pause]

At the end of the shearing there is a feast with food and ale. There is singing and dancing on the grass.

[pause]

The sheep give milk too, which is made into cheese. And their dung is precious for the fields. At night, the sheep are sometimes kept in small pens on the resting fields. The pens are moved each day. So the dung falls on the land and makes it rich. The villagers value this very much.

[long-pause]

And on summer evenings, the shepherd sits on the hillside above the village. He watches the sun go down over the valley and the river. The sheep graze around him. The first lights come on in the village below. And he plays a little tune on his pipe that drifts down softly over the fields.

[long-pause]

{img:midsummer-eve-bonfire-medieval-village-hill-dancing-silhouettes-twilight}
On the twenty-third of June it is Midsummer Eve. It is the evening before the feast of Saint John.

[pause]

It is the longest day of the year, or close to it. It is the shortest night. The sun sets late, after nine o'clock. The twilight lasts almost until midnight. And in the north, the sky never gets quite dark.

[long-pause]

On Midsummer Eve, villages all over England light bonfires. Elton does too. Great fires of wood and branches burn on the green and on the hill above the village. People dance around them and sing. The young men and the girls hold hands and leap over the flames, for luck.

[pause]

The doors of the houses are decorated with green birch branches. They hang up a little yellow flower called Saint John's wort. People say it keeps away bad spirits. And the lamps are left burning all night long.

[pause]

{img:midsummer-doorway-decorated-birch-st-johns-wort-flowers-lamp}
Some people say the fairies are out on Midsummer Eve. They say strange things can happen in the woods and fields. If a girl looks into a well or a pool at midnight, she will see the face of the man she will marry. Some girls are brave enough to try. They creep to the well in the half dark and look down. The old women tell stories by the fire about Midsummer nights long ago.

[long-pause]

The children may stay up late to see the fires. They run about in the half light. They wave burning sticks and make circles of light in the air. Then they fall asleep on the grass, wrapped in their mothers' cloaks.

[pause]

The next day is the feast of Saint John. It is a holiday. There is a service in the church. And rents are paid on Midsummer Day.

[pause]

{img:short-summer-night-village-fields-glowing-north-sky-embers}
The fires slowly die down to glowing embers. In the middle of the night, people go home to bed. The northern sky still glows faintly. And only a few hours later, the birds start to sing again. Dawn comes so early on midsummer morning.

[long-pause]

After Midsummer there is a quiet time between the haymaking and the harvest. The grain grows in the fields, first green and then golden. The villagers mend their tools and carts and barns. They weed the fields. And they wait and watch the sky. Will the weather hold until harvest?

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-village-church-interior-painted-walls-rood-screen-candles-light}
In the middle of Elton stands the church of All Saints.

[pause]

It is the biggest building in the village, and the oldest, and the most beautiful. It is built of stone with thick walls. It has a little tower with bells. You can see it from everywhere across the fields and the meadows. And when the bells ring, you can hear it from everywhere too.

[long-pause]

Inside it is cool and dim and quiet. It smells of stone and candle wax and incense. There are no benches. People stand or kneel on the stone floor, which is covered with rushes. Only the old and the sick may sit, on a stone bench along the wall. Some say that is where an old saying comes from. The weakest go to the wall.

[pause]

The walls are covered with paintings in red and yellow and black and white. They show the saints and the life of Jesus. Above the arch there is a painting of the end of the world. Good souls go up to heaven on one side. Bad souls go down on the other. Most villagers cannot read. So the paintings are their book. They tell them the stories of the Bible.

[pause]

{img:rood-screen-carved-wood-crucifix-chancel-medieval-candles-altar}
A carved wooden screen stands across the church. It divides the people from the altar. On top of it stands a great cross, lit by candles.

[long-pause]

Beyond the screen, the priest says the service every day at the altar. He speaks Latin. The villagers do not understand it. But they know the sounds and the rhythm and the bells. They know when to kneel and when to cross themselves. And they know when to look up, as the priest lifts the bread above his head and the little bell rings.

[pause]

The church is the centre of village life. Every baby in Elton is baptised here, at the big stone bowl by the door. Every couple is married here, at the church door. And every villager is buried here, in the churchyard, in the shadow of the tower.

[pause]

{img:medieval-wedding-church-porch-couple-priest-villagers-ring-spring}
On Sundays and feast days, everyone comes in their best clothes. Afterwards they gather in the churchyard. They talk and arrange business and hear the news. And the children play among the graves.

[long-pause]

In Elton's churchyard there are still two old stone crosses today. They were carved more than a thousand years ago. They were already very old in 1300. The villagers would have known them and touched them and walked past them every Sunday.

[pause]

The church year gives the village its rhythm. Christmas and Easter and the feasts of the saints. Each one is a holy day and a holiday. Nobody works. There is church and feasting and games. There are about forty or fifty of them every year, as well as the Sundays.

[pause]

{img:medieval-church-tower-bells-evening-swifts-fields-village-roofs}
And the church bells ring every day to mark the hours of prayer. At dawn and noon and dusk. People out in the fields, far from the village, can hear them. They know the time. They stop for a moment and say a prayer. And then they go on with their work.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-pilgrims-walking-road-staffs-scallop-badges-spring-fields}
Once in their lives, perhaps, some people of Elton go on a pilgrimage.

[pause]

A pilgrimage is a journey to a holy place. Often it is a place where a saint is buried. People go there to pray and give thanks and ask for help. Help with an illness, or for a child, or to be forgiven. Or simply out of love.

[long-pause]

The great pilgrimages to Rome or Jerusalem are far too long and costly for most villagers. But there are holy places in England too. The most famous is at Canterbury, the grave of Saint Thomas. Closer to Elton is a shrine of Mary at Walsingham. People come there from all over England, even kings and queens.

[pause]

And there are smaller holy places nearby, which can be reached in a day or two. The great abbeys in the area all have the bones of saints.

[pause]

The pilgrims set out in spring or summer, when the roads are dry and the days are long. They go in a little group, with walking sticks and bags and cloaks. They sew a little money into their clothes, in case of robbers. The priest blesses them at the church before they go.

[long-pause]

They walk all day along roads and tracks, through fields and woods and towns. They sing hymns and tell stories to pass the time. At night they stay in inns or in the guest houses of monasteries. Sometimes they sleep in barns.

[pause]

They meet other pilgrims on the road and share their stories and their food. And they see things they have never seen before. New villages and towns. Great cathedrals. And even the sea.

[pause]

{img:candlelit-shrine-medieval-pilgrims-kneeling-offerings-candles-gold}
At last they arrive. They go into the great church. It is full of candles and gold and the smell of incense and the murmur of prayers. They kneel before the shrine and pray. They leave a gift. A coin, or a candle. Or a little wax model of what they want healed. An arm, or a leg, or an eye.

[long-pause]

Then they buy a little badge made of metal, from the shrine. It shows the saint or a holy picture. They pin it to their hat or their cloak. So everyone they meet on the way home knows where they have been.

[pause]

Many of these little badges have been found by archaeologists. Some were found on river banks. Pilgrims lost them long ago, or threw them in for luck.

[pause]

{img:returning-pilgrim-village-lane-family-welcome-badge-hat-evening}
When the pilgrims come home to Elton after a week or two, the whole village comes out to meet them. They are tired and footsore and happy. And they tell their stories again and again by the fire, for the rest of their lives. The long road. The great church. The candles and the crowds. And the sea.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-parish-priest-simple-robes-walking-lane-greeting-villagers}
The priest of Elton lives beside the church. His house is a little bigger than the others. He has a garden and a barn. And he has some land of his own in the fields, which he farms like the villagers.

[pause]

He is not a monk like the monks of Ramsey. He is a village priest. He lives among his people. He knows them all, from their baptism to their burial.

[long-pause]

The villagers give him one tenth of everything they grow and make each year. This is called the tithe. A tenth of the grain and the hay. A tenth of the lambs and the wool and the eggs. At harvest, every tenth bundle of grain in every field is left aside for the priest. It is carried to his own barn.

[pause]

The villagers do not always like paying it. There are arguments and grumbles. But they also know the priest uses some of it to help the poor and the sick, and to look after the church.

[pause]

{img:priest-visiting-sick-villager-cottage-bed-candle-comfort}
The priest is often a local man. He may be the son of a farmer or a craftsman. He has learned to read and some Latin from another priest. He knows the service and the prayers and the stories of the saints by heart. Some priests are learned and some are not. Some are very good men and some are not so good. But most are simply ordinary men, doing their best.

[long-pause]

He holds the service every morning. He baptises the babies and marries the young couples and buries the dead. Once a year at least, the villagers tell him their sins, and he tells them how to make up for them. He visits the sick and the dying. He carries holy oil and bread through the village, with a little bell and a candle. As he passes, the people kneel down in the lane.

[pause]

He teaches the children their prayers in Latin. They learn them by heart without understanding every word. Sometimes he teaches a clever boy to read and write. One day that boy may become a priest himself, or a clerk.

[pause]

{img:priest-teaching-boy-letters-wax-tablet-church-porch-sunlight}
He helps the villagers in other ways too. He writes letters for them and reads documents. He witnesses their agreements. He gives advice and settles quarrels. And sometimes he speaks up for them to the lord's men.

[long-pause]

In the evening, when his work is done, the priest sits in his garden among his beehives and apple trees. He reads his prayer book in the last of the light. The bees hum. And the swallows fly in and out of the church tower beside him.

[long-pause]

{img:manor-court-hallmote-great-hall-steward-clerk-villagers-standing}
Several times a year, Elton holds its village court.

[pause]

It meets in the great hall of the lord's house. In summer it sometimes meets outside, under a big tree on the green. Every villager must come. The day before, a man goes round the village to tell everyone. Those who stay away without a good reason are fined.

[long-pause]

The steward leads the court. He is the abbot's man and rides over from the abbey. A clerk comes with him to write everything down on the roll. But the decisions are made by the villagers themselves. A group of about twelve older respected men decide. They know the customs of the village and what is fair.

[pause]

The court deals with everything that happens in the village. Land and inheritance and marriage. Quarrels and debts and damage. And the breaking of village rules.

[pause]

{img:clerk-writing-court-roll-quill-parchment-table-steward-listening}
When a villager dies, his son or widow comes to the court to take over his land. They pay a fee to the lord. The clerk writes it down. When a young man wants to marry and needs land, the court arranges it. When someone wants to move away from the village, they must ask permission and pay a little every year.

[long-pause]

And then there are the little wrongs of village life. They fill the old rolls year after year.

[pause]

Someone's cow got into the lord's wheat and ate it. Someone's pigs got into the meadow. Someone ploughed one furrow too far, into a neighbour's strip. Someone cut wood in the lord's wood without asking. Someone did not come to work on the lord's land. Someone's ale was too weak. Someone left a dung heap in the lane. Someone called their neighbour a thief in a quarrel at the well.

[pause]

{img:villagers-arguing-politely-court-witnesses-jurors-medieval-hall}
Everyone waits to hear their name called. For each of these things the court gives a small fine. A few pennies, or sixpence. Or it orders that the damage be paid for, or the fence mended by a certain day. And the clerk writes it all down in his neat Latin. John, son of Richard, for his cows in the lord's corn, sixpence.

[long-pause]

The court also makes the village rules. When the harvest will start, so nobody begins too early. Who may pick up leftover grain after the harvest. How many animals each family may keep on the common. Who must keep the ditches clean.

[pause]

Thanks to these rolls, we know about the little things of ordinary people seven hundred years ago. The cows and the fences and the quarrels and the ale. Without the rolls, they would have been forgotten long ago.

[pause]

{img:medieval-village-ale-tasters-testing-ale-cup-doorway-bush-sign}
There are also two ale tasters, chosen every year from the villagers. Their job is to taste the ale of every ale-wife. They check that it is good and sold at the right price. It sounds like a nice job. But the tasters must report their own neighbours and friends to the court if the ale is bad. And that is not always easy.

[long-pause]

{img:view-of-frankpledge-villagers-gathered-tithing-groups-steward-green}
In Elton, every man over twelve belongs to a small group called a tithing.

[pause]

A tithing is about ten men, all neighbours. They are responsible for each other. If one of them does something wrong, the others must bring him to the court. If they do not, the whole group is fined. It is a very old system. It keeps the peace by making everyone look after everyone else.

[long-pause]

Each group has a leader. Once or twice a year, all the groups must come to a special session of the court. They must report anything that has happened in the village that the court should know about.

[pause]

So the court hears about the broken fences and the blocked ditches. About the stray animals and the quarrels and the weak ale. And about new people who have come to live in the village. They must join a group too.

[pause]

{img:village-watch-night-lantern-men-walking-lane-quiet-houses}
There is also a night watch. In the summer months, some men take turns to walk through the village at night. They carry a lantern and a stick. They watch for fire and for strangers. A fire in one straw roof could spread to the whole lane in minutes. So they keep the village safe while the others sleep.

[long-pause]

And if something goes wrong, there is the hue and cry. Anyone who sees something wrong must shout loudly. Everyone who hears must come running to help. They follow from village to village if they must, until the matter is settled. If someone shouts for nothing, he is fined. If someone does not come when he should, he is fined too.

[pause]

Most of what happens in Elton is small and ordinary. It is settled peacefully among neighbours. People quarrel and make up. They borrow and lend and forget to pay back, and are reminded at the court. They help each other at harvest and in sickness and in trouble.

[pause]

{img:neighbours-helping-rebuild-fence-lane-handshake-medieval-village}
The village is held together not only by the lord and the court and the rules. It is held together by the people themselves. They must live together all their lives in the same small place. And they depend on each other for everything. For the ploughing and the harvest. For the oxen and the hay. And for help when it is needed.

[long-pause]

They know each other's families and stories and quarrels and kindnesses, going back for generations. They stand beside each other at baptisms and weddings and funerals. They pray together and work together and feast together. And when the court needs twelve men to judge a quarrel, they turn to their neighbours. The old wise men of the village.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-harvest-golden-wheat-reapers-sickles-women-binding-sheaves-august}
In August, it is harvest time. It is the most important time of the whole year.

[pause]

The grain in the fields has turned from green to gold. Wheat and barley and oats stand tall and heavy and ripe. They rustle in the warm wind under a blue sky. The whole year depends on the next few weeks. If the harvest is good, there will be bread all winter. If it is bad, there will be hunger.

[long-pause]

On the first of August there is a feast called Lammas. That means loaf mass. The first loaf made from the new wheat is brought to the church and blessed. People give thanks for the harvest that is coming.

[pause]

Then the harvest begins. The court has decided the day. Nobody may start before it. And then everyone who can work goes out into the fields. Every man and woman and child. They work from dawn to dusk for weeks.

[pause]

{img:reapers-row-sickles-cutting-wheat-binders-following-stooks-sun}
The grain is cut with small curved knives. The reaper grabs a bunch of stalks with one hand. With the other he cuts them low, near the ground. He lays them down in a row. It is slow hard work. They bend over all day long in the heat. The sweat runs down. The dust and the straw scratch.

[long-pause]

Behind the reapers come the binders, often the women. They gather the cut grain and tie it into bundles with a twist of straw. Then they stand the bundles up together in little groups. The heads of grain point up so they can dry in the sun and wind.

[pause]

The lord's grain must be cut first. On special days, every family must send everyone who can work. Sometimes hundreds of people cut the lord's fields all at once.

[pause]

{img:boon-work-harvest-lunch-field-bread-cheese-ale-herrings-workers-resting}
On those days the lord gives the workers food and drink in the fields. Bread and cheese and fish and ale. Sometimes meat and soup. At midday they sit down in the stubble in the shade of the bundles. They eat and drink and rest together in the heat.

[long-pause]

When the grain has dried, it is loaded onto carts. It is carried home to the barns and piled up in big stacks. The stacks are covered with straw against the rain. Everyone watches the sky. One heavy storm now could ruin a whole field. Only when the last cart is in the barn can they breathe easy.

[pause]

After the harvest, the poor of the village may go into the fields. The old, the widows and the children. They pick up the heads of grain left behind on the ground, one by one. They take them home in their aprons and baskets. It is a precious right. And the court makes sure it is respected.

[pause]

{img:gleaners-women-children-stubble-field-evening-picking-ears-corn}
When the last bundle is cut in the last field, there is a great shout. The last bundle is decorated with flowers and ribbons. It is carried home in triumph on the last cart. The reapers ride on top, singing.

[long-pause]

And then there is the harvest supper, the greatest feast of the year. There is meat and pies and bread and cheese and ale. There is music and dancing in the lord's barn or on the green. It goes on late into the night. And the harvest moon rises, huge and golden, over the stubble fields.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-st-ives-fair-river-ouse-stalls-booths-merchants-crowds-spring}
Once a year, at Easter, some villagers of Elton go to the greatest fair in England. It is the fair at Saint Ives.

[pause]

Saint Ives is a small town on another river, about twenty-five miles from Elton. Like Elton, it belongs to the abbot of Ramsey. Every year from Easter, for several weeks, there is a great fair. Traders come from all over England, and from across the sea.

[long-pause]

The fair fills the whole town. The main street and the fields around it are covered with stalls and tents in long rows. The traders lay out their goods. And the river is crowded with boats bringing goods from the coast.

[pause]

There are traders from Flanders with fine cloth. There are traders from France with wine. From Italy with silks and spices. From Germany and the north with furs and wood and iron. And English traders from London and York come to buy wool, the great wealth of England.

[pause]

{img:medieval-fair-cloth-merchant-booth-bolts-colourful-cloth-buyers}
There is everything to buy. Cloth of every colour, leather and furs. Rope and iron and pots and knives and tools. Horses and cattle and sheep. Salt and dried fish. Spices like pepper and ginger and cinnamon. Almonds and figs and raisins. And sugar, which is very rare and very costly.

[long-pause]

For the villagers of Elton, it is a great adventure. They walk or ride in a cart for a whole day to get there. They arrive in the crowded town. They wander wide-eyed among the stalls. There is noise and strange smells and strange languages and people from far away. And there are pickpockets in the crowd. So they keep one hand on their purse.

[pause]

They come to sell a few fleeces, or some cheeses, or a calf. And they come to buy. A new knife or an iron pot. A pair of shoes. Some cloth for a new tunic. A few spices for a special feast. Or a little present, a ribbon or a brooch, for a wife or a daughter.

[pause]

{img:medieval-fair-entertainers-jugglers-musicians-dancing-bear-crowd}
There is fun too. Jugglers and acrobats and musicians. Singers and storytellers and puppet shows. Wrestling matches. And a man with a dancing bear.

[long-pause]

The abbot runs the fair. He collects rent for the stalls. He has a special court every day for the traders. It settles their quarrels quickly, before they leave. It is called the court of dusty feet, because the traders come straight in from the dusty roads.

[pause]

At the end of the day, the villagers of Elton find a place to sleep. In a barn, or an inn, or under their cart. The next morning they go home with their new things. And with their stories, to tell to their neighbours by the fire for the rest of the year.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-road-near-village-travellers-cart-friar-pedlar-horseman}
A few miles from Elton runs a great old road. The Romans built it more than a thousand years before. It runs straight from London to the north. In the year 1300 it is still one of the main roads of England.

[pause]

Along it, and along the smaller roads that join it, come travellers. Some of them pass through Elton.

[long-pause]

There are pedlars walking from village to village. They carry packs full of little things to sell. Needles and pins and thread. Ribbons and buttons and combs. Little mirrors and knives and small bags of spices. When a pedlar comes, the women of the village gather round. They look and buy and hear the news.

[pause]

There are friars. They are wandering monks in long grey or black robes, with ropes around their waists. They walk barefoot or in sandals from place to place. They preach in the villages, on the green or in the churchyard. Their sermons are simple and lively, full of stories. The villagers love them. And the friars beg for food and a place to sleep.

[pause]

{img:friar-preaching-village-green-villagers-listening-sitting-grass}
There are messengers riding fast on horses, with letters for the abbot and the bishop. There are drovers walking cattle and sheep slowly to the markets in the towns, with their dogs and sticks.

[long-pause]

There are traders with packhorses loaded with wool and cloth and salt and wine. There are carts creaking along the bumpy roads. There are wandering builders and carpenters going to work on a church or a castle far away.

[pause]

And sometimes there are great people. A lord or a bishop rides by with his whole household. Servants and knights and packhorses and carts full of furniture and kitchen pots. It is a long slow colourful procession. The villagers stop and stare. And they talk about it for weeks.

[pause]

{img:noble-procession-riders-banners-carts-medieval-road-villagers-watching}
Only ten years before our story, a very sad procession passed along this road. Queen Eleanor, the beloved wife of King Edward, had passed away in the north. Her body was carried slowly all the way to London. At every place where the procession stopped for the night, the king later built a tall beautiful stone cross in her memory. One of them stands not far from Elton. It still stands today.

[long-pause]

So most people of Elton never travel far. But the world comes to them, along the roads and the river. It brings news and goods and stories and strangers. And then it goes on, down the road and over the hill, and is gone.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-autumn-village-threshing-barn-flails-dust-light-doorway}
On the twenty-ninth of September, it is Michaelmas, the feast of Saint Michael. It is the end of the farming year.

[pause]

The harvest is in. The grain is safe in the barns. Apples and pears are being picked and stored on straw in the lofts. Nuts are falling in the woods. And the swallows gather on the church roof, getting ready to fly away for the winter.

[long-pause]

At Michaelmas the reeve gives his accounts to the clerk from the abbey. A new reeve is chosen for the new year. The rents are paid. And the villagers eat a goose for luck. It has grown fat on the leftover grain in the fields.

[pause]

And in the barns the threshing begins.

[pause]

{img:two-men-threshing-flails-barn-floor-rhythm-grain-chaff}
All through autumn and winter, the men beat the grain on the barn floor with flails. A flail is two sticks joined by a leather strap. You hold the long one. The short one swings round and beats the heads of grain. That knocks the seeds loose.

[long-pause]

Two or three men stand in a circle and beat in turn, in a steady rhythm. Thump, thump, thump, all day long. All through autumn and winter you can hear the sound of the flails from the barns all over the village.

[pause]

Then the grain must be cleaned. On a windy day they open the barn doors at both ends. The wind blows through. They toss the grain up into the air from a wide flat basket. The wind blows away the light husks and the dust. And the heavy grain falls back clean into the basket.

[pause]

{img:winnowing-grain-basket-barn-doors-open-wind-chaff-flying-light}
In autumn the pigs are taken into the woods. They eat the acorns and nuts that fall from the trees. They grow fat for the winter. The villagers pay the lord a few pennies for each pig.

[long-pause]

And the fields are ploughed again. The wheat is sown for next year's harvest. And the circle begins again.

[pause]

So the year turns in Elton, as it has turned for centuries. From ploughing to sowing, to hay, to harvest, to threshing, and back to ploughing. The villagers feel it in their bones and their hands and their hearts. Every season has its own work and feasts and songs. And its own light and smell and sound.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-orchard-autumn-apples-pears-ladder-baskets-family-picking}
In autumn, the orchards and gardens of Elton are full of fruit.

[pause]

Behind the houses and around the manor there are fruit trees. Apples and pears and plums and cherries and walnuts. And in the hedges and woods there are wild fruits. Blackberries and wild plums and crab apples and hazelnuts.

[long-pause]

In September and October, families pick the fruit. They climb the trees on wooden ladders and fill their baskets and aprons. Some fruit is eaten fresh. Some is stewed or baked with honey. Apples and pears are laid out carefully on straw in the lofts. They must not touch each other. One rotten apple can spoil all the others. Some are crushed and pressed to make cider.

[pause]

And the children go into the woods and lanes to pick blackberries and gather nuts. They come home with purple fingers and purple mouths.

[pause]

{img:beehives-skeps-straw-garden-bench-bees-flowers-sunny-afternoon}
In the gardens there are beehives. They are round baskets woven of straw, standing on little wooden benches out of the rain.

[long-pause]

Bees are very important in the year 1300. Honey is the only sweetness most people know. Sugar is rare and costly and comes from far away. So honey is used in cooking and baking and medicine. It is used to make a sweet strong drink called mead. And the wax is precious too. The church needs lots of it, because only beeswax candles may burn on the altar.

[pause]

The villagers keep bees in their gardens. Sometimes in summer a swarm of bees leaves the hive and flies away. The owner runs after it, banging a pan to make the bees settle. Then he catches them in an empty basket and brings them home. But if the swarm settles on a neighbour's land, there may be a quarrel. Then the court must decide whose bees they are.

[pause]

{img:herb-garden-medieval-cottage-lavender-sage-rosemary-woman-picking}
In the gardens there are also herbs, which the women grow for cooking and medicine. Sage and rosemary and thyme. Parsley and mint and fennel. Lavender and chamomile and marigolds.

[long-pause]

The women know the herbs from their mothers and grandmothers. They know what each one is good for. Chamomile for sleep and calm. Mint for the stomach. Sage for a sore throat. Marigold for cuts. Lavender for a sweet smell in the linen chests.

[pause]

In the autumn evenings, the women hang bunches of herbs to dry from the beams. The whole house smells sweetly of sage and thyme and lavender all winter long. The fire burns. The rain falls on the straw roof. And the family sleeps inside, warm and safe.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-village-rainy-summer-flooded-fields-1315-grey-sky-quiet}
The year 1300 is a good time in Elton and in England. For two hundred years, the number of people has grown. The villages are full. The fields stretch further and further into the woods and the wet lands. There are more people in England than there will be again for three hundred years.

[pause]

But in the years that follow, hard times come.

[long-pause]

Fifteen years later, the summers turn cold and wet. The rain falls and falls. The grain rots in the fields. The harvests fail all over northern Europe, three years in a row. People call it the Great Famine. In the villages, people go hungry. Many of the old and the poor and the very young do not survive. Then the cattle and the sheep fall sick too.

[pause]

And about fifty years after our story, something even worse comes. A great sickness sweeps across Europe from the east. People call it the Black Death. In England, perhaps a third or even half of all the people die, in little more than a year. In Elton too, many houses stand empty. Many strips in the fields have nobody left to work them.

[pause]

{img:empty-cottage-overgrown-garden-medieval-village-1350-quiet-sorrow}
We will leave those sad years there, gently, in the past.

[long-pause]

But afterwards the world changes. With so few people left, workers are precious. And they know it. They ask for higher pay and better terms. They refuse to work for the lord for free. They move to other villages where the pay is better. And the lords cannot stop them.

[pause]

Some years later there is a great rising. Villagers from the south and east of England march on London. They want to be free. The rising fails. But slowly, over the next hundred years, the old ties to the lord fade away. The villagers become free. They pay rent in money for their land, instead of working for the lord. By the year 1500, very few unfree people are left in England.

[pause]

{img:tudor-village-elton-farmhouse-stone-1500s-fields-hedges-sheep}
The open fields slowly change too. In many places over the following centuries, the strips are joined together. Hedges and fences are put around them. They become the fields we know today. At Elton, the old open fields lasted for a long time yet.

[long-pause]

The abbey of Ramsey owned Elton for six hundred years. Then King Henry the Eighth closed all the monasteries of England. The monks were sent away. The abbey was pulled down, and its stones were used for other buildings. Its lands were sold. Elton passed into other hands.

[pause]

But the village went on. The people of Elton went on living and working and marrying and raising their children beside the river. Generation after generation. Century after century. Through all the changes of the world beyond.

[long-pause]

{img:elton-village-today-stone-cottages-thatch-lane-church-tower-summer}
Today, Elton is still there.

[pause]

It is still a small village in the countryside, beside the river Nene. About seven hundred people live there. Its houses are now built of pale golden-grey local stone. Some have stone roofs, and some still have straw. Gardens full of flowers line the winding lanes and the little green.

[long-pause]

The church of All Saints still stands in the middle of the village. It is bigger now, with a tall tower added later. In the churchyard, the two old crosses still stand. They are weathered and worn, among the gravestones and the yew trees. They stand just as they did when the villagers of 1300 walked past them on their way to church.

[pause]

Down by the river there is still a mill at Elton. It is a later one, built of stone, beside the little waterfall. The water still pours over. The river still winds slowly through the green meadows, with their willows and reeds. And cows still graze in the mist on summer mornings.

[pause]

Near the village stands a great country house with towers and gardens. It was built over many centuries, partly where an old manor house once stood. The same family has lived there for more than three hundred and fifty years.

[long-pause]

And in the fields around Elton, and all over the middle of England, you can still see the old ridges if you know how to look. The long gentle waves in the grass. They were left by the ploughs of the villagers in their strips, seven hundred years ago.

[pause]

The old court rolls and accounts of Elton are kept today in archives, carefully stored in boxes in the dry and the dark. Historians have read them and translated them from Latin. They have written books about them. So the people of Elton in the year 1300 are not forgotten. The reeves and the ale-wives and the millers and the ploughmen. Their quarrels and their cows and their weddings.

[pause]

{img:english-countryside-ridge-and-furrow-meadow-church-tower-distance-golden-evening}
So we leave Elton tonight, in the year 1300, as the evening falls at the end of a summer day.

[long-pause]

The ploughmen are coming home slowly from the fields with their oxen, along the lane. Smoke rises from the straw roofs. Women stand in the doorways calling the children in. The geese are being driven home from the green, hissing and grumbling. The church bell rings over the fields. Down by the river, the mill wheel has stopped. The water pours softly over the dam. Mist rises from the meadows as the light fades. And the first stars come out over the quiet village beside the river.
