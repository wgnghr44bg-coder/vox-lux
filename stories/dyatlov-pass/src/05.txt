{img:51-cedar-at-forest-edge-snow}
The footprints that the searchers have found lead down the slope in a nearly straight line. They go toward the edge of the forest, which lies more than a kilometre below the tent, where the bare mountain ends and the first cedars and pines begin. The searchers follow the line on skis, slowly, in the fading light, with their lanterns lit.

At the very edge of the trees, they find a tall old cedar standing alone, with its lowest branches broken off high above the ground. Beneath it lie the burnt remains of a small fire, a ring of ashes and charred twigs in the snow. Someone had tried to light a fire here, in the dark, in the cold, with very little to work with. Around the tree, the snow has been trampled flat.

It is here that the search turns into something far sadder. In this place, and in the days that follow, the searchers find the first of the friends. They were not far from one another. They had come down the mountain together, and had tried to warm themselves, and the cold had been stronger than they were. The findings are described in the reports in plain, careful language, and nothing needs to be added to them here.

{img:52-footprint-trail-down-slope-dusk}
In the days after that, the search continues. The line of footprints between the cedar and the tent is followed again and again, and three more of the friends are found along that line, spaced out across the slope, as though they had been trying to climb back toward the tent, to the warm clothes and the sleeping bags, but had not been able to reach it. Among them is Igor Dyatlov, the leader, who stayed with his friends to the very end.

By the beginning of March, five of the nine have been found. Four are still missing. The search party works on, probing the snow with long poles, ski by ski, metre by metre, across the slope and the edge of the forest. The snow is deep, in some places three or four metres, and the wind has packed it hard. It is slow, patient, silent work.

{img:53-search-camp-lanterns-night}
At the search camp, in the evenings, the volunteers gather in a big tent around a stove. They are quiet. Some are the friends of the lost group, young people who studied with them and sang with them and walked with them on earlier trips. They write their reports, drink their tea, and listen to the radio crackling messages down the mountain. Outside, the wind moves in the trees.

The news reaches Sverdlovsk. In the city, the first five funerals are held on a cold March day. Hundreds of people come, students, teachers, neighbours, workers, and a long, silent line of young people in dark coats stands in the snow at the cemetery gate. The families hold one another. A band plays a slow tune, and the air is full of breath and steam. It is a day that many in the city will never forget.

{img:54-funeral-gathering-sverdlovsk-snow}
Among the volunteers who have joined the search is a young engineer named Yevgeny Maslennikov, a skilled hiker who studied at the same institute and knew several of the missing friends. He is one of those who examine the tent on the slope, and he notices, with a careful eye, that the cut in the canvas was made from the inside. He is also one of those who will carry the heavy weight of this search for the rest of his life, and who will speak of it, years later, with gentleness and with respect for the families.

By then, the authorities have opened an official investigation. In the town of Ivdel, a prosecutor named Lev Ivanov is put in charge. He is a thorough, serious man, with a lined face and a deep sense of duty, and he travels to the mountain, studies the tent, and speaks with the searchers. He reads the diaries. He reads the notes. He looks at the photographs, and puts every object in the tent on a list.

The questions he must answer seem simple, but they are not. Who were these people? What had they been doing? Why did they leave their tent in the middle of a freezing night? Why did they not take their boots? What happened on that slope? And, perhaps most of all, was anybody else there?

{img:55-prosecutor-desk-files-lamp}
He begins with the possibility that other people might have been involved. The mountain is remote, but not empty. The Mansi hunters live in the forest. The district has old prison camps, and the investigators have to ask whether anyone might have escaped from one of them. But the evidence does not point that way. On the snow around the tent, there are no footprints but the group's own. The valuables are all there, the money, the cameras, the watches, the food. And the Mansi, who have been questioned with great patience and respect, have no quarrel with anyone, and no reason at all to harm a group of young skiers passing through.

Ivanov looks at the weather. He looks at the tent. He looks at the slope, and he does not see any sign of an avalanche. The angle of the hillside is gentle, only about twenty degrees, and no mass of snow has swept across it, because the skis and poles around the tent are still standing upright. Nothing has been buried or broken. So the avalanche theory, in the first months, is put aside.

{img:56-mansi-elders-questioned-room}
In the spring, the melting begins. Day by day, the great white blanket over the forest shrinks. Streams trickle. The snow sags and cracks, and the ground begins to show through, dark and wet. At the beginning of May, the search party returns to the slope, and begins to work along the little stream that runs through a ravine in the forest, a short distance beyond the cedar.

It is here, under a thick layer of old snow, that the last four friends are finally found. They are lying near one another in the ravine, about seventy-five metres from the cedar and the ashes of the fire. Their discovery brings a certain sorrow, but it also brings, at last, an end to the long uncertainty for the families.

{img:57-ravine-forest-spring-thaw}
What the doctors find is not entirely simple. The first five friends are found to have died of the cold. That is a sad but a plain explanation, and it seems to fit the story of a group that left its tent without proper clothing and tried to survive in a freezing forest. But among the last four, three have serious injuries that the doctors find hard to explain, injuries of a kind that might normally come from a very heavy blow, though there are no marks on the skin to show where it came from.

Another small detail adds to the puzzle. Some of the friends are wearing garments that do not belong to them. A piece of clothing has been taken from one friend and worn by another. It seems that those who were still able to move took warm things from those who could not, and shared them out. It is a quiet, human detail, a small story of kindness in the darkest of hours, and it is part of what makes the mystery so affecting.

{img:58-investigators-table-cameras-notebooks}
The investigators examine everything. They develop the film from the cameras. They read through the diaries again and again, looking for a hint of conflict or fear, and find none. They study the clothing and the equipment. A few pieces of clothing, they report, carry small traces of radioactivity. The finding is noted in the file, though later researchers will say that it was very small, and probably had ordinary causes, such as the minerals in the soil, or the materials of old lanterns.

Others tell of lights in the sky. On the nights of February and March, in that same part of the northern Urals, some people said that they had seen glowing orange spheres floating high above the mountains, silent and slow. A group of hikers camped some fifty kilometres to the south reported such a sight, and so did a few local people. In those years, rockets were being launched and tested in the far north of the country, and some have suggested that what was seen might have come from them. Others think it was simply a trick of the clouds and the cold.

{img:59-orange-lights-night-sky-urals}
In the end, after months of work, the investigators have to write something down. At the end of May, the prosecutor's office closes the case. The final report is short, and its wording has become famous. It says that the cause of the deaths was a compelling natural force, which the hikers were not able to overcome.

That is all. The report does not say what the force was. It does not say whether it was snow, or wind, or cold, or something else. It simply puts a small, careful line around the mystery, and closes the folder.

The mountains are closed to hikers for three years. The files are sealed and stored in a secret archive. For decades, the families and friends have only the briefest official answer, and a great deal of silence. The rest is left to rumour, memory and imagination.

{img:60-closed-case-folder-sealed-lamp}
And so the story of the Dyatlov group goes quiet for a while, like a stone dropped into deep water. But it does not disappear. In the apartments and dormitories of Sverdlovsk, in the tourist clubs, in the memories of those who joined the search, the questions remain. People tell the story again, with more details, and more theories, and in time it becomes one of the best-known mysteries of the twentieth century.

Let us now look at the questions that remain, one by one, as calmly as we can.

The first is the tent. Why did the friends leave it? The cut in the canvas shows that they left in a hurry, so something must have made them believe that it was no longer safe to stay inside. Whatever happened must have happened suddenly, and it must have been strong enough to make nine experienced hikers leave their warm sleeping bags and their boots, in a wind of thirty degrees below zero.

{img:61-tent-interior-boots-warm-clothes}
The second is the clothing. Experienced hikers know that their boots and their warm clothes are what keep them alive. To leave them behind, in such cold, is to put oneself in great danger. It tells us that the people who left the tent were not thinking about the cold. They were thinking about something else, and they believed that they would be able to come back for their things within a few minutes.

The third is the direction. When the friends left the tent, they did not scatter. They went down the slope in a line, toward the forest, a long distance in the dark. They seem to have had a purpose, and a plan. They made a fire at the edge of the trees. They broke branches from the cedar, perhaps to feed the flames, or perhaps to climb. They tried to make a shelter in the ravine. All of this speaks of people who were thinking clearly, who were working together, and who were doing what trained hikers do.

{img:62-ravine-shelter-branches-forest}
The fourth is the injuries. The cold alone does not explain them. The doctors believe that a strong force, such as a heavy fall, or a great weight of snow, or a blow from something, may have caused them. Yet there is no sign of a fight, and no sign of a struggle. The friends do not seem to have harmed one another. They seem, rather, to have helped one another until the very end.

And the fifth is the silence. For many years, the official file said so little that it left room for every kind of story. When at last the papers were opened in the nineteen nineties, some pages had gone missing, and people wondered about that, and went on wondering.

It is on these five questions that the later theories are built.

{img:63-investigator-reading-old-pages}
In the decades that followed, hundreds of explanations were put forward. Some were practical and scientific. Some were dramatic and strange. Many of them came from people who had walked on that slope, or lived in the mountains, or spent long years studying the case. Each one tried to answer the same few questions, and none of them was able to answer them all.

Let us go through the main ones, calmly, and see what each one explains, and what it leaves open.
