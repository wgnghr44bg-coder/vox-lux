{img:36-forest-edge-morning-breakfast}
The first of February, nineteen fifty-nine, is a Sunday. In the forest at the edge of the upper Auspiya, the morning begins slowly. The friends wake in their warm tent, listen to the stove tick as it cools, and take their time getting up. Nobody is in a hurry. The day's plan is a short and simple one, a climb to the high ground, a look at the pass, and a camp somewhere near the foot of Otorten.

They cook breakfast, drink their tea, and then start the long job of packing. The felt boots, which hung drying by the stove during the night, are slid into the rucksacks, to be worn again in the evening. Ski bindings are checked. Fingers are warmed. Around noon, in thin, bright sunlight, the group shoulders its packs, leaves the cache of spare things behind, and sets out on skis along the track they have already broken through the snow.

It is worth remembering how short the day is at this time of the year, so far to the north. The sun rises late, climbs only a little way above the southern ridge, and sinks again by the middle of the afternoon. By four o'clock the light has already begun to turn blue, and by five it is almost gone. A group that sets out at noon has only a few hours of daylight in which to climb, to find a camp, and to pitch a tent.

The sky is a pale, milky grey, with no clear edge to the clouds. The temperature is about twenty degrees below zero, but the air is almost still, and in the shelter of the trees it feels gentler than it sounds. They follow the little valley upward, passing through the last scattered stands of spruce and cedar. The trees grow shorter. The ground grows more open. After a while, the forest simply stops.

{img:37-skiers-climbing-open-ridge}
They stop once more at the very edge of the forest, where the last spruce stands, tall and a little crooked, with its dark branches heavy with snow. It is a good place for a short pause. Someone checks the map, someone tightens a strap, someone takes a photograph of the friends standing together with their skis planted beside them. In the picture, nine faces look out from under fur hats, cheeks red with cold, eyes bright. Behind them the open mountain rises, pale and smooth, into the cloud.

Beyond the last trees, the land opens into a vast, white, rounded landscape, with no tree or rock to break it. The snow is crisp and polished by the wind. Far ahead, a long, smooth ridge lifts against the sky. The skiers stop for a moment to catch their breath and look. It is a wide, empty, magnificent country, the kind of view that fills the chest and makes a person feel very small.

As they climb, the weather begins to change. Slowly, almost without anybody noticing, a wind starts to stir from the west and south. It lifts a thin veil of powder snow off the ground, and the powder blows across the slope in long, whispering ribbons. Then the clouds thicken, and the sun, which was only a pale disc, fades out of sight altogether.

The wind gets stronger as the afternoon wears on. It pulls at the hoods of the jackets and sets the ends of the scarves flapping. Snow begins to fall in fine, sharp grains that sting the cheeks. In the exposed places the skiers bend their heads and push on, one behind another, each following the dark shape of the person in front. Visibility shrinks to a few dozen metres, and then to less.

{img:38-whiteout-slope-skiers}
In weather like this, even skilled hikers can lose their way. On a wide slope in the falling snow, the ground and the sky become the same shade of white. There is no horizon. There are no landmarks. A skier may think he is travelling straight, and in fact be curving slowly to one side, as if pulled by an invisible hand. The compass helps, but only if the ground allows the group to follow it.

This is what seems to happen to the friends. Their plan was to go up the valley and cross over the low pass to the next side, where they would find shelter in the trees and camp for the night. But in the whiteout, with the wind driving at their faces, the group drifts to the left. Instead of reaching the pass, they find themselves climbing the open eastern slope of Kholat Syakhl, the mountain whose name means Dead Mountain, a bare, rounded summit more than a thousand metres high.

It is late in the afternoon. The light is going fast. The group has been climbing for hours and the wind is bitter. Somewhere ahead is the pass, and somewhere below is the forest, but in this swirling white world nobody can be sure where. Dyatlov stops, looks round, and makes a decision.

{img:39-skiers-digging-platform-snow}
They will not turn back. Going down in the dark and the wind, to find a camp among the trees, would cost time, and it would mean losing the height they have climbed. Instead, he decides to pitch the tent right here, on the slope, and to continue in the morning. It is not an unusual choice for an experienced group. In fact, some say the leader wanted to give his friends a lesson in camping on open ground in high winds, which is a useful thing to know in the mountains.

So the work begins. The friends take off their skis and dig into the slope. They cut out a level terrace in the snow, a shelf large enough for the tent, using their shovels and skis. They stamp it flat. The wind howls and hisses around them, and the light fades. Their hands are numb, and their cheeks are stiff, but they work together as they have worked every day of the journey.

One of them lifts a camera and takes a few pictures. These will be the last photographs on the roll. They show the group in the blue dusk, wrapped in hoods and scarves, bending over the snow with their shovels, while blown snow streams across the slope in pale veils. In one frame, a figure steps toward the camera, and the tent is going up behind. The shots are blurred and grainy, but they carry the mood of the moment, which is one of work, of cold, and of ordinary determination.

{img:40-tent-pitched-dusk-lantern}
At last the tent is up. The ridge pole is raised. The canvas is stretched and tied down. The ski poles and the skis are driven into the snow around it, to give it some extra support against the wind. A line of footprints leads to the entrance, and the group squeezes inside, one by one, out of the whistling dark.

Inside, the little stove is lit, the lantern is hung, and the sleeping bags are spread on the floor. The temperature quickly begins to climb. Boots are taken off. Warm socks come out of the rucksacks. Hands are pressed to the hot iron, and the friends begin to laugh and talk again, the way people do when they have come in from the cold. Here, in the middle of the evening, there is warmth, light and company.

Supper is prepared. They have a hot meal, which they eat from their mugs and bowls, sitting shoulder to shoulder on their sleeping bags. Later, the investigators will be able to tell that their last meal was eaten some hours before they left the tent, and the notebooks and the supplies show that the evening was a normal one. There is nothing to suggest an argument, a quarrel or a sense of alarm.

{img:41-inside-tent-evening-warm}
The wind keeps blowing, steady and low, around the canvas. It sounds, perhaps, like a long breath. Beyond the thin walls, high on the open flank of the mountain, the snow hisses and drifts, and the stars are hidden by cloud. Inside, the stove ticks, a pot cools, a mug is passed around, and the friends begin to turn toward sleep, one after another, in the dim yellow light.

This is the last moment of the journey of which anything at all is known.

After that, there are no diaries, no photographs, no witnesses. The next time anybody will see the tent, it will be empty, covered with snow, and sagging on its poles. And the question that has followed the group ever since will be a simple and haunting one. What happened on the slope of Kholat Syakhl that night?

{img:42-night-sky-over-kholat-syakhl}
---
Let us pause here for a moment, on the slope, at the end of that evening.

Above the tent, the mountain rises into the dark. The summit is a smooth white curve, and the wind sweeps over it with a long, low sound. Below, far down the slope, lies the edge of the forest, a black line against the snow. Between them, the tent stands alone on its terrace, with its canvas shaking a little in the gusts, and a thin glow of light showing through the cloth.

The temperature is about thirty degrees below zero, perhaps colder. The cloud drifts. The snow whispers. A small cloud of smoke rises from the stove pipe, and the wind carries it away, over the ridge, into the empty north.

Somewhere in the course of the night, the light in the tent goes out. And somewhere in the course of the night, for a reason that nobody has been able to say with certainty, the nine friends leave their tent.

{img:43-empty-tent-in-snow-moonlight}
They go out onto the slope in the dark. They are dressed lightly, some in their socks, one or two in a single boot. They walk down the mountain, away from their tent, in the direction of the forest. They walk together, and they do not return.

It is an extraordinary thing. A group of strong, sensible, experienced hikers, with warm clothes lying just inside the tent, walks out into a night of bitter cold and wind without putting on their boots. And nothing inside the tent suggests why.

The tent itself will show one more strange detail. When it is found, there will be a long cut in the canvas, on the side of the tent, made from the inside, as if someone had slit it open with a knife to get out quickly. Inside, the sleeping bags and the rucksacks lie more or less where they were left.

{img:44-kholat-syakhl-dawn-empty}
Morning comes, as it always does. The sky grows grey, then pale blue, and the first light touches the summit and slides slowly down. The wind drops. The clouds thin. The mountain lies silent and white and empty beneath a huge, cold sky, and the tent stands on its slope with its cut side flapping gently. The snow, little by little, begins to cover the footprints.

Far away, in the city, nobody yet knows anything at all. In Sverdlovsk, the tourist club goes about its work. Families sit down to their Sunday dinners, a letter is written, a lecture is planned. A group of friends is a little overdue, but there is no reason to worry, for nothing about a winter trip is ever on time.

The days pass. The twelfth of February comes, the date on which the group was due to send a telegram from Vizhay. No telegram arrives. At the sports club, a few people look at the calendar and shrug. Dyatlov had said that they might be late. Winter, weather and heavy rucksacks do this to people. Perhaps they will arrive tomorrow.

{img:45-families-waiting-telegram}
But a few more days go by, and still there is nothing. The parents of the hikers begin to ask questions at the institute. A mother stands in a corridor and asks, politely, whether there has been any news. A brother knocks on a door. Someone makes a telephone call to Ivdel and is told that the group was last seen heading north, some weeks before.

On the twentieth of February, the institute makes a decision. A search will be organised. Volunteers are called for, and the response is immediate. Students from the tourist club, young teachers, experienced hikers, men who knew the mountains, all of them come forward and ask how they can help. They gather in a hall at the Polytechnic, pore over the map, and begin to plan.

{img:46-search-volunteers-institute-hall}
The first small groups leave the city by train and truck. They are followed, in the days after, by larger ones. Soldiers join them, and local people from the villages. The authorities send aircraft, which fly low over the forests and the ridges in the clear cold weather, looking for any sign of a tent, a fire, or a line of ski tracks.

At the same time, Mansi hunters, who know the land best of all, take part in the search. They ski in their fur-lined boots across the valleys, reading the snow as another person might read a page. They look for broken branches, old fire-sites, the marks of skis. They say little. They simply keep going, hour after hour, up the long valleys of the north.

{img:47-search-plane-over-winter-forest}
The searchers follow the route that Dyatlov described in his plan. They go up the Lozva. They go up the Auspiya. They find a few traces, an old campsite, a trail of ski marks half-filled with snow, and the little platform in the forest where the friends left their spare things. Everything on it is untouched, wrapped in canvas and tied down exactly as it was left.

This is a good sign, and at the same time a puzzling one. The group never returned to collect their things. So they must be somewhere further up, on the open mountain. The searchers climb out of the forest and start to look at the high ridge.

{img:48-search-party-skiing-valley}
On the twenty-sixth of February, in the middle of the afternoon, two students from the search party, skiing along the slope of Kholat Syakhl, see something strange. It is a small shape on the snow, a darker line against the white, half hidden by a drift. They move closer. It is a tent.

The tent is sagging. One end is partly collapsed under the weight of the snow. The canvas is frozen stiff. Some of the skis and ski poles that were planted around it still stand in the snow. A few things lie scattered nearby. There is no sign of anyone.

{img:49-searchers-approach-tent-slope}
Carefully, the students call out, and no one answers. They step to the tent. The side of the canvas has been cut open from the inside, in a long, clean slit. Through the opening, they can see the interior, with sleeping bags, rucksacks, boots, warm clothing, notebooks, cameras and food. It looks as though the people who lived there have only just gone out, and might be back at any moment.

A little further down, there is a line of footprints in the snow. They are the prints of people walking in socks, or with a single shoe, or even with bare feet, and they lead away from the tent down the slope toward the forest. For a few hundred metres, the searchers can follow them. Then the wind and snow have erased them, and the trail disappears.

{img:50-abandoned-tent-snow-slope-day}
The news travels fast. By the evening, more of the search party have arrived at the tent, and the first reports are sent down the mountain by radio. The tent is photographed, measured and described. Everything is noted down. And the young men stand there for a long time in the late afternoon light, looking down the slope into the forest, and wondering where their friends have gone.
