{img:64-snow-slab-slope-theory-dusk}
The first theory is the one that many people thought of right away, and that the first investigators put aside. It is the avalanche.

A snow slope can look perfectly safe and still carry a hidden weakness. Over the winter, the snow lies down in layers, and some layers are firm while others are loose and sugary. If a heavy slab of firm snow comes to rest on a loose layer beneath, it can hang there on the hillside, quietly, until something disturbs it. A footstep can be enough. So can a cut.

Cutting a terrace into a slope, as the hikers did, to make a flat floor for their tent, is just the kind of change that can start the process. The cut leaves the snow above it without support. At the same time, a wind blowing across the top of the mountain can carry fresh snow and deposit it along the slope above the tent, adding weight, slowly, hour after hour, through the evening.

In the year two thousand and twenty-one, two scientists, Johan Gaume and Alexander Puzrin, published a study of this very question. They built a computer model of the slope and the tent, using what is known about snow and about the wind of that night. Their model suggested that a small slab might indeed have broken loose some hours after the terrace was cut, perhaps after nightfall, with a soft, dull sound. It would not have buried the tent. It would have been only a short, heavy slide of snow, across one side of it.

{img:65-tent-in-night-wind-snow-gusts}
For the people inside, that would have been startling. A few hundred kilograms of snow sliding onto a canvas wall, in the dark, at thirty degrees below zero. The side of the tent would sag. Someone might be pressed against the stove. Someone might cry out. And in the first confusion, with the wind howling and the canvas collapsing, the group might well decide to cut their way out and leave, believing that the whole slope above them was about to come down.

This is a powerful idea, because it can explain several of the questions at once. It explains why the group left in such haste, and why they went out of the tent through a cut. It explains why the heavy injuries appeared in some of the friends, for a slab of snow can press on a body with great force without leaving a mark. And it explains why an experienced group might leave their boots, because at the moment of the slide they would have been thinking only about getting out.

In two thousand and twenty, the Russian prosecutor's office, after a new review of the old case, announced that an avalanche was the most likely cause. They said that the hikers had probably left the tent in fear of a second avalanche, and that they had died of the cold in the following hours, though some had also suffered injuries.

Not everybody agrees. People who have studied the slope point out that it is quite gentle, and that avalanches on such slopes are rare. They say that there was no clear sign of a slide when the tent was found. They add that the group had enough experience to know that a small slide did not mean the whole slope would come down. The debate continues, politely, in books and conferences and on the internet.

{img:66-wind-over-rounded-summit-snow-plume}
The second theory is about the wind itself. On the open slopes of the northern Urals, the wind can blow with astonishing force. On a winter night, cold air pours down off the summit like water down a hill, picking up speed as it goes. Meteorologists call this a katabatic wind. It can reach the speed of a storm, and it can last for hours.

For the nine friends in their tent, that wind would have been a continuous roar. The canvas would have drummed. Snow would have been forced through the seams. It would have been difficult to speak, and hard to sleep. In the dark, some of them might have begun to worry that the tent was being torn from its moorings, and to fear that it would be swept away.

But experienced hikers are used to wind, and a strong wind alone seems too ordinary to explain such a dramatic decision. Perhaps it was part of the story, a push in a certain direction. It may have loaded the slope with snow, or made it impossible to stay calm. But most people think that something more than wind alone was needed to make the group leave.

{img:67-infrasound-vortices-summit-evening}
There is a human side to the wind theory, too. Anyone who has spent a night in a tent in a gale knows how tiring it is. The noise never stops. The canvas pulls at its ropes. Every few minutes, someone has to check that the ropes are holding and that the snow is not building up against the wall. After a long day of climbing, in the dark, with the temperature falling, it would be natural to be anxious. And an anxious group, hearing a loud, strange sound outside, might reach a decision very quickly.

A third idea is a stranger one, and it came from a writer who walked to the slope himself. In his book about the mystery, published in two thousand and thirteen, Donnie Eichar offered an explanation based on the way wind moves around a rounded mountain top. When a steady wind flows past a smooth, rounded obstacle, it can form a regular pattern of whirling currents behind it, which scientists call vortices. In the right conditions, these vortices can produce a very low sound, deeper than the human ear can hear, called infrasound.

Infrasound is known to affect people in unusual ways. A person who is exposed to it may feel a sense of dread, a pressure in the chest, a feeling of dizziness, without knowing why. If the hikers in the tent experienced such a feeling, so the idea goes, they might have been seized by sudden, powerful, unexplained fear, and rushed out into the night.

It is a poetic idea, and it has attracted a great deal of interest. But it is also very difficult to prove. Nobody measured sounds on the slope that night, and scientists who have looked at the idea say that the effect is not well understood, and may not be strong enough to cause such behaviour. For now, it remains an interesting possibility, one among several.

{img:68-secret-tests-sky-theory-night}
The fourth family of theories is the one that appears most often in films and television. It says that the group stumbled into something they were never meant to see. In the nineteen fifties, the Soviet Union was testing new weapons across its vast territory. Rockets were launched from remote sites. Strange new devices were being developed. The northern Urals, empty and far from the cities, would have been a quiet place for such work.

Those who favour this theory point to the lights in the sky that were seen that winter, to the traces of radiation on a few pieces of clothing, to the fact that the area was closed and the files were sealed. They say that the group might have been frightened by a flash, or a blast of air from a distant explosion, and that the friends might have fled from it.

It is true that the files were sealed, and it is true that the authorities of the time said very little. But sealing a file does not prove that a secret lies inside it. When the files were later opened, researchers found nothing to show that any test took place on the mountain that night. There were no craters, no burns, no damage to the trees, no damage to the tent. The lights may have been rockets, or atmospheric tricks, or something that happened on another night altogether. The question is still open, but there is no real evidence to answer it.

{img:69-snowman-legend-cartoon-newspaper}
There are also ideas that belong more to legend than to science. Some people say that the group was frightened by a creature of the mountains, the legendary snowman, or Yeti, which was in the newspapers that winter. The friends had made jokes about it in their little paper. But there is no sign whatsoever of such a creature, no tracks and no remains, and the group's own diaries show that they were cheerful and at ease.

Others have suggested that the Mansi attacked the group to protect their sacred mountain. But the Mansi were questioned at the time with great care, and the investigators found no evidence against them, and no footprints but those of the nine friends. Today, the Mansi people themselves say that the story has been told about them unfairly, and that their ancestors would have welcomed travellers in need.

Before we turn to what researchers think today, it helps to remember what the friends left behind in their own words. Their diaries and notes, written on the evenings of the journey, are cheerful. They describe good food and tired legs, a funny remark at lunch, a song in the tent, the beauty of the pines in the sunlight. There is no mention of any quarrel, no sign of illness or fear. On the last pages, there is only the account of an ordinary day, and the plan for the next one. Whatever happened on the slope, it came without warning, and it came to people who were in good spirits and good company.

So what are we left with? Most of the researchers who have looked at the case in recent years lean toward a combination of ordinary forces, the cold, the wind, the slope, and a sudden slide of snow, which may have set off a chain of events in which a group of skilled friends made quick decisions in the dark and fought bravely against the cold. This explanation is not complete. It leaves small questions unanswered, and it will probably never satisfy everyone.

It is worth noticing what this explanation does not need. It needs no monster, no plot, no terrible secret on that mountain, only the mountain itself, with its snow and its wind and its night. And it shows us nine people at their best, helping one another to the end.
