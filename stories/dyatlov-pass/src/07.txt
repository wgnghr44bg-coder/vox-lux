{img:70-yudin-older-man-holding-photograph}
Yuri Yudin, the friend who turned back at the Second Northern, goes home and lives the rest of his life with a heavy and tender memory. He is twenty-one years old when the news reaches him. He has already said goodbye to nine friends on a snowy morning, and he will never see them again. For years afterward, he is asked about it. He answers, patiently, in a quiet voice, and he never stops thinking about them.

He finishes his studies, finds work, and builds an ordinary, useful life, and he is known to his neighbours for his gentle manner. He keeps the photographs, the letters, and the handwritten notes. Over the years he goes back to the north more than once, and he speaks to journalists and to young hikers who want to hear the story from someone who was there.

He often says that the friends were not a group of careless students, as some newspapers would later suggest. They were strong, serious, and kind to one another, and he is certain that they did everything they could. He keeps their memory alive until his own death, in the year two thousand and thirteen, at the age of seventy-five. He is the last of the ten.

{img:71-cemetery-snow-memorial-stones-yekaterinburg}
In the city, the families of the lost friends face their grief in their own ways. Mothers and fathers keep their children's rooms as they were. Brothers and sisters tell their own children the stories of the uncles and aunts who went to the mountains. Old classmates meet, year after year, on the first of February, and light a candle, and drink a quiet glass of tea.

Most of the friends are buried in the Mikhailovskoe cemetery, in Sverdlovsk, which is today called Yekaterinburg, since the city took back its old name in the nineteen nineties. Their graves lie close together, in a row among tall birches and pines, with dark stone markers and small photographs set into them. Each photograph shows a smiling young face, someone who was loved and who is still remembered. In winter, snow covers the stones. In summer, there are flowers.

The institute that they studied at is now a large university, and it keeps their memory. In a corridor near the lecture halls, there is a memorial, with photographs and a few small objects. Every year, in the first days of February, students gather there to remember the group, and to talk about the lessons that the story holds about friendship, about courage and about caution.

{img:72-memorial-plaque-at-the-pass}
On the slope itself, far in the north, there is a memorial too. Not long after the tragedy, friends and fellow hikers placed a small plaque on a rock near the place where the tent had stood. Later, other memorials were added. There is a stone in the forest near the old cedar. There is a plaque with the nine names, and a few photographs. There are flags and small tokens, a cup, a candle, a pair of gloves, a woollen hat left by someone who walked a very long way to be there.

The place has become known as the Dyatlov Pass, named after the leader of the group, even though the tent was not exactly on a pass at all, but on the slope of the mountain beside it. Mountain tourists make the long trip to the area each year, usually in winter, by snowmobile and on skis. They set up tents near the forest edge, and sometimes they stay for a night, and talk quietly around a fire.

They bring warm clothes. They look at the weather. They remember that the mountains, however gentle they look, deserve respect. Many winter hikers say that the story of the Dyatlov group taught them a rule that they now follow almost without thinking: when the weather turns, go down to the trees.

{img:73-foundation-museum-yekaterinburg-evening}
After the political changes of the early nineteen nineties, when the files of the case were opened to the public, a foundation was created in Yekaterinburg to study the story and to keep its memory. Retired investigators, relatives, former search party members and mountain tourists helped to gather documents, photographs and recollections. A small museum was set up. Books were written, and expeditions were made, and a great many people, in Russia and far beyond it, became fascinated by the mystery.

The case has been reopened more than once. In the year two thousand and nineteen, Russian prosecutors announced that they would take another look, and a team travelled to the slope in the middle of winter, to measure it, to study the snow and to ask the old questions again. In two thousand and twenty, their report suggested that an avalanche was the likely cause. Scientists from other countries have studied the case, too, and have written papers, and given talks, and argued gently with one another about snow and wind and sound.

Songs have been written about the group, too. Young people in Yekaterinburg still sing about the road north, about the tent on the white slope, and about the friends who did not come home. Some of the songs are written by hikers who have walked the same route and slept in the same forest, and they are sung quietly by a campfire, with a guitar, in the old tradition of the tourist clubs that the nine friends loved so much.

Perhaps that is the most remarkable thing about it. The story of nine young friends in a tent on a cold mountain has been told for nearly seventy years, and it is still being told, in many languages, to people who were not born when it happened. It has been the subject of films, novels, songs, and documentaries. And every one of them has had to admit the same plain thing, that we do not know everything, and that some things we may never know.

{img:74-stars-over-kholat-syakhl-quiet}
Yet it would be a pity to remember the group only by the mystery. They were not a puzzle. They were nine people with names and faces and plans.

Igor Dyatlov, the careful leader with the slide rule and the notebook of numbers. Zina Kolmogorova, who wrote her diary every evening and sang in a clear voice. Lyudmila Dubinina, the youngest, with her quick laughter. Alexander Kolevatov, the quiet physicist with a camera. Rustem Slobodin, the sturdy one, who was never tired. Nikolai Thibeaux-Brignolles, whose warm voice ended many a long day. Yuri Doroshenko and Yuri Krivonischenko, two of the three Yuris, one making people laugh, the other fixing anything that broke. And Alexander Zolotaryov, the veteran, who came along to share what he knew.

They had spent the last days of their lives doing what they loved. They travelled through a beautiful country, in company, in good spirits, on skis. They wrote their diaries and sang their songs and made a joke newspaper. They helped each other in the cold, and they shared what warmth they had. We cannot say what happened in the dark, but we can say how they lived, and that is perhaps the part that matters.

{img:75-nine-skis-in-snow-sunrise}
It is a good thing to think about what the tourist movement of those years meant. In the nineteen fifties, thousands of young Soviet people spent their holidays on skis and on foot, in the mountains and forests of their enormous country. For many of them, it was the first taste of freedom, a chance to choose a route, to decide for themselves when to stop and when to go on, to rely on a friend and be relied upon. Songs were written about it, and poems, and a whole culture grew up around the campfire. The Dyatlov group belonged to that world. It was the world of people who believed that a group of friends, with a good map, strong legs and a warm stove, could go almost anywhere.

And so we leave the mountain as it is now, on a winter night, far from any road. The pines stand in the dark at the edge of the forest. The wind moves lightly over the bare ridge. Under a huge sky full of stars, the long white slope lies silent and undisturbed, and a small stone and a few ribbons mark the place where, in the winter of nineteen fifty-nine, a group of friends once put up their tent.
