{img:23-forty-first-district-logging-camp}
The forty-first district turns out to be a logging camp strung along a muddy track, a few low wooden barracks, a canteen, a sawmill and a stack of freshly cut timber higher than a house. Smoke rises from a dozen stove pipes, and the air smells of resin and wet sawdust. In the evening, the workers come home from the forest with frost in their eyebrows, and the hikers are given a place to sleep in one of the hostels.

They lay out their sleeping bags along a wall, and for the last time on the journey, there is a proper roof overhead. A woodcutter's wife brings in a pot of hot soup. In the corner, someone has left a battered guitar, and one of the students picks it up and strums a few chords. The loggers listen from their beds, and one of them asks if the young people really plan to walk up into the mountains in this weather.

On the morning of the twenty-seventh of January, the hikers load their skis and rucksacks onto another truck and ride north as far as the road allows. Then the lorry stops, the engine falls silent, and the friends climb down onto the snow. The driver wishes them luck and turns around. Behind him, the sound of the engine slowly dies away, and the real quiet begins.

{img:24-skiing-out-of-settlement}
They put on their skis. They tighten their straps, adjust their hats, and look at one another with the same grin. Dyatlov nods, and the line of ten figures begins to move, one behind the other, up the valley of the river. The leader goes first, and the others follow in his tracks. The whole group is soon strung out along the white ground like a line of dark beads.

Skiing with a heavy rucksack is not the same as skiing for pleasure. The rhythm is slow and steady. Push, glide, push, glide. The packs creak. The poles tap on the crust. The skier in front breaks the trail through new snow, which is the hardest work, and every half hour or so he steps aside and lets the next in line take over. In this way the burden is shared, and nobody grows too tired.

By the afternoon, they reach a small settlement of old wooden huts that the hikers call the Second Northern. It was once a mining village, and its houses stand now half empty, with snow piled up to the windowsills and a few thin threads of smoke rising from the one or two that are still lived in. The hikers pass slowly between the huts, and a dog watches them from a doorway with its head on its paws.

{img:25-yudin-farewell-at-hut}
It is here that the first difficulty comes. Yuri Yudin, the economics student, has been feeling unwell since the start of the trip. A sharp pain has settled in his knee and the lower part of his back, an old problem that has been waking up in the cold. At first he tried to ignore it. But as the day goes on, the pain grows worse, and each step with a heavy pack becomes harder than the last.

That evening he speaks to Dyatlov. It is not an easy thing to say. He has looked forward to this trip for months, and he does not want to let down his friends. But the route ahead is long, and the cold will only grow deeper. If he continues, he may slow the whole group, and even put them in difficulty. Dyatlov listens, thinks, and agrees that it is better for him to turn back.

The next morning, the twenty-eighth of January, the group stands outside the hut for a while in the pale light, and says goodbye. There are handshakes and embraces. Someone gives Yudin a few things to carry back. Someone makes a joke, and everybody laughs, a little more loudly than the joke deserves. Yudin asks them to take good care of one another, and Igor promises that he will send a telegram from Vizhay in two weeks.

{img:26-yudin-walking-back-alone}
Then the group sets off up the valley, and Yudin turns south. For a time, he stands in the snow and watches the nine figures grow smaller, their skis flashing in the sun and the sound of their voices carried back to him on the thin air. At the edge of the forest, one of them turns and waves a pole. Then the trees close behind them, and he is alone.

He will remember that moment for the rest of his life. He will say that he took one more look at them, and that nothing in their faces or their voices suggested anything but happiness. They were strong, they were well equipped, and they were off to do something they loved. He turned, adjusted his pack, and began the long walk back to the settlement.

{img:27-lozva-river-valley-skiers}
For the nine who continue, the days that follow have a steady rhythm. They wake early, melt snow for tea, eat a breakfast of porridge or biscuits, pack the tent, and set off along the valley of the Lozva. The river lies buried under ice and snow, a broad, flat road through the forest, and the skiers glide along it in a line with the dark banks rising on either side.

The valley is beautiful in the pale winter light. The snow is untouched, rippled by the wind into long waves, and the shadows of the trees fall across it in blue stripes. Here and there, small birds flutter from branch to branch, and the tracks of a hare or a fox run across the white surface and vanish into the trees. The skiers talk little while they travel. They save their breath, and the silence is large and clean.

At midday the group stops for lunch. They do not light a fire for this. The leader looks at his watch, raises a hand, and the line of skiers comes to a halt among the trees. Rucksacks are lowered onto the snow, and everyone sits on them, close together, to keep out of the cold. A flask of hot sweet tea is passed from hand to hand. There are biscuits, a slice of dry sausage, and thin slices of lard on black bread, which taste better than any restaurant dinner, because everyone is hungry and everything is shared. After twenty minutes, the packs go back on, the skis slide forward, and the long afternoon begins.

In the afternoon, as the sun slips behind the ridge and the cold deepens, the group looks for a place to camp. They need a flat spot, with dry standing trees nearby for firewood and shelter from the wind. When they find one, the work begins at once. Some of the friends clear the snow with their skis, stamping out a flat floor. Others fetch firewood, and others unpack the tent.

{img:28-evening-camp-stove-glow}
There are jobs for everyone, and the jobs rotate each day. One person is on duty to cook, and one to gather wood. One keeps the stove alight through the evening and the night. Another makes sure the boots are placed where they will not freeze. The tent is stretched over its ridge pole, the sides are weighted with snow, and the little stove is set up inside with its pipe poking out through a hole in the canvas.

Within half an hour, the tent glows from within like a lantern. Inside, the air grows warm. Wet socks are hung from the line above the stove. A pot of soup begins to bubble. Everyone sits cross-legged on spread-out sleeping bags, hands cupped around hot mugs, faces pink from the heat. Outside the canvas, the temperature may be twenty degrees below zero, and inside it is cosy enough to take off a jacket.

After supper they talk, tell stories and sing. Sometimes Kolya starts a tune in his warm voice, and the others hum along. Zolotaryov, the oldest, tells stories of other mountains, other winters, and the younger members listen with the particular respect that young people give to someone who has really been through things. Sometimes the talk turns to the future, to jobs and marriages and the places they hope to see.

{img:29-diary-writing-by-lantern}
Before sleep, a few of them take out their notebooks. Zina writes down the day's journey in neat, patient lines, and Lyudmila adds her own thoughts in her lively hand. They record the distances, the times, the temperature and the mood of the group. They write about the beauty of the forest, about a funny thing someone said at lunch, about how good the porridge tasted. These small, ordinary notes will later become very precious.

Dyatlov keeps the group's official log. It is a more serious record, with sketches of the route and details about the terrain, and it is written in the plain language of an engineer. Each evening, he studies his map by lantern light, marks the place where they stopped, and calculates how far they have to go. Then he closes the notebook and puts it away.

At last the stove is banked, the lantern is turned down, and the tent grows quiet. Someone coughs. Someone turns over. A small gust of wind moves the canvas and a little powdery snow slides off the roof. Outside, the forest stands in total silence, and over it all the stars shine hard and bright in the frozen black sky.

{img:30-deep-snow-forest-trail}
On the following days, the going becomes slower. The valley narrows. The river bends, and in places it has opened up under the ice, leaving dark patches that the skiers must go around. The snow grows deeper under the trees, soft as flour, and the leader sinks to his knees with every stride. The group has to change the person at the front every few minutes.

In the middle of the forest, they come across a trail that has been cut by hunters, a line of notches on tree trunks, which are the Mansi blazes. The notches mark a path through the trees, and the hikers use it as a guide, following it up the valley. Sometimes the path vanishes under fresh snow, and they must search for the next cut in the bark, like sailors looking for a buoy in fog.

{img:31-mansi-blaze-on-pine-trunk}
The weather is changeable. On some days the sky is clear and blue, and the light on the snow is almost too bright to look at. On others, low grey cloud sinks over the valley, and a light snowfall begins, soft as down. The temperature hovers around twenty below, rising a little in the middle of the day, and falling again as the sun disappears. It is cold, but it is the kind of cold that these hikers know well, and they dress for it.

Photographs from these days show tired, happy faces. There is a picture of someone making tea over a fire, with a blackened pot and a ring of friends bent over it. There is a picture of two skiers with frost on their eyebrows, grinning into the camera. There is a picture of a long line of skis planted upright in the snow, beside a tent glowing in the dusk. In none of them is there anything but ease.

{img:32-auspiya-valley-dawn-light}
On the thirty-first of January, the group reaches the upper valley of the Auspiya, a quiet, snowy basin closed in by low hills. Here the forest starts to thin out. The trees grow smaller and more crooked, and between them the open, windswept ground begins to show. Ahead, a long ridge rises above the trees, white and bare against the sky. It is the beginning of the mountains proper.

The next stage of the route will lead them up onto the ridge and toward Otorten, over open ground where the wind can blow at will. To make the climb easier, Dyatlov decides to lighten the load. Near the edge of the forest, the hikers build a little storage platform, a small raised floor of logs on posts, a kind of cache, where they can leave the things that they will not need for the next two days. They hope to return to it after the climb.

{img:33-cache-platform-forest-edge}
They leave behind spare food, some of their winter clothing, and a pair of skis. Everything is wrapped in canvas and tied down, and the whole bundle is lifted onto the platform, out of reach of foxes and mice. It is a sensible, experienced decision. Only the things that are needed for the push to Otorten will go with them.

Later, people will often note that the group left behind some of their warm things. But it is worth remembering how normal this is. On a long expedition, hikers usually lighten the load before a difficult stage, and they come back for the stored equipment afterwards. The group had planned to go up to Otorten, to look at the view, and to descend again to the cache, where they would spend another night in the shelter of the forest.

In the late afternoon, the friends make camp for what will be the last night under the trees. They do their usual chores. They light the stove. They cook a supper of soup and cereal. Afterwards, with the stove glowing and the day's work done, they settle into a particularly good mood, since the hardest part of the trip is behind them, and tomorrow, they will see the open mountains.

{img:34-evening-otorten-newspaper-tent}
That night the friends make a small joke newspaper. It is a tradition on many student trips, a single sheet of paper, handwritten and decorated, with funny headlines and little drawings. They call it "The Evening Otorten", after the mountain they will climb. It carries news of the day's journey, light remarks about their own habits, and a cheerful mention of the legendary snowman, the creature of the high mountains that every newspaper in the world has been writing about that winter.

Several people contribute. Someone adds a drawing. Someone teases the cook. Someone writes a mock announcement about the weather and the group's progress, and the others laugh until the tent shakes. The whole thing is warm, silly and good-humoured, the sort of thing that friends make when they feel at home with one another.

When the paper is finished, they pin it up inside the tent and lie back to enjoy the last evening of the forest. The lantern burns low. Outside, the stars are bright, and the snow squeaks under the boots of the person on stove duty who steps out to fetch more wood. It is the last night they will spend in the shelter of the trees.

{img:35-last-night-forest-tent-glow}
Nobody knows that, of course. They are tired and content and thinking of tomorrow. In the morning, the sun will rise over the Auspiya. The friends will pack their rucksacks, shoulder their skis and set off toward the white ridge, and from that moment on, the story of their journey will become something that people will tell for generations.
