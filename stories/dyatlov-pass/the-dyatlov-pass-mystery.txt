{img:01-tram-snow-evening}

Tonight we are going about sixty-seven years back, to the Ural Mountains of Russia, a land of dark pine forests and long white ridges, where the snow lies deep from October until April.

[long-pause]

In the city of Sverdlovsk, a tram rolls slowly down a wide street, its windows glowing yellow in the blue winter dusk. The rails hum under the wheels. Frost has grown thick ferns of ice across the glass, and the passengers have rubbed little round holes in it with their gloves, so that they can see where they are.

[pause]

Outside, the snow falls in large, quiet flakes. It settles on the shoulders of people in heavy coats and fur hats, who hurry along the pavements with string bags full of bread and cabbage. It is the middle of January, nineteen fifty-nine, and the thermometer on the corner reads twenty degrees below zero.

[pause]

The city lies on the eastern slope of the Urals, beside the small, slow river Iset. Not far to the west, an old stone marker stands in the forest, and it says that here Europe ends and Asia begins. A traveller can put one foot on each continent and look at nothing but pine trees.

[pause]

Sverdlovsk is a city of factories. Its great machine works, which people call Uralmash, build the heavy equipment that other factories use to make steel, to dig mines and to lift rock. Early in the morning, before the sky is light, whole streets of workers walk toward the gates, their breath rising in clouds, and the long red buildings begin to hum and glow.

{img:02-factory-gate-morning}

At seven, a whistle sounds across the rooftops. Another follows from the other side of town, and then another, until the whole city seems to be singing in different notes. Chimneys send up their columns of white smoke, and in the cold, windless air the smoke rises straight, like pillars holding up the sky.

[pause]

For most families, home is a shared apartment. Three or four households live along one corridor, each in a single room, and they share a kitchen at the end of the hall. In the evening that kitchen is the warmest, busiest room in the building. A kettle sits on the stove. A grandmother stirs a pot of buckwheat. Somebody has hung wet mittens on a line above the cooker, and they drip steadily onto a newspaper laid out on the floor.

[pause]

People speak quietly there, in the kind of voice that carries easily through thin walls. They talk about the price of potatoes, about a nephew who has joined the army, and about the new apartment blocks that are slowly rising on the edge of town. In those years, thousands of families are waiting to leave the shared corridors for a flat of their own, with a door that closes on a kitchen that belongs to them alone.

{img:03-communal-kitchen-evening}

On a cold evening, the dish that Urals families love best is pelmeni. These are small dumplings of minced meat wrapped in thin dough, and in this part of Russia they are almost a way of life. A family may spend a whole Sunday afternoon at the kitchen table, rolling out the dough and pinching the edges, with the children helping and getting flour on their noses. The finished dumplings are laid out on boards and carried to the windowsill or the balcony, where the frost turns them hard as pebbles in an hour. Then they are stored in a sack, and boiled in salted water whenever a hungry guest arrives.

[pause]

There is tea, of course. There is always tea. It is poured from a dented kettle into thick glasses set in metal holders, and it is drunk very hot, with a lump of sugar held between the teeth. A slice of black rye bread with a little butter is a good supper. A boiled egg is a small celebration.

[pause]

Across the city, in a quarter of tall trees and old brick, stands a great institute of engineering. It is called the Ural Polytechnic, and it was founded long before the revolution, when this region first needed men who understood iron and steam. Its main building has a wide front with columns and a broad stone staircase, and on winter mornings the steps are sprinkled with sand so that nobody slips.

{img:04-polytechnic-institute-steps}

Inside, the corridors smell of floor polish, chalk and wet wool. Students hang their coats in a crowded cloakroom and climb the stairs to lecture halls where the radiators knock and hiss. They study radio engineering, building construction, chemistry, metallurgy and the physics of heat. They fill notebooks with small, careful handwriting. Many of them are the first in their families ever to go to a university.

[pause]

The students do not have much money. A small monthly grant pays for their bread, their tram tickets and a bowl of soup in the canteen. Many live in dormitories, four or five young men to a room, with iron beds, a table covered with books, a kettle that is not strictly allowed and a window that never quite closes. At night, someone studies under a lamp while the others pretend to sleep.

[pause]

Still, the students find ways to enjoy the city. On Saturday evenings, there are dances at the institute, with a record player on a table and a line of shy young men by the wall. There is a skating rink, lit by lamps, where couples glide in circles to the sound of waltzes from a loudspeaker. There is the cinema, where for a few kopecks one can sit in the dark and watch a film about distant seas. And in the centre of town stands the opera house, a handsome building with white columns, where the cheapest seats, high up under the roof, are always full of students in their best jackets.

[pause]

But what they lack in money, they make up for in energy. This is a generation that has grown up in the shadow of a great war, and they are determined to build things. They want to make bridges and radios and power stations. They want to travel. They want to see the vast country around them with their own eyes, and the easiest way to do that, when you have no money, is on foot or on skis.

{img:05-dormitory-room-students}

That is why almost every institute in the Soviet Union has a tourist club. The word means something a little different here than it does elsewhere. A tourist, in Russia in those years, is not someone in a hotel with a camera. A tourist is someone with a rucksack, a map, a compass and a pair of strong legs, who spends a holiday walking, skiing or paddling through wild country, sleeping in a tent and cooking on a fire.

[pause]

The tourist clubs hold meetings in the evenings, in rooms with long tables and a map pinned to the wall. Experienced hikers share advice about boots, about the best way to dry socks, about which rivers freeze early and which stay open. Beginners listen with shining eyes. Everything is organised. Every trip has a leader, a route, a plan for food, and a date by which the group must report back.

[pause]

Hiking is graded in categories, from the first up to the third. A first-category hike is a gentle introduction: a few days on a marked trail, close to a village. A third-category hike is the hardest that students can earn, a long journey through remote country, in difficult weather, far from any road. To lead a hike of that kind, a young man must already have completed others, and must have shown that he can look after his friends.

{img:06-tourist-club-meeting-map}

Winter hiking has its own particular beauty. On skis, the hikers glide across frozen rivers and silent clearings. They cross wide valleys where the snow lies as smooth as a sheet. At night they pitch a canvas tent in the shelter of the trees, light a small iron stove, and warm their hands around mugs of tea, while the cold presses on the walls outside and the stars glitter over the forest.

[pause]

They carry everything on their backs. A rucksack for a long winter trip holds a sleeping bag, spare socks, felt boots, a change of warm clothes, a pot, an axe, a saw, a first-aid kit, rope, a lantern, a few tools for mending skis, and food for two weeks. Most of the weight is food. Nobody buys anything on the way, because there is nothing to buy.

[pause]

The food is simple, and chosen with care. There are dry biscuits and crusts of bread, dried into rusks so that they will never go mouldy. There are tins of meat and stew, lard, butter, sugar, tea, cereal for porridge, a little dried fruit, and sweets for the evenings. Everything is weighed out and divided among the rucksacks, so that each person carries a fair share.

[pause]

The tourists sing as they walk. In the evenings they sing around the stove, in the tent or by an open fire, songs about rivers and railways and distant towns, about girls left behind, and about the mountains that wait ahead. There is always someone with a guitar, or someone with a good voice, and always a few who sing a little out of tune and do not mind at all.

{img:07-ural-map-on-wall}

Those who have not seen the Urals often imagine them as towering peaks, like the Alps. They are not. The Urals are old, among the oldest mountains on earth, and time has worn them down into long, rounded ridges that run north and south like the backbone of an enormous sleeping animal. They stretch for more than two thousand kilometres, from the Arctic Ocean almost to the steppes of Kazakhstan.

[pause]

In the south, they are gentle and green, with meadows and orchards on the lower slopes. But in the far north, beyond the last villages, the mountains grow bare and wild. Here the forest thins out into twisted birches and scrubby pines, and above the trees the ridges are bald and stony, covered with moss and lichen in summer and swept bare by wind in winter.

[pause]

The wind is something the people who live here talk about a great deal. On the open ridges, above the tree line, it can blow for days from the north, steady and bitter, hissing over the snow and carrying fine powder with it so that the whole mountain seems to smoke. In the forest below, a person might stand in perfect calm and see the clouds of snow streaming over the summit, high above, like a flag.

{img:08-northern-taiga-winter}

The northern Urals are among the emptiest places in Europe. In an area as large as a small country, there are a few logging camps, a few old mining towns, a few scattered villages along the rivers, and a great deal of forest. Pine and spruce and cedar stand shoulder to shoulder, mile after mile, with deep snow lying between the trunks. Elk move through the trees. Sables and squirrels leave small prints along the frozen streams.

[pause]

Here, for many centuries, the land has belonged to a people called the Mansi. They are hunters, fishermen and reindeer herders, who followed the animals through the forest in winter and fished the rivers in summer long before any road was built. They speak a language of their own, and they know the mountains by names that have been handed down from grandparent to grandchild.

{img:09-mansi-hunter-forest}

A Mansi hunter in winter wears a coat of reindeer skin with the fur turned inward, and boots of soft hide. He travels on short, broad skis lined with fur, which let him slide uphill without slipping back. He walks the same paths his father walked, cutting a notch in a tree at certain places, so that those who follow will know the way. These blazed trails run through the forest like threads, and a traveller who finds one can follow it for days.

[pause]

The Mansi have a great respect for certain places. A hill may be a place where a spirit lives, or a mountain may be a place where it is better not to hunt. Some of these places are marked by old stories, and strangers who walk through the country are sometimes told which slopes the people leave alone. It is not that the mountains are cruel. It is that they are old, and that they are treated with care.

[pause]

One of these mountains lies far up in the north of the Sverdlovsk region, on a long ridge not far from the border of the neighbouring province. Its name in the Mansi language is Kholat Syakhl. The words are often translated as Dead Mountain, though some say they mean something more like the mountain of the dead, or simply the barren mountain, because few animals live on its bare, windy slopes and the hunters rarely find game there.

{img:10-mansi-fireside-stories}

Not far away stands another mountain, a little taller, with the name Otorten. It is sometimes said that this name means something like "do not go there". Later, people will argue about whether that is really so, and the Mansi who know their own language will say that the meaning is more modest and more difficult to translate. In any case, Otorten is a quiet, remote, rounded summit, and most people who live in the cities have never heard of it.

[pause]

The Mansi tell stories at night. In their winter camps, the family gathers around a fire in a hut or a tent. Someone cuts strips of dried fish, and the children listen as the old people tell of ancient heroes, of the birth of the world, of the animals who talk, and of the sky people who live in the stars. The stories are long, and they go on late, and the fire is fed with dry birch until the very last moment before sleep.

{img:11-ski-workshop-evening}

In the city of Sverdlovsk, in the winter of nineteen fifty-nine, the tourist club of the Polytechnic is busy with its own preparations. In a workshop near the dormitories, in the evenings, there is the smell of wax and wood shavings. Skis are being waxed, bindings tightened, straps mended. Tents are being checked for tears and sewn again with strong thread. A map of the northern Urals lies unrolled on a table, held down at the corners with mugs.

[pause]

The map is a rough one. There are rivers, blue and thin, and contour lines that wander like the marks of a pencil. In the north, there are many white patches where almost nothing is marked at all. The students trace the lines with a finger, and say the strange names aloud, Lozva, Auspiya, Ivdel, Vizhay, Otorten, and try to imagine what the real country looks like beneath the ink.

[pause]

Someone has an idea. A group of friends will go there, in the middle of winter, in the coldest, darkest, most silent part of the year. They will go on skis, carrying everything they need. They will cross the forest and climb the mountains, and they will spend their nights in a tent, and they will come home with photographs and songs and stories that they can tell for the rest of their lives.

{img:12-student-planning-route}

It is a bold plan, but not a reckless one. Young people have made such journeys before, and many of them have come home with nothing worse than frozen fingers and a fine pride. The planners go over each day in detail, how far they will travel, where they will stop, how much food they will carry. They write it all out in a neat plan and take it to the sports committee, whose members will read it and either sign or send it back with questions.

[pause]

The person who has drawn up the plan is a young man called Igor Dyatlov. He is a fifth-year student of radio engineering, tall and calm, with a quick mind and a straight, serious way of looking at things. He has been hiking since his first year at the institute, and has already taken part in several long trips through the mountains, including one in the Sayan Mountains of Siberia. Other students say that if you have to go somewhere difficult, Igor is the person you would like to go with.

[pause]

He is twenty-three. He has a slide rule in his pocket, a notebook full of numbers, and a plan.

{img:13-dyatlov-at-desk-lamp}

Igor Dyatlov works late on the plan. In the evenings, after lectures, he sits at a table in the dormitory under a green lamp, with a ruler, a pencil and a sheet of squared paper, and he draws the route in thin, careful lines. He measures the distances with a piece of thread. He counts days, kilometres, and loaves of bread. Then he counts them all again.

[pause]

The idea is a long loop through the northern Urals. The group will take a train north to the old town of Ivdel, then travel by truck to the last logging settlements, and from there set out on skis, up the valley of the river Lozva and then along its tributary, the Auspiya. After that they will climb to a high ridge, make their way to the summit of Otorten, and cross over to another valley to return south, to the village of Vizhay. In all, it will be about three hundred kilometres, in some sixteen days.

[pause]

He gives the trip a purpose. Like many student expeditions of the time, it is dedicated to a great political occasion, the twenty-first congress of the Communist Party, which opens at the end of January. It is the sort of thing a sports committee likes to read. But for the young people, the purpose is simply the thing itself, the long white journey into the unknown.

[pause]

At the end of the plan, Dyatlov writes a date. By the twelfth of February, the group should arrive at Vizhay and send a telegram to the sports club in the city. Then he adds a note, being a careful man. If they are delayed, the telegram may come a few days later, perhaps by the fifteenth. Mountains do not keep to schedules.

{img:14-ten-students-group}

Now the group must be chosen. A winter trip of this kind needs people who are strong, patient and good-humoured, since for two weeks they will share every meal and every night in a small tent. In the end, ten friends sign up. Most are students or recent graduates of the Polytechnic, and several have known each other for years.

[pause]

There is Zinaida Kolmogorova, whom everyone calls Zina, a clever, cheerful young woman who studies radio engineering like Igor, and who has already walked hundreds of kilometres in the mountains. She writes a diary during every trip, in a neat hand. Lyudmila Dubinina is the youngest of the group, only twenty, a student of construction, full of energy, who loves to laugh, to sing, and to jump into any adventure with both feet. She, too, is a keen writer of diaries.

[pause]

Alexander Kolevatov, twenty-four, is a physics student, a quiet and thoughtful young man, a little bookish, who likes to take photographs and who has spent several summers on trips in the mountains. Rustem Slobodin is twenty-three, a sturdy engineer with a ready grin and a reputation for never being tired. Nikolai Thibeaux-Brignolles, whom his friends call Kolya, is a construction student with a French-sounding family name that goes back to an ancestor who once travelled to Russia, and with a warm voice that people like to hear at the end of a long day.

{img:15-three-yuris-laughing}

Then there are the three Yuris, which causes a certain amount of friendly confusion. Yuri Doroshenko is twenty-one, a lively, dark-haired young man who has a talent for making the others laugh. Yuri Krivonischenko is twenty-three, a graduate who has worked at a plant in the region, a sensible, experienced hiker who knows how to fix almost anything. And Yuri Yudin, twenty-one, an economics student, who is known for his good manners and his steady cheerfulness. When someone shouts "Yura!" in the dormitory corridor, three young men look up at once.

[pause]

The tenth member is a little older than the rest. Alexander Zolotaryov is thirty-seven, a veteran of the war, and he has worked for many years as a guide and instructor on mountain trips. He has a lean, weathered face and a quiet manner, and the students look up to him a little. He is joining the trip because he wants to earn a higher qualification in mountain tourism, and because he enjoys the company of young people who are keen to learn.

[pause]

All of them are strong skiers. All of them have slept in tents in the winter before. They are not reckless adventurers, but a well-prepared group of friends who trust one another.

{img:16-sewing-tent-dormitory}

In the days before the trip, the dormitory room turns into a small workshop. The group has a large tent, big enough for ten, which has been made by sewing together two smaller canvas tents. It has a ridge pole, a pair of side flaps, and a sloping roof that sheds the snow. Along the inside of the ridge, the hikers can hang a line to dry their socks.

[pause]

On the longest nights of the trip they will use a small iron stove, light enough to carry in a rucksack. It is no bigger than a bread bin, with a pipe that lets the smoke escape through a hole in the tent wall. When it burns, the tent grows warm, and wet boots steam beside it. Without it, a winter night can be long and hard.

[pause]

The friends check their skis. They tighten the straps on their rucksacks. Each one packs a sleeping bag, a spare jumper, thick woollen socks, felt boots for evenings in camp, a fur hat, and a windproof jacket. In a separate bag they pack the food, the repair kit, the maps and compasses, a couple of cameras, and some notebooks and pencils for the diaries.

{img:17-packing-rucksacks-food}

The food is laid out on a long table and sorted into piles. Dry biscuits and rusks. Tins of meat. Slabs of lard, which gives strength in the cold. Sugar, in white lumps. Tea, in a small paper packet. Cereals, such as oats and buckwheat, for porridge. Dried soup. A bar of chocolate for emergencies. Each pile is divided into ten, so that nobody carries more than another, and each portion is tied in a cloth bag and marked with a pencil.

[pause]

It all weighs a good deal. A fully packed rucksack can weigh thirty kilograms or more, and the hikers lift each other's bags onto their shoulders with a grunt and a laugh. Once the load is on, a strap is pulled across the chest, and the whole thing settles into a weight that feels almost like a part of the body.

[pause]

The two women of the group take special care over the small comforts. Zina sews a few extra patches onto the lining of the tent. Lyudmila packs a little bag of boiled sweets, to be handed out on the hardest days. Someone slips a pack of playing cards into a pocket, and someone else a small notebook of poems. These are the things that make a long journey feel like a holiday, and they weigh almost nothing.

[pause]

On the evening before the departure, the friends gather in the dormitory for one last meeting. Dyatlov stands by the map and goes through the route again, from the first day to the last. He reminds them to look after their feet, to drink enough tea, to speak up if something hurts. He is a leader who listens, and nobody is afraid to ask him a question.

{img:18-railway-station-night-sverdlovsk}

On the twenty-third of January, they walk to the railway station. The old station building stands at the centre of the city, with a big clock above the entrance and a waiting hall full of travellers, sacks, bundles and sleepy children. The ten friends arrive in a cheerful group, carrying skis on their shoulders, rucksacks on their backs, and a lot of luggage in their pockets, and they attract a few friendly glances.

[pause]

A photographer, or perhaps a friend with a camera, takes their picture on the platform. They stand close together, in fur hats and padded jackets, some smiling, some squinting, their breath rising in the cold air. A few of them hold their skis upright like staffs. They look like what they are, a group of young people setting off on the best trip of their lives.

[pause]

Steam hisses from the engine. A conductor in a thick coat walks along the platform and calls the passengers aboard. The friends climb up with their gear, pack the skis in the corridor, and find their seats in a wooden carriage that smells of coal smoke, tea and old varnish. The train gives a long, low whistle, and the platform begins to slide past the window.

{img:19-train-carriage-night-singing}

The journey north is slow. The train travels through the night, through forests, over frozen rivers, and past small stations where a single lamp burns on a pole. In the carriage, the friends sit shoulder to shoulder on the wooden benches. Someone opens a tin of sprats. Someone passes round a bag of bread. A conversation begins in the corridor and slowly drifts into song.

[pause]

It is a long evening of singing. They sing the old songs of the tourists, which everyone knows, and the new songs that are just becoming popular. Zina has a clear, light voice. Kolya has a deep one. The others join in, in all kinds of keys, and when they forget the words they laugh and start another. The other passengers listen, smile, and sometimes join in themselves.

[pause]

Outside the window the dark forest glides by, with a pale line of snow between the trees and the occasional sparkle of a village light. The train rocks gently. After midnight, the singing fades. One by one, the friends fall asleep against their rucksacks, with their hats pulled over their eyes.

{img:20-serov-station-morning}

In the grey light of the early morning, the train stops at Serov, an industrial town in the northern part of the region, with tall chimneys, long rows of low houses and a great deal of snow. The hikers climb down, stamp their feet on the platform, and drink hot tea from the station buffet. Their first day of the journey is already behind them.

[pause]

They have to wait a few hours for the connecting train to Ivdel, and they use the time well. Together they visit the town, look at the shop windows, and buy a few small things, a bag of sweets, some extra bread. Some of them find a place to write postcards. Others simply stand outside in the sun, enjoying the cold and the open sky after the stuffy carriage.

[pause]

At the station, they meet a few local people who ask where they are going. A young woman at a kiosk looks at the skis, then at the rucksacks, and asks if they are not afraid of the cold. They laugh and tell her that the cold is the whole point.

{img:21-ivdel-school-hall-evening}

By the next day, they have reached Ivdel, a small town on the edge of the great forest, with wooden houses, blue-painted window frames and smoke curling from every chimney. It is the last place of any size before the wilderness. Beyond it, there is only a thin road, a few logging villages, and the endless taiga.

[pause]

A local school offers the hikers a place to sleep, as is often done for visiting groups. They are given a classroom with a stove and a floor wide enough for ten sleeping bags. In the evening, the friends are invited to talk to the pupils and the teachers, and they tell stories of earlier trips, show their maps, and answer the many questions of the children, who gaze at the skis and the ice axes with open mouths.

[pause]

The next morning, after a good breakfast, they gather their things and head out to the road. A truck is waiting for them, an old lorry with an open back and a canvas cover, and the driver, a cheerful man in a quilted jacket, helps them load the skis. They climb in and sit on their rucksacks, close together to keep warm. The engine coughs, and the lorry begins to bump up the road to the north.

{img:22-lorry-forest-road-winter}

The road runs through the forest like a white ribbon. The lorry grinds along it slowly, sometimes slipping, sometimes stopping while the driver gets out to clear a drift. In the back, the friends wrap blankets round their knees. Outside, the pines slide by, huge and dark, laden with snow. The sun is low and gold, and the long shadows of the trees fall across the road like the bars of a ladder.

[pause]

In the late afternoon they arrive at the village of Vizhay. It is a modest settlement of timber houses and wooden sidewalks, with a small hostel, a shop and a clinic, mostly inhabited by families of foresters and woodcutters. Dogs bark. Smoke rises. A boy on a sledge stares at the visitors.

[pause]

They spend the night at a small hostel, and in the evening, they dry their socks by the hostel stove and sort their gear one more time. Beyond Vizhay, a smaller road continues north to a logging settlement, a place so remote that it is known simply by a number, the forty-first district. It is the last place on their route where people live all year round, and it is where their ski trip will really begin.

{img:23-forty-first-district-logging-camp}

The forty-first district turns out to be a logging camp strung along a muddy track, a few low wooden barracks, a canteen, a sawmill and a stack of freshly cut timber higher than a house. Smoke rises from a dozen stove pipes, and the air smells of resin and wet sawdust. In the evening, the workers come home from the forest with frost in their eyebrows, and the hikers are given a place to sleep in one of the hostels.

[pause]

They lay out their sleeping bags along a wall, and for the last time on the journey, there is a proper roof overhead. A woodcutter's wife brings in a pot of hot soup. In the corner, someone has left a battered guitar, and one of the students picks it up and strums a few chords. The loggers listen from their beds, and one of them asks if the young people really plan to walk up into the mountains in this weather.

[pause]

On the morning of the twenty-seventh of January, the hikers load their skis and rucksacks onto another truck and ride north as far as the road allows. Then the lorry stops, the engine falls silent, and the friends climb down onto the snow. The driver wishes them luck and turns around. Behind him, the sound of the engine slowly dies away, and the real quiet begins.

{img:24-skiing-out-of-settlement}

They put on their skis. They tighten their straps, adjust their hats, and look at one another with the same grin. Dyatlov nods, and the line of ten figures begins to move, one behind the other, up the valley of the river. The leader goes first, and the others follow in his tracks. The whole group is soon strung out along the white ground like a line of dark beads.

[pause]

Skiing with a heavy rucksack is not the same as skiing for pleasure. The rhythm is slow and steady. Push, glide, push, glide. The packs creak. The poles tap on the crust. The skier in front breaks the trail through new snow, which is the hardest work, and every half hour or so he steps aside and lets the next in line take over. In this way the burden is shared, and nobody grows too tired.

[pause]

By the afternoon, they reach a small settlement of old wooden huts that the hikers call the Second Northern. It was once a mining village, and its houses stand now half empty, with snow piled up to the windowsills and a few thin threads of smoke rising from the one or two that are still lived in. The hikers pass slowly between the huts, and a dog watches them from a doorway with its head on its paws.

{img:25-yudin-farewell-at-hut}

It is here that the first difficulty comes. Yuri Yudin, the economics student, has been feeling unwell since the start of the trip. A sharp pain has settled in his knee and the lower part of his back, an old problem that has been waking up in the cold. At first he tried to ignore it. But as the day goes on, the pain grows worse, and each step with a heavy pack becomes harder than the last.

[pause]

That evening he speaks to Dyatlov. It is not an easy thing to say. He has looked forward to this trip for months, and he does not want to let down his friends. But the route ahead is long, and the cold will only grow deeper. If he continues, he may slow the whole group, and even put them in difficulty. Dyatlov listens, thinks, and agrees that it is better for him to turn back.

[pause]

The next morning, the twenty-eighth of January, the group stands outside the hut for a while in the pale light, and says goodbye. There are handshakes and embraces. Someone gives Yudin a few things to carry back. Someone makes a joke, and everybody laughs, a little more loudly than the joke deserves. Yudin asks them to take good care of one another, and Igor promises that he will send a telegram from Vizhay in two weeks.

{img:26-yudin-walking-back-alone}

Then the group sets off up the valley, and Yudin turns south. For a time, he stands in the snow and watches the nine figures grow smaller, their skis flashing in the sun and the sound of their voices carried back to him on the thin air. At the edge of the forest, one of them turns and waves a pole. Then the trees close behind them, and he is alone.

[pause]

He will remember that moment for the rest of his life. He will say that he took one more look at them, and that nothing in their faces or their voices suggested anything but happiness. They were strong, they were well equipped, and they were off to do something they loved. He turned, adjusted his pack, and began the long walk back to the settlement.

{img:27-lozva-river-valley-skiers}

For the nine who continue, the days that follow have a steady rhythm. They wake early, melt snow for tea, eat a breakfast of porridge or biscuits, pack the tent, and set off along the valley of the Lozva. The river lies buried under ice and snow, a broad, flat road through the forest, and the skiers glide along it in a line with the dark banks rising on either side.

[pause]

The valley is beautiful in the pale winter light. The snow is untouched, rippled by the wind into long waves, and the shadows of the trees fall across it in blue stripes. Here and there, small birds flutter from branch to branch, and the tracks of a hare or a fox run across the white surface and vanish into the trees. The skiers talk little while they travel. They save their breath, and the silence is large and clean.

[pause]

At midday the group stops for lunch. They do not light a fire for this. The leader looks at his watch, raises a hand, and the line of skiers comes to a halt among the trees. Rucksacks are lowered onto the snow, and everyone sits on them, close together, to keep out of the cold. A flask of hot sweet tea is passed from hand to hand. There are biscuits, a slice of dry sausage, and thin slices of lard on black bread, which taste better than any restaurant dinner, because everyone is hungry and everything is shared. After twenty minutes, the packs go back on, the skis slide forward, and the long afternoon begins.

[pause]

In the afternoon, as the sun slips behind the ridge and the cold deepens, the group looks for a place to camp. They need a flat spot, with dry standing trees nearby for firewood and shelter from the wind. When they find one, the work begins at once. Some of the friends clear the snow with their skis, stamping out a flat floor. Others fetch firewood, and others unpack the tent.

{img:28-evening-camp-stove-glow}

There are jobs for everyone, and the jobs rotate each day. One person is on duty to cook, and one to gather wood. One keeps the stove alight through the evening and the night. Another makes sure the boots are placed where they will not freeze. The tent is stretched over its ridge pole, the sides are weighted with snow, and the little stove is set up inside with its pipe poking out through a hole in the canvas.

[pause]

Within half an hour, the tent glows from within like a lantern. Inside, the air grows warm. Wet socks are hung from the line above the stove. A pot of soup begins to bubble. Everyone sits cross-legged on spread-out sleeping bags, hands cupped around hot mugs, faces pink from the heat. Outside the canvas, the temperature may be twenty degrees below zero, and inside it is cosy enough to take off a jacket.

[pause]

After supper they talk, tell stories and sing. Sometimes Kolya starts a tune in his warm voice, and the others hum along. Zolotaryov, the oldest, tells stories of other mountains, other winters, and the younger members listen with the particular respect that young people give to someone who has really been through things. Sometimes the talk turns to the future, to jobs and marriages and the places they hope to see.

{img:29-diary-writing-by-lantern}

Before sleep, a few of them take out their notebooks. Zina writes down the day's journey in neat, patient lines, and Lyudmila adds her own thoughts in her lively hand. They record the distances, the times, the temperature and the mood of the group. They write about the beauty of the forest, about a funny thing someone said at lunch, about how good the porridge tasted. These small, ordinary notes will later become very precious.

[pause]

Dyatlov keeps the group's official log. It is a more serious record, with sketches of the route and details about the terrain, and it is written in the plain language of an engineer. Each evening, he studies his map by lantern light, marks the place where they stopped, and calculates how far they have to go. Then he closes the notebook and puts it away.

[pause]

At last the stove is banked, the lantern is turned down, and the tent grows quiet. Someone coughs. Someone turns over. A small gust of wind moves the canvas and a little powdery snow slides off the roof. Outside, the forest stands in total silence, and over it all the stars shine hard and bright in the frozen black sky.

{img:30-deep-snow-forest-trail}

On the following days, the going becomes slower. The valley narrows. The river bends, and in places it has opened up under the ice, leaving dark patches that the skiers must go around. The snow grows deeper under the trees, soft as flour, and the leader sinks to his knees with every stride. The group has to change the person at the front every few minutes.

[pause]

In the middle of the forest, they come across a trail that has been cut by hunters, a line of notches on tree trunks, which are the Mansi blazes. The notches mark a path through the trees, and the hikers use it as a guide, following it up the valley. Sometimes the path vanishes under fresh snow, and they must search for the next cut in the bark, like sailors looking for a buoy in fog.

{img:31-mansi-blaze-on-pine-trunk}

The weather is changeable. On some days the sky is clear and blue, and the light on the snow is almost too bright to look at. On others, low grey cloud sinks over the valley, and a light snowfall begins, soft as down. The temperature hovers around twenty below, rising a little in the middle of the day, and falling again as the sun disappears. It is cold, but it is the kind of cold that these hikers know well, and they dress for it.

[pause]

Photographs from these days show tired, happy faces. There is a picture of someone making tea over a fire, with a blackened pot and a ring of friends bent over it. There is a picture of two skiers with frost on their eyebrows, grinning into the camera. There is a picture of a long line of skis planted upright in the snow, beside a tent glowing in the dusk. In none of them is there anything but ease.

{img:32-auspiya-valley-dawn-light}

On the thirty-first of January, the group reaches the upper valley of the Auspiya, a quiet, snowy basin closed in by low hills. Here the forest starts to thin out. The trees grow smaller and more crooked, and between them the open, windswept ground begins to show. Ahead, a long ridge rises above the trees, white and bare against the sky. It is the beginning of the mountains proper.

[pause]

The next stage of the route will lead them up onto the ridge and toward Otorten, over open ground where the wind can blow at will. To make the climb easier, Dyatlov decides to lighten the load. Near the edge of the forest, the hikers build a little storage platform, a small raised floor of logs on posts, a kind of cache, where they can leave the things that they will not need for the next two days. They hope to return to it after the climb.

{img:33-cache-platform-forest-edge}

They leave behind spare food, some of their winter clothing, and a pair of skis. Everything is wrapped in canvas and tied down, and the whole bundle is lifted onto the platform, out of reach of foxes and mice. It is a sensible, experienced decision. Only the things that are needed for the push to Otorten will go with them.

[pause]

Later, people will often note that the group left behind some of their warm things. But it is worth remembering how normal this is. On a long expedition, hikers usually lighten the load before a difficult stage, and they come back for the stored equipment afterwards. The group had planned to go up to Otorten, to look at the view, and to descend again to the cache, where they would spend another night in the shelter of the forest.

[pause]

In the late afternoon, the friends make camp for what will be the last night under the trees. They do their usual chores. They light the stove. They cook a supper of soup and cereal. Afterwards, with the stove glowing and the day's work done, they settle into a particularly good mood, since the hardest part of the trip is behind them, and tomorrow, they will see the open mountains.

{img:34-evening-otorten-newspaper-tent}

That night the friends make a small joke newspaper. It is a tradition on many student trips, a single sheet of paper, handwritten and decorated, with funny headlines and little drawings. They call it "The Evening Otorten", after the mountain they will climb. It carries news of the day's journey, light remarks about their own habits, and a cheerful mention of the legendary snowman, the creature of the high mountains that every newspaper in the world has been writing about that winter.

[pause]

Several people contribute. Someone adds a drawing. Someone teases the cook. Someone writes a mock announcement about the weather and the group's progress, and the others laugh until the tent shakes. The whole thing is warm, silly and good-humoured, the sort of thing that friends make when they feel at home with one another.

[pause]

When the paper is finished, they pin it up inside the tent and lie back to enjoy the last evening of the forest. The lantern burns low. Outside, the stars are bright, and the snow squeaks under the boots of the person on stove duty who steps out to fetch more wood. It is the last night they will spend in the shelter of the trees.

{img:35-last-night-forest-tent-glow}

Nobody knows that, of course. They are tired and content and thinking of tomorrow. In the morning, the sun will rise over the Auspiya. The friends will pack their rucksacks, shoulder their skis and set off toward the white ridge, and from that moment on, the story of their journey will become something that people will tell for generations.

{img:36-forest-edge-morning-breakfast}

The first of February, nineteen fifty-nine, is a Sunday. In the forest at the edge of the upper Auspiya, the morning begins slowly. The friends wake in their warm tent, listen to the stove tick as it cools, and take their time getting up. Nobody is in a hurry. The day's plan is a short and simple one, a climb to the high ground, a look at the pass, and a camp somewhere near the foot of Otorten.

[pause]

They cook breakfast, drink their tea, and then start the long job of packing. The felt boots, which hung drying by the stove during the night, are slid into the rucksacks, to be worn again in the evening. Ski bindings are checked. Fingers are warmed. Around noon, in thin, bright sunlight, the group shoulders its packs, leaves the cache of spare things behind, and sets out on skis along the track they have already broken through the snow.

[pause]

It is worth remembering how short the day is at this time of the year, so far to the north. The sun rises late, climbs only a little way above the southern ridge, and sinks again by the middle of the afternoon. By four o'clock the light has already begun to turn blue, and by five it is almost gone. A group that sets out at noon has only a few hours of daylight in which to climb, to find a camp, and to pitch a tent.

[pause]

The sky is a pale, milky grey, with no clear edge to the clouds. The temperature is about twenty degrees below zero, but the air is almost still, and in the shelter of the trees it feels gentler than it sounds. They follow the little valley upward, passing through the last scattered stands of spruce and cedar. The trees grow shorter. The ground grows more open. After a while, the forest simply stops.

{img:37-skiers-climbing-open-ridge}

They stop once more at the very edge of the forest, where the last spruce stands, tall and a little crooked, with its dark branches heavy with snow. It is a good place for a short pause. Someone checks the map, someone tightens a strap, someone takes a photograph of the friends standing together with their skis planted beside them. In the picture, nine faces look out from under fur hats, cheeks red with cold, eyes bright. Behind them the open mountain rises, pale and smooth, into the cloud.

[pause]

Beyond the last trees, the land opens into a vast, white, rounded landscape, with no tree or rock to break it. The snow is crisp and polished by the wind. Far ahead, a long, smooth ridge lifts against the sky. The skiers stop for a moment to catch their breath and look. It is a wide, empty, magnificent country, the kind of view that fills the chest and makes a person feel very small.

[pause]

As they climb, the weather begins to change. Slowly, almost without anybody noticing, a wind starts to stir from the west and south. It lifts a thin veil of powder snow off the ground, and the powder blows across the slope in long, whispering ribbons. Then the clouds thicken, and the sun, which was only a pale disc, fades out of sight altogether.

[pause]

The wind gets stronger as the afternoon wears on. It pulls at the hoods of the jackets and sets the ends of the scarves flapping. Snow begins to fall in fine, sharp grains that sting the cheeks. In the exposed places the skiers bend their heads and push on, one behind another, each following the dark shape of the person in front. Visibility shrinks to a few dozen metres, and then to less.

{img:38-whiteout-slope-skiers}

In weather like this, even skilled hikers can lose their way. On a wide slope in the falling snow, the ground and the sky become the same shade of white. There is no horizon. There are no landmarks. A skier may think he is travelling straight, and in fact be curving slowly to one side, as if pulled by an invisible hand. The compass helps, but only if the ground allows the group to follow it.

[pause]

This is what seems to happen to the friends. Their plan was to go up the valley and cross over the low pass to the next side, where they would find shelter in the trees and camp for the night. But in the whiteout, with the wind driving at their faces, the group drifts to the left. Instead of reaching the pass, they find themselves climbing the open eastern slope of Kholat Syakhl, the mountain whose name means Dead Mountain, a bare, rounded summit more than a thousand metres high.

[pause]

It is late in the afternoon. The light is going fast. The group has been climbing for hours and the wind is bitter. Somewhere ahead is the pass, and somewhere below is the forest, but in this swirling white world nobody can be sure where. Dyatlov stops, looks round, and makes a decision.

{img:39-skiers-digging-platform-snow}

They will not turn back. Going down in the dark and the wind, to find a camp among the trees, would cost time, and it would mean losing the height they have climbed. Instead, he decides to pitch the tent right here, on the slope, and to continue in the morning. It is not an unusual choice for an experienced group. In fact, some say the leader wanted to give his friends a lesson in camping on open ground in high winds, which is a useful thing to know in the mountains.

[pause]

So the work begins. The friends take off their skis and dig into the slope. They cut out a level terrace in the snow, a shelf large enough for the tent, using their shovels and skis. They stamp it flat. The wind howls and hisses around them, and the light fades. Their hands are numb, and their cheeks are stiff, but they work together as they have worked every day of the journey.

[pause]

One of them lifts a camera and takes a few pictures. These will be the last photographs on the roll. They show the group in the blue dusk, wrapped in hoods and scarves, bending over the snow with their shovels, while blown snow streams across the slope in pale veils. In one frame, a figure steps toward the camera, and the tent is going up behind. The shots are blurred and grainy, but they carry the mood of the moment, which is one of work, of cold, and of ordinary determination.

{img:40-tent-pitched-dusk-lantern}

At last the tent is up. The ridge pole is raised. The canvas is stretched and tied down. The ski poles and the skis are driven into the snow around it, to give it some extra support against the wind. A line of footprints leads to the entrance, and the group squeezes inside, one by one, out of the whistling dark.

[pause]

Inside, the little stove is lit, the lantern is hung, and the sleeping bags are spread on the floor. The temperature quickly begins to climb. Boots are taken off. Warm socks come out of the rucksacks. Hands are pressed to the hot iron, and the friends begin to laugh and talk again, the way people do when they have come in from the cold. Here, in the middle of the evening, there is warmth, light and company.

[pause]

Supper is prepared. They have a hot meal, which they eat from their mugs and bowls, sitting shoulder to shoulder on their sleeping bags. Later, the investigators will be able to tell that their last meal was eaten some hours before they left the tent, and the notebooks and the supplies show that the evening was a normal one. There is nothing to suggest an argument, a quarrel or a sense of alarm.

{img:41-inside-tent-evening-warm}

The wind keeps blowing, steady and low, around the canvas. It sounds, perhaps, like a long breath. Beyond the thin walls, a hundred metres below the top of the mountain, the snow hisses and drifts, and the stars are hidden by cloud. Inside, the stove ticks, a pot cools, a mug is passed around, and the friends begin to turn toward sleep, one after another, in the dim yellow light.

[pause]

This is the last moment of the journey of which anything at all is known.

[pause]

After that, there are no diaries, no photographs, no witnesses. The next time anybody will see the tent, it will be empty, covered with snow, and sagging on its poles. And the question that has followed the group ever since will be a simple and haunting one. What happened on the slope of Kholat Syakhl that night?

{img:42-night-sky-over-kholat-syakhl}

[long-pause]

Let us pause here for a moment, on the slope, at the end of that evening.

[pause]

Above the tent, the mountain rises into the dark. The summit is a smooth white curve, and the wind sweeps over it with a long, low sound. Below, far down the slope, lies the edge of the forest, a black line against the snow. Between them, the tent stands alone on its terrace, with its canvas shaking a little in the gusts, and a thin glow of light showing through the cloth.

[pause]

The temperature is about thirty degrees below zero, perhaps colder. The cloud drifts. The snow whispers. A small cloud of smoke rises from the stove pipe, and the wind carries it away, over the ridge, into the empty north.

[pause]

Somewhere in the course of the night, the light in the tent goes out. And somewhere in the course of the night, for a reason that nobody has been able to say with certainty, the nine friends leave their tent.

{img:43-empty-tent-in-snow-moonlight}

They go out onto the slope in the dark. They are dressed lightly, some in their socks, one or two in a single boot. They walk down the mountain, away from their tent, in the direction of the forest. They walk together, and they do not return.

[pause]

It is an extraordinary thing. A group of strong, sensible, experienced hikers, with warm clothes lying just inside the tent, walks out into a night of bitter cold and wind without putting on their boots. And nothing inside the tent suggests why.

[pause]

The tent itself will show one more strange detail. When it is found, there will be a long cut in the canvas, on the side of the tent, made from the inside, as if someone had slit it open with a knife to get out quickly. Inside, the sleeping bags and the rucksacks lie more or less where they were left.

{img:44-kholat-syakhl-dawn-empty}

Morning comes, as it always does. The sky grows grey, then pale blue, and the first light touches the summit and slides slowly down. The wind drops. The clouds thin. The mountain lies silent and white and empty beneath a huge, cold sky, and the tent stands on its slope with its cut side flapping gently. The snow, little by little, begins to cover the footprints.

[pause]

Far away, in the city, nobody yet knows anything at all. In Sverdlovsk, the tourist club goes about its work. Families sit down to their Sunday dinners, a letter is written, a lecture is planned. A group of friends is a little overdue, but there is no reason to worry, for nothing about a winter trip is ever on time.

[pause]

The days pass. The twelfth of February comes, the date on which the group was due to send a telegram from Vizhay. No telegram arrives. At the sports club, a few people look at the calendar and shrug. Dyatlov had said that they might be late. Winter, weather and heavy rucksacks do this to people. Perhaps they will arrive tomorrow.

{img:45-families-waiting-telegram}

But a few more days go by, and still there is nothing. The parents of the hikers begin to ask questions at the institute. A mother stands in a corridor and asks, politely, whether there has been any news. A brother knocks on a door. Someone makes a telephone call to Ivdel and is told that the group was last seen heading north, some weeks before.

[pause]

On the twentieth of February, the institute makes a decision. A search will be organised. Volunteers are called for, and the response is immediate. Students from the tourist club, young teachers, experienced hikers, men who knew the mountains, all of them come forward and ask how they can help. They gather in a hall at the Polytechnic, pore over the map, and begin to plan.

{img:46-search-volunteers-institute-hall}

The first small groups leave the city by train and truck. They are followed, in the days after, by larger ones. Soldiers join them, and local people from the villages. The authorities send aircraft, which fly low over the forests and the ridges in the clear cold weather, looking for any sign of a tent, a fire, or a line of ski tracks.

[pause]

At the same time, Mansi hunters, who know the land best of all, take part in the search. They ski in their fur-lined boots across the valleys, reading the snow as another person might read a page. They look for broken branches, old fire-sites, the marks of skis. They say little. They simply keep going, hour after hour, up the long valleys of the north.

{img:47-search-plane-over-winter-forest}

The searchers follow the route that Dyatlov described in his plan. They go up the Lozva. They go up the Auspiya. They find a few traces, an old campsite, a trail of ski marks half-filled with snow, and the little platform in the forest where the friends left their spare things. Everything on it is untouched, wrapped in canvas and tied down exactly as it was left.

[pause]

This is a good sign, and at the same time a puzzling one. The group never returned to collect their things. So they must be somewhere further up, on the open mountain. The searchers climb out of the forest and start to look at the high ridge.

{img:48-search-party-skiing-valley}

On the twenty-sixth of February, in the middle of the afternoon, two students from the search party, skiing along the slope of Kholat Syakhl, see something strange. It is a small shape on the snow, a darker line against the white, half hidden by a drift. They move closer. It is a tent.

[pause]

The tent is sagging. One end is partly collapsed under the weight of the snow. The canvas is frozen stiff. Some of the skis and ski poles that were planted around it still stand in the snow. A few things lie scattered nearby. There is no sign of anyone.

{img:49-searchers-approach-tent-slope}

Carefully, the students call out, and no one answers. They step to the tent. The side of the canvas has been cut open from the inside, in a long, clean slit. Through the opening, they can see the interior, with sleeping bags, rucksacks, boots, warm clothing, notebooks, cameras and food. It looks as though the people who lived there have only just gone out, and might be back at any moment.

[pause]

A little further down, there is a line of footprints in the snow. They are the prints of people walking in socks, or with a single shoe, or even with bare feet, and they lead away from the tent down the slope toward the forest. For a few hundred metres, the searchers can follow them. Then the wind and snow have erased them, and the trail disappears.

{img:50-abandoned-tent-snow-slope-day}

The news travels fast. By the evening, more of the search party have arrived at the tent, and the first reports are sent down the mountain by radio. The tent is photographed, measured and described. Everything is noted down. And the young men stand there for a long time in the late afternoon light, looking down the slope into the forest, and wondering where their friends have gone.

{img:51-cedar-at-forest-edge-snow}

The footprints that the searchers have found lead down the slope in a nearly straight line. They go toward the edge of the forest, which lies more than a kilometre below the tent, where the bare mountain ends and the first cedars and pines begin. The searchers follow the line on skis, slowly, in the fading light, with their lanterns lit.

[pause]

At the very edge of the trees, they find a tall old cedar standing alone, with its lowest branches broken off high above the ground. Beneath it lie the burnt remains of a small fire, a ring of ashes and charred twigs in the snow. Someone had tried to light a fire here, in the dark, in the cold, with very little to work with. Around the tree, the snow has been trampled flat.

[pause]

It is here that the search turns into something far sadder. In this place, and in the days that follow, the searchers find the first of the friends. They were not far from one another. They had come down the mountain together, and had tried to warm themselves, and the cold had been stronger than they were. The findings are described in the reports in plain, careful language, and nothing needs to be added to them here.

{img:52-footprint-trail-down-slope-dusk}

In the days after that, the search continues. The line of footprints between the cedar and the tent is followed again and again, and three more of the friends are found along that line, spaced out across the slope, as though they had been trying to climb back toward the tent, to the warm clothes and the sleeping bags, but had not been able to reach it. Among them is Igor Dyatlov, the leader, who stayed with his friends to the very end.

[pause]

By the beginning of March, five of the nine have been found. Four are still missing. The search party works on, probing the snow with long poles, ski by ski, metre by metre, across the slope and the edge of the forest. The snow is deep, in some places three or four metres, and the wind has packed it hard. It is slow, patient, silent work.

{img:53-search-camp-lanterns-night}

At the search camp, in the evenings, the volunteers gather in a big tent around a stove. They are quiet. Some are the friends of the lost group, young people who studied with them and sang with them and walked with them on earlier trips. They write their reports, drink their tea, and listen to the radio crackling messages down the mountain. Outside, the wind moves in the trees.

[pause]

The news reaches Sverdlovsk. In the city, the first five funerals are held on a cold March day. Hundreds of people come, students, teachers, neighbours, workers, and a long, silent line of young people in dark coats stands in the snow at the cemetery gate. The families hold one another. A band plays a slow tune, and the air is full of breath and steam. It is a day that many in the city will never forget.

{img:54-funeral-gathering-sverdlovsk-snow}

Among the volunteers who have joined the search is a young engineer named Yevgeny Maslennikov, a skilled hiker who studied at the same institute and knew several of the missing friends. He is one of those who examine the tent on the slope, and he notices, with a careful eye, that the cut in the canvas was made from the inside. He is also one of those who will carry the heavy weight of this search for the rest of his life, and who will speak of it, years later, with gentleness and with respect for the families.

[pause]

By then, the authorities have opened an official investigation. In the town of Ivdel, a prosecutor named Lev Ivanov is put in charge. He is a thorough, serious man, with a lined face and a deep sense of duty, and he travels to the mountain, studies the tent, and speaks with the searchers. He reads the diaries. He reads the notes. He looks at the photographs, and puts every object in the tent on a list.

[pause]

The questions he must answer seem simple, but they are not. Who were these people? What had they been doing? Why did they leave their tent in the middle of a freezing night? Why did they not take their boots? What happened on that slope? And, perhaps most of all, was anybody else there?

{img:55-prosecutor-desk-files-lamp}

He begins with the possibility that other people might have been involved. The mountain is remote, but not empty. The Mansi hunters live in the forest. The district has old prison camps, and the investigators have to ask whether anyone might have escaped from one of them. But the evidence does not point that way. On the snow around the tent, there are no footprints but the group's own. The valuables are all there, the money, the cameras, the watches, the food. And the Mansi, who have been questioned with great patience and respect, have no quarrel with anyone, and no reason at all to harm a group of young skiers passing through.

[pause]

Ivanov looks at the weather. He looks at the tent. He looks at the slope, and he does not see any sign of an avalanche. The angle of the hillside is gentle, only about twenty degrees, and no mass of snow has swept across it, because the skis and poles around the tent are still standing upright. Nothing has been buried or broken. So the avalanche theory, in the first months, is put aside.

{img:56-mansi-elders-questioned-room}

In the spring, the melting begins. Day by day, the great white blanket over the forest shrinks. Streams trickle. The snow sags and cracks, and the ground begins to show through, dark and wet. At the beginning of May, the search party returns to the slope, and begins to work along the little stream that runs through a ravine in the forest, a short distance beyond the cedar.

[pause]

It is here, under a thick layer of old snow, that the last four friends are finally found. They are lying near one another in the ravine, about seventy-five metres from the cedar and the ashes of the fire. Their discovery brings a certain sorrow, but it also brings, at last, an end to the long uncertainty for the families.

{img:57-ravine-forest-spring-thaw}

What the doctors find is not entirely simple. The first five friends are found to have died of the cold. That is a sad but a plain explanation, and it seems to fit the story of a group that left its tent without proper clothing and tried to survive in a freezing forest. But among the last four, three have serious injuries that the doctors find hard to explain, injuries of a kind that might normally come from a very heavy blow, though there are no marks on the skin to show where it came from.

[pause]

Another small detail adds to the puzzle. Some of the friends are wearing garments that do not belong to them. A piece of clothing has been taken from one friend and worn by another. It seems that those who were still able to move took warm things from those who could not, and shared them out. It is a quiet, human detail, a small story of kindness in the darkest of hours, and it is part of what makes the mystery so affecting.

{img:58-investigators-table-cameras-notebooks}

The investigators examine everything. They develop the film from the cameras. They read through the diaries again and again, looking for a hint of conflict or fear, and find none. They study the clothing and the equipment. A few pieces of clothing, they report, carry small traces of radioactivity. The finding is noted in the file, though later researchers will say that it was very small, and probably had ordinary causes, such as the minerals in the soil, or the materials of old lanterns.

[pause]

Others tell of lights in the sky. On the nights of February and March, in that same part of the northern Urals, some people said that they had seen glowing orange spheres floating high above the mountains, silent and slow. A group of hikers camped some fifty kilometres to the south reported such a sight, and so did a few local people. In those years, rockets were being launched and tested in the far north of the country, and some have suggested that what was seen might have come from them. Others think it was simply a trick of the clouds and the cold.

{img:59-orange-lights-night-sky-urals}

In the end, after months of work, the investigators have to write something down. At the end of May, the prosecutor's office closes the case. The final report is short, and its wording has become famous. It says that the cause of the deaths was a compelling natural force, which the hikers were not able to overcome.

[pause]

That is all. The report does not say what the force was. It does not say whether it was snow, or wind, or cold, or something else. It simply puts a small, careful line around the mystery, and closes the folder.

[pause]

The mountains are closed to hikers for three years. The files are sealed and stored in a secret archive. For decades, the families and friends have only the briefest official answer, and a great deal of silence. The rest is left to rumour, memory and imagination.

{img:60-closed-case-folder-sealed-lamp}

And so the story of the Dyatlov group goes quiet for a while, like a stone dropped into deep water. But it does not disappear. In the apartments and dormitories of Sverdlovsk, in the tourist clubs, in the memories of those who joined the search, the questions remain. People tell the story again, with more details, and more theories, and in time it becomes one of the best-known mysteries of the twentieth century.

[pause]

Let us now look at the questions that remain, one by one, as calmly as we can.

[pause]

The first is the tent. Why did the friends leave it? The cut in the canvas shows that they left in a hurry, so something must have made them believe that it was no longer safe to stay inside. Whatever happened must have happened suddenly, and it must have been strong enough to make nine experienced hikers leave their warm sleeping bags and their boots, in a wind of thirty degrees below zero.

{img:61-tent-interior-boots-warm-clothes}

The second is the clothing. Experienced hikers know that their boots and their warm clothes are what keep them alive. To leave them behind, in such cold, is to put oneself in great danger. It tells us that the people who left the tent were not thinking about the cold. They were thinking about something else, and they believed that they would be able to come back for their things within a few minutes.

[pause]

The third is the direction. When the friends left the tent, they did not scatter. They went down the slope in a line, toward the forest, a long distance in the dark. They seem to have had a purpose, and a plan. They made a fire at the edge of the trees. They broke branches from the cedar, perhaps to feed the flames, or perhaps to climb. They tried to make a shelter in the ravine. All of this speaks of people who were thinking clearly, who were working together, and who were doing what trained hikers do.

{img:62-ravine-shelter-branches-forest}

The fourth is the injuries. The cold alone does not explain them. The doctors believe that a strong force, such as a heavy fall, or a great weight of snow, or a blow from something, may have caused them. Yet there is no sign of a fight, and no sign of a struggle. The friends do not seem to have harmed one another. They seem, rather, to have helped one another until the very end.

[pause]

And the fifth is the silence. For many years, the official file said so little that it left room for every kind of story. When at last the papers were opened in the nineteen nineties, some pages had gone missing, and people wondered about that, and went on wondering.

[pause]

It is on these five questions that the later theories are built.

{img:63-investigator-reading-old-pages}

In the decades that followed, hundreds of explanations were put forward. Some were practical and scientific. Some were dramatic and strange. Many of them came from people who had walked on that slope, or lived in the mountains, or spent long years studying the case. Each one tried to answer the same few questions, and none of them was able to answer them all.

[pause]

Let us go through the main ones, calmly, and see what each one explains, and what it leaves open.

{img:64-snow-slab-slope-theory-dusk}

The first theory is the one that many people thought of right away, and that the first investigators put aside. It is the avalanche.

[pause]

A snow slope can look perfectly safe and still carry a hidden weakness. Over the winter, the snow lies down in layers, and some layers are firm while others are loose and sugary. If a heavy slab of firm snow comes to rest on a loose layer beneath, it can hang there on the hillside, quietly, until something disturbs it. A footstep can be enough. So can a cut.

[pause]

Cutting a terrace into a slope, as the hikers did, to make a flat floor for their tent, is just the kind of change that can start the process. The cut leaves the snow above it without support. At the same time, a wind blowing across the top of the mountain can carry fresh snow and deposit it along the slope above the tent, adding weight, slowly, hour after hour, through the evening.

[pause]

In the year two thousand and twenty-one, two scientists, Johan Gaume and Alexander Puzrin, published a study of this very question. They built a computer model of the slope and the tent, using what is known about snow and about the wind of that night. Their model suggested that a small slab might indeed have broken loose some hours after the terrace was cut, perhaps after nightfall, with a soft, dull sound. It would not have buried the tent. It would have been only a short, heavy slide of snow, across one side of it.

{img:65-tent-in-night-wind-snow-gusts}

For the people inside, that would have been startling. A few hundred kilograms of snow sliding onto a canvas wall, in the dark, at thirty degrees below zero. The side of the tent would sag. Someone might be pressed against the stove. Someone might cry out. And in the first confusion, with the wind howling and the canvas collapsing, the group might well decide to cut their way out and leave, believing that the whole slope above them was about to come down.

[pause]

This is a powerful idea, because it can explain several of the questions at once. It explains why the group left in such haste, and why they went out of the tent through a cut. It explains why the heavy injuries appeared in some of the friends, for a slab of snow can press on a body with great force without leaving a mark. And it explains why an experienced group might leave their boots, because at the moment of the slide they would have been thinking only about getting out.

[pause]

In two thousand and twenty, the Russian prosecutor's office, after a new review of the old case, announced that an avalanche was the most likely cause. They said that the hikers had probably left the tent in fear of a second avalanche, and that they had died of the cold in the following hours, though some had also suffered injuries.

[pause]

Not everybody agrees. People who have studied the slope point out that it is quite gentle, and that avalanches on such slopes are rare. They say that there was no clear sign of a slide when the tent was found. They add that the group had enough experience to know that a small slide did not mean the whole slope would come down. The debate continues, politely, in books and conferences and on the internet.

{img:66-wind-over-rounded-summit-snow-plume}

The second theory is about the wind itself. On the open slopes of the northern Urals, the wind can blow with astonishing force. On a winter night, cold air pours down off the summit like water down a hill, picking up speed as it goes. Meteorologists call this a katabatic wind. It can reach the speed of a storm, and it can last for hours.

[pause]

For the nine friends in their tent, that wind would have been a continuous roar. The canvas would have drummed. Snow would have been forced through the seams. It would have been difficult to speak, and hard to sleep. In the dark, some of them might have begun to worry that the tent was being torn from its moorings, and to fear that it would be swept away.

[pause]

But experienced hikers are used to wind, and a strong wind alone seems too ordinary to explain such a dramatic decision. Perhaps it was part of the story, a push in a certain direction. It may have loaded the slope with snow, or made it impossible to stay calm. But most people think that something more than wind alone was needed to make the group leave.

{img:67-infrasound-vortices-summit-evening}

There is a human side to the wind theory, too. Anyone who has spent a night in a tent in a gale knows how tiring it is. The noise never stops. The canvas pulls at its ropes. Every few minutes, someone has to check that the ropes are holding and that the snow is not building up against the wall. After a long day of climbing, in the dark, with the temperature falling, it would be natural to be anxious. And an anxious group, hearing a loud, strange sound outside, might reach a decision very quickly.

[pause]

A third idea is a stranger one, and it came from a writer who walked to the slope himself. In his book about the mystery, published in two thousand and thirteen, Donnie Eichar offered an explanation based on the way wind moves around a rounded mountain top. When a steady wind flows past a smooth, rounded obstacle, it can form a regular pattern of whirling currents behind it, which scientists call vortices. In the right conditions, these vortices can produce a very low sound, deeper than the human ear can hear, called infrasound.

[pause]

Infrasound is known to affect people in unusual ways. A person who is exposed to it may feel a sense of dread, a pressure in the chest, a feeling of dizziness, without knowing why. If the hikers in the tent experienced such a feeling, so the idea goes, they might have been seized by sudden, powerful, unexplained fear, and rushed out into the night.

[pause]

It is a poetic idea, and it has attracted a great deal of interest. But it is also very difficult to prove. Nobody measured sounds on the slope that night, and scientists who have looked at the idea say that the effect is not well understood, and may not be strong enough to cause such behaviour. For now, it remains an interesting possibility, one among several.

{img:68-secret-tests-sky-theory-night}

The fourth family of theories is the one that appears most often in films and television. It says that the group stumbled into something they were never meant to see. In the nineteen fifties, the Soviet Union was testing new weapons across its vast territory. Rockets were launched from remote sites. Strange new devices were being developed. The northern Urals, empty and far from the cities, would have been a quiet place for such work.

[pause]

Those who favour this theory point to the lights in the sky that were seen that winter, to the traces of radiation on a few pieces of clothing, to the fact that the area was closed and the files were sealed. They say that the group might have been frightened by a flash, or a blast of air from a distant explosion, and that the friends might have fled from it.

[pause]

It is true that the files were sealed, and it is true that the authorities of the time said very little. But sealing a file does not prove that a secret lies inside it. When the files were later opened, researchers found nothing to show that any test took place on the mountain that night. There were no craters, no burns, no damage to the trees, no damage to the tent. The lights may have been rockets, or atmospheric tricks, or something that happened on another night altogether. The question is still open, but there is no real evidence to answer it.

{img:69-snowman-legend-cartoon-newspaper}

There are also ideas that belong more to legend than to science. Some people say that the group was frightened by a creature of the mountains, the legendary snowman, or Yeti, which was in the newspapers that winter. The friends had made jokes about it in their little paper. But there is no sign whatsoever of such a creature, no tracks and no remains, and the group's own diaries show that they were cheerful and at ease.

[pause]

Others have suggested that the Mansi attacked the group to protect their sacred mountain. But the Mansi were questioned at the time with great care, and the investigators found no evidence against them, and no footprints but those of the nine friends. Today, the Mansi people themselves say that the story has been told about them unfairly, and that their ancestors would have welcomed travellers in need.

[pause]

Before we turn to what researchers think today, it helps to remember what the friends left behind in their own words. Their diaries and notes, written on the evenings of the journey, are cheerful. They describe good food and tired legs, a funny remark at lunch, a song in the tent, the beauty of the pines in the sunlight. There is no mention of any quarrel, no sign of illness or fear. On the last pages, there is only the account of an ordinary day, and the plan for the next one. Whatever happened on the slope, it came without warning, and it came to people who were in good spirits and good company.

[pause]

So what are we left with? Most of the researchers who have looked at the case in recent years lean toward a combination of ordinary forces, the cold, the wind, the slope, and a sudden slide of snow, which may have set off a chain of events in which a group of skilled friends made quick decisions in the dark and fought bravely against the cold. This explanation is not complete. It leaves small questions unanswered, and it will probably never satisfy everyone.

[pause]

It is worth noticing what this explanation does not need. It needs no monster, no plot, no terrible secret on that mountain, only the mountain itself, with its snow and its wind and its night. And it shows us nine people at their best, helping one another to the end.

{img:70-yudin-older-man-holding-photograph}

Yuri Yudin, the friend who turned back at the Second Northern, goes home and lives the rest of his life with a heavy and tender memory. He is twenty-one years old when the news reaches him. He has already said goodbye to nine friends on a snowy morning, and he will never see them again. For years afterward, he is asked about it. He answers, patiently, in a quiet voice, and he never stops thinking about them.

[pause]

He finishes his studies, finds work, and builds an ordinary, useful life, and he is known to his neighbours for his gentle manner. He keeps the photographs, the letters, and the handwritten notes. Over the years he goes back to the north more than once, and he speaks to journalists and to young hikers who want to hear the story from someone who was there.

[pause]

He often says that the friends were not a group of careless students, as some newspapers would later suggest. They were strong, serious, and kind to one another, and he is certain that they did everything they could. He keeps their memory alive until his own death, in the year two thousand and thirteen, at the age of seventy-five. He is the last of the ten.

{img:71-cemetery-snow-memorial-stones-yekaterinburg}

In the city, the families of the lost friends face their grief in their own ways. Mothers and fathers keep their children's rooms as they were. Brothers and sisters tell their own children the stories of the uncles and aunts who went to the mountains. Old classmates meet, year after year, on the first of February, and light a candle, and drink a quiet glass of tea.

[pause]

Most of the friends are buried in the Mikhailovskoe cemetery, in Sverdlovsk, which is today called Yekaterinburg, since the city took back its old name in the nineteen nineties. Their graves lie close together, in a row among tall birches and pines, with dark stone markers and small photographs set into them. Each photograph shows a smiling young face, someone who was loved and who is still remembered. In winter, snow covers the stones. In summer, there are flowers.

[pause]

The institute that they studied at is now a large university, and it keeps their memory. In a corridor near the lecture halls, there is a memorial, with photographs and a few small objects. Every year, in the first days of February, students gather there to remember the group, and to talk about the lessons that the story holds about friendship, about courage and about caution.

{img:72-memorial-plaque-at-the-pass}

On the slope itself, far in the north, there is a memorial too. Not long after the tragedy, friends and fellow hikers placed a small plaque on a rock near the place where the tent had stood. Later, other memorials were added. There is a stone in the forest near the old cedar. There is a plaque with the nine names, and a few photographs. There are flags and small tokens, a cup, a candle, a pair of gloves, a woollen hat left by someone who walked a very long way to be there.

[pause]

The place has become known as the Dyatlov Pass, named after the leader of the group, even though the tent was not exactly on a pass at all, but on the slope of the mountain beside it. Mountain tourists make the long trip to the area each year, usually in winter, by snowmobile and on skis. They set up tents near the forest edge, and sometimes they stay for a night, and talk quietly around a fire.

[pause]

They bring warm clothes. They look at the weather. They remember that the mountains, however gentle they look, deserve respect. Many winter hikers say that the story of the Dyatlov group taught them a rule that they now follow almost without thinking: when the weather turns, go down to the trees.

{img:73-foundation-museum-yekaterinburg-evening}

After the political changes of the early nineteen nineties, when the files of the case were opened to the public, a foundation was created in Yekaterinburg to study the story and to keep its memory. Retired investigators, relatives, former search party members and mountain tourists helped to gather documents, photographs and recollections. A small museum was set up. Books were written, and expeditions were made, and a great many people, in Russia and far beyond it, became fascinated by the mystery.

[pause]

The case has been reopened more than once. In the year two thousand and nineteen, Russian prosecutors announced that they would take another look, and a team travelled to the slope in the middle of winter, to measure it, to study the snow and to ask the old questions again. In two thousand and twenty, their report suggested that an avalanche was the likely cause. Scientists from other countries have studied the case, too, and have written papers, and given talks, and argued gently with one another about snow and wind and sound.

[pause]

Songs have been written about the group, too. Young people in Yekaterinburg still sing about the road north, about the tent on the white slope, and about the friends who did not come home. Some of the songs are written by hikers who have walked the same route and slept in the same forest, and they are sung quietly by a campfire, with a guitar, in the old tradition of the tourist clubs that the nine friends loved so much.

[pause]

Perhaps that is the most remarkable thing about it. The story of nine young friends in a tent on a cold mountain has been told for nearly seventy years, and it is still being told, in many languages, to people who were not born when it happened. It has been the subject of films, novels, songs, and documentaries. And every one of them has had to admit the same plain thing, that we do not know everything, and that some things we may never know.

{img:74-stars-over-kholat-syakhl-quiet}

Yet it would be a pity to remember the group only by the mystery. They were not a puzzle. They were nine people with names and faces and plans.

[pause]

Igor Dyatlov, the careful leader with the slide rule and the notebook of numbers. Zina Kolmogorova, who wrote her diary every evening and sang in a clear voice. Lyudmila Dubinina, the youngest, with her quick laughter. Alexander Kolevatov, the quiet physicist with a camera. Rustem Slobodin, the sturdy one, who was never tired. Nikolai Thibeaux-Brignolles, whose warm voice ended many a long day. Yuri Doroshenko and Yuri Krivonischenko, two of the three Yuris, one making people laugh, the other fixing anything that broke. And Alexander Zolotaryov, the veteran, who came along to share what he knew.

[pause]

They had spent the last days of their lives doing what they loved. They travelled through a beautiful country, in company, in good spirits, on skis. They wrote their diaries and sang their songs and made a joke newspaper. They helped each other in the cold, and they shared what warmth they had. We cannot say what happened in the dark, but we can say how they lived, and that is perhaps the part that matters.

{img:75-nine-skis-in-snow-sunrise}

It is a good thing to think about what the tourist movement of those years meant. In the nineteen fifties, thousands of young Soviet people spent their holidays on skis and on foot, in the mountains and forests of their enormous country. For many of them, it was the first taste of freedom, a chance to choose a route, to decide for themselves when to stop and when to go on, to rely on a friend and be relied upon. Songs were written about it, and poems, and a whole culture grew up around the campfire. The Dyatlov group belonged to that world. It was the world of people who believed that a group of friends, with a good map, strong legs and a warm stove, could go almost anywhere.

[pause]

And so we leave the mountain as it is now, on a winter night, far from any road. The pines stand in the dark at the edge of the forest. The wind moves lightly over the bare ridge. Under a huge sky full of stars, the long white slope lies silent and undisturbed, and a small stone and a few ribbons mark the place where, in the winter of nineteen fifty-nine, a group of friends once put up their tent.

[pause]

If you enjoyed this quiet story, you are welcome to subscribe. It helps other sleepy listeners find their way here.
