Tonight we are going nearly two thousand years back, to the city of Rome, in the year 80 after Christ.

[long-pause]

In a valley between the hills of Rome, where a few years ago there was a lake in an emperor's garden, a huge new building now stands. It is an enormous oval of pale stone, four storeys high, with eighty arches on each level and statues looking out from the arches. It is the greatest building the Romans have ever made. And this year, it is about to open.

[pause]

The Romans call it the Flavian Amphitheatre. We call it the Colosseum. Tonight we will spend a little time around it. With the builders, and the sailors who raise its great sunshade. With the gladiators in their training school, the animal keepers and the crowds, and the ordinary people of Rome.

[pause]

If you enjoy slow journeys into the past, you are welcome to subscribe to the channel, so that you can find the next one easily.

[long-pause]

Rome in the year 80 is the capital of an empire that stretches from Britain to Syria, all around the Mediterranean Sea. It is the largest city in the world, with perhaps a million people.

[pause]

It has changed a lot since the time of Julius Caesar. Rome is now ruled by emperors. It has become a city of marble and gold, with temples, baths, aqueducts, theatres and palaces.

[long-pause]

But the last few years have not been easy.

[pause]

Sixteen years ago, a great fire burned for nine days and destroyed much of the city. The emperor at the time, Nero, took the burned land in the middle of the city and built himself an enormous palace on it. It was called the Golden House. It had gardens, woods, vineyards, a great artificial lake, and a giant bronze statue of Nero himself, more than thirty metres tall. It was called the Colossus.

[pause]

{img:golden-house-gardens-lake-colossus-statue-nero-palace-sunset}
The Romans hated it. Nero had taken the heart of their city for himself.

[pause]

Then Nero died, and there was a terrible year of civil war, with four emperors one after another. The last of them won. He was a tough, practical, good-humoured old soldier called Vespasian. He became emperor and brought peace.

[long-pause]

Vespasian decided to give the land of Nero's palace back to the people of Rome. He drained Nero's lake. And on that very spot he began to build a great amphitheatre, a place of entertainment for the people, the largest in the world.

[pause]

He did not live to see it finished. He died last summer, at sixty-nine. It is said that as he felt death coming, he joked: oh dear, I think I am becoming a god. Because the Romans turned their dead emperors into gods.

[pause]

{img:vespasian-old-emperor-portrait-practical-face-humorous-calm}
His son Titus became emperor. And now, in the year 80, Titus must finish the amphitheatre and open it. He plans a great festival of games that will last a hundred days. But this year has already brought one disaster after another. Will Rome be ready to celebrate?

[long-pause]

{img:emperor-titus-portrait-young-kind-face-laurel-toga-palace}
The emperor Titus, in the year 80, is forty years old.

[pause]

He is the elder son of Vespasian. He grew up at the emperor's court and became a soldier and a general. He fought in Britain and Germany, and led the Roman army that captured Jerusalem ten years ago. He is clever, charming and handsome. He is a good speaker. He can write fast in shorthand, copy anyone's handwriting, sing and play the harp.

[long-pause]

Before he became emperor, many Romans were afraid of him. He had commanded the emperor's bodyguard, and people said he was harsh. And he was in love with a foreign queen. The Romans still remembered Cleopatra, and did not want another foreign queen in Rome.

[pause]

But when Titus became emperor, he surprised everyone. He sadly sent the queen away, because the Romans would not accept her. And he became one of the most loved emperors in Roman history.

[pause]

An old writer says Titus never sent anyone away without giving them some hope. Once, at dinner, he realised he had done nothing kind for anyone all day. And he said: my friends, I have lost a day.

[long-pause]

But his short reign is full of disasters.

[pause]

Only two months after he became emperor, the volcano Vesuvius, near Naples, erupted. It buried the towns of Pompeii and Herculaneum. Titus went there himself and sent help, money and officials for the survivors.

[pause]

{img:vesuvius-distant-bay-of-naples-quiet-smoke-plume-79ad-far}
Then this spring, while Titus was away helping the people near Naples, a great fire broke out in Rome. It burned for three days and destroyed the great temple of Jupiter and many other buildings. And after that came a terrible sickness.

[pause]

Titus gave his own money, and even the furniture of his own palaces, to help rebuild Rome. He did everything he could for the people.

[long-pause]

And now, in the summer of 80, he will open the great new amphitheatre with a hundred days of games. He wants to cheer up the people of Rome after all their troubles, and show them that their emperor cares about them.

[pause]

At the games, he will throw little wooden balls into the crowd. Inside each one is a ticket for a gift. Food or clothes, silver or gold, a horse, and sometimes even a house.

[pause]

{img:wooden-gift-balls-thrown-into-crowd-amphitheatre-hands-reaching}
Titus will reign for only two years. In September of next year, he will fall ill with a fever on a journey to his family's home in the hills, and die at forty-one. The people of Rome will mourn him as if they had lost a member of their own family. But tonight, nobody knows that yet.

[pause]

His brother Domitian will become emperor after him. He will finish the last parts of the amphitheatre, including the tunnels under the arena.

[long-pause]

{img:bay-of-naples-79ad-vesuvius-green-slopes-vineyards-villas-calm}
A year before the Colosseum opened, something happened in the south of Italy that shocked the whole Roman world.

[pause]

Around the beautiful Bay of Naples there were rich towns, country houses, vineyards and farms, on the green slopes of a mountain called Vesuvius. Nobody knew it was a volcano. It had been quiet for hundreds of years. Its slopes were covered with vineyards, woods and gardens, and its wine was famous.

[long-pause]

Seventeen years before, there had been earthquakes that damaged the towns nearby. People were still repairing their houses and temples. Then one day in the late summer or autumn of the year 79, the mountain woke up.

[pause]

We will watch only from very far away, as one young man did from across the bay. He was seventeen. Later he wrote two letters about what he saw. They still exist. They are the first eyewitness description of a volcano erupting in all of history.

[pause]

{img:distant-tall-cloud-umbrella-pine-shape-over-bay-misenum-view}
He wrote that at about one o'clock in the afternoon, his mother pointed out a cloud of strange size and shape rising from a mountain across the bay. It rose very high, he said, like an umbrella pine tree. A tall trunk, and then branches spreading out at the top. Sometimes it was white, sometimes dark and patchy.

[long-pause]

His uncle was a famous scholar and the admiral of the Roman fleet. At first he wanted to look more closely, as a scientist. Then a message came from a friend on the other side of the bay, asking for help. So he took the warships and sailed across to rescue people. He did not come back. He died on the shore, peacefully, his nephew says, as if he were asleep.

[pause]

The towns of Pompeii and Herculaneum were buried under metres of ash and stone. They were forgotten for more than sixteen hundred years, until they were found again about three hundred years ago.

[pause]

{img:pompeii-excavated-street-forum-columns-vesuvius-behind-quiet-later}
And because they were buried so quickly, they were preserved almost exactly as they were on that day. The streets and houses, the shops and paintings, the writing scratched on walls, bread in the ovens. Even a gladiator school, with the helmets and armour of the gladiators who trained there.

[pause]

Today, Pompeii is one of the most visited places in the world. It is the best picture we have of an ordinary Roman town in the time of the Colosseum.

[long-pause]

{img:colosseum-exterior-four-storeys-arches-columns-tuscan-ionic-corinthian}
The Colosseum is the largest amphitheatre ever built.

[pause]

An amphitheatre is a round or oval open-air theatre. Rows of seats rise up all around a flat space in the middle, where the shows take place. That space is called the arena. The word arena means sand, because the floor is covered with sand.

[long-pause]

The Colosseum is an oval about a hundred and ninety metres long and a hundred and fifty-six metres wide. It is nearly fifty metres high, as tall as a building of fifteen storeys today. It can hold perhaps fifty thousand people or more, sitting on stone seats in rows all around the arena.

[pause]

The outside wall has four storeys. The bottom three each have eighty arches, one above the other. Each arch is framed by half-columns in three different styles. Plain and sturdy at the bottom. Columns with curled tops in the middle. And elegant columns with carved leaves at the top. The highest storey has small square windows instead of arches, and holders for the great poles of the sunshade.

[pause]

{img:statues-in-arches-upper-storeys-colosseum-sunlit-travertine}
In each arch of the second and third storeys stands a statue of a god, a hero or an emperor. The whole outside is built of a creamy white stone from quarries about thirty kilometres away. In the sunshine it glows pale gold.

[long-pause]

There are eighty entrances at ground level, each with a number carved above it. You can still see those numbers today. Every visitor has a ticket, a little piece of pottery or bone. It shows the number of the entrance, the section, the row and the seat. So fifty thousand people can come in and find their places in about fifteen minutes. And they can leave just as quickly through the corridors and staircases.

[pause]

Four entrances are special. Two at the ends are for the gladiators and the processions. The other two are for the emperor and the most important guests.

[pause]

{img:numbered-entrance-arches-crowd-entering-tokens-morning-sun}
Inside, the seats are arranged by rank, in rings, one above another. In the front row, closest to the arena, on a wide platform, sit the senators in their white togas with purple stripes. Their names are carved on their marble seats. The priestesses of the goddess Vesta sit there too. And the emperor has his own special box in the middle of one long side.

[long-pause]

Behind them sit the knights, the rich men of Rome. Then the ordinary citizens, who must wear their white togas. Then, higher up, the poorer citizens. And at the very top, in a wooden gallery, sit the women, the slaves and the poorest people. A law made long ago says the women must sit up there, separately.

[pause]

The higher you sit, the further you are from the arena, and the less important you are. But from the top, you have a wonderful view over the whole amphitheatre, and over the roofs of Rome to the hills.

[long-pause]

{img:rome-80ad-morning-street-shops-opening-bakery-crowds-insula}
Let us walk through the streets of Rome on an ordinary morning in the year 80, a few weeks before the great games.

[pause]

The city wakes early, at first light. Shopkeepers take down the wooden shutters from their shops, which open straight onto the street under tall apartment buildings. The bakers have been working since before dawn. The smell of fresh bread fills the narrow streets.

[long-pause]

The streets are crowded and noisy and full of life. Porters carry loads on their backs. Rich people are carried through the crowds by slaves, in covered chairs with curtains. Children run to school. Barbers shave men on stools right in the street. Cooks fry sausages and sell hot food from counters. Sellers call out: hot bread, cakes, old clothes!

[pause]

Everyone is talking about the new amphitheatre and the games. When will they begin? Who will fight? How many animals will there be? Will the emperor give away gifts?

[pause]

But Rome is also still recovering from the great fire this spring, and from the sickness that followed. There are building sites everywhere. Scaffolding and cranes, carts loaded with stone and timber, and the sound of hammering and sawing and shouting. Will everything really be ready in time?

[long-pause]

Later, the next emperor will rebuild the burned temple of Jupiter, more splendid than ever, with a roof of golden bronze tiles that shine across the whole city.

[pause]

And all the time, the new amphitheatre rises above the roofs in its valley, enormous and pale and new. Sailors climb the masts at the top, practising with the great sunshade. Workmen add the last touches to the seats and corridors.

[pause]

A poet wrote that where Nero's hated palace had stood, there now stood the people's delight. Rome had been given back to itself.

[long-pause]

{img:roman-tavern-counter-wine-jars-morning-customers-bread-olives}
In the taverns near the new amphitheatre, the innkeepers are getting ready for the crowds. They buy extra wine, bread and sausages, and hire extra servants. They paint pictures of gladiators and animals on their walls to attract customers. It is going to be a good summer for business.

[long-pause]

{img:colosseum-under-construction-cranes-treadwheels-scaffold-workers-72ad}
How was the Colosseum built?

[pause]

Work began around the year 72 under Vespasian. It opens in the year 80 under Titus. That is only about eight years, for one of the largest buildings in the world. It is an enormous feat of engineering and organisation.

[long-pause]

First the builders drained Nero's lake. Then they dug down and laid huge foundations. A great ring of concrete, twelve metres deep, runs under the whole building, to carry its enormous weight on the soft, wet ground of the old lake.

[pause]

Then they built the walls, arches and vaults. They used the creamy white stone for the main pillars and outer walls. They used a lighter volcanic stone and bricks for the inner walls. And they used concrete, the great Roman invention. It is made of lime, volcanic ash, rubble and water, and it sets as hard as rock.

[pause]

They needed an enormous amount of stone. It was brought from the quarries in ox carts along a special road, perhaps more than two hundred cartloads a day, for years. The blocks were not held with mortar. They were held together with iron clamps, perhaps three hundred tons of iron. In the Middle Ages, people dug out the clamps to reuse the iron. They left holes that can still be seen all over the walls today.

[long-pause]

Thousands of people worked on it. Architects and engineers, stone cutters and masons, carpenters and sculptors, labourers and ox-cart drivers. Many were skilled free craftsmen, well paid. Many others were slaves and prisoners. Many historians believe that thousands of them were Jewish prisoners, brought to Rome after the fall of Jerusalem. An old inscription suggests the building was paid for with treasure taken from the temple in Jerusalem.

[pause]

The cranes were worked by men walking inside huge wooden wheels. As the wheel turned, it wound up a rope and lifted heavy stone blocks to the top of the walls.

[pause]

{img:architect-plans-wax-tablet-measuring-arches-site-sunlight}
We do not know the name of the architect. But he designed a building so strong and so practical that it has stood for nearly two thousand years, through earthquakes, fires, wars and centuries of people taking its stones. And it is still mostly standing today.

[long-pause]

Next to the amphitheatre, Nero's giant bronze statue still stood. But it had been given a new head. Not Nero's, but the sun god's, with rays around it like a crown.

[pause]

The Romans began to call that spot the place of the Colossus. And later, in the Middle Ages, the amphitheatre took its name from the statue. It became the Colosseum.

[pause]

{img:bronze-colossus-sun-god-rays-crown-beside-amphitheatre-blue-sky}
There is an old saying, written down by a monk in England more than a thousand years ago. As long as the Colosseum stands, Rome shall stand. When the Colosseum falls, Rome shall fall. And when Rome falls, the world shall end.

[long-pause]

{img:roman-concrete-vault-workers-wooden-formwork-pouring-mortar}
How did the Romans build such enormous buildings nearly two thousand years ago that are still standing today?

[pause]

The secret is the arch, the vault, and concrete.

[long-pause]

The Romans did not invent the arch. But they used it more boldly and cleverly than anyone before them. An arch is a curve of wedge-shaped stones that push against each other. The weight of the wall above is carried down along the curve to the ground on both sides. An arch can hold up an enormous weight and span a wide opening, much wider than a flat stone beam could.

[pause]

A vault is like an arch stretched out into a tunnel. And a dome is like an arch turned around in a circle. With arches, vaults and domes, the Romans could build bridges, aqueducts, great halls and amphitheatres larger than anything built before.

[pause]

{img:aqueduct-arches-countryside-rome-water-channel-top-sunset}
And they had concrete. Roman concrete is made of lime, a special volcanic ash from around Naples and Rome, water, and pieces of stone or broken brick. It is poured into wooden moulds and sets as hard as rock. It can even set under water, so the Romans used it for harbours and bridges.

[long-pause]

Scientists have recently found out something remarkable about Roman concrete. It can heal itself. If a crack forms and water gets in, little lumps of lime inside the concrete react with the water and fill the crack with new crystals. So the concrete gets stronger over time instead of weaker. That is one reason Roman buildings have lasted so long, while some modern concrete crumbles in a few decades.

[pause]

In the Colosseum, the vaults that hold up the seats are made of concrete. The outer walls and main pillars are of strong white stone. The inner walls are of brick and lighter stone. Each material is used where it works best.

[pause]

{img:pantheon-dome-oculus-light-beam-interior-coffers-quiet}
Forty years after the Colosseum, another emperor will build the Pantheon in Rome, a temple to all the gods. It has a great concrete dome more than forty-three metres wide, with a round opening at the top. Sunlight shines through the opening and moves slowly around the inside of the dome during the day, like a sundial. Nearly two thousand years later, it is still standing, and still used as a church.

[long-pause]

{img:velarium-awning-colosseum-sailors-ropes-masts-shade-crowd}
On hot summer days, the Colosseum has a roof. It is a great cloth sunshade that can be pulled across the top, to shade the crowd from the burning sun.

[pause]

It is one of the most remarkable things about the building.

[long-pause]

Around the top of the outer wall stand two hundred and forty wooden masts, held in stone brackets that can still be seen today. From the masts, ropes stretch toward the middle, like the spokes of a wheel, or the rigging of a ship. Along the ropes, great strips of canvas are pulled out and unrolled. They cover most of the seats, but leave an opening over the arena. So the sun shines on the sand, and the crowd sits in the shade.

[pause]

Nobody is quite sure exactly how it worked. Engineers today still argue about it.

[pause]

{img:sailors-misenum-fleet-hauling-awning-ropes-top-of-wall-wind}
But we know who worked it. Sailors. More than a thousand sailors from the Roman navy are stationed in Rome, in barracks near the Colosseum, just to work the sunshade. Sailors know about sails, ropes, wind and climbing. They haul on the ropes, climb the masts and adjust the canvas as the sun moves and the wind changes, just like sailors on a great ship. If they get it wrong in a strong wind, the whole thing could tear apart.

[long-pause]

On windy days, the sunshade cannot be used. Then people bring their own wide-brimmed hats and little parasols. The painted adverts for the games in Pompeii sometimes promise: there will be awnings. That made people more likely to come.

[pause]

The sunshade is often coloured. A Roman poet describes sunlight shining through red, yellow and purple awnings in a theatre. It colours the whole crowd, the stage and the senators, so everything glows.

[pause]

{img:coloured-light-through-awning-crowd-glowing-red-yellow-amphitheatre}
And on the hottest days, there are fountains and sprays of scented water, smelling of saffron, sprinkled over the crowd to cool them down.

[long-pause]

Imagine sitting high up in the Colosseum on a summer afternoon, in the cool, coloured shade of the great sunshade. Fifty thousand people sit around you. The smell of saffron hangs in the air. High above, the sailors call to each other as they pull on the ropes. And far below, bright sunlight shines on the sand of the arena.

[long-pause]

{img:aqueduct-claudia-arches-campagna-countryside-sunset-sheep}
In the year 80, Rome has more fresh water than any city in the world will have until modern times.

[pause]

The water comes from the hills, many kilometres away, through the great aqueducts. These are channels of stone and concrete that carry water gently downhill all the way to the city. For most of the way they run underground. But across valleys they run on top of great rows of arches that march across the countryside for kilometres.

[long-pause]

In the year 80 there are nine aqueducts. Together they bring about a thousand litres of water a day for every person in Rome. That is far more than most modern cities. The newest ones have arches nearly thirty metres high, which still stand in the countryside outside Rome.

[pause]

The water flows into great tanks, and from there through lead pipes to the public fountains on almost every street corner. People fill their jars and buckets there all day long. Water also goes to the public baths, the gardens, and the houses of the rich, who pay for their own supply.

[pause]

The water never stops flowing. The fountains run day and night. The overflow washes the streets and runs into the great sewers, which carry it, with the city's waste, to the river Tiber.

[long-pause]

A few years after the Colosseum, the official in charge of Rome's water wrote a book about the aqueducts. It still exists. He was very proud of them. Compare these useful works, he wrote, with the idle pyramids, or the useless, though famous, works of the Greeks.

[pause]

The Colosseum needed water too. For the fountains and the toilets, and for the scented sprays. It got its water from a branch of one of the aqueducts, which came down from a nearby hill.

[pause]

{img:roman-latrine-row-stone-seats-running-water-channel-sponges}
And under the Colosseum there is a great system of drains that carry away rainwater and waste to the great sewer, and on to the Tiber. Archaeologists exploring these drains have found food, coins and little things that people dropped during the games, two thousand years ago.

[long-pause]

{img:gladiators-training-yard-ludus-wooden-swords-morning-light}
Who were the gladiators?

[pause]

The name comes from gladius, the short sword of the Roman soldier. Gladiators were trained fighters. They fought each other in pairs in the arena, to entertain the people of Rome and of cities all over the Roman Empire.

[long-pause]

The games began long ago as part of funerals. About two hundred and fifty years before the Colosseum, at the funeral of a Roman nobleman, his sons had three pairs of slaves fight in his honour. Over the centuries, the games grew larger and grander. They became the most popular entertainment in the Roman world. Rich men, politicians and emperors gave games to win the favour of the people.

[pause]

Most gladiators were slaves, prisoners of war or condemned criminals. They had no choice. They were bought, owned and trained by a manager, who hired them out to the men giving the games.

[pause]

But some gladiators were free men who chose this life, for the money, the fame and the excitement. They signed a contract and swore a terrible oath. For a while, they gave up their rights as free citizens.

[long-pause]

In a strange way, gladiators were both looked down on and admired.

[pause]

The Romans thought of them as people of the lowest kind. And yet they were also heroes and celebrities, like sports stars today. Their names were written on walls. Their pictures were painted on lamps, pots and glass cups. Children played at being gladiators.

[pause]

{img:clay-oil-lamp-gladiator-picture-glass-cup-souvenir-names}
And here is something that surprises many people. Most gladiator fights did not end in death. Gladiators were expensive to buy, train and feed. Their owners did not want them killed. So the fights had rules and referees. Usually a fight ended when one fighter was wounded or exhausted, or gave up by raising one finger to ask for mercy. Very often mercy was given, and the loser was sent away to fight another day.

[long-pause]

Historians who have studied gravestones and old writings think that in the time of the Colosseum, perhaps only one fight in five or ten ended in death. A gladiator might fight two or three times a year. If he was skilful and lucky, he could hope to survive for years. And perhaps, one day, he would win his freedom.

[pause]

When a gladiator won his freedom, he was given a wooden sword as a sign that he was free. Some freed gladiators became trainers or referees. Some even went back to the arena, because they missed the excitement and the fame.

[pause]

{img:wooden-sword-rudis-presented-freedom-arena-crowd-cheering}
We will not watch the fights tonight. Instead, we will spend time with the gladiators in their school and their daily lives. How they lived and trained, ate and slept. And how they hoped, every single day, for that wooden sword.

[long-pause]

{img:roman-wall-graffiti-scratched-gladiator-drawings-names-plaster}
The Romans loved to write on walls.

[pause]

All over the Roman world, on houses, shops, taverns and public buildings, even on the Colosseum itself, people scratched messages, drawings, jokes and names. They used nails or knives, or wrote with charcoal or paint. These scratches are called graffiti, from an Italian word.

[long-pause]

In Pompeii, where the volcano preserved the walls, more than eleven thousand pieces of graffiti and painted signs have been found. Many are about gladiators.

[pause]

There are drawings of gladiators fighting, with their names and how many fights they had won. There are painted adverts for the games. Something like: the gladiators of so-and-so will fight here on the sixteenth of May. There will be a hunt, and there will be awnings.

[pause]

{img:painted-advertisement-red-letters-wall-pompeii-games-notice}
And there are messages that have nothing to do with gladiators. They tell us about the everyday lives of ordinary Romans. Love notes and insults, jokes, shopping lists and complaints.

[pause]

One says: a weaver loves the innkeeper's slave girl, but she does not care for him. Another says: I am amazed, wall, that you have not fallen down under the weight of so many boring writings. Another, at an inn, is a sheepish apology to the innkeeper. And one is a child's alphabet, scratched low on the wall, at a child's height: A, B, C, D.

[long-pause]

In the Colosseum itself there are graffiti too, scratched into the stone seats and corridor walls. Drawings of gladiators, hunters, animals and boats. Names of visitors. And little game boards scratched on the steps, where people played with nuts or dice while they waited for the shows to begin.

[pause]

The people who scratched them were bored, or excited, or in love, or just wanted to leave a mark. Thanks to them, we can still faintly hear the voices of ordinary Romans across two thousand years. Laughing and grumbling, boasting and loving, just as people do today.

[long-pause]

{img:roman-child-alphabet-scratched-low-wall-plaster-letters-light}
Sometimes the graffiti are just one word, or a name and a date, scratched by someone two thousand years ago who simply wanted to say: I was here. Just like visitors to the Colosseum today, who are forbidden to scratch their names on its stones, but sometimes still try.

[long-pause]

{img:ludus-magnus-small-oval-training-arena-cells-around-colosseum-behind}
Right next to the Colosseum, to the east, stands the great gladiator school. It was rebuilt a little later by the next emperor, but there was already a school on or near that spot in the time of Titus.

[pause]

It is a large rectangular building with a courtyard in the middle. The courtyard has its own little oval arena, a small copy of the Colosseum, with a few rows of seats. There the gladiators train, and people can come and watch them practise.

[long-pause]

Around the courtyard, two or three floors of small rooms hold the gladiators' cells, where they live and sleep, one or two to a room. There are kitchens and storerooms, a locked armoury for the weapons, a hospital and baths.

[pause]

And there is a tunnel underground that links the school to the Colosseum. On the day of the games, the gladiators walk through it to the arena, out of sight of the crowd. Every gladiator knows that tunnel. And every gladiator wonders how many more times he will walk through it.

[pause]

You can still see the ruins of this school today, next to the Colosseum, with the curved seats of its little arena below the level of the modern street.

[long-pause]

Life in the gladiator school is strict and disciplined, like the army.

[pause]

The gladiators are guarded, because many are slaves or prisoners who might try to escape or rebel. Everyone in Rome remembers Spartacus, the famous gladiator who escaped from a school with seventy companions, a hundred and fifty years ago, and fought against Rome for two years.

[pause]

But the gladiators are also well cared for, because they are valuable. They get good food, doctors, massages, baths and the best trainers.

[pause]

{img:gladiators-eating-together-long-table-barley-porridge-beans}
They live, train and eat together, and become a kind of family. They have their own customs and favourite gods, like the strong hero Hercules and the goddess of fate. They form clubs and pay into a shared fund. If one of them dies, his family receives money for a proper funeral and a gravestone.

[long-pause]

Some gladiators have wives and children who live outside the school. Gladiator gravestones have been found all over the Roman Empire. Often they were put up by a wife or by fellow gladiators, with loving words.

[pause]

One says: for Urbicus, a gladiator from Florence, who fought thirteen times and lived twenty-two years. His daughter, whom he left at five months old, and his wife, with whom he lived seven years, made this for her well-deserving husband.

[pause]

{img:gravestone-gladiator-relief-carved-name-family-inscription-moss}
And another, put up by a gladiator's friends, says simply: for Pinnas, from his comrades.

[long-pause]

{img:capua-gladiator-school-1st-century-bc-courtyard-men-training}
The most famous gladiator in history lived a hundred and fifty years before the Colosseum. His name was Spartacus.

[pause]

He came from the mountains of what is now Bulgaria. The Roman writers say he served in the Roman army, then ran away, was captured and sold as a slave. He was sent to a gladiator school near Naples.

[long-pause]

One day, Spartacus and about seventy fellow gladiators escaped from the school, armed with knives and spits from the kitchen. On the road they found wagons carrying gladiators' weapons, and took them. Then they climbed up the slopes of Mount Vesuvius, which was then a quiet, green, wooded mountain, and camped there.

[pause]

Thousands of slaves and poor farm workers ran away from the great estates of southern Italy and joined them. Spartacus became the leader of a great army, perhaps seventy thousand people or more.

[pause]

{img:vesuvius-wooded-slopes-camp-escaped-slaves-vines-ropes-legend}
The Roman army came and blocked the only path down the mountain. They thought Spartacus was trapped. But his followers made ropes and ladders from the wild vines growing on the mountain. At night they climbed down the cliffs on the other side, and surprised the Romans from behind.

[long-pause]

For two years, Spartacus and his army marched up and down Italy, beating one Roman army after another. Even the Roman writers, who hated him, admired him. One says he was not only brave and strong, but also clever and gentle.

[pause]

It seems he wanted to lead his followers out of Italy, over the Alps, so they could go home to their own countries. But many of them wanted to stay and keep fighting.

[pause]

{img:mountain-pass-alps-distant-army-turning-back-snow-peaks}
In the end, Spartacus was defeated in the south of Italy, and fell in battle. His body was never found. We will not describe what happened to his followers.

[long-pause]

But his name was never forgotten. Two thousand years later, Spartacus became a hero and a symbol of the struggle for freedom. There are books about him, a famous ballet, and a famous film. At the end of the film, when the Romans ask the captured slaves which one of them is Spartacus, they all stand up, one after another, and say: I am Spartacus.

[long-pause]

{img:dawn-gladiator-school-courtyard-trainers-wooden-post-practice}
Let us spend an ordinary day in the gladiator school beside the Colosseum, in the spring of the year 80, a few weeks before the great games.

[pause]

The day begins at dawn with a horn or a bell. The gladiators get up from their straw mattresses in their little cells. They wash at the fountain in the courtyard and eat breakfast.

[long-pause]

What do gladiators eat? A few years ago, scientists studied the bones of gladiators buried in a gladiators' cemetery in Turkey. They found that the gladiators ate mostly barley and beans, and very little meat. Roman writers even called them the barley men. A diet like this may have given them a layer of fat that protected them from cuts in the arena.

[pause]

The scientists found something else too. The bones suggest the gladiators drank a special drink made of plant ashes mixed with water or vinegar, to make their bones stronger and help them heal. A Roman writer mentions it. It was a kind of ancient sports drink.

[pause]

After breakfast, the training begins.

[long-pause]

The trainers are often former gladiators who have won their freedom. Each kind of gladiator has its own trainer and its own way of fighting.

[pause]

Beginners start with wooden swords and wicker shields, which are heavier than the real ones. They strike a wooden post again and again for hours, to build up strength and learn the movements. Then they practise in pairs with wooden weapons, slowly at first, then faster.

[pause]

{img:pairs-practising-wooden-weapons-sand-trainer-watching-shade}
They practise footwork and blocking, striking and falling and getting up again. They run, lift weights and wrestle. The training is hard, long and repetitive, like the training of athletes or soldiers or dancers today. And every man knows that the games are only weeks away now.

[long-pause]

At midday they rest, eat again and bathe. They are massaged with oil, and the doctors treat their injuries.

[pause]

The doctors of the gladiator schools are among the best in the Roman world, because they see so many injuries. A hundred years later, the most famous doctor of the ancient world will start his career as a doctor for gladiators. He will say he learned more about the human body there than anywhere else.

[pause]

{img:afternoon-gladiators-resting-shade-colonnade-dice-talking}
In the afternoon there is more training. Then in the evening comes supper and rest. The men talk and play dice, and some write letters home. Then they sleep in their little cells, with the sounds of the city outside, and the great dark shape of the Colosseum rising against the stars next door.

[long-pause]

{img:mosaic-zliten-arena-musicians-water-organ-horns-players}
The games in the Colosseum are never silent. There is always music.

[pause]

A band plays throughout the shows. There are long, straight trumpets. There are great curved horns shaped like a big letter G, which wrap around the player's body and make a deep, loud, booming sound. And there is the water organ.

[long-pause]

The water organ is a wonderful instrument, invented in Egypt three hundred years before. It has rows of bronze pipes of different lengths, and a keyboard. Pumps push air through the pipes, and water in a tank underneath keeps the pressure steady. It makes a loud, sweet, powerful sound that fills the whole amphitheatre. It is the ancestor of the church organ.

[pause]

An old floor picture made of tiny stones, found in North Africa, shows the music at the games. A woman plays the water organ. Two men play the great curved horns. Another plays a trumpet, beside the arena.

[pause]

The music follows the action, like music in a film today. It is slow and calm at the beginning, when the fighters circle and watch each other. It gets faster and louder as things get exciting. And there is a great fanfare at the end, when the winner is announced.

[long-pause]

The trumpets also announce the start of the games, the arrival of the emperor, the entrance of the gladiators in their grand parade, and each new part of the show.

[pause]

When the emperor enters his box, the whole crowd stands up, cheers and claps in rhythm. They chant his name and wish him good luck and long life, in great choruses, like football crowds today.

[pause]

{img:crowd-standing-cheering-emperor-arriving-box-trumpets-sunlight}
Imagine the sound of it. Fifty thousand people cheering and chanting, with the trumpets, the horns and the great water organ, all echoing around the huge stone bowl of the amphitheatre. The sound rolls out over the roofs of Rome, so people far away in the streets can hear it, and know the games have begun.

[long-pause]

{img:roman-surgeon-instruments-bronze-case-bandages-calm-clinic}
The doctors who looked after the gladiators were among the most skilful in the Roman world.

[pause]

Roman doctors, many of them Greeks, had a wonderful set of tools made of bronze and iron. Knives and probes, tweezers and hooks, needles and little spoons for medicine. Many look very much like a modern surgeon's tools. Hundreds of them were found in the house of a surgeon in Pompeii, and they are now in a museum in Naples.

[long-pause]

The doctors cleaned wounds with wine and vinegar, which kill germs, even though they did not know about germs. They stitched wounds with thread and wrapped them in clean linen. They set broken bones with splints. They used plants as medicine. Poppy juice for pain. Willow bark, which contains the same medicine as aspirin. And honey, which helps wounds heal.

[pause]

When scientists studied the bones of gladiators buried in Turkey, they found many old injuries that had healed well. Broken bones had been set properly and mended. This shows the gladiators were cared for carefully by good doctors, and given time to recover.

[pause]

{img:healed-bone-study-scientist-laboratory-calm-careful-light-later}
The most famous doctor of the Roman world started his career looking after gladiators, a hundred years later. He called their wounds windows into the body, through which he learned how the body works. He paid great attention to their food, exercise and rest. And he was proud that in his four years with them, very few of his gladiators died of their wounds, far fewer than before.

[long-pause]

{img:gladiator-types-murmillo-retiarius-thraex-armour-display-rack}
There are many kinds of gladiators, each with their own armour, weapons and way of fighting. Each kind usually fights one particular other kind, so the fight is balanced and exciting to watch.

[long-pause]

One kind wears a great bronze helmet with a wide brim and a tall crest shaped like a fish. He carries a large curved shield, like a Roman soldier's, and a short sword. He has a padded sleeve on his sword arm and a guard on one leg. He is heavy, strong and slow.

[pause]

Another kind is called the Thracian. His helmet has a high crest topped with a griffin's head. He carries a small square shield and a short curved sword. He has tall guards on both legs, because his shield is small. He is quick and nimble.

[pause]

{img:retiarius-net-trident-light-armour-shoulder-guard-sand}
The strangest of all is the net man. He has almost no armour. No helmet and no shield, just a metal guard on his left shoulder. He fights with a net, which he tries to throw over his opponent, and a trident, a long fishing spear with three points. He is the fastest gladiator of all. And because he wears no helmet, the crowd can see his face and recognise him.

[long-pause]

The net man usually fights the chaser. The chaser wears a smooth round helmet with tiny eyeholes, so the trident cannot catch on it, and carries a large shield and a sword. Their fight is like a fisherman trying to catch a fish. The net man dances around, throwing his net, while the chaser tries to get close.

[pause]

There are other kinds too. Fighters with a small round shield and a spear, like Greek soldiers. Fighters on horseback. Even fighters on chariots. And many more.

[pause]

{img:gladiator-helmets-bronze-crests-griffin-fish-museum-light}
The armour and helmets are often beautiful, decorated with gold, engravings and coloured plumes. Some helmets found in the gladiator barracks at Pompeii, buried by the volcano, are among the most beautiful armour that survives from ancient times.

[long-pause]

The crowd has its favourites. Some people cheer for gladiators with big shields. Others cheer for gladiators with small shields. Like football fans today, they argue about which is better. The emperor Titus himself is a fan of the small shields, and jokes with the crowd about it.

[pause]

And gladiators have stage names, like wrestlers or actors today. The Flame. The Dove. The Little Arrow. Triumph. Names the crowd shouts and writes on the walls.

[long-pause]

{img:circus-maximus-chariot-race-four-horse-teams-colours-crowd-palatine}
The Colosseum was not the only great place of entertainment in Rome. For many Romans, the favourite of all was chariot racing in the Circus Maximus.

[pause]

The Circus Maximus is even larger than the Colosseum. It is a great long racetrack, about six hundred metres long, in the valley between two hills. Seats rise up on both sides, for perhaps a hundred and fifty thousand people, or even more.

[long-pause]

There are four teams, each with its own colour. The Reds, the Whites, the Blues and the Greens. Every Roman supports one of them passionately, from childhood, like a football club today. Even the emperors have their favourite team.

[pause]

The drivers race light chariots pulled by four horses, seven times around the long, narrow track. In the middle runs a barrier decorated with statues, an obelisk and fountains. On it, big wooden eggs and bronze dolphins are turned to count the laps.

[pause]

The drivers wrap the reins around their waists, so they can steer with their whole body. They carry a knife in their belt, to cut themselves free if the chariot crashes. The races are fast and thrilling and dangerous. On every turn, the crowd holds its breath.

[long-pause]

The best drivers are the biggest celebrities in Rome, far more famous and richer than the gladiators. In the time of the Colosseum, the most famous is Scorpus of the Greens, who won more than two thousand races. When he died in a race at only twenty-six, a poet wrote: I am Scorpus, the glory of the noisy circus, the applause of Rome, and its short-lived darling.

[pause]

A little later, a driver from Spain raced for twenty-four years and won almost fifteen hundred races. He retired at forty-two with a huge fortune in prize money. He may have been the richest sportsman in history.

[pause]

{img:horses-winning-team-crowned-palm-wreaths-stables-evening}
The horses are famous too. They have names and their own fans. Some even have statues. The people know their names and records as well as the drivers'.

[long-pause]

{img:animal-cages-port-ostia-ships-lions-elephants-keepers-unloading}
The games in the Colosseum are not only about gladiators. There are also animals, thousands of them, from every part of the Roman Empire and beyond.

[pause]

The Romans bring wild animals to Rome from all over the world. Lions and leopards from North Africa. Tigers from India and Persia. Bears from Germany. Elephants from Africa. Crocodiles and hippos from Egypt. Giraffes, ostriches and zebras. Wild boar, deer, bulls and bison.

[long-pause]

Catching and moving these animals is a huge business. Hunters and soldiers catch them alive with nets, pits and traps. They put them in wooden cages and carry them by ox cart and by ship across the Mediterranean to the port of Rome, and then up the river to the city.

[pause]

At the port, there are floor pictures in the offices of the shipping companies showing elephants and other animals being loaded onto ships. In a great country house in Sicily, there is a long floor picture made three hundred years later. It shows lions, tigers and antelopes being caught, and an elephant walking up a plank onto a ship.

[pause]

{img:mosaic-great-hunt-elephant-gangplank-ship-tigers-cages}
In Rome, the animals are kept in enclosures outside the city walls. Keepers and trainers look after them until the day of the games.

[long-pause]

Some animals are trained to do tricks. The Romans love to see elephants walk on tightropes, or write in the sand with their trunks, or kneel before the emperor. There are lions trained to catch hares and carry them gently in their mouths without hurting them. There are dancing bears, and bulls that let boys stand on their backs.

[pause]

A poet who was at the opening games of the Colosseum wrote a little book of poems about them. He says an elephant knelt down by itself before the emperor Titus, as if it understood that he was a god.

[pause]

{img:trained-elephant-kneeling-arena-sand-crowd-emperor-box-sunlight}
In the mornings there are also hunts, where trained hunters face wild animals with spears and bows in the arena. Sometimes the arena is decorated like a forest or a mountain, with real trees, rocks and bushes that rise up through trapdoors in the floor.

[long-pause]

We will not watch the hunts. But it is sad to think of the thousands of animals taken from their homes all over the world and brought to Rome. In the centuries that followed, so many were caught for the games that some animals disappeared from parts of the Roman Empire. The elephants of North Africa. The lions of Greece. The hippos of the lower Nile.

[long-pause]

{img:animal-keeper-feeding-lion-enclosure-vivarium-outside-rome-calm}
For every animal brought to Rome for the games, there are people who look after it. These keepers and trainers live with the animals in the enclosures outside the city. They feed them and care for them, sometimes for months, before the games.

[long-pause]

The keepers are skilled people who know their animals well. They know how to feed them, calm them, move them and treat them when they are ill. Many come from the same lands as the animals. Africans who know lions and elephants. Indians who know tigers. Egyptians who know crocodiles and hippos.

[pause]

Some keepers grow very fond of their animals. There are old stories of lions who loved their keepers, and elephants who recognised their old trainers years later in the crowd.

[pause]

The most famous story was told by a Roman writer who said he heard it from someone who was there. A runaway slave called Androcles was hiding in a cave in Africa. A lion came in, limping, with a big thorn in its paw. Androcles was terrified. But he carefully pulled out the thorn. And the lion became his friend. For three years it lived with him and brought him food.

[long-pause]

Later, Androcles was captured and sent to Rome to face the beasts in the arena. And the lion had been captured too, and sent to Rome. When the lion was let into the arena, it ran toward Androcles. The crowd held its breath. And then the lion stopped. It recognised him. It lay down at his feet, wagging its tail like a dog, and licked his hands.

[pause]

The crowd was amazed. The emperor asked Androcles to explain. When he told the story, the crowd shouted for him to be set free, and to be given the lion. Afterwards, the writer says, Androcles walked through the streets of Rome with the lion on a light leash. People gave him money and said: here is the lion who was a man's friend, and the man who was a lion's doctor.

[pause]

{img:man-walking-lion-on-leash-roman-street-crowd-wonder-calm}
It is probably only a story. But it shows that the Romans, too, could be moved by kindness and friendship between people and animals.

[long-pause]

{img:opening-games-80ad-procession-arena-banners-emperor-box-crowd}
In the summer of the year 80, the emperor Titus opens the great amphitheatre with games that last a hundred days.

[pause]

People come to Rome from all over the empire and beyond to see them. The poet who wrote about the games says no people was so far away that it did not send someone. Farmers from the mountains of the Balkans. Riders from the far east. People from the source of the Nile and the shores of the Atlantic. Arabs and Ethiopians. All speaking different languages, he says. And yet all speaking with one voice when they cheered Titus as the father of his country.

[long-pause]

These are the greatest games Rome has ever seen. An old historian says nine thousand animals, tame and wild, were shown during the hundred days. There were gladiator fights, hunts and shows of every kind. And it is said there was even a sea battle.

[pause]

Because at the very beginning, before the tunnels under the floor were built, the arena could be flooded with water and drained again. The Romans put on a show of an old sea battle, with small real ships rowed by men, and horses and bulls trained to swim.

[pause]

{img:flooded-arena-small-ships-water-show-crowd-amazed-distant}
Scholars still argue whether this really happened in the Colosseum or somewhere nearby. But several old writers describe water shows at the opening games. Imagine the crowd's amazement, seeing ships sail where moments before there had been sand.

[long-pause]

On the last day of the games, an old writer says, Titus wept openly in front of the people. Perhaps he was tired. Perhaps he already knew he was not well. A year later, he was dead.

[pause]

The poet wrote that the Colosseum was the greatest wonder of the world. Greater than the pyramids of Egypt and the walls of Babylon. Let Egypt stop boasting about its pyramids, he wrote. Every work gives way to Caesar's amphitheatre. Fame will speak of this one work instead of all the others.

[pause]

{img:crowd-leaving-colosseum-evening-arches-lamps-streets-filling}
And in the evenings, during those hundred days, the streets around the Colosseum were full of people going home after the games. They talked and laughed and argued about the gladiators and the animals. They stopped at the taverns and food stalls, in the warm summer night.

[long-pause]

{img:poet-martial-small-room-rome-writing-tablets-third-floor-window}
One of the people who watched the opening games of the Colosseum and wrote about them was a poet called Martial.

[pause]

He was born in Spain, in a little town in the mountains. As a young man he came to Rome to make his fortune as a poet. He lived there for thirty-five years.

[long-pause]

He was not rich. For many years he lived in a little flat on the third floor, up a long flight of stairs. He often complained about it in his poems. About the noise, the stairs, the cold and the rent. And about the bakers, the schoolteachers and the money changers who kept him awake from dawn with their racket.

[pause]

He wrote very short, witty, clever poems of a few lines each. They are about everything. His friends and his enemies, the people of Rome and their habits, their dinner parties and their slaves, the gladiators and the games. More than fifteen hundred of them survive.

[pause]

His first book was a little collection of about thirty poems about the opening games of the Colosseum, written for the emperor Titus. It is one of the main ways we know about those games.

[long-pause]

Martial is funny and sharp, sometimes rude, and sometimes very tender. He wrote a beautiful little poem for a little slave girl who died at almost six. He asked the earth to lie lightly on her, because she had been so light on it.

[pause]

He wrote about the simple life he longed for. A little house in the country, a good fire, sleep that makes the night short, and a mind at peace.

[pause]

{img:spanish-hill-town-countryside-vines-old-poet-garden-peace}
In the end he got it. After thirty-five years in Rome, tired and older, he went home to his little town in Spain. A kind lady gave him a house, a garden and a farm. There, he wrote, he slept as long as he liked. He no longer had to wear a toga. And he no longer heard the noise of Rome.

[long-pause]

{img:spectators-arriving-dawn-colosseum-cushions-food-baskets-tickets}
Let us spend a day at the games in the Colosseum in the summer of the year 80, as an ordinary Roman.

[pause]

He is a baker from a crowded neighbourhood behind the Forum. He has a ticket, a little piece of bone. It was free, because the emperor pays for the games. He gets up before dawn, puts on his white toga, which he must wear at the games, and walks through the grey streets to the Colosseum with his friends and his young son.

[long-pause]

The streets around the amphitheatre are already full. Thousands of people stream toward it from every direction. Sellers offer food and drink, and cushions, because the stone seats are hard. There are souvenirs, like little clay lamps and gladiator figures. And there are little programmes listing which gladiators will fight that day.

[pause]

The baker finds his entrance by the number carved above the arch. He climbs the stairs, finds his section, his row and his seat, high up among the ordinary citizens.

[pause]

From there he can see the whole amphitheatre. The great oval of seats filling with people in white togas. The arena far below, with its yellow sand gleaming in the morning sun. And the emperor's box, hung with purple cloth.

[long-pause]

The day has a programme, like a show today.

[pause]

In the morning there are the animals, first the trained animals with their tricks, then the hunts. At midday, many people go out to eat and rest. There are lesser shows then, comedians and acrobats and jugglers, and grim things we will not watch. The baker takes his son out to buy bread, sausages and wine at a stall outside.

[pause]

And in the afternoon comes the main event. The gladiators.

[pause]

{img:pompa-procession-gladiators-entering-arena-musicians-trumpets}
It begins with a grand parade. The gladiators march into the arena in purple and gold cloaks, with trumpets and horns and the water organ playing. They walk around the arena and salute the emperor, and the crowd cheers.

[long-pause]

Then the referees check the weapons, and the fights begin, one pair at a time. The music grows louder and faster as each fight grows more exciting.

[pause]

We will stay high up with our baker, far from the arena, and watch only from a distance. Tiny figures move and circle on the yellow sand. A referee in a white tunic, with a long stick, watches closely and steps in when he must. And the crowd shouts and cheers and groans, and calls out the names of their favourites.

[pause]

{img:referee-white-tunic-stick-arena-distant-figures-crowd-roar}
At the end of each fight, the winner gets a palm branch and a prize of money on a silver plate. He runs around the arena waving the palm while the crowd cheers. And the loser, if he has fought well, is usually sent away alive. The crowd shouts: let him go!

[long-pause]

In the evening the games end. In a few minutes, the crowd pours out through the eighty arches into the streets. The baker, his friends and his son walk home in the evening light, talking about the gladiators, the animals and the emperor. They stop at a tavern for a cup of wine. Then they go home to bed, tired, and hoarse from shouting.

[long-pause]

{img:street-food-stall-outside-colosseum-sausages-bread-chickpeas-crowd}
What do the Romans eat at the games?

[pause]

The games last all day, from morning to evening, and the crowd gets hungry. So people bring food in baskets and bags. And they buy food from sellers who walk up and down the rows, and from stalls around the outside of the amphitheatre.

[long-pause]

Archaeologists digging in the drains of the Colosseum have found out what people ate two thousand years ago. People dropped their leftovers on the floor, and the rain washed them into the drains.

[pause]

They found the stones and seeds of olives, figs, grapes, melons, peaches, plums and cherries. Walnuts, hazelnuts and pine nuts. Chicken bones and pig bones. The shells of mussels, oysters and snails.

[pause]

The sellers offer hot sausages, bread and cheese, boiled chickpeas and roasted nuts, honey cakes, fruit and pastries, and wine mixed with water.

[long-pause]

And sometimes the emperor gives food to the crowd. Baskets of bread, meat, cakes, fruit and wine are handed out along the rows. A few years later, the next emperor will hold a great feast in the Colosseum for the whole crowd. Sweet cakes, dates, figs and nuts will rain down on the people from ropes stretched over the seats. And the crowd will shout with joy.

[pause]

A poet describes it. A whole day of feasting in the amphitheatre, rich and poor, men and women, children and senators, all eating together, as if the whole city were one great family at one great table.

[pause]

{img:sweets-dates-figs-raining-down-from-ropes-crowd-reaching-joy}
So for ordinary Romans, a day at the games was not only a day of shows. It was a day out, a picnic and a festival, with friends and family, food and wine, sunshine and the shade of the great sunshade, and noise and music and excitement.

[long-pause]

{img:two-gladiators-facing-each-other-arena-afternoon-respect-distant}
The most famous gladiator fight of the opening games was described by the poet Martial, who saw it with his own eyes. It was between two gladiators called Priscus and Verus.

[pause]

They were perfectly matched, equal in strength, skill and courage. They fought for a long time in front of the emperor and all of Rome. And neither of them could win.

[long-pause]

The crowd began to shout for both of them to be given their freedom. But Titus followed the rule of the games. The fight had to go on until one of them raised a finger to ask for mercy.

[pause]

So they kept fighting, for a long time. The whole amphitheatre watched in suspense. And still neither of them gave way.

[pause]

And then, at last, Martial says, both of them raised their fingers at exactly the same moment. They had fought so bravely and so equally that neither would give up first, and neither could win.

[pause]

Titus was delighted. He sent them both gifts. And then he did something that had never been done before. He gave them both the wooden sword, the sign of freedom. Both of them were set free, on the same day, together.

[long-pause]

Martial wrote: this has happened under no ruler but you, Caesar. Two fought, and two won.

[pause]

For the Romans, this story showed the best side of the games. Courage, skill and honour, and the mercy of a good emperor. It was told again and again for many years.

[pause]

We do not know what became of Priscus and Verus after that. Perhaps they became trainers. Perhaps they went home to their families. Perhaps they opened a tavern, as some retired gladiators did. And there they told their customers about the great day they fought before the emperor at the opening of the Colosseum, and both won.

[long-pause]

{img:martial-reading-poem-friends-evening-lamp-scroll-colosseum-book}
Martial's little poem about them is still read today, nearly two thousand years later. It is one of the very few times in all of ancient writing that two ordinary gladiators are remembered by name. Not because one won and one lost. But because they were equal, and both were spared, on a summer afternoon, in the new amphitheatre, by an emperor who wanted to be kind.

[long-pause]

{img:roman-women-upper-seats-colosseum-wooden-gallery-shade-talking}
Women are an important part of life around the Colosseum, in many ways.

[pause]

In the amphitheatre, they sit at the top, in the wooden gallery, separate from the men, as the old law says. Only the priestesses of Vesta and the women of the emperor's family sit at the front, near the arena.

[long-pause]

But women are passionate fans of the games and of the gladiators. On the walls of the gladiator barracks in Pompeii, there are scratched messages. One gladiator is called the sigh of the girls. Another is called the doctor of the night-time girls. The gladiators were the heartthrobs of their time.

[pause]

A Roman writer a few years later tells a story, perhaps exaggerated, about a senator's wife. She left her husband, her children and her comfortable life, and ran away to Egypt with a gladiator. He was not even handsome, the writer says, with a scarred face, a bad eye and a lump on his nose. But he was a gladiator. And that was enough.

[pause]

{img:pompeii-wall-graffiti-gladiator-names-scratched-plaster}
And there were women gladiators too.

[long-pause]

At the opening games, an old historian says, women took part in the animal hunts. And in later games, women fought as gladiators in the arena. The next emperor held games at night, by torchlight, with women gladiators.

[pause]

There is a stone carving found in Turkey, now in a museum in London. It shows two women gladiators fighting each other with shields and swords, without helmets. Their stage names are carved above them: Amazon and Achillia. The words say both were set free with honour, after a fight that ended in a draw.

[pause]

{img:stone-relief-two-women-gladiators-amazon-achillia-museum-light}
Many Romans did not approve of women gladiators. About a hundred and twenty years later, an emperor banned them. But for more than a century, some women fought in the arenas of the Roman world.

[long-pause]

And women worked in all the other jobs around the games. They sold food, drink, cushions and souvenirs outside the Colosseum. They ran the taverns, inns and cook shops where the crowds ate. They wove the cloth for the sunshade, the costumes and the banners. And in the gladiators' homes, wives, mothers and daughters waited after every fight for news.

[long-pause]

{img:roman-children-playing-gladiators-wooden-swords-street-laughing}
The children of Rome in the year 80 love the games, like everyone else.

[pause]

They play gladiators in the streets and courtyards, with wooden swords, little shields and nets. They shout the names of famous fighters. They pretend to be the emperor, raising their thumbs and setting the winners free. They collect little clay figures of gladiators and draw them on walls with charcoal.

[long-pause]

Roman children have many toys. Dolls of clay or wood or bone, with arms and legs that move, and little clothes. Little wooden horses on wheels. Little carts pulled by goats. Hoops and spinning tops, and balls made of leather or blown-up pig bladders. Rattles for babies. Little pottery animals. Kites and swings, and board games scratched on stone.

[pause]

They play hide and seek, blind man's buff and leapfrog. And they play a game with nuts, rolling them at a little pile of nuts to knock it over and win them. Nuts are so important to Roman children that when a boy grows up, people say he has left his nuts behind.

[pause]

{img:roman-girl-doll-jointed-clay-courtyard-mother-spinning-wool}
Children of rich families go to school from about seven, with a slave to carry their things and look after them. They learn to read, write and count on wax boards, and later Greek and Latin and public speaking. Poor children work with their parents from a young age, in shops, workshops and markets.

[long-pause]

At the games, children sit with their families high up in the seats. They watch and cheer and eat, and in the afternoon heat, they fall asleep in their mothers' laps.

[pause]

And a boy who sat with his father high up in the Colosseum at the opening games, at the age of eight, might live long enough to see the Pantheon built, fifty years later. As an old man, he might tell his grandchildren by the fire about the great day the Colosseum first opened. About the ships on the water, the kneeling elephant, and the two gladiators who both won their freedom.

[long-pause]

{img:colosseum-valley-meta-sudans-fountain-colossus-baths-of-titus-arch}
Let us walk around the outside of the Colosseum in the year 80, and look at the neighbourhood.

[pause]

The amphitheatre stands in a valley between three of the hills of Rome. On one of them live the emperors. Around the building lies a wide paved square, with a ring of tall stone posts. Perhaps they held the ropes of the sunshade, or helped to control the crowds.

[long-pause]

To the west, toward the Forum, stands a great fountain. It is a tall cone of brick and marble. Water bubbles out at the top and runs down its sides into a round basin. Travellers stop there to drink, wash their faces and rest in the cool spray.

[pause]

Next to it stands Nero's giant bronze statue, now with the head of the sun god, thirty metres tall, its bronze rays shining in the sun.

[pause]

{img:baths-of-titus-steps-colonnade-bathers-entering-afternoon-sun}
On a hillside to the northeast, Titus has built new public baths on part of Nero's old palace grounds. He opens them at the same time as the Colosseum. After the games, people can go there to bathe and relax, for free or for a tiny fee.

[long-pause]

And on the slope along the Sacred Way into the Forum, there will soon be a great white marble arch, built after Titus's death to celebrate his victory in Judaea.

[pause]

Inside the arch there are carvings. Titus rides in his victory chariot, crowned by the goddess of Victory. And there is the victory parade through Rome, carrying the treasures of the temple of Jerusalem. The great golden lampstand with its seven branches, the silver trumpets and the golden table.

[pause]

{img:arch-of-titus-white-marble-relief-procession-menorah-sacred-way}
The arch still stands today. The carving of the seven-branched lampstand can still be seen. It has become a symbol of the Jewish people. For many centuries, Jews living in Rome would not walk under that arch, out of sorrow.

[long-pause]

All around the Colosseum there are streets, houses, shops, taverns and workshops. There are the barracks of the sailors who work the sunshade, and the gladiator schools. There are storerooms for scenery and weapons, a hospital for wounded gladiators, and the offices of the people who organise the games.

[pause]

On game days, the whole neighbourhood is full of crowds and noise. On other days it is quieter. You hear the gladiators training in their schools, the animals roaring faintly in their enclosures, and the sailors mending the canvas of the sunshade in the sunshine.

[long-pause]

{img:baths-of-titus-grand-hall-marble-pools-bathers-light-steam}
After the games, many Romans go to the baths.

[pause]

The new baths of Titus, opened at the same time as the Colosseum, stand just above it on the hillside. They are large and splendid. There are great halls with high curved ceilings, walls of coloured marble and floors of tiny stone pictures. There are pools and fountains, statues and gardens, exercise yards, libraries, and rooms for massage and for talking.

[long-pause]

Romans go to the baths every day if they can, in the afternoon after work, before dinner. It costs almost nothing, the smallest coin. Children go free. And on special days, the emperor sometimes pays for everyone.

[pause]

First the bathers undress in the changing room and leave their clothes in little holes in the wall. Sometimes a slave guards them, because thieves are common. Then they exercise in the courtyard. They run, wrestle, lift weights, or play ball games.

[pause]

Then they go into the warm room, and then the hot room, full of steam. It is heated by hot air flowing under the floors and inside the walls, from great furnaces below, kept burning by slaves. They sweat, and scrape their skin clean with a curved metal tool and oil. Then they jump into the cold pool. And then they swim in the great outdoor pool, or sit and talk and relax.

[long-pause]

The baths are a place to meet friends, gossip, do business, hear the news and the latest jokes, and talk about the games and the gladiators. Sellers walk around calling out: sausages, cakes, drinks! Masseurs slap and knead. Poets recite their verses to anyone who will listen.

[pause]

One Roman thinker lived above a bath house and complained about the noise. The grunting of weightlifters, the slapping of masseurs, the shouts of ball players, the cries of sausage sellers. And the man who loved the sound of his own voice, singing in the bath.

[pause]

{img:bathers-relaxing-pool-edge-marble-statues-evening-glow}
And then, clean and refreshed, the Romans go home to dinner in the evening, as the lamps are lit. The streets fill with people coming home from the baths, the games and their work, in the warm summer evening.

[long-pause]

{img:hypogeum-underground-passages-brick-walls-lifts-cages-torchlight}
A few years after the opening, the next emperor made a big change to the Colosseum.

[pause]

Under the floor of the arena, he built a great underground world. Two levels of tunnels, passages, rooms, cages and machines, all under a wooden floor covered in sand. After that, the arena could no longer be flooded for sea battles. But it became a stage for amazing shows.

[long-pause]

Underground there are long brick corridors running the length of the arena, with rooms on each side. Gladiators wait there before their fights. Animals are kept there in cages. Scenery is stored there.

[pause]

And there are lifts, about sixty of them. They are wooden cages raised and lowered by ropes and pulleys. Teams of workers turn great wooden wheels to work them. The lifts carry animals, gladiators and scenery up to trapdoors in the arena floor. The trapdoors open suddenly. And to the crowd's amazement, a lion, a bear, or even a whole forest appears out of nowhere in the middle of the arena.

[pause]

{img:wooden-lift-capstan-workers-rope-pulley-cage-underground}
A few years ago, a team of archaeologists and engineers built a full-size working copy of one of these lifts. They tested it in the Colosseum, and lifted a wolf safely up into the arena, to show how it worked.

[long-pause]

On game days, this underground world was busy, dark, crowded and noisy, lit by torches and oil lamps. Stage hands and animal keepers, carpenters and engineers, and hundreds of men turning the wheels, all worked together to make the show run smoothly. Every lift had to rise at exactly the right moment. One mistake, and the whole show would go wrong in front of fifty thousand people.

[pause]

Today the arena floor is gone. You can look down into the underground world from above, and see the maze of brick walls and passages, and the grooves where the lifts once ran. Part of the floor has been rebuilt in wood. Visitors can stand on it and imagine what the gladiators saw as they came out into the arena and looked up at fifty thousand faces rising all around them.

[pause]

{img:reconstructed-arena-floor-wooden-visitors-standing-looking-up-tiers}
The underground passages also linked the Colosseum to the gladiator school next door, and to the storerooms and barracks. So everything could be moved in and out without the crowd seeing it.

[long-pause]

{img:colosseum-4th-century-quieter-arena-fewer-crowds-christian-rome}
The games in the Colosseum went on for more than four hundred years.

[pause]

Emperor after emperor gave great games there. One emperor, after a great victory, held games that lasted a hundred and twenty-three days. Another emperor even fought in the arena himself as a gladiator, which shocked the Romans.

[long-pause]

But slowly, over the centuries, things changed.

[pause]

About three hundred years after the Colosseum opened, the Roman Empire became Christian. The Christians did not approve of the gladiator games, and Christian writers wrote against them. The games also became harder to pay for, as the empire grew poorer and was attacked by enemies.

[pause]

There is a story about a monk called Telemachus. He came to Rome from the East, and during a gladiator fight, he ran down onto the sand and tried to separate the fighters. He begged them to stop. The angry crowd threw stones at him. And the emperor was so moved when he heard of it that he banned the gladiator games forever.

[pause]

We do not know if the story is true. But it seems the gladiator fights did end around that time.

[long-pause]

The animal hunts went on a little longer. The last one we know of in the Colosseum was held more than a hundred years later, when a Gothic king ruled Italy after the fall of the Western Roman Empire.

[pause]

After that, the Colosseum fell silent. The great building that once held fifty thousand cheering people stood empty, in a Rome that had shrunk from a million people to perhaps thirty thousand, living among the ruins.

[pause]

{img:empty-colosseum-grass-growing-arena-6th-century-quiet-ruin}
Grass grew in the arena. Trees took root in the seats. Birds nested in the arches. And the people of Rome began to use the great building for other things.

[long-pause]

{img:shepherd-goats-grazing-ancient-ruins-rome-middle-ages-quiet}
In the early Middle Ages, shepherds grazed their goats and sheep among the ruins of ancient Rome, in the old Forum, which they now called the cow field. And the great amphitheatre stood above them, silent, enormous and mysterious. It was a building from another age that nobody could build any more. People began to say it had been built by giants, or by magic.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-colosseum-houses-workshops-inside-arches-fortress-towers}
In the Middle Ages, the Colosseum became a strange kind of village.

[pause]

People built houses, workshops and stables inside the arches and under the vaults. Blacksmiths, shoemakers and carpenters lived and worked there. There was even a little church built into it. Later, a powerful Roman family turned it into a fortress, with walls and towers.

[long-pause]

Then, in 1349, a great earthquake struck Rome. The outer wall on the south side collapsed in a huge heap of stone. That is why the Colosseum today has a complete outer wall only on the north side.

[pause]

For the next four hundred years, the Colosseum was used as a quarry. The Romans took its stones to build palaces, churches and bridges. Much of the stone of some of the great buildings of Rome came from the Colosseum. They dug out the iron clamps too, leaving the holes. Little by little, the great building was disappearing. Would anything be left?

[pause]

{img:renaissance-carts-carrying-stones-from-colosseum-builders-1500s}
Then, about two hundred and seventy-five years ago, the popes decided to protect it. A pope declared the Colosseum a sacred place, in memory of the early Christians who were believed to have died there. He set up a great cross in the arena. Historians today are not sure how many Christians really died in the Colosseum itself. But that decision saved the building from being taken apart completely.

[long-pause]

Later, the popes and then the Italian government repaired the Colosseum. They built great brick supports at each end of the surviving wall to hold it up. Travellers, writers and artists came from all over the world to see it, especially by moonlight, which was thought to be the most romantic way to see it.

[pause]

The English poet Lord Byron walked there by moonlight and wrote about it. The American writer Mark Twain called it the king of all European ruins. And plant experts found more than four hundred different kinds of plants growing in the ruins. People liked to believe some of their seeds had come long ago from distant lands, in the fur and food of the animals brought for the games.

[pause]

{img:moonlit-colosseum-19th-century-travellers-lanterns-romantic-ruins}
Most of those plants were later cleared away to protect the stones. But in spring, there are still wild flowers growing in the cracks and on the high ledges of the Colosseum.

[long-pause]

{img:film-set-style-reconstructed-colosseum-arena-crowd-sunlight-later}
The Colosseum and the gladiators have fascinated people ever since the games ended, especially in the last hundred years, in books, paintings and films.

[pause]

About a hundred and fifty years ago, a French painter made famous pictures of the games. They gave people the idea that the crowd decided a defeated gladiator's fate by turning their thumbs up or down. In fact, historians are not sure what gesture the Romans really used. Perhaps a thumb hidden in the fist meant mercy. Nobody knows for certain.

[long-pause]

There is a famous sentence people think gladiators said to the emperor before they fought: Hail, emperor, those who are about to die salute you. But it was written down only once. And it was not said by gladiators at all, but by prisoners forced to fight in a show on a lake. It was probably not a regular custom.

[pause]

There have been famous films about gladiators. In 2000 there was a film called Gladiator, about a Roman general who becomes a gladiator and fights in the Colosseum. The Colosseum was rebuilt with computers to look as it did when it was new. The film was hugely popular. It made millions of people curious about ancient Rome.

[pause]

{img:tourists-gladiator-costumes-outside-colosseum-photographs-sunny}
A second film came out in 2024. It even showed a sea battle in the flooded arena, with sharks. That is certainly not true to history. But it shows how the old stories of the opening games still capture people's imagination.

[long-pause]

Today, outside the Colosseum, men dressed as gladiators and Roman soldiers, in plastic armour and red cloaks, pose for photographs with tourists for a few coins. Perhaps, two thousand years ago, on that same square, people did something similar, selling souvenirs and little clay figures to the crowds on their way to the games.

[long-pause]

{img:summer-evening-rome-80ad-colosseum-quiet-lamps-people-strolling}
Let us end with a summer evening in the year 80, during the hundred days of the opening games.

[pause]

The games are over for the day. The crowds have gone home through the eighty arches into the streets. The amphitheatre is quiet now, empty and enormous in the soft evening light. The last of the sun shines on its upper storeys, on the statues in the arches, and on the masts of the sunshade, black against the pink sky.

[long-pause]

The sailors have rolled up the great sunshade, climbed down and gone back to their barracks to eat and rest. Workers sweep the corridors and the seats, and rake the sand of the arena smooth again for tomorrow. In the school next door, the gladiators who fought today eat their supper, have their wounds treated, and talk quietly about the day. Tomorrow, others will walk through the tunnel.

[pause]

In the animal enclosures outside the walls, the keepers feed the animals. The elephants drink, and the lions lie quietly in the cool of the evening.

[pause]

{img:colosseum-dusk-empty-arena-raked-sand-pink-sky-swallows}
And in the streets around the Colosseum, the people of Rome walk slowly in the warm evening air, to the baths, the taverns and home. They talk about the games and the gladiators and the emperor. Swallows swoop and circle around the high arches. The first lamps are lit in the windows. The sky turns from pink to violet to deep blue. And the first stars come out over the great quiet amphitheatre and the sleeping city.

[long-pause]

{img:colosseum-today-rome-blue-sky-crowds-via-dei-fori-imperiali}
Today, the Colosseum is the most visited monument in Italy and one of the most famous buildings in the world.

[pause]

Every year, more than seven million people come to see it from every country on earth. That is more people each year than ever saw it in all the centuries of the games. Not long ago, it was chosen as one of the New Seven Wonders of the World.

[long-pause]

You walk to it along a wide avenue from the Forum, or come up out of the underground station right in front of it. And there it suddenly is, enormous, rising above you, with its great arches and its broken outer wall glowing gold in the sun.

[pause]

Inside, you can walk through the corridors where the crowds once streamed, and climb to the upper levels. You can look down at the arena, with its rebuilt wooden floor at one end, and the maze of underground passages open to the sky. You can see the remains of the seats, and the marble seats of the senators. And you can see the numbers carved above the entrance arches.

[pause]

In the corridors there is a little museum with things found in the Colosseum and its drains. Coins and pottery, animal bones, and the remains of food people ate at the games two thousand years ago. Olives and peaches, grapes and nuts, chicken bones and mussel shells.

[long-pause]

Every year at Easter, the Pope leads a procession by torchlight at the Colosseum, with thousands of people, in memory of the early Christians.

[pause]

And at night, the Colosseum is lit up golden against the dark sky. Whenever a country somewhere in the world stops using the death penalty, the lights of the Colosseum are changed from white to gold for a night, as a sign of hope and life.

[pause]

{img:colosseum-night-golden-light-quiet-street-cypress}
Next to it, you can still see the ruins of the gladiator school below the level of the street, with the curved rows of seats of its little practice arena. The arch of Titus still stands on the Sacred Way. And the places where the great fountain and the giant statue once stood are marked on the ground.

[long-pause]

So we leave the great amphitheatre for tonight, as the evening light turns its old stones gold, and the swallows circle high above the arches where the sailors once hauled on the ropes of the sunshade.

[pause]

The builders with their ox carts, the gladiators in their school eating their barley porridge, the baker and his son in the high seats, and two tired fighters walking out together, both free.

[pause]

{img:swallows-circling-colosseum-arches-sunset-sky-calm}
If you enjoyed this evening in ancient Rome, you are very welcome to subscribe to the channel, and to come back for another quiet story.

[pause]

There should be another quiet story waiting on the screen now, if you are still awake.

[pause]

Sleep well, and good night.
