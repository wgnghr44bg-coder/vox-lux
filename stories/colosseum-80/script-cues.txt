Tonight we are going nearly two thousand years back, to the city of Rome, in the year 80 after Christ.

[long-pause]

In a valley between the hills of Rome, where a few years before there was a lake in the gardens of an emperor, a vast new building now stands: an enormous oval of pale travertine stone, four storeys high, with eighty arches on each level, and statues looking out from every arch. It is the greatest building the Romans have ever made, and in this year, it is about to open.

[pause]

The Romans call it the Flavian Amphitheatre. We call it the Colosseum. And tonight, we will spend a little time around it: with the builders and the sailors who raise its great awning, with the gladiators in their training school, with the animal keepers and the crowds, and with the ordinary people of Rome.

[pause]

If you enjoy slow journeys into the past, you are welcome to subscribe to the channel, so that you can find the next one easily.

[long-pause]

Rome, in the year 80, is the capital of an empire that stretches from Britain to Syria, from the Rhine to the Sahara, around the whole Mediterranean Sea. It is the largest city in the world, with perhaps a million people.

[pause]

It has changed a great deal in the hundred and twenty years since the time of Julius Caesar. The Republic is gone. Rome is ruled by emperors. And it has become a city of marble, and gold, and great buildings: temples, and baths, and forums, and aqueducts, and theatres, and palaces.

[long-pause]

But the last few years have not been easy.

[pause]

Sixteen years before, in the year 64, a great fire burned for nine days, and destroyed much of the city. The emperor at the time, Nero, used the burned land, in the middle of the city, to build himself an enormous palace, called the Golden House, with gardens, and woods, and vineyards, and a great artificial lake, and a gigantic bronze statue of himself, more than thirty metres tall, called the Colossus.

[pause]

{img:golden-house-gardens-lake-colossus-statue-nero-palace-sunset}
The Romans hated it. Nero had taken the heart of their city for himself.

[pause]

Then, in the year 68, Nero died, and there was a terrible year of civil war, with four emperors, one after another. And the last of them, a tough, practical, good-humoured old soldier, called Vespasian, won, and became emperor, and brought peace.

[long-pause]

Vespasian decided to give the land of Nero's palace back to the people of Rome. He drained Nero's lake, and on the spot where it had been, he began to build a great amphitheatre: a place of entertainment, for the people, the largest in the world.

[pause]

He did not live to see it finished. He passed away in the summer of 79, at the age of sixty-nine. It is said that, as he felt death coming, he joked: oh dear, I think I am becoming a god. Because the Romans made their dead emperors into gods.

[pause]

{img:vespasian-old-emperor-portrait-practical-face-humorous-calm}
His son, Titus, became emperor. And in the year 80, he finished the amphitheatre, and opened it, with a great festival of games, which lasted for a hundred days.

[long-pause]

{img:emperor-titus-portrait-young-kind-face-laurel-toga-palace}
The emperor Titus, in the year 80, is forty years old.

[pause]

He is the elder son of Vespasian. He was brought up at the court of the emperors, with the son of the emperor Claudius, and was a soldier, and a general, in Britain, and Germany, and Judaea, where he commanded the Roman army that captured Jerusalem, in the year 70. He is clever, and charming, and handsome, and a good speaker, and can write fast in shorthand, and imitate anyone's handwriting, and sing, and play the harp.

[long-pause]

Before he became emperor, many Romans were afraid of him. He was the commander of the Praetorian Guard, the emperor's bodyguard, and was said to be harsh. And he was in love with a foreign queen, Berenice, the sister of the Jewish king Agrippa, and the Romans, remembering Cleopatra, did not want another foreign queen in Rome.

[pause]

But when Titus became emperor, he surprised everyone. He sent Berenice away, sadly, because the Romans would not accept her. And he became one of the most loved emperors in Roman history.

[pause]

The historian Suetonius says that Titus never sent anyone away from an audience without some hope. And that once, at dinner, he remembered that he had done nothing kind for anyone all day, and said: my friends, I have lost a day.

[long-pause]

His short reign was full of disasters.

[pause]

In the summer of 79, only two months after he became emperor, the volcano Vesuvius, near Naples, erupted, and buried the towns of Pompeii, and Herculaneum, and Stabiae. Titus went there himself, and sent help, and money, and officials, to help the survivors, and rebuild.

[pause]

{img:vesuvius-distant-bay-of-naples-quiet-smoke-plume-79ad-far}
Then, in the spring of 80, while Titus was away, in Campania, helping the people there, a great fire broke out in Rome, and burned for three days, and destroyed the temple of Jupiter on the Capitol, and the Pantheon, and many other buildings. And then there was a plague, one of the worst in Roman history.

[pause]

Titus gave his own money, and the furniture of his own palaces, to help rebuild Rome. He did everything he could for the people.

[long-pause]

And in the summer of 80, perhaps in June, he opened the great new amphitheatre, with a hundred days of games, to cheer up the people of Rome, after all their troubles, and to show them that their emperor cared for them.

[pause]

He gave away, at the games, little wooden balls, thrown into the crowd, with tickets inside them, for gifts: food, and clothes, and silver, and gold, and horses, and even slaves, and houses.

[pause]

{img:wooden-gift-balls-thrown-into-crowd-amphitheatre-hands-reaching}
Titus reigned for only two years. In September of the year 81, he fell ill, with a fever, on a journey to the Sabine country, the home of his family, and passed away, at the age of forty-one. The people of Rome mourned him, it is said, as if they had lost a member of their own family.

[pause]

His brother, Domitian, became emperor, and finished the last parts of the amphitheatre, and added the underground passages, beneath the arena.

[long-pause]

{img:bay-of-naples-79ad-vesuvius-green-slopes-vineyards-villas-calm}
A year before the Colosseum opened, in the summer of 79, something happened in the south of Italy which shocked the whole Roman world.

[pause]

Around the beautiful Bay of Naples, there were rich towns, and villas, and vineyards, and farms, on the green slopes of a mountain, called Vesuvius. Nobody knew that it was a volcano. It had been quiet for hundreds of years. Its slopes were covered with vineyards, and woods, and gardens, and the wine of Vesuvius was famous.

[long-pause]

There had been earthquakes, seventeen years before, in the year 62, which had damaged the towns of Pompeii and Herculaneum, and the people were still repairing their houses, and temples, when, on a day in late summer, or perhaps autumn, of the year 79, the mountain woke up.

[pause]

We will watch only from very far away, as a young man did, who was there, across the bay, at the town of Misenum: Pliny the Younger, who was seventeen, and later wrote two letters about it, to the historian Tacitus, which still exist. They are the first eyewitness description of a volcanic eruption in history.

[pause]

{img:distant-tall-cloud-umbrella-pine-shape-over-bay-misenum-view}
He wrote that, at about one o'clock in the afternoon, his mother pointed out to his uncle a cloud of unusual size and shape, rising from a mountain, across the bay. It rose very high, he said, like an umbrella pine tree: a tall trunk, and then branches, spreading out at the top. Sometimes it was white, and sometimes dark, and patchy.

[long-pause]

His uncle, Pliny the Elder, a famous scholar, and writer, and the admiral of the Roman fleet at Misenum, wanted to see it more closely, as a scientist, and then, when he received a message, asking for help, from a friend, on the other side of the bay, he took the warships, and sailed across, to rescue people. He did not come back. He passed away, on the shore, at Stabiae, peacefully, his nephew says, as if asleep.

[pause]

The towns of Pompeii, and Herculaneum, and Stabiae, were buried, under metres of ash, and pumice, and rock. They were forgotten, for more than sixteen hundred years, until they were found again, in the eighteenth century.

[pause]

{img:pompeii-excavated-street-forum-columns-vesuvius-behind-quiet-later}
And because they were buried so quickly, they were preserved, almost as they were, on that day, in the year 79: the streets, and the houses, and the shops, and the paintings on the walls, and the graffiti, and the bread in the ovens, and the gladiator barracks, with the helmets, and the armour, of the gladiators who had trained there.

[pause]

Today, Pompeii is one of the most visited places in the world, and the best picture we have of an ordinary Roman town, in the time of the Colosseum.

[long-pause]

{img:colosseum-exterior-four-storeys-arches-columns-tuscan-ionic-corinthian}
The Colosseum is the largest amphitheatre ever built.

[pause]

An amphitheatre is a round, or oval, open-air theatre, with seats all around, rising up in rows, and a flat space in the middle, the arena, where the shows take place. The word arena means sand, because the floor is covered with sand.

[long-pause]

The Colosseum is an oval, about a hundred and eighty-nine metres long, and a hundred and fifty-six metres wide, and nearly fifty metres high: as tall as a building of fifteen storeys today. It can hold perhaps fifty thousand people, or more, sitting on stone seats, in rows, all around the arena.

[pause]

The outside wall has four storeys. The bottom three have eighty arches each, one above the other, framed by half-columns, of three different styles: at the bottom, the plain, sturdy Tuscan style. In the middle, the Ionic style, with its scroll-shaped tops. And above, the elegant Corinthian style, with its leaves. The top storey has no arches, but small square windows, and tall, flat pilasters, and brackets, to hold the great poles of the awning.

[pause]

{img:statues-in-arches-upper-storeys-colosseum-sunlit-travertine}
In each of the arches of the second and third storeys, there is a statue: gods, and heroes, and emperors. The whole outside is built of travertine, a creamy white limestone, from quarries at Tibur, about thirty kilometres away. In the sunshine, it glows, pale gold.

[long-pause]

There are eighty entrances, at ground level, each with a number carved above it, which can still be seen today. Every spectator has a ticket, a little piece of pottery, or bone, with the number of his entrance, and his section, and his row, and his seat. So fifty thousand people can come in, and find their places, in about fifteen minutes, and leave again, just as quickly, through the corridors, and staircases, which the Romans called vomitoria, because they let the crowds pour out.

[pause]

Four of the entrances are special: the two at the ends of the long axis, for the gladiators, and the processions. And the two at the ends of the short axis, for the emperor, and his guests, and the most important people.

[pause]

{img:numbered-entrance-arches-crowd-entering-tokens-morning-sun}
Inside, the seats are arranged according to rank, in rings, one above another. In the front row, nearest the arena, on a wide platform, called the podium, there are the senators, in their white togas with purple stripes, on wide marble seats, with their names carved on them. And the Vestal Virgins, the priestesses of Vesta. And the emperor, in his special box, the pulvinar, in the middle of one of the long sides.

[long-pause]

Behind them, there are the knights, the rich men of Rome. Then the ordinary citizens, in their white togas, which they must wear. Then, higher up, the poorer citizens. And at the very top, in a wooden gallery, under the colonnade, the women, and the slaves, and the poorest people. Augustus, many years before, had made a law that women must sit at the top, separately.

[pause]

The higher you sit, the further you are from the arena, and the less important you are. But from the top, you have a wonderful view, over the whole amphitheatre, and over the roofs of Rome, to the hills.

[long-pause]

{img:rome-80ad-morning-street-shops-opening-bakery-crowds-insula}
Let us walk through the streets of Rome, on an ordinary morning, in the year 80, a few weeks before the great games.

[pause]

The city wakes early, at first light. The shopkeepers take down the wooden shutters, from the fronts of their shops, which open straight onto the street, under the tall apartment buildings. The bakers have been working since before dawn, and the smell of fresh bread fills the narrow streets.

[long-pause]

The streets are crowded, noisy, and full of life. Porters carry loads on their backs. Litters, carried by slaves, push through the crowds, with rich men, or ladies, inside, behind curtains. Children run to school. Barbers shave men, on stools, in the street. Cooks fry sausages, and sell hot food, from counters. Sellers cry their wares: hot bread, cakes, matches, sulphur, old clothes. Beggars sit on the steps of the temples.

[pause]

Everyone is talking about the new amphitheatre, and the games. When will they begin? Who will fight? How many animals will there be? Will the emperor give away gifts?

[pause]

In the year 80, Rome is also still recovering from the great fire, of the spring, which burned the temple of Jupiter, and many other buildings, and from the plague. There are building sites everywhere: scaffolding, and cranes, and carts loaded with stone, and bricks, and timber, and the sound of hammering, and sawing, and shouting.

[long-pause]

The emperor Domitian, later, will rebuild the burned temple of Jupiter, on the Capitol, more splendid than ever, with a roof of gilded bronze tiles, which shine in the sun, and can be seen from all over the city.

[pause]

And all the time, the new amphitheatre rises above the roofs, in its valley, enormous, and pale, and new, with the statues in its arches, and the sailors climbing the masts, at the top, practising with the awning, and the workmen putting the finishing touches to the seats, and the corridors, and the decorations.

[pause]

The poet Martial wrote that, where Nero's hated palace had stood, now there stood the people's delight. Rome had been given back to itself.

[long-pause]

{img:roman-tavern-counter-wine-jars-morning-customers-bread-olives}
In the taverns, along the streets near the new amphitheatre, the innkeepers are getting ready for the crowds that will come. They buy extra wine, and bread, and sausages, and hire extra servants, and paint signs on their walls, showing gladiators, and animals, to attract customers. It will be a good summer for business.

[long-pause]

{img:colosseum-under-construction-cranes-treadwheels-scaffold-workers-72ad}
How was the Colosseum built?

[pause]

It was begun around the year 72, under Vespasian, and opened in the year 80, under Titus: only about eight years, for one of the largest buildings in the world. It was an enormous achievement of engineering, and organization.

[long-pause]

First, the builders drained Nero's lake, and dug down, and laid enormous foundations: a great ring of concrete, twelve metres deep, under the whole building, to carry its enormous weight, on the soft, wet ground of the old lake bed.

[pause]

Then they built the walls, and the arches, and the vaults, using travertine, for the main piers, and outer walls, and tufa, a lighter volcanic stone, and brick, for the inner walls, and concrete, the great Roman invention, made of lime, and volcanic ash, and rubble, and water, which sets as hard as rock, for the vaults, that hold up the seats.

[pause]

About a hundred thousand cubic metres of travertine were needed: brought from the quarries at Tibur, along a special road, in ox carts, perhaps more than two hundred cartloads a day, for years. The blocks were held together, not with mortar, but with iron clamps, perhaps three hundred tons of iron. In the Middle Ages, people dug out the clamps, to reuse the iron, and left the holes which can still be seen all over the walls today.

[long-pause]

Thousands of people worked on it: architects, and engineers, and surveyors, and stone cutters, and masons, and carpenters, and sculptors, and labourers, and the drivers of the ox carts, and the men who worked the great cranes. Many of them were skilled, free craftsmen, well paid. Many others were slaves, and prisoners, including, many historians believe, thousands of Jewish prisoners, brought to Rome after the fall of Jerusalem, in the year 70. And an inscription, found in the Colosseum, suggests that it was paid for with the treasure taken from the temple in Jerusalem.

[pause]

The cranes were worked by men walking inside great wooden wheels, treadwheels, which turned a drum, and wound up a rope, and lifted the heavy blocks of stone, up to the top of the walls.

[pause]

{img:architect-plans-wax-tablet-measuring-arches-site-sunlight}
The architect is unknown. We do not know his name. But he designed a building so well, so strong, and so practical, that it has stood for nearly two thousand years, through earthquakes, and fires, and wars, and centuries of people taking its stones, and is still, more or less, standing today.

[long-pause]

And beside the amphitheatre, Nero's giant bronze statue, the Colossus, still stood, but with a new head: not Nero's, but the sun god's, with rays around it, like a crown.

[pause]

The Romans began to call the place near the statue the place of the Colossus. And later, in the Middle Ages, the amphitheatre itself took its name from the statue: the Colosseum.

[pause]

{img:bronze-colossus-sun-god-rays-crown-beside-amphitheatre-blue-sky}
There is an old saying, written down by a monk, called Bede, in England, in the eighth century: as long as the Colosseum stands, Rome shall stand. When the Colosseum falls, Rome shall fall. And when Rome falls, the world shall end.

[long-pause]

{img:roman-concrete-vault-workers-wooden-formwork-pouring-mortar}
How did the Romans build such enormous buildings, nearly two thousand years ago, that are still standing today?

[pause]

The secret is the arch, and the vault, and concrete.

[long-pause]

The Romans did not invent the arch. But they used it more boldly, and more cleverly, than anyone before them. An arch is a curve of wedge-shaped stones, which push against each other, so that the weight of the wall above is carried down, along the curve, to the ground, on either side. An arch can carry an enormous weight, and span a wide opening, much wider than a flat stone beam.

[pause]

A vault is an arch, extended, like a tunnel. And a dome is an arch, turned around, in a circle. With arches, and vaults, and domes, the Romans could build bridges, and aqueducts, and great halls, and amphitheatres, larger than anyone had built before.

[pause]

{img:aqueduct-arches-countryside-rome-water-channel-top-sunset}
And they had concrete. Roman concrete is made of lime, mixed with a special volcanic ash, called pozzolana, from the area around Naples, and Rome, and water, and pieces of stone, or broken brick, or rubble. It is poured into wooden moulds, or between walls of brick, and sets, hard as rock. It can even set under water, so the Romans used it for harbours, and bridges.

[long-pause]

Scientists have recently discovered that Roman concrete has a remarkable property: it heals itself. Little lumps of lime, inside the concrete, react with water, if a crack forms, and fill the crack with new crystals, so that the concrete becomes stronger over time, instead of weaker. That is one of the reasons why Roman buildings have lasted so long, while some modern concrete crumbles in a few decades.

[pause]

In the Colosseum, the vaults that hold up the seats are made of concrete, and the outer walls, and the main piers, of travertine, and the inner walls, of brick, and tufa, each material used where it is best.

[pause]

{img:pantheon-dome-oculus-light-beam-interior-coffers-quiet}
Forty years after the Colosseum, the emperor Hadrian will build the Pantheon, in Rome, a temple to all the gods, with a great dome of concrete, more than forty-three metres wide, with a round opening at the top, the oculus, through which the sunlight shines, and moves slowly around the inside of the dome, through the day, like a sundial. It is still the largest dome of unreinforced concrete in the world, nearly two thousand years later, and it is still standing, and still used, as a church.

[long-pause]

{img:velarium-awning-colosseum-sailors-ropes-masts-shade-crowd}
On hot days, in summer, the Colosseum has a roof: a great awning, of cloth, called the velarium, which can be pulled across the top, to shade the spectators from the burning sun.

[pause]

It is one of the most remarkable things about the building.

[long-pause]

Around the top of the outer wall, there are two hundred and forty wooden masts, held in stone brackets, and sockets, which can still be seen today. From the masts, ropes stretch across, toward the middle, like the spokes of a wheel, or the rigging of a ship. And along the ropes, great strips of canvas, or linen, are pulled, and unfurled, to cover most of the seats, leaving an opening in the middle, over the arena, so that the sun shines on the sand, and the spectators sit in the shade.

[pause]

Nobody is quite sure exactly how it worked. Engineers today still argue about it.

[pause]

{img:sailors-misenum-fleet-hauling-awning-ropes-top-of-wall-wind}
But we know who worked it: sailors. More than a thousand sailors from the Roman navy, from the great fleet at Misenum, near Naples, are stationed in Rome, in a barracks near the Colosseum, just to work the awning. Because sailors know about sails, and ropes, and wind, and climbing. They haul on the ropes, and climb the masts, and adjust the canvas, as the sun moves across the sky, and the wind changes, like sailors on a great ship.

[long-pause]

When it is windy, the awning cannot be used, because the wind would tear it, or pull down the masts. On such days, the spectators bring their own hats, with broad brims, and little parasols. And the advertisements for the games, painted on the walls of Pompeii, sometimes promise: there will be awnings. Because that made people more likely to come.

[pause]

The awning is often coloured. The poet Lucretius describes the sunlight shining through coloured awnings, red, and yellow, and purple, in a theatre, and colouring the whole crowd, and the stage, and the senators, so that everything glows in the colour of the awning.

[pause]

{img:coloured-light-through-awning-crowd-glowing-red-yellow-amphitheatre}
And on the hottest days, there are fountains, and sprays of perfumed water, scented with saffron, and balsam, sprayed over the crowd, to cool them, and to mask the smells of the crowd, and the animals.

[long-pause]

Imagine sitting high up in the Colosseum, on a summer afternoon, in the cool, coloured shade of the great awning, with fifty thousand other people, and the smell of saffron in the air, and the sound of the sailors' calls, high above, as they pull on the ropes, and the bright sunlight shining on the sand of the arena, far below.

[long-pause]

{img:aqueduct-claudia-arches-campagna-countryside-sunset-sheep}
Rome, in the year 80, has more fresh water than any city in the world, until modern times.

[pause]

The water comes from the hills, many kilometres away, through the great aqueducts: channels of stone, and concrete, which carry the water, sloping gently downhill, all the way to the city, underground, through tunnels, for most of their length, and over the valleys, on great rows of arches, which march across the countryside around Rome, for kilometres.

[long-pause]

In the year 80, there are nine aqueducts, bringing perhaps a million cubic metres of water a day into the city: about a thousand litres for every person, much more than most modern cities. The newest and greatest are the Aqua Claudia, and the Anio Novus, finished by the emperor Claudius, about thirty years before, whose arches, nearly thirty metres high, still stand, in the countryside, south east of Rome.

[pause]

The water flows into great tanks, and then, through lead pipes, to the public fountains, on almost every street corner, where the people fill their jars, and buckets, all day long, and to the public baths, and the gardens, and the houses of the rich, who pay for a private supply.

[pause]

The water never stops flowing. The fountains run day and night, and the overflow washes the streets, and flows into the great sewers, which carry it, with the waste of the city, to the river Tiber.

[long-pause]

A few years after the Colosseum, an official called Frontinus, who was in charge of the water supply of Rome, wrote a book about the aqueducts, which still exists. He was very proud of them. He wrote: with such an array of indispensable structures carrying so many waters, compare, if you will, the idle pyramids, or the useless, though famous, works of the Greeks.

[pause]

The Colosseum, too, needed water: for the fountains, and the latrines, and the sprays of perfumed water, and, at the beginning, for flooding the arena for the sea battles. It was supplied by a branch of one of the aqueducts, which came down from the Caelian Hill, nearby.

[pause]

{img:roman-latrine-row-stone-seats-running-water-channel-sponges}
And under the Colosseum, there is a great system of drains, which carry away the rainwater, and the waste, to the great sewer, the Cloaca Maxima, and to the Tiber. Archaeologists, exploring these drains, have found the remains of the food, and the coins, and the little things, that the spectators dropped, two thousand years ago.

[long-pause]

{img:gladiators-training-yard-ludus-wooden-swords-morning-light}
Who were the gladiators?

[pause]

The name comes from gladius, the short sword of the Roman soldier. Gladiators were trained fighters, who fought each other, in pairs, in the arena, to entertain the people of Rome, and of all the cities of the Roman Empire.

[long-pause]

The games began, long before, as part of funerals. Two hundred and fifty years before the Colosseum, in the year 264 before Christ, at the funeral of a Roman nobleman, his sons arranged for three pairs of slaves to fight, in his honour, in the cattle market of Rome. Over the following centuries, the games grew larger, and grander, and became the most popular entertainment in the Roman world, given by rich men, and politicians, and emperors, to win the favour of the people.

[pause]

Most gladiators were slaves, or prisoners of war, or condemned criminals, who had no choice. They were bought, and owned, and trained, by a manager, called a lanista, who hired them out to the men who gave the games.

[pause]

But some gladiators were free men, who chose to become gladiators, for the money, and the fame, and the excitement. They signed a contract, and swore a terrible oath: to be burned, to be chained, to be beaten, and to be killed by the sword, if necessary. And they gave up their rights as free citizens, for a time.

[long-pause]

Gladiators were, in a strange way, both despised and admired.

[pause]

The Romans looked down on them, as people of the lowest kind, like actors, and prostitutes. And yet, they were also heroes, celebrities, like the sports stars of today. Their names were written on the walls, and their pictures painted on lamps, and pots, and glass cups, and children played at being gladiators, and women admired them.

[pause]

{img:clay-oil-lamp-gladiator-picture-glass-cup-souvenir-names}
And it is important to know something, which surprises many people. Most gladiator fights did not end in death. Gladiators were expensive: they cost a great deal to buy, and train, and feed. Their owners did not want them killed. So the fights had rules, and referees, and usually ended when one of the fighters was wounded, or exhausted, or gave up, by raising one finger, to ask for mercy. And very often, mercy was given, and the defeated gladiator was sent away, to fight another day.

[long-pause]

Historians who have studied the records, the gravestones, and the graffiti, think that, in the time of the Colosseum, perhaps only one fight in five, or one in ten, ended in death. A gladiator might fight two or three times a year, and could hope, if he was skilful, and lucky, to survive for years, and perhaps, one day, to win his freedom.

[pause]

When a gladiator won his freedom, he was given a wooden sword, called a rudis, as a sign that he was free. Some freed gladiators became trainers, or referees, in the gladiator schools. And some went back to the arena, because they missed the excitement, and the fame, and the money.

[pause]

{img:wooden-sword-rudis-presented-freedom-arena-crowd-cheering}
We will not watch the fights tonight. Instead, we will spend time with the gladiators in their school, and in their daily lives, and see how they lived, and trained, and ate, and slept, and hoped, in the shadow of the great amphitheatre.

[long-pause]

{img:roman-wall-graffiti-scratched-gladiator-drawings-names-plaster}
The Romans loved to write on walls.

[pause]

All over the Roman world, on the walls of houses, and shops, and taverns, and public buildings, and even the Colosseum itself, people scratched messages, and drawings, and jokes, and names, with nails, or knives, or wrote them with charcoal, or paint. They are called graffiti, from the Italian word for scratches.

[long-pause]

In Pompeii, where the walls were preserved by the volcano, more than eleven thousand graffiti and painted inscriptions have been found. And many of them are about gladiators.

[pause]

There are drawings of gladiators fighting, with their names, and their records: how many fights they had won. There are painted advertisements, for the games: the gladiator troupe of Numerius Festus Ampliatus will fight at Pompeii, on the sixteenth of May. There will be a hunt, and awnings.

[pause]

{img:painted-advertisement-red-letters-wall-pompeii-games-notice}
And there are messages that have nothing to do with gladiators, but tell us about the everyday lives of ordinary Romans, two thousand years ago: love messages, and insults, and jokes, and shopping lists, and complaints, and greetings.

[pause]

One says: Successus the weaver loves the innkeeper's slave girl, Iris, but she does not care for him. Another: I am amazed, O wall, that you have not fallen down, under the weight of so many people's boring writings. Another, at an inn: we have wet the bed, host, we confess, we have done wrong. If you want to know why, there was no chamber pot. Another: Lucius painted this. Another, a child's alphabet, scratched low on the wall, at the height of a child: A, B, C, D.

[long-pause]

In the Colosseum itself, there are graffiti too, scratched into the stone seats, and the walls of the corridors: drawings of gladiators, and hunters, and animals, and boats, and the names of spectators, and the games they played, scratched on the stone steps, while they waited for the shows to begin, like the board for the game of merels, or for little games with nuts, or dice.

[pause]

The people who scratched them were bored, or excited, or in love, or just wanted to leave their mark. And thanks to them, we can still hear, faintly, across two thousand years, the voices of ordinary Romans, laughing, and complaining, and boasting, and loving, just as people do today.

[long-pause]

{img:roman-child-alphabet-scratched-low-wall-plaster-letters-light}
And sometimes, the graffiti are just a single word, or a name, and a date, scratched by someone, two thousand years ago, who simply wanted to say: I was here. Like the visitors to the Colosseum today, who are forbidden to scratch their names on its stones, but sometimes, still, try.

[long-pause]

{img:ludus-magnus-small-oval-training-arena-cells-around-colosseum-behind}
Next to the Colosseum, just to the east, there is the great gladiator school, the Ludus Magnus. It was built a little later, by the emperor Domitian, but there was already a school there, or nearby, in the time of Titus.

[pause]

It is a large rectangular building, with a courtyard in the middle, with its own little oval arena, a small copy of the Colosseum, with a few rows of seats around it, where the gladiators train, and where people can come and watch them practise.

[long-pause]

Around the courtyard, there are two or three storeys of small rooms, cells, where the gladiators live, and sleep, perhaps one or two in each. There are kitchens, and storerooms, and an armoury, where the weapons are kept, locked up, and a hospital, and baths.

[pause]

And there is a tunnel, underground, which connects the school to the Colosseum, so that the gladiators can walk, out of sight, from their school, to the arena, on the day of the games.

[pause]

The ruins of the Ludus Magnus can still be seen today, beside the Colosseum, with the curved seats of its little arena, below the level of the modern street.

[long-pause]

Life in the gladiator school is strict, and disciplined, like the army.

[pause]

The gladiators are guarded, because many of them are slaves, or prisoners, and might try to escape, or rebel. Everyone in Rome remembers Spartacus, the famous gladiator, who, a hundred and fifty years before, escaped from a gladiator school at Capua, with seventy of his companions, and gathered an army of slaves, and fought against Rome for two years.

[pause]

But the gladiators are also well looked after, because they are valuable. They are given good food, and doctors, and massages, and baths, and training, by the best trainers.

[pause]

{img:gladiators-eating-together-long-table-barley-porridge-beans}
They live together, train together, eat together, and become a kind of family, called a familia gladiatoria. They have their own customs, and their own gods, especially Hercules, the strong hero, and Nemesis, the goddess of fate. They form clubs, and pay into a common fund, so that, if one of them dies, his family will be given money for a proper funeral, and a gravestone.

[long-pause]

Some gladiators have wives, and children, who live outside the school, or sometimes, it seems, inside. Gravestones of gladiators, found all over the Roman Empire, were often put up by their wives, or their friends, or their fellow gladiators, with loving words.

[pause]

One says: to the spirits of the departed, and of Urbicus, a secutor, from Florence, who fought thirteen times, and lived twenty-two years. His daughter Olympias, whom he left aged five months, and Fortunensis, her slave, and Lauricia, his wife, made this, for her well-deserving husband, with whom she lived seven years.

[pause]

{img:gravestone-gladiator-relief-carved-name-family-inscription-moss}
And another, put up by a gladiator's fellow fighters, says simply: for Pinnas, from his comrades.

[long-pause]

{img:capua-gladiator-school-1st-century-bc-courtyard-men-training}
The most famous gladiator in history lived a hundred and fifty years before the Colosseum. His name was Spartacus.

[pause]

He was a Thracian, from the mountains of what is now Bulgaria. The Roman writers say that he served in the Roman army, as an auxiliary, then deserted, and was captured, and sold as a slave, and sent to a gladiator school, at Capua, near Naples, owned by a man called Lentulus Batiatus.

[long-pause]

In the year 73 before Christ, Spartacus, and about seventy of his fellow gladiators, escaped from the school, armed with knives, and spits, from the kitchen. On the road, they found wagons, carrying gladiators' weapons, and took them. And they went up onto the slopes of Mount Vesuvius, which was then a quiet, green, wooded mountain, and camped there.

[pause]

Thousands of slaves, and poor farm workers, ran away from the farms, and the estates, of southern Italy, and joined them. Spartacus became the leader of a great army, of perhaps seventy thousand, or more, slaves and free poor men, and women, and children.

[pause]

{img:vesuvius-wooded-slopes-camp-escaped-slaves-vines-ropes-legend}
The Roman army sent to besiege them, on Vesuvius, blocked the only path down the mountain. But Spartacus's followers made ropes, and ladders, from the wild vines, which grew on the mountain, and climbed down the cliffs, on the other side, at night, and attacked the Romans from behind, and won.

[long-pause]

For two years, Spartacus, and his army, marched up and down Italy, and defeated one Roman army after another. The Roman writers, who hated him, still admired him. Plutarch says that he was not only brave, and strong, but also intelligent, and gentle, more like a Greek than a barbarian.

[pause]

He wanted, it seems, to lead his followers out of Italy, over the Alps, so that they could go home, to their own countries. But many of them wanted to stay, and go on fighting.

[pause]

{img:mountain-pass-alps-distant-army-turning-back-snow-peaks}
In the end, in the year 71 before Christ, Spartacus was defeated, by the Roman general Crassus, in the south of Italy, and fell in battle. His body was never found. We will not describe what happened to his followers.

[long-pause]

But his name was never forgotten. Two thousand years later, Spartacus became a hero, a symbol of the struggle for freedom, of the oppressed against the powerful. There are books, and a famous ballet, by the Russian composer Khachaturian, and a famous film, made in 1960, with Kirk Douglas, in which, at the end, when the Romans ask the captured slaves which of them is Spartacus, they all stand up, one after another, and say: I am Spartacus.

[long-pause]

{img:dawn-gladiator-school-courtyard-trainers-wooden-post-practice}
Let us spend an ordinary day in the gladiator school, beside the Colosseum, in the spring of the year 80, a few weeks before the great games.

[pause]

The day begins at dawn, with a horn, or a bell. The gladiators get up, from their straw mattresses, in their little cells, and wash, at the fountain, in the courtyard, and eat breakfast.

[long-pause]

What do gladiators eat? In 2014, scientists studied the bones of gladiators, found in a gladiators' cemetery, at Ephesus, in Turkey, and discovered that they ate mostly barley, and beans, and very little meat. Roman writers called them hordearii, the barley men. A diet of barley and beans, with plenty of carbohydrates, may have given them a layer of fat, to protect them from cuts, in the arena.

[pause]

And the scientists found something else: the gladiators' bones had a lot of strontium, which may mean that they drank a special drink, made of plant ashes, mixed with water, or vinegar, which the Roman writer Pliny mentions, as a drink for gladiators, to strengthen their bones, and help them recover from injuries. A kind of ancient sports drink.

[pause]

After breakfast, the training begins.

[long-pause]

The trainers, called doctores, many of them former gladiators, who have won their freedom, teach the gladiators to fight, in their own particular style. Each kind of gladiator has its own trainer, and its own techniques.

[pause]

The beginners start with wooden swords, and wicker shields, which are heavier than the real ones, and practise against a wooden post, the palus, striking it, again and again, for hours, to build up their strength, and learn the movements. Then they practise in pairs, with wooden weapons, slowly at first, then faster.

[pause]

{img:pairs-practising-wooden-weapons-sand-trainer-watching-shade}
They practise footwork, and blocking, and striking, and falling, and getting up again. They run, and lift weights, and wrestle. The training is hard, and long, and repetitive, like the training of athletes, or soldiers, or dancers, today.

[long-pause]

At midday, they rest, and eat again, and bathe, and are massaged, with oil, by the masseurs, and their injuries are treated by the doctors.

[pause]

The doctors of the gladiator schools are among the best in the Roman world, because they have so much experience of injuries. A hundred years later, the great Greek doctor Galen, the most famous doctor of the ancient world, will begin his career as the doctor of the gladiators, at Pergamum, in Asia Minor, and will say that he learned more about the human body there than anywhere else. He was proud that, in his time, very few of his gladiators died of their wounds.

[pause]

{img:afternoon-gladiators-resting-shade-colonnade-dice-talking}
In the afternoon, more training. And then, in the evening, supper, and rest, and talk, and games of dice, and knucklebones, and letters home, for those who can write, and sleep, in their little cells, with the sound of the city outside, and the great dark shape of the Colosseum, rising against the stars, next door.

[long-pause]

{img:mosaic-zliten-arena-musicians-water-organ-horns-players}
The games in the Colosseum are never silent. There is always music.

[pause]

A band plays, throughout the shows: trumpets, called tubae, long and straight. Great curved horns, called cornua, shaped like a big letter G, which wrap around the player's body, with a crossbar on top, and make a deep, loud, booming sound. And the water organ, the hydraulis.

[long-pause]

The water organ is a wonderful instrument, invented in Alexandria, three hundred years before, by an engineer called Ctesibius. It has rows of bronze pipes, of different lengths, and a keyboard, and the air is pushed through the pipes by pumps, and kept at a steady pressure, by water, in a tank, underneath. It makes a loud, sweet, powerful sound, which can be heard all over the amphitheatre. It is the ancestor of the church organ.

[pause]

A mosaic, found in a villa at Zliten, in Libya, shows the music at the games: a woman playing the water organ, and two men playing the great curved horns, and another playing a trumpet, beside the arena, while the gladiators fight.

[pause]

The music follows the action, like the music in a film today: slow, and calm, at the beginning, when the gladiators are circling, and watching each other. Then faster, and louder, as the fight becomes exciting. And then a great fanfare, at the end, when the winner is announced.

[long-pause]

The trumpets also announce the beginning of the games, and the arrival of the emperor, and the entrance of the gladiators, in the grand procession, and the changes between the different parts of the show.

[pause]

And when the emperor enters his box, the whole crowd stands, and cheers, and claps, in rhythm, and chants his name, and his titles, and wishes him good luck, and long life, in great choruses, like the chants of football crowds today.

[pause]

{img:crowd-standing-cheering-emperor-arriving-box-trumpets-sunlight}
Imagine the sound of it: fifty thousand people, cheering, and chanting, and the trumpets, and the horns, and the great water organ, all echoing around the huge stone bowl of the amphitheatre, and out over the roofs of Rome, so that people far away, in the streets, could hear it, and know that the games were going on.

[long-pause]

{img:roman-surgeon-instruments-bronze-case-bandages-calm-clinic}
The doctors who looked after the gladiators were among the most skilful in the Roman world.

[pause]

Roman doctors, many of them Greeks, had a wonderful collection of instruments, made of bronze, and iron: scalpels, and probes, and forceps, and hooks, and needles, and spatulas, and little spoons, for medicines, many of them very like the instruments of a modern surgeon. Hundreds of them have been found, at Pompeii, in the house of a surgeon, and are now in the museum in Naples.

[long-pause]

They cleaned wounds, with wine, and vinegar, which kill germs, although they did not know about germs. They stitched them, with thread, and bandaged them with clean linen. They set broken bones, with splints. They used plants, as medicines: poppy juice, for pain, and willow bark, which contains the same medicine as aspirin, and honey, which helps wounds to heal.

[pause]

When scientists studied the bones of the gladiators, buried at Ephesus, they found many old injuries, which had healed well: broken bones which had been set properly, and had mended. This shows that the gladiators were looked after carefully, by good doctors, and given time to recover.

[pause]

{img:healed-bone-study-scientist-laboratory-calm-careful-light-later}
Galen, the most famous doctor of the Roman world, who began his career looking after gladiators, a hundred years later, wrote that he called the wounds of the gladiators windows into the body, through which he learned how the body works. He paid great attention to their diet, and their exercise, and their rest, and was proud that, in the four years he looked after them, very few of his gladiators died of their wounds, far fewer than before.

[long-pause]

{img:gladiator-types-murmillo-retiarius-thraex-armour-display-rack}
There are many different kinds of gladiators, each with their own armour, and weapons, and style of fighting. And each kind usually fights against one particular other kind, so that the fight is balanced, and interesting to watch.

[long-pause]

The murmillo wears a great bronze helmet, with a wide brim, and a tall crest, shaped like a fish, and carries a large, curved, rectangular shield, like a Roman soldier's, and a short sword, the gladius. He has a protective sleeve, of padded linen, or leather, on his sword arm, and a short guard on one leg. He is heavy, and strong, and slow.

[pause]

The thraex, the Thracian, wears a helmet with a high crest, topped by a griffin's head, and carries a small, square shield, and a short, curved sword, like a sickle. He has tall guards on both legs, because his shield is small. He is quick, and agile.

[pause]

{img:retiarius-net-trident-light-armour-shoulder-guard-sand}
The retiarius, the net man, is the strangest of all. He has almost no armour: no helmet, no shield, only a metal guard on his left shoulder, and a padded sleeve on his left arm. He fights with a net, which he tries to throw over his opponent, and a trident, a long, three-pronged fishing spear, and a dagger. He is the fastest, and the most mobile, of the gladiators. Because he does not wear a helmet, his face can be seen, and the crowd can recognize him.

[long-pause]

The retiarius usually fights against the secutor, the chaser, who wears a smooth, round helmet, with tiny eyeholes, so that the trident cannot catch in it, and carries a large shield, and a sword. The fight between them is like a fisherman trying to catch a fish: the net man dancing around, throwing his net, and the chaser trying to catch him, and get close.

[pause]

There are other kinds too: the hoplomachus, with a small round shield, and a spear, like a Greek soldier. The provocator, the challenger. The eques, on horseback, who fight only against each other, at the beginning of the games. The essedarius, who fights from a chariot, like the Britons. And many more.

[pause]

{img:gladiator-helmets-bronze-crests-griffin-fish-museum-light}
The armour, and the helmets, are often beautiful, decorated with gilding, and engravings, and feathers, and coloured plumes. Some of the helmets, found at Pompeii, in the gladiator barracks there, buried by Vesuvius, in the year 79, are among the most beautiful pieces of armour that survive from the ancient world.

[long-pause]

The crowd has its favourites. Some people support the gladiators with large shields, called the scutarii. Others support the gladiators with small shields, the parmularii. Like football supporters today, they argue about which is better, and cheer for their side. The emperor Titus himself was a supporter of the Thracians, the small shields, and joked with the crowd about it, at the games.

[pause]

And the gladiators have stage names, like wrestlers, or actors: Flamma, the flame. Columbus, the dove. Hermes. Spiculus, the little arrow. Triumphus. Names that the crowd shouts, and writes on the walls.

[long-pause]

{img:circus-maximus-chariot-race-four-horse-teams-colours-crowd-palatine}
The Colosseum was not the only great place of entertainment in Rome. The favourite of all, for many Romans, was the chariot racing, in the Circus Maximus.

[pause]

The Circus Maximus is even larger than the Colosseum: a great long racecourse, about six hundred metres long, in the valley between the Palatine and Aventine hills, with seats, rising up, on both sides, and at one end, for perhaps a hundred and fifty thousand spectators, or even more.

[long-pause]

In the time of the Colosseum, there are four teams, called factions, each with its own colour: the Reds, the Whites, the Blues, and the Greens. Every Roman supports one of them, passionately, from childhood, like a football club today. The emperors too: Domitian, the brother of Titus, will add two more colours, purple and gold, which do not last.

[pause]

The charioteers drive light chariots, pulled by four horses, seven times around the long, narrow track, around a central barrier, the spina, decorated with statues, and obelisks, and fountains, and the lap counters, the eggs and the dolphins.

[pause]

The charioteers wrap the reins around their waists, so that they can steer with their bodies, and carry a knife, in their belt, to cut the reins, if the chariot crashes. The races are fast, and exciting, and dangerous.

[long-pause]

The best charioteers are the greatest celebrities of Rome, far more famous, and richer, than the gladiators. In the time of the Colosseum, the most famous is Scorpus, of the Greens, who won more than two thousand races. When he passed away, in a race, at the age of twenty-six, the poet Martial wrote an epitaph for him: I am Scorpus, the glory of the noisy circus, the applause of Rome, and its short-lived darling.

[pause]

A little later, a charioteer from Spain, called Gaius Appuleius Diocles, raced for twenty-four years, and won more than one thousand four hundred races, and retired at the age of forty-two, and earned, in prize money, thirty-five million sesterces: perhaps the richest sportsman in history.

[pause]

{img:horses-winning-team-crowned-palm-wreaths-stables-evening}
The horses, too, are famous, and have names, and their own fans: Victor, and Tuscus, and Passerinus, and Hirpinus. Some of them have statues, and the people know their names, and their records, as well as the charioteers'.

[long-pause]

{img:animal-cages-port-ostia-ships-lions-elephants-keepers-unloading}
The games in the Colosseum are not only gladiators. There are also animals: thousands of them, from every part of the Roman Empire, and beyond.

[pause]

The Romans bring wild animals to Rome from all over the world: lions, and leopards, from North Africa. Tigers, from India, and Persia. Bears, from Germany, and Scotland, and the Balkans. Elephants, from Africa. Rhinoceroses, and hippopotamuses, and crocodiles, from Egypt, and Ethiopia. Giraffes, and ostriches, and zebras. Wild boar, and deer, and bulls, and wolves, and bison.

[long-pause]

Capturing and transporting these animals is an enormous business. Hunters, and soldiers, in Africa, and Asia, and Europe, catch them alive, with nets, and pits, and traps, and put them in wooden cages, and carry them, by ox cart, and by ship, across the Mediterranean, to the port of Ostia, at the mouth of the Tiber, and then up the river, to Rome.

[pause]

At Ostia, there are mosaics, on the floors of the offices of the shipping companies, showing elephants, and other animals, being loaded onto ships. In a great villa in Sicily, at Piazza Armerina, there is a wonderful long mosaic, from three hundred years later, called the Great Hunt, showing the capture and transport of wild animals, for the games: lions, and tigers, and antelopes, and ostriches, and an elephant, walking up a gangplank, onto a ship.

[pause]

{img:mosaic-great-hunt-elephant-gangplank-ship-tigers-cages}
In Rome, the animals are kept in enclosures, called vivaria, outside the city walls, near the Praenestine gate, and looked after by keepers, and trainers, until the day of the games.

[long-pause]

Some of the animals are trained to do tricks. The Romans love to see elephants walking on tightropes, or writing letters in the sand with their trunks, or kneeling before the emperor. Lions trained to catch hares, and carry them gently in their mouths, without harming them. Bears that dance. Bulls that let boys stand on their backs.

[pause]

The poet Martial, who was at the opening games of the Colosseum, in the year 80, and wrote a little book of poems about them, says that an elephant, which had just won a fight against a bull, knelt down, of its own accord, before the emperor Titus, as if it understood that he was a god.

[pause]

{img:trained-elephant-kneeling-arena-sand-crowd-emperor-box-sunlight}
There are also hunts, called venationes, in the morning, where trained hunters, called venatores, and bestiarii, fight with wild animals, with spears, and bows, in the arena. Sometimes, the arena is decorated like a forest, or a mountain, with real trees, and rocks, and bushes, which rise up out of trapdoors in the floor, so that the hunt looks like a real hunt, in the wild.

[long-pause]

We will not watch the hunts. But it is sad to think of the thousands of animals, taken from their homes, all over the world, and brought to Rome. In the following centuries, so many animals were captured for the games that some of them disappeared from parts of the Roman Empire: the elephants of North Africa, and the lions of Greece, and the hippopotamuses of the lower Nile.

[long-pause]

{img:animal-keeper-feeding-lion-enclosure-vivarium-outside-rome-calm}
For every animal brought to Rome for the games, there are people who look after it: the keepers, and the trainers, who live with the animals, in the enclosures, outside the city, and feed them, and water them, and care for them, sometimes for months, before the games.

[long-pause]

These keepers are skilled people, who know the animals well: how to feed them, how to calm them, how to move them, how to treat them when they are ill. Many of them come from the same lands as the animals: Africans, who know lions, and leopards, and elephants, and Indians, who know tigers, and Egyptians, who know crocodiles, and hippopotamuses.

[pause]

Some of them become very fond of their animals. There are stories, from ancient writers, of lions who loved their keepers, and of elephants who recognized their old trainers, years later, in the crowd.

[pause]

The most famous story is told by the Roman writer Aulus Gellius, who says he heard it from a man who saw it, in Rome. A runaway slave, called Androcles, in Africa, hid in a cave, and a lion came in, limping, with a great thorn in its paw. Androcles, very frightened, pulled out the thorn, and the lion became his friend, and lived with him, and brought him food, for three years.

[long-pause]

Later, Androcles was captured, and sent to Rome, to be thrown to the beasts, in the arena. And the lion, too, had been captured, and sent to Rome. And when the lion was let out into the arena, it ran toward Androcles, and then stopped, and recognized him, and lay down at his feet, wagging its tail, like a dog, and licked his hands.

[pause]

The crowd was amazed, and the emperor asked Androcles to explain. And when he told the story, the crowd shouted for him to be set free, and to be given the lion. And afterward, Gellius says, Androcles walked through the streets of Rome, with the lion on a light leash, and people gave him money, and said: this is the lion who was a man's friend, and this is the man who was a lion's doctor.

[pause]

{img:man-walking-lion-on-leash-roman-street-crowd-wonder-calm}
It is probably only a story. But it shows that the Romans, too, could be moved by kindness, and friendship, between people and animals.

[long-pause]

{img:opening-games-80ad-procession-arena-banners-emperor-box-crowd}
In the summer of the year 80, the emperor Titus opens the Flavian Amphitheatre, with games that last for a hundred days.

[pause]

People come to Rome from all over the empire, and beyond, to see them. The poet Martial, in his little book about the games, says that there was no people so far away, or so barbarous, that it did not send someone to Rome, to see them: farmers from the mountains of Thrace, and Sarmatians, who drink the blood of their horses, and people from the source of the Nile, and the shores of the Atlantic, and Arabs, and Sabaeans, and Cilicians, sprinkled with saffron, and Sygambrians, with their hair tied in knots, and Ethiopians, with their curly hair. All speaking different languages, Martial says, and yet all speaking with one voice, when they called Titus the father of his country.

[long-pause]

The games are the greatest ever given in Rome, up to that time. The historian Cassius Dio says that nine thousand animals, tame and wild, were shown, during the hundred days. Women took part in the hunts. There were gladiator fights, and animal hunts, and shows, and even, it is said, a naval battle.

[pause]

Because, at the very beginning, before the underground passages were built, the arena of the Colosseum could be flooded with water, through pipes, and channels, and drained again. And the Romans put on a show of a sea battle, with real ships, small ones, rowed by men, re-enacting an ancient battle, between the Corinthians and the Corcyraeans, in the arena, filled with water, and horses and bulls, trained to swim.

[pause]

{img:flooded-arena-small-ships-water-show-crowd-amazed-distant}
Scholars still argue about whether this really happened in the Colosseum, or in another place, nearby. But Martial, and Suetonius, and Cassius Dio, all describe water shows, at the opening games.

[long-pause]

On the last day of the games, Suetonius says, Titus wept openly, in front of the people. Perhaps he was tired. Perhaps he knew that he was not well. A year later, he was dead.

[pause]

Martial wrote, in his book of the games, that the Colosseum was the greatest wonder of the world, greater than the pyramids of Egypt, and the walls of Babylon, and the temple of Artemis at Ephesus, and the Mausoleum of Halicarnassus, and all the other wonders of the past: let barbarous Memphis cease to speak of the wonders of its pyramids, he wrote. Every work gives way to Caesar's amphitheatre. Fame will speak of one work instead of all.

[pause]

{img:crowd-leaving-colosseum-evening-arches-lamps-streets-filling}
And in the evenings, during the hundred days, the streets around the Colosseum were full of people: going home, after the games, talking, and laughing, and arguing about the gladiators, and the animals, and the shows, and stopping at the taverns, and the food stalls, and the shops, in the warm summer night.

[long-pause]

{img:poet-martial-small-room-rome-writing-tablets-third-floor-window}
One of the people who watched the opening games of the Colosseum, and wrote about them, was a poet called Marcus Valerius Martialis. We call him Martial.

[pause]

He was born in Spain, around the year 40, in a little town, called Bilbilis, in the mountains of Aragon. He came to Rome, as a young man, to make his fortune as a poet, and lived there for thirty-five years.

[long-pause]

He was not rich. He lived, for many years, in a little apartment, on the third floor of a building, on the Quirinal Hill, up a long flight of stairs, and complained about it, often, in his poems: about the noise, and the stairs, and the cold, and the rent, and the bakers, and the schoolteachers, and the money changers, who kept him awake, from dawn, with their noise.

[pause]

He wrote epigrams: short, witty, clever poems, of a few lines, about everything: his friends, and his enemies, and his patrons, and the people of Rome, and their habits, and their vices, and their meals, and their houses, and their slaves, and their dinner parties, and the gladiators, and the games. More than fifteen hundred of them survive.

[pause]

His first book was the Book of Spectacles, a little collection of about thirty poems, about the opening games of the Colosseum, in the year 80, written for the emperor Titus. It is one of the main sources we have for those games.

[long-pause]

Martial is funny, and sharp, and sometimes rude, and sometimes very tender. He wrote a beautiful little poem, for a little slave girl, called Erotion, who passed away, at the age of almost six, asking the earth to lie lightly on her, because she had been so light on it.

[pause]

He wrote about the simple life that he longed for: a little house in the country, a good fire, a quiet wife, sleep that makes the night short, and a mind at peace.

[pause]

{img:spanish-hill-town-countryside-vines-old-poet-garden-peace}
In the end, he got it. After thirty-five years in Rome, tired, and older, he went home, to Spain, to his little town of Bilbilis, where a kind lady gave him a little house, and a garden, and a farm. And there, he wrote, he slept as long as he liked, and did not have to put on a toga, and did not hear the noise of Rome any more. He passed away there, around the year 104.

[long-pause]

{img:spectators-arriving-dawn-colosseum-cushions-food-baskets-tickets}
Let us spend a day at the games in the Colosseum, in the summer of the year 80, as an ordinary Roman citizen.

[pause]

He is a baker, perhaps, from the Subura, the crowded neighbourhood behind the Forum. He has a ticket, a little piece of bone, given to him free, because the games are free for the citizens of Rome. It is paid for by the emperor. He gets up before dawn, and puts on his white toga, which he must wear, at the games, and goes, with his friends, and his young son, through the streets, in the grey light, to the Colosseum.

[long-pause]

The streets around the amphitheatre are already full of people: thousands of them, streaming toward the building, from every direction. There are sellers of food, and drink, and cushions, to sit on, because the stone seats are hard, and souvenirs, little clay lamps, and cups, and figures of gladiators, and programmes, listing the pairs of gladiators who will fight that day, called libelli.

[pause]

They find their entrance, number fifty-three, perhaps, by the number carved above the arch, and go in, and up the stairs, and along the corridors, to their section, and their row, and their seats, high up, among the ordinary citizens.

[pause]

From there, they can see the whole amphitheatre: the great oval of seats, filling with people, in their white togas, and the arena, far below, with its yellow sand, gleaming in the morning sun. And the emperor's box, in the middle of one side, decorated with purple cloth.

[long-pause]

The day has a programme, like a show today.

[pause]

In the morning, there are the animal shows: the trained animals, doing their tricks, and then the hunts. At midday, while many people go out to eat, and rest, there are lesser shows: comedians, and acrobats, and jugglers, and clowns, and mock fights, with wooden weapons, and the punishment of criminals, which we will not watch. Our baker takes his son out, to buy some bread, and sausages, and wine, at a stall outside.

[pause]

And in the afternoon, the main event: the gladiators.

[pause]

{img:pompa-procession-gladiators-entering-arena-musicians-trumpets}
The afternoon begins with a grand procession, called the pompa: the gladiators, in purple and gold cloaks, march into the arena, with music, trumpets, and horns, and a water organ, and walk around the arena, and salute the emperor, and the crowd cheers.

[long-pause]

Then the referees check the weapons. And the fights begin, one pair at a time, to the music of the band, which plays louder, and faster, as the fight becomes exciting, like the music in a film.

[pause]

We will stay high up, with our baker, far from the arena, and watch only from a distance: the tiny figures, on the yellow sand, moving, circling, and the referee, in his white tunic, with his long stick, watching closely, and separating them, when necessary. And the crowd, shouting, and cheering, and groaning, and calling out the names of their favourites.

[pause]

{img:referee-white-tunic-stick-arena-distant-figures-crowd-roar}
At the end of each fight, the winner receives a palm branch, and a prize of money, on a silver plate, and runs around the arena, waving the palm, while the crowd cheers. And the loser, if he has fought well, is usually sent away, alive, to the cheers of the crowd, who shout: missum, let him go.

[long-pause]

In the evening, the games end. The crowd pours out, through the eighty arches, into the streets, in a few minutes. And our baker, and his friends, and his son, walk home, through the streets of Rome, in the evening light, talking about the gladiators, and the animals, and the emperor, and stop at a tavern, for a cup of wine, before going home, tired, and hoarse, from shouting, to bed.

[long-pause]

{img:street-food-stall-outside-colosseum-sausages-bread-chickpeas-crowd}
What do the Romans eat, at the games?

[pause]

The games last all day, from morning until evening. And the spectators get hungry. So they bring food with them, in baskets, and bags, and buy food from the sellers, who walk up and down the rows of seats, and from the stalls, around the outside of the amphitheatre.

[long-pause]

The archaeologists, digging in the drains of the Colosseum, have found what the spectators ate, two thousand years ago, because they threw their leftovers on the floor, and they were washed down into the drains.

[pause]

They found the seeds, and stones, of olives, and figs, and grapes, and melons, and peaches, and plums, and cherries. Nuts: walnuts, and hazelnuts, and pine nuts, and almonds. The bones of chickens, and pigs. The shells of mussels, and oysters, and snails. And the bones of bears, and lions, and other animals, from the games, which may have been cooked, and given to the poor, afterward.

[pause]

The sellers sell hot sausages, and bread, and cheese, and boiled chickpeas, and roasted nuts, and honey cakes, and fruit, and pastries, and wine, mixed with water, and spiced wine, warm, in winter.

[long-pause]

And sometimes, the emperor gives food to the crowd, at the games: baskets of bread, and meat, and cakes, and fruit, and wine, handed out, along the rows, by servants. The emperor Domitian, a few years later, will give a great banquet, in the Colosseum, for the whole crowd, with sweet cakes, and dates, and figs, and nuts, and wine, thrown down to the people, from ropes, stretched over the seats, like a rain of food. And the crowd will shout with joy.

[pause]

The poet Statius describes it: a whole day of feasting, in the amphitheatre, rich and poor, men and women, children, and senators, all eating together, at the emperor's expense, as if the whole city were one great family, at one great table.

[pause]

{img:sweets-dates-figs-raining-down-from-ropes-crowd-reaching-joy}
So, for the ordinary people of Rome, a day at the games was not only a day of shows. It was also a day out, a picnic, a festival, with friends, and family, and food, and wine, and the sunshine, and the shade of the great awning, and the noise, and the music, and the excitement.

[long-pause]

{img:two-gladiators-facing-each-other-arena-afternoon-respect-distant}
The most famous gladiator fight of the opening games of the Colosseum was described by the poet Martial, who saw it. It was between two gladiators, called Priscus, and Verus.

[pause]

They were well matched: equal in strength, and skill, and courage. They fought for a long time, in the arena, in front of the emperor Titus, and the whole of Rome, and neither could win.

[long-pause]

The crowd, watching, began to shout for both of them to be given their freedom. But Titus followed the rule of the games: the fight must go on, until one of them raised his finger, to ask for mercy.

[pause]

So they went on fighting, for a long time. And still, neither of them gave way.

[pause]

And then, at last, Martial says, both of them, at the same moment, raised their fingers, to ask for mercy. They had both fought so bravely, and so equally, that neither would give up first, and neither could win.

[pause]

And Titus, delighted, sent them both gifts. And then, he did something that had never been done before: he gave them both the wooden sword, the rudis, the sign of freedom. Both of them were set free, on the same day, together.

[long-pause]

Martial wrote: this has happened under no prince but you, Caesar: two fought, and two won.

[pause]

It is a story that shows the best side of the games, as the Romans saw it: courage, and skill, and honour, and the mercy of a good emperor. And it was told, and retold, for many years afterward.

[pause]

We do not know what became of Priscus and Verus after that. Perhaps they became trainers, in a gladiator school. Perhaps they went home, to wherever they came from, to their families. Perhaps they opened a tavern, as some retired gladiators did, and told stories, to their customers, of the great day when they fought before the emperor, at the opening of the Colosseum, and both won.

[long-pause]

{img:martial-reading-poem-friends-evening-lamp-scroll-colosseum-book}
Martial's little poem about them is still read today, nearly two thousand years later. It is one of the few times, in all of ancient literature, that two ordinary gladiators are remembered by name, not because they won, or lost, but because they were equal, and both were spared, on a summer afternoon, in the new amphitheatre, by an emperor who wanted to be kind.

[long-pause]

{img:roman-women-upper-seats-colosseum-wooden-gallery-shade-talking}
Women are an important part of the life around the Colosseum, in many ways.

[pause]

In the seats, they sit at the top, in the wooden gallery, under the colonnade, separately from the men, by the law of Augustus. Only the Vestal Virgins, and the women of the emperor's family, sit in front, near the arena.

[long-pause]

But women are passionate fans of the games, and of the gladiators. On the walls of Pompeii, in the gladiator barracks there, there are graffiti, scratched by gladiators, or their admirers: Celadus, the Thracian, the sigh of the girls. And: Crescens, the net fighter, doctor of the night-time girls. The gladiators were the heartthrobs of their time.

[pause]

The Roman satirist Juvenal, a few years later, tells a story, perhaps exaggerated, of a senator's wife, Eppia, who left her husband, and her children, and her comfortable life in Rome, and ran away, to Egypt, with a gladiator called Sergius, who was not even handsome, Juvenal says, with a scarred face, and a bad eye, and a lump on his nose. But he was a gladiator. And that was enough.

[pause]

{img:pompeii-wall-graffiti-gladiator-names-scratched-plaster}
And there were women gladiators.

[long-pause]

At the opening games of the Colosseum, Cassius Dio says, women took part in the animal hunts. And at other games, in the following years, women fought as gladiators, in the arena. The emperor Domitian, the brother of Titus, held games at night, by torchlight, with women gladiators.

[pause]

There is a stone relief, found at Halicarnassus, in Turkey, now in the British Museum, which shows two women gladiators, fighting each other, with shields, and swords, without helmets, with their stage names carved above them: Amazon, and Achillia. And an inscription says that both were released, honourably, after a fight that ended in a draw.

[pause]

{img:stone-relief-two-women-gladiators-amazon-achillia-museum-light}
Many Romans disapproved of women gladiators, and in the year 200, the emperor Septimius Severus banned them. But for more than a century, there were some women who fought in the arenas of the Roman world.

[long-pause]

And there were women in all the other jobs around the games: selling food, and drink, and cushions, and souvenirs, outside the Colosseum. Running the taverns, and the inns, and the cook shops, where the crowds ate, before and after the games. Weaving the cloth for the awning, and the costumes, and the banners. And, in the households of the gladiators, wives, and mothers, and daughters, waiting at home, after every fight, for news.

[long-pause]

{img:roman-children-playing-gladiators-wooden-swords-street-laughing}
The children of Rome, in the year 80, love the games, like everyone else.

[pause]

They play at being gladiators, in the streets, and the courtyards, with wooden swords, and little shields, and nets, and shout the names of the famous fighters, and pretend to be the emperor, raising their thumbs, and giving freedom to the winners. They collect little clay figures of gladiators, and lamps, with pictures of gladiators, and draw gladiators on the walls, with charcoal.

[long-pause]

Roman children have many toys: dolls, of clay, or wood, or bone, with jointed arms and legs, which can be dressed in little clothes. Little wooden horses, on wheels. Little carts, pulled by mice, or goats. Hoops, and spinning tops, and balls, made of leather, stuffed with feathers, or pig's bladders, blown up, like balloons. Rattles, for babies. Little pottery animals. Kites. Swings. Board games, scratched on stone.

[pause]

They play hide and seek, and blind man's buff, and leapfrog, and odds and evens, and a game with nuts, rolling them down a board, at a little pile of nuts, to knock them over, and win them. Nuts are so important to Roman children that, when a boy grows up, people say he has left his nuts behind.

[pause]

{img:roman-girl-doll-jointed-clay-courtyard-mother-spinning-wool}
Children of rich families go to school, from about the age of seven, with a slave, the paedagogus, to carry their books, and look after them. They learn to read, and write, and count, on wax tablets, and later, Greek, and Latin literature, and speaking. The poor children work, with their parents, in the shops, and the workshops, and the markets, from a young age.

[long-pause]

At the games, children sit with their families, high up in the seats, and watch, and cheer, and eat, and fall asleep, in the afternoon heat, in their mothers' laps.

[pause]

And a boy, who sat with his father, high up in the Colosseum, at the opening games, in the year 80, perhaps at the age of eight, might have lived long enough to see the emperor Hadrian build the Pantheon, fifty years later, and to tell his grandchildren, as an old man, by the fire, about the great day when the Colosseum first opened, and the two gladiators, Priscus and Verus, both won their freedom.

[long-pause]

{img:colosseum-valley-meta-sudans-fountain-colossus-baths-of-titus-arch}
Let us walk around the outside of the Colosseum, in the year 80, and look at the neighbourhood.

[pause]

The amphitheatre stands in a valley, between the Palatine Hill, where the emperors live, the Caelian Hill, and the Esquiline Hill. Around it, there is a wide paved square, of travertine, with a row of tall stone posts, around the building, which may have been used to hold the ropes of the awning, or to control the crowds.

[long-pause]

To the west, toward the Forum, there is a great fountain, called the Meta Sudans, the sweating turning post: a tall cone of brick and marble, shaped like the turning posts of the chariot racecourse, from which water bubbles out at the top, and runs down the sides, into a round basin. Travellers stop there, to drink, and wash their faces, and rest in the cool spray.

[pause]

Beside it, there is Nero's giant statue, the Colossus, now with the head of the sun god, shining in the sun, thirty metres tall, with its bronze rays.

[pause]

{img:baths-of-titus-steps-colonnade-bathers-entering-afternoon-sun}
On the slope of the Esquiline Hill, to the north east, Titus has built new public baths, the Baths of Titus, on part of the land of Nero's Golden House, and opened them at the same time as the Colosseum. The people can go there, after the games, to bathe, and exercise, and relax. Free, or for a tiny fee.

[long-pause]

And on the slope of the Palatine, along the Sacred Way, leading into the Forum, there will soon be a great arch of white marble, the Arch of Titus, built by his brother Domitian, after his death, to celebrate his victory in Judaea, and the capture of Jerusalem.

[pause]

On the inside of the arch, there are carvings: Titus, in his triumphal chariot, crowned by the goddess Victory. And the triumphal procession of the year 71, carrying the treasures of the temple of Jerusalem through the streets of Rome: the great golden lampstand, the menorah, with its seven branches, and the silver trumpets, and the golden table.

[pause]

{img:arch-of-titus-white-marble-relief-procession-menorah-sacred-way}
The Arch of Titus still stands today, and the carving of the menorah can still be seen, and has become one of the symbols of the Jewish people, and of the state of Israel. For many centuries, Jews living in Rome would not walk under the arch, out of sorrow.

[long-pause]

All around the Colosseum, there are streets, and houses, and shops, and taverns, and workshops, and the barracks of the sailors who work the awning, and the gladiator schools, and the storerooms for the scenery, and the weapons, and the hospital for the wounded gladiators, and the mortuary, and the offices of the people who organize the games.

[pause]

On the days of the games, the whole neighbourhood is full of life, and noise, and crowds. And on the other days, it is quieter, with the sound of the gladiators training, in the schools, and the animals roaring, faintly, in their enclosures, and the sailors mending the canvas of the awning, in the sunshine.

[long-pause]

{img:baths-of-titus-grand-hall-marble-pools-bathers-light-steam}
After the games, many Romans go to the baths.

[pause]

The new Baths of Titus, opened at the same time as the Colosseum, on the slope of the Esquiline Hill, just above it, are large, and splendid: great halls, with high, vaulted ceilings, and walls of coloured marble, and floors of mosaic, and pools, and fountains, and statues, and gardens, and exercise yards, and libraries, and rooms for massage, and for talking.

[long-pause]

The Romans go to the baths every day, if they can, in the afternoon, after work, before dinner. It costs almost nothing: the smallest coin, a quadrans. Children go free. And the emperors, sometimes, pay for everyone, on special days.

[pause]

First, the bathers undress, in the changing room, and leave their clothes in little niches in the wall, sometimes with a slave to guard them. Then they exercise, in the courtyard: running, wrestling, lifting weights, or playing ball games, with a leather ball, or a heavy ball, filled with sand.

[pause]

Then they go into the warm room, and then the hot room, full of steam, heated by hot air, flowing under the floors, and inside the walls, from great furnaces, stoked by slaves, below. They sweat, and scrape their skin with a curved metal tool, a strigil, and oil. Then they plunge into the cold pool, in the cold room. And then they swim, in the great open-air pool, the natatio, or sit, and talk, and relax.

[long-pause]

The baths are a place to meet friends, and gossip, and do business, and hear the news, and the latest jokes, and talk about the games, and the gladiators. Sellers walk around, crying their wares: sausages, and cakes, and drinks. Barbers pluck hair. Masseurs slap and knead. Poets recite their verses, to anyone who will listen.

[pause]

The philosopher Seneca, who lived above a bath house, complained about the noise: the grunting of the weightlifters, the slapping of the masseurs, the shouts of the ball players, the cries of the sausage sellers, and the man who loved the sound of his own voice, singing in the bath.

[pause]

{img:bathers-relaxing-pool-edge-marble-statues-evening-glow}
And then, clean, and refreshed, the Romans go home, to dinner, in the evening, as the lamps are lit, and the streets fill with people, going home, from the baths, and the games, and their work, in the warm summer evening.

[long-pause]

{img:hypogeum-underground-passages-brick-walls-lifts-cages-torchlight}
A few years after the opening, the emperor Domitian, the brother of Titus, made a great change to the Colosseum.

[pause]

He built, under the floor of the arena, a great underground world, called the hypogeum: two levels of tunnels, and passages, and rooms, and cages, and machines, under the wooden floor of the arena, covered with sand. After that, the arena could no longer be flooded for sea battles. But it became a stage for spectacular shows.

[long-pause]

In the hypogeum, there are long corridors, of brick, and stone, running the length of the arena, with rooms on either side, where the gladiators wait, before their fights, and the animals are kept, in cages, and the scenery is stored.

[pause]

And there are lifts: about sixty of them, wooden cages, raised and lowered by ropes, and pulleys, and capstans, great wooden wheels, turned by teams of workers, or slaves, pushing on wooden bars. The lifts carry animals, and gladiators, and scenery, up from the underground, to trapdoors, in the floor of the arena, which open suddenly, so that, to the amazement of the crowd, a lion, or a bear, or a whole forest, or a mountain, appears out of nowhere, in the middle of the arena.

[pause]

{img:wooden-lift-capstan-workers-rope-pulley-cage-underground}
In 2015, a team of archaeologists and engineers built a full-size working copy of one of these lifts, and tested it, in the Colosseum, and lifted a wolf, safely, up into the arena, to show how it worked.

[long-pause]

The hypogeum was a busy, dark, crowded, noisy place, on the days of the games, lit by torches, and oil lamps, full of people working: the stage hands, and the animal keepers, and the carpenters, and the engineers, and the men turning the capstans, hundreds of them, all working together, to make the show run smoothly, like the backstage of a great theatre.

[pause]

Today, the floor of the arena is gone, and you can look down into the hypogeum, from above, and see the maze of brick walls, and passages, and the grooves where the lifts once ran. And in recent years, part of the floor has been rebuilt, of wood, so that visitors can stand on it, and imagine what the gladiators saw, as they came out into the arena, and looked up at the fifty thousand faces, rising all around them.

[pause]

{img:reconstructed-arena-floor-wooden-visitors-standing-looking-up-tiers}
The underground passages also connected the Colosseum to the gladiator school, the Ludus Magnus, next door, and to the stores, and the barracks, so that everything could be moved, in and out, without the crowd seeing it.

[long-pause]

{img:colosseum-4th-century-quieter-arena-fewer-crowds-christian-rome}
The games in the Colosseum went on for more than four hundred years.

[pause]

Emperor after emperor gave great games there: Trajan, after his victories in Dacia, in the year 107, gave games that lasted a hundred and twenty-three days, with ten thousand gladiators, and eleven thousand animals. The emperor Commodus, the son of the wise Marcus Aurelius, even fought in the arena himself, as a gladiator, which shocked the Romans.

[long-pause]

But slowly, over the centuries, things changed.

[pause]

The Roman Empire became Christian, in the fourth century, under the emperor Constantine, and his successors. The Christians disapproved of the gladiator games. Christian writers, like Augustine, and Tertullian, wrote against them. The games became more expensive, and harder to pay for, as the empire grew poorer, and was attacked by enemies.

[pause]

There is a story, told by a Christian historian, Theodoret, about a monk called Telemachus, from the East, who came to Rome, in the year 404, and, during a gladiator fight in the arena, ran down onto the sand, and tried to separate the gladiators, and begged them to stop. The crowd, angry, threw stones at him. And the emperor Honorius, hearing of it, was so moved, that he banned the gladiator games forever.

[pause]

We do not know if the story is true. But it seems that the gladiator fights did end, around that time, at the beginning of the fifth century.

[long-pause]

The animal hunts went on a little longer. The last recorded hunt in the Colosseum was in the year 523, under the Gothic king Theodoric, who ruled Italy after the fall of the Western Roman Empire.

[pause]

After that, the Colosseum was silent. The great amphitheatre, which had once held fifty thousand cheering people, stood empty, in the middle of a Rome which had shrunk, from a million people, to perhaps thirty thousand, living among the ruins.

[pause]

{img:empty-colosseum-grass-growing-arena-6th-century-quiet-ruin}
Grass grew in the arena. Trees took root in the seats. Birds nested in the arches. And the people of Rome began to use the great building for other things.

[long-pause]

{img:shepherd-goats-grazing-ancient-ruins-rome-middle-ages-quiet}
In the early Middle Ages, shepherds grazed their goats and sheep among the ruins of ancient Rome, in the old Forum, which they called the Campo Vaccino, the cow field. And the great amphitheatre stood above them, silent, enormous, and mysterious, a building from another age, which nobody could have built any more, and which people began to say had been built by giants, or by magic, or that it had once been a temple of the sun.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-colosseum-houses-workshops-inside-arches-fortress-towers}
In the Middle Ages, the Colosseum became a strange kind of village.

[pause]

People built houses, and workshops, and stables, inside the arches, and under the vaults, of the old amphitheatre. There were blacksmiths, and shoemakers, and carpenters, living and working there. There was a little church, built into the building. In the twelfth century, a powerful Roman family, the Frangipani, turned it into a fortress, with walls, and towers.

[long-pause]

Then, in the year 1349, a great earthquake struck Rome, and the outer wall, on the south side, collapsed, and fell, in a great heap of stone. That is why, today, the Colosseum has a complete outer wall only on the north side, and the south side is lower.

[pause]

For the next four hundred years, the Colosseum was used as a quarry. The Romans took its stones, the great blocks of travertine, which had fallen, and many more, which they pulled down, to build new buildings: palaces, and churches, and bridges. Much of the stone of Saint Peter's Basilica, and the Palazzo Venezia, and other great buildings of Renaissance Rome, came from the Colosseum. They dug out the iron clamps, too, leaving the holes.

[pause]

{img:renaissance-carts-carrying-stones-from-colosseum-builders-1500s}
And then, in the eighteenth century, the popes decided to protect it. In 1749, Pope Benedict the Fourteenth declared the Colosseum a sacred place, in memory of the early Christian martyrs, who, it was believed, had died there, and set up the Stations of the Cross, in the arena, and a great cross. Historians today are not sure how many Christians were really killed in the Colosseum itself. But the decision saved the building from being taken apart completely.

[long-pause]

In the nineteenth century, the popes, and later the government of Italy, repaired the Colosseum, and built great brick buttresses, at each end of the surviving wall, to hold it up. And travellers, and writers, and artists, from all over the world, came to see it, especially by moonlight, which was thought to be the most romantic way to see it.

[pause]

The English poet Lord Byron, in 1817, walked in the Colosseum by moonlight, and wrote about it. The American writer Mark Twain visited it, and wrote that it was the monarch of all European ruins. And the botanists found that more than four hundred different kinds of plants were growing in the ruins: flowers, and grasses, and trees, some of them rare, whose seeds, people liked to believe, had come from distant lands, in the fur, and the feed, of the animals brought for the games.

[pause]

{img:moonlit-colosseum-19th-century-travellers-lanterns-romantic-ruins}
Most of the plants were cleared away, later, to protect the stones. But in the spring, there are still wild flowers, growing in the cracks, and on the high ledges, of the Colosseum.

[long-pause]

{img:film-set-style-reconstructed-colosseum-arena-crowd-sunlight-later}
The Colosseum, and the gladiators, have fascinated people ever since the games ended, and especially in the last hundred years, in books, and paintings, and films.

[pause]

In the nineteenth century, the French painter Jean-Léon Gérôme painted famous pictures of the games, which showed the gladiators saluting the emperor, and the crowd, with their thumbs, and gave people the idea, which has lasted ever since, that the crowd decided the fate of a defeated gladiator by turning their thumbs up, or down. In fact, historians are not sure what gesture the Romans really used. Perhaps a thumb hidden in the fist meant mercy, and an outstretched thumb meant the opposite. Nobody knows for certain.

[long-pause]

The famous words, which, people say, the gladiators spoke to the emperor, before they fought: Hail, emperor, those who are about to die salute you, are recorded only once, in Suetonius, and not by gladiators at all, but by prisoners, condemned to fight in a sea battle, on a lake, for the emperor Claudius. It was probably not a regular custom.

[pause]

In 1960, there was the film Spartacus. And in 2000, the film Gladiator, directed by Ridley Scott, with Russell Crowe as a Roman general, who becomes a gladiator, and fights in the Colosseum, which was rebuilt, on film, with computers, to look as it did when it was new. The film was enormously popular, and won many prizes, and made millions of people curious about ancient Rome, and the Colosseum.

[pause]

{img:tourists-gladiator-costumes-outside-colosseum-photographs-sunny}
In 2024, there was a second film, Gladiator II, which even showed a sea battle, in the flooded arena of the Colosseum, with sharks, which is certainly not true to history, but shows how the old stories of the opening games still capture people's imagination.

[long-pause]

Today, outside the Colosseum, men dressed as gladiators, and Roman soldiers, in plastic armour, and red cloaks, pose for photographs with tourists, for a few coins, as people dressed as gladiators may have done, in some way, two thousand years ago, on the same square, selling souvenirs, and little clay figures, to the crowds on their way to the games.

Let us end with a summer evening, in the year 80, during the hundred days of the opening games.

[pause]

The games are over for the day. The crowds have gone home, through the eighty arches, into the streets. The amphitheatre is quiet now, empty, enormous, in the soft evening light, with the last of the sun shining on its upper storeys, and the statues in its arches, and the masts of the awning, black against the pink sky.

[long-pause]

The sailors have rolled up the great awning, and climbed down, and gone back to their barracks, nearby, to eat, and rest. The workers are sweeping the corridors, and the seats, and raking the sand of the arena, smooth again, for tomorrow. The gladiators who fought today are back in their school, next door, eating their supper, and having their wounds treated, and talking quietly, about the day.

[pause]

In the animal enclosures, outside the walls, the keepers are feeding the animals, and the elephants are drinking, and the lions are lying, quietly, in the cool of the evening.

[pause]

{img:colosseum-dusk-empty-arena-raked-sand-pink-sky-swallows}
And in the streets around the Colosseum, the people of Rome are walking, slowly, in the warm evening air, to the baths, and the taverns, and home, talking about the games, and the gladiators, and the emperor, while the swallows swoop and circle, around the high arches, and the first lamps are lit, in the windows of the apartment buildings, and the sky turns from pink, to violet, to deep blue, and the first stars come out, over the great, quiet amphitheatre, and the sleeping city.

[long-pause]

{img:colosseum-today-rome-blue-sky-crowds-via-dei-fori-imperiali}
Today, the Colosseum is the most visited monument in Italy, and one of the most famous buildings in the world.

[pause]

Every year, more than seven million people come to see it, from every country on earth: more people, every year, than ever saw it in all the centuries of the games. In 2007, it was chosen as one of the New Seven Wonders of the World.

[long-pause]

You approach it along a wide avenue, from the Forum, or come up out of the underground station, called Colosseo, right in front of it, and there it is, suddenly, enormous, rising above you, with its great arches, and its broken outer wall, glowing gold, in the sunshine.

[pause]

Inside, you can walk through the corridors, where the crowds once streamed, and climb the stairs, to the upper levels, and look down at the arena, with its rebuilt wooden floor, at one end, and the maze of the hypogeum, open to the sky, below. You can see the remains of the seats, and the marble seats of the senators, with their names. You can see the numbers, carved above the entrance arches: twenty-three, twenty-four, twenty-five, fifty-two, fifty-three.

[pause]

In the corridors, there is a little museum, with things found in the Colosseum, and its drains: coins, and pottery, and the bones of animals, and the remains of the food that people ate, at the games, two thousand years ago: olives, and peaches, and grapes, and figs, and nuts, and chicken bones, and the shells of mussels.

[long-pause]

Every year, on Good Friday, the Pope leads the Way of the Cross, at night, by torchlight, at the Colosseum, with thousands of people, in memory of the early Christians.

[pause]

And at night, the Colosseum is lit up, golden, against the dark sky. Since 2000, every time a country in the world abolishes the death penalty, or a person condemned to death is spared, the lights of the Colosseum are changed, from white, to gold, for a night, as a sign of hope, and life.

[pause]

Beside it, the ruins of the Ludus Magnus, the gladiator school, can still be seen, below the level of the street, with the curved rows of seats, of its little practice arena. The Arch of Titus still stands, on the Sacred Way. The remains of the Meta Sudans fountain, and the base of the Colossus, are marked on the ground, near the Arch of Constantine.
