The Romans loved to write on walls.

All over the Roman world, on houses, shops, taverns and public buildings, even on the Colosseum itself, people scratched messages, drawings, jokes and names. They used nails or knives, or wrote with charcoal or paint. These scratches are called graffiti, from an Italian word.

--

In Pompeii, where the volcano preserved the walls, more than eleven thousand pieces of graffiti and painted signs have been found. Many are about gladiators.

There are drawings of gladiators fighting, with their names and how many fights they had won. There are painted adverts for the games. Something like: the gladiators of so-and-so will fight here on the sixteenth of May. There will be a hunt, and there will be awnings.

{img:painted-advertisement-red-letters-wall-pompeii-games-notice}
And there are messages that have nothing to do with gladiators. They tell us about the everyday lives of ordinary Romans. Love notes and insults, jokes, shopping lists and complaints.

One says: a weaver loves the innkeeper's slave girl, but she does not care for him. Another says: I am amazed, wall, that you have not fallen down under the weight of so many boring writings. Another, at an inn, is a sheepish apology to the innkeeper. And one is a child's alphabet, scratched low on the wall, at a child's height: A, B, C, D.

--

In the Colosseum itself there are graffiti too, scratched into the stone seats and corridor walls. Drawings of gladiators, hunters, animals and boats. Names of visitors. And little game boards scratched on the steps, where people played with nuts or dice while they waited for the shows to begin.

The people who scratched them were bored, or excited, or in love, or just wanted to leave a mark. Thanks to them, we can still faintly hear the voices of ordinary Romans across two thousand years. Laughing and grumbling, boasting and loving, just as people do today.

--

{img:roman-child-alphabet-scratched-low-wall-plaster-letters-light}
Sometimes the graffiti are just one word, or a name and a date, scratched by someone two thousand years ago who simply wanted to say: I was here. Just like visitors to the Colosseum today, who are forbidden to scratch their names on its stones, but sometimes still try.
