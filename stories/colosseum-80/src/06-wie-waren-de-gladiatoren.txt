Who were the gladiators?

The name comes from gladius, the short sword of the Roman soldier. Gladiators were trained fighters, who fought each other, in pairs, in the arena, to entertain the people of Rome, and of all the cities of the Roman Empire.

--

The games began, long before, as part of funerals. Two hundred and fifty years before the Colosseum, in the year 264 before Christ, at the funeral of a Roman nobleman, his sons arranged for three pairs of slaves to fight, in his honour, in the cattle market of Rome. Over the following centuries, the games grew larger, and grander, and became the most popular entertainment in the Roman world, given by rich men, and politicians, and emperors, to win the favour of the people.

Most gladiators were slaves, or prisoners of war, or condemned criminals, who had no choice. They were bought, and owned, and trained, by a manager, called a lanista, who hired them out to the men who gave the games.

But some gladiators were free men, who chose to become gladiators, for the money, and the fame, and the excitement. They signed a contract, and swore a terrible oath: to be burned, to be chained, to be beaten, and to be killed by the sword, if necessary. And they gave up their rights as free citizens, for a time.

--

Gladiators were, in a strange way, both despised and admired.

The Romans looked down on them, as people of the lowest kind, like actors, and prostitutes. And yet, they were also heroes, celebrities, like the sports stars of today. Their names were written on the walls, and their pictures painted on lamps, and pots, and glass cups, and children played at being gladiators, and women admired them.

{img:clay-oil-lamp-gladiator-picture-glass-cup-souvenir-names}
And it is important to know something, which surprises many people. Most gladiator fights did not end in death. Gladiators were expensive: they cost a great deal to buy, and train, and feed. Their owners did not want them killed. So the fights had rules, and referees, and usually ended when one of the fighters was wounded, or exhausted, or gave up, by raising one finger, to ask for mercy. And very often, mercy was given, and the defeated gladiator was sent away, to fight another day.

--

Historians who have studied the records, the gravestones, and the graffiti, think that, in the time of the Colosseum, perhaps only one fight in five, or one in ten, ended in death. A gladiator might fight two or three times a year, and could hope, if he was skilful, and lucky, to survive for years, and perhaps, one day, to win his freedom.

When a gladiator won his freedom, he was given a wooden sword, called a rudis, as a sign that he was free. Some freed gladiators became trainers, or referees, in the gladiator schools. And some went back to the arena, because they missed the excitement, and the fame, and the money.

We will not watch the fights tonight. Instead, we will spend time with the gladiators in their school, and in their daily lives, and see how they lived, and trained, and ate, and slept, and hoped, in the shadow of the great amphitheatre.
