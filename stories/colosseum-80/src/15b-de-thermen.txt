After the games, many Romans go to the baths.

The new Baths of Titus, opened at the same time as the Colosseum, on the slope of the Esquiline Hill, just above it, are large, and splendid: great halls, with high, vaulted ceilings, and walls of coloured marble, and floors of mosaic, and pools, and fountains, and statues, and gardens, and exercise yards, and libraries, and rooms for massage, and for talking.

--

The Romans go to the baths every day, if they can, in the afternoon, after work, before dinner. It costs almost nothing: the smallest coin, a quadrans. Children go free. And the emperors, sometimes, pay for everyone, on special days.

First, the bathers undress, in the changing room, and leave their clothes in little niches in the wall, sometimes with a slave to guard them. Then they exercise, in the courtyard: running, wrestling, lifting weights, or playing ball games, with a leather ball, or a heavy ball, filled with sand.

Then they go into the warm room, and then the hot room, full of steam, heated by hot air, flowing under the floors, and inside the walls, from great furnaces, stoked by slaves, below. They sweat, and scrape their skin with a curved metal tool, a strigil, and oil. Then they plunge into the cold pool, in the cold room. And then they swim, in the great open-air pool, the natatio, or sit, and talk, and relax.

--

The baths are a place to meet friends, and gossip, and do business, and hear the news, and the latest jokes, and talk about the games, and the gladiators. Sellers walk around, crying their wares: sausages, and cakes, and drinks. Barbers pluck hair. Masseurs slap and knead. Poets recite their verses, to anyone who will listen.

The philosopher Seneca, who lived above a bath house, complained about the noise: the grunting of the weightlifters, the slapping of the masseurs, the shouts of the ball players, the cries of the sausage sellers, and the man who loved the sound of his own voice, singing in the bath.

{img:bathers-relaxing-pool-edge-marble-statues-evening-glow}
And then, clean, and refreshed, the Romans go home, to dinner, in the evening, as the lamps are lit, and the streets fill with people, going home, from the baths, and the games, and their work, in the warm summer evening.
