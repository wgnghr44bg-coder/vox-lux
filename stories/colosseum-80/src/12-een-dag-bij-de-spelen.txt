Let us spend a day at the games in the Colosseum, in the summer of the year 80, as an ordinary Roman citizen.

He is a baker, perhaps, from the Subura, the crowded neighbourhood behind the Forum. He has a ticket, a little piece of bone, given to him free, because the games are free for the citizens of Rome. It is paid for by the emperor. He gets up before dawn, and puts on his white toga, which he must wear, at the games, and goes, with his friends, and his young son, through the streets, in the grey light, to the Colosseum.

--

The streets around the amphitheatre are already full of people: thousands of them, streaming toward the building, from every direction. There are sellers of food, and drink, and cushions, to sit on, because the stone seats are hard, and souvenirs, little clay lamps, and cups, and figures of gladiators, and programmes, listing the pairs of gladiators who will fight that day, called libelli.

They find their entrance, number fifty-three, perhaps, by the number carved above the arch, and go in, and up the stairs, and along the corridors, to their section, and their row, and their seats, high up, among the ordinary citizens.

From there, they can see the whole amphitheatre: the great oval of seats, filling with people, in their white togas, and the arena, far below, with its yellow sand, gleaming in the morning sun. And the emperor's box, in the middle of one side, decorated with purple cloth.

--

The day has a programme, like a show today.

In the morning, there are the animal shows: the trained animals, doing their tricks, and then the hunts. At midday, while many people go out to eat, and rest, there are lesser shows: comedians, and acrobats, and jugglers, and clowns, and mock fights, with wooden weapons, and the punishment of criminals, which we will not watch. Our baker takes his son out, to buy some bread, and sausages, and wine, at a stall outside.

And in the afternoon, the main event: the gladiators.

{img:pompa-procession-gladiators-entering-arena-musicians-trumpets}
The afternoon begins with a grand procession, called the pompa: the gladiators, in purple and gold cloaks, march into the arena, with music, trumpets, and horns, and a water organ, and walk around the arena, and salute the emperor, and the crowd cheers.

--

Then the referees check the weapons. And the fights begin, one pair at a time, to the music of the band, which plays louder, and faster, as the fight becomes exciting, like the music in a film.

We will stay high up, with our baker, far from the arena, and watch only from a distance: the tiny figures, on the yellow sand, moving, circling, and the referee, in his white tunic, with his long stick, watching closely, and separating them, when necessary. And the crowd, shouting, and cheering, and groaning, and calling out the names of their favourites.

{img:referee-white-tunic-stick-arena-distant-figures-crowd-roar}
At the end of each fight, the winner receives a palm branch, and a prize of money, on a silver plate, and runs around the arena, waving the palm, while the crowd cheers. And the loser, if he has fought well, is usually sent away, alive, to the cheers of the crowd, who shout: missum, let him go.

--

In the evening, the games end. The crowd pours out, through the eighty arches, into the streets, in a few minutes. And our baker, and his friends, and his son, walk home, through the streets of Rome, in the evening light, talking about the gladiators, and the animals, and the emperor, and stop at a tavern, for a cup of wine, before going home, tired, and hoarse, from shouting, to bed.
