Let us spend a day at the games in the Colosseum in the summer of the year 80, as an ordinary Roman.

He is a baker from a crowded neighbourhood behind the Forum. He has a ticket, a little piece of bone. It was free, because the emperor pays for the games. He gets up before dawn, puts on his white toga, which he must wear at the games, and walks through the grey streets to the Colosseum with his friends and his young son.

--

The streets around the amphitheatre are already full. Thousands of people stream toward it from every direction. Sellers offer food and drink, and cushions, because the stone seats are hard. There are souvenirs, like little clay lamps and gladiator figures. And there are little programmes listing which gladiators will fight that day.

The baker finds his entrance by the number carved above the arch. He climbs the stairs, finds his section, his row and his seat, high up among the ordinary citizens.

From there he can see the whole amphitheatre. The great oval of seats filling with people in white togas. The arena far below, with its yellow sand gleaming in the morning sun. And the emperor's box, hung with purple cloth.

--

The day has a programme, like a show today.

In the morning there are the animals, first the trained animals with their tricks, then the hunts. At midday, many people go out to eat and rest. There are lesser shows then, comedians and acrobats and jugglers, and grim things we will not watch. The baker takes his son out to buy bread, sausages and wine at a stall outside.

And in the afternoon comes the main event. The gladiators.

{img:pompa-procession-gladiators-entering-arena-musicians-trumpets}
It begins with a grand parade. The gladiators march into the arena in purple and gold cloaks, with trumpets and horns and the water organ playing. They walk around the arena and salute the emperor, and the crowd cheers.

--

Then the referees check the weapons, and the fights begin, one pair at a time. The music grows louder and faster as each fight grows more exciting.

We will stay high up with our baker, far from the arena, and watch only from a distance. Tiny figures move and circle on the yellow sand. A referee in a white tunic, with a long stick, watches closely and steps in when he must. And the crowd shouts and cheers and groans, and calls out the names of their favourites.

{img:referee-white-tunic-stick-arena-distant-figures-crowd-roar}
At the end of each fight, the winner gets a palm branch and a prize of money on a silver plate. He runs around the arena waving the palm while the crowd cheers. And the loser, if he has fought well, is usually sent away alive. The crowd shouts: let him go!

--

In the evening the games end. In a few minutes, the crowd pours out through the eighty arches into the streets. The baker, his friends and his son walk home in the evening light, talking about the gladiators, the animals and the emperor. They stop at a tavern for a cup of wine. Then they go home to bed, tired, and hoarse from shouting.
