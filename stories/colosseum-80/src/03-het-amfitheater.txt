The Colosseum is the largest amphitheatre ever built.

An amphitheatre is a round or oval open-air theatre. Rows of seats rise up all around a flat space in the middle, where the shows take place. That space is called the arena. The word arena means sand, because the floor is covered with sand.

--

The Colosseum is an oval about a hundred and ninety metres long and a hundred and fifty-six metres wide. It is nearly fifty metres high, as tall as a building of fifteen storeys today. It can hold perhaps fifty thousand people or more, sitting on stone seats in rows all around the arena.

The outside wall has four storeys. The bottom three each have eighty arches, one above the other. Each arch is framed by half-columns in three different styles. Plain and sturdy at the bottom. Columns with curled tops in the middle. And elegant columns with carved leaves at the top. The highest storey has small square windows instead of arches, and holders for the great poles of the sunshade.

{img:statues-in-arches-upper-storeys-colosseum-sunlit-travertine}
In each arch of the second and third storeys stands a statue of a god, a hero or an emperor. The whole outside is built of a creamy white stone from quarries about thirty kilometres away. In the sunshine it glows pale gold.

--

There are eighty entrances at ground level, each with a number carved above it. You can still see those numbers today. Every visitor has a ticket, a little piece of pottery or bone. It shows the number of the entrance, the section, the row and the seat. So fifty thousand people can come in and find their places in about fifteen minutes. And they can leave just as quickly through the corridors and staircases.

Four entrances are special. Two at the ends are for the gladiators and the processions. The other two are for the emperor and the most important guests.

{img:numbered-entrance-arches-crowd-entering-tokens-morning-sun}
Inside, the seats are arranged by rank, in rings, one above another. In the front row, closest to the arena, on a wide platform, sit the senators in their white togas with purple stripes. Their names are carved on their marble seats. The priestesses of the goddess Vesta sit there too. And the emperor has his own special box in the middle of one long side.

--

Behind them sit the knights, the rich men of Rome. Then the ordinary citizens, who must wear their white togas. Then, higher up, the poorer citizens. And at the very top, in a wooden gallery, sit the women, the slaves and the poorest people. A law made long ago says the women must sit up there, separately.

The higher you sit, the further you are from the arena, and the less important you are. But from the top, you have a wonderful view over the whole amphitheatre, and over the roofs of Rome to the hills.
