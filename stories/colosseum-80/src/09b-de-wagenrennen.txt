The Colosseum was not the only great place of entertainment in Rome. The favourite of all, for many Romans, was the chariot racing, in the Circus Maximus.

The Circus Maximus is even larger than the Colosseum: a great long racecourse, about six hundred metres long, in the valley between the Palatine and Aventine hills, with seats, rising up, on both sides, and at one end, for perhaps a hundred and fifty thousand spectators, or even more.

--

In the time of the Colosseum, there are four teams, called factions, each with its own colour: the Reds, the Whites, the Blues, and the Greens. Every Roman supports one of them, passionately, from childhood, like a football club today. The emperors too: Domitian, the brother of Titus, will add two more colours, purple and gold, which do not last.

The charioteers drive light chariots, pulled by four horses, seven times around the long, narrow track, around a central barrier, the spina, decorated with statues, and obelisks, and fountains, and the lap counters, the eggs and the dolphins.

The charioteers wrap the reins around their waists, so that they can steer with their bodies, and carry a knife, in their belt, to cut the reins, if the chariot crashes. The races are fast, and exciting, and dangerous.

--

The best charioteers are the greatest celebrities of Rome, far more famous, and richer, than the gladiators. In the time of the Colosseum, the most famous is Scorpus, of the Greens, who won more than two thousand races. When he passed away, in a race, at the age of twenty-six, the poet Martial wrote an epitaph for him: I am Scorpus, the glory of the noisy circus, the applause of Rome, and its short-lived darling.

A little later, a charioteer from Spain, called Gaius Appuleius Diocles, raced for twenty-four years, and won more than one thousand four hundred races, and retired at the age of forty-two, and earned, in prize money, thirty-five million sesterces: perhaps the richest sportsman in history.

The horses, too, are famous, and have names, and their own fans: Victor, and Tuscus, and Passerinus, and Hirpinus. Some of them have statues, and the people know their names, and their records, as well as the charioteers'.
