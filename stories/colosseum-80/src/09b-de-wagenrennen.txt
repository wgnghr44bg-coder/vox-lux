The Colosseum was not the only great place of entertainment in Rome. For many Romans, the favourite of all was chariot racing in the Circus Maximus.

The Circus Maximus is even larger than the Colosseum. It is a great long racetrack, about six hundred metres long, in the valley between two hills. Seats rise up on both sides, for perhaps a hundred and fifty thousand people, or even more.

--

There are four teams, each with its own colour. The Reds, the Whites, the Blues and the Greens. Every Roman supports one of them passionately, from childhood, like a football club today. Even the emperors have their favourite team.

The drivers race light chariots pulled by four horses, seven times around the long, narrow track. In the middle runs a barrier decorated with statues, an obelisk and fountains. On it, big wooden eggs and bronze dolphins are turned to count the laps.

The drivers wrap the reins around their waists, so they can steer with their whole body. They carry a knife in their belt, to cut themselves free if the chariot crashes. The races are fast and thrilling and dangerous. On every turn, the crowd holds its breath.

--

The best drivers are the biggest celebrities in Rome, far more famous and richer than the gladiators. In the time of the Colosseum, the most famous is Scorpus of the Greens, who won more than two thousand races. When he died in a race at only twenty-six, a poet wrote: I am Scorpus, the glory of the noisy circus, the applause of Rome, and its short-lived darling.

A little later, a driver from Spain raced for twenty-four years and won almost fifteen hundred races. He retired at forty-two with a huge fortune in prize money. He may have been the richest sportsman in history.

The horses are famous too. They have names and their own fans. Some even have statues. The people know their names and records as well as the drivers'.
