The most famous gladiator fight of the opening games of the Colosseum was described by the poet Martial, who saw it. It was between two gladiators, called Priscus, and Verus.

They were well matched: equal in strength, and skill, and courage. They fought for a long time, in the arena, in front of the emperor Titus, and the whole of Rome, and neither could win.

--

The crowd, watching, began to shout for both of them to be given their freedom. But Titus followed the rule of the games: the fight must go on, until one of them raised his finger, to ask for mercy.

So they went on fighting, for a long time. And still, neither of them gave way.

And then, at last, Martial says, both of them, at the same moment, raised their fingers, to ask for mercy. They had both fought so bravely, and so equally, that neither would give up first, and neither could win.

And Titus, delighted, sent them both gifts. And then, he did something that had never been done before: he gave them both the wooden sword, the rudis, the sign of freedom. Both of them were set free, on the same day, together.

--

Martial wrote: this has happened under no prince but you, Caesar: two fought, and two won.

It is a story that shows the best side of the games, as the Romans saw it: courage, and skill, and honour, and the mercy of a good emperor. And it was told, and retold, for many years afterward.

We do not know what became of Priscus and Verus after that. Perhaps they became trainers, in a gladiator school. Perhaps they went home, to wherever they came from, to their families. Perhaps they opened a tavern, as some retired gladiators did, and told stories, to their customers, of the great day when they fought before the emperor, at the opening of the Colosseum, and both won.

--

{img:martial-reading-poem-friends-evening-lamp-scroll-colosseum-book}
Martial's little poem about them is still read today, nearly two thousand years later. It is one of the few times, in all of ancient literature, that two ordinary gladiators are remembered by name, not because they won, or lost, but because they were equal, and both were spared, on a summer afternoon, in the new amphitheatre, by an emperor who wanted to be kind.
