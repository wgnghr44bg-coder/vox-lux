The most famous gladiator fight of the opening games was described by the poet Martial, who saw it with his own eyes. It was between two gladiators called Priscus and Verus.

They were perfectly matched, equal in strength, skill and courage. They fought for a long time in front of the emperor and all of Rome. And neither of them could win.

--

The crowd began to shout for both of them to be given their freedom. But Titus followed the rule of the games. The fight had to go on until one of them raised a finger to ask for mercy.

So they kept fighting, for a long time. The whole amphitheatre watched in suspense. And still neither of them gave way.

And then, at last, Martial says, both of them raised their fingers at exactly the same moment. They had fought so bravely and so equally that neither would give up first, and neither could win.

Titus was delighted. He sent them both gifts. And then he did something that had never been done before. He gave them both the wooden sword, the sign of freedom. Both of them were set free, on the same day, together.

--

Martial wrote: this has happened under no ruler but you, Caesar. Two fought, and two won.

For the Romans, this story showed the best side of the games. Courage, skill and honour, and the mercy of a good emperor. It was told again and again for many years.

We do not know what became of Priscus and Verus after that. Perhaps they became trainers. Perhaps they went home to their families. Perhaps they opened a tavern, as some retired gladiators did. And there they told their customers about the great day they fought before the emperor at the opening of the Colosseum, and both won.

--

{img:martial-reading-poem-friends-evening-lamp-scroll-colosseum-book}
Martial's little poem about them is still read today, nearly two thousand years later. It is one of the very few times in all of ancient writing that two ordinary gladiators are remembered by name. Not because one won and one lost. But because they were equal, and both were spared, on a summer afternoon, in the new amphitheatre, by an emperor who wanted to be kind.
