Next to the Colosseum, just to the east, there is the great gladiator school, the Ludus Magnus. It was built a little later, by the emperor Domitian, but there was already a school there, or nearby, in the time of Titus.

It is a large rectangular building, with a courtyard in the middle, with its own little oval arena, a small copy of the Colosseum, with a few rows of seats around it, where the gladiators train, and where people can come and watch them practise.

--

Around the courtyard, there are two or three storeys of small rooms, cells, where the gladiators live, and sleep, perhaps one or two in each. There are kitchens, and storerooms, and an armoury, where the weapons are kept, locked up, and a hospital, and baths.

And there is a tunnel, underground, which connects the school to the Colosseum, so that the gladiators can walk, out of sight, from their school, to the arena, on the day of the games.

The ruins of the Ludus Magnus can still be seen today, beside the Colosseum, with the curved seats of its little arena, below the level of the modern street.

--

Life in the gladiator school is strict, and disciplined, like the army.

The gladiators are guarded, because many of them are slaves, or prisoners, and might try to escape, or rebel. Everyone in Rome remembers Spartacus, the famous gladiator, who, a hundred and fifty years before, escaped from a gladiator school at Capua, with seventy of his companions, and gathered an army of slaves, and fought against Rome for two years.

But the gladiators are also well looked after, because they are valuable. They are given good food, and doctors, and massages, and baths, and training, by the best trainers.

{img:gladiators-eating-together-long-table-barley-porridge-beans}
They live together, train together, eat together, and become a kind of family, called a familia gladiatoria. They have their own customs, and their own gods, especially Hercules, the strong hero, and Nemesis, the goddess of fate. They form clubs, and pay into a common fund, so that, if one of them dies, his family will be given money for a proper funeral, and a gravestone.

--

Some gladiators have wives, and children, who live outside the school, or sometimes, it seems, inside. Gravestones of gladiators, found all over the Roman Empire, were often put up by their wives, or their friends, or their fellow gladiators, with loving words.

One says: to the spirits of the departed, and of Urbicus, a secutor, from Florence, who fought thirteen times, and lived twenty-two years. His daughter Olympias, whom he left aged five months, and Fortunensis, her slave, and Lauricia, his wife, made this, for her well-deserving husband, with whom she lived seven years.

{img:gravestone-gladiator-relief-carved-name-family-inscription-moss}
And another, put up by a gladiator's fellow fighters, says simply: for Pinnas, from his comrades.
