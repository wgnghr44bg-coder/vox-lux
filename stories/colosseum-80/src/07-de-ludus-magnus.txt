Right next to the Colosseum, to the east, stands the great gladiator school. It was rebuilt a little later by the next emperor, but there was already a school on or near that spot in the time of Titus.

It is a large rectangular building with a courtyard in the middle. The courtyard has its own little oval arena, a small copy of the Colosseum, with a few rows of seats. There the gladiators train, and people can come and watch them practise.

--

Around the courtyard, two or three floors of small rooms hold the gladiators' cells, where they live and sleep, one or two to a room. There are kitchens and storerooms, a locked armoury for the weapons, a hospital and baths.

And there is a tunnel underground that links the school to the Colosseum. On the day of the games, the gladiators walk through it to the arena, out of sight of the crowd. Every gladiator knows that tunnel. And every gladiator wonders how many more times he will walk through it.

You can still see the ruins of this school today, next to the Colosseum, with the curved seats of its little arena below the level of the modern street.

--

Life in the gladiator school is strict and disciplined, like the army.

The gladiators are guarded, because many are slaves or prisoners who might try to escape or rebel. Everyone in Rome remembers Spartacus, the famous gladiator who escaped from a school with seventy companions, a hundred and fifty years ago, and fought against Rome for two years.

But the gladiators are also well cared for, because they are valuable. They get good food, doctors, massages, baths and the best trainers.

{img:gladiators-eating-together-long-table-barley-porridge-beans}
They live, train and eat together, and become a kind of family. They have their own customs and favourite gods, like the strong hero Hercules and the goddess of fate. They form clubs and pay into a shared fund. If one of them dies, his family receives money for a proper funeral and a gravestone.

--

Some gladiators have wives and children who live outside the school. Gladiator gravestones have been found all over the Roman Empire. Often they were put up by a wife or by fellow gladiators, with loving words.

One says: for Urbicus, a gladiator from Florence, who fought thirteen times and lived twenty-two years. His daughter, whom he left at five months old, and his wife, with whom he lived seven years, made this for her well-deserving husband.

{img:gravestone-gladiator-relief-carved-name-family-inscription-moss}
And another, put up by a gladiator's friends, says simply: for Pinnas, from his comrades.
