In the Middle Ages, the Colosseum became a strange kind of village.

People built houses, and workshops, and stables, inside the arches, and under the vaults, of the old amphitheatre. There were blacksmiths, and shoemakers, and carpenters, living and working there. There was a little church, built into the building. In the twelfth century, a powerful Roman family, the Frangipani, turned it into a fortress, with walls, and towers.

--

Then, in the year 1349, a great earthquake struck Rome, and the outer wall, on the south side, collapsed, and fell, in a great heap of stone. That is why, today, the Colosseum has a complete outer wall only on the north side, and the south side is lower.

For the next four hundred years, the Colosseum was used as a quarry. The Romans took its stones, the great blocks of travertine, which had fallen, and many more, which they pulled down, to build new buildings: palaces, and churches, and bridges. Much of the stone of Saint Peter's Basilica, and the Palazzo Venezia, and other great buildings of Renaissance Rome, came from the Colosseum. They dug out the iron clamps, too, leaving the holes.

{img:renaissance-carts-carrying-stones-from-colosseum-builders-1500s}
And then, in the eighteenth century, the popes decided to protect it. In 1749, Pope Benedict the Fourteenth declared the Colosseum a sacred place, in memory of the early Christian martyrs, who, it was believed, had died there, and set up the Stations of the Cross, in the arena, and a great cross. Historians today are not sure how many Christians were really killed in the Colosseum itself. But the decision saved the building from being taken apart completely.

--

In the nineteenth century, the popes, and later the government of Italy, repaired the Colosseum, and built great brick buttresses, at each end of the surviving wall, to hold it up. And travellers, and writers, and artists, from all over the world, came to see it, especially by moonlight, which was thought to be the most romantic way to see it.

The English poet Lord Byron, in 1817, walked in the Colosseum by moonlight, and wrote about it. The American writer Mark Twain visited it, and wrote that it was the monarch of all European ruins. And the botanists found that more than four hundred different kinds of plants were growing in the ruins: flowers, and grasses, and trees, some of them rare, whose seeds, people liked to believe, had come from distant lands, in the fur, and the feed, of the animals brought for the games.

{img:moonlit-colosseum-19th-century-travellers-lanterns-romantic-ruins}
Most of the plants were cleared away, later, to protect the stones. But in the spring, there are still wild flowers, growing in the cracks, and on the high ledges, of the Colosseum.
