How did the Romans build such enormous buildings nearly two thousand years ago that are still standing today?

The secret is the arch, the vault, and concrete.

--

The Romans did not invent the arch. But they used it more boldly and cleverly than anyone before them. An arch is a curve of wedge-shaped stones that push against each other. The weight of the wall above is carried down along the curve to the ground on both sides. An arch can hold up an enormous weight and span a wide opening, much wider than a flat stone beam could.

A vault is like an arch stretched out into a tunnel. And a dome is like an arch turned around in a circle. With arches, vaults and domes, the Romans could build bridges, aqueducts, great halls and amphitheatres larger than anything built before.

{img:aqueduct-arches-countryside-rome-water-channel-top-sunset}
And they had concrete. Roman concrete is made of lime, a special volcanic ash from around Naples and Rome, water, and pieces of stone or broken brick. It is poured into wooden moulds and sets as hard as rock. It can even set under water, so the Romans used it for harbours and bridges.

--

Scientists have recently found out something remarkable about Roman concrete. It can heal itself. If a crack forms and water gets in, little lumps of lime inside the concrete react with the water and fill the crack with new crystals. So the concrete gets stronger over time instead of weaker. That is one reason Roman buildings have lasted so long, while some modern concrete crumbles in a few decades.

In the Colosseum, the vaults that hold up the seats are made of concrete. The outer walls and main pillars are of strong white stone. The inner walls are of brick and lighter stone. Each material is used where it works best.

{img:pantheon-dome-oculus-light-beam-interior-coffers-quiet}
Forty years after the Colosseum, another emperor will build the Pantheon in Rome, a temple to all the gods. It has a great concrete dome more than forty-three metres wide, with a round opening at the top. Sunlight shines through the opening and moves slowly around the inside of the dome during the day, like a sundial. Nearly two thousand years later, it is still standing, and still used as a church.
