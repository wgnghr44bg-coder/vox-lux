How did the Romans build such enormous buildings, nearly two thousand years ago, that are still standing today?

The secret is the arch, and the vault, and concrete.

--

The Romans did not invent the arch. But they used it more boldly, and more cleverly, than anyone before them. An arch is a curve of wedge-shaped stones, which push against each other, so that the weight of the wall above is carried down, along the curve, to the ground, on either side. An arch can carry an enormous weight, and span a wide opening, much wider than a flat stone beam.

A vault is an arch, extended, like a tunnel. And a dome is an arch, turned around, in a circle. With arches, and vaults, and domes, the Romans could build bridges, and aqueducts, and great halls, and amphitheatres, larger than anyone had built before.

{img:aqueduct-arches-countryside-rome-water-channel-top-sunset}
And they had concrete. Roman concrete is made of lime, mixed with a special volcanic ash, called pozzolana, from the area around Naples, and Rome, and water, and pieces of stone, or broken brick, or rubble. It is poured into wooden moulds, or between walls of brick, and sets, hard as rock. It can even set under water, so the Romans used it for harbours, and bridges.

--

Scientists have recently discovered that Roman concrete has a remarkable property: it heals itself. Little lumps of lime, inside the concrete, react with water, if a crack forms, and fill the crack with new crystals, so that the concrete becomes stronger over time, instead of weaker. That is one of the reasons why Roman buildings have lasted so long, while some modern concrete crumbles in a few decades.

In the Colosseum, the vaults that hold up the seats are made of concrete, and the outer walls, and the main piers, of travertine, and the inner walls, of brick, and tufa, each material used where it is best.

{img:pantheon-dome-oculus-light-beam-interior-coffers-quiet}
Forty years after the Colosseum, the emperor Hadrian will build the Pantheon, in Rome, a temple to all the gods, with a great dome of concrete, more than forty-three metres wide, with a round opening at the top, the oculus, through which the sunlight shines, and moves slowly around the inside of the dome, through the day, like a sundial. It is still the largest dome of unreinforced concrete in the world, nearly two thousand years later, and it is still standing, and still used, as a church.
