The games in the Colosseum are not only gladiators. There are also animals: thousands of them, from every part of the Roman Empire, and beyond.

The Romans bring wild animals to Rome from all over the world: lions, and leopards, from North Africa. Tigers, from India, and Persia. Bears, from Germany, and Scotland, and the Balkans. Elephants, from Africa. Rhinoceroses, and hippopotamuses, and crocodiles, from Egypt, and Ethiopia. Giraffes, and ostriches, and zebras. Wild boar, and deer, and bulls, and wolves, and bison.

--

Capturing and transporting these animals is an enormous business. Hunters, and soldiers, in Africa, and Asia, and Europe, catch them alive, with nets, and pits, and traps, and put them in wooden cages, and carry them, by ox cart, and by ship, across the Mediterranean, to the port of Ostia, at the mouth of the Tiber, and then up the river, to Rome.

At Ostia, there are mosaics, on the floors of the offices of the shipping companies, showing elephants, and other animals, being loaded onto ships. In a great villa in Sicily, at Piazza Armerina, there is a wonderful long mosaic, from three hundred years later, called the Great Hunt, showing the capture and transport of wild animals, for the games: lions, and tigers, and antelopes, and ostriches, and an elephant, walking up a gangplank, onto a ship.

{img:mosaic-great-hunt-elephant-gangplank-ship-tigers-cages}
In Rome, the animals are kept in enclosures, called vivaria, outside the city walls, near the Praenestine gate, and looked after by keepers, and trainers, until the day of the games.

--

Some of the animals are trained to do tricks. The Romans love to see elephants walking on tightropes, or writing letters in the sand with their trunks, or kneeling before the emperor. Lions trained to catch hares, and carry them gently in their mouths, without harming them. Bears that dance. Bulls that let boys stand on their backs.

The poet Martial, who was at the opening games of the Colosseum, in the year 80, and wrote a little book of poems about them, says that an elephant, which had just won a fight against a bull, knelt down, of its own accord, before the emperor Titus, as if it understood that he was a god.

{img:trained-elephant-kneeling-arena-sand-crowd-emperor-box-sunlight}
There are also hunts, called venationes, in the morning, where trained hunters, called venatores, and bestiarii, fight with wild animals, with spears, and bows, in the arena. Sometimes, the arena is decorated like a forest, or a mountain, with real trees, and rocks, and bushes, which rise up out of trapdoors in the floor, so that the hunt looks like a real hunt, in the wild.

--

We will not watch the hunts. But it is sad to think of the thousands of animals, taken from their homes, all over the world, and brought to Rome. In the following centuries, so many animals were captured for the games that some of them disappeared from parts of the Roman Empire: the elephants of North Africa, and the lions of Greece, and the hippopotamuses of the lower Nile.
