The games in the Colosseum went on for more than four hundred years.

Emperor after emperor gave great games there: Trajan, after his victories in Dacia, in the year 107, gave games that lasted a hundred and twenty-three days, with ten thousand gladiators, and eleven thousand animals. The emperor Commodus, the son of the wise Marcus Aurelius, even fought in the arena himself, as a gladiator, which shocked the Romans.

--

But slowly, over the centuries, things changed.

The Roman Empire became Christian, in the fourth century, under the emperor Constantine, and his successors. The Christians disapproved of the gladiator games. Christian writers, like Augustine, and Tertullian, wrote against them. The games became more expensive, and harder to pay for, as the empire grew poorer, and was attacked by enemies.

There is a story, told by a Christian historian, Theodoret, about a monk called Telemachus, from the East, who came to Rome, in the year 404, and, during a gladiator fight in the arena, ran down onto the sand, and tried to separate the gladiators, and begged them to stop. The crowd, angry, threw stones at him. And the emperor Honorius, hearing of it, was so moved, that he banned the gladiator games forever.

We do not know if the story is true. But it seems that the gladiator fights did end, around that time, at the beginning of the fifth century.

--

The animal hunts went on a little longer. The last recorded hunt in the Colosseum was in the year 523, under the Gothic king Theodoric, who ruled Italy after the fall of the Western Roman Empire.

After that, the Colosseum was silent. The great amphitheatre, which had once held fifty thousand cheering people, stood empty, in the middle of a Rome which had shrunk, from a million people, to perhaps thirty thousand, living among the ruins.

{img:empty-colosseum-grass-growing-arena-6th-century-quiet-ruin}
Grass grew in the arena. Trees took root in the seats. Birds nested in the arches. And the people of Rome began to use the great building for other things.

--

{img:shepherd-goats-grazing-ancient-ruins-rome-middle-ages-quiet}
In the early Middle Ages, shepherds grazed their goats and sheep among the ruins of ancient Rome, in the old Forum, which they called the Campo Vaccino, the cow field. And the great amphitheatre stood above them, silent, enormous, and mysterious, a building from another age, which nobody could have built any more, and which people began to say had been built by giants, or by magic, or that it had once been a temple of the sun.
