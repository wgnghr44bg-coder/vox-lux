Let us walk through the streets of Rome on an ordinary morning in the year 80, a few weeks before the great games.

The city wakes early, at first light. Shopkeepers take down the wooden shutters from their shops, which open straight onto the street under tall apartment buildings. The bakers have been working since before dawn. The smell of fresh bread fills the narrow streets.

--

The streets are crowded and noisy and full of life. Porters carry loads on their backs. Rich people are carried through the crowds by slaves, in covered chairs with curtains. Children run to school. Barbers shave men on stools right in the street. Cooks fry sausages and sell hot food from counters. Sellers call out: hot bread, cakes, old clothes!

Everyone is talking about the new amphitheatre and the games. When will they begin? Who will fight? How many animals will there be? Will the emperor give away gifts?

But Rome is also still recovering from the great fire this spring, and from the sickness that followed. There are building sites everywhere. Scaffolding and cranes, carts loaded with stone and timber, and the sound of hammering and sawing and shouting. Will everything really be ready in time?

--

Later, the next emperor will rebuild the burned temple of Jupiter, more splendid than ever, with a roof of golden bronze tiles that shine across the whole city.

And all the time, the new amphitheatre rises above the roofs in its valley, enormous and pale and new. Sailors climb the masts at the top, practising with the great sunshade. Workmen add the last touches to the seats and corridors.

A poet wrote that where Nero's hated palace had stood, there now stood the people's delight. Rome had been given back to itself.

--

In the taverns near the new amphitheatre, the innkeepers are getting ready for the crowds. They buy extra wine, bread and sausages, and hire extra servants. They paint pictures of gladiators and animals on their walls to attract customers. It is going to be a good summer for business.
