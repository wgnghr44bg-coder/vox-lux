Let us walk through the streets of Rome, on an ordinary morning, in the year 80, a few weeks before the great games.

The city wakes early, at first light. The shopkeepers take down the wooden shutters, from the fronts of their shops, which open straight onto the street, under the tall apartment buildings. The bakers have been working since before dawn, and the smell of fresh bread fills the narrow streets.

--

The streets are crowded, noisy, and full of life. Porters carry loads on their backs. Litters, carried by slaves, push through the crowds, with rich men, or ladies, inside, behind curtains. Children run to school. Barbers shave men, on stools, in the street. Cooks fry sausages, and sell hot food, from counters. Sellers cry their wares: hot bread, cakes, matches, sulphur, old clothes. Beggars sit on the steps of the temples.

Everyone is talking about the new amphitheatre, and the games. When will they begin? Who will fight? How many animals will there be? Will the emperor give away gifts?

In the year 80, Rome is also still recovering from the great fire, of the spring, which burned the temple of Jupiter, and many other buildings, and from the plague. There are building sites everywhere: scaffolding, and cranes, and carts loaded with stone, and bricks, and timber, and the sound of hammering, and sawing, and shouting.

--

The emperor Domitian, later, will rebuild the burned temple of Jupiter, on the Capitol, more splendid than ever, with a roof of gilded bronze tiles, which shine in the sun, and can be seen from all over the city.

And all the time, the new amphitheatre rises above the roofs, in its valley, enormous, and pale, and new, with the statues in its arches, and the sailors climbing the masts, at the top, practising with the awning, and the workmen putting the finishing touches to the seats, and the corridors, and the decorations.

The poet Martial wrote that, where Nero's hated palace had stood, now there stood the people's delight. Rome had been given back to itself.

--

In the taverns, along the streets near the new amphitheatre, the innkeepers are getting ready for the crowds that will come. They buy extra wine, and bread, and sausages, and hire extra servants, and paint signs on their walls, showing gladiators, and animals, to attract customers. It will be a good summer for business.
