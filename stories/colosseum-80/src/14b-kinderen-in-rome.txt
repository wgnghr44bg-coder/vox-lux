The children of Rome, in the year 80, love the games, like everyone else.

They play at being gladiators, in the streets, and the courtyards, with wooden swords, and little shields, and nets, and shout the names of the famous fighters, and pretend to be the emperor, raising their thumbs, and giving freedom to the winners. They collect little clay figures of gladiators, and lamps, with pictures of gladiators, and draw gladiators on the walls, with charcoal.

--

Roman children have many toys: dolls, of clay, or wood, or bone, with jointed arms and legs, which can be dressed in little clothes. Little wooden horses, on wheels. Little carts, pulled by mice, or goats. Hoops, and spinning tops, and balls, made of leather, stuffed with feathers, or pig's bladders, blown up, like balloons. Rattles, for babies. Little pottery animals. Kites. Swings. Board games, scratched on stone.

They play hide and seek, and blind man's buff, and leapfrog, and odds and evens, and a game with nuts, rolling them down a board, at a little pile of nuts, to knock them over, and win them. Nuts are so important to Roman children that, when a boy grows up, people say he has left his nuts behind.

{img:roman-girl-doll-jointed-clay-courtyard-mother-spinning-wool}
Children of rich families go to school, from about the age of seven, with a slave, the paedagogus, to carry their books, and look after them. They learn to read, and write, and count, on wax tablets, and later, Greek, and Latin literature, and speaking. The poor children work, with their parents, in the shops, and the workshops, and the markets, from a young age.

--

At the games, children sit with their families, high up in the seats, and watch, and cheer, and eat, and fall asleep, in the afternoon heat, in their mothers' laps.

And a boy, who sat with his father, high up in the Colosseum, at the opening games, in the year 80, perhaps at the age of eight, might have lived long enough to see the emperor Hadrian build the Pantheon, fifty years later, and to tell his grandchildren, as an old man, by the fire, about the great day when the Colosseum first opened, and the two gladiators, Priscus and Verus, both won their freedom.
