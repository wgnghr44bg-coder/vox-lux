Rome, in the year 80, has more fresh water than any city in the world, until modern times.

The water comes from the hills, many kilometres away, through the great aqueducts: channels of stone, and concrete, which carry the water, sloping gently downhill, all the way to the city, underground, through tunnels, for most of their length, and over the valleys, on great rows of arches, which march across the countryside around Rome, for kilometres.

--

In the year 80, there are nine aqueducts, bringing perhaps a million cubic metres of water a day into the city: about a thousand litres for every person, much more than most modern cities. The newest and greatest are the Aqua Claudia, and the Anio Novus, finished by the emperor Claudius, about thirty years before, whose arches, nearly thirty metres high, still stand, in the countryside, south east of Rome.

The water flows into great tanks, and then, through lead pipes, to the public fountains, on almost every street corner, where the people fill their jars, and buckets, all day long, and to the public baths, and the gardens, and the houses of the rich, who pay for a private supply.

The water never stops flowing. The fountains run day and night, and the overflow washes the streets, and flows into the great sewers, which carry it, with the waste of the city, to the river Tiber.

--

A few years after the Colosseum, an official called Frontinus, who was in charge of the water supply of Rome, wrote a book about the aqueducts, which still exists. He was very proud of them. He wrote: with such an array of indispensable structures carrying so many waters, compare, if you will, the idle pyramids, or the useless, though famous, works of the Greeks.

The Colosseum, too, needed water: for the fountains, and the latrines, and the sprays of perfumed water, and, at the beginning, for flooding the arena for the sea battles. It was supplied by a branch of one of the aqueducts, which came down from the Caelian Hill, nearby.

{img:roman-latrine-row-stone-seats-running-water-channel-sponges}
And under the Colosseum, there is a great system of drains, which carry away the rainwater, and the waste, to the great sewer, the Cloaca Maxima, and to the Tiber. Archaeologists, exploring these drains, have found the remains of the food, and the coins, and the little things, that the spectators dropped, two thousand years ago.
