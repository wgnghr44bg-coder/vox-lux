In the year 80, Rome has more fresh water than any city in the world will have until modern times.

The water comes from the hills, many kilometres away, through the great aqueducts. These are channels of stone and concrete that carry water gently downhill all the way to the city. For most of the way they run underground. But across valleys they run on top of great rows of arches that march across the countryside for kilometres.

--

In the year 80 there are nine aqueducts. Together they bring about a thousand litres of water a day for every person in Rome. That is far more than most modern cities. The newest ones have arches nearly thirty metres high, which still stand in the countryside outside Rome.

The water flows into great tanks, and from there through lead pipes to the public fountains on almost every street corner. People fill their jars and buckets there all day long. Water also goes to the public baths, the gardens, and the houses of the rich, who pay for their own supply.

The water never stops flowing. The fountains run day and night. The overflow washes the streets and runs into the great sewers, which carry it, with the city's waste, to the river Tiber.

--

A few years after the Colosseum, the official in charge of Rome's water wrote a book about the aqueducts. It still exists. He was very proud of them. Compare these useful works, he wrote, with the idle pyramids, or the useless, though famous, works of the Greeks.

The Colosseum needed water too. For the fountains and the toilets, and for the scented sprays. It got its water from a branch of one of the aqueducts, which came down from a nearby hill.

And under the Colosseum there is a great system of drains that carry away rainwater and waste to the great sewer, and on to the Tiber. Archaeologists exploring these drains have found food, coins and little things that people dropped during the games, two thousand years ago.
