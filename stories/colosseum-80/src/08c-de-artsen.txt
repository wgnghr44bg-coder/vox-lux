The doctors who looked after the gladiators were among the most skilful in the Roman world.

Roman doctors, many of them Greeks, had a wonderful collection of instruments, made of bronze, and iron: scalpels, and probes, and forceps, and hooks, and needles, and spatulas, and little spoons, for medicines, many of them very like the instruments of a modern surgeon. Hundreds of them have been found, at Pompeii, in the house of a surgeon, and are now in the museum in Naples.

--

They cleaned wounds, with wine, and vinegar, which kill germs, although they did not know about germs. They stitched them, with thread, and bandaged them with clean linen. They set broken bones, with splints. They used plants, as medicines: poppy juice, for pain, and willow bark, which contains the same medicine as aspirin, and honey, which helps wounds to heal.

When scientists studied the bones of the gladiators, buried at Ephesus, they found many old injuries, which had healed well: broken bones which had been set properly, and had mended. This shows that the gladiators were looked after carefully, by good doctors, and given time to recover.

{img:healed-bone-study-scientist-laboratory-calm-careful-light-later}
Galen, the most famous doctor of the Roman world, who began his career looking after gladiators, a hundred years later, wrote that he called the wounds of the gladiators windows into the body, through which he learned how the body works. He paid great attention to their diet, and their exercise, and their rest, and was proud that, in the four years he looked after them, very few of his gladiators died of their wounds, far fewer than before.
