How was the Colosseum built?

It was begun around the year 72, under Vespasian, and opened in the year 80, under Titus: only about eight years, for one of the largest buildings in the world. It was an enormous achievement of engineering, and organization.

--

First, the builders drained Nero's lake, and dug down, and laid enormous foundations: a great ring of concrete, twelve metres deep, under the whole building, to carry its enormous weight, on the soft, wet ground of the old lake bed.

Then they built the walls, and the arches, and the vaults, using travertine, for the main piers, and outer walls, and tufa, a lighter volcanic stone, and brick, for the inner walls, and concrete, the great Roman invention, made of lime, and volcanic ash, and rubble, and water, which sets as hard as rock, for the vaults, that hold up the seats.

About a hundred thousand cubic metres of travertine were needed: brought from the quarries at Tibur, along a special road, in ox carts, perhaps more than two hundred cartloads a day, for years. The blocks were held together, not with mortar, but with iron clamps, perhaps three hundred tons of iron. In the Middle Ages, people dug out the clamps, to reuse the iron, and left the holes which can still be seen all over the walls today.

--

Thousands of people worked on it: architects, and engineers, and surveyors, and stone cutters, and masons, and carpenters, and sculptors, and labourers, and the drivers of the ox carts, and the men who worked the great cranes. Many of them were skilled, free craftsmen, well paid. Many others were slaves, and prisoners, including, many historians believe, thousands of Jewish prisoners, brought to Rome after the fall of Jerusalem, in the year 70. And an inscription, found in the Colosseum, suggests that it was paid for with the treasure taken from the temple in Jerusalem.

The cranes were worked by men walking inside great wooden wheels, treadwheels, which turned a drum, and wound up a rope, and lifted the heavy blocks of stone, up to the top of the walls.

{img:architect-plans-wax-tablet-measuring-arches-site-sunlight}
The architect is unknown. We do not know his name. But he designed a building so well, so strong, and so practical, that it has stood for nearly two thousand years, through earthquakes, and fires, and wars, and centuries of people taking its stones, and is still, more or less, standing today.

--

And beside the amphitheatre, Nero's giant bronze statue, the Colossus, still stood, but with a new head: not Nero's, but the sun god's, with rays around it, like a crown.

The Romans began to call the place near the statue the place of the Colossus. And later, in the Middle Ages, the amphitheatre itself took its name from the statue: the Colosseum.

{img:bronze-colossus-sun-god-rays-crown-beside-amphitheatre-blue-sky}
There is an old saying, written down by a monk, called Bede, in England, in the eighth century: as long as the Colosseum stands, Rome shall stand. When the Colosseum falls, Rome shall fall. And when Rome falls, the world shall end.
