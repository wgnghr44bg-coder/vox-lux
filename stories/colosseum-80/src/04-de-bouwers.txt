How was the Colosseum built?

Work began around the year 72 under Vespasian. It opens in the year 80 under Titus. That is only about eight years, for one of the largest buildings in the world. It is an enormous feat of engineering and organisation.

--

First the builders drained Nero's lake. Then they dug down and laid huge foundations. A great ring of concrete, twelve metres deep, runs under the whole building, to carry its enormous weight on the soft, wet ground of the old lake.

Then they built the walls, arches and vaults. They used the creamy white stone for the main pillars and outer walls. They used a lighter volcanic stone and bricks for the inner walls. And they used concrete, the great Roman invention. It is made of lime, volcanic ash, rubble and water, and it sets as hard as rock.

They needed an enormous amount of stone. It was brought from the quarries in ox carts along a special road, perhaps more than two hundred cartloads a day, for years. The blocks were not held with mortar. They were held together with iron clamps, perhaps three hundred tons of iron. In the Middle Ages, people dug out the clamps to reuse the iron. They left holes that can still be seen all over the walls today.

--

Thousands of people worked on it. Architects and engineers, stone cutters and masons, carpenters and sculptors, labourers and ox-cart drivers. Many were skilled free craftsmen, well paid. Many others were slaves and prisoners. Many historians believe that thousands of them were Jewish prisoners, brought to Rome after the fall of Jerusalem. An old inscription suggests the building was paid for with treasure taken from the temple in Jerusalem.

The cranes were worked by men walking inside huge wooden wheels. As the wheel turned, it wound up a rope and lifted heavy stone blocks to the top of the walls.

{img:architect-plans-wax-tablet-measuring-arches-site-sunlight}
We do not know the name of the architect. But he designed a building so strong and so practical that it has stood for nearly two thousand years, through earthquakes, fires, wars and centuries of people taking its stones. And it is still mostly standing today.

--

Next to the amphitheatre, Nero's giant bronze statue still stood. But it had been given a new head. Not Nero's, but the sun god's, with rays around it like a crown.

The Romans began to call that spot the place of the Colossus. And later, in the Middle Ages, the amphitheatre took its name from the statue. It became the Colosseum.

{img:bronze-colossus-sun-god-rays-crown-beside-amphitheatre-blue-sky}
There is an old saying, written down by a monk in England more than a thousand years ago. As long as the Colosseum stands, Rome shall stand. When the Colosseum falls, Rome shall fall. And when Rome falls, the world shall end.
