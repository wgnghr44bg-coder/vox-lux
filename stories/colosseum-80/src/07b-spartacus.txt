The most famous gladiator in history lived a hundred and fifty years before the Colosseum. His name was Spartacus.

He was a Thracian, from the mountains of what is now Bulgaria. The Roman writers say that he served in the Roman army, as an auxiliary, then deserted, and was captured, and sold as a slave, and sent to a gladiator school, at Capua, near Naples, owned by a man called Lentulus Batiatus.

--

In the year 73 before Christ, Spartacus, and about seventy of his fellow gladiators, escaped from the school, armed with knives, and spits, from the kitchen. On the road, they found wagons, carrying gladiators' weapons, and took them. And they went up onto the slopes of Mount Vesuvius, which was then a quiet, green, wooded mountain, and camped there.

Thousands of slaves, and poor farm workers, ran away from the farms, and the estates, of southern Italy, and joined them. Spartacus became the leader of a great army, of perhaps seventy thousand, or more, slaves and free poor men, and women, and children.

{img:vesuvius-wooded-slopes-camp-escaped-slaves-vines-ropes-legend}
The Roman army sent to besiege them, on Vesuvius, blocked the only path down the mountain. But Spartacus's followers made ropes, and ladders, from the wild vines, which grew on the mountain, and climbed down the cliffs, on the other side, at night, and attacked the Romans from behind, and won.

--

For two years, Spartacus, and his army, marched up and down Italy, and defeated one Roman army after another. The Roman writers, who hated him, still admired him. Plutarch says that he was not only brave, and strong, but also intelligent, and gentle, more like a Greek than a barbarian.

He wanted, it seems, to lead his followers out of Italy, over the Alps, so that they could go home, to their own countries. But many of them wanted to stay, and go on fighting.

{img:mountain-pass-alps-distant-army-turning-back-snow-peaks}
In the end, in the year 71 before Christ, Spartacus was defeated, by the Roman general Crassus, in the south of Italy, and fell in battle. His body was never found. We will not describe what happened to his followers.

--

But his name was never forgotten. Two thousand years later, Spartacus became a hero, a symbol of the struggle for freedom, of the oppressed against the powerful. There are books, and a famous ballet, by the Russian composer Khachaturian, and a famous film, made in 1960, with Kirk Douglas, in which, at the end, when the Romans ask the captured slaves which of them is Spartacus, they all stand up, one after another, and say: I am Spartacus.
