In the summer of the year 80, the emperor Titus opens the Flavian Amphitheatre, with games that last for a hundred days.

People come to Rome from all over the empire, and beyond, to see them. The poet Martial, in his little book about the games, says that there was no people so far away, or so barbarous, that it did not send someone to Rome, to see them: farmers from the mountains of Thrace, and Sarmatians, who drink the blood of their horses, and people from the source of the Nile, and the shores of the Atlantic, and Arabs, and Sabaeans, and Cilicians, sprinkled with saffron, and Sygambrians, with their hair tied in knots, and Ethiopians, with their curly hair. All speaking different languages, Martial says, and yet all speaking with one voice, when they called Titus the father of his country.

--

The games are the greatest ever given in Rome, up to that time. The historian Cassius Dio says that nine thousand animals, tame and wild, were shown, during the hundred days. Women took part in the hunts. There were gladiator fights, and animal hunts, and shows, and even, it is said, a naval battle.

Because, at the very beginning, before the underground passages were built, the arena of the Colosseum could be flooded with water, through pipes, and channels, and drained again. And the Romans put on a show of a sea battle, with real ships, small ones, rowed by men, re-enacting an ancient battle, between the Corinthians and the Corcyraeans, in the arena, filled with water, and horses and bulls, trained to swim.

{img:flooded-arena-small-ships-water-show-crowd-amazed-distant}
Scholars still argue about whether this really happened in the Colosseum, or in another place, nearby. But Martial, and Suetonius, and Cassius Dio, all describe water shows, at the opening games.

--

On the last day of the games, Suetonius says, Titus wept openly, in front of the people. Perhaps he was tired. Perhaps he knew that he was not well. A year later, he was dead.

Martial wrote, in his book of the games, that the Colosseum was the greatest wonder of the world, greater than the pyramids of Egypt, and the walls of Babylon, and the temple of Artemis at Ephesus, and the Mausoleum of Halicarnassus, and all the other wonders of the past: let barbarous Memphis cease to speak of the wonders of its pyramids, he wrote. Every work gives way to Caesar's amphitheatre. Fame will speak of one work instead of all.

{img:crowd-leaving-colosseum-evening-arches-lamps-streets-filling}
And in the evenings, during the hundred days, the streets around the Colosseum were full of people: going home, after the games, talking, and laughing, and arguing about the gladiators, and the animals, and the shows, and stopping at the taverns, and the food stalls, and the shops, in the warm summer night.
