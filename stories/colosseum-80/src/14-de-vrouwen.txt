Women are an important part of the life around the Colosseum, in many ways.

In the seats, they sit at the top, in the wooden gallery, under the colonnade, separately from the men, by the law of Augustus. Only the Vestal Virgins, and the women of the emperor's family, sit in front, near the arena.

--

But women are passionate fans of the games, and of the gladiators. On the walls of Pompeii, in the gladiator barracks there, there are graffiti, scratched by gladiators, or their admirers: Celadus, the Thracian, the sigh of the girls. And: Crescens, the net fighter, doctor of the night-time girls. The gladiators were the heartthrobs of their time.

The Roman satirist Juvenal, a few years later, tells a story, perhaps exaggerated, of a senator's wife, Eppia, who left her husband, and her children, and her comfortable life in Rome, and ran away, to Egypt, with a gladiator called Sergius, who was not even handsome, Juvenal says, with a scarred face, and a bad eye, and a lump on his nose. But he was a gladiator. And that was enough.

{img:pompeii-wall-graffiti-gladiator-names-scratched-plaster}
And there were women gladiators.

--

At the opening games of the Colosseum, Cassius Dio says, women took part in the animal hunts. And at other games, in the following years, women fought as gladiators, in the arena. The emperor Domitian, the brother of Titus, held games at night, by torchlight, with women gladiators.

There is a stone relief, found at Halicarnassus, in Turkey, now in the British Museum, which shows two women gladiators, fighting each other, with shields, and swords, without helmets, with their stage names carved above them: Amazon, and Achillia. And an inscription says that both were released, honourably, after a fight that ended in a draw.

{img:stone-relief-two-women-gladiators-amazon-achillia-museum-light}
Many Romans disapproved of women gladiators, and in the year 200, the emperor Septimius Severus banned them. But for more than a century, there were some women who fought in the arenas of the Roman world.

--

And there were women in all the other jobs around the games: selling food, and drink, and cushions, and souvenirs, outside the Colosseum. Running the taverns, and the inns, and the cook shops, where the crowds ate, before and after the games. Weaving the cloth for the awning, and the costumes, and the banners. And, in the households of the gladiators, wives, and mothers, and daughters, waiting at home, after every fight, for news.
