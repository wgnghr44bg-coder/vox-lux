TOPIC: Christmas morning, and a game of football
===
IMG: christmas-dawn-frost-mist-no-mans-land-gold | Christmas morning 1914 dawning over frosty Flanders fields, pink and golden mist over No Man's Land, perfectly still
HEAD: Christmas morning 1914 dawned cold, clear and frosty over Flanders.
BODY: On the morning of the twenty-fifth of December, the sun rose over a landscape covered in white frost. A thin mist hung over the fields and turned pink and gold in the early light. Soldiers stood to at dawn as usual, watching No Man's Land. In many sectors, there was no firing. Historians describe how, in several places, German soldiers climbed onto their parapets and waved, sometimes holding up signs wishing the British a merry Christmas. Some shouted greetings in English. The British watched cautiously at first. Raising a head above the parapet was normally very dangerous. Even so, nobody fired. The truce did not begin at the same time everywhere. In some places, contacts had already started on Christmas Eve. In others, they began only on Christmas morning or not at all. Estimates suggest that tens of thousands of soldiers, perhaps around a hundred thousand, took part in some form along the British front.
===
IMG: british-soldier-climbing-out-trench-hesitant | A British soldier in a 1914 greatcoat climbing hesitantly out of his trench onto the frosty field on Christmas morning, others watching
HEAD: On Christmas morning, soldiers from both sides climbed out of their trenches and met in No Man's Land.
BODY: Encouraged by the friendly gestures, British soldiers began to climb out of their trenches and stand in the open. When nobody fired, more followed. Slowly, soldiers on both sides walked into No Man's Land and met in the middle, among the frozen shell holes and frosted grass. They shook hands and wished each other a merry Christmas. Historians note that officers often took the lead in arranging these meetings, agreeing on boundaries and times. In some places, officers met first to agree a truce, and their men followed. In others, ordinary soldiers acted on their own initiative. Many letters describe the strange feeling of standing in the open, in daylight, close to the enemy. Even so, the soldiers remained cautious. Most stayed within a short distance of their own lines. Some kept their rifles nearby. Yet the meetings were generally friendly. Soldiers exchanged greetings in a mixture of English, German and gestures.
===
IMG: soldiers-exchanging-photos-cigars-frost | British and German soldiers in 1914 exchanging cigarettes, cigars and showing each other family photographs in a frosty field, smiling
HEAD: During the truce, soldiers exchanged gifts such as food, cigarettes, buttons and badges.
BODY: Once the soldiers met, they began to exchange small gifts. British soldiers offered tins of bully beef, chocolate, cigarettes and Christmas pudding. German soldiers gave sausages, cigars, schnapps and beer. Many swapped buttons, cap badges and other souvenirs, and showed each other photographs of their families. Some Germans spoke good English, having worked in Britain before the war. One German soldier, according to British accounts, had been a barber in London and offered haircuts in No Man's Land. Near Saint-Yvon, Bruce Bairnsfather went out and met soldiers of the Saxon regiment. He exchanged buttons with a German officer and later described the scene in his memoir. Historians note that Kurt Zehmisch, on the German side, recorded the same meetings in his diary. Even so, the two accounts differ in details, such as times and who spoke first. Together, they confirm that friendly exchanges took place across this part of the line.
===
IMG: german-officer-talking-english-soldiers-smiling | A young German officer talking with smiling British soldiers in a frosty field on Christmas Day 1914, friendly conversation
HEAD: Near Armentières, German soldiers rolled barrels of beer across to the Royal Welch Fusiliers.
BODY: In the sector held by the Royal Welch Fusiliers near Armentières, the truce included an exchange of drink. According to Frank Richards, the Germans rolled two barrels of beer across No Man's Land as a Christmas present. The British soldiers drank it and shared their own supplies in return. Richards noted, with characteristic honesty, that the French beer was rather weak. Historians often quote this detail because it adds a human, humorous touch to the story. In the same sector, German and British soldiers talked, exchanged gifts and wished each other well. Richards also described a German soldier who spoke excellent English, having worked in England before the war. Even so, Richards was careful to note that the truce was a local arrangement between soldiers. It was not approved by senior commanders. His account, written many years later, remains one of the most vivid descriptions of Christmas Day in the trenches.
===
IMG: many-soldiers-mingling-no-mans-land-sunshine | Many British and German soldiers in 1914 uniforms mingling together in a frosty field in bright winter sunshine, talking and laughing, trenches in the distance
HEAD: A few soldiers took photographs of the Christmas truce, although cameras were officially forbidden.
BODY: British soldiers were not allowed to carry cameras at the front, but some had brought small ones from home anyway. During the truce, several took photographs. The pictures show British and German soldiers standing together in No Man's Land, in greatcoats and caps, smiling at the camera. Some of these photographs were published in British newspapers in January 1915. Historians note that they are among the most important pieces of evidence for the truce. They prove that the events described in letters really took place. The photographs are now held in museums such as the Imperial War Museum in London. Even so, there are fewer images than many people imagine. Only a small number of genuine photographs exist, mostly from the area around Ploegsteert. Some images often shown today are from later reconstructions or films. Historians therefore treat pictures of the truce carefully, checking their origins before using them as evidence.
===
IMG: football-kicked-frosty-field-soldiers-greatcoats | British and German soldiers in greatcoats kicking a football about on a frozen bumpy field on Christmas Day 1914, laughing, bright winter sun
HEAD: In some places along the line, soldiers played football during the Christmas truce.
BODY: Football is one of the best-known parts of the story of the Christmas truce. Several letters and diaries mention soldiers kicking a ball around in No Man's Land. Historians note, however, that the evidence is limited. Most reports describe informal kickabouts rather than organised matches with teams and scores. The frozen and broken ground made proper games difficult. Some soldiers used a real football, and others used a tin or a bundle of rags. Even so, the evidence is strong enough to show that football took place in at least a few locations. A German officer, Johannes Niemann of the 133rd Saxon Regiment, later described a match in which the Germans won three goals to two. A British letter also mentioned a game ending with a three to two score. Historians debate whether these accounts refer to the same event. Football became the central image of the truce in popular memory, especially in the late twentieth century.
===
IMG: diary-page-candle-german-handwriting | An old diary open by candlelight with German handwriting in pencil, a soldier's cap beside it, calm
HEAD: Some accounts describe soldiers teaching each other games and the ball bursting on barbed wire.
BODY: Several surviving accounts mention details about play during the truce. One German account describes a game that had to stop when the ball landed on barbed wire and burst. Others describe British soldiers trying to teach the Germans their favourite games, and Germans showing British soldiers their own. Football was very popular in Britain by 1914, and it was growing in Germany as well. Historians use diaries, letters and later interviews with veterans to piece together these stories. Some details were recorded at the time, and others were remembered decades later. Memory can change over time, so historians compare different sources carefully. Even so, a consistent picture emerges. Soldiers on both sides enjoyed a rare chance to relax, play and laugh together. For a few hours, the frozen fields of No Man's Land became a place of games rather than war. Many participants later remembered these scenes as the highlight of the day.
===
IMG: soldiers-swapping-caps-laughing-frosty-field | British and German soldiers laughing as they swap caps and helmets in a frosty field on Christmas Day 1914
HEAD: Not all soldiers took part in the truce, and fighting continued in some parts of the line.
BODY: The Christmas truce was widespread but not universal. In some sectors, fighting continued on Christmas Day, and soldiers were killed. In others, officers ordered their men to remain in the trenches, and contact was limited to shouted greetings. French and Belgian troops, whose countries had been invaded, were generally less willing to take part than the British. Historians note that recent fighting and local commanders strongly influenced whether a truce took place. One famous story describes soldiers from both sides chasing a hare across No Man's Land together, laughing as they slipped on the frozen ground, until someone caught it. Such incidents show the relaxed mood in places where the truce held. Even so, many soldiers remained suspicious of the enemy. Some disapproved of the truce altogether. Among them was a young German soldier named Adolf Hitler, who, according to a fellow soldier, criticised the fraternisation. The truce was a local and varied event.
===
IMG: soldiers-walking-back-trenches-sunset-frost | Soldiers walking back toward their trenches across a frosty field at sunset on Christmas Day 1914, waving goodbye, golden light
HEAD: Many soldiers wrote home describing the truce as the strangest and most memorable Christmas of their lives.
BODY: As the short winter day ended, soldiers returned to their trenches. Many wrote letters that evening or in the days that followed. They described the meetings, the gifts, the photographs and the games. Some wrote that it was the most extraordinary Christmas they had ever known. Historians have collected hundreds of these letters. They are held in archives, museums and family collections. Many were published in local newspapers in Britain in early 1915. Henry Williamson wrote to his mother on the twenty-sixth of December, describing how he had talked with German soldiers and exchanged gifts. Even so, the letters vary in tone. Some soldiers were joyful, while others were puzzled or uneasy. A few expressed concern that the truce might be a trick. Yet together, the letters show that the experience made a deep impression. For many families at home, these letters were the first news that such a thing had happened.

TOPIC: Christmas night, and the peace
===
IMG: christmas-night-trench-dugout-candles-dinner | British soldiers sharing Christmas pudding and dinner in a candlelit dugout on Christmas night 1914, souvenirs in their hands, warm and happy
HEAD: On the evening of Christmas Day, soldiers ate Christmas dinners in their trenches and billets.
BODY: Christmas dinner in the trenches was simple but welcome. British soldiers ate bully beef, stew and potatoes, with Christmas pudding from home heated over small fires. Some had a little rum. In a few places, soldiers had bought geese or chickens from local farms. Officers sometimes shared special meals with their men. Behind the lines, units in rest areas enjoyed larger dinners organised by their officers, sometimes with roast meat, plum pudding and beer. Historians note that army authorities made considerable efforts to provide festive food for the troops. Plum puddings were sent in large numbers. German soldiers also had special meals, with sausages, cakes and beer. Even so, conditions in the front line limited what could be prepared. Many soldiers remembered the company more than the food. Singing continued in many places on Christmas night. In some sectors, it was agreed that the truce would continue for another day.
===
IMG: family-reading-newspaper-fireside-january-1915 | An English family reading a newspaper together by the fireside in January 1915, amazed and moved faces, warm lamplight
HEAD: Newspapers in Britain published letters and photographs of the truce in early January 1915.
BODY: When letters describing the truce reached Britain, families shared them with local newspapers. National papers also published reports and photographs. On the eighth of January 1915, the Daily Mirror printed photographs of British and German soldiers together in No Man's Land. Historians note that British newspapers were generally sympathetic. They presented the truce as a moving example of Christmas spirit. Some readers were shocked, believing it was wrong to fraternise with the enemy. Most, however, were moved. In Germany, newspapers reported the truce less widely. In France, where much territory was occupied, there was less sympathy for such contacts. In many places, the truce did not end on Christmas Day. On Boxing Day, soldiers met again, exchanged more gifts and buried their dead. Even so, the length of the truce varied. In some sectors, it lasted only a few hours. In others, it continued for several days, and in a few places, into the new year.
===
IMG: officer-reading-orders-candle-dugout-january | A British officer reading a paper of orders by candlelight in a dugout in January 1915, serious and thoughtful
HEAD: After Christmas, firing resumed gradually along the line, and senior commanders forbade further truces.
BODY: In the days after Christmas, the truce came to an end in most places. Officers received orders to resume hostilities. In some sectors, both sides agreed on a signal to mark the end of the truce. According to one account, a British officer and a German officer met in No Man's Land, saluted each other, shook hands and returned to their trenches before firing began again. In other places, soldiers deliberately fired high for a time. Historians note that senior commanders on both sides were concerned about the truce. They feared that friendly contact would weaken the willingness to fight. Orders were issued to prevent fraternisation in the future. In later years, there were some smaller truces, but nothing on the scale of 1914. Even so, the memory of the truce remained among those who had taken part. The war continued for nearly four more years and became far more destructive than anyone had expected.
===
IMG: winter-fields-flanders-snow-quiet-dusk | Flat Flanders fields covered in light snow at dusk, poplar trees, a quiet trench line in the distance, calm
HEAD: Many veterans remembered the Christmas truce for the rest of their lives.
BODY: For the soldiers who took part, the Christmas truce became a lasting memory. Many talked about it decades later, describing the candles on the parapets, the singing in the night, the handshakes in No Man's Land and the football on frozen ground. Historians who interviewed veterans in later years found that the truce remained one of their clearest recollections of the war. In the 1960s and 1980s, new books and documentary films brought renewed attention to the event. Historians such as Malcolm Brown and Shirley Seaton collected many accounts and published a detailed study of the truce. Even so, memory changed over time. Some details became exaggerated or merged with popular images. Historians therefore compare later memories with letters and diaries written in 1914. The truce did not shorten the war or change its course. Yet it showed that soldiers on both sides could see each other as fellow human beings, even in the middle of war.
===
IMG: november-morning-mist-fields-1918-quiet | A quiet misty November morning in 1918 over flat fields, bare trees, soldiers standing in the open, still and silent
HEAD: On the eleventh of November 1918, the fighting of the First World War came to an end.
BODY: Nearly four years after the first Christmas in the trenches, the war ended with an armistice. It was signed early in the morning of the eleventh of November 1918, in a railway carriage in the forest of Compiègne in northern France. The fighting stopped at eleven o'clock that morning, on the eleventh day of the eleventh month. Along the Western Front, the guns fell silent. Church bells rang in Britain, France, Belgium and other countries. Historians note that around nine million soldiers died during the war, along with millions of civilians. Flanders had seen some of the fiercest fighting, including several battles around Ypres. The landscape of 1914, with its farms, woods and villages, had been almost completely destroyed in places. Even so, the end of the war brought enormous relief. Soldiers who had survived began to return home. Many of those who had taken part in the Christmas truce had been killed or wounded in the years that followed.
===
IMG: village-church-bells-ringing-crowd-1918 | A crowd celebrating outside an English village church in November 1918, bells ringing, flags, joyful and moving
HEAD: After the armistice, soldiers slowly returned home to their families over the following months.
BODY: When the war ended, people celebrated in many countries. In Britain, France and Belgium, crowds gathered in the streets, and church bells rang. In Germany, people were relieved that the war was over, though their country had lost and faced political turmoil and hardship. Demobilisation, the process of sending soldiers home, took many months. Soldiers travelled by ship and train back to their towns and villages. Historians note that returning home was not always easy. Many soldiers had been physically or mentally injured. Others found that jobs and homes were scarce. Families had changed while they were away. Even so, many veterans rebuilt their lives. They returned to work, married and raised families. Some rarely spoke about the war. Others joined veterans' associations and kept in touch with old comrades. The memory of the war shaped the rest of their lives, and many remembered both its horrors and its rare moments of humanity.
===
IMG: flemish-family-returning-ruined-village-spring | A Flemish family with a handcart returning to their ruined village in spring 1919, green shoots in the fields, hopeful
HEAD: After the war, the people of Flanders returned and rebuilt their farms, villages and towns.
BODY: By 1918, large parts of Flanders were devastated. Villages had been reduced to rubble, forests destroyed and fields churned into mud and craters. Many civilians had fled during the war. After the armistice, they began to return. They cleared the land, filled in trenches and shell holes, removed unexploded shells and ploughed the fields again. Historians note that the reconstruction of Flanders took many years and was supported by the Belgian government. Farmhouses, churches and town halls were rebuilt, often in traditional styles. In Ypres, the medieval Cloth Hall, almost completely destroyed, was rebuilt over several decades and completed in 1967. Even so, the war left lasting marks. Farmers still find unexploded shells and other remains in their fields every year. These are collected by the Belgian army, a practice sometimes called the iron harvest. Yet the countryside recovered, and farms once again surrounded the villages.
===
IMG: ypres-cloth-hall-rebuilt-market-square-evening | A large medieval-style cloth hall with a tall belfry on a market square in a Flemish town at evening, soft lights, peaceful
HEAD: In Ypres, the Menin Gate memorial was unveiled in 1927 to remember soldiers with no known grave.
BODY: After the war, the Imperial War Graves Commission, now known as the Commonwealth War Graves Commission, built cemeteries and memorials across France and Belgium. Soldiers who died were buried close to where they fell, in cemeteries of white headstones. Those whose bodies were never found or identified were remembered on large memorials. In Ypres, the Menin Gate was built on the road leading out of the town toward the battlefields. It was designed by the architect Reginald Blomfield and unveiled in 1927. Historians note that the memorials and cemeteries of Flanders form one of the largest commemorative landscapes in the world. Hundreds of cemeteries are scattered across the countryside, in fields, beside roads and at the edges of woods. Even so, many visitors are surprised by their quiet and order. Among those remembered are soldiers who took part in the Christmas truce of 1914, many of whom were killed in the years that followed.
===
IMG: red-poppies-field-flanders-summer-sky | A field of red poppies in flat Flanders countryside under a summer sky, poplar trees, peaceful
HEAD: After the war, people began wearing red poppies every November in memory of those who died.
BODY: The red poppy became a symbol of remembrance because poppies grew in large numbers on the disturbed ground of the battlefields. In 1915, the Canadian doctor John McCrae wrote a poem called In Flanders Fields, which describes poppies growing between the crosses of soldiers' graves. The poem became very popular. After the war, an American teacher named Moina Michael began wearing a poppy in memory of the dead, and the idea spread. In 1921, the British Legion adopted the poppy as its symbol and sold paper poppies to raise money for veterans. Historians note that the poppy remains one of the most widely recognised symbols of remembrance. Every year, on the eleventh of November, people in many countries observe two minutes of silence at eleven o'clock in the morning. Even so, customs vary between countries. In some places, other flowers, such as the cornflower in France, are used instead.

TOPIC: Their lives, and Flanders today
===
IMG: writer-cottage-devon-river-otter-evening | A small thatched cottage by a river in Devon in the 1920s, a man writing at a window, an otter in the river, evening light
HEAD: Henry Williamson survived the war and became a famous writer about nature and the countryside.
BODY: Henry Williamson served throughout the war, from the Christmas truce in Ploegsteert Wood to the later stages of the conflict. He was commissioned as an officer and served in several units. After the war, he found it difficult to settle in London. He moved to North Devon and lived in a cottage in the village of Georgeham. There, he wrote about the countryside and its wildlife. His most famous book, Tarka the Otter, published in 1927, follows the life of an otter in the rivers of Devon. It won a major literary prize and remains popular today. Williamson also wrote a long series of novels about the war and the years around it. Historians note that his writing about the Christmas truce helped keep its memory alive. Even so, Williamson was a controversial figure. In the 1930s, he expressed support for fascist ideas, which damaged his reputation. He died in 1977.
===
IMG: cartoonist-older-drawing-studio-sketches | An older cartoonist with a moustache drawing at a desk in a studio in the 1920s, cartoon sketches pinned around him, warm light
HEAD: Bruce Bairnsfather described the Christmas truce in his book Bullets and Billets, published in 1916.
BODY: In 1916, Bruce Bairnsfather published a memoir called Bullets and Billets. In it, he described his experiences in the trenches, including the Christmas truce at Saint-Yvon. He wrote that he would not have missed that Christmas for anything. He described meeting German soldiers, exchanging buttons and watching a German barber cutting a British soldier's hair. Bairnsfather's cartoon character, Old Bill, became famous across Britain. His work was adapted into a successful stage play called The Better 'Ole, and later into films. During the Second World War, he worked as an official cartoonist with American forces in Europe. He died in 1959. Frank Richards also survived the whole war, serving from 1914 to 1918, which was rare for a soldier who had been at the front from the beginning. He later published Old Soldiers Never Die in 1933. Historians value both books as vivid, personal records of the war.
===
IMG: german-schoolteacher-classroom-1920s-chalkboard | A German schoolteacher teaching children in a classroom in the 1920s, a chalkboard and wooden desks, calm morning light
HEAD: Kurt Zehmisch returned to teaching after the war, and his diary was later rediscovered by his son.
BODY: Kurt Zehmisch survived the war and returned to Saxony, where he worked as a teacher. In his diary, he wrote that the day of the Christmas truce had been wonderful and strange, and that he would never forget it. His diary remained with his family for many years. His son, Rudolf Zehmisch, later found it and shared it with historians. It has since been used in books, documentaries and exhibitions about the truce. Historians value it because it offers a German perspective on the same events described by Bruce Bairnsfather. Even so, it reflects the view of one officer in one sector. Many soldiers kept souvenirs from the truce. The brass Princess Mary gift boxes were kept by thousands of British soldiers as treasured reminders. Today, many survive in museums and family homes across Britain. Some still contain their original contents, more than a century later, preserved as quiet links to the first Christmas of the war.
===
IMG: very-old-veteran-armchair-telling-story-grandchild | A very old man in an armchair telling a story to a child, a framed photograph of young soldiers on the wall, soft lamplight
HEAD: Alfred Anderson, one of the last surviving veterans of the Christmas truce, died in 2005 at the age of 109.
BODY: Alfred Anderson was born in 1896 in Scotland. In 1914, he served with the Black Watch, a Scottish regiment, in France. He took part in the Christmas truce, though his unit did not meet the Germans in No Man's Land. In later interviews, he remembered the silence of Christmas morning more than anything else. He described it as an eerie silence and recalled that soldiers shouted greetings across the lines. Anderson later served as a batman, an officer's servant, and was wounded in 1916. After the war, he returned to Scotland and worked as a joiner. He became widely known in his final years as one of the last links to the truce. Historians note that interviews with very old veterans must be treated carefully, because memories fade and change. Even so, his recollections were consistent over many years. When he died in 2005, his death was reported widely in Britain and abroad.
===
IMG: flanders-today-green-fields-poplars-church-spire | Peaceful green Flemish farmland today with rows of poplar trees, a farmhouse with red roof and a church spire, a wide sky
HEAD: Today, Flanders is once again a peaceful farming region with fields, villages and poplar-lined roads.
BODY: More than a century after the war, the landscape of Flanders has largely returned to its former character. Flat fields stretch under wide skies, green in spring and summer, golden at harvest and brown in winter. Poplar trees line the roads, and farmhouses with red-tiled roofs stand among the fields. Cows graze in pastures that were once part of No Man's Land. Historians and visitors note that traces of the war remain, if one knows where to look. Small cemeteries appear among the fields, and memorials stand at crossroads and village centres. Some trench lines and craters are preserved at sites such as Hill 60 and Sanctuary Wood. Even so, everyday life in Flanders continues as in any rural area. Farmers plough their fields, children go to school and markets are held in the towns. Tourism linked to the war is an important part of the local economy, bringing many visitors each year.
===
IMG: small-cemetery-inside-wood-bluebells-spring | A small war cemetery with white headstones among the trees of a wood in spring, bluebells on the ground, peaceful
HEAD: The Ploegsteert Memorial records the names of more than eleven thousand soldiers with no known grave.
BODY: Near Ploegsteert Wood stands a memorial built by the Imperial War Graves Commission and unveiled in 1931. It is a circular building of white stone, guarded by two stone lions. On its walls are carved the names of more than eleven thousand soldiers who died in the surrounding area and have no known grave. Inside and around the wood, several small cemeteries contain the graves of soldiers who died nearby. In spring, bluebells cover the ground among the trees. Historians note that the area of Ploegsteert is closely associated with the Christmas truce. Near Saint-Yvon, where Bruce Bairnsfather and Kurt Zehmisch met in 1914, a simple wooden cross was erected in 1999 to mark the truce. Visitors often leave small footballs, poppies and messages beside it. Even so, the site remains modest and quiet. The fields around it are farmed as usual, and few signs of the war remain on the surface.
===
IMG: wooden-cross-field-footballs-poppies-winter | A simple wooden memorial cross in a winter field beside a road, footballs, poppies, scarves and candles left at its foot
HEAD: In December 2014, a memorial to the Christmas truce was unveiled near Ploegsteert to mark its hundredth anniversary.
BODY: The centenary of the Christmas truce was marked by many events in 2014. Near Ploegsteert, a new memorial was unveiled in December, in a ceremony attended by football officials and representatives from several countries. In Britain, soldiers from the British and German armies played a commemorative football match. Schools, football clubs and churches held events to remember the truce. Historians note that the anniversary brought new interest in the original sources. Letters, diaries and photographs were displayed in exhibitions and published online. Many families shared documents they had kept for generations. Even so, historians reminded the public that the truce was a brief and limited event. It did not end the war, and in most sectors fighting resumed within days. Yet the centenary showed how deeply the story continues to matter. It is remembered as an example of peace and shared humanity in the middle of a terrible war.
===
IMG: menin-gate-evening-buglers-crowd-quiet | A great white stone memorial arch at evening, buglers in uniform playing, a quiet crowd standing in respect, soft lights
HEAD: Every evening at eight o'clock, buglers play the Last Post under the Menin Gate in Ypres.
BODY: Since 1928, members of the local fire brigade in Ypres have sounded the Last Post at the Menin Gate each evening. The ceremony was interrupted only during the German occupation of Belgium in the Second World War, when it was held in England instead. It resumed on the evening Ypres was liberated in September 1944. The walls of the Menin Gate bear the names of more than fifty-four thousand soldiers of the British Empire who died in the Ypres area and have no known grave. Every evening, visitors gather in silence as the buglers play, and wreaths of poppies are laid. Historians note that the ceremony has become one of the most famous acts of remembrance in the world. At Christmas, local communities in Flanders also remember the truce with concerts, exhibitions and football matches. Even so, the daily ceremony remains simple. A few minutes of music and silence end the day under the great stone arch.
===
IMG: winter-dusk-flanders-farmhouse-lights-stars-frost | A Flemish farmhouse with warm lit windows at winter dusk, frost on the flat fields, poplar trees and the first stars
HEAD: The Christmas truce of 1914 is remembered as a brief moment of peace in a long and terrible war.
BODY: The truce took place during the first winter of the First World War, in the frozen fields of Flanders and northern France. Along much of the British front, soldiers stopped fighting, sang carols, exchanged gifts, shook hands and in some places played football. Bruce Bairnsfather, Frank Richards, Henry Williamson, Kurt Zehmisch and many others recorded what they saw. Historians have used their letters, diaries and memoirs, together with photographs, to reconstruct the events. The truce did not change the course of the war. It lasted only a short time, and the fighting continued for nearly four more years. Even so, it has become one of the most famous episodes of the twentieth century. It shows that ordinary people on opposite sides could share moments of kindness and hope. Today, in the small cemeteries among the fields, white headstones stand in rows under the winter stars, and frost settles on what was once No Man's Land.
