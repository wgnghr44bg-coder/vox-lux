TOPIC: Flanders, a flat green land
===
IMG: flanders-flat-fields-poplars-misty-morning | Flat Flemish farmland on a grey misty December morning in 1914, rows of tall poplar trees along a road, a farmhouse with a red-tiled roof, a distant church spire, ditches of still water
HEAD: In the winter of 1914, the flat, quiet farmland of Flanders lay under frost and low grey skies.
BODY: It's a grey morning in early December. Picture yourself standing in a frozen field, with your breath rising in little clouds. A low mist lies over the fields. The land here is almost perfectly flat, stretching away in every direction, with rows of tall, thin poplar trees marking the roads and the edges of the fields. And here and there a farmhouse with a red-tiled roof, a church spire in the distance, a little wood, a windmill. Ditches full of still, grey water run along the edges of the fields. Flanders is the northern part of Belgium, and the corner of France just beside it. For a thousand years, it has been a land of farmers and weavers and merchants. The soil is rich and heavy, made of clay, and the farmers grow wheat and barley, sugar beet and potatoes. And keep cows and pigs on the wet green meadows. In the towns, there are old market squares, with tall cloth halls and belfries and churches, built in the Middle Ages when Flanders was one of the richest places in Europe. The people are quiet, hardworking, devout. They speak Flemish, a kind of Dutch, in the Belgian villages, and French in the villages across the border. On Sundays, the church bells ring across the flat land. And the families walk to Mass along the muddy lanes, in their best clothes. But in the winter of 1914, the land isn't peaceful. Since August, there has been a war. It began far away, in the Balkans, and spread, in a few short weeks, across the whole of Europe. Germany and Austria-Hungary on one side. You'd feel the silence of the land all around you. France and Russia and Britain and Belgium on the other.
===
IMG: soldiers-marching-poplar-road-autumn-1914 | A column of soldiers in 1914 uniforms marching along a cobbled road lined with tall poplar trees through flat autumn fields in Belgium, seen from a distance, soft light
HEAD: In August, the German armies marched through Belgium, toward France.
BODY: Through the autumn, the armies fought and marched and fought again, across northern France and Flanders, until, in October and November, they came to a stop here, in the flat fields of Flanders, facing each other. You'd have read about it in every newspaper that summer. And dug themselves into the ground. Now, a long line of trenches runs across the land. It begins on the coast of the North Sea, near the little Belgian town of Nieuwpoort. And runs south, through the fields of Flanders, past the old town of Ypres, through the hills and woods and villages of northern France. And on, all the way to the mountains of Switzerland, more than four hundred miles away. On one side of the line, there are the German soldiers. On the other, the French and the Belgians and the British. Between them, there's a strip of empty land, sometimes only a few dozen yards wide, sometimes a few hundred, which the soldiers call No Man's Land. In this story, we'll spend a little time in one small part of that long line. It's a stretch of about thirty miles in Flanders, south of Ypres, between the Belgian village of Ploegsteert, which the British soldiers call Plugstreet. And the French town of Armentières, and the little villages around them.
===
IMG: village-church-ruined-tower-winter-sky | A Flemish village church with a damaged tower against a pale winter sky in 1914, bare trees, a few houses, quiet and still
HEAD: Here, in the winter of 1914, the British army faces the German army across the fields.
BODY: And here, on Christmas Day, something will happen that nobody expects. You'd see the two lines of trenches zigzagging across the fields. And that people will still be talking about more than a hundred years later. But that's still a few weeks away. On this grey December morning, the soldiers on both sides are waking up in their trenches, cold and stiff and muddy. And lighting little fires to boil water for their tea or their coffee. You'd hardly believe how close the two sides were. And looking out across the fields at the mist, and thinking about home.
===
IMG: soldiers-waking-trench-dawn-smoke-small-fire | British soldiers in 1914 greatcoats waking in a muddy sandbagged trench at dawn, lighting a small fire to boil water, breath steaming, grey light
HEAD: Most of them are young men.
BODY: Many of them have been soldiers for only a few months. If you'd met them, you'd have found them cheerful, homesick and very young. A few months ago, they were clerks and farmers, students and miners, shop assistants and factory workers, schoolteachers and butchers and bakers. They didn't imagine that they'd spend Christmas in a muddy ditch in Flanders. Behind the lines, in the villages and farms, the Flemish and French families are still living in their houses, as best they can. The farmers still try to look after their animals and their land. The women still bake bread, and wash clothes, and keep house. The children still go to school, when there's school. Many of the soldiers lodge in their farmhouses and barns, when they come out of the trenches to rest. The farmers' wives sell them coffee and eggs and bread and fried potatoes. And the soldiers, who are far from home, are glad of a warm kitchen and a kind face. In the evening, the mist comes down again over the fields, and the light fades early, because it's December. Along the line, the soldiers settle down for the night, in their trenches and their dugouts, wrapped in their greatcoats and their blankets. Sentries stand on watch, peering out into the darkness. Now and then, a flare goes up into the sky. Can you imagine being so far from home at that age? And drifts down slowly, lighting the fields with a pale, white light, and then goes out.
===
TOPIC: The summer of 1914 and the journey to the front
BRIDGE: The fields of Flanders are quiet under the frost. But how did all these young men come to be here? Let's go back a few months, to a warm summer evening.
===
IMG: summer-1914-english-village-cricket-sunshine | An English village cricket match on a green in the golden summer of 1914, players in white, families watching under trees, a church tower, peaceful
HEAD: The summer of 1914 was one of the most beautiful summers that anyone could remember.
BODY: In England, the days were long and warm and golden. You'd remember long, golden evenings and picnics by the river. Families went to the seaside, and sat in striped deckchairs on the beach, and paddled in the sea. In the villages, there were cricket matches on the green, and garden parties, and fêtes. In the cities, people sat in the parks in the evening, listening to brass bands playing in the bandstands. In Germany, too, it was a lovely summer. Families walked in the forests on Sundays. And sat in the beer gardens under the chestnut trees, drinking beer and eating sausages and listening to the music. Students went hiking in the mountains, singing as they walked. In the villages, the farmers brought in the harvest. Nobody, in England or in Germany or in France, imagined that within a few weeks, their countries would be at war with each other. Then, at the end of June, an Austrian archduke was shot in the city of Sarajevo, far away in the Balkans. At first, most people paid little attention. There had been troubles in the Balkans before. But this time, one thing led to another, very quickly. Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia. Russia prepared to help Serbia. Germany declared war on Russia, and then on France. You'd never have guessed what was coming. And on the fourth of August, when the German army marched into Belgium, Britain declared war on Germany.
===
IMG: crowds-outside-palace-august-evening-1914 | Large crowds in 1914 clothes and straw hats gathered outside a grand palace on a summer evening, waving flags, seen from a distance
HEAD: In London, on the evening of the fourth of August, great crowds gathered outside Buckingham Palace, singing and cheering.
BODY: In Berlin and Paris and Vienna, there were crowds too, singing and cheering in the streets. You'd hear the crowd singing outside the palace gates. Everyone said that the war would be short. It will all be over by Christmas, people said. The soldiers would march off, and fight a few great battles. And then they'd all come home, in time for Christmas dinner. In Britain, the army was small, and made up of volunteers. So the government asked for more men to join. Lord Kitchener, the Secretary of State for War, a tall, stern man with a great moustache, appeared on posters all over the country, pointing his finger, saying that his country needed you. And the young men came, in their hundreds of thousands. They queued outside the recruiting offices, in the summer sunshine, laughing and joking with their friends, eager not to miss the adventure. Many of them joined up together with their friends, from the same street, or the same factory, or the same football team, or the same office. They wanted to serve together, side by side. Whole battalions were formed of friends and neighbours, from the same town or the same trade: the Liverpool Pals, the Accrington Pals, the Grimsby Chums, the Footballers' Battalion. They marched off through the streets of their towns, with brass bands playing and flags flying, while their mothers and sisters and sweethearts waved from the pavements. And the little boys ran alongside, shouting and cheering. In Germany, too, the young men went off to war with flowers in their caps and their rifles. The trains were decorated with branches and flowers. And the soldiers wrote on the sides of the carriages, in chalk, that they were off on an outing to Paris. You'd feel caught up in the excitement, I think. Students left their universities, and workers their factories, and farmers their fields, and went off singing.
===
IMG: german-troop-train-flowers-chalk-station | A 1914 German troop train decorated with flowers and branches at a station, young soldiers leaning out of the windows smiling, families waving, sunny
HEAD: In France, the soldiers marched off in their bright blue coats and red trousers, the same uniforms their grandfathers had worn, and the crowds threw flowers at them as they passed.
BODY: But the war wasn't short. You'd see bright red trousers flashing in the summer sun. By the autumn, hundreds of thousands of men had been killed or wounded, on both sides, in great battles in Belgium and France. The armies had marched and fought across the countryside, until they were exhausted, and could go no further. Then, to protect themselves, they began to dig. And by the end of November 1914, the two great armies faced each other across a line of trenches that stretched across the whole of western Europe. Among the young men who joined the British army in that summer of 1914, there was a young clerk from London named Henry Williamson. He was eighteen years old, a thin, dreamy, sensitive young man, who loved the countryside. And the birds and the animals, and wanted to be a writer. He worked as a clerk in an office in the city of London, which he found dull. He had joined a part-time volunteer regiment, the London Rifle Brigade, a few months before the war, partly for the fun of the summer camps.
===
IMG: young-clerk-london-office-window-dreaming | A thin dreamy young clerk of eighteen in a 1914 suit sitting at a desk in a London office, looking out of the window at the sky, ledgers in front of him
HEAD: When the war began, the London Rifle Brigade was sent to France, and Henry went with them.
BODY: He turned nineteen on the first of December, in the trenches of Flanders, near Plugstreet Wood. You'd see office clerks and shop assistants marching off in new uniforms. On Christmas Day, he'd write a letter home to his mother that he'd remember for the rest of his life.
===
IMG: troopship-leaving-southampton-night-1914 | A steamship full of soldiers leaving an English port at night in 1914, lights on the quay, calm dark water, seen from a distance
HEAD: For the young British soldiers of 1914, the journey to the front began at a railway station, somewhere in England, early in the morning.
BODY: They marched through the streets of their towns to the station, with their packs on their backs and their rifles on their shoulders, while the people came out of their houses to wave and cheer. You'd feel the deck rocking under your boots as the ship left the harbour. At the station, their families were waiting on the platform, to say goodbye. There were mothers and fathers, wives and children, brothers and sisters and sweethearts. They hugged and kissed and shook hands, and tried to smile. Some of the mothers gave their sons a last packet of sandwiches, or a pair of socks, or a little Bible to keep in their pocket. Then the whistle blew, and the soldiers climbed into the carriages. And leaned out of the windows, waving, as the train pulled slowly out of the station. And the platform full of waving handkerchiefs grew smaller and smaller behind them. The train took them south, through the green English countryside, to the ports on the coast: Southampton, or Folkestone, or Dover. At the port, they boarded a ship. It was usually at night, so that German submarines wouldn't see them. The soldiers crowded onto the decks of the steamers, in the darkness, and watched the lights of England disappearing behind them. You'd watch the white cliffs fade behind you. Some of them had never been to sea before, and the crossing was rough, and many of them were seasick.
===
IMG: soldiers-on-ship-deck-night-crossing-channel | British soldiers in 1914 greatcoats crowded on the deck of a steamer crossing the Channel at night, watching distant lights, calm sea
HEAD: In the early morning, they arrived in France, at the ports of Le Havre or Boulogne or Calais, and climbed down the gangways onto the quays, stiff and cold and tired, and marched through the grey streets of the French towns to great camps of tents on the hills above the harbour.
BODY: The French people watched them come. You'd step off the ship onto the quay, stiff and sleepy. They had never seen so many British soldiers. The children ran alongside them, shouting for souvenirs and chocolate. The women waved from the windows, and threw flowers. The soldiers tried out the few words of French that they knew. And the French laughed, and tried out the few words of English that they knew. In the camps, the soldiers were given more training, and new equipment. And then, after a few days, they were put on a train to the front. The trains that took the soldiers to the front weren't passenger trains. They were goods trains, made of little wooden wagons that were usually used for carrying cattle and horses. On the side of each wagon, there were words painted in French: Hommes quarante, chevaux huit. Men forty, horses eight. This meant that each wagon could carry forty men, or eight horses.
===
IMG: soldiers-in-wooden-goods-wagon-train-france | British soldiers sitting on straw inside a small wooden goods wagon of a slow train through France in 1914, the door open to flat fields, some sleeping, some singing
HEAD: The soldiers crowded into the wagons, forty at a time, sitting on the straw on the floor, with their packs and their rifles, and the train set off, very slowly, rattling and jolting through the flat French countryside, for a whole day and a night, or longer.
BODY: They passed through little towns and villages, past fields and farms and churches, past rivers and canals and woods. You'd sit on the wooden floor, knees up, swaying with the train. At every station, the train stopped, and the soldiers climbed out to stretch their legs, and to fill their water bottles. And to buy bread and coffee and wine from the French people who came to the station to sell them. At night, they slept on the straw, curled up together for warmth, with the wagon swaying and rattling around them.
===
IMG: train-stopped-french-village-station-soldiers-coffee | A troop train stopped at a small French village station in 1914, soldiers stretching their legs, villagers selling bread and coffee from baskets, soft light
HEAD: The soldiers sang, to pass the time.
BODY: They sang the songs of the music halls, and the songs of their regiments, and the hymns of their chapels. You'd soon know all the words yourself. And their voices drifted out of the open doors of the wagons across the fields. At last, the train stopped, at a little station somewhere in the north of France, or in Belgium, near the front. The soldiers climbed out, and formed up in the road, and began to march. They marched along long, straight roads, paved with cobblestones, lined with tall poplar trees, through the flat, wet country of Flanders. The cobblestones were hard on their feet, and their boots were new and stiff, and their packs were heavy. But they kept going, singing, mile after mile, through the villages and past the farms, where the people came to their doors to watch them pass. As they got closer to the front, they began to hear a sound, far away, like distant thunder: the sound of the guns. They passed through villages where some of the houses were damaged, and the church towers were broken. They passed wagons and ambulances coming back from the front. Do you know any of those old songs? And tired, muddy soldiers marching the other way, who looked at them, the new arrivals, with tired, kindly eyes.
===
IMG: new-soldiers-passing-tired-soldiers-dusk-road | New soldiers marching up a poplar-lined road at dusk in 1914 passing tired muddy soldiers walking the other way, calm and quiet
HEAD: And at last, at dusk, they arrived at the villages behind the lines, where they'd rest for a night or two, before going up into the trenches for the first time.
BODY: They were a long way from home now. You'd see a church spire and a few lit windows ahead of you.

TOPIC: The trenches
BRIDGE: The train has stopped, and the long march is over. Now you'll see where the soldiers spent that first winter.
===
IMG: trench-sandbags-duckboards-winter-morning | A 1914 trench in Flanders with walls of sandbags and wooden duckboards on the muddy floor, zigzagging away, soldiers in greatcoats, grey winter morning
HEAD: A trench, in the winter of 1914, is a long, narrow ditch dug into the earth, about six or seven feet deep, and wide enough for two men to pass each other, if they squeeze.
BODY: It doesn't run in a straight line. You'd feel the cold mud seeping into your boots. It zigzags, every few yards, back and forth, so that if a shell falls into one part of it, the walls of earth will protect the men in the next part. In Flanders, digging trenches is difficult. The ground is flat and low, and the water lies just beneath the surface. Dig down two or three feet, and the trench begins to fill with water. So the soldiers here often can't dig very deep. Instead, they build up walls of sandbags above the ground, piled one on top of another, filled with the heavy, wet clay of the fields. The front wall of the trench, facing the enemy, is called the parapet. The back wall is called the parados. Along the bottom of the parapet, there's a little step, called the fire step, where the soldiers stand to look out over the top, or to keep watch. On the floor of the trench, the soldiers lay wooden boards, like little ladders laid flat, called duckboards, so that they can walk without sinking into the mud. But in the wet winter of 1914, the mud is everywhere. It's thick and sticky and grey and cold. And it gets into everything: into the men's boots and their clothes, their food and their blankets, their rifles and their hair. In some places, the water in the trenches comes up to the men's knees. You'd never quite get your feet dry, all winter long. And they stand in it for hours, and their feet grow numb with cold.
===
IMG: soldier-pumping-water-trench-wooden-pump | A soldier in 1914 working a wooden hand pump to clear water from a flooded trench, sandbags and mud around him, grey light
HEAD: They work all the time to keep the water out, with buckets and pumps, and to repair the walls when they fall in, which they often do, after heavy rain.
BODY: In the walls of the trench, the soldiers dig little shelters, called dugouts. You'd hear the slosh of buckets all day long. Some are just holes scooped out of the earth, big enough for a man to crawl into and curl up, out of the rain. Others are larger, with a roof of wooden planks and corrugated iron, covered with sandbags and earth. And a blanket hung over the doorway. Inside, there might be a little table made of a packing case, a candle stuck in a bottle, a few shelves for food and letters. And straw on the floor to sleep on. The officers' dugouts are a little more comfortable, with a bed of chicken wire stretched over a wooden frame. And perhaps a chair, and a little stove. Behind the front trench, there are other trenches. There's a support trench, a little way back, where more soldiers wait. And there are long, winding communication trenches, running back from the front, through which the soldiers walk to and from the trenches, carrying food and water and ammunition and post. And bringing back the wounded.
===
IMG: communication-trench-soldiers-carrying-supplies | Soldiers in 1914 carrying sacks and water cans in single file along a narrow winding communication trench, bare trees above, winter light
HEAD: A few miles further back, there are the villages and farms where the soldiers go to rest, and the roads and railways that bring up the supplies.
BODY: In front of the trenches, the soldiers have put up barbed wire. You'd hear cows and church bells there, as if there were no war at all. It's fixed to wooden posts, in long tangled rows, across the fields, to stop the enemy from coming across. Every night, men crawl out into the darkness to mend the wire, or to put up more of it, very quietly, while the sentries keep watch.
===
IMG: barbed-wire-posts-frosty-field-dawn | Rows of barbed wire on wooden posts across a frosty field at dawn in Flanders, mist beyond, quiet and still
HEAD: Beyond the wire, there's No Man's Land: a strip of muddy fields, ditches, shell holes, broken fences, a ruined farmhouse, perhaps an old haystack or a few bare trees.
BODY: And beyond that, a few hundred yards away, or sometimes much closer, there's another line of wire. You'd peer across it through a little gap between the sandbags. And sandbags, and the German trenches. In many places, the trenches are so close that the soldiers can hear each other. They can hear the Germans talking and laughing and coughing in their trenches. And the clatter of their mess tins, and sometimes their singing. They can smell the smoke of their fires, and their cooking. In the mornings, they can see the smoke rising from the German side, as the Germans make their breakfast, just as they themselves are doing. Sometimes, they shout across to each other. A German soldier who used to work as a waiter in London might call out, asking how things are in the city. A British soldier might shout back a joke. And for a moment, the enemy seems not like an enemy at all, but like another young man, far from home, cold and tired and muddy, just like themselves. The soldiers don't spend all their time in the front trenches. Usually, a battalion stays in the front line for three or four days, and then it's relieved by another battalion. And goes back to the support trenches for a few days. And then further back, to the villages, to rest, for a few days more. Then it comes back to the front again.
===
IMG: soldiers-relief-night-walking-single-file | Soldiers in 1914 greatcoats with packs walking in single file along a dark road at night during the relief, poplar trees silhouetted, quiet
HEAD: The relief takes place at night, in the dark, so that the enemy will not see.
BODY: The new men come up through the communication trenches, in single file, carrying everything they own on their backs. You'd walk in silence, keeping close to the man in front of you. And the old men file out, tired and muddy. And walk back along the dark roads to the villages, where a hot meal. You'd be glad to see the dugout at last. And a dry barn, and perhaps a bath, are waiting.
===
TOPIC: A day in the line
BRIDGE: Night has fallen over the trenches. Let's stay with the men from one dawn to the next, you and I.
===
IMG: stand-to-dawn-soldiers-fire-step-mist | British soldiers standing on the fire step of a trench before dawn in 1914, rifles ready, looking out into grey mist, breath steaming, calm
HEAD: A day in the trenches begins before dawn.
BODY: About an hour before the sun comes up, the order comes down the line, in a whisper: stand to. You'd be shaken awake in the dark and handed a tin mug of tea. All along the trench, the soldiers wake up, climb out of their dugouts, stiff and cold. And take their places on the fire step, with their rifles, looking out over the parapet into the grey darkness. This is the time when an attack is most likely to come, in the half-light before dawn. So everyone must be awake and ready. The men stand there in silence, in their greatcoats, with their breath steaming in the cold air, watching the mist slowly growing paler over No Man's Land. And the dark shapes of the trees and the farmhouses slowly appearing out of the darkness. On the other side, the German soldiers are doing exactly the same thing. Then, as the sun comes up, and nothing happens, the order comes to stand down. And the men climb down from the fire step, and think about breakfast. Breakfast is the best moment of the day. The men light little fires, in braziers made of old buckets with holes punched in them, or on little stoves. And boil water in their mess tins, and make tea. Tea is the most important thing in the British soldier's life. It's hot and sweet and strong, made with condensed milk from a tin. You'd wrap your cold fingers around the mug and feel a little better. And it warms the men from the inside, and cheers them up.
===
IMG: soldiers-brewing-tea-mess-tins-brazier | British soldiers in 1914 brewing tea in mess tins over a small brazier in a trench, frying bacon, smiling, steam rising, morning light
HEAD: With the tea, there might be bacon, fried over the fire, if the bacon has come up with the rations, and bread, or hard army biscuits, and jam.
BODY: The smell of frying bacon drifts along the trench, and the men gather round, with their hands held out to the fire. You'd smell it frying before you were properly awake. After breakfast, there's an inspection. The officers walk along the trench, looking at the men's rifles, to make sure they are clean. And their feet, to make sure they aren't suffering from the cold and the wet. The men must rub their feet with whale oil every day. And change into dry socks if they have any, to protect their feet from the damp. Then the day's work begins. There's always work to be done in the trenches. The walls must be repaired, where they have fallen in. The water must be pumped out, or bailed out with buckets. New sandbags must be filled and laid. The duckboards must be mended. Food and water and ammunition must be carried up from the rear. But during the day, it isn't safe to move around too much, or to show your head above the parapet. So much of the day is spent quietly, waiting. The soldiers pass the time as best they can. They write letters home, sitting in their dugouts, with a stub of pencil and a writing pad on their knees. They read the letters and the newspapers that come up with the post, again and again. They play cards, for cigarettes or pennies. They clean their rifles. You'd remember that smell for the rest of your life. They mend their clothes, and darn their socks.
===
IMG: soldier-writing-letter-dugout-pencil-candle | A young soldier in a 1914 greatcoat writing a letter with a pencil in a small dugout by candlelight, writing pad on his knee, calm
HEAD: They sleep, when they can, curled up in a corner of a dugout, or sitting on the fire step with their heads on their knees.
BODY: They talk, about home and their families, about football and girls and food, and about when the war will end. You'd learn to sleep almost anywhere, even standing up. They grumble about the mud, and the rain, and the food, and the officers, and the army, as soldiers always do. At midday, there's dinner. It comes up from the cookers behind the lines, in big metal containers, carried by men through the communication trenches: a stew of meat and vegetables, which the soldiers call Maconochie, after the company that makes it in tins, or a stew of corned beef, which they call bully beef. It's often cold by the time it arrives. But it's food, and the men are hungry.
===
IMG: soldiers-eating-stew-mess-tins-trench-noon | Soldiers in 1914 eating stew from mess tins sitting on the fire step of a trench at midday, chatting, winter light
HEAD: In the afternoon, the light begins to fade early, because it's winter.
BODY: By four o'clock, it's getting dark. You'd watch the sky turn grey and then dark by four o'clock. At dusk, there's another stand-to, just like the one at dawn. Then night comes, and the trench comes alive. At night, the men can move about more freely, because the enemy can't see them. Working parties go out to repair the wire and the trenches. Ration parties go back to fetch the food and the water. Patrols crawl out into No Man's Land, very quietly, to listen. The sentries stand on the fire step, two hours on and four hours off, watching the darkness. The men who aren't on duty try to sleep, wrapped in their greatcoats and blankets, in their dugouts, while the cold creeps in. And the rain patters on the roof, and the rats scurry about in the dark. And so the days go by, one after another, much the same.
===
IMG: winter-sunset-flanders-trench-silhouettes | The silhouettes of a trench line and bare poplar trees against a red winter sunset over flat Flanders fields in 1914, peaceful
HEAD: Rain and mud and cold.
BODY: Tea and bully beef and biscuits. Letters and cards and sleep. Stand-to at dawn and dusk. The flares rising and falling in the night. The sound of a distant gun. Could you have kept your spirits up, day after day? And every day, Christmas comes a little closer.
===
TOPIC: Letters, parcels and Christmas at home
BRIDGE: The rum ration has been handed out, and the sentries are on watch. And then, the best part of the day: can you hear someone calling out names?
===
IMG: postman-sorting-letters-parcels-field-post | An army postman in 1914 sorting letters and brown paper parcels in a farm barn behind the lines, sacks of mail around him, soft light
HEAD: The most important moment of the day, for many soldiers, is when the post arrives.
BODY: Every day, letters and parcels from home come up to the trenches, carried by the ration parties along with the food and the water. You'd hear someone call out your name, and your heart would jump. A sergeant calls out the names, and the men crowd round, hoping to hear their own. The army post is wonderfully quick. A letter posted in a village in England on Monday can be in the hands of a soldier in the trenches in Flanders on Wednesday or Thursday. Every day, thousands of sacks of letters and parcels cross the English Channel by boat. And are carried by train and lorry and cart and on men's backs to the front line. The letters are full of news from home. The baby has a new tooth. The garden is looking bare now that winter is here. Your father has a cold. The vicar asked after you. Your sister has a new young man. The football team lost again on Saturday. Everybody sends their love. Come home safe. The soldiers read them again and again, until the paper is soft and creased. And keep them in their pockets, close to their hearts. The parcels are even better. Mothers and wives and sisters at home spend hours packing them. And sending them off to the front, wrapped in brown paper and string. Inside, there are socks and scarves and gloves and woollen balaclava helmets, knitted by hand. There's chocolate and toffee, fruit cake and biscuits and tins of sardines, cigarettes and tobacco and soap. Sometimes there's a little bottle of something to keep out the cold.
===
IMG: soldiers-opening-parcels-dugout-knitted-socks | Soldiers in a dugout in 1914 opening a parcel from home by candlelight, knitted socks, a fruit cake and chocolate, sharing with friends, smiling
HEAD: The soldiers share everything with their friends.
BODY: A parcel is opened in a dugout, by the light of a candle. You'd never eat a slice of cake alone. And the cake is cut into pieces, and handed round, and everyone eats it slowly, and thinks of home. All over Britain, people are knitting for the soldiers. Their letters are read by an officer before they are sent, to make sure that they don't give away any secrets, so the men can't say exactly where they are, or what they are doing.

HEAD: So they write about the weather, and the mud, and the food, and their friends.
BODY: They thank their families for the parcels. You might think they'd write about the fighting. Mostly, they didn't. They ask for more socks, or cigarettes, or candles. They tell them not to worry. There's also a field postcard, a printed card with sentences on it, such as I am quite well, or I have received your letter, or Letter follows at first opportunity. The soldier crosses out the sentences that aren't true, and signs his name. It's the quickest way to tell his family that he is safe.

HEAD: At home in Britain, in December 1914, people were preparing for Christmas, as they always did.
BODY: The shops in the high streets were decorated with holly and ivy and paper chains. You'd see shop windows full of gifts for the soldiers at the front. The windows of the toy shops were full of dolls and teddy bears, tin soldiers and toy trains and rocking horses. The butchers hung geese and turkeys outside their shops. And the grocers piled up oranges and nuts and dried fruit for the Christmas puddings. But this Christmas was different. In almost every street, in almost every village, there was a family with a son or a husband or a brother at the front. Many of the young men of the town were gone. The football matches on Saturday afternoons were smaller. The church choirs had fewer men's voices. In the kitchens, the mothers made the Christmas puddings, as they always did, mixing the flour and the suet and the dried fruit in a great bowl. Every member of the family took a turn to stir it, and make a wish. And this year, almost everyone made the same wish: that the war would end soon, and the soldiers would come home. Some of the puddings were wrapped up carefully and sent to the front, by post, so that the soldiers could have a taste of home on Christmas Day. The children made Christmas cards for their fathers and brothers, with pictures of holly and robins and snowmen. And wrote messages inside in their best handwriting. You'd want to send something, too, if you could. The wives and mothers wrote long letters, and knitted socks and scarves, and packed parcels.
