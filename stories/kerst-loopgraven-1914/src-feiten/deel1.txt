TOPIC: Flanders, a flat green land
===
IMG: flanders-flat-fields-poplars-misty-morning | Flat Flemish farmland on a grey misty December morning in 1914, rows of tall poplar trees along a road, a farmhouse with a red-tiled roof, a distant church spire, ditches of still water
HEAD: In the winter of 1914, the flat farmland of Flanders lay under frost and low grey skies.
BODY: Flanders is a region in the west of Belgium, close to the border with France and not far from the North Sea. Its landscape is very flat. Fields stretch toward the horizon, divided by ditches and lined by rows of tall poplar trees along the roads. Small farms with red-tiled roofs stand among the fields, and church towers rise above the villages. The soil is heavy clay, and the water lies close to the surface. In wet weather, the land quickly turns to mud. Before 1914, Flanders was a quiet agricultural region, known for its farms, markets and old trading towns such as Ypres. Historians note that this ordinary countryside became one of the most famous battlefields in history. Even so, in the first months of the war, much of the landscape still looked as it had for centuries. Farmhouses, hedges and woods survived between the lines. In December 1914, frost and mist settled over these fields.
===
IMG: soldiers-marching-poplar-road-autumn-1914 | A column of soldiers in 1914 uniforms marching along a cobbled road lined with tall poplar trees through flat autumn fields in Belgium, seen from a distance, soft light
HEAD: In August 1914, German armies marched through Belgium toward France.
BODY: The First World War began in the summer of 1914. Germany planned to defeat France quickly by sending its armies through neutral Belgium. This plan is often known as the Schlieffen Plan, after the general who first developed it. The invasion of Belgium brought Britain into the war on the fourth of August, because Britain had promised to protect Belgian neutrality. Through August and September, great battles were fought in Belgium and northern France. The German advance was stopped at the Battle of the Marne, east of Paris. Afterwards, each side tried to move around the other's northern flank, in a series of movements later called the Race to the Sea. By October and November, the armies reached Flanders. There, heavy fighting took place around Ypres. Historians describe the First Battle of Ypres as a turning point. When it ended, both sides dug trenches. The war of movement had become a war of fixed positions.
===
IMG: village-church-ruined-tower-winter-sky | A Flemish village church with a damaged tower against a pale winter sky in 1914, bare trees, a few houses, quiet and still
HEAD: By the winter of 1914, lines of trenches stretched across Europe from the North Sea to Switzerland.
BODY: After the battles of the autumn, the Western Front became a continuous line of trenches. It ran for more than four hundred miles, from the coast of Belgium to the border of Switzerland. In Flanders, British, French and Belgian troops faced the German army. In some places, the opposing trenches were several hundred yards apart. In others, they were much closer, sometimes less than a hundred yards. Between them lay an area known as No Man's Land. Villages near the front were damaged by shellfire. Church towers, which could be used as observation posts, were often hit. Historians note that the soldiers of 1914 did not expect this kind of war. Most had imagined rapid marches and decisive battles. Instead, they faced a long, cold winter in muddy ditches. Even so, the line was not yet as heavily fortified as it later became. Trench systems in 1914 were still relatively simple and improvised.
===
IMG: soldiers-waking-trench-dawn-smoke-small-fire | British soldiers in 1914 greatcoats waking in a muddy sandbagged trench at dawn, lighting a small fire to boil water, breath steaming, grey light
HEAD: Many of the soldiers in the trenches in 1914 were young men with only a few months of training.
BODY: The British army of 1914 was small compared with those of France and Germany. It was made up of professional soldiers, together with reservists who had served before and were recalled to the colours. By the end of 1914, many of these regular soldiers had been killed or wounded. They were joined by men of the Territorial Force, part-time volunteers who had trained in the evenings and at weekends. Territorial units such as the London Rifle Brigade arrived in France in late 1914. Their soldiers came from many occupations, including clerks, shop assistants, miners and farm workers. On the German side, many soldiers were also young volunteers or reservists. Historians use letters, diaries and regimental records to understand who these men were. Many were in their late teens or early twenties. Even so, the armies included older men as well, some of them experienced soldiers. Most had never imagined spending Christmas in a trench.
===
TOPIC: The summer of 1914 and the journey to the front
===
IMG: summer-1914-english-village-cricket-sunshine | An English village cricket match on a green in the golden summer of 1914, players in white, families watching under trees, a church tower, peaceful
HEAD: The summer of 1914 was remembered as warm and sunny in England.
BODY: Many people later described the summer of 1914 as golden. The weather in England was warm and settled, and people enjoyed holidays at the seaside, cricket matches on village greens and picnics by rivers. Bank holiday crowds filled the beaches. Historians note that this memory of an idyllic summer was strengthened by what came afterwards. Looking back after years of war, people remembered the last months of peace with special affection. Weather records show that the summer was indeed fine in many places, though not exceptional throughout. Political tensions had been growing in Europe for years. On the twenty-eighth of June, Archduke Franz Ferdinand of Austria-Hungary was assassinated in Sarajevo. During July, a diplomatic crisis developed among the great powers. Even so, many ordinary people in Britain paid little attention at first. Newspapers were more concerned with events in Ireland. Within a few weeks, however, the whole of Europe was at war.
===
IMG: crowds-outside-palace-august-evening-1914 | Large crowds in 1914 clothes and straw hats gathered outside a grand palace on a summer evening, waving flags, seen from a distance
HEAD: On the evening of the fourth of August 1914, large crowds gathered outside Buckingham Palace in London.
BODY: Britain declared war on Germany late on the fourth of August 1914. Large crowds gathered in central London, outside Buckingham Palace and in Whitehall, waiting for news. When the declaration was announced, many people cheered and sang. Similar crowds had gathered in Berlin, Paris, Vienna and other capitals. Historians debate how widespread this enthusiasm really was. Studies of diaries and local newspapers suggest that many people felt anxious, worried or uncertain rather than joyful. Even so, there was a strong sense of duty and patriotism. In the following weeks, hundreds of thousands of men volunteered for the army. Recruiting offices were crowded. Many people believed that the war would be short. A popular phrase claimed that it would be over by Christmas. Some military leaders, such as Lord Kitchener, warned that it could last years. His warning proved correct. The war continued for more than four years.
===
IMG: german-troop-train-flowers-chalk-station | A 1914 German troop train decorated with flowers and branches at a station, young soldiers leaning out of the windows smiling, families waving, sunny
HEAD: In 1914, French soldiers went to war in blue coats and bright red trousers.
BODY: At the beginning of the war, French infantry wore a uniform that had changed little since the nineteenth century. It included a dark blue coat and red trousers, known in French as the pantalon rouge. These bright colours made soldiers easy targets for modern rifles and machine guns. Historians note that the French army had considered changing the uniform before the war, but the decision had been delayed. In 1915, the French adopted a pale blue-grey uniform, called horizon blue. German soldiers wore field grey, and British soldiers wore khaki, both chosen to blend into the landscape. In all the warring countries, crowds cheered troops as they left for the front. Flowers were thrown, and trains were decorated with slogans. Photographs from the period record these scenes. Even so, the first months of the war brought enormous losses. By the end of 1914, hundreds of thousands of soldiers on both sides had been killed, wounded or captured.
===
IMG: young-clerk-london-office-window-dreaming | A thin dreamy young clerk of eighteen in a 1914 suit sitting at a desk in a London office, looking out of the window at the sky, ledgers in front of him
HEAD: Henry Williamson, a young clerk from London, served with the London Rifle Brigade in 1914.
BODY: Henry Williamson was born in London on the first of December 1895. Before the war, he worked as a clerk and spent his free time exploring the countryside of Kent, watching birds and animals. He had joined the London Rifle Brigade, a Territorial battalion, earlier in 1914. When the war began, the battalion was mobilised, and in November it was sent to France. Williamson celebrated his nineteenth birthday in the trenches near Ploegsteert Wood in Belgium. Historians know about his experiences from his letters and from the many books he wrote later. On the twenty-sixth of December, he wrote a famous letter to his mother describing the Christmas truce. The letter survives and is often quoted. Even so, his later writings mixed memory with fiction, so historians read them carefully. Williamson became well known as an author after the war. His early experience of the truce stayed with him throughout his life.
===
IMG: troopship-leaving-southampton-night-1914 | A steamship full of soldiers leaving an English port at night in 1914, lights on the quay, calm dark water, seen from a distance
HEAD: British soldiers travelled to France by train to the coast and then by ship across the Channel.
BODY: For British soldiers in 1914, the journey to the front began at home. Units marched through their towns to railway stations, often cheered by crowds. Trains took them to ports on the south coast, especially Southampton. From there, troopships carried them across the English Channel, usually at night to reduce the risk of attack. The crossing could be rough and cold, and many soldiers were seasick. The Royal Navy protected the transport routes, and remarkably few troopships were lost during the war. Historians note that the movement of the British Expeditionary Force in August 1914 was a major logistical achievement. Within a few weeks, more than a hundred thousand men and their horses, guns and supplies were transported to France. Later units followed the same routes. Even so, for individual soldiers, the journey was a step into the unknown. Many had never been abroad before, and some had never seen the sea.
===
IMG: soldiers-on-ship-deck-night-crossing-channel | British soldiers in 1914 greatcoats crowded on the deck of a steamer crossing the Channel at night, watching distant lights, calm sea
HEAD: British troops landed at French ports such as Le Havre and Boulogne.
BODY: After crossing the Channel, British troops disembarked at ports including Le Havre, Boulogne and Rouen. From the quays, they marched to large camps on the hills above the towns. There, they rested, received equipment and waited for orders. French civilians often welcomed them warmly. Children ran beside the marching columns, asking for souvenirs, biscuits and badges. Soldiers tried out a few words of French, often with amusing results. Historians use letters and diaries to describe these first impressions. Many soldiers noted the unfamiliar sights, from French uniforms and street signs to cobbled roads and cafés. Base depots at the ports became large centres for supplies, hospitals and training. Even so, the soldiers did not stay long. Within days, they were sent toward the front. For many, the warm welcome in the French ports was their first experience of a foreign country, and it left lasting memories.
===
IMG: soldiers-in-wooden-goods-wagon-train-france | British soldiers sitting on straw inside a small wooden goods wagon of a slow train through France in 1914, the door open to flat fields, some sleeping, some singing
HEAD: Soldiers were carried toward the front in goods wagons marked for forty men or eight horses.
BODY: Trains carried troops from the ports toward the front lines. Many soldiers travelled in goods wagons rather than passenger carriages. French wagons were marked with a sign stating that each could carry forty men or eight horses. In French, this was written as hommes 40, chevaux 8. The soldiers sat on straw on the wooden floor, with their packs and rifles. The trains moved slowly and stopped often, so a journey of a hundred miles could take a day or more. Historians note that railways were essential to the war. They moved troops, food, ammunition and the wounded on a huge scale. Soldiers' letters often describe long, uncomfortable journeys. At stations, they climbed out to stretch their legs, refill water bottles and buy food. Even so, many soldiers remembered these journeys with some fondness. They passed through peaceful countryside, sang songs together and saw unfamiliar farms, towns and churches.
===
IMG: train-stopped-french-village-station-soldiers-coffee | A troop train stopped at a small French village station in 1914, soldiers stretching their legs, villagers selling bread and coffee from baskets, soft light
HEAD: Soldiers sang popular music-hall songs, hymns and regimental songs to pass the time.
BODY: Singing was an important part of soldiers' lives in the First World War. On trains, on the march and in billets, men sang together. Popular songs came from British music halls, which were the main form of popular entertainment before the war. Songs such as It's a Long Way to Tipperary became famous during the first months of the conflict. Soldiers also sang hymns from their churches and chapels, and invented their own humorous verses. Welsh soldiers were known for their singing, especially in harmony. Historians note that songs helped soldiers cope with fear, boredom and homesickness. They also built a sense of unity within units. Many of these songs were collected and published after the war. Even so, some of the soldiers' own versions were too rude to print. At railway stations in France, civilians sometimes offered coffee and bread to passing troops. Such small kindnesses were often mentioned in letters home.
===
IMG: new-soldiers-passing-tired-soldiers-dusk-road | New soldiers marching up a poplar-lined road at dusk in 1914 passing tired muddy soldiers walking the other way, calm and quiet
HEAD: Before entering the trenches, soldiers usually rested in villages behind the front line.
BODY: Troops arriving at the front did not go directly into the trenches. They first stayed in villages a few miles behind the lines. There, they were billeted in barns, farmhouses, schools and other buildings. They received final instructions and prepared their equipment. Units already serving in the trenches often guided newcomers on their first trips into the line. Historians note that this system of gradual introduction helped new soldiers learn practical skills, such as how to move safely and keep their rifles working in the mud. On the roads, fresh troops marching toward the front often passed tired, muddy soldiers coming out. Many letters describe this contrast. Even so, the first night in the trenches was a memorable experience for every soldier. The sound of rifle fire, the light of flares and the closeness of the enemy made the war suddenly real. Many described the experience in detail when writing home.

TOPIC: The trenches
===
IMG: trench-sandbags-duckboards-winter-morning | A 1914 trench in Flanders with walls of sandbags and wooden duckboards on the muddy floor, zigzagging away, soldiers in greatcoats, grey winter morning
HEAD: A trench in 1914 was a narrow ditch about six or seven feet deep, built in a zigzag pattern.
BODY: Trenches were dug to protect soldiers from rifle and artillery fire. A typical front-line trench in 1914 was about six or seven feet deep, so that a soldier could stand without being seen. It was narrow, to reduce the danger from shells. Trenches were built in a zigzag or stepped pattern, with sections called fire bays and traverses. This meant that an explosion or an enemy soldier entering the trench could not affect a long straight section. A raised fire step allowed soldiers to look over the top and fire. The front wall was protected by sandbags, forming a parapet. In Flanders, digging was difficult because the water table was high. Trenches quickly filled with water. In many places, soldiers built breastworks, walls of sandbags above ground, instead of digging deep. Historians note that trench systems were still improvised in 1914. Wooden duckboards were laid on the floor to help men walk above the mud.
===
IMG: soldier-pumping-water-trench-wooden-pump | A soldier in 1914 working a wooden hand pump to clear water from a flooded trench, sandbags and mud around him, grey light
HEAD: Soldiers worked constantly to drain water from trenches and repair collapsed walls.
BODY: Water was one of the greatest problems in the trenches of Flanders during the winter of 1914. Heavy rain filled trenches and dugouts. Soldiers used buckets and hand pumps to remove water, and they dug drainage channels where possible. Walls often collapsed after rain and had to be rebuilt. Standing in cold water for long periods caused a painful condition known as trench foot. Feet became swollen and numb, and in serious cases, the tissue died. Thousands of soldiers suffered from it during the first winter. Historians note that the army later introduced measures to prevent trench foot, including regular foot inspections, dry socks and whale oil rubbed into the skin. Soldiers also dug small shelters into the walls of the trench, called dugouts. These ranged from simple holes to larger rooms with roofs of timber and corrugated iron. Even so, dugouts in 1914 were often damp, cramped and cold.
===
IMG: communication-trench-soldiers-carrying-supplies | Soldiers in 1914 carrying sacks and water cans in single file along a narrow winding communication trench, bare trees above, winter light
HEAD: Communication trenches connected the front line with support lines and the areas behind.
BODY: The trench system included more than the front line. Behind it were support trenches and reserve trenches. These were connected by communication trenches, which ran roughly at right angles to the front. Soldiers used them to move forward and back, carrying food, water, ammunition and other supplies. Wounded men were carried back along them. A few miles behind the front lay villages and farms, where soldiers rested, and the roads and railways that brought supplies. Historians note that the trench systems of late 1914 were less complex than those of later years. In places, communication trenches were incomplete, and soldiers had to move across open ground at night. Barbed wire was placed in front of the trenches to slow down any enemy attack. Working parties went out at night to repair and extend it. Even so, the wire in 1914 was often thinner than in later years. Defences grew stronger as the war continued.
===
IMG: barbed-wire-posts-frosty-field-dawn | Rows of barbed wire on wooden posts across a frosty field at dawn in Flanders, mist beyond, quiet and still
HEAD: The area between the opposing trenches was known as No Man's Land.
BODY: No Man's Land was the ground between the front lines of the two armies. In Flanders in 1914, it often consisted of muddy fields, ditches, broken hedges and shell holes. Ruined farm buildings, haystacks and isolated trees sometimes stood in it. Its width varied greatly. In some places, the trenches were several hundred yards apart. In others, they were only a few dozen yards apart. Where the lines were close, soldiers on both sides could hear each other talking, coughing and singing. Historians note that this closeness played an important role in the events of Christmas 1914. Soldiers became aware that the enemy faced the same conditions of cold, wet and mud. Sometimes they shouted to each other across the gap. Even so, No Man's Land was dangerous. Snipers watched it during the day, and patrols crept across it at night. Any movement in the open could attract rifle or machine gun fire.
===
IMG: soldiers-relief-night-walking-single-file | Soldiers in 1914 greatcoats with packs walking in single file along a dark road at night during the relief, poplar trees silhouetted, quiet
HEAD: Units in the front line were relieved at night, usually after a few days.
BODY: Soldiers did not stay in the front-line trenches continuously. Units rotated between the front line, support positions and rest areas behind the lines. A typical pattern in 1914 was a few days in the front line, followed by a period in reserve or at rest. The exact length varied with the situation and the number of troops available. Reliefs took place at night, so that the enemy would not see the movement. Incoming soldiers walked in single file through communication trenches or across open ground, carrying their equipment. Outgoing soldiers then marched back along dark roads to villages, where they could rest, wash and eat hot meals. Historians note that the relief system helped soldiers endure the strain of trench life. Diaries often describe the relief of coming out of the line. Even so, the walk could be dangerous and exhausting. Enemy artillery sometimes shelled roads known to be used for reliefs.
===
TOPIC: A day in the line
===
IMG: stand-to-dawn-soldiers-fire-step-mist | British soldiers standing on the fire step of a trench before dawn in 1914, rifles ready, looking out into grey mist, breath steaming, calm
HEAD: Each day in the trenches began with stand-to, about an hour before dawn.
BODY: Dawn and dusk were considered the most likely times for an enemy attack, because the half-light made it hard to see. For this reason, all soldiers in the front line were ordered to stand to arms, usually called stand-to, about an hour before dawn. They climbed onto the fire step with rifles loaded and fixed bayonets, and watched No Man's Land. When full daylight came, the order was given to stand down. In some places, both sides fired their rifles during stand-to, a practice called the morning hate. Historians note that daily routines in the trenches were very regular. Soldiers' diaries describe the same pattern day after day. After stand-down, men cleaned their rifles and had breakfast. Tea was the most important part of the British soldier's morning. It was brewed in mess tins or in large containers. Even so, making tea in the trenches was difficult, because fuel and clean water were scarce.
===
IMG: soldiers-brewing-tea-mess-tins-brazier | British soldiers in 1914 brewing tea in mess tins over a small brazier in a trench, frying bacon, smiling, steam rising, morning light
HEAD: British soldiers' rations included bully beef, hard biscuits, bread, jam, bacon and tea.
BODY: The British army provided a daily ration for each soldier. It included meat, often in the form of tinned corned beef, known as bully beef, and bread or hard biscuits. Soldiers also received bacon, cheese, jam, sugar, tea and vegetables when available. A tinned meat and vegetable stew, made by the company Maconochie, was common. Jam made from plum and apple was so frequent that soldiers joked about it in songs. Historians note that the British army was generally well supplied with food, though rations did not always reach the front line in good condition. Food was cooked in field kitchens behind the lines and carried forward, or prepared by soldiers in the trenches on small fires and braziers. Smoke could attract enemy fire, so cooking had to be done carefully. Even so, a hot meal was greatly valued. Officers inspected rifles and equipment after breakfast, and the day's work then began.
===
IMG: soldier-writing-letter-dugout-pencil-candle | A young soldier in a 1914 greatcoat writing a letter with a pencil in a small dugout by candlelight, writing pad on his knee, calm
HEAD: During quiet periods in the trenches, soldiers rested, talked, wrote letters and carried out repairs.
BODY: Much of the time in the trenches was spent in routine work and waiting. Soldiers filled sandbags, repaired walls and drained water. They took turns as sentries, watching No Man's Land through periscopes or small gaps in the parapet. When off duty, they rested in dugouts or sat on the fire step. Sleep was often short and broken. Soldiers learned to sleep in uncomfortable positions and at odd hours. They talked about home, families, football and food, and complained about the weather and the army, as soldiers always have. Writing letters was a major activity. Historians have collected thousands of letters from the war, which give a detailed picture of daily life. Soldiers wrote in pencil, often on paper sent from home. Even so, many periods in the trenches were dangerous. Snipers and occasional shells caused casualties even on quiet days. Life in the line combined boredom with sudden risk.
===
IMG: soldiers-eating-stew-mess-tins-trench-noon | Soldiers in 1914 eating stew from mess tins sitting on the fire step of a trench at midday, chatting, winter light
HEAD: In winter, darkness fell early, and much of the work in the trenches took place at night.
BODY: In December, the sun set in Flanders around four o'clock in the afternoon. At dusk, there was another stand-to, just as at dawn. After dark, the trenches became busier. Soldiers could move more freely, because the enemy could not see them. Working parties repaired the parapets and wire. Ration parties brought up food, water and post from the rear. Patrols went out into No Man's Land to gather information or listen for enemy activity. Flares, known as Very lights, were fired into the sky to light up the ground. When a flare went up, soldiers in the open stood perfectly still. Historians note that night was the most active time for many units. Diaries describe nights spent digging, carrying and watching. Even so, the cold made night work very hard. Soldiers wrapped themselves in greatcoats, scarves and gloves knitted at home. Many letters mention how long the winter nights felt.
===
IMG: winter-sunset-flanders-trench-silhouettes | The silhouettes of a trench line and bare poplar trees against a red winter sunset over flat Flanders fields in 1914, peaceful
HEAD: The routine of trench life in winter was dominated by rain, mud and cold.
BODY: During the winter of 1914, soldiers on both sides experienced similar conditions. The weather was often wet, with frequent rain and occasional frost. Trenches filled with water, and mud covered everything. Clothes, boots and blankets were rarely dry. Historians describe the first winter as one of the hardest periods of the war for the troops in Flanders, because trench systems were new and poorly drained. Daily life followed a regular pattern of stand-to, meals, work, sentry duty and sleep. Food was plain but usually sufficient. Letters from home and small comforts were extremely important for morale. Soldiers' diaries show both suffering and humour. Many men coped by joking about their conditions. Bruce Bairnsfather later turned such jokes into famous cartoons. Even so, the strain was real. Sickness, exhaustion and trench foot were common. As December continued, the approach of Christmas was on many soldiers' minds, on both sides of the line.

TOPIC: Letters, parcels and Christmas at home
===
IMG: postman-sorting-letters-parcels-field-post | An army postman in 1914 sorting letters and brown paper parcels in a farm barn behind the lines, sacks of mail around him, soft light
HEAD: The army postal service delivered millions of letters and parcels to soldiers at the front.
BODY: Post was enormously important to soldiers in the First World War. The British army ran a large postal service, operated by the Royal Engineers. Letters and parcels from Britain often reached soldiers at the front within two or three days. They were carried by train and lorry to the rear areas and then brought up to the trenches with the rations. Historians estimate that during the war, the British army postal service handled about two billion letters and over a hundred million parcels. A large sorting office was built in Regent's Park in London. Letters gave soldiers news of their families and reminded them of home. Parcels brought food, clothing and small comforts. Officers and sergeants called out names when the post arrived. Even so, not every soldier received post regularly. Those without family sometimes received parcels from charities or shared what their comrades received. The arrival of the post was often the brightest moment of the day.
===
IMG: soldiers-opening-parcels-dugout-knitted-socks | Soldiers in a dugout in 1914 opening a parcel from home by candlelight, knitted socks, a fruit cake and chocolate, sharing with friends, smiling
HEAD: Families and volunteers in Britain knitted socks, scarves and gloves for the soldiers.
BODY: Parcels from home often contained cakes, chocolate, cigarettes, soap, candles and warm clothing. Knitted items were especially common. Across Britain, women, children and some men knitted socks, scarves, mittens, balaclava helmets and cardigans for the troops. Charities and organisations, such as Queen Mary's Needlework Guild, coordinated much of this work. Historians note that knitting became a way for civilians to feel connected to the war effort. Patterns were published in newspapers and magazines. Some knitted items included notes from the knitters, wishing the soldiers well. Soldiers usually shared the contents of parcels with friends in their section. Cake and chocolate were handed round in dugouts by candlelight. Letters often thank families for these gifts and ask for more socks, because wet feet were a constant problem. Even so, the quality of some homemade items varied, and soldiers sometimes joked about them. Warm, dry socks were among the most valued possessions in the trenches.
===
IMG: officer-reading-censoring-letters-candle | A young British officer in 1914 reading soldiers' letters at a small table in a dugout by candlelight, a pencil in hand, calm
HEAD: Soldiers' letters were read by officers, who censored information that might be useful to the enemy.
BODY: All letters sent by British soldiers from the front were subject to censorship. Junior officers read their men's letters and removed or blacked out any information about locations, troop movements, casualties or plans. Soldiers knew this and often avoided writing about fighting. Instead, they wrote about the weather, the food, their friends and the things they needed. Many tried to reassure their families that they were well. Historians note that censorship, combined with soldiers' wish to protect their families, means that letters do not give a complete picture of the war. Diaries, which were officially forbidden at the front but kept by many, sometimes reveal more. Even so, letters are valuable sources. They show soldiers' feelings, humour and concerns. Some letters about the Christmas truce passed the censors and were published in British newspapers in early January 1915. Officers also wrote letters, which they could censor themselves, and these were often more detailed.
===
IMG: field-postcard-pencil-mud-stained-hands | Close view of muddy hands holding a small printed army field postcard and a pencil in 1914, soft light
HEAD: The Field Service Postcard allowed soldiers to send brief messages quickly by crossing out printed lines.
BODY: When soldiers had no time or energy to write a full letter, they could send a Field Service Postcard. This card had printed sentences, stating that the sender was quite well, or had been admitted into hospital, or that a letter would follow at the first opportunity. The soldier crossed out the sentences that did not apply and signed the card. Nothing else could be written on it, or it would be destroyed. Because the card contained no other information, it did not need to be censored and could be sent quickly. Historians note that families treasured these cards, even though they said very little. For relatives at home, the postman's arrival could bring relief or terrible news. The deaths of soldiers were reported through official telegrams or letters. As December 1914 went on, the volume of post increased greatly. Families sent puddings, cakes, chocolate, cards and photographs for Christmas. Even so, deliveries generally kept pace with the demand.
===
IMG: family-fireside-empty-chair-christmas-1914 | An English family sitting by the fire in their parlour in December 1914, an empty armchair, a photograph of a soldier on the mantelpiece, warm firelight
HEAD: By December 1914, it was clear that the war would not be over by Christmas.
BODY: In August 1914, many people had expected a short war. By December, that hope had gone. The front lines had become fixed, and casualties had been heavy. The British army alone had suffered around ninety thousand casualties by the end of the year. In France and Germany, the losses were much larger. Historians note that the first months of the war were among the bloodiest of the entire conflict. Many families were already grieving. At home, people adjusted to wartime conditions. Prices began to rise, and newspapers were filled with casualty lists. Even so, many still hoped that the war would end within a year. Governments encouraged people to remain confident. Churches held special services and prayers for soldiers. At Christmas, families thought of absent sons, husbands and brothers. Many set a place or raised a glass in their honour. The first Christmas of the war was celebrated with a mixture of hope and sadness.
===
IMG: english-high-street-christmas-1914-shops-snow | An English high street in December 1914 decorated for Christmas, shop windows with toys and geese, light snow, people in coats and hats, warm lights
HEAD: At home in Britain, people prepared for Christmas in December 1914, despite the war.
BODY: Christmas traditions in Britain continued during the first winter of the war. Shops were decorated with holly, ivy and paper chains. Toy shops displayed dolls, teddy bears, tin soldiers, toy trains and rocking horses. Some toys had a military theme, reflecting the mood of the time. Butchers hung geese and turkeys in their windows, and grocers sold puddings, nuts and fruit. Many shops advertised gifts suitable for sending to soldiers at the front, such as tobacco, writing cases and warm clothing. Historians use newspapers, advertisements and photographs from December 1914 to study how the war affected Christmas. Many families continued to celebrate as normally as possible. Charities collected money and gifts for soldiers and for Belgian refugees, many of whom had arrived in Britain in the autumn. Even so, prices were rising, and some goods were becoming scarce. The war was beginning to shape everyday life at home.
===
IMG: child-drawing-christmas-card-for-father-table | A small child in 1914 clothes drawing a Christmas card with holly and a robin at a kitchen table, a mother knitting nearby, lamplight
HEAD: Families in Britain decorated Christmas trees and kept photographs of absent soldiers on the mantelpiece.
BODY: By 1914, Christmas trees were common in British homes. The custom had become popular in the nineteenth century, partly because of Queen Victoria and Prince Albert, who was German. Families decorated trees with candles, glass ornaments and paper decorations. Children hung stockings by the fireplace. Historians note that many British Christmas customs, including the tree, had strong German connections. This was one reason why soldiers on both sides recognised each other's celebrations. In homes with a family member at the front, photographs of the absent soldier were often placed among the Christmas cards. Children made cards and drawings to send to their fathers and brothers. Letters from the front, read aloud at Christmas, brought news and reassurance. Even so, families could not know exactly what their soldiers were experiencing. Most could not imagine that, on the front line, some of their men would spend Christmas Day meeting German soldiers in the open.
===
IMG: family-christmas-dinner-raised-glasses-empty-chair | An English family at Christmas dinner in 1914 raising their glasses, a flaming pudding with holly, one empty chair at the table, warm candlelight
HEAD: In Germany, families celebrated Christmas on Christmas Eve with decorated trees and candles.
BODY: In Germany, the main Christmas celebration traditionally took place on Christmas Eve, called Heiligabend. Families decorated a tree, often with candles, glass ornaments and straw stars, and gathered to exchange gifts. Carols such as Stille Nacht, known in English as Silent Night, were sung. This carol was written in Austria in 1818 and had become popular in many countries. In 1914, many German families had sons and husbands at the front. They sent parcels containing sausages, cakes, cigars and warm clothing. Historians note that German soldiers also received official gifts at Christmas. The army sent small Christmas trees to the front, and soldiers received presents such as pipes and cigars. Even so, German families shared the same anxieties as British ones. Casualty lists were long, and the war showed no sign of ending. On both sides, Christmas 1914 brought thoughts of home to millions of soldiers and their families.
