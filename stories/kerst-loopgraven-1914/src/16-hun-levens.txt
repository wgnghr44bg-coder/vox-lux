Henry Williamson survived the war.

He served through it all, from that first Christmas in Plugstreet Wood to the end, and came home in 1919, a quiet, troubled young man of twenty-three. He could not settle in London. So he went to live in a little thatched cottage in a village in Devon, in the west of England, near the sea, among the fields and the rivers and the moors that he loved.

--

There, he began to write.

He wrote about nature, about the birds and the animals and the rivers. In 1927, he published a book about the life of an otter, living in the rivers of Devon. It was called Tarka the Otter. It became one of the most famous books about animals ever written, and has been loved by readers all over the world ever since.

Later, he wrote many more books, including a long series of novels about the war, in which he wrote about the Christmas truce, just as he had seen it.

He never forgot that Christmas. All his life, he said that it had changed him, and made him think that the soldiers on both sides had been the same, just young men far from home.

--

Bruce Bairnsfather became famous.

His cartoons of Old Bill, the old soldier with the walrus moustache, were printed in a magazine in London during the war, and became enormously popular. Millions of people laughed at them, and soldiers pinned them up in their dugouts. There were Old Bill books, and Old Bill plays, and Old Bill films, and even Old Bill pottery.

{img:cartoonist-older-drawing-studio-sketches}
In 1916, Bairnsfather wrote about the Christmas truce in a book called Bullets and Billets, and drew a picture of it. He said that he would not have missed that Christmas for anything. Later in his life, he drew cartoons in the Second World War too. He died in 1959.

--

Frank Richards also survived the whole war, which was very rare.

He served from the very beginning in 1914 to the very end in 1918, almost always in the front line, and was never seriously wounded. He was given medals for his bravery, and for his work as a signaller, laying telephone wires under fire.

After the war, he went home to Wales, and worked as a labourer and a miner again. In 1933, he published his book, Old Soldiers Never Die, which told the story of his war, simply and honestly and with humour. He described the Christmas truce, and the two barrels of beer, and the Germans he had met. It is still read today, and is one of the best books ever written about life in the trenches.

--

Kurt Zehmisch, the young German schoolteacher, also survived the war.

He went home to Saxony, and became a teacher again, and taught children for many years. His diary, with his description of the Christmas truce, and of the football game, was kept by his family, and found by his son many years later, and published, so that people could read it.

{img:german-schoolteacher-classroom-1920s-chalkboard}
He wrote in it that he would never forget that day. And he wrote that he hoped that the people of England and Germany would one day be friends.

--

The little brass boxes from Princess Mary were kept by many of the soldiers, all their lives, as treasures.

Today, you can still find them in attics and drawers all over Britain, sometimes still with a few old cigarettes or a little Christmas card inside, passed down from fathers to sons and daughters to grandchildren.

The letters that the soldiers wrote home that Christmas were kept too, in boxes and drawers, and many of them are now in museums and archives, where people can still read them, written in pencil, in the trenches, by the light of candles, more than a hundred years ago.

--

The last of the soldiers who were there at the Christmas truce lived to be very old men.

Some of them lived until the beginning of this century. In their old age, they were often asked about that Christmas, and they told the story again and again, to their grandchildren, to schoolchildren, to journalists and historians.

{img:very-old-veteran-armchair-telling-story-grandchild}
One of the last of them, a Scottish soldier named Alfred Anderson, who lived until 2005, at the age of one hundred and nine, remembered the silence of that Christmas morning, more than anything else. He said it was an eerie silence, and that all he could hear was the silence.
