On the evening of Christmas Day, the soldiers sat down to their Christmas dinner in their trenches.

In the British trenches, it was bully beef and potatoes, if they were lucky, and Christmas pudding from home, heated over a little fire, and perhaps a little rum. In some places, there was even a goose, or a chicken, bought from a farm behind the lines. In the German trenches, there was sausage and black bread and sauerkraut, and cake from home, and beer.

--

They ate in their dugouts, crowded together, by the light of candles, and passed around the cake and the chocolate and the cigarettes, and talked about the day.

They talked about the Germans they had met, and the presents they had exchanged, and the football, and the photographs, and the barber, and the hare. They could hardly believe that it had really happened.

They looked at the buttons and badges and cigars that they had been given, and turned them over in their hands, and put them away carefully, to keep.

--

Then they wrote letters home.

Henry Williamson wrote to his mother. Frank Richards wrote to his friends. Thousands of soldiers, on both sides, sat in their dugouts that night, and wrote about what they had seen.

Many of these letters were sent to the newspapers in Britain, by proud families, and printed. And so, in the first days of January 1915, the people of Britain read in their newspapers the extraordinary story of how their soldiers had spent Christmas Day with the Germans, in No Man's Land.

{img:family-reading-newspaper-fireside-january-1915}
Some people were shocked. Most people were moved. Some of the newspapers printed photographs of British and German soldiers standing together in the field, smiling.

--

In many places, the truce did not end on Christmas Day.

The next day, Boxing Day, the soldiers met again. And the day after. In some parts of the line, the truce went on for a week, until the New Year, and in a few places, even longer. The soldiers went on talking and exchanging gifts, and agreed not to shoot at each other, and went about their work in the open, repairing their trenches, without being shot at.

In one place, the British and German soldiers helped each other to repair their trenches and their wire. In another, they arranged to give each other a warning, if the officers ordered them to start shooting again, so that everyone would have time to get under cover.

--

On New Year's Eve, the soldiers celebrated again.

In some places, they sang Auld Lang Syne together. At midnight, by German time, which was an hour ahead of British time, the Germans fired their rifles into the air, to welcome the New Year, and an hour later, the British did the same, but all in the air, harmlessly, at the stars.

And then, slowly, in the first days of January, the truce came to an end.

--

The generals on both sides were not pleased.

They had heard about the truce, and they did not like it. They worried that soldiers who had shaken hands with the enemy would not want to fight them. So they gave orders that there must be no more meetings with the enemy, and no more truces. Some officers were punished. New regiments were sent to the front, who had not been there at Christmas.

{img:officer-reading-orders-candle-dugout-january}
And slowly, along the line, the shooting began again.

--

But in some places, the soldiers on both sides were still reluctant.

In one place, a German officer came out into No Man's Land, at the end of the truce, and saluted, and the British officer saluted back, and they shook hands, and went back to their trenches, and then the German fired two shots into the air, to show that the war had begun again.

In another, the British soldiers shouted across that they were sorry, but they had orders to start shooting again tomorrow, and the Germans shouted back that they understood, and that they were sorry too.

--

The war went on for nearly four more years.

There was never another Christmas truce like the one in 1914. In the following years, the generals made sure that there was fighting, even on Christmas Day. The war grew larger, and harder, and longer, than anyone had ever imagined.

{img:winter-fields-flanders-snow-quiet-dusk}
But the soldiers who were there that Christmas never forgot it. Many years later, when they were old men, they still talked about it, with wonder: about the candles on the parapets, and the singing in the night, and the handshakes in No Man's Land, and the football in the frost.

For one day, in the middle of the greatest war the world had ever seen, there had been peace on earth.
