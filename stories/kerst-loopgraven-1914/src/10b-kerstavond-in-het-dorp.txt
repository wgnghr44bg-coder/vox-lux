Behind the lines, in the villages and farms of Flanders, Christmas Eve came quietly.

In the little village of Ploegsteert, and in the other villages around, the Flemish families who had stayed in their homes were getting ready for Christmas, as they always had, as best they could, in the middle of the war.

--

In Flanders, Christmas is a quiet, religious feast.

The great day for presents is not Christmas, but the feast of Saint Nicholas, on the sixth of December, when the children put out their shoes in the evening, and wake up to find them filled with sweets and oranges and little toys. That year, Saint Nicholas had come to the children of Flanders as usual, though there was not much to put in their shoes.

On Christmas Eve, the families decorate their houses with greenery, and set up a little crib, with figures of Mary and Joseph and the baby Jesus, and the shepherds and the animals, on a table in the corner of the room, with a candle in front of it.

--

In the farmhouse kitchens, the farmers' wives baked Christmas bread, a special sweet loaf, with raisins, shaped like a baby wrapped in swaddling clothes, which the Flemish call a kerstbrood, and the French-speaking villagers a cougnou.

They gave some of it to the soldiers who were lodging in their barns, and the soldiers were very touched. Many of them had not tasted fresh, sweet bread for weeks.

{img:farmers-wife-giving-christmas-bread-soldiers-kitchen}
In some farmhouses, the soldiers and the family sat together that evening, around the big kitchen table, by the stove, sharing the bread, and coffee, and a little of the chocolate and the cake from the soldiers' parcels, and singing carols, in English and in Flemish, to each other.

--

At the convent in Ploegsteert, the nuns were preparing for Christmas too.

They decorated their little chapel with greenery and candles, and set up a crib, with the figures that they had kept for many years. They made little presents for the village children, and for the wounded soldiers in their care.

In the evening, they invited some of the soldiers who were resting in the village to come to the chapel, and sing carols with them. The soldiers came, in their muddy boots and their greatcoats, and stood at the back of the little chapel, and sang, with their caps in their hands.

--

At midnight, in some of the village churches behind the lines, there was a Christmas Mass.

The churches were cold, and some of them were damaged, with broken windows and holes in the roofs. But the villagers came, wrapped in their coats and shawls, carrying candles and lanterns along the frozen lanes. And some of the soldiers came too: the Catholic soldiers, the Irish and the Scottish and the English, and some who were not Catholic, but who wanted to be in a church on Christmas Eve.

{img:midnight-mass-damaged-church-candles-villagers-soldiers}
They knelt together on the cold stone floor, the Flemish farmers and their wives and children, and the soldiers from far away, while the old priest said the Mass in Latin, and the candles flickered, and the frost glittered on the broken windows. And at the end, they sang the old Christmas hymns together, in Latin, which everyone knew.

--

After the Mass, the villagers walked home through the frozen lanes, under the stars, with their lanterns.

The air was very still and very cold. The frost crunched under their boots. From far away, toward the front, they could hear something strange: not the sound of guns, but the faint sound of singing, drifting across the fields from the trenches.

They stopped, and listened, in wonder. Then they went home, and climbed into their beds, under their heavy quilts, and blew out their candles.

--

In the barns, the soldiers who were resting settled down to sleep in the straw, wrapped in their blankets, with their Christmas presents beside them.

The horses shifted and stamped in their stalls. An owl called from the orchard. The frost settled on the roofs. And over the flat, frozen land of Flanders, the stars shone down on the farmhouses, and the villages, and the church towers, and the long line of the trenches, where, for one night, the guns were silent.

{img:barn-soldiers-sleeping-straw-moonlight-window}
It was, for many of them, the quietest night they had known since the war began.
