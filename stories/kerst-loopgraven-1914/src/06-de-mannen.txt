In a trench near the little village of Saint-Yvon, at the edge of Plugstreet Wood, there is a young officer named Bruce Bairnsfather.

He is twenty-seven years old, a lieutenant in the Royal Warwickshire Regiment, in charge of a machine gun team. He has a round, cheerful face, a small moustache, and a great sense of humour. Before the war, he was an engineer, installing electric lighting in country houses and hotels. But what he really loves is drawing.

--

In his dugout, by the light of a candle, Bairnsfather draws cartoons.

He draws the soldiers around him, in their muddy greatcoats and their woollen balaclavas, sitting in the rain, brewing their tea, grumbling about the food, sleeping in the mud. He draws them with great affection, and great humour, and they love his pictures, and pass them from hand to hand along the trench.

Later, he will invent a famous character, an old soldier with a walrus moustache and a pipe, called Old Bill. In his most famous cartoon, Old Bill is sitting in a shell hole with another soldier, with shells bursting all around them, and the other soldier is grumbling, and Old Bill says: Well, if you knows of a better 'ole, go to it.

Millions of people in Britain will laugh at that cartoon, and soldiers will repeat it to each other for years.

--

Bairnsfather's men like him. He is kind and cheerful, and he shares their hardships, and makes them laugh.

His dugout is a small, damp hole in the side of the trench, with a roof of planks and sandbags, so low that he cannot stand up in it. Water drips through the roof when it rains. He sleeps on a bed of straw and sandbags, with his feet in the cold. But he writes home that he is quite comfortable, really, and that the men are wonderful.

{img:low-damp-dugout-straw-bed-dripping-roof}
At Christmas, he will be one of the men who walk out into No Man's Land to meet the Germans. And he will write about it, and draw it, so that people at home will know what happened.

--

A few miles to the south, near the town of Armentières, there is a regiment called the Royal Welch Fusiliers.

Among its soldiers is a private named Frank Richards. He is about thirty, a former coal miner from South Wales, a tough, quiet, practical man, who has served in the army before, in India and Burma, and was called back from the reserves when the war began.

{img:welsh-soldier-pipe-trench-calm-face}
Frank is an old soldier. He knows how to look after himself, how to keep warm and dry, how to find a little extra food, and how to keep out of trouble with the sergeants. He is not interested in glory, or in speeches. He just wants to do his job, and look after his mates, and get home in one piece.

Many years later, he will write a book about his war, called Old Soldiers Never Die, which tells the story of the trenches, honestly and simply, from the point of view of an ordinary soldier.

--

The Welsh soldiers love to sing.

In the evenings, in their trenches, they sing the old hymns and songs of Wales, in their beautiful, deep, rich voices, in harmony, as they sang them in the chapels and the pubs of their villages at home. Their singing drifts across No Man's Land in the darkness, and the Germans on the other side stop what they are doing, and listen.

{img:welsh-soldiers-singing-trench-night-lantern}
Sometimes, the Germans sing back.

--

Along the line, in the trenches near Plugstreet Wood, Henry Williamson and the young men of the London Rifle Brigade are settling into life in the trenches.

They are city boys, clerks and office workers and shop assistants, and the trenches are a shock to them. Henry has never been so cold or so wet or so tired in his life. His feet are always freezing. His uniform is caked with mud. He is often frightened.

But he is also fascinated by everything he sees: the strange, ruined landscape, the flares in the night sky, the birds that still sing in the bare trees of Plugstreet Wood, the robins and the wrens that hop along the parapet looking for crumbs. He notices everything, and he writes it all down, in his letters to his mother, and in a little diary.

--

Like all the soldiers, these three men, Bairnsfather and Richards and Williamson, have been told that the Germans are cruel and wicked, the enemy of everything that is good.

And like all the soldiers, they are beginning, slowly, to wonder.

{img:sentry-looking-across-no-mans-land-dusk}
Because from their trenches, looking across No Man's Land, they can see the smoke of the German fires rising in the morning, just like their own, and hear the Germans coughing in the cold, and sometimes laughing, and sometimes singing. And the Germans, it seems, are just as cold and wet and tired and muddy as they are.
