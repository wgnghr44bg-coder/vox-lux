On the other side of No Man's Land, a few hundred yards away, the German soldiers are living in their own trenches, in the same mud and the same cold.

They wear grey-green uniforms, and tall leather helmets with a spike on top, covered with grey cloth, which they call the Pickelhaube. Many of them have long moustaches, and some have beards. They drink coffee instead of tea, and eat black bread and sausage instead of biscuits and bully beef. But otherwise, their lives are very much the same.

--

Many of the German soldiers along this part of the line come from Saxony and Bavaria and Württemberg, in the south and east of Germany.

They are farmers and craftsmen, students and teachers, factory workers and miners. They come from little villages among forests and hills, and from old towns with half-timbered houses and church spires, and from big cities like Leipzig and Dresden and Munich.

Many of them have been to England, or have relatives there, or have worked there, as waiters or barbers or clerks or musicians. Some of them speak good English. Some of them say that they have no quarrel with the English, and that they would much rather be fighting the Russians.

--

Among them, in a Saxon regiment, near the village of Saint-Yvon, facing Bairnsfather's men across the fields, there is a young officer named Kurt Zehmisch.

He is a schoolteacher from a little town in Saxony, about twenty-five years old, an educated, thoughtful young man, who speaks English and French. He keeps a diary, in which he writes, every day, about life in the trenches.

{img:young-german-officer-diary-dugout-lamp}
He writes about the rain, and the mud, and the cold. He writes about his men, whom he cares for, and about the food, and the post from home. He writes about the English soldiers on the other side, whom he can see and hear, and about how strange it is to be so close to them, in such a quiet, ordinary way.

--

The German trenches are, in some places, a little better built than the British ones.

The Germans have been here a little longer, and they have had time to dig deeper and to build stronger dugouts, with wooden walls and floors, and even stoves and beds. In some places, they have chosen the higher ground, which is a little drier.

But in the wet clay of Flanders, everyone suffers from the water and the mud.

--

The German soldiers pass their time just as the British do.

They write letters home, and read the letters and the newspapers that come from Germany. They play cards and chess. They smoke their pipes and their cigars. They clean their rifles and mend their clothes. And they sing.

{img:german-soldiers-singing-around-candle-trench}
The Germans love to sing, perhaps even more than the Welsh. They sing old folk songs, and soldiers' songs, and hymns, and songs about their homes and their sweethearts, in four-part harmony, beautifully, in the evenings, in their trenches. And their singing drifts across No Man's Land to the British trenches on the other side.

--

In the first weeks of December, some strange things begin to happen along the line.

In some places, the soldiers on both sides have stopped shooting at each other at mealtimes. Without anyone giving an order, both sides seem to have agreed that breakfast time, and dinner time, should be quiet, so that everyone can eat in peace.

In other places, they have stopped shooting at the men who carry the food and water up to the trenches, or at the men who go out at night to mend the wire. It is called live and let live. Nobody arranges it. It just happens.

--

Sometimes, the soldiers shout to each other across No Man's Land.

A German shouts across: Good morning, Tommy! And a British soldier shouts back: Good morning, Fritz! Sometimes they ask each other questions: how the weather is in London, what the food is like, when the war will end. Sometimes they make jokes.

{img:soldiers-shouting-across-fields-hands-cupped}
Once, in one part of the line, the Germans hold up a blackboard, with a message chalked on it, asking for news. In another, they throw over a tin of cigars, wrapped in a newspaper, and the British throw back a tin of jam.

--

On the seventh of December, far away in Rome, the Pope, Benedict the Fifteenth, makes an appeal to all the countries at war.

He asks them to stop fighting, at least for Christmas. He asks that the guns should be silent, at least on the night when the angels sang. The governments of the warring countries do not agree. The war must go on, they say.

But in the trenches, in the cold and the mud of Flanders, the soldiers on both sides are beginning to think the same thing.

--

In Germany, the government has decided that every soldier at the front should have a Christmas present.

Hundreds of thousands of little Christmas trees, with candles to put on them, are being sent by train to the front, so that the soldiers can have a proper German Christmas, in their trenches. There are gifts from the Kaiser too, pipes and cigars, and from the people of Germany, parcels of food and sweets and warm clothes.

{img:christmas-trees-loaded-train-wagons-snow}
In Britain, too, presents are on their way. Every soldier and sailor is to receive a little brass box, a gift from the King's daughter, Princess Mary, filled with tobacco and cigarettes, or chocolate and sweets for those who do not smoke, and a Christmas card, and a photograph of the princess.

The soldiers on both sides are looking forward to Christmas.
