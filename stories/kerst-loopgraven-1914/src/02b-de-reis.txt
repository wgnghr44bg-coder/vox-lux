For the young British soldiers of 1914, the journey to the front began at a railway station, somewhere in England, early in the morning.

They marched through the streets of their towns to the station, with their packs on their backs and their rifles on their shoulders, while the people came out of their houses to wave and cheer. At the station, their families were waiting on the platform, to say goodbye.

--

There were mothers and fathers, wives and children, brothers and sisters and sweethearts. They hugged and kissed and shook hands, and tried to smile. Some of the mothers gave their sons a last packet of sandwiches, or a pair of socks, or a little Bible to keep in their pocket.

Then the whistle blew, and the soldiers climbed into the carriages, and leaned out of the windows, waving, as the train pulled slowly out of the station, and the platform full of waving handkerchiefs grew smaller and smaller behind them.

The train took them south, through the green English countryside, to the ports on the coast: Southampton, or Folkestone, or Dover.

--

At the port, they boarded a ship.

It was usually at night, so that German submarines would not see them. The soldiers crowded onto the decks of the steamers, in the darkness, and watched the lights of England disappearing behind them. Some of them had never been to sea before, and the crossing was rough, and many of them were seasick.

{img:soldiers-on-ship-deck-night-crossing-channel}
In the early morning, they arrived in France, at the ports of Le Havre or Boulogne or Calais, and climbed down the gangways onto the quays, stiff and cold and tired, and marched through the grey streets of the French towns to great camps of tents on the hills above the harbour.

--

The French people watched them come.

They had never seen so many British soldiers. The children ran alongside them, shouting for souvenirs and chocolate. The women waved from the windows, and threw flowers. The soldiers tried out the few words of French that they knew, and the French laughed, and tried out the few words of English that they knew.

In the camps, the soldiers were given more training, and new equipment, and then, after a few days, they were put on a train to the front.

--

The trains that took the soldiers to the front were not passenger trains.

They were goods trains, made of little wooden wagons that were usually used for carrying cattle and horses. On the side of each wagon, there were words painted in French: Hommes quarante, chevaux huit. Men forty, horses eight. This meant that each wagon could carry forty men, or eight horses.

{img:soldiers-in-wooden-goods-wagon-train-france}
The soldiers crowded into the wagons, forty at a time, sitting on the straw on the floor, with their packs and their rifles, and the train set off, very slowly, rattling and jolting through the flat French countryside, for a whole day and a night, or longer.

--

They passed through little towns and villages, past fields and farms and churches, past rivers and canals and woods.

At every station, the train stopped, and the soldiers climbed out to stretch their legs, and to fill their water bottles, and to buy bread and coffee and wine from the French people who came to the station to sell them. At night, they slept on the straw, curled up together for warmth, with the wagon swaying and rattling around them.

{img:train-stopped-french-village-station-soldiers-coffee}
The soldiers sang, to pass the time. They sang the songs of the music halls, and the songs of their regiments, and the hymns of their chapels, and their voices drifted out of the open doors of the wagons across the fields.

--

At last, the train stopped, at a little station somewhere in the north of France, or in Belgium, near the front.

The soldiers climbed out, and formed up in the road, and began to march. They marched along long, straight roads, paved with cobblestones, lined with tall poplar trees, through the flat, wet country of Flanders.

The cobblestones were hard on their feet, and their boots were new and stiff, and their packs were heavy. But they kept going, singing, mile after mile, through the villages and past the farms, where the people came to their doors to watch them pass.

--

As they got closer to the front, they began to hear a sound, far away, like distant thunder: the sound of the guns.

They passed through villages where some of the houses were damaged, and the church towers were broken. They passed wagons and ambulances coming back from the front, and tired, muddy soldiers marching the other way, who looked at them, the new arrivals, with tired, kindly eyes.

{img:new-soldiers-passing-tired-soldiers-dusk-road}
And at last, at dusk, they arrived at the villages behind the lines, where they would rest for a night or two, before going up into the trenches for the first time.

They were a long way from home now.
