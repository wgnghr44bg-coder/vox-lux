A trench, in the winter of 1914, is a long, narrow ditch dug into the earth, about six or seven feet deep, and wide enough for two men to pass each other, if they squeeze.

It does not run in a straight line. It zigzags, every few yards, back and forth, so that if a shell falls into one part of it, the walls of earth will protect the men in the next part.

--

In Flanders, digging trenches is difficult.

The ground is flat and low, and the water lies just beneath the surface. Dig down two or three feet, and the trench begins to fill with water. So the soldiers here often cannot dig very deep. Instead, they build up walls of sandbags above the ground, piled one on top of another, filled with the heavy, wet clay of the fields.

The front wall of the trench, facing the enemy, is called the parapet. The back wall is called the parados. Along the bottom of the parapet, there is a little step, called the fire step, where the soldiers stand to look out over the top, or to keep watch.

--

On the floor of the trench, the soldiers lay wooden boards, like little ladders laid flat, called duckboards, so that they can walk without sinking into the mud.

But in the wet winter of 1914, the mud is everywhere. It is thick and sticky and grey and cold, and it gets into everything: into the men's boots and their clothes, their food and their blankets, their rifles and their hair. In some places, the water in the trenches comes up to the men's knees, and they stand in it for hours, and their feet grow numb with cold.

{img:soldier-pumping-water-trench-wooden-pump}
They work all the time to keep the water out, with buckets and pumps, and to repair the walls when they fall in, which they often do, after heavy rain.

--

In the walls of the trench, the soldiers dig little shelters, called dugouts.

Some are just holes scooped out of the earth, big enough for a man to crawl into and curl up, out of the rain. Others are larger, with a roof of wooden planks and corrugated iron, covered with sandbags and earth, and a blanket hung over the doorway.

Inside, there might be a little table made of a packing case, a candle stuck in a bottle, a few shelves for food and letters, and straw on the floor to sleep on. The officers' dugouts are a little more comfortable, with a bed of chicken wire stretched over a wooden frame, and perhaps a chair, and a little stove.

--

Behind the front trench, there are other trenches.

There is a support trench, a little way back, where more soldiers wait. And there are long, winding communication trenches, running back from the front, through which the soldiers walk to and from the trenches, carrying food and water and ammunition and post, and bringing back the wounded.

{img:communication-trench-soldiers-carrying-supplies}
A few miles further back, there are the villages and farms where the soldiers go to rest, and the roads and railways that bring up the supplies.

--

In front of the trenches, the soldiers have put up barbed wire.

It is fixed to wooden posts, in long tangled rows, across the fields, to stop the enemy from coming across. Every night, men crawl out into the darkness to mend the wire, or to put up more of it, very quietly, while the sentries keep watch.

{img:barbed-wire-posts-frosty-field-dawn}
Beyond the wire, there is No Man's Land: a strip of muddy fields, ditches, shell holes, broken fences, a ruined farmhouse, perhaps an old haystack or a few bare trees. And beyond that, a few hundred yards away, or sometimes much closer, there is another line of wire, and sandbags, and the German trenches.

--

In many places, the trenches are so close that the soldiers can hear each other.

They can hear the Germans talking and laughing and coughing in their trenches, and the clatter of their mess tins, and sometimes their singing. They can smell the smoke of their fires, and their cooking. In the mornings, they can see the smoke rising from the German side, as the Germans make their breakfast, just as they themselves are doing.

Sometimes, they shout across to each other. A German soldier who used to work as a waiter in London might call out, asking how things are in the city. A British soldier might shout back a joke. And for a moment, the enemy seems not like an enemy at all, but like another young man, far from home, cold and tired and muddy, just like themselves.

--

The soldiers do not spend all their time in the front trenches.

Usually, a battalion stays in the front line for three or four days, and then it is relieved by another battalion, and goes back to the support trenches for a few days, and then further back, to the villages, to rest, for a few days more. Then it comes back to the front again.

{img:soldiers-relief-night-walking-single-file}
The relief takes place at night, in the dark, so that the enemy will not see. The new men come up through the communication trenches, in single file, carrying everything they own on their backs, and the old men file out, tired and muddy, and walk back along the dark roads to the villages, where a hot meal, and a dry barn, and perhaps a bath, are waiting.
