Tonight we are going a little more than a hundred and ten years back, to the flat, quiet farmland of Flanders, in the winter of 1914.

--

It is a grey morning in early December.

A low mist lies over the fields. The land here is almost perfectly flat, stretching away in every direction, with rows of tall, thin poplar trees marking the roads and the edges of the fields, and here and there a farmhouse with a red-tiled roof, a church spire in the distance, a little wood, a windmill. Ditches full of still, grey water run along the edges of the fields.

If calm stories from history help you rest, you are welcome to subscribe to the channel. Now, let us begin.

--

Flanders is the northern part of Belgium, and the corner of France just beside it.

For a thousand years, it has been a land of farmers and weavers and merchants. The soil is rich and heavy, made of clay, and the farmers grow wheat and barley, sugar beet and potatoes, and keep cows and pigs on the wet green meadows. In the towns, there are old market squares, with tall cloth halls and belfries and churches, built in the Middle Ages when Flanders was one of the richest places in Europe.

The people are quiet, hardworking, devout. They speak Flemish, a kind of Dutch, in the Belgian villages, and French in the villages across the border. On Sundays, the church bells ring across the flat land, and the families walk to Mass along the muddy lanes, in their best clothes.

--

But in the winter of 1914, the land is not peaceful.

Since August, there has been a war. It began far away, in the Balkans, and spread, in a few short weeks, across the whole of Europe. Germany and Austria-Hungary on one side. France and Russia and Britain and Belgium on the other.

{img:soldiers-marching-poplar-road-autumn-1914}
In August, the German armies marched through Belgium, toward France. Through the autumn, the armies fought and marched and fought again, across northern France and Flanders, until, in October and November, they came to a stop here, in the flat fields of Flanders, facing each other, and dug themselves into the ground.

--

Now, a long line of trenches runs across the land.

It begins on the coast of the North Sea, near the little Belgian town of Nieuwpoort, and runs south, through the fields of Flanders, past the old town of Ypres, through the hills and woods and villages of northern France, and on, all the way to the mountains of Switzerland, more than four hundred miles away.

On one side of the line, there are the German soldiers. On the other, the French and the Belgians and the British. Between them, there is a strip of empty land, sometimes only a few dozen yards wide, sometimes a few hundred, which the soldiers call No Man's Land.

--

In this story, we will spend a little time in one small part of that long line.

It is a stretch of about thirty miles in Flanders, south of Ypres, between the Belgian village of Ploegsteert, which the British soldiers call Plugstreet, and the French town of Armentières, and the little villages around them.

{img:village-church-ruined-tower-winter-sky}
Here, in the winter of 1914, the British army faces the German army across the fields. And here, on Christmas Day, something will happen that nobody expects, and that people will still be talking about more than a hundred years later.

--

But that is still a few weeks away.

On this grey December morning, the soldiers on both sides are waking up in their trenches, cold and stiff and muddy, and lighting little fires to boil water for their tea or their coffee, and looking out across the fields at the mist, and thinking about home.

{img:soldiers-waking-trench-dawn-smoke-small-fire}
Most of them are young men. Many of them have been soldiers for only a few months. A few months ago, they were clerks and farmers, students and miners, shop assistants and factory workers, schoolteachers and butchers and bakers.

They did not imagine that they would spend Christmas in a muddy ditch in Flanders.

--

Behind the lines, in the villages and farms, the Flemish and French families are still living in their houses, as best they can.

The farmers still try to look after their animals and their land. The women still bake bread, and wash clothes, and keep house. The children still go to school, when there is school. Many of the soldiers lodge in their farmhouses and barns, when they come out of the trenches to rest.

The farmers' wives sell them coffee and eggs and bread and fried potatoes, and the soldiers, who are far from home, are glad of a warm kitchen and a kind face.

--

In the evening, the mist comes down again over the fields, and the light fades early, because it is December.

Along the line, the soldiers settle down for the night, in their trenches and their dugouts, wrapped in their greatcoats and their blankets. Sentries stand on watch, peering out into the darkness. Now and then, a flare goes up into the sky, and drifts down slowly, lighting the fields with a pale, white light, and then goes out.

{img:quiet-night-trench-sentry-flare-distant}
And in the farmhouses behind the lines, the families say their prayers, and blow out their lamps, and go to bed, and listen to the wind in the poplar trees, and hope for peace.
