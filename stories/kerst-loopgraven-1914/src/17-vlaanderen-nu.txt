Flanders is peaceful today.

The flat fields stretch away under the wide sky, green in spring and summer, golden at harvest, brown and bare in winter. The poplar trees still stand in long rows along the roads. The farmhouses still have their red-tiled roofs, and their orchards, and their cows grazing in the wet meadows. The church spires still rise over the villages, and the bells still ring on Sundays.

--

If you did not know, you might never guess what happened here, more than a hundred years ago.

But if you look carefully, there are signs everywhere.

Every spring, when the farmers plough their fields, they still find things in the earth: old buttons and buckles, bits of barbed wire, broken bottles, sometimes a helmet or a mess tin. The farmers call it the iron harvest. And everywhere, in the middle of the fields, beside the roads, at the edge of the woods, there are the little cemeteries, with their white stones and their roses.

--

Plugstreet Wood is still there, near the village of Ploegsteert.

It is a quiet wood again, of oak and ash and hazel, with paths winding among the trees, and birds singing in the branches, and bluebells in the spring. Inside it, among the trees, there are several small cemeteries, where soldiers who fell in the wood lie at rest, with their names on white stones, among the trees they knew.

{img:small-cemetery-inside-wood-bluebells-spring}
At the edge of the wood, there is a great memorial, a round building of white stone, guarded by two stone lions, with the names of more than eleven thousand soldiers who have no known grave, carved on its walls.

--

Not far away, near the little village of Saint-Yvon, at the edge of the wood, where Bruce Bairnsfather and Kurt Zehmisch met in No Man's Land on Christmas Day, 1914, there is a small memorial to the Christmas truce.

It is a simple wooden cross, standing in the field, beside the road. People leave things at its foot: little wooden crosses and poppies, footballs, scarves from football clubs in England and Germany, candles, Christmas decorations, and messages of peace.

{img:wooden-cross-field-footballs-poppies-winter}
In 2014, on the hundredth anniversary of the truce, a new memorial was unveiled nearby, showing two hands, one British and one German, holding a football between them. And that winter, British and German soldiers played friendly games of football together once more, in memory of the soldiers of 1914.

--

In Ypres, every evening, there is a ceremony that has taken place almost every evening since 1928.

At eight o'clock, as the darkness falls, the traffic is stopped beneath the great white arch of the Menin Gate. A small crowd gathers, people from the town and visitors from all over the world. And buglers from the local fire brigade step forward, in their uniforms, and play the Last Post, the slow, sad bugle call that marks the end of a soldier's day.

{img:menin-gate-evening-buglers-crowd-quiet}
On the walls of the Menin Gate, there are the names of more than fifty-four thousand soldiers who fell in the fields around Ypres and have no known grave. The buglers play, the notes echo under the great arch, and everyone stands in silence. Then the people lay wreaths of poppies, and go quietly home.

--

At Christmas, the people of Flanders still remember the truce.

There are concerts and services in the churches, and people sing Silent Night, in English and German and Flemish and French. Children from Britain and Germany and Belgium visit the battlefields together, and play football together, and lay wreaths.

And in the frosty fields near Plugstreet Wood, at the little wooden cross, people leave candles, glowing in the dark, on Christmas Eve, as the German soldiers once lit the candles on their little Christmas trees, on the parapet of their trench, more than a hundred years ago.

--

On a winter evening in Flanders, the sky is very wide, and the light fades early.

The sun goes down behind the poplar trees, red and golden, and the frost begins to settle on the fields. The lights come on in the farmhouses. A dog barks in a farmyard. The church bell rings for the evening. And over the flat, quiet land, the first stars come out, one by one, as they did on that Christmas Eve, long ago.

{img:winter-dusk-flanders-farmhouse-lights-stars-frost}
In the little cemeteries, among the fields, the white stones stand in their rows, under the stars, in the silence. And the frost glitters on the grass of what was once No Man's Land, where, for one day, the soldiers of two armies met, and shook hands, and sang, and wished each other a merry Christmas.

--

So we leave Flanders for tonight, the quiet flat land under the winter stars, with its poplar trees and its farmhouses and its little church spires, and its long memory of a silent night.
