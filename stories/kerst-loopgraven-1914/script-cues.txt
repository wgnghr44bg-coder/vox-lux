In December 1914, the First World War was only a few months old. In the flat, frozen fields of Flanders, British and German soldiers faced each other from muddy trenches, sometimes only a stone's throw apart. This story looks at the journey to the front, at daily life in the trenches, at letters and parcels from home, and at the quiet Christmas when soldiers from both sides met in No Man's Land. It ends in Flanders today, among green fields and calm memorials.

In the winter of 1914, the flat farmland of Flanders lay under frost and low grey skies. Flanders is a region in the west of Belgium, close to the border with France and not far from the North Sea. Its landscape is very flat. Fields stretch toward the horizon, divided by ditches and lined by rows of tall poplar trees along the roads. Small farms with red-tiled roofs stand among the fields, and church towers rise above the villages. The soil is heavy clay, and the water lies close to the surface. In wet weather, the land quickly turns to mud. Before 1914, Flanders was a quiet agricultural region, known for its farms, markets and old trading towns such as Ypres. Historians note that this ordinary countryside became one of the most famous battlefields in history. Even so, in the first months of the war, much of the landscape still looked as it had for centuries. Farmhouses, hedges and woods survived between the lines. In December 1914, frost and mist settled over these fields.

[long-pause]

{img:02-soldiers-marching-poplar-road-autumn-1914}

In August 1914, German armies marched through Belgium toward France. The First World War began in the summer of 1914. Germany planned to defeat France quickly by sending its armies through neutral Belgium. This plan is often known as the Schlieffen Plan, after the general who first developed it. The invasion of Belgium brought Britain into the war on the fourth of August, because Britain had promised to protect Belgian neutrality. Through August and September, great battles were fought in Belgium and northern France. The German advance was stopped at the Battle of the Marne, east of Paris. Afterwards, each side tried to move around the other's northern flank, in a series of movements later called the Race to the Sea. By October and November, the armies reached Flanders. There, heavy fighting took place around Ypres. Historians describe the First Battle of Ypres as a turning point. When it ended, both sides dug trenches. The war of movement had become a war of fixed positions.

[long-pause]

{img:03-village-church-ruined-tower-winter-sky}

By the winter of 1914, lines of trenches stretched across Europe from the North Sea to Switzerland. After the battles of the autumn, the Western Front became a continuous line of trenches. It ran for more than four hundred miles, from the coast of Belgium to the border of Switzerland. In Flanders, British, French and Belgian troops faced the German army. In some places, the opposing trenches were several hundred yards apart. In others, they were much closer, sometimes less than a hundred yards. Between them lay an area known as No Man's Land. Villages near the front were damaged by shellfire. Church towers, which could be used as observation posts, were often hit. Historians note that the soldiers of 1914 did not expect this kind of war. Most had imagined rapid marches and decisive battles. Instead, they faced a long, cold winter in muddy ditches. Even so, the line was not yet as heavily fortified as it later became. Trench systems in 1914 were still relatively simple and improvised.

[long-pause]

{img:04-soldiers-waking-trench-dawn-smoke-small-fire}

Many of the soldiers in the trenches in 1914 were young men with only a few months of training. The British army of 1914 was small compared with those of France and Germany. It was made up of professional soldiers, together with reservists who had served before and were recalled to the colours. By the end of 1914, many of these regular soldiers had been killed or wounded. They were joined by men of the Territorial Force, part-time volunteers who had trained in the evenings and at weekends. Territorial units such as the London Rifle Brigade arrived in France in late 1914. Their soldiers came from many occupations, including clerks, shop assistants, miners and farm workers. On the German side, many soldiers were also young volunteers or reservists. Historians use letters, diaries and regimental records to understand who these men were. Many were in their late teens or early twenties. Even so, the armies included older men as well, some of them experienced soldiers. Most had never imagined spending Christmas in a trench.

[long-pause]

{img:05-summer-1914-english-village-cricket-sunshine}

The summer of 1914 was remembered as warm and sunny in England. Many people later described the summer of 1914 as golden. The weather in England was warm and settled, and people enjoyed holidays at the seaside, cricket matches on village greens and picnics by rivers. Bank holiday crowds filled the beaches. Historians note that this memory of an idyllic summer was strengthened by what came afterwards. Looking back after years of war, people remembered the last months of peace with special affection. Weather records show that the summer was indeed fine in many places, though not exceptional throughout. Political tensions had been growing in Europe for years. On the twenty-eighth of June, Archduke Franz Ferdinand of Austria-Hungary was assassinated in Sarajevo. During July, a diplomatic crisis developed among the great powers. Even so, many ordinary people in Britain paid little attention at first. Newspapers were more concerned with events in Ireland. Within a few weeks, however, the whole of Europe was at war.

[long-pause]

{img:06-crowds-outside-palace-august-evening-1914}

On the evening of the fourth of August 1914, large crowds gathered outside Buckingham Palace in London. Britain declared war on Germany late on the fourth of August 1914. Large crowds gathered in central London, outside Buckingham Palace and in Whitehall, waiting for news. When the declaration was announced, many people cheered and sang. Similar crowds had gathered in Berlin, Paris, Vienna and other capitals. Historians debate how widespread this enthusiasm really was. Studies of diaries and local newspapers suggest that many people felt anxious, worried or uncertain rather than joyful. Even so, there was a strong sense of duty and patriotism. In the following weeks, hundreds of thousands of men volunteered for the army. Recruiting offices were crowded. Many people believed that the war would be short. A popular phrase claimed that it would be over by Christmas. Some military leaders, such as Lord Kitchener, warned that it could last years. His warning proved correct. The war continued for more than four years.

[long-pause]

{img:07-german-troop-train-flowers-chalk-station}

In 1914, French soldiers went to war in blue coats and bright red trousers. At the beginning of the war, French infantry wore a uniform that had changed little since the nineteenth century. It included a dark blue coat and red trousers, known in French as the pantalon rouge. These bright colours made soldiers easy targets for modern rifles and machine guns. Historians note that the French army had considered changing the uniform before the war, but the decision had been delayed. In 1915, the French adopted a pale blue-grey uniform, called horizon blue. German soldiers wore field grey, and British soldiers wore khaki, both chosen to blend into the landscape. In all the warring countries, crowds cheered troops as they left for the front. Flowers were thrown, and trains were decorated with slogans. Photographs from the period record these scenes. Even so, the first months of the war brought enormous losses. By the end of 1914, hundreds of thousands of soldiers on both sides had been killed, wounded or captured.

[long-pause]

{img:08-young-clerk-london-office-window-dreaming}

Henry Williamson, a young clerk from London, served with the London Rifle Brigade in 1914. Henry Williamson was born in London on the first of December 1895. Before the war, he worked as a clerk and spent his free time exploring the countryside of Kent, watching birds and animals. He had joined the London Rifle Brigade, a Territorial battalion, earlier in 1914. When the war began, the battalion was mobilised, and in November it was sent to France. Williamson celebrated his nineteenth birthday in the trenches near Ploegsteert Wood in Belgium. Historians know about his experiences from his letters and from the many books he wrote later. On the twenty-sixth of December, he wrote a famous letter to his mother describing the Christmas truce. The letter survives and is often quoted. Even so, his later writings mixed memory with fiction, so historians read them carefully. Williamson became well known as an author after the war. His early experience of the truce stayed with him throughout his life.

[long-pause]

{img:09-troopship-leaving-southampton-night-1914}

British soldiers travelled to France by train to the coast and then by ship across the Channel. For British soldiers in 1914, the journey to the front began at home. Units marched through their towns to railway stations, often cheered by crowds. Trains took them to ports on the south coast, especially Southampton. From there, troopships carried them across the English Channel, usually at night to reduce the risk of attack. The crossing could be rough and cold, and many soldiers were seasick. The Royal Navy protected the transport routes, and remarkably few troopships were lost during the war. Historians note that the movement of the British Expeditionary Force in August 1914 was a major logistical achievement. Within a few weeks, more than a hundred thousand men and their horses, guns and supplies were transported to France. Later units followed the same routes. Even so, for individual soldiers, the journey was a step into the unknown. Many had never been abroad before, and some had never seen the sea.

[long-pause]

{img:10-soldiers-on-ship-deck-night-crossing-channel}

British troops landed at French ports such as Le Havre and Boulogne. After crossing the Channel, British troops disembarked at ports including Le Havre, Boulogne and Rouen. From the quays, they marched to large camps on the hills above the towns. There, they rested, received equipment and waited for orders. French civilians often welcomed them warmly. Children ran beside the marching columns, asking for souvenirs, biscuits and badges. Soldiers tried out a few words of French, often with amusing results. Historians use letters and diaries to describe these first impressions. Many soldiers noted the unfamiliar sights, from French uniforms and street signs to cobbled roads and cafés. Base depots at the ports became large centres for supplies, hospitals and training. Even so, the soldiers did not stay long. Within days, they were sent toward the front. For many, the warm welcome in the French ports was their first experience of a foreign country, and it left lasting memories.

[long-pause]

{img:11-soldiers-in-wooden-goods-wagon-train-france}

Soldiers were carried toward the front in goods wagons marked for forty men or eight horses. Trains carried troops from the ports toward the front lines. Many soldiers travelled in goods wagons rather than passenger carriages. French wagons were marked with a sign stating that each could carry forty men or eight horses. In French, this was written as hommes 40, chevaux 8. The soldiers sat on straw on the wooden floor, with their packs and rifles. The trains moved slowly and stopped often, so a journey of a hundred miles could take a day or more. Historians note that railways were essential to the war. They moved troops, food, ammunition and the wounded on a huge scale. Soldiers' letters often describe long, uncomfortable journeys. At stations, they climbed out to stretch their legs, refill water bottles and buy food. Even so, many soldiers remembered these journeys with some fondness. They passed through peaceful countryside, sang songs together and saw unfamiliar farms, towns and churches.

[long-pause]

{img:12-train-stopped-french-village-station-soldiers-coffee}

Soldiers sang popular music-hall songs, hymns and regimental songs to pass the time. Singing was an important part of soldiers' lives in the First World War. On trains, on the march and in billets, men sang together. Popular songs came from British music halls, which were the main form of popular entertainment before the war. Songs such as It's a Long Way to Tipperary became famous during the first months of the conflict. Soldiers also sang hymns from their churches and chapels, and invented their own humorous verses. Welsh soldiers were known for their singing, especially in harmony. Historians note that songs helped soldiers cope with fear, boredom and homesickness. They also built a sense of unity within units. Many of these songs were collected and published after the war. Even so, some of the soldiers' own versions were too rude to print. At railway stations in France, civilians sometimes offered coffee and bread to passing troops. Such small kindnesses were often mentioned in letters home.

[long-pause]

{img:13-new-soldiers-passing-tired-soldiers-dusk-road}

Before entering the trenches, soldiers usually rested in villages behind the front line. Troops arriving at the front did not go directly into the trenches. They first stayed in villages a few miles behind the lines. There, they were billeted in barns, farmhouses, schools and other buildings. They received final instructions and prepared their equipment. Units already serving in the trenches often guided newcomers on their first trips into the line. Historians note that this system of gradual introduction helped new soldiers learn practical skills, such as how to move safely and keep their rifles working in the mud. On the roads, fresh troops marching toward the front often passed tired, muddy soldiers coming out. Many letters describe this contrast. Even so, the first night in the trenches was a memorable experience for every soldier. The sound of rifle fire, the light of flares and the closeness of the enemy made the war suddenly real. Many described the experience in detail when writing home.

[long-pause]

{img:14-trench-sandbags-duckboards-winter-morning}

A trench in 1914 was a narrow ditch about six or seven feet deep, built in a zigzag pattern. Trenches were dug to protect soldiers from rifle and artillery fire. A typical front-line trench in 1914 was about six or seven feet deep, so that a soldier could stand without being seen. It was narrow, to reduce the danger from shells. Trenches were built in a zigzag or stepped pattern, with sections called fire bays and traverses. This meant that an explosion or an enemy soldier entering the trench could not affect a long straight section. A raised fire step allowed soldiers to look over the top and fire. The front wall was protected by sandbags, forming a parapet. In Flanders, digging was difficult because the water table was high. Trenches quickly filled with water. In many places, soldiers built breastworks, walls of sandbags above ground, instead of digging deep. Historians note that trench systems were still improvised in 1914. Wooden duckboards were laid on the floor to help men walk above the mud.

[long-pause]

{img:15-soldier-pumping-water-trench-wooden-pump}

Soldiers worked constantly to drain water from trenches and repair collapsed walls. Water was one of the greatest problems in the trenches of Flanders during the winter of 1914. Heavy rain filled trenches and dugouts. Soldiers used buckets and hand pumps to remove water, and they dug drainage channels where possible. Walls often collapsed after rain and had to be rebuilt. Standing in cold water for long periods caused a painful condition known as trench foot. Feet became swollen and numb, and in serious cases, the tissue died. Thousands of soldiers suffered from it during the first winter. Historians note that the army later introduced measures to prevent trench foot, including regular foot inspections, dry socks and whale oil rubbed into the skin. Soldiers also dug small shelters into the walls of the trench, called dugouts. These ranged from simple holes to larger rooms with roofs of timber and corrugated iron. Even so, dugouts in 1914 were often damp, cramped and cold.

[long-pause]

{img:16-communication-trench-soldiers-carrying-supplies}

Communication trenches connected the front line with support lines and the areas behind. The trench system included more than the front line. Behind it were support trenches and reserve trenches. These were connected by communication trenches, which ran roughly at right angles to the front. Soldiers used them to move forward and back, carrying food, water, ammunition and other supplies. Wounded men were carried back along them. A few miles behind the front lay villages and farms, where soldiers rested, and the roads and railways that brought supplies. Historians note that the trench systems of late 1914 were less complex than those of later years. In places, communication trenches were incomplete, and soldiers had to move across open ground at night. Barbed wire was placed in front of the trenches to slow down any enemy attack. Working parties went out at night to repair and extend it. Even so, the wire in 1914 was often thinner than in later years. Defences grew stronger as the war continued.

[long-pause]

{img:17-barbed-wire-posts-frosty-field-dawn}

The area between the opposing trenches was known as No Man's Land. No Man's Land was the ground between the front lines of the two armies. In Flanders in 1914, it often consisted of muddy fields, ditches, broken hedges and shell holes. Ruined farm buildings, haystacks and isolated trees sometimes stood in it. Its width varied greatly. In some places, the trenches were several hundred yards apart. In others, they were only a few dozen yards apart. Where the lines were close, soldiers on both sides could hear each other talking, coughing and singing. Historians note that this closeness played an important role in the events of Christmas 1914. Soldiers became aware that the enemy faced the same conditions of cold, wet and mud. Sometimes they shouted to each other across the gap. Even so, No Man's Land was dangerous. Snipers watched it during the day, and patrols crept across it at night. Any movement in the open could attract rifle or machine gun fire.

[long-pause]

{img:18-soldiers-relief-night-walking-single-file}

Units in the front line were relieved at night, usually after a few days. Soldiers did not stay in the front-line trenches continuously. Units rotated between the front line, support positions and rest areas behind the lines. A typical pattern in 1914 was a few days in the front line, followed by a period in reserve or at rest. The exact length varied with the situation and the number of troops available. Reliefs took place at night, so that the enemy would not see the movement. Incoming soldiers walked in single file through communication trenches or across open ground, carrying their equipment. Outgoing soldiers then marched back along dark roads to villages, where they could rest, wash and eat hot meals. Historians note that the relief system helped soldiers endure the strain of trench life. Diaries often describe the relief of coming out of the line. Even so, the walk could be dangerous and exhausting. Enemy artillery sometimes shelled roads known to be used for reliefs.

[long-pause]

{img:19-stand-to-dawn-soldiers-fire-step-mist}

Each day in the trenches began with stand-to, about an hour before dawn. Dawn and dusk were considered the most likely times for an enemy attack, because the half-light made it hard to see. For this reason, all soldiers in the front line were ordered to stand to arms, usually called stand-to, about an hour before dawn. They climbed onto the fire step with rifles loaded and fixed bayonets, and watched No Man's Land. When full daylight came, the order was given to stand down. In some places, both sides fired their rifles during stand-to, a practice called the morning hate. Historians note that daily routines in the trenches were very regular. Soldiers' diaries describe the same pattern day after day. After stand-down, men cleaned their rifles and had breakfast. Tea was the most important part of the British soldier's morning. It was brewed in mess tins or in large containers. Even so, making tea in the trenches was difficult, because fuel and clean water were scarce.

[long-pause]

{img:20-soldiers-brewing-tea-mess-tins-brazier}

British soldiers' rations included bully beef, hard biscuits, bread, jam, bacon and tea. The British army provided a daily ration for each soldier. It included meat, often in the form of tinned corned beef, known as bully beef, and bread or hard biscuits. Soldiers also received bacon, cheese, jam, sugar, tea and vegetables when available. A tinned meat and vegetable stew, made by the company Maconochie, was common. Jam made from plum and apple was so frequent that soldiers joked about it in songs. Historians note that the British army was generally well supplied with food, though rations did not always reach the front line in good condition. Food was cooked in field kitchens behind the lines and carried forward, or prepared by soldiers in the trenches on small fires and braziers. Smoke could attract enemy fire, so cooking had to be done carefully. Even so, a hot meal was greatly valued. Officers inspected rifles and equipment after breakfast, and the day's work then began.

[long-pause]

{img:21-soldier-writing-letter-dugout-pencil-candle}

During quiet periods in the trenches, soldiers rested, talked, wrote letters and carried out repairs. Much of the time in the trenches was spent in routine work and waiting. Soldiers filled sandbags, repaired walls and drained water. They took turns as sentries, watching No Man's Land through periscopes or small gaps in the parapet. When off duty, they rested in dugouts or sat on the fire step. Sleep was often short and broken. Soldiers learned to sleep in uncomfortable positions and at odd hours. They talked about home, families, football and food, and complained about the weather and the army, as soldiers always have. Writing letters was a major activity. Historians have collected thousands of letters from the war, which give a detailed picture of daily life. Soldiers wrote in pencil, often on paper sent from home. Even so, many periods in the trenches were dangerous. Snipers and occasional shells caused casualties even on quiet days. Life in the line combined boredom with sudden risk.

[long-pause]

{img:22-soldiers-eating-stew-mess-tins-trench-noon}

In winter, darkness fell early, and much of the work in the trenches took place at night. In December, the sun set in Flanders around four o'clock in the afternoon. At dusk, there was another stand-to, just as at dawn. After dark, the trenches became busier. Soldiers could move more freely, because the enemy could not see them. Working parties repaired the parapets and wire. Ration parties brought up food, water and post from the rear. Patrols went out into No Man's Land to gather information or listen for enemy activity. Flares, known as Very lights, were fired into the sky to light up the ground. When a flare went up, soldiers in the open stood perfectly still. Historians note that night was the most active time for many units. Diaries describe nights spent digging, carrying and watching. Even so, the cold made night work very hard. Soldiers wrapped themselves in greatcoats, scarves and gloves knitted at home. Many letters mention how long the winter nights felt.

[long-pause]

{img:23-winter-sunset-flanders-trench-silhouettes}

The routine of trench life in winter was dominated by rain, mud and cold. During the winter of 1914, soldiers on both sides experienced similar conditions. The weather was often wet, with frequent rain and occasional frost. Trenches filled with water, and mud covered everything. Clothes, boots and blankets were rarely dry. Historians describe the first winter as one of the hardest periods of the war for the troops in Flanders, because trench systems were new and poorly drained. Daily life followed a regular pattern of stand-to, meals, work, sentry duty and sleep. Food was plain but usually sufficient. Letters from home and small comforts were extremely important for morale. Soldiers' diaries show both suffering and humour. Many men coped by joking about their conditions. Bruce Bairnsfather later turned such jokes into famous cartoons. Even so, the strain was real. Sickness, exhaustion and trench foot were common. As December continued, the approach of Christmas was on many soldiers' minds, on both sides of the line.

[long-pause]

{img:24-postman-sorting-letters-parcels-field-post}

The army postal service delivered millions of letters and parcels to soldiers at the front. Post was enormously important to soldiers in the First World War. The British army ran a large postal service, operated by the Royal Engineers. Letters and parcels from Britain often reached soldiers at the front within two or three days. They were carried by train and lorry to the rear areas and then brought up to the trenches with the rations. Historians estimate that during the war, the British army postal service handled about two billion letters and over a hundred million parcels. A large sorting office was built in Regent's Park in London. Letters gave soldiers news of their families and reminded them of home. Parcels brought food, clothing and small comforts. Officers and sergeants called out names when the post arrived. Even so, not every soldier received post regularly. Those without family sometimes received parcels from charities or shared what their comrades received. The arrival of the post was often the brightest moment of the day.

[long-pause]

{img:25-soldiers-opening-parcels-dugout-knitted-socks}

Families and volunteers in Britain knitted socks, scarves and gloves for the soldiers. Parcels from home often contained cakes, chocolate, cigarettes, soap, candles and warm clothing. Knitted items were especially common. Across Britain, women, children and some men knitted socks, scarves, mittens, balaclava helmets and cardigans for the troops. Charities and organisations, such as Queen Mary's Needlework Guild, coordinated much of this work. Historians note that knitting became a way for civilians to feel connected to the war effort. Patterns were published in newspapers and magazines. Some knitted items included notes from the knitters, wishing the soldiers well. Soldiers usually shared the contents of parcels with friends in their section. Cake and chocolate were handed round in dugouts by candlelight. Letters often thank families for these gifts and ask for more socks, because wet feet were a constant problem. Even so, the quality of some homemade items varied, and soldiers sometimes joked about them. Warm, dry socks were among the most valued possessions in the trenches.

[long-pause]

{img:26-officer-reading-censoring-letters-candle}

Soldiers' letters were read by officers, who censored information that might be useful to the enemy. All letters sent by British soldiers from the front were subject to censorship. Junior officers read their men's letters and removed or blacked out any information about locations, troop movements, casualties or plans. Soldiers knew this and often avoided writing about fighting. Instead, they wrote about the weather, the food, their friends and the things they needed. Many tried to reassure their families that they were well. Historians note that censorship, combined with soldiers' wish to protect their families, means that letters do not give a complete picture of the war. Diaries, which were officially forbidden at the front but kept by many, sometimes reveal more. Even so, letters are valuable sources. They show soldiers' feelings, humour and concerns. Some letters about the Christmas truce passed the censors and were published in British newspapers in early January 1915. Officers also wrote letters, which they could censor themselves, and these were often more detailed.

[long-pause]

{img:27-field-postcard-pencil-mud-stained-hands}

The Field Service Postcard allowed soldiers to send brief messages quickly by crossing out printed lines. When soldiers had no time or energy to write a full letter, they could send a Field Service Postcard. This card had printed sentences, stating that the sender was quite well, or had been admitted into hospital, or that a letter would follow at the first opportunity. The soldier crossed out the sentences that did not apply and signed the card. Nothing else could be written on it, or it would be destroyed. Because the card contained no other information, it did not need to be censored and could be sent quickly. Historians note that families treasured these cards, even though they said very little. For relatives at home, the postman's arrival could bring relief or terrible news. The deaths of soldiers were reported through official telegrams or letters. As December 1914 went on, the volume of post increased greatly. Families sent puddings, cakes, chocolate, cards and photographs for Christmas. Even so, deliveries generally kept pace with the demand.

[long-pause]

{img:28-family-fireside-empty-chair-christmas-1914}

By December 1914, it was clear that the war would not be over by Christmas. In August 1914, many people had expected a short war. By December, that hope had gone. The front lines had become fixed, and casualties had been heavy. The British army alone had suffered around ninety thousand casualties by the end of the year. In France and Germany, the losses were much larger. Historians note that the first months of the war were among the bloodiest of the entire conflict. Many families were already grieving. At home, people adjusted to wartime conditions. Prices began to rise, and newspapers were filled with casualty lists. Even so, many still hoped that the war would end within a year. Governments encouraged people to remain confident. Churches held special services and prayers for soldiers. At Christmas, families thought of absent sons, husbands and brothers. Many set a place or raised a glass in their honour. The first Christmas of the war was celebrated with a mixture of hope and sadness.

[long-pause]

{img:29-english-high-street-christmas-1914-shops-snow}

At home in Britain, people prepared for Christmas in December 1914, despite the war. Christmas traditions in Britain continued during the first winter of the war. Shops were decorated with holly, ivy and paper chains. Toy shops displayed dolls, teddy bears, tin soldiers, toy trains and rocking horses. Some toys had a military theme, reflecting the mood of the time. Butchers hung geese and turkeys in their windows, and grocers sold puddings, nuts and fruit. Many shops advertised gifts suitable for sending to soldiers at the front, such as tobacco, writing cases and warm clothing. Historians use newspapers, advertisements and photographs from December 1914 to study how the war affected Christmas. Many families continued to celebrate as normally as possible. Charities collected money and gifts for soldiers and for Belgian refugees, many of whom had arrived in Britain in the autumn. Even so, prices were rising, and some goods were becoming scarce. The war was beginning to shape everyday life at home.

[long-pause]

{img:30-child-drawing-christmas-card-for-father-table}

Families in Britain decorated Christmas trees and kept photographs of absent soldiers on the mantelpiece. By 1914, Christmas trees were common in British homes. The custom had become popular in the nineteenth century, partly because of Queen Victoria and Prince Albert, who was German. Families decorated trees with candles, glass ornaments and paper decorations. Children hung stockings by the fireplace. Historians note that many British Christmas customs, including the tree, had strong German connections. This was one reason why soldiers on both sides recognised each other's celebrations. In homes with a family member at the front, photographs of the absent soldier were often placed among the Christmas cards. Children made cards and drawings to send to their fathers and brothers. Letters from the front, read aloud at Christmas, brought news and reassurance. Even so, families could not know exactly what their soldiers were experiencing. Most could not imagine that, on the front line, some of their men would spend Christmas Day meeting German soldiers in the open.

[long-pause]

{img:31-family-christmas-dinner-raised-glasses-empty-chair}

In Germany, families celebrated Christmas on Christmas Eve with decorated trees and candles. In Germany, the main Christmas celebration traditionally took place on Christmas Eve, called Heiligabend. Families decorated a tree, often with candles, glass ornaments and straw stars, and gathered to exchange gifts. Carols such as Stille Nacht, known in English as Silent Night, were sung. This carol was written in Austria in 1818 and had become popular in many countries. In 1914, many German families had sons and husbands at the front. They sent parcels containing sausages, cakes, cigars and warm clothing. Historians note that German soldiers also received official gifts at Christmas. The army sent small Christmas trees to the front, and soldiers received presents such as pipes and cigars. Even so, German families shared the same anxieties as British ones. Casualty lists were long, and the war showed no sign of ending. On both sides, Christmas 1914 brought thoughts of home to millions of soldiers and their families.

[long-pause]

{img:32-cartoonist-officer-sketching-dugout-candle}

Bruce Bairnsfather, a British officer and artist, served near the village of Saint-Yvon in December 1914. Bruce Bairnsfather was born in 1887 in India, where his father was an army officer. He had served briefly in the army before the war and then trained as an electrical engineer. He also had a talent for drawing. When the war began, he rejoined the army and became a lieutenant in the Royal Warwickshire Regiment. In late 1914, he was in charge of a machine gun section in trenches near Saint-Yvon, at the edge of Ploegsteert Wood in Belgium. He was twenty-seven years old. During quiet moments, Bairnsfather sketched scenes of trench life, often with gentle humour. His drawings showed tired, muddy soldiers coping with absurd and uncomfortable situations. Historians regard his work as one of the most important records of the soldier's view of the war. Even so, his cartoons were not simply jokes. They reflected the real hardships of trench life, and soldiers recognised themselves in them.

[long-pause]

{img:33-low-damp-dugout-straw-bed-dripping-roof}

Frank Richards, a former coal miner and experienced soldier, served with the Royal Welch Fusiliers near Armentières. Frank Richards was born in 1883 and grew up in Monmouthshire in South Wales. He worked as a coal miner and served as a regular soldier with the Royal Welch Fusiliers, including years in India. When war broke out in 1914, he was recalled from the reserve and went to France with his battalion. By December, the battalion was holding trenches near the town of Armentières, close to the Belgian border, near the village of Frelinghien. Richards later wrote a book about his experiences, called Old Soldiers Never Die, published in 1933. It is valued by historians for its honest, unsentimental view of an ordinary soldier's life. In it, he described the Christmas truce in detail. Even so, historians note that the book was written many years after the events and was edited with help from others. Bairnsfather, Richards and Williamson all experienced the truce, and each left a written account of what they saw.

[long-pause]

{img:34-welsh-soldier-pipe-trench-calm-face}

Experienced soldiers such as Frank Richards knew many practical ways to cope with life in the trenches. Regular soldiers who had served for years before the war brought valuable experience to the trenches. They knew how to keep warm and dry, how to make shelters, how to cook with very little and how to avoid trouble with officers. Frank Richards described these skills in his memoir with humour and frankness. He showed little interest in patriotic speeches or glory. His main concern was looking after himself and his friends. Historians note that such accounts are important because they balance the more idealistic writing of some officers and volunteers. They show the war from the perspective of ordinary soldiers. Richards also described the singing of the Welsh soldiers in the trenches at night. Welsh regiments were well known for their choirs and hymns. Accounts from several parts of the line record that German soldiers sometimes answered with their own songs. Even so, these musical exchanges took place alongside sniping and shelling.

[long-pause]

{img:35-welsh-soldiers-singing-trench-night-lantern}

Soldiers on opposite sides of No Man's Land sometimes heard and answered each other's songs. In the winter of 1914, the opposing trenches in many places were close enough for sound to carry across No Man's Land. Soldiers heard each other talking, coughing and singing. Some exchanged songs in the evenings. Accounts from British and German soldiers describe one side singing and the other responding with applause or a song of its own. Historians see these exchanges as part of a wider pattern called live and let live. In quiet sectors, soldiers sometimes avoided firing at each other at certain times, such as during meals. They recognised that both sides faced the same misery. Henry Williamson and the London Rifle Brigade were serving near Ploegsteert Wood at this time. For these young city volunteers, the cold and wet of the trenches came as a shock. Even so, they adapted gradually. Williamson's letters describe both the hardship and his curiosity about the enemy soldiers so close by.

[long-pause]

{img:36-sentry-looking-across-no-mans-land-dusk}

Soldiers could often see smoke from enemy cooking fires and hear voices from the opposite trenches. From their positions, soldiers watched the enemy lines carefully, using periscopes and small observation holes. In the mornings, they could see smoke rising from German trenches as breakfast was cooked, just as from their own. They heard men coughing, talking and sometimes laughing. Historians note that this close observation helped soldiers see the enemy as human beings facing the same conditions. Many letters from December 1914 mention this awareness. Soldiers began to understand that the Germans were just as cold, wet and tired as they were. Some British soldiers had worked in Germany before the war, and many German soldiers had worked in Britain, especially in London. Even so, the war continued. Snipers on both sides remained active, and soldiers who raised their heads above the parapet risked being shot. The sense of shared humanity existed alongside real danger. It was this combination that made the events of Christmas so remarkable.

[long-pause]

{img:37-german-trench-soldiers-pickelhaube-cloth-covers}

German soldiers in 1914 wore field-grey uniforms and spiked leather helmets called Pickelhauben. The German army of 1914 wore uniforms of a grey-green colour known as field grey. On their heads, many soldiers wore the Pickelhaube, a leather helmet with a metal spike on top. In the field, the helmet was usually covered with a grey cloth cover, often marked with the regiment's number. The Pickelhaube offered little protection against shrapnel and was later replaced by a steel helmet in 1916. Many German soldiers in Flanders came from Saxony and Bavaria, as well as Prussia and other German states. Historians note that regional differences mattered. Saxon regiments in particular were later remembered for their friendly attitude during the truce. German rations included bread, sausage, coffee and tinned meat, and German soldiers also received parcels from home. Their trenches faced the same problems of water and mud. Even so, in some sectors, German positions were on slightly higher ground, which gave them better drainage and observation.

[long-pause]

{img:38-young-german-officer-diary-dugout-lamp}

Kurt Zehmisch, a German schoolteacher serving as a lieutenant, kept a detailed diary during the war. Kurt Zehmisch was a teacher from Saxony who served as a lieutenant in the 134th Saxon Infantry Regiment. In December 1914, his unit held trenches opposite the British near Saint-Yvon, facing the Royal Warwickshire Regiment, Bruce Bairnsfather's battalion. Zehmisch kept a diary in which he described daily life in the trenches. He wrote about the rain, the mud and the cold, about his men and about the post from home. He also wrote about the British soldiers opposite, whom he could see and hear. Zehmisch spoke English and French, which helped him communicate during the truce. His diary was rediscovered by his son many decades later and has since been used by historians. It is one of the most important German sources for the Christmas truce. Even so, like all diaries, it reflects one person's experience. Historians compare it with British accounts of the same sector to build a fuller picture.

[long-pause]

{img:39-german-soldiers-singing-around-candle-trench}

German soldiers often sang carols and folk songs in their trenches in the evenings. Singing was an important part of German military and civilian culture. Soldiers sang folk songs, marching songs, hymns and Christmas carols, often in several parts. Many had sung in choirs or clubs at home. In the trenches in December 1914, singing helped pass the long evenings. British soldiers frequently mentioned German singing in their letters. They described it as beautiful, especially when voices carried across No Man's Land on a still night. Historians note that singing often played a central role in starting the Christmas truce. Carols such as Stille Nacht and O Tannenbaum were familiar to soldiers on both sides, even if the words were different. Shared music created a sense of connection. Even so, singing did not always lead to a truce. In some places, songs were exchanged but no meetings took place. The response depended on local officers, the distance between the lines and recent fighting in that sector.

[long-pause]

{img:40-soldiers-shouting-across-fields-hands-cupped}

On the seventh of December 1914, Pope Benedict the Fifteenth called for a Christmas truce. Benedict the Fifteenth became Pope in September 1914, shortly after the war began. He was deeply concerned by the conflict and made many appeals for peace. On the seventh of December 1914, he proposed an official truce for Christmas. He asked that the guns fall silent at least on the night the angels sang. The governments of the warring countries did not accept the proposal, and no official truce was arranged. Historians note that the Pope's appeal had little direct effect on the soldiers, many of whom never heard of it. Even so, it reflected a widespread wish for peace at Christmas. In the trenches, soldiers sometimes made their own informal contacts. There are reports of messages chalked on boards and held up for the other side to read. In some places, small gifts such as tins of food or newspapers were thrown across. These exchanges were local and unofficial, and often forbidden by commanders.

[long-pause]

{img:41-christmas-trees-loaded-train-wagons-snow}

Every British soldier and sailor received a brass gift box from Princess Mary at Christmas 1914. Princess Mary, the seventeen-year-old daughter of King George the Fifth and Queen Mary, started a fund to provide a Christmas gift for every member of the armed forces serving abroad. The public donated generously. The gift was a small embossed brass box, filled with items such as tobacco, cigarettes and a pipe. Non-smokers received sweets and a writing case instead, and nurses received chocolate. The box also contained a Christmas card and a photograph of the princess. More than four hundred thousand boxes were delivered by Christmas Day, and many more were sent in the following months. Historians note that the fund was one of the largest charitable efforts of the early war. German soldiers also received official gifts. Small Christmas trees were sent to the front by train in large numbers. Even so, the volume of Christmas post was enormous on both sides. Railways and postal services worked hard to deliver it.

[long-pause]

{img:42-soldiers-walking-back-village-road-evening}

Soldiers coming out of the trenches rested in billets in villages behind the lines. After several days in the front line, soldiers were relieved and marched back to villages a few miles behind the trenches. They were usually exhausted, muddy and wet. In the rest areas, they slept in barns, farm buildings, schools and houses. Officers were often billeted in farmhouses or the homes of local families. Soldiers washed, changed their clothes, cleaned their equipment and ate hot meals. Large baths were set up in breweries and other buildings, where men could wash in hot water. Historians note that these rest periods were essential for morale and health. The army also organised training, inspections and working parties during this time, so soldiers were rarely idle. Even so, the villages offered a welcome change. They were dry, relatively safe and more comfortable than the trenches. Flemish villages near the front remained partly inhabited in 1914. Many farming families stayed in their homes despite the danger from shelling.

[long-pause]

{img:43-farm-kitchen-soldiers-family-stove-evening}

Small cafés in Flemish villages, known as estaminets, were popular with soldiers. Belgian and French families living near the front often welcomed soldiers into their homes. Soldiers shared food from their parcels with local children, who followed them around the villages. Many villages had small cafés, called estaminets, often run by families in the front room of a house. These offered coffee, beer, wine and simple meals, such as eggs and chips. For soldiers, estaminets were warm, friendly places to relax away from the army. Historians note that they played an important role in soldiers' memories of the war. Many letters and memoirs describe evenings spent in estaminets, talking and singing. Communication with local people was often a mixture of English, French and Flemish, with many misunderstandings. Even so, relations between soldiers and civilians were not always easy. Some farmers complained about damage, theft and the burden of housing troops. Yet many friendships formed during these months, and some lasted long after the war.

[long-pause]

{img:44-armentieres-town-square-shops-soldiers-1914}

The town of Armentières gave its name to one of the most famous songs of the war. Armentières was a textile town in northern France, close to the Belgian border and only a few miles from the front line. In 1914, it was still inhabited, with shops, cafés and businesses open despite occasional shelling. British soldiers visited it often when out of the line. A song called Mademoiselle from Armentières became extremely popular among British troops. It described a girl working in an estaminet, and soldiers invented many verses, some humorous and some rude. Historians note that the origins of the song are uncertain. Several versions and tunes existed, and it was sung throughout the war. Sunday church services were held for soldiers in village churches or in the open air, led by army chaplains. Even so, attendance was not always enthusiastic. Many soldiers preferred to spend their free time resting or visiting cafés. After a few days, units marched back toward the trenches, usually at dusk.

[long-pause]

{img:45-column-marching-night-road-poplars-singing}

When rest periods ended, soldiers marched back to the trenches along roads lined with poplar trees. At the end of a rest period, battalions formed up in the villages and marched back toward the front, usually in the evening. Local families often watched them leave from their doorways. The soldiers marched along straight Flemish roads lined with poplar trees, through the darkness, toward the light of flares above the trenches. Sometimes they sang as they marched, until they came close to the front, where silence was required. Historians note that soldiers often described these marches in letters, with a mixture of sadness and determination. The contrast between the warm billets and the cold trenches was strong. Even so, many soldiers felt a sense of comradeship on these marches. They were returning with friends who shared the same experience. In the weeks before Christmas, the thought of the coming festival was present on both sides of the line. Soldiers wondered how they would spend Christmas in the trenches.

[long-pause]

{img:46-plugstreet-wood-bare-trees-paths-winter}

British soldiers called the wood near the Belgian village of Ploegsteert Plugstreet Wood. Ploegsteert Wood lies just inside Belgium, south of Ypres and close to the French border. British soldiers found the Flemish name difficult to pronounce and called it Plugstreet Wood. It was a large old wood of oak, ash and hazel, with paths and rides running through it. During the war, the wood lay just behind the British front line. Soldiers laid wooden paths through it and gave the paths names such as Hunter Avenue and Mud Lane. The trees provided shelter and hid soldiers from enemy observation. Historians note that the Ploegsteert sector was relatively quiet for much of the war, compared with the area around Ypres to the north. Several units that took part in the Christmas truce were stationed there. Even so, the wood was not completely safe. Shells and stray bullets sometimes struck it. Over the course of the war, many soldiers who died nearby were buried in small cemeteries within and around the wood.

[long-pause]

{img:47-robin-on-trench-parapet-frosty-morning}

Henry Williamson often wrote about nature, including the birds and trees of Ploegsteert Wood. Henry Williamson had loved the countryside since childhood, and even at the front, he noticed the natural world. His letters mention birds, frost on branches and the light in the wood. Many soldiers shared this interest. Robins, sparrows and other birds lived near the trenches, and soldiers sometimes fed them crumbs. Historians note that nature writing was common in soldiers' letters and poems, offering a contrast with the destruction of war. In the village of Ploegsteert, a convent of nuns remained during the early months of the war. The nuns cared for local people and offered help to soldiers. Even so, the village was close enough to the front to be shelled. In later years, it was badly damaged. Williamson later became a famous nature writer. His best-known book, Tarka the Otter, was published in 1927. His wartime experiences in Flanders shaped many of his later books.

[long-pause]

{img:48-frosty-field-two-trench-lines-close-wood-edge}

Near Saint-Yvon, the British and German trenches were separated by fields sloping toward a small stream. Saint-Yvon was a small hamlet at the northern edge of Ploegsteert Wood. In December 1914, the British trenches ran nearby, close to a ruined farmhouse. Opposite them, a short distance away, lay the trenches of the German 134th Saxon Regiment. The ground sloped gently down toward a small stream between the lines. Bruce Bairnsfather's machine gun section held a position here. He made his dugout as comfortable as possible, with a candle, a few drawings and a small stove. Historians have studied this sector in detail because both British and German accounts survive. Bairnsfather's memoir and Zehmisch's diary describe the same events from opposite sides. Even so, the descriptions differ in some details. Historians compare them carefully to establish what happened. In the days before Christmas, the sector was fairly quiet. Occasional rifle shots and bursts of machine gun fire broke the silence, but there was little heavy fighting.

[long-pause]

{img:49-candlelit-dugout-drawings-pinned-wall}

Bairnsfather's cartoons were published in a London magazine and became very popular during the war. Bruce Bairnsfather sent some of his drawings to a weekly magazine in London called The Bystander. The first was published in early 1915, and many more followed. His cartoons showed the humour and hardship of trench life. The most famous character was Old Bill, a middle-aged soldier with a walrus moustache, who faced every difficulty with weary patience. In one well-known cartoon, Old Bill sits in a shell hole with another soldier and says that if he knows a better hole, he should go to it. Historians note that the cartoons were extremely popular with both soldiers and civilians. They were collected in a series of books called Fragments from France. Some officials worried that the cartoons did not show soldiers in a heroic enough way. Even so, the public loved them. Bairnsfather's work helped people at home understand something of trench life. It also helped soldiers laugh at their own situation.

[long-pause]

{img:50-flooded-trench-rain-soldiers-bailing-buckets}

In December 1914, small local attacks in the mud of Flanders achieved very little. During December 1914, commanders on both sides ordered several small attacks in Flanders. On the British side, attacks were made near Wytschaete and Ploegsteert in the middle of the month. They were intended partly to support French offensives and partly to keep pressure on the enemy. Historians note that these attacks were costly and achieved almost nothing. Soldiers struggled through deep mud and were caught by machine gun fire and barbed wire. Bodies of soldiers killed in these attacks lay in No Man's Land for days. In some places, the Christmas truce later allowed both sides to bury their dead. Heavy rain continued through much of December. Trenches flooded, and soldiers spent much of their time bailing water. Even so, many sectors remained relatively quiet. Soldiers on both sides hoped for drier weather. Letters from this period often express a wish for frost, which would at least make the ground firm.

[long-pause]

{img:51-frost-morning-fields-white-trees-clear-sky}

A few days before Christmas, the rain stopped and a hard frost settled over Flanders. After weeks of rain, the weather changed shortly before Christmas. The skies cleared, and the temperature fell below freezing. Many soldiers' accounts describe waking to find the landscape white with frost. The mud froze solid, making it easier to walk. Trenches were still cold, but the ground was firmer and drier. Historians note that the change in weather was often mentioned in letters and diaries as one of the reasons for improved morale before Christmas. Clear skies also meant bright moonlight at night and brilliant sunshine during the day. Frost decorated trees, wire and grass. Even so, the cold brought its own difficulties. Soldiers needed extra blankets and fuel, and water in containers froze. Yet many welcomed the change. The frosty weather created the setting in which the truce took place. Soldiers later remembered the white fields and clear skies as part of the strange beauty of that Christmas.

[long-pause]

{img:52-brass-gift-tin-princess-christmas-1914-hands}

Soldiers also received a Christmas card from King George the Fifth and Queen Mary. Along with Princess Mary's gift box, British soldiers received a Christmas card from the King and Queen. It carried their portraits and a printed message: With our best wishes for Christmas 1914. May God protect you and bring you home safe. Many soldiers were touched by the gift. Historians note that thousands of the brass boxes survive today in museums and private collections. Families kept them as souvenirs, and some still contain their original contents, such as cigarettes or chocolate. The boxes were often reused to hold small items such as photographs or letters. In the trenches, soldiers opened their gifts and parcels from home in the days before Christmas. They shared puddings, cakes, chocolate and tobacco with friends. Even so, the volume of Christmas post put pressure on supply lines. Some parcels arrived damaged or late. Yet the gifts helped create a festive atmosphere in the trenches, even in difficult conditions.

[long-pause]

{img:53-clear-frosty-no-mans-land-both-lines-visible}

In the days just before Christmas, there was little firing in many parts of the line. As Christmas approached, many sectors of the British front became unusually quiet. Soldiers continued their routine work, repairing trenches and preparing food, but there was less shooting. In some places, soldiers began to call out to each other across No Man's Land. They exchanged greetings and jokes, and sometimes arranged not to fire. Historians note that informal truces had already occurred in some places before Christmas, for example to collect the dead after attacks. These local arrangements show that the Christmas truce did not appear from nowhere. It grew out of the live and let live attitudes that had developed during the first weeks of trench warfare. Even so, senior commanders disapproved. On both sides, orders were issued warning against fraternisation with the enemy. Officers feared that friendly contact would weaken the soldiers' fighting spirit. In many sectors, however, these orders had little effect during the days around Christmas.

[long-pause]

{img:54-moonrise-frozen-flanders-fields-poplars}

On the night before Christmas Eve, the air over Flanders was still, cold and clear. With the frost came stillness. Accounts describe nights so quiet that sounds carried a long way across the frozen fields. A cough, a laugh or the clink of a mess tin could be heard from the opposite trench. Soldiers wrapped themselves in greatcoats, blankets and scarves, and tried to sleep in their dugouts. Sentries stood on the fire step, watching the moonlit ground. Historians note that the weather on the nights of the twenty-third and twenty-fourth of December was cold and clear across much of the British front. Moonlight made it possible to see further at night than usual. This increased awareness of the enemy so close by. Even so, sentries remained alert. Neither side knew what the other might do. A raid or attack was still possible. Yet in many sectors, the stillness continued. Soldiers who wrote about these nights described them as peaceful and beautiful, despite the cold and the war.

[long-pause]

{img:55-flemish-village-christmas-eve-church-lights-frost}

Behind the lines, Flemish families celebrated Christmas Eve in their villages as best they could. Many civilians remained in their homes in the villages of Flanders close to the front. They continued to observe Christmas traditions, despite the war. In Belgium, Christmas Eve was an important family occasion, often followed by midnight Mass. Families decorated their homes and prepared special foods, such as bread and cakes. In 1914, supplies were limited because of the war, and much of Belgium was under German occupation. The small area of western Flanders still held by the Allies was crowded with soldiers. Historians note that Belgian civilians in this area lived alongside large numbers of British and French troops. Relations were generally friendly. Soldiers billeted with families often shared in their celebrations. Even so, life for civilians near the front was difficult and dangerous. Shelling damaged houses and churches, and many families eventually had to leave. In December 1914, however, many still remained, keeping their traditions alive in difficult circumstances.

[long-pause]

{img:56-farmers-wife-giving-christmas-bread-soldiers-kitchen}

In some farmhouses, soldiers and Flemish families shared food and sang carols together on Christmas Eve. Soldiers billeted in Flemish farmhouses over Christmas sometimes joined in the family celebrations. They shared food from their parcels, such as chocolate, cake and tea, and the families offered bread, coffee and other local food. In some homes, soldiers and families sang carols in English and Flemish. Historians use letters and diaries to reconstruct these scenes. They show the friendships that developed between soldiers and civilians in the early months of the war. At the convent in Ploegsteert, the nuns prepared for Christmas with decorations and a nativity scene. Some soldiers attended services there or visited the nuns. Even so, accounts of such gatherings are scattered and personal. Not every soldier was out of the line at Christmas, and many civilians had already left the area. Yet the shared celebrations in farmhouses and villages formed part of the wider atmosphere of goodwill that surrounded the Christmas truce.

[long-pause]

{img:57-midnight-mass-damaged-church-candles-villagers-soldiers}

Some soldiers attended midnight Mass with local villagers in damaged Flemish churches on Christmas Eve. In parts of Flanders, local churches held midnight Mass on Christmas Eve as usual, even if their buildings had been damaged by shelling. Villagers attended with their families, and some soldiers joined them, particularly Catholic soldiers from Ireland and elsewhere. The Mass was said in Latin, which was familiar to Catholics of every nationality. Candles lit the churches, and the congregation sang traditional hymns. Historians note that religious services were important to many soldiers and civilians during the war. Army chaplains also held Christmas services for troops, sometimes in barns or in the open air. Even so, attendance depended on duties and circumstances. Many soldiers spent Christmas Eve in the trenches. After the services, villagers walked home through frozen lanes. The night was cold and clear, and the stars were bright. For the soldiers in the front line, Christmas Eve brought one of the most remarkable events of the war.

[long-pause]

{img:58-german-parapet-row-of-lit-christmas-trees-night}

On Christmas Eve 1914, German soldiers placed small lighted Christmas trees on their parapets. As darkness fell on the twenty-fourth of December, British soldiers in many sectors noticed lights appearing along the German trenches. German soldiers had placed small Christmas trees, decorated with candles, on top of their parapets. At first, some British sentries suspected a trick and called their officers. But no attack came. Instead, the Germans began to sing carols. Historians have found many British letters describing this sight. They mention rows of lights stretching along the German line. The trees had been sent to the front as part of the Christmas gifts for German troops. Even so, the custom was not universal. In some sectors, there were no trees, and firing continued. The truce developed in different ways in different places. Where trees appeared, they often marked the beginning of friendly contact. The soldiers on both sides recognised the Christmas tree as a shared symbol, and many were deeply moved by it.

[long-pause]

{img:59-british-soldiers-listening-in-trench-moonlight}

German soldiers sang Stille Nacht, known in English as Silent Night, across No Man's Land. On Christmas Eve, German soldiers in many places sang carols. Stille Nacht was one of the most common. The British soldiers knew the tune as Silent Night, although the English words were less familiar to them than they are today. They listened in their trenches in the frost and moonlight. Some answered with carols of their own, such as The First Noel and O Come, All Ye Faithful. Sometimes both sides sang O Come, All Ye Faithful together, the Germans in Latin as Adeste Fideles. Historians use letters to show how powerful these moments were. Many soldiers wrote that they thought of home, their families and Christmases in the past. Even so, not every soldier was moved. Some remained suspicious or angry, particularly those who had recently lost friends. Yet the shared music crossed the gap between the lines. In many sectors, the singing led to shouted greetings and the arrangement of meetings the next day.

[long-pause]

{img:60-two-soldiers-shaking-hands-moonlit-field}

On Christmas Eve, the German tenor Walter Kirchhoff sang carols to soldiers at the front. Walter Kirchhoff was a well-known tenor at the Berlin Royal Opera. In 1914, he was serving as an officer in the German army. On Christmas Eve, he visited the front near the French lines and sang carols for the soldiers. His trained voice carried across No Man's Land. According to German accounts, French soldiers in the opposite trenches listened and applauded. Historians note that Kirchhoff's performance is one of the most frequently retold stories of the truce. Some details are uncertain, as accounts were written down afterwards and vary. Near Ploegsteert Wood, Henry Williamson also watched the lights on the German parapet and listened to the singing with wonder. He later described the scene in his letters and books. Even so, Kirchhoff's performance was only one of many musical moments that night. Along much of the British front, singing and calling continued late into the night of Christmas Eve.

[long-pause]

{img:61-singer-standing-trench-starlit-night-listeners}

On Christmas Eve, British soldiers shared parcels, sang songs and wrote letters in their dugouts. While carols drifted across No Man's Land, British soldiers in their trenches opened Christmas parcels and shared puddings and cakes. They sang songs, told stories and talked about home late into the night. Candles lit their dugouts. Some soldiers wrote letters to their families describing the lights and the singing. Historians note that many of the best-known letters about the truce were written in the days immediately after Christmas. Families at home sent some of these letters to local newspapers, which published them. As a result, the British public learned about the truce in early January 1915. According to some accounts, Walter Kirchhoff crossed briefly to the French lines after his performance and was given a small gift. Historians treat this story with caution, as it comes from later retellings. Even so, it reflects the spirit of the night. Along much of the line, soldiers on both sides spent Christmas Eve in unexpected peace.

[long-pause]

{img:62-last-candles-flickering-parapet-frost-quiet}

Along much of the British front, Christmas Eve 1914 passed without shooting. As the night went on, soldiers on both sides settled down to sleep, wrapped in greatcoats and blankets, while sentries kept watch. In many sectors, there was no rifle fire and no flares. Historians note that this was highly unusual. Normally, sentries fired occasional shots and flares lit No Man's Land throughout the night. On Christmas Eve 1914, along much of the line held by the British, these sounds stopped. The candles on the German parapets slowly burned down. Frost settled on the sandbags and the wire. Even so, the silence was not complete everywhere. In some sectors, especially where French or Belgian troops faced the Germans, fighting continued. In others, recent losses made soldiers unwilling to trust the enemy. Yet for many soldiers, it was the first quiet night in weeks. Accounts describe the stillness, the cold and the clear stars, and a sense that something unusual was about to happen.

[long-pause]

{img:63-christmas-dawn-frost-mist-no-mans-land-gold}

Christmas morning 1914 dawned cold, clear and frosty over Flanders. On the morning of the twenty-fifth of December, the sun rose over a landscape covered in white frost. A thin mist hung over the fields and turned pink and gold in the early light. Soldiers stood to at dawn as usual, watching No Man's Land. In many sectors, there was no firing. Historians describe how, in several places, German soldiers climbed onto their parapets and waved, sometimes holding up signs wishing the British a merry Christmas. Some shouted greetings in English. The British watched cautiously at first. Raising a head above the parapet was normally very dangerous. Even so, nobody fired. The truce did not begin at the same time everywhere. In some places, contacts had already started on Christmas Eve. In others, they began only on Christmas morning or not at all. Estimates suggest that tens of thousands of soldiers, perhaps around a hundred thousand, took part in some form along the British front.

[long-pause]

{img:64-british-soldier-climbing-out-trench-hesitant}

On Christmas morning, soldiers from both sides climbed out of their trenches and met in No Man's Land. Encouraged by the friendly gestures, British soldiers began to climb out of their trenches and stand in the open. When nobody fired, more followed. Slowly, soldiers on both sides walked into No Man's Land and met in the middle, among the frozen shell holes and frosted grass. They shook hands and wished each other a merry Christmas. Historians note that officers often took the lead in arranging these meetings, agreeing on boundaries and times. In some places, officers met first to agree a truce, and their men followed. In others, ordinary soldiers acted on their own initiative. Many letters describe the strange feeling of standing in the open, in daylight, close to the enemy. Even so, the soldiers remained cautious. Most stayed within a short distance of their own lines. Some kept their rifles nearby. Yet the meetings were generally friendly. Soldiers exchanged greetings in a mixture of English, German and gestures.

[long-pause]

{img:65-soldiers-exchanging-photos-cigars-frost}

During the truce, soldiers exchanged gifts such as food, cigarettes, buttons and badges. Once the soldiers met, they began to exchange small gifts. British soldiers offered tins of bully beef, chocolate, cigarettes and Christmas pudding. German soldiers gave sausages, cigars, schnapps and beer. Many swapped buttons, cap badges and other souvenirs, and showed each other photographs of their families. Some Germans spoke good English, having worked in Britain before the war. One German soldier, according to British accounts, had been a barber in London and offered haircuts in No Man's Land. Near Saint-Yvon, Bruce Bairnsfather went out and met soldiers of the Saxon regiment. He exchanged buttons with a German officer and later described the scene in his memoir. Historians note that Kurt Zehmisch, on the German side, recorded the same meetings in his diary. Even so, the two accounts differ in details, such as times and who spoke first. Together, they confirm that friendly exchanges took place across this part of the line.

[long-pause]

{img:66-german-officer-talking-english-soldiers-smiling}

Near Armentières, German soldiers rolled barrels of beer across to the Royal Welch Fusiliers. In the sector held by the Royal Welch Fusiliers near Armentières, the truce included an exchange of drink. According to Frank Richards, the Germans rolled two barrels of beer across No Man's Land as a Christmas present. The British soldiers drank it and shared their own supplies in return. Richards noted, with characteristic honesty, that the French beer was rather weak. Historians often quote this detail because it adds a human, humorous touch to the story. In the same sector, German and British soldiers talked, exchanged gifts and wished each other well. Richards also described a German soldier who spoke excellent English, having worked in England before the war. Even so, Richards was careful to note that the truce was a local arrangement between soldiers. It was not approved by senior commanders. His account, written many years later, remains one of the most vivid descriptions of Christmas Day in the trenches.

[long-pause]

{img:67-many-soldiers-mingling-no-mans-land-sunshine}

A few soldiers took photographs of the Christmas truce, although cameras were officially forbidden. British soldiers were not allowed to carry cameras at the front, but some had brought small ones from home anyway. During the truce, several took photographs. The pictures show British and German soldiers standing together in No Man's Land, in greatcoats and caps, smiling at the camera. Some of these photographs were published in British newspapers in January 1915. Historians note that they are among the most important pieces of evidence for the truce. They prove that the events described in letters really took place. The photographs are now held in museums such as the Imperial War Museum in London. Even so, there are fewer images than many people imagine. Only a small number of genuine photographs exist, mostly from the area around Ploegsteert. Some images often shown today are from later reconstructions or films. Historians therefore treat pictures of the truce carefully, checking their origins before using them as evidence.

[long-pause]

{img:68-football-kicked-frosty-field-soldiers-greatcoats}

In some places along the line, soldiers played football during the Christmas truce. Football is one of the best-known parts of the story of the Christmas truce. Several letters and diaries mention soldiers kicking a ball around in No Man's Land. Historians note, however, that the evidence is limited. Most reports describe informal kickabouts rather than organised matches with teams and scores. The frozen and broken ground made proper games difficult. Some soldiers used a real football, and others used a tin or a bundle of rags. Even so, the evidence is strong enough to show that football took place in at least a few locations. A German officer, Johannes Niemann of the 133rd Saxon Regiment, later described a match in which the Germans won three goals to two. A British letter also mentioned a game ending with a three to two score. Historians debate whether these accounts refer to the same event. Football became the central image of the truce in popular memory, especially in the late twentieth century.

[long-pause]

{img:69-diary-page-candle-german-handwriting}

Some accounts describe soldiers teaching each other games and the ball bursting on barbed wire. Several surviving accounts mention details about play during the truce. One German account describes a game that had to stop when the ball landed on barbed wire and burst. Others describe British soldiers trying to teach the Germans their favourite games, and Germans showing British soldiers their own. Football was very popular in Britain by 1914, and it was growing in Germany as well. Historians use diaries, letters and later interviews with veterans to piece together these stories. Some details were recorded at the time, and others were remembered decades later. Memory can change over time, so historians compare different sources carefully. Even so, a consistent picture emerges. Soldiers on both sides enjoyed a rare chance to relax, play and laugh together. For a few hours, the frozen fields of No Man's Land became a place of games rather than war. Many participants later remembered these scenes as the highlight of the day.

[long-pause]

{img:70-soldiers-swapping-caps-laughing-frosty-field}

Not all soldiers took part in the truce, and fighting continued in some parts of the line. The Christmas truce was widespread but not universal. In some sectors, fighting continued on Christmas Day, and soldiers were killed. In others, officers ordered their men to remain in the trenches, and contact was limited to shouted greetings. French and Belgian troops, whose countries had been invaded, were generally less willing to take part than the British. Historians note that recent fighting and local commanders strongly influenced whether a truce took place. One famous story describes soldiers from both sides chasing a hare across No Man's Land together, laughing as they slipped on the frozen ground, until someone caught it. Such incidents show the relaxed mood in places where the truce held. Even so, many soldiers remained suspicious of the enemy. Some disapproved of the truce altogether. Among them was a young German soldier named Adolf Hitler, who, according to a fellow soldier, criticised the fraternisation. The truce was a local and varied event.

[long-pause]

{img:71-soldiers-walking-back-trenches-sunset-frost}

Many soldiers wrote home describing the truce as the strangest and most memorable Christmas of their lives. As the short winter day ended, soldiers returned to their trenches. Many wrote letters that evening or in the days that followed. They described the meetings, the gifts, the photographs and the games. Some wrote that it was the most extraordinary Christmas they had ever known. Historians have collected hundreds of these letters. They are held in archives, museums and family collections. Many were published in local newspapers in Britain in early 1915. Henry Williamson wrote to his mother on the twenty-sixth of December, describing how he had talked with German soldiers and exchanged gifts. Even so, the letters vary in tone. Some soldiers were joyful, while others were puzzled or uneasy. A few expressed concern that the truce might be a trick. Yet together, the letters show that the experience made a deep impression. For many families at home, these letters were the first news that such a thing had happened.

[long-pause]

{img:72-christmas-night-trench-dugout-candles-dinner}

On the evening of Christmas Day, soldiers ate Christmas dinners in their trenches and billets. Christmas dinner in the trenches was simple but welcome. British soldiers ate bully beef, stew and potatoes, with Christmas pudding from home heated over small fires. Some had a little rum. In a few places, soldiers had bought geese or chickens from local farms. Officers sometimes shared special meals with their men. Behind the lines, units in rest areas enjoyed larger dinners organised by their officers, sometimes with roast meat, plum pudding and beer. Historians note that army authorities made considerable efforts to provide festive food for the troops. Plum puddings were sent in large numbers. German soldiers also had special meals, with sausages, cakes and beer. Even so, conditions in the front line limited what could be prepared. Many soldiers remembered the company more than the food. Singing continued in many places on Christmas night. In some sectors, it was agreed that the truce would continue for another day.

[long-pause]

{img:73-family-reading-newspaper-fireside-january-1915}

Newspapers in Britain published letters and photographs of the truce in early January 1915. When letters describing the truce reached Britain, families shared them with local newspapers. National papers also published reports and photographs. On the eighth of January 1915, the Daily Mirror printed photographs of British and German soldiers together in No Man's Land. Historians note that British newspapers were generally sympathetic. They presented the truce as a moving example of Christmas spirit. Some readers were shocked, believing it was wrong to fraternise with the enemy. Most, however, were moved. In Germany, newspapers reported the truce less widely. In France, where much territory was occupied, there was less sympathy for such contacts. In many places, the truce did not end on Christmas Day. On Boxing Day, soldiers met again, exchanged more gifts and buried their dead. Even so, the length of the truce varied. In some sectors, it lasted only a few hours. In others, it continued for several days, and in a few places, into the new year.

[long-pause]

{img:74-officer-reading-orders-candle-dugout-january}

After Christmas, firing resumed gradually along the line, and senior commanders forbade further truces. In the days after Christmas, the truce came to an end in most places. Officers received orders to resume hostilities. In some sectors, both sides agreed on a signal to mark the end of the truce. According to one account, a British officer and a German officer met in No Man's Land, saluted each other, shook hands and returned to their trenches before firing began again. In other places, soldiers deliberately fired high for a time. Historians note that senior commanders on both sides were concerned about the truce. They feared that friendly contact would weaken the willingness to fight. Orders were issued to prevent fraternisation in the future. In later years, there were some smaller truces, but nothing on the scale of 1914. Even so, the memory of the truce remained among those who had taken part. The war continued for nearly four more years and became far more destructive than anyone had expected.

[long-pause]

{img:75-winter-fields-flanders-snow-quiet-dusk}

Many veterans remembered the Christmas truce for the rest of their lives. For the soldiers who took part, the Christmas truce became a lasting memory. Many talked about it decades later, describing the candles on the parapets, the singing in the night, the handshakes in No Man's Land and the football on frozen ground. Historians who interviewed veterans in later years found that the truce remained one of their clearest recollections of the war. In the 1960s and 1980s, new books and documentary films brought renewed attention to the event. Historians such as Malcolm Brown and Shirley Seaton collected many accounts and published a detailed study of the truce. Even so, memory changed over time. Some details became exaggerated or merged with popular images. Historians therefore compare later memories with letters and diaries written in 1914. The truce did not shorten the war or change its course. Yet it showed that soldiers on both sides could see each other as fellow human beings, even in the middle of war.

[long-pause]

{img:76-november-morning-mist-fields-1918-quiet}

On the eleventh of November 1918, the fighting of the First World War came to an end. Nearly four years after the first Christmas in the trenches, the war ended with an armistice. It was signed early in the morning of the eleventh of November 1918, in a railway carriage in the forest of Compiègne in northern France. The fighting stopped at eleven o'clock that morning, on the eleventh day of the eleventh month. Along the Western Front, the guns fell silent. Church bells rang in Britain, France, Belgium and other countries. Historians note that around nine million soldiers died during the war, along with millions of civilians. Flanders had seen some of the fiercest fighting, including several battles around Ypres. The landscape of 1914, with its farms, woods and villages, had been almost completely destroyed in places. Even so, the end of the war brought enormous relief. Soldiers who had survived began to return home. Many of those who had taken part in the Christmas truce had been killed or wounded in the years that followed.

[long-pause]

{img:77-village-church-bells-ringing-crowd-1918}

After the armistice, soldiers slowly returned home to their families over the following months. When the war ended, people celebrated in many countries. In Britain, France and Belgium, crowds gathered in the streets, and church bells rang. In Germany, people were relieved that the war was over, though their country had lost and faced political turmoil and hardship. Demobilisation, the process of sending soldiers home, took many months. Soldiers travelled by ship and train back to their towns and villages. Historians note that returning home was not always easy. Many soldiers had been physically or mentally injured. Others found that jobs and homes were scarce. Families had changed while they were away. Even so, many veterans rebuilt their lives. They returned to work, married and raised families. Some rarely spoke about the war. Others joined veterans' associations and kept in touch with old comrades. The memory of the war shaped the rest of their lives, and many remembered both its horrors and its rare moments of humanity.

[long-pause]

{img:78-flemish-family-returning-ruined-village-spring}

After the war, the people of Flanders returned and rebuilt their farms, villages and towns. By 1918, large parts of Flanders were devastated. Villages had been reduced to rubble, forests destroyed and fields churned into mud and craters. Many civilians had fled during the war. After the armistice, they began to return. They cleared the land, filled in trenches and shell holes, removed unexploded shells and ploughed the fields again. Historians note that the reconstruction of Flanders took many years and was supported by the Belgian government. Farmhouses, churches and town halls were rebuilt, often in traditional styles. In Ypres, the medieval Cloth Hall, almost completely destroyed, was rebuilt over several decades and completed in 1967. Even so, the war left lasting marks. Farmers still find unexploded shells and other remains in their fields every year. These are collected by the Belgian army, a practice sometimes called the iron harvest. Yet the countryside recovered, and farms once again surrounded the villages.

[long-pause]

{img:79-ypres-cloth-hall-rebuilt-market-square-evening}

In Ypres, the Menin Gate memorial was unveiled in 1927 to remember soldiers with no known grave. After the war, the Imperial War Graves Commission, now known as the Commonwealth War Graves Commission, built cemeteries and memorials across France and Belgium. Soldiers who died were buried close to where they fell, in cemeteries of white headstones. Those whose bodies were never found or identified were remembered on large memorials. In Ypres, the Menin Gate was built on the road leading out of the town toward the battlefields. It was designed by the architect Reginald Blomfield and unveiled in 1927. Historians note that the memorials and cemeteries of Flanders form one of the largest commemorative landscapes in the world. Hundreds of cemeteries are scattered across the countryside, in fields, beside roads and at the edges of woods. Even so, many visitors are surprised by their quiet and order. Among those remembered are soldiers who took part in the Christmas truce of 1914, many of whom were killed in the years that followed.

[long-pause]

{img:80-red-poppies-field-flanders-summer-sky}

After the war, people began wearing red poppies every November in memory of those who died. The red poppy became a symbol of remembrance because poppies grew in large numbers on the disturbed ground of the battlefields. In 1915, the Canadian doctor John McCrae wrote a poem called In Flanders Fields, which describes poppies growing between the crosses of soldiers' graves. The poem became very popular. After the war, an American teacher named Moina Michael began wearing a poppy in memory of the dead, and the idea spread. In 1921, the British Legion adopted the poppy as its symbol and sold paper poppies to raise money for veterans. Historians note that the poppy remains one of the most widely recognised symbols of remembrance. Every year, on the eleventh of November, people in many countries observe two minutes of silence at eleven o'clock in the morning. Even so, customs vary between countries. In some places, other flowers, such as the cornflower in France, are used instead.

[long-pause]

{img:81-writer-cottage-devon-river-otter-evening}

Henry Williamson survived the war and became a famous writer about nature and the countryside. Henry Williamson served throughout the war, from the Christmas truce in Ploegsteert Wood to the later stages of the conflict. He was commissioned as an officer and served in several units. After the war, he found it difficult to settle in London. He moved to North Devon and lived in a cottage in the village of Georgeham. There, he wrote about the countryside and its wildlife. His most famous book, Tarka the Otter, published in 1927, follows the life of an otter in the rivers of Devon. It won a major literary prize and remains popular today. Williamson also wrote a long series of novels about the war and the years around it. Historians note that his writing about the Christmas truce helped keep its memory alive. Even so, Williamson was a controversial figure. In the 1930s, he expressed support for fascist ideas, which damaged his reputation. He died in 1977.

[long-pause]

{img:82-cartoonist-older-drawing-studio-sketches}

Bruce Bairnsfather described the Christmas truce in his book Bullets and Billets, published in 1916. In 1916, Bruce Bairnsfather published a memoir called Bullets and Billets. In it, he described his experiences in the trenches, including the Christmas truce at Saint-Yvon. He wrote that he would not have missed that Christmas for anything. He described meeting German soldiers, exchanging buttons and watching a German barber cutting a British soldier's hair. Bairnsfather's cartoon character, Old Bill, became famous across Britain. His work was adapted into a successful stage play called The Better 'Ole, and later into films. During the Second World War, he worked as an official cartoonist with American forces in Europe. He died in 1959. Frank Richards also survived the whole war, serving from 1914 to 1918, which was rare for a soldier who had been at the front from the beginning. He later published Old Soldiers Never Die in 1933. Historians value both books as vivid, personal records of the war.

[long-pause]

{img:83-german-schoolteacher-classroom-1920s-chalkboard}

Kurt Zehmisch returned to teaching after the war, and his diary was later rediscovered by his son. Kurt Zehmisch survived the war and returned to Saxony, where he worked as a teacher. In his diary, he wrote that the day of the Christmas truce had been wonderful and strange, and that he would never forget it. His diary remained with his family for many years. His son, Rudolf Zehmisch, later found it and shared it with historians. It has since been used in books, documentaries and exhibitions about the truce. Historians value it because it offers a German perspective on the same events described by Bruce Bairnsfather. Even so, it reflects the view of one officer in one sector. Many soldiers kept souvenirs from the truce. The brass Princess Mary gift boxes were kept by thousands of British soldiers as treasured reminders. Today, many survive in museums and family homes across Britain. Some still contain their original contents, more than a century later, preserved as quiet links to the first Christmas of the war.

[long-pause]

{img:84-very-old-veteran-armchair-telling-story-grandchild}

Alfred Anderson, one of the last surviving veterans of the Christmas truce, died in 2005 at the age of 109. Alfred Anderson was born in 1896 in Scotland. In 1914, he served with the Black Watch, a Scottish regiment, in France. He took part in the Christmas truce, though his unit did not meet the Germans in No Man's Land. In later interviews, he remembered the silence of Christmas morning more than anything else. He described it as an eerie silence and recalled that soldiers shouted greetings across the lines. Anderson later served as a batman, an officer's servant, and was wounded in 1916. After the war, he returned to Scotland and worked as a joiner. He became widely known in his final years as one of the last links to the truce. Historians note that interviews with very old veterans must be treated carefully, because memories fade and change. Even so, his recollections were consistent over many years. When he died in 2005, his death was reported widely in Britain and abroad.

[long-pause]

{img:85-flanders-today-green-fields-poplars-church-spire}

Today, Flanders is once again a peaceful farming region with fields, villages and poplar-lined roads. More than a century after the war, the landscape of Flanders has largely returned to its former character. Flat fields stretch under wide skies, green in spring and summer, golden at harvest and brown in winter. Poplar trees line the roads, and farmhouses with red-tiled roofs stand among the fields. Cows graze in pastures that were once part of No Man's Land. Historians and visitors note that traces of the war remain, if one knows where to look. Small cemeteries appear among the fields, and memorials stand at crossroads and village centres. Some trench lines and craters are preserved at sites such as Hill 60 and Sanctuary Wood. Even so, everyday life in Flanders continues as in any rural area. Farmers plough their fields, children go to school and markets are held in the towns. Tourism linked to the war is an important part of the local economy, bringing many visitors each year.

[long-pause]

{img:86-small-cemetery-inside-wood-bluebells-spring}

The Ploegsteert Memorial records the names of more than eleven thousand soldiers with no known grave. Near Ploegsteert Wood stands a memorial built by the Imperial War Graves Commission and unveiled in 1931. It is a circular building of white stone, guarded by two stone lions. On its walls are carved the names of more than eleven thousand soldiers who died in the surrounding area and have no known grave. Inside and around the wood, several small cemeteries contain the graves of soldiers who died nearby. In spring, bluebells cover the ground among the trees. Historians note that the area of Ploegsteert is closely associated with the Christmas truce. Near Saint-Yvon, where Bruce Bairnsfather and Kurt Zehmisch met in 1914, a simple wooden cross was erected in 1999 to mark the truce. Visitors often leave small footballs, poppies and messages beside it. Even so, the site remains modest and quiet. The fields around it are farmed as usual, and few signs of the war remain on the surface.

[long-pause]

{img:87-wooden-cross-field-footballs-poppies-winter}

In December 2014, a memorial to the Christmas truce was unveiled near Ploegsteert to mark its hundredth anniversary. The centenary of the Christmas truce was marked by many events in 2014. Near Ploegsteert, a new memorial was unveiled in December, in a ceremony attended by football officials and representatives from several countries. In Britain, soldiers from the British and German armies played a commemorative football match. Schools, football clubs and churches held events to remember the truce. Historians note that the anniversary brought new interest in the original sources. Letters, diaries and photographs were displayed in exhibitions and published online. Many families shared documents they had kept for generations. Even so, historians reminded the public that the truce was a brief and limited event. It did not end the war, and in most sectors fighting resumed within days. Yet the centenary showed how deeply the story continues to matter. It is remembered as an example of peace and shared humanity in the middle of a terrible war.

[long-pause]

{img:88-menin-gate-evening-buglers-crowd-quiet}

Every evening at eight o'clock, buglers play the Last Post under the Menin Gate in Ypres. Since 1928, members of the local fire brigade in Ypres have sounded the Last Post at the Menin Gate each evening. The ceremony was interrupted only during the German occupation of Belgium in the Second World War, when it was held in England instead. It resumed on the evening Ypres was liberated in September 1944. The walls of the Menin Gate bear the names of more than fifty-four thousand soldiers of the British Empire who died in the Ypres area and have no known grave. Every evening, visitors gather in silence as the buglers play, and wreaths of poppies are laid. Historians note that the ceremony has become one of the most famous acts of remembrance in the world. At Christmas, local communities in Flanders also remember the truce with concerts, exhibitions and football matches. Even so, the daily ceremony remains simple. A few minutes of music and silence end the day under the great stone arch.

{img:89-winter-dusk-flanders-farmhouse-lights-stars-frost}

The Christmas truce of 1914 is remembered as a brief moment of peace in a long and terrible war. The truce took place during the first winter of the First World War, in the frozen fields of Flanders and northern France. Along much of the British front, soldiers stopped fighting, sang carols, exchanged gifts, shook hands and in some places played football. Bruce Bairnsfather, Frank Richards, Henry Williamson, Kurt Zehmisch and many others recorded what they saw. Historians have used their letters, diaries and memoirs, together with photographs, to reconstruct the events. The truce did not change the course of the war. It lasted only a short time, and the fighting continued for nearly four more years. Even so, it has become one of the most famous episodes of the twentieth century. It shows that ordinary people on opposite sides could share moments of kindness and hope. Today, in the small cemeteries among the fields, white headstones stand in rows under the winter stars, and frost settles on what was once No Man's Land.
