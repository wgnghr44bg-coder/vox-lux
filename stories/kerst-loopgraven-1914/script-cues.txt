Tonight we are going a little more than a hundred and ten years back, to the flat, quiet farmland of Flanders, in the winter of 1914.

[long-pause]

It is a grey morning in early December.

[pause]

A low mist lies over the fields. The land here is almost perfectly flat, stretching away in every direction, with rows of tall, thin poplar trees marking the roads and the edges of the fields, and here and there a farmhouse with a red-tiled roof, a church spire in the distance, a little wood, a windmill. Ditches full of still, grey water run along the edges of the fields.

[pause]

If calm stories from history help you rest, you are welcome to subscribe to the channel. Now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

Flanders is the northern part of Belgium, and the corner of France just beside it.

[pause]

For a thousand years, it has been a land of farmers and weavers and merchants. The soil is rich and heavy, made of clay, and the farmers grow wheat and barley, sugar beet and potatoes, and keep cows and pigs on the wet green meadows. In the towns, there are old market squares, with tall cloth halls and belfries and churches, built in the Middle Ages when Flanders was one of the richest places in Europe.

[pause]

The people are quiet, hardworking, devout. They speak Flemish, a kind of Dutch, in the Belgian villages, and French in the villages across the border. On Sundays, the church bells ring across the flat land, and the families walk to Mass along the muddy lanes, in their best clothes.

[long-pause]

But in the winter of 1914, the land is not peaceful.

[pause]

Since August, there has been a war. It began far away, in the Balkans, and spread, in a few short weeks, across the whole of Europe. Germany and Austria-Hungary on one side. France and Russia and Britain and Belgium on the other.

[pause]

{img:soldiers-marching-poplar-road-autumn-1914}
In August, the German armies marched through Belgium, toward France. Through the autumn, the armies fought and marched and fought again, across northern France and Flanders, until, in October and November, they came to a stop here, in the flat fields of Flanders, facing each other, and dug themselves into the ground.

[long-pause]

Now, a long line of trenches runs across the land.

[pause]

It begins on the coast of the North Sea, near the little Belgian town of Nieuwpoort, and runs south, through the fields of Flanders, past the old town of Ypres, through the hills and woods and villages of northern France, and on, all the way to the mountains of Switzerland, more than four hundred miles away.

[pause]

On one side of the line, there are the German soldiers. On the other, the French and the Belgians and the British. Between them, there is a strip of empty land, sometimes only a few dozen yards wide, sometimes a few hundred, which the soldiers call No Man's Land.

[long-pause]

In this story, we will spend a little time in one small part of that long line.

[pause]

It is a stretch of about thirty miles in Flanders, south of Ypres, between the Belgian village of Ploegsteert, which the British soldiers call Plugstreet, and the French town of Armentières, and the little villages around them.

[pause]

{img:village-church-ruined-tower-winter-sky}
Here, in the winter of 1914, the British army faces the German army across the fields. And here, on Christmas Day, something will happen that nobody expects, and that people will still be talking about more than a hundred years later.

[long-pause]

But that is still a few weeks away.

[pause]

On this grey December morning, the soldiers on both sides are waking up in their trenches, cold and stiff and muddy, and lighting little fires to boil water for their tea or their coffee, and looking out across the fields at the mist, and thinking about home.

[pause]

{img:soldiers-waking-trench-dawn-smoke-small-fire}
Most of them are young men. Many of them have been soldiers for only a few months. A few months ago, they were clerks and farmers, students and miners, shop assistants and factory workers, schoolteachers and butchers and bakers.

[pause]

They did not imagine that they would spend Christmas in a muddy ditch in Flanders.

[long-pause]

Behind the lines, in the villages and farms, the Flemish and French families are still living in their houses, as best they can.

[pause]

The farmers still try to look after their animals and their land. The women still bake bread, and wash clothes, and keep house. The children still go to school, when there is school. Many of the soldiers lodge in their farmhouses and barns, when they come out of the trenches to rest.

[pause]

The farmers' wives sell them coffee and eggs and bread and fried potatoes, and the soldiers, who are far from home, are glad of a warm kitchen and a kind face.

[long-pause]

In the evening, the mist comes down again over the fields, and the light fades early, because it is December.

[pause]

Along the line, the soldiers settle down for the night, in their trenches and their dugouts, wrapped in their greatcoats and their blankets. Sentries stand on watch, peering out into the darkness. Now and then, a flare goes up into the sky, and drifts down slowly, lighting the fields with a pale, white light, and then goes out.

[pause]

{img:quiet-night-trench-sentry-flare-distant}
And in the farmhouses behind the lines, the families say their prayers, and blow out their lamps, and go to bed, and listen to the wind in the poplar trees, and hope for peace.

[long-pause]

{img:summer-1914-english-village-cricket-sunshine}
The summer of 1914 was one of the most beautiful summers that anyone could remember.

[pause]

In England, the days were long and warm and golden. Families went to the seaside, and sat in striped deckchairs on the beach, and paddled in the sea. In the villages, there were cricket matches on the green, and garden parties, and fêtes. In the cities, people sat in the parks in the evening, listening to brass bands playing in the bandstands.

[long-pause]

In Germany, too, it was a lovely summer.

[pause]

Families walked in the forests on Sundays, and sat in the beer gardens under the chestnut trees, drinking beer and eating sausages and listening to the music. Students went hiking in the mountains, singing as they walked. In the villages, the farmers brought in the harvest.

[pause]

Nobody, in England or in Germany or in France, imagined that within a few weeks, their countries would be at war with each other.

[long-pause]

Then, at the end of June, an Austrian archduke was shot in the city of Sarajevo, far away in the Balkans.

[pause]

At first, most people paid little attention. There had been troubles in the Balkans before. But this time, one thing led to another, very quickly. Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia. Russia prepared to help Serbia. Germany declared war on Russia, and then on France. And on the fourth of August, when the German army marched into Belgium, Britain declared war on Germany.

[pause]

{img:crowds-outside-palace-august-evening-1914}
In London, on the evening of the fourth of August, great crowds gathered outside Buckingham Palace, singing and cheering. In Berlin and Paris and Vienna, there were crowds too, singing and cheering in the streets.

[long-pause]

Everyone said that the war would be short.

[pause]

It will all be over by Christmas, people said. The soldiers would march off, and fight a few great battles, and then they would all come home, in time for Christmas dinner.

[pause]

In Britain, the army was small, and made up of volunteers. So the government asked for more men to join. Lord Kitchener, the Secretary of State for War, a tall, stern man with a great moustache, appeared on posters all over the country, pointing his finger, saying that his country needed you.

[pause]

And the young men came, in their hundreds of thousands. They queued outside the recruiting offices, in the summer sunshine, laughing and joking with their friends, eager not to miss the adventure.

[long-pause]

Many of them joined up together with their friends, from the same street, or the same factory, or the same football team, or the same office.

[pause]

They wanted to serve together, side by side. Whole battalions were formed of friends and neighbours, from the same town or the same trade: the Liverpool Pals, the Accrington Pals, the Grimsby Chums, the Footballers' Battalion.

[pause]

They marched off through the streets of their towns, with brass bands playing and flags flying, while their mothers and sisters and sweethearts waved from the pavements, and the little boys ran alongside, shouting and cheering.

[long-pause]

In Germany, too, the young men went off to war with flowers in their caps and their rifles.

[pause]

The trains were decorated with branches and flowers, and the soldiers wrote on the sides of the carriages, in chalk, that they were off on an outing to Paris. Students left their universities, and workers their factories, and farmers their fields, and went off singing.

[pause]

{img:german-troop-train-flowers-chalk-station}
In France, the soldiers marched off in their bright blue coats and red trousers, the same uniforms their grandfathers had worn, and the crowds threw flowers at them as they passed.

[long-pause]

But the war was not short.

[pause]

By the autumn, hundreds of thousands of men had been killed or wounded, on both sides, in great battles in Belgium and France. The armies had marched and fought across the countryside, until they were exhausted, and could go no further.

[pause]

Then, to protect themselves, they began to dig. And by the end of November 1914, the two great armies faced each other across a line of trenches that stretched across the whole of western Europe.

[long-pause]

Among the young men who joined the British army in that summer of 1914, there was a young clerk from London named Henry Williamson.

[pause]

He was eighteen years old, a thin, dreamy, sensitive young man, who loved the countryside, and the birds and the animals, and wanted to be a writer. He worked as a clerk in an office in the city of London, which he found dull. He had joined a part-time volunteer regiment, the London Rifle Brigade, a few months before the war, partly for the fun of the summer camps.

[pause]

{img:young-clerk-london-office-window-dreaming}
When the war began, the London Rifle Brigade was sent to France, and Henry went with them. He turned nineteen on the first of December, in the trenches of Flanders, near Plugstreet Wood.

[pause]

On Christmas Day, he would write a letter home to his mother that he would remember for the rest of his life.

[long-pause]

{img:troopship-leaving-southampton-night-1914}
For the young British soldiers of 1914, the journey to the front began at a railway station, somewhere in England, early in the morning.

[pause]

They marched through the streets of their towns to the station, with their packs on their backs and their rifles on their shoulders, while the people came out of their houses to wave and cheer. At the station, their families were waiting on the platform, to say goodbye.

[long-pause]

There were mothers and fathers, wives and children, brothers and sisters and sweethearts. They hugged and kissed and shook hands, and tried to smile. Some of the mothers gave their sons a last packet of sandwiches, or a pair of socks, or a little Bible to keep in their pocket.

[pause]

Then the whistle blew, and the soldiers climbed into the carriages, and leaned out of the windows, waving, as the train pulled slowly out of the station, and the platform full of waving handkerchiefs grew smaller and smaller behind them.

[pause]

The train took them south, through the green English countryside, to the ports on the coast: Southampton, or Folkestone, or Dover.

[long-pause]

At the port, they boarded a ship.

[pause]

It was usually at night, so that German submarines would not see them. The soldiers crowded onto the decks of the steamers, in the darkness, and watched the lights of England disappearing behind them. Some of them had never been to sea before, and the crossing was rough, and many of them were seasick.

[pause]

{img:soldiers-on-ship-deck-night-crossing-channel}
In the early morning, they arrived in France, at the ports of Le Havre or Boulogne or Calais, and climbed down the gangways onto the quays, stiff and cold and tired, and marched through the grey streets of the French towns to great camps of tents on the hills above the harbour.

[long-pause]

The French people watched them come.

[pause]

They had never seen so many British soldiers. The children ran alongside them, shouting for souvenirs and chocolate. The women waved from the windows, and threw flowers. The soldiers tried out the few words of French that they knew, and the French laughed, and tried out the few words of English that they knew.

[pause]

In the camps, the soldiers were given more training, and new equipment, and then, after a few days, they were put on a train to the front.

[long-pause]

The trains that took the soldiers to the front were not passenger trains.

[pause]

They were goods trains, made of little wooden wagons that were usually used for carrying cattle and horses. On the side of each wagon, there were words painted in French: Hommes quarante, chevaux huit. Men forty, horses eight. This meant that each wagon could carry forty men, or eight horses.

[pause]

{img:soldiers-in-wooden-goods-wagon-train-france}
The soldiers crowded into the wagons, forty at a time, sitting on the straw on the floor, with their packs and their rifles, and the train set off, very slowly, rattling and jolting through the flat French countryside, for a whole day and a night, or longer.

[long-pause]

They passed through little towns and villages, past fields and farms and churches, past rivers and canals and woods.

[pause]

At every station, the train stopped, and the soldiers climbed out to stretch their legs, and to fill their water bottles, and to buy bread and coffee and wine from the French people who came to the station to sell them. At night, they slept on the straw, curled up together for warmth, with the wagon swaying and rattling around them.

[pause]

{img:train-stopped-french-village-station-soldiers-coffee}
The soldiers sang, to pass the time. They sang the songs of the music halls, and the songs of their regiments, and the hymns of their chapels, and their voices drifted out of the open doors of the wagons across the fields.

[long-pause]

At last, the train stopped, at a little station somewhere in the north of France, or in Belgium, near the front.

[pause]

The soldiers climbed out, and formed up in the road, and began to march. They marched along long, straight roads, paved with cobblestones, lined with tall poplar trees, through the flat, wet country of Flanders.

[pause]

The cobblestones were hard on their feet, and their boots were new and stiff, and their packs were heavy. But they kept going, singing, mile after mile, through the villages and past the farms, where the people came to their doors to watch them pass.

[long-pause]

As they got closer to the front, they began to hear a sound, far away, like distant thunder: the sound of the guns.

[pause]

They passed through villages where some of the houses were damaged, and the church towers were broken. They passed wagons and ambulances coming back from the front, and tired, muddy soldiers marching the other way, who looked at them, the new arrivals, with tired, kindly eyes.

[pause]

{img:new-soldiers-passing-tired-soldiers-dusk-road}
And at last, at dusk, they arrived at the villages behind the lines, where they would rest for a night or two, before going up into the trenches for the first time.

[pause]

They were a long way from home now.

[long-pause]

{img:trench-sandbags-duckboards-winter-morning}
A trench, in the winter of 1914, is a long, narrow ditch dug into the earth, about six or seven feet deep, and wide enough for two men to pass each other, if they squeeze.

[pause]

It does not run in a straight line. It zigzags, every few yards, back and forth, so that if a shell falls into one part of it, the walls of earth will protect the men in the next part.

[long-pause]

In Flanders, digging trenches is difficult.

[pause]

The ground is flat and low, and the water lies just beneath the surface. Dig down two or three feet, and the trench begins to fill with water. So the soldiers here often cannot dig very deep. Instead, they build up walls of sandbags above the ground, piled one on top of another, filled with the heavy, wet clay of the fields.

[pause]

The front wall of the trench, facing the enemy, is called the parapet. The back wall is called the parados. Along the bottom of the parapet, there is a little step, called the fire step, where the soldiers stand to look out over the top, or to keep watch.

[long-pause]

On the floor of the trench, the soldiers lay wooden boards, like little ladders laid flat, called duckboards, so that they can walk without sinking into the mud.

[pause]

But in the wet winter of 1914, the mud is everywhere. It is thick and sticky and grey and cold, and it gets into everything: into the men's boots and their clothes, their food and their blankets, their rifles and their hair. In some places, the water in the trenches comes up to the men's knees, and they stand in it for hours, and their feet grow numb with cold.

[pause]

{img:soldier-pumping-water-trench-wooden-pump}
They work all the time to keep the water out, with buckets and pumps, and to repair the walls when they fall in, which they often do, after heavy rain.

[long-pause]

In the walls of the trench, the soldiers dig little shelters, called dugouts.

[pause]

Some are just holes scooped out of the earth, big enough for a man to crawl into and curl up, out of the rain. Others are larger, with a roof of wooden planks and corrugated iron, covered with sandbags and earth, and a blanket hung over the doorway.

[pause]

Inside, there might be a little table made of a packing case, a candle stuck in a bottle, a few shelves for food and letters, and straw on the floor to sleep on. The officers' dugouts are a little more comfortable, with a bed of chicken wire stretched over a wooden frame, and perhaps a chair, and a little stove.

[long-pause]

Behind the front trench, there are other trenches.

[pause]

There is a support trench, a little way back, where more soldiers wait. And there are long, winding communication trenches, running back from the front, through which the soldiers walk to and from the trenches, carrying food and water and ammunition and post, and bringing back the wounded.

[pause]

{img:communication-trench-soldiers-carrying-supplies}
A few miles further back, there are the villages and farms where the soldiers go to rest, and the roads and railways that bring up the supplies.

[long-pause]

In front of the trenches, the soldiers have put up barbed wire.

[pause]

It is fixed to wooden posts, in long tangled rows, across the fields, to stop the enemy from coming across. Every night, men crawl out into the darkness to mend the wire, or to put up more of it, very quietly, while the sentries keep watch.

[pause]

{img:barbed-wire-posts-frosty-field-dawn}
Beyond the wire, there is No Man's Land: a strip of muddy fields, ditches, shell holes, broken fences, a ruined farmhouse, perhaps an old haystack or a few bare trees. And beyond that, a few hundred yards away, or sometimes much closer, there is another line of wire, and sandbags, and the German trenches.

[long-pause]

In many places, the trenches are so close that the soldiers can hear each other.

[pause]

They can hear the Germans talking and laughing and coughing in their trenches, and the clatter of their mess tins, and sometimes their singing. They can smell the smoke of their fires, and their cooking. In the mornings, they can see the smoke rising from the German side, as the Germans make their breakfast, just as they themselves are doing.

[pause]

Sometimes, they shout across to each other. A German soldier who used to work as a waiter in London might call out, asking how things are in the city. A British soldier might shout back a joke. And for a moment, the enemy seems not like an enemy at all, but like another young man, far from home, cold and tired and muddy, just like themselves.

[long-pause]

The soldiers do not spend all their time in the front trenches.

[pause]

Usually, a battalion stays in the front line for three or four days, and then it is relieved by another battalion, and goes back to the support trenches for a few days, and then further back, to the villages, to rest, for a few days more. Then it comes back to the front again.

[pause]

{img:soldiers-relief-night-walking-single-file}
The relief takes place at night, in the dark, so that the enemy will not see. The new men come up through the communication trenches, in single file, carrying everything they own on their backs, and the old men file out, tired and muddy, and walk back along the dark roads to the villages, where a hot meal, and a dry barn, and perhaps a bath, are waiting.

[long-pause]

{img:stand-to-dawn-soldiers-fire-step-mist}
A day in the trenches begins before dawn.

[pause]

About an hour before the sun comes up, the order comes down the line, in a whisper: stand to. All along the trench, the soldiers wake up, climb out of their dugouts, stiff and cold, and take their places on the fire step, with their rifles, looking out over the parapet into the grey darkness.

[long-pause]

This is the time when an attack is most likely to come, in the half-light before dawn. So everyone must be awake and ready.

[pause]

The men stand there in silence, in their greatcoats, with their breath steaming in the cold air, watching the mist slowly growing paler over No Man's Land, and the dark shapes of the trees and the farmhouses slowly appearing out of the darkness.

[pause]

On the other side, the German soldiers are doing exactly the same thing.

[pause]

Then, as the sun comes up, and nothing happens, the order comes to stand down. And the men climb down from the fire step, and think about breakfast.

[long-pause]

Breakfast is the best moment of the day.

[pause]

The men light little fires, in braziers made of old buckets with holes punched in them, or on little stoves, and boil water in their mess tins, and make tea. Tea is the most important thing in the British soldier's life. It is hot and sweet and strong, made with condensed milk from a tin, and it warms the men from the inside, and cheers them up.

[pause]

{img:soldiers-brewing-tea-mess-tins-brazier}
With the tea, there might be bacon, fried over the fire, if the bacon has come up with the rations, and bread, or hard army biscuits, and jam. The smell of frying bacon drifts along the trench, and the men gather round, with their hands held out to the fire.

[long-pause]

After breakfast, there is an inspection.

[pause]

The officers walk along the trench, looking at the men's rifles, to make sure they are clean, and their feet, to make sure they are not suffering from the cold and the wet. The men must rub their feet with whale oil every day, and change into dry socks if they have any, to protect their feet from the damp.

[pause]

Then the day's work begins.

[long-pause]

There is always work to be done in the trenches.

[pause]

The walls must be repaired, where they have fallen in. The water must be pumped out, or bailed out with buckets. New sandbags must be filled and laid. The duckboards must be mended. Food and water and ammunition must be carried up from the rear.

[pause]

But during the day, it is not safe to move around too much, or to show your head above the parapet. So much of the day is spent quietly, waiting.

[long-pause]

The soldiers pass the time as best they can.

[pause]

They write letters home, sitting in their dugouts, with a stub of pencil and a writing pad on their knees. They read the letters and the newspapers that come up with the post, again and again. They play cards, for cigarettes or pennies. They clean their rifles. They mend their clothes, and darn their socks.

[pause]

{img:soldier-writing-letter-dugout-pencil-candle}
They sleep, when they can, curled up in a corner of a dugout, or sitting on the fire step with their heads on their knees. They talk, about home and their families, about football and girls and food, and about when the war will end. They grumble about the mud, and the rain, and the food, and the officers, and the army, as soldiers always do.

[long-pause]

At midday, there is dinner.

[pause]

It comes up from the cookers behind the lines, in big metal containers, carried by men through the communication trenches: a stew of meat and vegetables, which the soldiers call Maconochie, after the company that makes it in tins, or a stew of corned beef, which they call bully beef. It is often cold by the time it arrives. But it is food, and the men are hungry.

[pause]

{img:soldiers-eating-stew-mess-tins-trench-noon}
In the afternoon, the light begins to fade early, because it is winter. By four o'clock, it is getting dark.

[long-pause]

At dusk, there is another stand-to, just like the one at dawn.

[pause]

Then night comes, and the trench comes alive.

[pause]

At night, the men can move about more freely, because the enemy cannot see them. Working parties go out to repair the wire and the trenches. Ration parties go back to fetch the food and the water. Patrols crawl out into No Man's Land, very quietly, to listen. The sentries stand on the fire step, two hours on and four hours off, watching the darkness.

[pause]

The men who are not on duty try to sleep, wrapped in their greatcoats and blankets, in their dugouts, while the cold creeps in, and the rain patters on the roof, and the rats scurry about in the dark.

[long-pause]

And so the days go by, one after another, much the same.

[pause]

{img:winter-sunset-flanders-trench-silhouettes}
Rain and mud and cold. Tea and bully beef and biscuits. Letters and cards and sleep. Stand-to at dawn and dusk. The flares rising and falling in the night. The sound of a distant gun.

[pause]

And every day, Christmas comes a little closer.

[long-pause]

{img:postman-sorting-letters-parcels-field-post}
The most important moment of the day, for many soldiers, is when the post arrives.

[pause]

Every day, letters and parcels from home come up to the trenches, carried by the ration parties along with the food and the water. A sergeant calls out the names, and the men crowd round, hoping to hear their own.

[long-pause]

The army post is wonderfully quick.

[pause]

A letter posted in a village in England on Monday can be in the hands of a soldier in the trenches in Flanders on Wednesday or Thursday. Every day, thousands of sacks of letters and parcels cross the English Channel by boat, and are carried by train and lorry and cart and on men's backs to the front line.

[pause]

The letters are full of news from home. The baby has a new tooth. The garden is looking bare now that winter is here. Your father has a cold. The vicar asked after you. Your sister has a new young man. The football team lost again on Saturday. Everybody sends their love. Come home safe.

[pause]

The soldiers read them again and again, until the paper is soft and creased, and keep them in their pockets, close to their hearts.

[long-pause]

The parcels are even better.

[pause]

Mothers and wives and sisters at home spend hours packing them, and sending them off to the front, wrapped in brown paper and string. Inside, there are socks and scarves and gloves and woollen balaclava helmets, knitted by hand. There is chocolate and toffee, fruit cake and biscuits and tins of sardines, cigarettes and tobacco and soap. Sometimes there is a little bottle of something to keep out the cold.

[pause]

{img:soldiers-opening-parcels-dugout-knitted-socks}
The soldiers share everything with their friends. A parcel is opened in a dugout, by the light of a candle, and the cake is cut into pieces, and handed round, and everyone eats it slowly, and thinks of home.

[long-pause]

All over Britain, people are knitting for the soldiers.

[pause]

Women and girls, and even some men and boys, knit in the evenings by the fire, and on trains, and in the parks, and at their work. Schools and churches and clubs organize knitting circles. Newspapers print knitting patterns for socks and mittens and mufflers. By Christmas, so many socks and scarves have been sent to the front that some soldiers have more than they know what to do with.

[pause]

In Germany, too, the women are knitting, and packing parcels, and sending them to the front, with sausages and cake and cigars, and little sprigs of fir for Christmas.

[long-pause]

The soldiers write letters too.

[pause]

They write home whenever they can, on little writing pads, with stubs of pencil, sitting in their dugouts or in the barns behind the lines. Their letters are read by an officer before they are sent, to make sure that they do not give away any secrets, so the men cannot say exactly where they are, or what they are doing.

[pause]

{img:officer-reading-censoring-letters-candle}
So they write about the weather, and the mud, and the food, and their friends. They thank their families for the parcels. They ask for more socks, or cigarettes, or candles. They tell them not to worry. They say that they are quite all right, really, and in good spirits.

[pause]

Many of them try to make their families laugh, with funny stories about army life, so that they will not be afraid for them.

[long-pause]

There is also a field postcard, a printed card with sentences on it, such as I am quite well, or I have received your letter, or Letter follows at first opportunity. The soldier crosses out the sentences that are not true, and signs his name. It is the quickest way to tell his family that he is safe.

[pause]

{img:field-postcard-pencil-mud-stained-hands}
For many families at home, the arrival of the postman is the most important and most anxious moment of the day.

[long-pause]

As December goes on, the post grows heavier and heavier.

[pause]

Christmas is coming, and everyone at home wants to send something to the soldiers at the front. Puddings and cakes, chocolate and sweets, cards and photographs, scarves and socks and little presents of every kind. The army post office is overwhelmed. Extra boats, extra trains, extra lorries, are needed, to carry it all.

[pause]

In the trenches, the soldiers wait for their Christmas parcels with growing excitement, and when they come, they share them out, and stack the puddings and the cakes in their dugouts, ready for the great day.

[long-pause]

And at home, in thousands of houses all over Britain and Germany and France, families sit by their fires in the long December evenings, looking at the empty chair by the fireside, and wondering what their sons and husbands and brothers will be doing on Christmas Day.

[pause]

{img:family-fireside-empty-chair-christmas-1914}
It will all be over by Christmas, they had said, in the summer. Now everyone knows that it will not. But they hope, still, that it will be over soon. And they pray that their soldiers will come home safe.

[long-pause]

{img:english-high-street-christmas-1914-shops-snow}
At home in Britain, in December 1914, people were preparing for Christmas, as they always did.

[pause]

The shops in the high streets were decorated with holly and ivy and paper chains. The windows of the toy shops were full of dolls and teddy bears, tin soldiers and toy trains and rocking horses. The butchers hung geese and turkeys outside their shops, and the grocers piled up oranges and nuts and dried fruit for the Christmas puddings.

[long-pause]

But this Christmas was different.

[pause]

In almost every street, in almost every village, there was a family with a son or a husband or a brother at the front. Many of the young men of the town were gone. The football matches on Saturday afternoons were smaller. The church choirs had fewer men's voices.

[pause]

In the kitchens, the mothers made the Christmas puddings, as they always did, mixing the flour and the suet and the dried fruit in a great bowl. Every member of the family took a turn to stir it, and make a wish. And this year, almost everyone made the same wish: that the war would end soon, and the soldiers would come home.

[long-pause]

Some of the puddings were wrapped up carefully and sent to the front, by post, so that the soldiers could have a taste of home on Christmas Day.

[pause]

The children made Christmas cards for their fathers and brothers, with pictures of holly and robins and snowmen, and wrote messages inside in their best handwriting. The wives and mothers wrote long letters, and knitted socks and scarves, and packed parcels.

[pause]

{img:child-drawing-christmas-card-for-father-table}
On Christmas Eve, the families put up their Christmas trees in the parlour, and decorated them with candles and ornaments and little paper flags. They hung up the children's stockings by the fireplace. And they put a photograph of the soldier on the mantelpiece, beside the Christmas cards, so that he would be with them, in a way.

[long-pause]

On Christmas morning, the children woke up early, and found their stockings filled with an orange, some nuts, a few sweets, a little toy, and perhaps a shiny new penny.

[pause]

The families went to church, and sang the Christmas carols, and prayed for the soldiers and sailors far away. The vicar read out the names of the young men of the parish who were serving at the front, and everyone bowed their heads.

[pause]

Then they went home to Christmas dinner: roast goose or chicken, with roast potatoes and vegetables, and the Christmas pudding, carried in with a sprig of holly on top, and brandy flaming around it.

[long-pause]

At the table, there was often an empty chair.

[pause]

And before they began to eat, the father of the family would stand up, and raise his glass, and say: To absent friends. And everyone would raise their glasses, and drink, and think of the soldier in the trenches, far away in Flanders, and wonder what he was doing.

[pause]

{img:family-christmas-dinner-raised-glasses-empty-chair}
None of them could have imagined that, at that very moment, he might be standing in a frozen field, in No Man's Land, shaking hands with a German soldier, and swapping a tin of bully beef for a sausage.

[long-pause]

In Germany, too, the families were celebrating Christmas.

[pause]

In Germany, Christmas is celebrated on Christmas Eve. The families decorate their Christmas trees in the afternoon, with candles and glass ornaments and straw stars and tinsel, and in the evening, when it grows dark, they light the candles, and open the door to the parlour, and the children come in, and see the tree glowing in the darkness, and the presents underneath it.

[pause]

That evening, in thousands of German homes, the families stood around their Christmas trees, and sang Stille Nacht, and thought of their fathers and sons and brothers at the front, singing the same carol, in their trenches, far away.

[long-pause]

On the other side of the world, in Britain and Germany and France and Belgium, and in Canada and India and Australia and New Zealand, families sat by their fires that Christmas night, and looked at the photographs on their mantelpieces.

[pause]

{img:mother-by-fire-holding-photograph-christmas-night}
And many mothers, when the children had gone to bed, and the house was quiet, sat alone for a while by the fading fire, with a photograph in their hands, and said a quiet prayer.

[long-pause]

{img:cartoonist-officer-sketching-dugout-candle}
In a trench near the little village of Saint-Yvon, at the edge of Plugstreet Wood, there is a young officer named Bruce Bairnsfather.

[pause]

He is twenty-seven years old, a lieutenant in the Royal Warwickshire Regiment, in charge of a machine gun team. He has a round, cheerful face, a small moustache, and a great sense of humour. Before the war, he was an engineer, installing electric lighting in country houses and hotels. But what he really loves is drawing.

[long-pause]

In his dugout, by the light of a candle, Bairnsfather draws cartoons.

[pause]

He draws the soldiers around him, in their muddy greatcoats and their woollen balaclavas, sitting in the rain, brewing their tea, grumbling about the food, sleeping in the mud. He draws them with great affection, and great humour, and they love his pictures, and pass them from hand to hand along the trench.

[pause]

Later, he will invent a famous character, an old soldier with a walrus moustache and a pipe, called Old Bill. In his most famous cartoon, Old Bill is sitting in a shell hole with another soldier, with shells bursting all around them, and the other soldier is grumbling, and Old Bill says: Well, if you knows of a better 'ole, go to it.

[pause]

Millions of people in Britain will laugh at that cartoon, and soldiers will repeat it to each other for years.

[long-pause]

Bairnsfather's men like him. He is kind and cheerful, and he shares their hardships, and makes them laugh.

[pause]

His dugout is a small, damp hole in the side of the trench, with a roof of planks and sandbags, so low that he cannot stand up in it. Water drips through the roof when it rains. He sleeps on a bed of straw and sandbags, with his feet in the cold. But he writes home that he is quite comfortable, really, and that the men are wonderful.

[pause]

{img:low-damp-dugout-straw-bed-dripping-roof}
At Christmas, he will be one of the men who walk out into No Man's Land to meet the Germans. And he will write about it, and draw it, so that people at home will know what happened.

[long-pause]

A few miles to the south, near the town of Armentières, there is a regiment called the Royal Welch Fusiliers.

[pause]

Among its soldiers is a private named Frank Richards. He is about thirty, a former coal miner from South Wales, a tough, quiet, practical man, who has served in the army before, in India and Burma, and was called back from the reserves when the war began.

[pause]

{img:welsh-soldier-pipe-trench-calm-face}
Frank is an old soldier. He knows how to look after himself, how to keep warm and dry, how to find a little extra food, and how to keep out of trouble with the sergeants. He is not interested in glory, or in speeches. He just wants to do his job, and look after his mates, and get home in one piece.

[pause]

Many years later, he will write a book about his war, called Old Soldiers Never Die, which tells the story of the trenches, honestly and simply, from the point of view of an ordinary soldier.

[long-pause]

The Welsh soldiers love to sing.

[pause]

In the evenings, in their trenches, they sing the old hymns and songs of Wales, in their beautiful, deep, rich voices, in harmony, as they sang them in the chapels and the pubs of their villages at home. Their singing drifts across No Man's Land in the darkness, and the Germans on the other side stop what they are doing, and listen.

[pause]

{img:welsh-soldiers-singing-trench-night-lantern}
Sometimes, the Germans sing back.

[long-pause]

Along the line, in the trenches near Plugstreet Wood, Henry Williamson and the young men of the London Rifle Brigade are settling into life in the trenches.

[pause]

They are city boys, clerks and office workers and shop assistants, and the trenches are a shock to them. Henry has never been so cold or so wet or so tired in his life. His feet are always freezing. His uniform is caked with mud. He is often frightened.

[pause]

But he is also fascinated by everything he sees: the strange, ruined landscape, the flares in the night sky, the birds that still sing in the bare trees of Plugstreet Wood, the robins and the wrens that hop along the parapet looking for crumbs. He notices everything, and he writes it all down, in his letters to his mother, and in a little diary.

[long-pause]

Like all the soldiers, these three men, Bairnsfather and Richards and Williamson, have been told that the Germans are cruel and wicked, the enemy of everything that is good.

[pause]

And like all the soldiers, they are beginning, slowly, to wonder.

[pause]

{img:sentry-looking-across-no-mans-land-dusk}
Because from their trenches, looking across No Man's Land, they can see the smoke of the German fires rising in the morning, just like their own, and hear the Germans coughing in the cold, and sometimes laughing, and sometimes singing. And the Germans, it seems, are just as cold and wet and tired and muddy as they are.

[long-pause]

{img:german-trench-soldiers-pickelhaube-cloth-covers}
On the other side of No Man's Land, a few hundred yards away, the German soldiers are living in their own trenches, in the same mud and the same cold.

[pause]

They wear grey-green uniforms, and tall leather helmets with a spike on top, covered with grey cloth, which they call the Pickelhaube. Many of them have long moustaches, and some have beards. They drink coffee instead of tea, and eat black bread and sausage instead of biscuits and bully beef. But otherwise, their lives are very much the same.

[long-pause]

Many of the German soldiers along this part of the line come from Saxony and Bavaria and Württemberg, in the south and east of Germany.

[pause]

They are farmers and craftsmen, students and teachers, factory workers and miners. They come from little villages among forests and hills, and from old towns with half-timbered houses and church spires, and from big cities like Leipzig and Dresden and Munich.

[pause]

Many of them have been to England, or have relatives there, or have worked there, as waiters or barbers or clerks or musicians. Some of them speak good English. Some of them say that they have no quarrel with the English, and that they would much rather be fighting the Russians.

[long-pause]

Among them, in a Saxon regiment, near the village of Saint-Yvon, facing Bairnsfather's men across the fields, there is a young officer named Kurt Zehmisch.

[pause]

He is a schoolteacher from a little town in Saxony, about twenty-five years old, an educated, thoughtful young man, who speaks English and French. He keeps a diary, in which he writes, every day, about life in the trenches.

[pause]

{img:young-german-officer-diary-dugout-lamp}
He writes about the rain, and the mud, and the cold. He writes about his men, whom he cares for, and about the food, and the post from home. He writes about the English soldiers on the other side, whom he can see and hear, and about how strange it is to be so close to them, in such a quiet, ordinary way.

[long-pause]

The German trenches are, in some places, a little better built than the British ones.

[pause]

The Germans have been here a little longer, and they have had time to dig deeper and to build stronger dugouts, with wooden walls and floors, and even stoves and beds. In some places, they have chosen the higher ground, which is a little drier.

[pause]

But in the wet clay of Flanders, everyone suffers from the water and the mud.

[long-pause]

The German soldiers pass their time just as the British do.

[pause]

They write letters home, and read the letters and the newspapers that come from Germany. They play cards and chess. They smoke their pipes and their cigars. They clean their rifles and mend their clothes. And they sing.

[pause]

{img:german-soldiers-singing-around-candle-trench}
The Germans love to sing, perhaps even more than the Welsh. They sing old folk songs, and soldiers' songs, and hymns, and songs about their homes and their sweethearts, in four-part harmony, beautifully, in the evenings, in their trenches. And their singing drifts across No Man's Land to the British trenches on the other side.

[long-pause]

In the first weeks of December, some strange things begin to happen along the line.

[pause]

In some places, the soldiers on both sides have stopped shooting at each other at mealtimes. Without anyone giving an order, both sides seem to have agreed that breakfast time, and dinner time, should be quiet, so that everyone can eat in peace.

[pause]

In other places, they have stopped shooting at the men who carry the food and water up to the trenches, or at the men who go out at night to mend the wire. It is called live and let live. Nobody arranges it. It just happens.

[long-pause]

Sometimes, the soldiers shout to each other across No Man's Land.

[pause]

A German shouts across: Good morning, Tommy! And a British soldier shouts back: Good morning, Fritz! Sometimes they ask each other questions: how the weather is in London, what the food is like, when the war will end. Sometimes they make jokes.

[pause]

{img:soldiers-shouting-across-fields-hands-cupped}
Once, in one part of the line, the Germans hold up a blackboard, with a message chalked on it, asking for news. In another, they throw over a tin of cigars, wrapped in a newspaper, and the British throw back a tin of jam.

[long-pause]

On the seventh of December, far away in Rome, the Pope, Benedict the Fifteenth, makes an appeal to all the countries at war.

[pause]

He asks them to stop fighting, at least for Christmas. He asks that the guns should be silent, at least on the night when the angels sang. The governments of the warring countries do not agree. The war must go on, they say.

[pause]

But in the trenches, in the cold and the mud of Flanders, the soldiers on both sides are beginning to think the same thing.

[long-pause]

In Germany, the government has decided that every soldier at the front should have a Christmas present.

[pause]

Hundreds of thousands of little Christmas trees, with candles to put on them, are being sent by train to the front, so that the soldiers can have a proper German Christmas, in their trenches. There are gifts from the Kaiser too, pipes and cigars, and from the people of Germany, parcels of food and sweets and warm clothes.

[pause]

{img:christmas-trees-loaded-train-wagons-snow}
In Britain, too, presents are on their way. Every soldier and sailor is to receive a little brass box, a gift from the King's daughter, Princess Mary, filled with tobacco and cigarettes, or chocolate and sweets for those who do not smoke, and a Christmas card, and a photograph of the princess.

[pause]

The soldiers on both sides are looking forward to Christmas.

[long-pause]

{img:soldiers-walking-back-village-road-evening}
When the soldiers come out of the trenches, after three or four days, they walk back along the dark country roads to the villages behind the lines.

[pause]

They are tired and muddy and wet, and their feet are aching. But they are happy. For a few days, they will be able to rest, and sleep, and eat a hot meal, and be dry.

[long-pause]

The villages and farms of Flanders are full of soldiers.

[pause]

They sleep in the barns and the haylofts of the farms, in the empty rooms of the houses, in the schools and the breweries and the town halls. In the barns, they sleep on straw, rolled up in their blankets, in long rows, with their rifles beside them. The barns are cold and draughty, and full of rats. But after the trenches, they seem like palaces.

[pause]

In the mornings, the soldiers wash in the farmyard, at the pump, with cold water, stripped to the waist, laughing and splashing each other. Then they shave, and clean their uniforms, scraping off the mud, and polish their buttons and their boots.

[long-pause]

The Flemish and French families who still live in the villages are kind to the soldiers.

[pause]

The farmers' wives sell them coffee and eggs, bread and butter and fried potatoes, in their warm kitchens. In the evenings, the soldiers sit around the big kitchen tables, by the stove, drinking coffee or thin beer, playing cards, and talking with the farmer and his family, in a strange mixture of English and French and Flemish, with a great deal of pointing and laughing.

[pause]

{img:farm-kitchen-soldiers-family-stove-evening}
The children love the soldiers. They follow them around the village, and the soldiers give them chocolate and biscuits from their parcels, and teach them English songs.

[long-pause]

In the villages, there are little cafés, which the soldiers call estaminets.

[pause]

They are small, cosy rooms, with a few wooden tables and chairs, a counter, a stove, and perhaps an old piano in the corner. They are run by the women of the village, who serve the soldiers coffee and beer and wine, and plates of egg and chips, which the soldiers call oeufs and frites, and which they love more than anything.

[pause]

In the evenings, the estaminets are crowded with soldiers, laughing and singing and playing cards, and someone plays the piano, and everyone sings the songs of the music halls of home. Keep the home fires burning. It's a long way to Tipperary.

[long-pause]

One of the greatest pleasures of the rest days is a bath.

[pause]

In some of the villages, the army has set up baths in old breweries, where there are great wooden vats, which can be filled with hot water. The soldiers come in, in groups of twenty or thirty, strip off their dirty, muddy clothes, and climb into the vats, and soak in the hot water, sighing with happiness, for the first time in weeks.

[pause]

While they are in the water, their uniforms are taken away to be cleaned, and their underclothes are exchanged for clean ones. They come out pink and clean and warm, feeling like new men.

[long-pause]

Not far from the front, there is the town of Armentières.

[pause]

It is a busy little town, with shops and cafés, a church and a square. The shops are still open, and the soldiers can buy chocolate and postcards and souvenirs, and silk handkerchiefs embroidered with flowers, to send home to their mothers and sweethearts. There is even a theatre, where the soldiers put on concerts and shows.

[pause]

{img:armentieres-town-square-shops-soldiers-1914}
The soldiers make up a song about the town, about a girl who works in an estaminet there, the Mademoiselle from Armentières. Everyone sings it, and it becomes one of the most famous songs of the war.

[long-pause]

On Sundays, there are church services, in the village churches, or in the open air, in a field, with the chaplain standing on a cart, and the soldiers standing in rows in front of him, singing the old hymns.

[pause]

The chaplains are much loved by the soldiers. They visit the trenches, and talk to the men, and bring them cigarettes and newspapers and comfort. Some of them help to write letters for those who cannot write.

[long-pause]

And then, after a few days, the rest is over.

[pause]

The soldiers pack up their kit, and put on their greatcoats and their packs, and march back along the dark roads toward the trenches, in long columns, singing to keep their spirits up.

[pause]

{img:column-marching-night-road-poplars-singing}
The farmers' wives and children stand at the doors of the farmhouses and wave goodbye. And the soldiers march on, through the darkness, under the bare poplar trees, back to the mud and the cold and the rain, and to the enemy on the other side, who, at this time of year, is thinking about Christmas just as much as they are.

[long-pause]

{img:plugstreet-wood-bare-trees-paths-winter}
Just behind the British lines, near the Belgian village of Ploegsteert, there is a wood.

[pause]

The soldiers call it Plugstreet Wood, because they cannot pronounce its Flemish name. It is a large, old wood, of oak and ash and hazel, with paths and rides running through it, and little streams, and clearings, where, before the war, the people of the village came to gather firewood, and to pick mushrooms and berries, and the children played.

[long-pause]

In the winter of 1914, Plugstreet Wood has become a strange, quiet place, full of soldiers.

[pause]

The trees are bare, and the paths are deep in mud. But the British soldiers have made it their home. They have built huts and shelters among the trees, out of logs and branches and planks and corrugated iron, and given the paths names, like the streets of London: Regent Street, Oxford Street, Piccadilly, Hyde Park Corner.

[pause]

There are cookhouses and first aid posts, stores and workshops, all hidden among the trees. Along the paths, the soldiers have laid duckboards over the mud, and the men walk along them in single file, carrying rations and water and ammunition up to the trenches at the edge of the wood.

[long-pause]

The wood is surprisingly peaceful.

[pause]

Because it is so close to the front, the woodland animals have mostly been left alone. There are rabbits and hares, foxes, and many birds. In the mornings, the robins and the blackbirds sing in the bare trees, and the wrens creep among the brambles. Pheasants strut along the rides. Owls call at night.

[pause]

{img:robin-on-trench-parapet-frosty-morning}
Henry Williamson loves the wood. Even in the cold and the mud, he notices the birds, and the frost on the branches, and the way the light comes through the trees in the morning, and he writes about them in his letters home.

[long-pause]

In the village of Ploegsteert, a little way behind the wood, some of the villagers are still living in their houses.

[pause]

There is a church, with a tall spire, and a little square, and a few cafés, and a convent of nuns, who run a school and look after the sick. The nuns have stayed in the village, despite the war, and they help the soldiers too, giving them coffee and bread, and washing their clothes, and caring for the wounded.

[pause]

The soldiers are very fond of the nuns, and of the village children, who follow them everywhere.

[long-pause]

At the edge of the wood, the trenches run along the fields, facing the German lines across No Man's Land.

[pause]

In some places, the trenches are only fifty or sixty yards apart. From the British trench, a soldier who peeps carefully over the parapet can see the German sandbags, the German wire, and sometimes a German helmet, moving along the top of their trench.

[pause]

{img:frosty-field-two-trench-lines-close-wood-edge}
Just in front of the British trenches, at one place, there is a little ruined farmhouse, and beyond it, the fields slope gently down toward a stream. It is very quiet. Only the occasional crack of a rifle, or the rattle of a machine gun, breaks the silence.

[long-pause]

Bruce Bairnsfather is here, with his machine gun team, at the edge of the wood near Saint-Yvon.

[pause]

He has made his dugout as comfortable as he can. He has a little shelf for his drawing paper and his pencils, and a candle in a bottle, and a few books, and a photograph of his family. In the evenings, he sits there, drawing his cartoons, while the rain drips through the roof.

[pause]

{img:candlelit-dugout-drawings-pinned-wall}
His drawings are already becoming popular. The men pass them around the trenches and the billets, and laugh at them, and recognize themselves in them. Soon, he will send some of them to a magazine in London, and they will be printed, and all of Britain will laugh at them too.

[long-pause]

One of Bairnsfather's favourite subjects is the cold.

[pause]

He draws soldiers huddled in their greatcoats, with their balaclavas pulled down over their ears, and their breath frozen in clouds, standing in water up to their knees. He draws them trying to light a fire in the rain, and trying to sleep in a dugout full of water, and trying to eat their biscuits, which are so hard that they could break a tooth.

[pause]

He draws them with love. He knows how they feel, because he feels the same.

[long-pause]

In the middle of December, the weather grows worse.

[pause]

It rains, and rains, and rains. The ditches overflow, and the trenches fill with water. The mud grows deeper and deeper. In some places, the men cannot walk along the trenches at all, and must climb out and walk along the top, at night, in the dark. The walls of the trenches collapse. The dugouts flood. Everyone is soaked, and cold, and miserable.

[pause]

{img:flooded-trench-rain-soldiers-bailing-buckets}
Along the line, there are a few small attacks, in the mud, which achieve nothing.

[pause]

And everyone, on both sides, is thinking the same thing. If only it would stop raining. If only it would freeze. If only it were Christmas.

[long-pause]

{img:frost-morning-fields-white-trees-clear-sky}
A few days before Christmas, the weather changes.

[pause]

The rain stops. The clouds clear away. And on the night of the twenty-third of December, the temperature falls below freezing, and a hard white frost settles over the land.

[long-pause]

In the morning, the soldiers wake up to a different world.

[pause]

The mud has frozen hard. The water in the trenches is covered with a thin layer of ice. Every blade of grass, every twig, every strand of barbed wire, is covered with white frost, glittering in the pale winter sunshine. The air is clear and cold and still, and the sky is a pale, clean blue.

[pause]

The soldiers, who have been wet for weeks, are delighted. The frost is cold, but it is dry. They can walk along the trenches without sinking into the mud. They can dry their clothes. They stamp their feet, and blow on their hands, and laugh.

[long-pause]

On the twenty-third and the twenty-fourth of December, the Christmas presents arrive.

[pause]

On the British side, the Princess Mary boxes are handed out: small, shiny brass boxes, with a picture of the princess on the lid, surrounded by a wreath, and the words Christmas 1914. Inside, there is tobacco and a pipe and cigarettes, or chocolate and sweets, and a little Christmas card, and a picture of the princess.

[pause]

{img:brass-gift-tin-princess-christmas-1914-hands}
There is also a Christmas card from the King and Queen, with a message: With our best wishes for Christmas 1914. May God protect you and bring you home safe.

[pause]

The soldiers are very touched. Many of them will keep their brass boxes for the rest of their lives, and pass them down to their children and grandchildren.

[long-pause]

There are parcels from home too, piles of them. Christmas puddings and cakes, chocolate and sweets, socks and scarves and gloves. And the newspapers in London have organized a great collection of gifts for the soldiers, so there are more puddings and more chocolate and more cigarettes, from people the soldiers have never met.

[pause]

In some places, there is even plum pudding for every man in the battalion.

[long-pause]

On the German side, the Christmas trees arrive.

[pause]

They are little fir trees, a few feet tall, with small candles to fix to their branches. The soldiers set them up in their dugouts, and decorate them with whatever they can find: candles, paper stars, cotton wool for snow, little ornaments sent from home.

[pause]

There are gifts from home, and from the Kaiser: a pipe for each man, and cigars, and in some regiments, beer and sausage and schnapps. The German soldiers write home that they are going to have a proper Christmas, even in the trenches.

[long-pause]

On the morning of Christmas Eve, the soldiers on both sides can see each other more clearly than for weeks.

[pause]

The frost has made everything sharp and clear. Across No Man's Land, the British soldiers can see the German sandbags, and the German wire, and the smoke rising from the German fires. And the Germans can see the British.

[pause]

{img:clear-frosty-no-mans-land-both-lines-visible}
There is very little shooting that day. In many places along the line, there is none at all. The soldiers go about their work quietly, repairing the trenches, cooking their food, opening their parcels, and getting ready for Christmas.

[long-pause]

In some places, the soldiers begin to call out to each other.

[pause]

Merry Christmas, Tommy! the Germans shout. And the British shout back: Merry Christmas, Fritz! In one place, a German soldier holds up a board with the words written on it in English: You no fight, we no fight. In another, the soldiers agree, by shouting across, not to shoot at each other the next day.

[pause]

Nobody has ordered any of this. Nobody has arranged it. It simply begins to happen, here and there, along the line, in the clear, cold air of Christmas Eve.

[long-pause]

As the afternoon goes on, the sun sinks low in the sky, red and golden, behind the poplar trees, and the frost grows sharper.

[pause]

The sky turns pink, and then deep blue, and then the first stars come out, very bright, over the frozen fields. The moon rises, and lights up the whole frozen landscape in silver.

[pause]

{img:moonrise-frozen-flanders-fields-poplars}
It is very still. The guns are silent. The air is so clear and cold that sounds carry a long way: a cough, a laugh, the clink of a mess tin, the crunch of boots on frozen ground.

[pause]

And on both sides of No Man's Land, the soldiers settle down in their trenches, wrapped in their greatcoats, with their parcels and their presents, and wait for Christmas.

[long-pause]

{img:flemish-village-christmas-eve-church-lights-frost}
Behind the lines, in the villages and farms of Flanders, Christmas Eve came quietly.

[pause]

In the little village of Ploegsteert, and in the other villages around, the Flemish families who had stayed in their homes were getting ready for Christmas, as they always had, as best they could, in the middle of the war.

[long-pause]

In Flanders, Christmas is a quiet, religious feast.

[pause]

The great day for presents is not Christmas, but the feast of Saint Nicholas, on the sixth of December, when the children put out their shoes in the evening, and wake up to find them filled with sweets and oranges and little toys. That year, Saint Nicholas had come to the children of Flanders as usual, though there was not much to put in their shoes.

[pause]

On Christmas Eve, the families decorate their houses with greenery, and set up a little crib, with figures of Mary and Joseph and the baby Jesus, and the shepherds and the animals, on a table in the corner of the room, with a candle in front of it.

[long-pause]

In the farmhouse kitchens, the farmers' wives baked Christmas bread, a special sweet loaf, with raisins, shaped like a baby wrapped in swaddling clothes, which the Flemish call a kerstbrood, and the French-speaking villagers a cougnou.

[pause]

They gave some of it to the soldiers who were lodging in their barns, and the soldiers were very touched. Many of them had not tasted fresh, sweet bread for weeks.

[pause]

{img:farmers-wife-giving-christmas-bread-soldiers-kitchen}
In some farmhouses, the soldiers and the family sat together that evening, around the big kitchen table, by the stove, sharing the bread, and coffee, and a little of the chocolate and the cake from the soldiers' parcels, and singing carols, in English and in Flemish, to each other.

[long-pause]

At the convent in Ploegsteert, the nuns were preparing for Christmas too.

[pause]

They decorated their little chapel with greenery and candles, and set up a crib, with the figures that they had kept for many years. They made little presents for the village children, and for the wounded soldiers in their care.

[pause]

In the evening, they invited some of the soldiers who were resting in the village to come to the chapel, and sing carols with them. The soldiers came, in their muddy boots and their greatcoats, and stood at the back of the little chapel, and sang, with their caps in their hands.

[long-pause]

At midnight, in some of the village churches behind the lines, there was a Christmas Mass.

[pause]

The churches were cold, and some of them were damaged, with broken windows and holes in the roofs. But the villagers came, wrapped in their coats and shawls, carrying candles and lanterns along the frozen lanes. And some of the soldiers came too: the Catholic soldiers, the Irish and the Scottish and the English, and some who were not Catholic, but who wanted to be in a church on Christmas Eve.

[pause]

{img:midnight-mass-damaged-church-candles-villagers-soldiers}
They knelt together on the cold stone floor, the Flemish farmers and their wives and children, and the soldiers from far away, while the old priest said the Mass in Latin, and the candles flickered, and the frost glittered on the broken windows. And at the end, they sang the old Christmas hymns together, in Latin, which everyone knew.

[long-pause]

After the Mass, the villagers walked home through the frozen lanes, under the stars, with their lanterns.

[pause]

The air was very still and very cold. The frost crunched under their boots. From far away, toward the front, they could hear something strange: not the sound of guns, but the faint sound of singing, drifting across the fields from the trenches.

[pause]

They stopped, and listened, in wonder. Then they went home, and climbed into their beds, under their heavy quilts, and blew out their candles.

[long-pause]

In the barns, the soldiers who were resting settled down to sleep in the straw, wrapped in their blankets, with their Christmas presents beside them.

[pause]

The horses shifted and stamped in their stalls. An owl called from the orchard. The frost settled on the roofs. And over the flat, frozen land of Flanders, the stars shone down on the farmhouses, and the villages, and the church towers, and the long line of the trenches, where, for one night, the guns were silent.

[pause]

{img:barn-soldiers-sleeping-straw-moonlight-window}
It was, for many of them, the quietest night they had known since the war began.

[long-pause]

{img:german-parapet-row-of-lit-christmas-trees-night}
On Christmas Eve, as darkness falls over the frozen fields of Flanders, the British soldiers in the trenches see something strange.

[pause]

All along the German line, little lights are appearing.

[long-pause]

At first, the British sentries think it might be some kind of trick, or a signal for an attack. They call their officers, and everyone peers anxiously over the parapet into the darkness.

[pause]

Then they see what the lights are.

[pause]

The German soldiers have lifted their little Christmas trees up onto the top of their trenches, onto the parapet, and lit the candles on them. All along the German line, as far as the eye can see, there are little Christmas trees, glowing with candles, in the frosty darkness.

[pause]

One British soldier will write home that it looked like the footlights of a theatre. Another will say that it was the most beautiful thing he had ever seen.

[long-pause]

Then, from the German trenches, the soldiers begin to sing.

[pause]

They sing the old German Christmas carols, softly at first, and then louder, in beautiful harmony, the deep voices of the men rising and falling in the still, cold air, drifting across No Man's Land.

[pause]

And then they sing the most famous carol of all, the carol that every German child knows, written nearly a hundred years before, in a little church in the Austrian Alps.

[pause]

Stille Nacht, heilige Nacht. Silent night, holy night.

[long-pause]

{img:british-soldiers-listening-in-trench-moonlight}
The British soldiers stand in their trenches, in the frost and the moonlight, and listen.

[pause]

Some of them have tears in their eyes. They know the tune. They have sung it themselves, in English, in their churches at home, at Christmas, with their families. And they think of home, and their mothers and fathers, and their wives and children, and the Christmas trees and the candles and the fires in their own houses, far away across the sea.

[long-pause]

When the Germans finish singing, there is a moment of silence.

[pause]

Then, from the British trenches, the soldiers begin to clap, and to cheer, and to shout: Good old Fritz! More! More!

[pause]

And then the British sing back. They sing The First Noel, and O Come All Ye Faithful, and While Shepherds Watched Their Flocks by Night. Some of the Welsh regiments sing their own beautiful hymns, in Welsh, in harmony.

[pause]

And when the British sing O Come All Ye Faithful, the Germans join in, singing the same tune, in Latin, Adeste Fideles, so that for a few minutes, the soldiers on both sides of No Man's Land are singing the same carol, together, under the stars.

[long-pause]

All along the line, in different places, the same kind of thing is happening.

[pause]

In some places, the soldiers shout across to each other, wishing each other a merry Christmas, and making jokes. In others, they agree not to shoot the next day. In others, a few brave men climb out of their trenches, in the darkness, and walk out into No Man's Land, and meet in the middle, and shake hands, and exchange cigarettes, and talk for a while, in a mixture of English and German, and then go back to their trenches.

[pause]

{img:two-soldiers-shaking-hands-moonlit-field}
Near Plugstreet Wood, Henry Williamson stands in his trench, listening to the singing, and watching the little lights on the German parapet, in wonder.

[long-pause]

In one part of the line, there is a famous German opera singer, a tenor named Walter Kirchhoff, who is serving as an officer.

[pause]

On Christmas Eve, he sings carols for the soldiers, in his beautiful trained voice, in the frosty night. On the other side, the French soldiers, who are facing his regiment, listen in amazement, and when he finishes, they climb up onto their parapet, and applaud him, and call for more.

[pause]

{img:singer-standing-trench-starlit-night-listeners}
Afterwards, it is said, Kirchhoff walks across No Man's Land to the French trenches, and bows to them, and is given a little present.

[long-pause]

In the British trenches, the soldiers open their Christmas parcels, and share their puddings and their cakes, by the light of candles in their dugouts.

[pause]

They sing songs, and tell stories, and talk about home, late into the night. Some of them write letters, describing what they have seen and heard, to their families. They do not know if their families will believe them.

[pause]

In the German trenches, too, the soldiers celebrate. They light the candles on their little trees, and open their parcels, and drink a little beer or schnapps, and sing, and think of their families at home.

[long-pause]

Late in the night, the singing slowly dies away.

[pause]

The candles on the little trees burn down, one by one, and go out. The frost grows harder. The stars shine bright over the frozen fields, and the moon sinks slowly in the west.

[pause]

{img:last-candles-flickering-parapet-frost-quiet}
In the trenches, the soldiers on both sides settle down to sleep, wrapped in their greatcoats and their blankets, in their dugouts, while the sentries keep watch. But the sentries do not see anything. There is no shooting. There are no flares.

[pause]

For the first time in months, along much of the line, there is a silent night.

[long-pause]

{img:christmas-dawn-frost-mist-no-mans-land-gold}
Christmas morning, 1914, dawns cold and clear and beautiful over Flanders.

[pause]

The frost lies white over everything. A thin mist hangs over the fields, and as the sun comes up, it turns pink and gold. The air is perfectly still. There is no sound of guns.

[long-pause]

In the trenches, the soldiers stand to at dawn, as always, and look out over the parapet into the mist.

[pause]

And then, here and there along the line, someone sees something that seems impossible. On the other side of No Man's Land, a German soldier has climbed up out of his trench, and is standing on top of the parapet, in full view, without his rifle, waving.

[pause]

In some places, the Germans hold up signs, written in English: Merry Christmas. In others, they shout across: Come out, Tommy! We no shoot, you no shoot!

[long-pause]

At first, the British soldiers do not know what to do.

[pause]

They have been told that the Germans cannot be trusted. Their officers are uncertain. But the Germans are standing there, unarmed, in the open, smiling and waving. And it is Christmas.

[pause]

{img:british-soldier-climbing-out-trench-hesitant}
So, one by one, a few brave British soldiers climb up out of their trenches too, and stand on top, and wave back. Nobody shoots. More of them climb out. And then, slowly, carefully, the soldiers on both sides begin to walk out into No Man's Land, toward each other.

[long-pause]

They meet in the middle, among the frozen shell holes and the frosted grass.

[pause]

At first, they are shy and awkward. They stand and look at each other. Then someone holds out a hand, and the other takes it. And soon, all along that part of the line, the soldiers are shaking hands, and wishing each other a happy Christmas, in English and in German, and laughing.

[pause]

Henry Williamson, near Plugstreet Wood, walks out into No Man's Land with the other young men of the London Rifle Brigade, and meets the Germans. Afterwards, he writes to his mother that the British and the Germans have met and shaken hands in the ground between the trenches, and exchanged souvenirs, all through Christmas Day. And he adds: Marvellous, isn't it?

[long-pause]

The soldiers exchange presents.

[pause]

The British give the Germans cigarettes and tobacco, chocolate and plum pudding and tins of bully beef and jam. The Germans give the British cigars and sausages, sauerkraut and black bread, and bottles of beer and schnapps. They swap buttons from their uniforms, and badges, and caps, as souvenirs. They show each other photographs of their wives and children and sweethearts, and talk about their families.

[pause]

{img:soldiers-exchanging-photos-cigars-frost}
Many of the Germans speak a little English, and some of them have lived in England. One of them, it turns out, used to be a barber in London, and he sets up a chair in No Man's Land, and gives haircuts to the British soldiers, for free.

[long-pause]

Bruce Bairnsfather walks out into No Man's Land too, near Saint-Yvon, and meets the Saxon soldiers there.

[pause]

He talks with a German officer, and they get on well. They want to exchange souvenirs, and Bairnsfather has nothing to give. So he takes out his wire cutters, and cuts two buttons off the German officer's coat, and puts them in his pocket, and gives the German two of his own buttons in return.

[pause]

Later, he will write about that morning, and draw pictures of it, and say that there was not an atom of hate on either side that day.

[long-pause]

Across the field, the young German officer Kurt Zehmisch is talking with the British soldiers too, in his good English.

[pause]

That evening, he will write in his diary: How fantastically wonderful and strange it was. The English brought a football from their trenches, and soon a lively game began. How wonderful and strange it is.

[pause]

{img:german-officer-talking-english-soldiers-smiling}
Further south, near Armentières, Frank Richards and the Royal Welch Fusiliers are meeting the Germans too. The Germans roll two barrels of beer across No Man's Land as a Christmas present. Frank tastes the beer, and says, honestly, that it is rather weak. But everyone drinks it anyway, and wishes each other a merry Christmas.

[long-pause]

There is also a sad and gentle task to be done that morning.

[pause]

In No Man's Land, there are soldiers who fell in the fighting of the past weeks, and who could not be brought back. So, during the truce, the soldiers on both sides help each other to bring them in, and to lay them to rest, with dignity.

[pause]

Near the village of Fleurbaix, a Scottish chaplain and a German divinity student hold a service together, in the open field, with British and German soldiers standing side by side, with their caps in their hands. The chaplain reads the Twenty-third Psalm, The Lord is my shepherd, in English, and the German reads it in German. And the soldiers of both armies bow their heads together.

[long-pause]

All morning, the soldiers wander about No Man's Land, talking and laughing and exchanging gifts, in the bright, frosty sunshine.

[pause]

{img:many-soldiers-mingling-no-mans-land-sunshine}
Some of them take photographs, with little cameras that they have brought from home, against the rules. In the pictures, which still exist today, the British and German soldiers stand together in the field, in their greatcoats and caps, with their hands in their pockets, smiling at the camera, like old friends at a football match.

[long-pause]

{img:football-kicked-frosty-field-soldiers-greatcoats}
In some places along the line, on that Christmas Day, the soldiers played football.

[pause]

It is one of the most famous parts of the story of the Christmas truce. Nobody knows exactly how many games there were, or where, or who won. There are only a few letters and diaries that mention them. But there are enough to be sure that, here and there, in the frozen fields of No Man's Land, British and German soldiers kicked a ball about together on Christmas Day, 1914.

[long-pause]

It was not a proper match, most of the time.

[pause]

There were no teams in shirts, no goalposts, no referee. Someone produced a football, perhaps one that had been sent from home in a parcel. Or, if there was no ball, they used a tin can, or a sandbag stuffed with straw, or a cap rolled into a ball.

[pause]

The soldiers put their caps on the ground to mark the goals. And then, in their heavy boots and their greatcoats, on the hard, frozen, bumpy ground, among the shell holes, they kicked the ball about, back and forth, laughing and shouting and slipping and falling over, while their friends stood around and cheered.

[long-pause]

Kurt Zehmisch, the German schoolteacher, wrote in his diary that the English brought a football from their trenches, and that soon a lively game began. He wrote that it was wonderful and strange, and that the English officers, too, felt the same.

[pause]

{img:diary-page-candle-german-handwriting}
In other places, there were other games. One British soldier wrote home that his regiment had played the Germans, and lost three goals to two. Another wrote that the game had to stop when the ball landed on the barbed wire, and burst.

[long-pause]

The British soldiers loved football more than almost anything.

[pause]

Before the war, many of them had played every Saturday afternoon, in parks and on village greens and in the streets, or watched their local team, standing on the terraces, in their caps and scarves, cheering. Some of them had played for famous clubs. Football was part of their lives, and of their homes.

[pause]

The Germans played football too, though it was newer in Germany, and not quite as popular. But they knew the game, and they joined in gladly.

[long-pause]

For an hour or two, in the middle of the war, they were not soldiers any more, but young men, playing a game, on a cold, bright winter's day.

[pause]

Some of them were thinking, perhaps, of other Christmas Days, at home, when they had played football in the morning with their brothers and their friends, before going home to Christmas dinner.

[long-pause]

Other soldiers, who did not play football, found other things to do.

[pause]

Some of them simply walked about, talking. Some of them sat on the frozen ground, sharing cigarettes and chocolate and beer. Some of them sang. Some of them showed each other how their rifles worked, or swapped helmets and caps, and put them on and laughed at each other.

[pause]

{img:soldiers-swapping-caps-laughing-frosty-field}
In one place, the soldiers found a hare in the field, and both sides chased it together across No Man's Land, laughing and shouting, until someone caught it, and it was shared out for Christmas dinner.

[long-pause]

Not everyone took part in the truce.

[pause]

In some parts of the line, the fighting went on, even on Christmas Day. In others, the officers forbade their men to leave the trenches, and the soldiers stayed where they were, and only shouted greetings to each other. Some soldiers, on both sides, did not want to shake hands with the enemy, and stayed in their trenches, and watched.

[pause]

But along much of the British line in Flanders, on that Christmas Day, the guns were silent, and the soldiers met, and talked, and played, and for a few hours, there was peace.

[long-pause]

As the afternoon went on, the short winter day began to fade.

[pause]

The sun sank low in the west, red and golden, behind the poplar trees, and the frost began to harden again. The soldiers began to drift back to their own trenches, in twos and threes, saying goodbye to the men they had met, shaking hands one last time, and promising to meet again.

[pause]

{img:soldiers-walking-back-trenches-sunset-frost}
Many of them would write home that night, describing what had happened. Many of them would say that it was the strangest, and the best, Christmas of their lives.

[long-pause]

{img:christmas-night-trench-dugout-candles-dinner}
On the evening of Christmas Day, the soldiers sat down to their Christmas dinner in their trenches.

[pause]

In the British trenches, it was bully beef and potatoes, if they were lucky, and Christmas pudding from home, heated over a little fire, and perhaps a little rum. In some places, there was even a goose, or a chicken, bought from a farm behind the lines. In the German trenches, there was sausage and black bread and sauerkraut, and cake from home, and beer.

[long-pause]

They ate in their dugouts, crowded together, by the light of candles, and passed around the cake and the chocolate and the cigarettes, and talked about the day.

[pause]

They talked about the Germans they had met, and the presents they had exchanged, and the football, and the photographs, and the barber, and the hare. They could hardly believe that it had really happened.

[pause]

They looked at the buttons and badges and cigars that they had been given, and turned them over in their hands, and put them away carefully, to keep.

[long-pause]

Then they wrote letters home.

[pause]

Henry Williamson wrote to his mother. Frank Richards wrote to his friends. Thousands of soldiers, on both sides, sat in their dugouts that night, and wrote about what they had seen.

[pause]

Many of these letters were sent to the newspapers in Britain, by proud families, and printed. And so, in the first days of January 1915, the people of Britain read in their newspapers the extraordinary story of how their soldiers had spent Christmas Day with the Germans, in No Man's Land.

[pause]

{img:family-reading-newspaper-fireside-january-1915}
Some people were shocked. Most people were moved. Some of the newspapers printed photographs of British and German soldiers standing together in the field, smiling.

[long-pause]

In many places, the truce did not end on Christmas Day.

[pause]

The next day, Boxing Day, the soldiers met again. And the day after. In some parts of the line, the truce went on for a week, until the New Year, and in a few places, even longer. The soldiers went on talking and exchanging gifts, and agreed not to shoot at each other, and went about their work in the open, repairing their trenches, without being shot at.

[pause]

In one place, the British and German soldiers helped each other to repair their trenches and their wire. In another, they arranged to give each other a warning, if the officers ordered them to start shooting again, so that everyone would have time to get under cover.

[long-pause]

On New Year's Eve, the soldiers celebrated again.

[pause]

In some places, they sang Auld Lang Syne together. At midnight, by German time, which was an hour ahead of British time, the Germans fired their rifles into the air, to welcome the New Year, and an hour later, the British did the same, but all in the air, harmlessly, at the stars.

[pause]

And then, slowly, in the first days of January, the truce came to an end.

[long-pause]

The generals on both sides were not pleased.

[pause]

They had heard about the truce, and they did not like it. They worried that soldiers who had shaken hands with the enemy would not want to fight them. So they gave orders that there must be no more meetings with the enemy, and no more truces. Some officers were punished. New regiments were sent to the front, who had not been there at Christmas.

[pause]

{img:officer-reading-orders-candle-dugout-january}
And slowly, along the line, the shooting began again.

[long-pause]

But in some places, the soldiers on both sides were still reluctant.

[pause]

In one place, a German officer came out into No Man's Land, at the end of the truce, and saluted, and the British officer saluted back, and they shook hands, and went back to their trenches, and then the German fired two shots into the air, to show that the war had begun again.

[pause]

In another, the British soldiers shouted across that they were sorry, but they had orders to start shooting again tomorrow, and the Germans shouted back that they understood, and that they were sorry too.

[long-pause]

The war went on for nearly four more years.

[pause]

There was never another Christmas truce like the one in 1914. In the following years, the generals made sure that there was fighting, even on Christmas Day. The war grew larger, and harder, and longer, than anyone had ever imagined.

[pause]

{img:winter-fields-flanders-snow-quiet-dusk}
But the soldiers who were there that Christmas never forgot it. Many years later, when they were old men, they still talked about it, with wonder: about the candles on the parapets, and the singing in the night, and the handshakes in No Man's Land, and the football in the frost.

[pause]

For one day, in the middle of the greatest war the world had ever seen, there had been peace on earth.

[long-pause]

{img:november-morning-mist-fields-1918-quiet}
On the eleventh of November, 1918, nearly four years after that first Christmas in the trenches, the war came to an end.

[pause]

The armistice was signed in the early morning, in a railway carriage in a forest in France. And at eleven o'clock in the morning, on the eleventh day of the eleventh month, the guns along the whole long line, from the sea to the mountains, fell silent.

[long-pause]

In the trenches, the soldiers did not quite know what to do.

[pause]

For a few minutes, there was a strange, deep silence, after years of noise. Then, here and there, the soldiers began to cheer. Some of them climbed out of their trenches and stood in the open, in the November mist, looking around them. Some of them sat down and wept. Some of them simply lit a cigarette, and stood quietly, thinking of their friends who were not there to see it.

[pause]

In some places, the British and German soldiers walked out into No Man's Land once more, and met, and shook hands, as their comrades had done at Christmas, four years before.

[long-pause]

At home, in Britain, the church bells rang out all over the country, for the first time in years.

[pause]

People poured out into the streets of London and every town and village, cheering and singing and dancing and crying. Crowds gathered outside Buckingham Palace, as they had in August 1914, and sang, and the King and Queen came out onto the balcony.

[pause]

{img:village-church-bells-ringing-crowd-1918}
In Germany, too, people were glad that the war was over, though their country had lost. In France and Belgium, people danced in the streets.

[long-pause]

Slowly, over the following months, the soldiers came home.

[pause]

They came home by ship and by train, in their thousands, to their towns and villages, to their families. They came home to their mothers and fathers, their wives and children, their brothers and sisters and sweethearts. Many of them had been away for years.

[pause]

They were different now. They had seen things that they could never forget, and that they often could not talk about. But they were home.

[long-pause]

In Flanders, the people who had fled the fighting came back to their villages, and found them in ruins.

[pause]

The towns and villages near the front were destroyed. The fields were churned into mud and craters, full of barbed wire and old trenches. The woods were reduced to stumps. Ypres, the old town with its great cloth hall and its cathedral, was a field of rubble.

[pause]

{img:flemish-family-returning-ruined-village-spring}
But the people of Flanders did not give up. They cleared the fields, filling in the trenches and the craters, and ploughed them again, and planted crops. They rebuilt their farmhouses and their churches and their towns, brick by brick. It took many years. But in the end, the fields were green again, and the poplar trees grew along the roads, and the church bells rang across the flat land on Sundays, as they always had.

[long-pause]

In the old town of Ypres, the people rebuilt their great cloth hall and their cathedral, exactly as they had been before, stone by stone, from the old drawings and photographs. It took them forty years. But today, if you stand in the market square of Ypres, it looks almost as it did in the Middle Ages.

[pause]

{img:ypres-cloth-hall-rebuilt-market-square-evening}
And at one end of the town, where the road leads out toward the old battlefields, there is a great arch of white stone, called the Menin Gate.

[long-pause]

All across Flanders and northern France, the soldiers who fell in the war were laid to rest in cemeteries, near where they had fallen.

[pause]

There are hundreds of these cemeteries, large and small, scattered across the countryside: in the middle of fields, beside country roads, at the edge of woods, on the slopes of little hills. They are peaceful, beautiful places, with green lawns and flowers, and rows of white stones, all the same size, each with a name, if the name is known.

[pause]

They are cared for, every day, by gardeners, who mow the grass, and tend the roses, and keep the stones clean and white. Visitors come from all over the world, to find the names of their grandfathers and great-grandfathers, and to leave a little cross or a poppy.

[long-pause]

The poppy became the flower of remembrance.

[pause]

During the war, in the spring and summer, the fields of Flanders were covered with red poppies, which grow wherever the ground has been disturbed. A Canadian doctor named John McCrae, who served near Ypres, wrote a poem about them in 1915, which began: In Flanders fields the poppies blow, between the crosses, row on row.

[pause]

{img:red-poppies-field-flanders-summer-sky}
After the war, people began to wear little red poppies made of paper or cloth, every November, in memory of those who did not come home. They still do today.

[long-pause]

And every year, on the eleventh of November, at eleven o'clock in the morning, people in many countries stop what they are doing, wherever they are, and stand in silence for two minutes, to remember.

[long-pause]

{img:writer-cottage-devon-river-otter-evening}
Henry Williamson survived the war.

[pause]

He served through it all, from that first Christmas in Plugstreet Wood to the end, and came home in 1919, a quiet, troubled young man of twenty-three. He could not settle in London. So he went to live in a little thatched cottage in a village in Devon, in the west of England, near the sea, among the fields and the rivers and the moors that he loved.

[long-pause]

There, he began to write.

[pause]

He wrote about nature, about the birds and the animals and the rivers. In 1927, he published a book about the life of an otter, living in the rivers of Devon. It was called Tarka the Otter. It became one of the most famous books about animals ever written, and has been loved by readers all over the world ever since.

[pause]

Later, he wrote many more books, including a long series of novels about the war, in which he wrote about the Christmas truce, just as he had seen it.

[pause]

He never forgot that Christmas. All his life, he said that it had changed him, and made him think that the soldiers on both sides had been the same, just young men far from home.

[long-pause]

Bruce Bairnsfather became famous.

[pause]

His cartoons of Old Bill, the old soldier with the walrus moustache, were printed in a magazine in London during the war, and became enormously popular. Millions of people laughed at them, and soldiers pinned them up in their dugouts. There were Old Bill books, and Old Bill plays, and Old Bill films, and even Old Bill pottery.

[pause]

{img:cartoonist-older-drawing-studio-sketches}
In 1916, Bairnsfather wrote about the Christmas truce in a book called Bullets and Billets, and drew a picture of it. He said that he would not have missed that Christmas for anything. Later in his life, he drew cartoons in the Second World War too. He died in 1959.

[long-pause]

Frank Richards also survived the whole war, which was very rare.

[pause]

He served from the very beginning in 1914 to the very end in 1918, almost always in the front line, and was never seriously wounded. He was given medals for his bravery, and for his work as a signaller, laying telephone wires under fire.

[pause]

After the war, he went home to Wales, and worked as a labourer and a miner again. In 1933, he published his book, Old Soldiers Never Die, which told the story of his war, simply and honestly and with humour. He described the Christmas truce, and the two barrels of beer, and the Germans he had met. It is still read today, and is one of the best books ever written about life in the trenches.

[long-pause]

Kurt Zehmisch, the young German schoolteacher, also survived the war.

[pause]

He went home to Saxony, and became a teacher again, and taught children for many years. His diary, with his description of the Christmas truce, and of the football game, was kept by his family, and found by his son many years later, and published, so that people could read it.

[pause]

{img:german-schoolteacher-classroom-1920s-chalkboard}
He wrote in it that he would never forget that day. And he wrote that he hoped that the people of England and Germany would one day be friends.

[long-pause]

The little brass boxes from Princess Mary were kept by many of the soldiers, all their lives, as treasures.

[pause]

Today, you can still find them in attics and drawers all over Britain, sometimes still with a few old cigarettes or a little Christmas card inside, passed down from fathers to sons and daughters to grandchildren.

[pause]

The letters that the soldiers wrote home that Christmas were kept too, in boxes and drawers, and many of them are now in museums and archives, where people can still read them, written in pencil, in the trenches, by the light of candles, more than a hundred years ago.

[long-pause]

The last of the soldiers who were there at the Christmas truce lived to be very old men.

[pause]

Some of them lived until the beginning of this century. In their old age, they were often asked about that Christmas, and they told the story again and again, to their grandchildren, to schoolchildren, to journalists and historians.

[pause]

{img:very-old-veteran-armchair-telling-story-grandchild}
One of the last of them, a Scottish soldier named Alfred Anderson, who lived until 2005, at the age of one hundred and nine, remembered the silence of that Christmas morning, more than anything else. He said it was an eerie silence, and that all he could hear was the silence.

[long-pause]

{img:flanders-today-green-fields-poplars-church-spire}
Flanders is peaceful today.

[pause]

The flat fields stretch away under the wide sky, green in spring and summer, golden at harvest, brown and bare in winter. The poplar trees still stand in long rows along the roads. The farmhouses still have their red-tiled roofs, and their orchards, and their cows grazing in the wet meadows. The church spires still rise over the villages, and the bells still ring on Sundays.

[long-pause]

If you did not know, you might never guess what happened here, more than a hundred years ago.

[pause]

But if you look carefully, there are signs everywhere.

[pause]

Every spring, when the farmers plough their fields, they still find things in the earth: old buttons and buckles, bits of barbed wire, broken bottles, sometimes a helmet or a mess tin. The farmers call it the iron harvest. And everywhere, in the middle of the fields, beside the roads, at the edge of the woods, there are the little cemeteries, with their white stones and their roses.

[long-pause]

Plugstreet Wood is still there, near the village of Ploegsteert.

[pause]

It is a quiet wood again, of oak and ash and hazel, with paths winding among the trees, and birds singing in the branches, and bluebells in the spring. Inside it, among the trees, there are several small cemeteries, where soldiers who fell in the wood lie at rest, with their names on white stones, among the trees they knew.

[pause]

{img:small-cemetery-inside-wood-bluebells-spring}
At the edge of the wood, there is a great memorial, a round building of white stone, guarded by two stone lions, with the names of more than eleven thousand soldiers who have no known grave, carved on its walls.

[long-pause]

Not far away, near the little village of Saint-Yvon, at the edge of the wood, where Bruce Bairnsfather and Kurt Zehmisch met in No Man's Land on Christmas Day, 1914, there is a small memorial to the Christmas truce.

[pause]

It is a simple wooden cross, standing in the field, beside the road. People leave things at its foot: little wooden crosses and poppies, footballs, scarves from football clubs in England and Germany, candles, Christmas decorations, and messages of peace.

[pause]

{img:wooden-cross-field-footballs-poppies-winter}
In 2014, on the hundredth anniversary of the truce, a new memorial was unveiled nearby, showing two hands, one British and one German, holding a football between them. And that winter, British and German soldiers played friendly games of football together once more, in memory of the soldiers of 1914.

[long-pause]

In Ypres, every evening, there is a ceremony that has taken place almost every evening since 1928.

[pause]

At eight o'clock, as the darkness falls, the traffic is stopped beneath the great white arch of the Menin Gate. A small crowd gathers, people from the town and visitors from all over the world. And buglers from the local fire brigade step forward, in their uniforms, and play the Last Post, the slow, sad bugle call that marks the end of a soldier's day.

[pause]

{img:menin-gate-evening-buglers-crowd-quiet}
On the walls of the Menin Gate, there are the names of more than fifty-four thousand soldiers who fell in the fields around Ypres and have no known grave. The buglers play, the notes echo under the great arch, and everyone stands in silence. Then the people lay wreaths of poppies, and go quietly home.

[long-pause]

At Christmas, the people of Flanders still remember the truce.

[pause]

There are concerts and services in the churches, and people sing Silent Night, in English and German and Flemish and French. Children from Britain and Germany and Belgium visit the battlefields together, and play football together, and lay wreaths.

[pause]

And in the frosty fields near Plugstreet Wood, at the little wooden cross, people leave candles, glowing in the dark, on Christmas Eve, as the German soldiers once lit the candles on their little Christmas trees, on the parapet of their trench, more than a hundred years ago.

[long-pause]

On a winter evening in Flanders, the sky is very wide, and the light fades early.

[pause]

The sun goes down behind the poplar trees, red and golden, and the frost begins to settle on the fields. The lights come on in the farmhouses. A dog barks in a farmyard. The church bell rings for the evening. And over the flat, quiet land, the first stars come out, one by one, as they did on that Christmas Eve, long ago.

[pause]

{img:winter-dusk-flanders-farmhouse-lights-stars-frost}
In the little cemeteries, among the fields, the white stones stand in their rows, under the stars, in the silence. And the frost glitters on the grass of what was once No Man's Land, where, for one day, the soldiers of two armies met, and shook hands, and sang, and wished each other a merry Christmas.

[long-pause]

So we leave Flanders for tonight, the quiet flat land under the winter stars, with its poplar trees and its farmhouses and its little church spires, and its long memory of a silent night.
