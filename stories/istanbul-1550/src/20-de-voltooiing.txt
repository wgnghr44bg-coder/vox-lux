The years pass. Mehmet grows up. His voice gets deeper, and his shoulders get broader. He learns to carve stone almost as well as his father. And every year, the great mosque on the third hill grows higher. The walls are finished. The half domes are built. And then, at last, the great dome itself rises over the city.

--

In the year 1557, seven years after our story began, the Süleymaniye mosque is finished. Mehmet is now twenty years old. He is a real stonemason, a master of his trade. Fatma is sixteen, and she is going to be married soon, to a young carpenter from the mahalle. Nine has passed away peacefully, in her sleep, during the winter. The family misses her very much. But she lived to see the great dome rise over the city, and she was very proud.

The mosque is opened with a great ceremony. The sultan comes, with all his viziers and officials. There is a famous story about this day. Sinan stands before the sultan, holding the golden key of the mosque. The sultan asks who should open the doors. And he says that Sinan should open them, because no one deserves it more. Sinan opens the great doors, and the sultan walks in.

{img:suleymaniye-interior-vast-dome-windows-light-stained-glass-calm}
Inside, the mosque is breathtaking. The great dome floats high above, more than fifty metres up. Around it, there are many windows, letting in the light. Some of the windows are made of coloured glass, red, blue and green, like jewels. The walls are white and calm. Around the prayer niche, there are beautiful Iznik tiles, with tulips and carnations in blue and red. The calligraphy of Ahmed Karahisari runs around the walls, in gold and black.

The mosque is not covered with decorations everywhere. It is simple, clear and calm. Sinan wanted the space itself to be beautiful, with its perfect shapes, its light and its quiet. When you stand inside it, you feel peaceful. The sound is wonderful too. Sinan has hidden clay jars in the dome, to help the sound travel. So the voice of the imam can be heard clearly in every corner, without shouting.

--

{img:young-stonemason-and-father-standing-in-mosque-looking-up-proud-quiet}
On the first Friday after the opening, Mehmet and his father go to pray in the new mosque. They kneel on the carpet among the thousands of other men. Mehmet looks around him, at the walls and the columns and the windows. He knows many of these stones. He remembers carving some of them. Over there, at the bottom of that column, is a stone that he cut when he was fifteen. Nobody else knows that. But he knows. And God knows.

Mehmet's father has tears in his eyes. He is getting old now. This will be his last great building. He puts his hand on his son's shoulder and squeezes it, without saying anything. He does not need to. Mehmet knows exactly what he means.

{img:courtyard-suleymaniye-soup-kitchen-poor-people-eating-gardens-tombs}
Around the mosque, the other buildings are opening too. The schools fill with students. The hospital receives its first patients. And in the soup kitchen, the cooks begin to serve free food every day, to the poor, to students and to travellers. Bread and soup, and on Fridays, rice with meat. Hundreds of people are fed every day, for free. The great mosque is not only a place of prayer. It is a place of kindness too.

--

Sinan will build many more buildings after the Süleymaniye. He will call it the work of his journeyman years, the time when he was still learning. His masterpiece, he will say, is another mosque, in the city of Edirne, which he finishes when he is about eighty years old. But for the people of Istanbul, the Süleymaniye will always be the most beloved. It still stands on its hill today, watching over the city, as it has done for more than four hundred and fifty years.

In the evenings, the Süleymaniye is lit up by thousands of little oil lamps, hanging on long chains from the ceiling. From far away, people can see its windows glowing in the darkness. And the smoke of all those lamps rises up, as Sinan planned, into the little room above the entrance. There, it is collected as soot, and made into ink. With that ink, calligraphers write beautiful words, on paper, about God and about the light.
