The people of Istanbul love gardens. Behind many houses, even small ones, there is a little garden, with a fig tree, a vine, a few rose bushes and some herbs. The rich have big gardens, with pools, fountains, fruit trees and wooden pavilions. And all around the city, along the Bosphorus and the Golden Horn, there are great gardens belonging to the sultan.

--

For the Ottomans, a garden is a little piece of paradise. In the Quran, paradise is described as a beautiful garden, with flowing water, shady trees and fruit. So people try to make their gardens like that. They plant trees for shade. They make little channels and pools for water. And they plant flowers, many, many flowers.

The favourite flowers of the Ottomans are roses, carnations, hyacinths and tulips. Especially tulips. The tulip grows wild in the mountains of Central Asia, where the ancestors of the Turks came from. The Ottomans love it so much that they grow hundreds of different kinds, with beautiful names. Morning star, rose of dawn, the one who makes hearts burn. They love tulips with long, thin, pointed petals, like little daggers.

{img:single-ottoman-tulip-long-pointed-petals-red-glass-vase-window}
Tulips are everywhere in Istanbul. Not only in the gardens, but also in art. They are painted on tiles, woven into carpets, embroidered on clothes and carved in stone. Ayşe embroiders tulips on her silk work, with red and white thread. In the new mosque that Mehmet's father is building, there will be tiles with tulips, in blue, green and bright red.

A few years from now, a traveller from Flanders will see the tulips in the gardens of Istanbul. He will take some bulbs home with him to Europe. And there, in the Netherlands, people will fall in love with tulips too. So much that, about eighty years later, tulip bulbs will be sold for more money than a house. But that is another story.

In the spring, when the gardens are in flower, the people of Istanbul go out to enjoy them. On fine days, families go to the gardens and meadows outside the city, for picnics. They spread carpets on the grass under the trees. They bring food in baskets, and play music, and sing. The children run around and pick flowers. The old people sit in the shade and talk.

One Friday in the spring, Mehmet's family goes to a place by the water, outside the walls of the city. It is called Kağıthane, the Sweet Waters of Europe. It is a green valley with a little river, where two streams flow into the Golden Horn. On spring days, hundreds of families come here, from all over the city. There are tents and carpets everywhere on the grass.

{img:kagithane-sweet-waters-meadow-river-families-picnicking-caiques}
The family spreads its carpet under a big plane tree, beside the river. Ayşe has brought stuffed vine leaves, bread, cheese, cherries and a jar of sherbet. Nine sits on a cushion, wrapped in her shawl, and closes her eyes in the sun. Mehmet and Fatma run along the riverbank, watching the caiques go by. Their father lies on his back in the grass, with his hands behind his head. For once, he has nothing to do.

--

In the afternoon, a man comes by with a little puppet theatre. It is called Karagöz. The puppets are flat figures made of coloured leather, and they are shown behind a white cloth, with a lamp behind them. The children sit in front of the cloth and watch the shadows move. Karagöz is a funny, simple man, who always gets into trouble. His friend Hacivat is clever and proud. They argue and play tricks on each other. Fatma laughs so much that she gets the hiccups.

On the way home, as the sun is going down, Fatma falls asleep in her mother's arms in the caique. Mehmet trails his hand in the cool water and watches the city come closer. Nine says that it has been the best day of the whole spring. Their father agrees. He says that they must come again next year, when the tulips are in flower once more. And they do.
