On Fridays, there is no work on the building site. Friday is the holy day of the week for Muslims, when the men go to the big mosques for the noon prayer. And on one Friday afternoon, after the prayer, Mehmet's mother takes the children to the Grand Bazaar. She has some embroidery to sell. And Fatma has been begging to go for weeks.

--

The Grand Bazaar is one of the biggest covered markets in the world. In the middle of it, there are two great stone halls, with many small domes on top. They were built by Sultan Mehmed the Conqueror, almost a hundred years ago. Inside these halls, the most precious things are sold. Jewels, gold, silver, fine weapons and rich fabrics. At night, the heavy iron doors are locked, and guards watch over the treasures.

Around the stone halls, there are hundreds of little streets with shops. Many of them are covered with wooden roofs, to keep out the sun and rain. Each street has its own trade. There is a street of the slipper makers, a street of the jewellers, a street of the carpet sellers, a street of the copper workers. If you want to buy something, you go to its street, and you can compare the shops and their prices.

{img:bazaar-street-slipper-makers-colourful-leather-shoes-hanging-shopkeeper}
Ayşe and the children walk through the crowded streets. It is noisy and colourful and full of wonderful smells. Shopkeepers sit cross-legged in their little shops, which are raised up from the street, like little stages. They call out to the people walking by, inviting them to come and look. Some of them offer a glass of sherbet to good customers.

They pass the street of the slipper makers, where hundreds of soft leather slippers hang in rows, in red, yellow, blue and green. Each colour has a meaning. Muslims wear yellow slippers, Greeks wear black ones, Armenians purple and Jews blue. Fatma wants a pair of red slippers with little pointed toes. Her mother smiles and says, perhaps, if the embroidery sells well.

--

{img:merchant-examining-silk-embroidery-mother-daughter-bazaar-shop}
They go to the shop of the merchant who buys Ayşe's embroidery. He is an Armenian, an old friend of the family, called Bedros. He is a kind man with a big grey moustache. He unfolds the embroidery carefully on his counter, and looks at it for a long time. Then he says it is the best work he has seen this year. He pays Ayşe a good price. And he gives Fatma a little bag of sweets made from sugar and almonds.

With the money, Ayşe buys a few things for the house. A new copper pot, some cotton cloth and a little packet of spices. And, yes, a pair of red slippers for Fatma, with little pointed toes. Fatma is so happy that she wants to wear them at once. But her mother says that they are for special days. So Fatma carries them home in her arms, holding them tightly.

{img:spice-seller-baskets-pepper-cinnamon-saffron-colourful-heaps-bazaar}
On the way out, they pass the spice sellers. Their shops are full of baskets and sacks of spices, piled up in coloured heaps. Red pepper, yellow saffron, brown cinnamon and black pepper, cloves and cumin and nutmeg. The smell is so strong that Mehmet sneezes three times. The spices come from India, Persia, Arabia and the islands of the East. They have travelled for months to get here, by ship and by camel caravan.

--

At the edge of the bazaar, there are also the big stone buildings called hans. These are like warehouses and hotels together. Merchants from faraway countries stay in them, on the upper floor, with their goods stored below. Their camels and horses rest in the courtyard. In the evening, the merchants sit together and talk in many languages. Persian, Arabic, Greek, Italian, Armenian. They talk about trade, and prices, and the news from distant lands.

In the middle of the bazaar, there is a little fountain, where people stop to drink and rest. Ayşe and the children sit on the stone edge for a while, and watch the crowds go by. A porter walks past with a whole wardrobe on his back. A man leads a tiny monkey on a string. An old woman bargains loudly for a carpet, and wins. Fatma says the bazaar is better than any story.
