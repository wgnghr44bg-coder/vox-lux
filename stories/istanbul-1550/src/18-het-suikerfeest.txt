At the end of Ramadan, there is a great feast. It lasts for three days. In Turkish, it is called Şeker Bayramı, the Sugar Feast, because of all the sweets that people eat. It is the happiest time of the whole year. Everyone has been waiting for it for weeks.

--

On the morning of the feast, everyone gets up early and washes. They put on new clothes, or their very best ones. Mehmet's mother has made new clothes for the whole family. A new shirt and waistcoat for Mehmet. A new dress for Fatma, embroidered with little tulips. And Fatma finally gets to wear her red slippers with the pointed toes. She has been waiting for this day for months.

The men go to the mosque for the special feast prayer, early in the morning. The mosques are so full that people pray in the courtyards and even in the streets. After the prayer, everyone greets each other. They shake hands, hug, and wish each other a happy feast. Old quarrels are forgotten. Everyone smiles.

{img:children-kissing-grandmothers-hand-receiving-sweets-coins-ottoman-home}
Then the visits begin. During the three days of the feast, families visit each other. First, the young visit the old. Mehmet and Fatma go to Nine first. They kiss her hand and touch it to their foreheads, as a sign of respect. Nine gives them each a little coin and a handful of sweets, wrapped in a handkerchief. Then they visit their aunts and uncles, the neighbours and the old people of the mahalle.

Everywhere they go, they are given sweets. Little candies made of sugar, baklava, helva, dried fruits and nuts. By the end of the first day, Fatma says that she never wants to see a sweet again. By the next morning, she has changed her mind.

--

{img:feast-fair-swings-carousel-children-playing-square-istanbul-colourful}
In the open squares of the city, there are fairs for the children. There are wooden swings, and little carousels turned by hand. There are men selling toys, sweets and balloons made of animal bladders. There are jugglers, acrobats, wrestlers and puppet shows. The children spend their coins and run from one thing to the next. Mehmet and Fatma ride on the swings until they are dizzy.

The neighbours of other faiths also come to visit. Bedros, the Armenian merchant, and his family come with their best wishes for the feast. Doctor Moshe brings a little box of sugared almonds for Nine. Nikolaos comes from Galata, with Yannis. Mehmet's mother serves them all sweets and sherbet. The house is full of people and laughter all day long.

{img:courtyard-evening-feast-family-guests-lanterns-music-laughter}
In the evening of the first day, the family sits together in the courtyard, under the fig tree. Ayşe has hung up little lanterns in the branches. A neighbour plays a long-necked lute, called a saz, and sings old songs from Anatolia. Mehmet's father sings along, softly. He learned these songs as a boy, in his village. Nine listens with her eyes closed, nodding her head in time with the music.

--

Late that night, when everyone has gone home, Mehmet lies on his mattress and listens to the sounds of the city. Somewhere, people are still singing. A dog barks. A boat bell rings down at the water. He thinks about the year that has passed. His first year of work. Meeting Sinan. The bazaar, the hammam, the Bosphorus. It has been a good year. And he falls asleep with the taste of honey still in his mouth.
