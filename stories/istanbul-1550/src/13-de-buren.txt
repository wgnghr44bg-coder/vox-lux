Istanbul in 1550 is a city of many peoples. About half of the people are Muslims, mostly Turks. The other half are Christians and Jews. Greeks, Armenians, Jews, and also people from many other places. They speak many different languages. They go to different houses of prayer. And yet, they live together in the same city, mostly in peace.

--

In the Ottoman Empire, Christians and Jews are allowed to keep their own faith. They have their own churches and synagogues, their own schools and their own leaders. They pay a special tax, and there are some rules that they must follow. For example, their houses may not be taller than those of Muslims, and they must wear clothes of certain colours. Life is not completely equal. But compared with many other countries at that time, it is quite tolerant.

Mehmet's mahalle is mostly Muslim. But on the next street, there is an Armenian neighbourhood, with its own little church. And a little further, near the water, there is a Jewish neighbourhood. People from all these neighbourhoods meet every day, in the markets, in the streets and at work. They buy from each other, work together and sometimes become good friends.

{img:jewish-doctor-1550-istanbul-tall-hat-visiting-family-medicine-bag}
The Jewish community of Istanbul has a special story. About sixty years ago, in 1492, the king and queen of Spain forced all the Jews to leave their country. Many of them had nowhere to go. But the Ottoman sultan of the time, Bayezid the Second, welcomed them to his empire. Thousands of Jewish families came to Istanbul. They brought their language, their skills and their knowledge with them.

Many of these Spanish Jews are doctors, merchants and craftsmen. They brought the first printing press to Istanbul, and printed books in Hebrew. The doctor of Mehmet's family is one of them. He is called Doctor Moshe. He is a quiet, gentle man, with a long grey beard and a tall hat. When Nine has a bad cough in the winter, he comes to visit her. He gives her a syrup made of honey and herbs. And she always says that he is the best doctor in the whole city.

--

{img:armenian-family-courtyard-easter-red-eggs-neighbours-visiting}
The family's Armenian neighbours are the family of Bedros, the merchant from the bazaar. They live just around the corner. At their great feast of Easter, they paint eggs red, and give some to Mehmet's family. At the end of Ramadan, Mehmet's family brings them plates of sweets. The children of both families play together in the street. And the mothers exchange recipes over the garden wall.

The Greeks of Istanbul are the oldest inhabitants of the city. They were here long before the Ottomans came. Many of them are fishermen, sailors, merchants and craftsmen. Their leader is the Patriarch, the head of the Orthodox Church, who lives in a district called the Fener, on the Golden Horn. On their holy days, the Greek churches ring their bells, and their priests walk through the streets in processions, with candles and icons.

{img:greek-fishermen-mending-nets-shore-golden-horn-evening-boats}
Of course, not everything is always peaceful. Sometimes there are arguments and quarrels between neighbours of different faiths, as there are between neighbours everywhere. Sometimes there are unfair rules, or officials who treat some people worse than others. But on the whole, the people of Istanbul are used to living with each other. They have done so for generations.

--

In the evenings, from Mehmet's window, you can hear many sounds. The call to prayer from the minaret of the little mosque. The bells of the Armenian church. The singing of prayers from the synagogue, on Friday evenings. And the voices of mothers calling their children home, in Turkish, Greek, Armenian and Spanish. Nine says that it is like a garden, with many different flowers. Each one is different. But together, they make the garden beautiful.

On the corner of their street, there is a little shop that belongs to an old Greek man, who sells olive oil, soap and candles. Everyone in the street buys from him. He knows exactly what each family needs, and he often lets poor families pay later. When he was ill last winter, the Muslim neighbours brought him soup every day, and the Armenian neighbours looked after his shop. When he was better, he gave every child in the street a little candle.
