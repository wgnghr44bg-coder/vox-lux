In the old heart of the city, on the first hill, there stands the most famous building of Istanbul. It is Hagia Sophia. It was built more than a thousand years ago, by the Byzantine emperor Justinian, as a Christian church. For almost a thousand years, it was the greatest church in the world. And when the Ottomans came in 1453, Sultan Mehmed turned it into a mosque.

--

From the outside, Hagia Sophia looks heavy and strong. It is a huge building of brick and stone, painted a soft reddish colour. Over the centuries, big supporting walls have been added to the sides, to stop it from falling down in earthquakes. The Ottomans have added minarets, tall and thin, at the corners. And over everything rises the great dome, wide and low, like a sky made of stone.

One Friday, Mehmet's father takes him to the noon prayer at Hagia Sophia. He wants his son to see it, because it is the building that every architect in Istanbul studies. Even Sinan. They walk through a courtyard and through great bronze doors. And then they step inside.

{img:hagia-sophia-interior-vast-dome-light-windows-floating-people-small}
Mehmet stops and stares. He has never seen anything like it. The inside of Hagia Sophia is one enormous space. And high above, the great dome seems to float in the air. Around its bottom edge, there is a ring of forty windows. The light shines through them so brightly that the dome seems to be resting on a circle of light, not on stone. People say it looks like it hangs from heaven on a golden chain.

The walls are covered with marble of many colours. Green, red, white, grey and purple, with wavy patterns like water. The columns are made of polished stone, some of them brought from ancient temples in faraway lands. High up on the walls and in the dome, there are mosaics, made of tiny pieces of gold glass. Some of them still show the old Christian pictures, and the Ottomans have left many of them in place, glittering softly in the light.

--

{img:men-praying-rows-hagia-sophia-carpets-sunlight-beams-quiet}
The floor is covered with carpets. Thousands of men are kneeling on them in long, straight rows, all facing the same direction, toward Mecca. Because the old church was built facing east, and Mecca lies to the southeast, the rows of prayer are at a slight angle to the building. Mehmet and his father find a place in a row and kneel down. When the imam begins the prayer, thousands of men stand up, bow and kneel together, as one.

The sound is extraordinary. The voice of the imam floats up into the dome and comes back down, softly, from everywhere at once. When the thousands of men say amen together, the sound rolls around the building like gentle thunder. Mehmet feels very small. But he also feels very calm, and very safe.

{img:boy-looking-up-at-dome-hagia-sophia-golden-light-awe-father-beside}
After the prayer, they stay for a while. Mehmet's father points out the details of the building to him. Look at how the big dome rests on four great arches. Look at the half domes, which help to hold it up. Look at the windows, how they make the stone seem light. Every architect in Istanbul has tried to understand this building. And now, Sinan is trying to build something just as great.

--

As they walk out, Mehmet looks back at the great dome one last time. He thinks of the people who built it, a thousand years ago. Stonemasons like his father, and boys like him, carrying tools and sweeping dust. Their names are forgotten. But their work is still here, still standing, still beautiful. He thinks of what Sinan said. Do your best work, even on the stones that nobody will see.

Outside, in the square, the pigeons are pecking at crumbs on the warm stones. Mehmet's father buys them each a cup of cool water from a water seller. They sit for a while on the steps of a fountain, in the shade, and watch the people come and go. Then they walk home slowly, through the old streets, talking about domes and arches all the way.
