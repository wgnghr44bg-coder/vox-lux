The building site is on top of the third hill of Istanbul, high above the Golden Horn. It is enormous. Until recently, there was an old palace here, with gardens. Now everything has been cleared away. And in its place, a huge mosque is being built, for Sultan Suleiman. It will be called the Süleymaniye, the mosque of Suleiman.

--

But it is not just a mosque. Around the mosque, there will be a whole group of buildings. Schools for young scholars. A school of medicine and a hospital. A kitchen that will feed the poor every day. A guest house for travellers. A bath house. Shops. And a quiet garden with the tombs of the sultan and his wife. It is like a small town of its own, all built around the mosque.

Thousands of people work on the building site. There are stonemasons, like Mehmet's father. There are carpenters, blacksmiths, lead workers and glass makers. There are labourers who carry stones and dig foundations. Some of them are skilled craftsmen, who are paid well. Others are ordinary workers, and soldiers who help with the heavy work. About half of the workers are Christians, from the Greek and Armenian communities, and the rest are Muslims.

{img:stonemasons-carving-blocks-chisels-dust-sunlight-ottoman-workshop}
Mehmet works with his father in the stonemasons' area. They work under a simple wooden roof, in the shade. All day long, they cut and shape blocks of stone. The stone comes from quarries near the Sea of Marmara, and is brought to the city by ship. Each block must be cut exactly to the right size and shape, according to the drawings of the architect. If a block is even a finger too big, it will not fit.

Mehmet does not cut big blocks yet. He is still an apprentice. He sweeps away the dust, carries the tools and sharpens the chisels. He watches his father and the other masons carefully, to learn how they work. And sometimes, his father lets him try. He gives him a small stone and a chisel, and shows him how to hold it, and how hard to strike. Little by little, Mehmet is learning.

--

{img:wooden-crane-lifting-large-stone-block-ropes-pulleys-workers-pulling}
The most exciting thing on the building site is the lifting of the stones. The walls of the mosque are already high. The heavy blocks must be lifted up to the top. For this, there are big wooden cranes, with ropes and pulleys. The workers turn great wooden wheels, or pull on the ropes, and the stones rise slowly into the air. Mehmet loves to watch the stones go up. It seems like magic.

The building of the mosque will take seven years. It is very carefully organised. There are lists of every worker, every tool and every stone. Clerks write down everything in big books. How many workers came today. How much they were paid. How many stones arrived from the quarry. These books still exist today. That is why we know so much about how the mosque was built.

{img:clerks-writing-ledgers-building-site-ottoman-tent-scrolls}
At midday, the workers stop to pray. Then they eat their lunch, sitting in the shade. Mehmet's mother has packed bread, cheese and olives for them, and a few dried apricots. Mehmet and his father sit with the other masons. Some of them are Greek, some are Armenian. They talk and joke in a mixture of Turkish and Greek. Mehmet has learned quite a few Greek words this way.

--

In the afternoon, the work goes on until the late call to prayer. Then the workers put down their tools. Mehmet and his father walk home down the hill, tired and covered in white stone dust. At the fountain in the mahalle, they wash their faces and hands. And Mehmet looks back up at the hill, where the half-built walls of the great mosque are glowing pink and gold in the evening sun.

Some of the stone for the mosque comes from very far away. There are great columns of granite and marble, taken from old Roman and Byzantine buildings in other cities. One of them comes from the ancient city of Alexandria, in Egypt. Another, people say, once stood in the old palace of the Byzantine emperors. They are brought by ship, and dragged up the hill on wooden rollers, by hundreds of men pulling on ropes, singing to keep the rhythm.
