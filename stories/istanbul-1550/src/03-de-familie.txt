Mehmet lives with his family in a neighbourhood near the Golden Horn, not far from the new mosque. In Istanbul, a neighbourhood is called a mahalle. Each mahalle is like a small village inside the big city. It has its own little mosque, its own fountain, a bakery, a grocer and a little shop that sells sweet drinks called sherbet. Everyone knows everyone else.

--

The house of Mehmet's family is made of wood, like most houses in Istanbul. It has two floors. The upper floor sticks out over the street, supported by wooden beams. This gives a little more room inside, and makes the narrow streets shady and cool. The windows of the upper floor are covered with wooden lattices, so that people inside can look out, but people outside cannot look in.

Downstairs, there is a little courtyard with a fig tree and a well. There is a kitchen with a fireplace, and a storeroom. Upstairs, there are the living rooms. They are simple, but comfortable. There are no chairs or beds, the way we have them. Instead, along the walls, there are low benches with cushions, called sofas. During the day, the family sits on them. At night, mattresses and quilts are taken out of big cupboards, and spread out on the floor to sleep.

{img:ottoman-family-room-low-sofa-cushions-carpet-brazier-window-light}
The father of the family is called Hasan. He is a stonemason, a man who cuts and shapes stone for buildings. He is about forty years old, with a dark beard and strong, rough hands. He came to Istanbul as a young man from a village in Anatolia. He has worked on many buildings in the city. Now he works on the biggest one of all, the new mosque of Sultan Suleiman.

The mother is called Ayşe. She runs the house, and she is also a very good embroiderer. She sews beautiful flowers onto cloth, with silk thread in many colours. She sells her embroidery to a merchant in the bazaar. With the money, she buys things for the house, and saves a little for her daughter's future. She is a calm, cheerful woman, who sings while she works.

--

{img:mother-embroidering-silk-flowers-cushion-window-lattice-light}
There is also a grandmother, Hasan's mother, who lives with them. Everyone calls her Nine, which means granny in Turkish. She is very old and very wise. She knows all the old stories, and all the remedies for coughs and colds. She spends most of the day sitting on the sofa by the window, watching the street, and knows everything that happens in the mahalle before anyone else.

And there are the two children. Mehmet is thirteen. He is thin and quick, with curious brown eyes. For a year, he has been working with his father on the building site, learning to be a stonemason. And there is his little sister, Fatma, who is nine. She is learning embroidery from her mother, and reading from the teacher at the neighbourhood school.

{img:boy-and-little-sister-courtyard-fig-tree-well-morning-ottoman}
Mehmet and Fatma are good friends, although they sometimes argue. In the evenings, Mehmet tells Fatma about the building site. About the huge stones, the cranes and the hundreds of workers. Fatma wants to see it, but girls do not go to building sites. So Mehmet draws pictures for her in the dust of the courtyard, with a stick. He draws the domes and the minarets, as he imagines they will look when the mosque is finished.

--

In the mahalle, life is busy and friendly. The neighbours greet each other in the street, and ask about each other's families. When someone is sick, the neighbours bring soup. When someone has a baby, the whole street celebrates. When someone dies, everyone comes to help. In the evenings, the men sit together in front of the little mosque, and the women visit each other's houses. And the children play in the streets until it gets dark.

On cold winter evenings, the family gathers around a brazier, a big copper bowl full of glowing charcoal. They put a low table over it, and cover the table with a thick quilt. Then everyone sits around it, with their legs under the warm quilt. Nine tells stories, Ayşe sews, and the children crack walnuts. Outside, the wind howls over the Golden Horn. But under the quilt, everyone is warm and cosy.
