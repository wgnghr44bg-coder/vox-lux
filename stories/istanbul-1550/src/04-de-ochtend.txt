The day begins very early in Istanbul, with the first call to prayer, before sunrise. Mehmet's father gets up first. He goes down to the courtyard and washes his face, hands, arms and feet with cold water from the well. This washing is part of the prayer. Then he spreads a small rug on the floor of the living room, facing toward Mecca, and he prays.

--

Water is very important in Istanbul. Islam teaches that people should be clean before they pray, five times a day. So the city needs a great deal of water. It is brought from the forests and hills outside the city, through long channels and aqueducts, some of them built by the Romans. And all over the city, there are public fountains, where anyone can come and fill their jugs, or wash, or simply drink.

The fountain in Mehmet's mahalle is a little stone building in the middle of the street. It has a carved marble front, with a brass tap and a stone basin. Above it, there is a poem carved in beautiful letters, saying who paid for the fountain. Rich people often build fountains as a good deed, so that everyone can have water. Every morning, the women and children of the mahalle come here with their jugs, and stop to talk.

{img:breakfast-ottoman-family-low-round-table-bread-cheese-olives-honey}
After the prayer, the family has breakfast together. They sit on cushions on the floor, around a low, round table. There is fresh bread from the bakery, still warm. There is white cheese, black olives, honey and jam made from rose petals. There is also a soup, made from lentils or yoghurt. There is no tea or coffee yet. The family drinks water, or a little milk.

Fatma goes to the bakery every morning to fetch the bread. The baker is an old man with flour in his beard. He always gives her a little extra piece of bread, sprinkled with sesame seeds, to eat on the way home. Sometimes she shares it with the stray cats that sleep in front of the bakery. Istanbul is full of cats. People love them and feed them, and the cats are very well looked after.

--

{img:stray-cats-sleeping-bakery-doorstep-sunlight-ottoman-street}
After breakfast, Mehmet and his father set off for work. They walk up the hill, through the narrow streets. The city is waking up. Shopkeepers are opening their shutters. Porters are carrying huge loads on their backs, bent almost double. Street sellers are calling out. Water carriers walk by, with leather bags full of water on their backs, selling cups of water to thirsty people.

The streets are crowded with all kinds of people. There are Turks in long robes and white turbans. There are Greek fishermen, and Armenian merchants, and Jewish doctors with their special hats. There are soldiers, and scholars with books under their arms. Everyone wears clothes that show who they are. The colour of the turban, the shape of the hat, the colour of the shoes. You could tell a lot about a person just by looking at their clothes.

{img:crowded-istanbul-street-1550-porters-merchants-turbans-hats-colours}
Back at home, the women begin their work. Ayşe cleans the house and prepares the food for the day. Nine sits by the window and sorts lentils, picking out the little stones. Fatma goes to the neighbourhood school, near the mosque, where she learns to read the Quran and to write. The teacher is an old man, kind but strict. The children sit on the floor in a circle around him, and repeat the words after him, in a soft singsong.

--

Then the sun climbs higher over the city. The mist has gone from the water. The domes of the mosques shine in the sunlight. And from the building site on the hill, there comes the sound of hundreds of hammers and chisels, ringing on stone, like the sound of a thousand small bells.

On the way up the hill, Mehmet and his father always pass the same old man. He sits on a little stool by the corner, with a tray of roasted chickpeas and dried mulberries. Every morning, he greets them by name, and asks about the mosque. Every morning, Mehmet's father gives him a small coin, and the old man gives Mehmet a handful of chickpeas. Mehmet eats them one by one, all the way to work.
