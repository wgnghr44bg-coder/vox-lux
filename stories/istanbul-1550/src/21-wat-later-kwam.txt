Sultan Suleiman ruled for forty-six years, longer than any other Ottoman sultan. In 1566, when he was about seventy years old, he went with his army to Hungary, one last time. There, in his tent, he passed away peacefully. His body was brought back to Istanbul, and he was buried in the garden of his great mosque, next to his beloved Hürrem, who had passed away eight years before.

--

After Suleiman, the Ottoman Empire went on for more than three hundred and fifty years. For a long time, it remained one of the greatest powers in the world. Istanbul kept growing, and new mosques, palaces and fountains were built. In the early seventeen hundreds, there was even a period called the Tulip Era, when the sultan and his court held tulip festivals in the palace gardens, with candles placed among the flowers and tortoises carrying little lamps on their backs.

Coffee houses spread all over the city, as we said. They became places where men met to talk, read, play games and listen to music and stories. Later, newspapers were read aloud in them. And from Istanbul, coffee and the coffee house travelled to Europe, to Venice, London, Paris and Vienna. Without Istanbul, perhaps nobody in Europe would drink coffee today.

{img:istanbul-1800s-galata-bridge-crowds-steamships-trams-old-photo-feel-later}
But slowly, the world changed. The countries of Europe became richer and more powerful, with their new factories and their great ships. The Ottoman Empire grew weaker, and began to lose its lands, one after another. In the eighteen hundreds, Istanbul changed too. The first bridges were built across the Golden Horn. Steamships replaced many of the old caiques. Trams and a little underground railway were built. Many people began to wear European clothes.

In the First World War, the Ottoman Empire fought on the losing side. After the war, the old empire came to an end. In 1923, a new country was founded, the Republic of Turkey. Its leader was a general called Mustafa Kemal, later known as Atatürk. The new capital of Turkey was not Istanbul, but the city of Ankara, in the middle of Anatolia. After almost five hundred years, Istanbul was no longer the capital of an empire.

--

{img:topkapi-palace-museum-visitors-courtyard-cypress-trees-later-today}
The palace of the sultans became a museum. Today, visitors can walk through its courtyards and gardens, and see the rooms where the sultans lived. They can see the great kitchens, with their huge copper pots, and the treasury full of jewels. The silent second courtyard, with its cypress trees, is now full of tourists from all over the world, talking in a hundred different languages.

Hagia Sophia also changed. In 1934, it became a museum. The plaster was removed from many of the old Christian mosaics, and their gold shone again. People of every faith could visit it. Then, in 2020, it was turned back into a mosque. But visitors are still welcome, outside the times of prayer, to stand under the great dome and look up at the floating light.

{img:sinan-tomb-small-simple-corner-garden-suleymaniye-today-quiet}
Sinan lived to be about ninety years old. He passed away in 1588. He was buried in a small, simple tomb, which he designed himself, in a corner just outside the walls of the Süleymaniye. It is a very modest tomb, for such a great man. Above it, there is a little poem carved in stone, about his life and his work. Visitors still come to stand there quietly, and say a prayer for the old architect.

--

As for Mehmet and his family, they were ordinary people, and their names were not written in the history books. But perhaps the stones that Mehmet carved are still there, in the walls of the great mosque. Perhaps a family of stonemasons still lives somewhere in the city, descended from him. And perhaps, somewhere in an old wooden box, there is still a little sheet of marbled paper, with a red tulip in the middle.
