{img:istanbul-1550-dawn-golden-horn-domes-minarets-mist-boats}
Tonight we travel back almost five hundred years, to the year 1550. We go to one of the greatest cities in the world. It lies on two continents, Europe and Asia, on the shores of a narrow strip of sea. It has been called by many names. The Ottomans, who rule it now, call it Istanbul.

[long-pause]

It is early morning, just before sunrise. A soft mist lies over the water. Out of the mist rise the great grey domes of the mosques, and the tall, thin towers beside them, called minarets. They look like pencils pointing at the sky. Seagulls are crying over the water. Little wooden boats are already moving across the harbour. And the first pale light is spreading over the hills of the city.

[pause]

Then, from the top of a minaret, a voice begins to sing. It is the call to prayer. It floats out over the rooftops, slow and beautiful. A moment later, another voice joins it, from another minaret. And then another, and another, from all over the city. Hundreds of voices, rising and falling, echoing across the water, as the sun comes up over the hills of Asia.

[pause]

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

{img:ottoman-istanbul-panorama-1550-hills-mosques-wooden-houses-cypress-trees}
In 1550, Istanbul is the capital of the Ottoman Empire. It is a huge empire, stretching from Hungary in Europe to Iraq in Asia, and from the Crimea to Egypt and Algeria in Africa. And Istanbul is its beating heart. About half a million people live here. That makes it one of the biggest cities in the world, much bigger than London or Paris at the time.

[pause]

The city is built on seven hills, like ancient Rome. On the hills, there are great mosques, palaces and gardens. Below them, thousands and thousands of wooden houses crowd together along narrow, winding streets. Tall dark cypress trees grow everywhere, in the gardens and the graveyards. And on three sides, the city is surrounded by water, blue and sparkling.

[pause]

{img:boy-thirteen-ottoman-clothes-looking-at-mosque-construction-scaffolding}
Tonight, we will spend some time in this great city, with an ordinary family. The father is a stonemason, and the son, a boy of thirteen called Mehmet, is learning the same trade. They are working on the building of a new mosque. It will become one of the most beautiful buildings in the world. And it is being designed by the greatest architect of his time, a man called Sinan.

[long-pause]

We will walk through the Grand Bazaar, with its thousands of little shops. We will take a boat across the Golden Horn. We will visit a steam bath, a hammam, and sit in a garden full of tulips. We will see the old church of Hagia Sophia, and the palace of the sultan. And we will meet the neighbours of Mehmet's family, who come from many different peoples and faiths.

[pause]

{img:sultan-suleiman-procession-distance-horsemen-crowds-street-respectful}
We will also catch a glimpse of the sultan himself. His name is Suleiman. The Europeans call him Suleiman the Magnificent. His own people call him Suleiman the Lawgiver. He has ruled for thirty years, and he is the most powerful man in the world. But tonight, our story is mostly about ordinary people. So let us begin, on a spring morning in 1550, as the call to prayer drifts over the water, and the city of Istanbul wakes up.

[pause]

Down by the water, the fishermen are already pulling in their nets. The bakers have been up for hours, and the smell of fresh bread drifts through the streets. In the courtyards of the houses, people are washing their faces at the wells. Somewhere, a donkey brays. Somewhere, a baby cries and is quickly comforted. And slowly, the great city stretches and yawns, like a cat in the first sun.

[long-pause]

{img:ancient-byzantium-greek-town-on-hill-by-sea-boats-old-walls}
Istanbul is a very old city. More than two and a half thousand years ago, Greek sailors founded a small town here, on a hill by the sea. They called it Byzantium. It was in a perfect place. It stood on the narrow channel of water between the Black Sea and the Mediterranean, called the Bosphorus. Every ship sailing between the two seas had to pass right by it.

[long-pause]

About a thousand years later, the Roman emperor Constantine chose this town to be his new capital. He made it much bigger and more beautiful. He built walls, palaces, churches and a great racetrack for chariots. And he gave the city a new name, after himself. Constantinople, the city of Constantine. For more than a thousand years, it was the capital of the eastern Roman Empire, which we call the Byzantine Empire.

[pause]

Constantinople was the richest and most splendid city in the Christian world. Its most famous building was a huge church, called Hagia Sophia, which means Holy Wisdom. It had the biggest dome in the world. People came from far away to see it. Travellers wrote that when they stood inside it, they did not know whether they were in heaven or on earth.

[pause]

{img:theodosian-walls-istanbul-massive-towers-moat-grass-afternoon}
The city was protected by enormous walls on the land side. They were very thick and tall, with a moat in front, and many towers. For hundreds of years, no army could break through them. But slowly, the Byzantine Empire grew weaker and smaller. And a new power was rising in the east. The Ottoman Turks.

[pause]

In the year 1453, the young Ottoman sultan, Mehmed the Second, came with a great army. He was only twenty-one years old. He had huge cannons made, the biggest the world had ever seen. And after a long siege, his army entered the city. We will not follow the battle. It was the end of the Byzantine Empire, after more than a thousand years. Sultan Mehmed became known as Mehmed the Conqueror.

[long-pause]

{img:mehmed-conqueror-rebuilding-city-builders-new-mosque-market-1460s}
Sultan Mehmed made Constantinople his new capital. He wanted it to be the greatest city in the world again. Many people had left or had been lost during the siege. So he invited people to come and live there, from all over his empire. Turks, Greeks, Armenians, Jews and many others. He built a new palace, mosques, markets, schools and hospitals. And slowly, the city filled up with people again.

[pause]

The city now had several names. The Ottomans often still called it Kostantiniyye, from the old name Constantinople. But ordinary people also called it Istanbul. The name probably comes from a Greek phrase, meaning to the city, or in the city. Because for everyone who lived around it, this was simply the city, the only one that mattered.

[pause]

{img:istanbul-1550-skyline-new-mosques-domes-bosphorus-ships-sunny}
By 1550, about a hundred years later, the city has grown bigger than ever before. Sultan Suleiman rules over a huge empire. Every year, ships arrive in the harbour full of goods from all over the world. Silk from Persia, spices from India, dates from Arabia, grain from Egypt, furs from Russia. The markets are full. The streets are crowded. And all over the city, new mosques, fountains, schools and bridges are being built.

[long-pause]

It is a time of great confidence and great beauty. The Ottomans call it their golden age. Poets write poems, painters paint tiny, detailed pictures in books, and architects build buildings that will stand for hundreds of years. And on one of the hills of the city, right now, the greatest of these buildings is beginning to rise. That is where Mehmet and his father go to work every morning.

[pause]

The old Roman racetrack, the Hippodrome, is still there in 1550. It has become a large open square, where people gather for festivals and celebrations. In the middle of it, there still stands an ancient stone obelisk from Egypt, covered with strange carved pictures, and a twisted bronze column shaped like three snakes. Children play around them, and old men sit in the shade and watch the world go by.

[long-pause]

{img:ottoman-wooden-house-overhanging-upper-floor-lattice-windows-narrow-street}
Mehmet lives with his family in a neighbourhood near the Golden Horn, not far from the new mosque. In Istanbul, a neighbourhood is called a mahalle. Each mahalle is like a small village inside the big city. It has its own little mosque, its own fountain, a bakery, a grocer and a little shop that sells sweet drinks called sherbet. Everyone knows everyone else.

[long-pause]

The house of Mehmet's family is made of wood, like most houses in Istanbul. It has two floors. The upper floor sticks out over the street, supported by wooden beams. This gives a little more room inside, and makes the narrow streets shady and cool. The windows of the upper floor are covered with wooden lattices, so that people inside can look out, but people outside cannot look in.

[pause]

Downstairs, there is a little courtyard with a fig tree and a well. There is a kitchen with a fireplace, and a storeroom. Upstairs, there are the living rooms. They are simple, but comfortable. There are no chairs or beds, the way we have them. Instead, along the walls, there are low benches with cushions, called sofas. During the day, the family sits on them. At night, mattresses and quilts are taken out of big cupboards, and spread out on the floor to sleep.

[pause]

{img:ottoman-family-room-low-sofa-cushions-carpet-brazier-window-light}
The father of the family is called Hasan. He is a stonemason, a man who cuts and shapes stone for buildings. He is about forty years old, with a dark beard and strong, rough hands. He came to Istanbul as a young man from a village in Anatolia. He has worked on many buildings in the city. Now he works on the biggest one of all, the new mosque of Sultan Suleiman.

[pause]

The mother is called Ayşe. She runs the house, and she is also a very good embroiderer. She sews beautiful flowers onto cloth, with silk thread in many colours. She sells her embroidery to a merchant in the bazaar. With the money, she buys things for the house, and saves a little for her daughter's future. She is a calm, cheerful woman, who sings while she works.

[long-pause]

{img:mother-embroidering-silk-flowers-cushion-window-lattice-light}
There is also a grandmother, Hasan's mother, who lives with them. Everyone calls her Nine, which means granny in Turkish. She is very old and very wise. She knows all the old stories, and all the remedies for coughs and colds. She spends most of the day sitting on the sofa by the window, watching the street, and knows everything that happens in the mahalle before anyone else.

[pause]

And there are the two children. Mehmet is thirteen. He is thin and quick, with curious brown eyes. For a year, he has been working with his father on the building site, learning to be a stonemason. And there is his little sister, Fatma, who is nine. She is learning embroidery from her mother, and reading from the teacher at the neighbourhood school.

[pause]

{img:boy-and-little-sister-courtyard-fig-tree-well-morning-ottoman}
Mehmet and Fatma are good friends, although they sometimes argue. In the evenings, Mehmet tells Fatma about the building site. About the huge stones, the cranes and the hundreds of workers. Fatma wants to see it, but girls do not go to building sites. So Mehmet draws pictures for her in the dust of the courtyard, with a stick. He draws the domes and the minarets, as he imagines they will look when the mosque is finished.

[long-pause]

In the mahalle, life is busy and friendly. The neighbours greet each other in the street, and ask about each other's families. When someone is sick, the neighbours bring soup. When someone has a baby, the whole street celebrates. When someone dies, everyone comes to help. In the evenings, the men sit together in front of the little mosque, and the women visit each other's houses. And the children play in the streets until it gets dark.

[pause]

On cold winter evenings, the family gathers around a brazier, a big copper bowl full of glowing charcoal. They put a low table over it, and cover the table with a thick quilt. Then everyone sits around it, with their legs under the warm quilt. Nine tells stories, Ayşe sews, and the children crack walnuts. Outside, the wind howls over the Golden Horn. But under the quilt, everyone is warm and cosy.

[long-pause]

{img:ottoman-man-washing-at-street-fountain-dawn-marble-carved-tap}
The day begins very early in Istanbul, with the first call to prayer, before sunrise. Mehmet's father gets up first. He goes down to the courtyard and washes his face, hands, arms and feet with cold water from the well. This washing is part of the prayer. Then he spreads a small rug on the floor of the living room, facing toward Mecca, and he prays.

[long-pause]

Water is very important in Istanbul. Islam teaches that people should be clean before they pray, five times a day. So the city needs a great deal of water. It is brought from the forests and hills outside the city, through long channels and aqueducts, some of them built by the Romans. And all over the city, there are public fountains, where anyone can come and fill their jugs, or wash, or simply drink.

[pause]

The fountain in Mehmet's mahalle is a little stone building in the middle of the street. It has a carved marble front, with a brass tap and a stone basin. Above it, there is a poem carved in beautiful letters, saying who paid for the fountain. Rich people often build fountains as a good deed, so that everyone can have water. Every morning, the women and children of the mahalle come here with their jugs, and stop to talk.

[pause]

{img:breakfast-ottoman-family-low-round-table-bread-cheese-olives-honey}
After the prayer, the family has breakfast together. They sit on cushions on the floor, around a low, round table. There is fresh bread from the bakery, still warm. There is white cheese, black olives, honey and jam made from rose petals. There is also a soup, made from lentils or yoghurt. There is no tea or coffee yet. The family drinks water, or a little milk.

[pause]

Fatma goes to the bakery every morning to fetch the bread. The baker is an old man with flour in his beard. He always gives her a little extra piece of bread, sprinkled with sesame seeds, to eat on the way home. Sometimes she shares it with the stray cats that sleep in front of the bakery. Istanbul is full of cats. People love them and feed them, and the cats are very well looked after.

[long-pause]

{img:stray-cats-sleeping-bakery-doorstep-sunlight-ottoman-street}
After breakfast, Mehmet and his father set off for work. They walk up the hill, through the narrow streets. The city is waking up. Shopkeepers are opening their shutters. Porters are carrying huge loads on their backs, bent almost double. Street sellers are calling out. Water carriers walk by, with leather bags full of water on their backs, selling cups of water to thirsty people.

[pause]

The streets are crowded with all kinds of people. There are Turks in long robes and white turbans. There are Greek fishermen, and Armenian merchants, and Jewish doctors with their special hats. There are soldiers, and scholars with books under their arms. Everyone wears clothes that show who they are. The colour of the turban, the shape of the hat, the colour of the shoes. You could tell a lot about a person just by looking at their clothes.

[pause]

{img:crowded-istanbul-street-1550-porters-merchants-turbans-hats-colours}
Back at home, the women begin their work. Ayşe cleans the house and prepares the food for the day. Nine sits by the window and sorts lentils, picking out the little stones. Fatma goes to the neighbourhood school, near the mosque, where she learns to read the Quran and to write. The teacher is an old man, kind but strict. The children sit on the floor in a circle around him, and repeat the words after him, in a soft singsong.

[long-pause]

Then the sun climbs higher over the city. The mist has gone from the water. The domes of the mosques shine in the sunlight. And from the building site on the hill, there comes the sound of hundreds of hammers and chisels, ringing on stone, like the sound of a thousand small bells.

[pause]

On the way up the hill, Mehmet and his father always pass the same old man. He sits on a little stool by the corner, with a tray of roasted chickpeas and dried mulberries. Every morning, he greets them by name, and asks about the mosque. Every morning, Mehmet's father gives him a small coin, and the old man gives Mehmet a handful of chickpeas. Mehmet eats them one by one, all the way to work.

[long-pause]

{img:suleymaniye-construction-site-1550-scaffolding-stone-walls-cranes-workers}
The building site is on top of the third hill of Istanbul, high above the Golden Horn. It is enormous. Until recently, there was an old palace here, with gardens. Now everything has been cleared away. And in its place, a huge mosque is being built, for Sultan Suleiman. It will be called the Süleymaniye, the mosque of Suleiman.

[long-pause]

But it is not just a mosque. Around the mosque, there will be a whole group of buildings. Schools for young scholars. A school of medicine and a hospital. A kitchen that will feed the poor every day. A guest house for travellers. A bath house. Shops. And a quiet garden with the tombs of the sultan and his wife. It is like a small town of its own, all built around the mosque.

[pause]

Thousands of people work on the building site. There are stonemasons, like Mehmet's father. There are carpenters, blacksmiths, lead workers and glass makers. There are labourers who carry stones and dig foundations. Some of them are skilled craftsmen, who are paid well. Others are ordinary workers, and soldiers who help with the heavy work. About half of the workers are Christians, from the Greek and Armenian communities, and the rest are Muslims.

[pause]

{img:stonemasons-carving-blocks-chisels-dust-sunlight-ottoman-workshop}
Mehmet works with his father in the stonemasons' area. They work under a simple wooden roof, in the shade. All day long, they cut and shape blocks of stone. The stone comes from quarries near the Sea of Marmara, and is brought to the city by ship. Each block must be cut exactly to the right size and shape, according to the drawings of the architect. If a block is even a finger too big, it will not fit.

[pause]

Mehmet does not cut big blocks yet. He is still an apprentice. He sweeps away the dust, carries the tools and sharpens the chisels. He watches his father and the other masons carefully, to learn how they work. And sometimes, his father lets him try. He gives him a small stone and a chisel, and shows him how to hold it, and how hard to strike. Little by little, Mehmet is learning.

[long-pause]

{img:wooden-crane-lifting-large-stone-block-ropes-pulleys-workers-pulling}
The most exciting thing on the building site is the lifting of the stones. The walls of the mosque are already high. The heavy blocks must be lifted up to the top. For this, there are big wooden cranes, with ropes and pulleys. The workers turn great wooden wheels, or pull on the ropes, and the stones rise slowly into the air. Mehmet loves to watch the stones go up. It seems like magic.

[pause]

The building of the mosque will take seven years. It is very carefully organised. There are lists of every worker, every tool and every stone. Clerks write down everything in big books. How many workers came today. How much they were paid. How many stones arrived from the quarry. These books still exist today. That is why we know so much about how the mosque was built.

[pause]

{img:clerks-writing-ledgers-building-site-ottoman-tent-scrolls}
At midday, the workers stop to pray. Then they eat their lunch, sitting in the shade. Mehmet's mother has packed bread, cheese and olives for them, and a few dried apricots. Mehmet and his father sit with the other masons. Some of them are Greek, some are Armenian. They talk and joke in a mixture of Turkish and Greek. Mehmet has learned quite a few Greek words this way.

[long-pause]

In the afternoon, the work goes on until the late call to prayer. Then the workers put down their tools. Mehmet and his father walk home down the hill, tired and covered in white stone dust. At the fountain in the mahalle, they wash their faces and hands. And Mehmet looks back up at the hill, where the half-built walls of the great mosque are glowing pink and gold in the evening sun.

[pause]

Some of the stone for the mosque comes from very far away. There are great columns of granite and marble, taken from old Roman and Byzantine buildings in other cities. One of them comes from the ancient city of Alexandria, in Egypt. Another, people say, once stood in the old palace of the Byzantine emperors. They are brought by ship, and dragged up the hill on wooden rollers, by hundreds of men pulling on ropes, singing to keep the rhythm.

[long-pause]

{img:mimar-sinan-elderly-architect-white-beard-turban-holding-drawings}
Every few days, a special visitor comes to the building site. He is an old man, about sixty years old, with a white beard and a large white turban. He walks slowly around the site, looking at everything. He talks to the master builders, looks at drawings, touches the stones and asks questions. When he comes, everyone works a little harder. This is Sinan, the chief architect of the sultan.

[long-pause]

Sinan has had a remarkable life. He was born in a small village in Anatolia, in a Christian family, probably Greek or Armenian. When he was a young man, he was taken into the service of the sultan, as was the custom then. He became a Muslim, and joined the Janissaries, the sultan's famous soldiers. He learned to be a carpenter and a builder in the army.

[pause]

As a soldier, Sinan travelled all over the empire, and beyond. He saw many famous buildings, in many lands. He built bridges for the army to cross rivers, and ships to cross lakes. Everyone noticed how clever he was. And when he was about fifty years old, Sultan Suleiman made him the chief architect of the whole empire. It was the most important building job in the world.

[pause]

{img:sinan-studying-plans-dome-drawing-workshop-assistants-lamplight}
Sinan has already built many mosques, schools, bridges and baths. But the Süleymaniye is his greatest project so far. He wants it to be the most beautiful mosque in Istanbul. He has studied the old church of Hagia Sophia very carefully, with its huge dome. And he wants to build a dome just as wonderful, but in his own way.

[pause]

Sinan is very careful about every detail. He thinks about the light, and how it will fall through the many windows. He thinks about the sound, and how the voice of the imam will be heard in every corner. He even thinks about the smoke of the thousands of oil lamps that will light the mosque at night. He designs a special little room above the entrance, where the smoke is collected. Then the soot is used to make ink for writing.

[long-pause]

{img:architect-talking-with-old-stonemason-and-boy-building-site-respect}
One day, Sinan stops by the stonemasons' workshop. He looks at a carved stone that Mehmet's father has just finished. He runs his fingers over the edges. Then he nods and says that it is good work. Mehmet's father bows deeply. He is very proud. Then Sinan turns to Mehmet, who is standing beside his father, covered in dust. He asks the boy how long he has been learning.

[pause]

Mehmet is so nervous he can hardly speak. He says that he has been learning for one year. Sinan smiles. He says that he himself started as a carpenter, many, many years ago. He tells Mehmet to watch carefully, to be patient and to always do his best work, even on the stones that nobody will ever see. Because God sees them. Then he walks on.

[pause]

{img:old-man-walking-away-through-building-site-evening-light-dome-rising}
Mehmet never forgets those words. That evening, he tells his whole family about it, three times. Nine says that it is a sign of good fortune. Fatma wants to know exactly what Sinan looks like, and what his voice sounds like, and how big his turban is. And Mehmet's father sits quietly on the sofa, smiling to himself.

[long-pause]

Sinan will live to be about ninety years old. In his long life, he will design more than three hundred buildings. Mosques, schools, bridges, aqueducts, baths, palaces and fountains, all over the Ottoman Empire. Many of them still stand today. And he will be remembered as one of the greatest architects who ever lived.

[long-pause]

{img:grand-bazaar-istanbul-1550-vaulted-domed-halls-shops-lamps-crowds}
On Fridays, there is no work on the building site. Friday is the holy day of the week for Muslims, when the men go to the big mosques for the noon prayer. And on one Friday afternoon, after the prayer, Mehmet's mother takes the children to the Grand Bazaar. She has some embroidery to sell. And Fatma has been begging to go for weeks.

[long-pause]

The Grand Bazaar is one of the biggest covered markets in the world. In the middle of it, there are two great stone halls, with many small domes on top. They were built by Sultan Mehmed the Conqueror, almost a hundred years ago. Inside these halls, the most precious things are sold. Jewels, gold, silver, fine weapons and rich fabrics. At night, the heavy iron doors are locked, and guards watch over the treasures.

[pause]

Around the stone halls, there are hundreds of little streets with shops. Many of them are covered with wooden roofs, to keep out the sun and rain. Each street has its own trade. There is a street of the slipper makers, a street of the jewellers, a street of the carpet sellers, a street of the copper workers. If you want to buy something, you go to its street, and you can compare the shops and their prices.

[pause]

{img:bazaar-street-slipper-makers-colourful-leather-shoes-hanging-shopkeeper}
Ayşe and the children walk through the crowded streets. It is noisy and colourful and full of wonderful smells. Shopkeepers sit cross-legged in their little shops, which are raised up from the street, like little stages. They call out to the people walking by, inviting them to come and look. Some of them offer a glass of sherbet to good customers.

[pause]

They pass the street of the slipper makers, where hundreds of soft leather slippers hang in rows, in red, yellow, blue and green. Each colour has a meaning. Muslims wear yellow slippers, Greeks wear black ones, Armenians purple and Jews blue. Fatma wants a pair of red slippers with little pointed toes. Her mother smiles and says, perhaps, if the embroidery sells well.

[long-pause]

{img:merchant-examining-silk-embroidery-mother-daughter-bazaar-shop}
They go to the shop of the merchant who buys Ayşe's embroidery. He is an Armenian, an old friend of the family, called Bedros. He is a kind man with a big grey moustache. He unfolds the embroidery carefully on his counter, and looks at it for a long time. Then he says it is the best work he has seen this year. He pays Ayşe a good price. And he gives Fatma a little bag of sweets made from sugar and almonds.

[pause]

With the money, Ayşe buys a few things for the house. A new copper pot, some cotton cloth and a little packet of spices. And, yes, a pair of red slippers for Fatma, with little pointed toes. Fatma is so happy that she wants to wear them at once. But her mother says that they are for special days. So Fatma carries them home in her arms, holding them tightly.

[pause]

{img:spice-seller-baskets-pepper-cinnamon-saffron-colourful-heaps-bazaar}
On the way out, they pass the spice sellers. Their shops are full of baskets and sacks of spices, piled up in coloured heaps. Red pepper, yellow saffron, brown cinnamon and black pepper, cloves and cumin and nutmeg. The smell is so strong that Mehmet sneezes three times. The spices come from India, Persia, Arabia and the islands of the East. They have travelled for months to get here, by ship and by camel caravan.

[long-pause]

At the edge of the bazaar, there are also the big stone buildings called hans. These are like warehouses and hotels together. Merchants from faraway countries stay in them, on the upper floor, with their goods stored below. Their camels and horses rest in the courtyard. In the evening, the merchants sit together and talk in many languages. Persian, Arabic, Greek, Italian, Armenian. They talk about trade, and prices, and the news from distant lands.

[pause]

In the middle of the bazaar, there is a little fountain, where people stop to drink and rest. Ayşe and the children sit on the stone edge for a while, and watch the crowds go by. A porter walks past with a whole wardrobe on his back. A man leads a tiny monkey on a string. An old woman bargains loudly for a carpet, and wins. Fatma says the bazaar is better than any story.

[long-pause]

{img:golden-horn-1550-caiques-rowing-boats-ships-galata-tower-across-water}
Istanbul is a city of water. And the most important water of all is the Golden Horn. It is a long, narrow bay that curves into the land, like the horn of an animal. On one side of it lies the old city, with the mosques, the palace and the bazaar. On the other side lies the district of Galata. There is no bridge across the Golden Horn. So everyone crosses it by boat.

[long-pause]

On the shore, there are many little landing places, with wooden steps going down to the water. And there, waiting for passengers, are the boatmen with their caiques. A caique is a long, slender rowing boat, painted in bright colours, with a pointed front. The boatmen sit in the middle and row with long oars. Passengers sit on cushions at the back. There are thousands of caiques in Istanbul, crossing the water all day long.

[pause]

One Sunday, Mehmet's father takes him across the Golden Horn to Galata. They want to visit an old friend of his father, a Greek stonemason who lives there. They go down to the landing place and climb into a caique. The boatman is a big man with strong arms. He pushes away from the shore, and begins to row with long, smooth strokes.

[pause]

{img:boy-and-father-sitting-in-caique-cushions-boatman-rowing-golden-horn}
Out on the water, Mehmet looks around him with wide eyes. The Golden Horn is full of boats. Big merchant ships, with tall masts and furled sails. Fishing boats, coming home with their catch. Barges loaded with wood, stone and grain. And the slender caiques darting in between, like water beetles. Behind them, the old city rises up the hills, with its domes and minarets. And there, on the third hill, he can see the great walls of the new mosque, surrounded by scaffolding.

[pause]

Further along the Golden Horn, there is the great shipyard of the sultan, called the Arsenal. Here, the warships of the Ottoman navy are built. There are dozens of covered slipways, where new galleys are being built. Thousands of workers hammer and saw. The Ottoman navy is the strongest in the Mediterranean. Its great admiral, Barbarossa, became famous all over Europe, before he passed away a few years ago.

[long-pause]

{img:galata-tower-genoese-stone-round-tower-houses-hill-1550-afternoon}
Galata is a very different place from the old city. For hundreds of years, it was a colony of merchants from the Italian city of Genoa. They built a tall, round stone tower on the hill, which can be seen from everywhere. It is called the Galata Tower. Today, many Europeans live here. Merchants from Venice, Genoa, France and other countries. There are also Greeks, Jews and Armenians. There are churches and synagogues, as well as mosques.

[pause]

The streets of Galata are steep and narrow. Some of them are made of stairs. Mehmet hears people speaking Italian, French and many other languages. He sees men in tight European clothes and big hats, with short beards and swords at their sides. Women walk in the streets with uncovered heads. It seems very strange to him. It is almost like visiting another country.

[pause]

{img:greek-house-galata-family-welcoming-guests-courtyard-vine-table}
The Greek friend of his father is called Nikolaos. He lives in a little house with a vine growing over the courtyard. His wife brings out a plate of fried fish, bread and olives, and a bowl of cherries. The two men talk about their work and their families. Nikolaos has a son of Mehmet's age, called Yannis. The boys go out to explore the streets of Galata together, while the men talk.

[long-pause]

In the late afternoon, Mehmet and his father take a caique back across the Golden Horn. The sun is setting behind the old city. The domes and minarets are black against the orange sky. The water is golden. Perhaps that is why it is called the Golden Horn, Mehmet thinks. His father says that nobody knows for sure. But tonight, it certainly looks like gold.

[pause]

Mehmet and Yannis climb up the steep streets to the foot of the Galata Tower. It is much taller than it looked from across the water. A guard stands at the door, and does not let them in. But from the hill, they can see the whole Golden Horn spread out below them, with the old city on the other side. Yannis points out his favourite fishing places. Mehmet points out the new mosque. They both agree that their own side of the water is the best.

[long-pause]

{img:ottoman-hammam-dome-star-shaped-skylights-steam-marble-light-beams}
Once a week, Mehmet's family goes to the hammam. A hammam is a public steam bath. There are hundreds of them in Istanbul. Almost every mahalle has one. Very few houses have their own bath, so everyone goes to the hammam to get clean. But it is much more than a place to wash. It is also a place to relax, to meet friends and to talk.

[long-pause]

Men and women go to the hammam at different times, or to different parts of the building. In the mornings, the hammam is for men. In the afternoons, it is for women. Or sometimes, a big hammam has two separate halves, one for men and one for women, each with its own entrance.

[pause]

Mehmet goes with his father, early on a Friday morning. First, they enter a large, cool hall with a high dome. In the middle, there is a marble fountain, splashing quietly. Around the walls, there are raised platforms with cushions. Here, the men take off their clothes, wrap a cotton cloth around their waist, and put on wooden sandals, called nalin. The sandals are very high, so that their feet do not get wet on the floor.

[pause]

{img:hammam-cool-entrance-hall-fountain-wooden-platforms-towels-quiet}
Then they walk through a warm room, into the hot room. The hot room is the heart of the hammam. It is a big room made of marble, with a dome on top. In the dome, there are little round holes, covered with glass. The sunlight shines through them in thin beams, like stars, into the steamy room. The air is very warm and damp. The floor is heated from below by a fire.

[pause]

In the middle of the hot room, there is a large, raised marble platform. It is warm, almost hot. People lie down on it, and let the heat soak into their bodies. They sweat, and their muscles relax. Mehmet lies on his back and looks up at the little stars of light in the dome. After a long week of carrying stones, it feels wonderful.

[long-pause]

{img:bath-attendant-scrubbing-with-rough-mitten-marble-platform-steam}
After a while, a bath attendant comes. He is a strong man with a rough cloth mitten on his hand. He scrubs Mehmet's back and arms and legs, very hard. Little grey rolls of old skin and dirt come off. Mehmet laughs, because it tickles. Then the attendant covers him with a great cloud of soap foam, made by blowing into a cloth bag full of soapy water. And then he rinses him with bowls of warm water, poured from a marble basin.

[pause]

Around the walls of the hot room, there are small marble basins, with taps for hot and cold water. People sit beside them and pour water over themselves with metal bowls. Men talk and laugh. Some of them sing a little. The sounds echo softly under the dome. It is one of the most peaceful places in the whole busy city.

[pause]

{img:women-hammam-afternoon-talking-laughing-copper-bowls-steam-soft-light}
In the afternoon, it is the women's turn. For the women of Istanbul, the hammam is one of the most important places in their lives. It is where they meet their friends and neighbours, away from the men. Ayşe, Nine and Fatma go together, with a bundle of towels, soap, combs and food. They stay for hours. They wash and relax, and then they sit together in the cool hall, eating and talking.

[long-pause]

The women talk about everything. Who is getting married, who has had a baby, who is ill. They share recipes and remedies. Mothers look at the young girls, to find a good wife for their sons. Sometimes there are special parties at the hammam, for a bride before her wedding, or for a new mother. There is music and singing and delicious food. Fatma loves going to the hammam. She says it is better than the bazaar.

[pause]

After the bath, everyone feels clean, warm and sleepy. They drink a cool glass of sherbet in the entrance hall, made from fruit juice and sugar, with a little rose water. Then they walk slowly home, through the evening streets, with rosy faces and wet hair. That night, everyone in the family sleeps very well.

[pause]

The hammam is also a place where news is shared. Men talk about the price of bread, about the sultan's latest campaign, about a ship that has arrived from Egypt. The barber, who also works at the hammam, knows more news than anyone. While he shaves the men's heads and trims their beards, he tells them everything that is happening in the city. Mehmet's father says that the barber knows the news even before the sultan does.

[long-pause]

{img:hagia-sophia-1550-exterior-great-dome-buttresses-minarets-morning}
In the old heart of the city, on the first hill, there stands the most famous building of Istanbul. It is Hagia Sophia. It was built more than a thousand years ago, by the Byzantine emperor Justinian, as a Christian church. For almost a thousand years, it was the greatest church in the world. And when the Ottomans came in 1453, Sultan Mehmed turned it into a mosque.

[long-pause]

From the outside, Hagia Sophia looks heavy and strong. It is a huge building of brick and stone, painted a soft reddish colour. Over the centuries, big supporting walls have been added to the sides, to stop it from falling down in earthquakes. The Ottomans have added minarets, tall and thin, at the corners. And over everything rises the great dome, wide and low, like a sky made of stone.

[pause]

One Friday, Mehmet's father takes him to the noon prayer at Hagia Sophia. He wants his son to see it, because it is the building that every architect in Istanbul studies. Even Sinan. They walk through a courtyard and through great bronze doors. And then they step inside.

[pause]

{img:hagia-sophia-interior-vast-dome-light-windows-floating-people-small}
Mehmet stops and stares. He has never seen anything like it. The inside of Hagia Sophia is one enormous space. And high above, the great dome seems to float in the air. Around its bottom edge, there is a ring of forty windows. The light shines through them so brightly that the dome seems to be resting on a circle of light, not on stone. People say it looks like it hangs from heaven on a golden chain.

[pause]

The walls are covered with marble of many colours. Green, red, white, grey and purple, with wavy patterns like water. The columns are made of polished stone, some of them brought from ancient temples in faraway lands. High up on the walls and in the dome, there are mosaics, made of tiny pieces of gold glass. Some of them still show the old Christian pictures, and the Ottomans have left many of them in place, glittering softly in the light.

[long-pause]

{img:men-praying-rows-hagia-sophia-carpets-sunlight-beams-quiet}
The floor is covered with carpets. Thousands of men are kneeling on them in long, straight rows, all facing the same direction, toward Mecca. Because the old church was built facing east, and Mecca lies to the southeast, the rows of prayer are at a slight angle to the building. Mehmet and his father find a place in a row and kneel down. When the imam begins the prayer, thousands of men stand up, bow and kneel together, as one.

[pause]

The sound is extraordinary. The voice of the imam floats up into the dome and comes back down, softly, from everywhere at once. When the thousands of men say amen together, the sound rolls around the building like gentle thunder. Mehmet feels very small. But he also feels very calm, and very safe.

[pause]

{img:boy-looking-up-at-dome-hagia-sophia-golden-light-awe-father-beside}
After the prayer, they stay for a while. Mehmet's father points out the details of the building to him. Look at how the big dome rests on four great arches. Look at the half domes, which help to hold it up. Look at the windows, how they make the stone seem light. Every architect in Istanbul has tried to understand this building. And now, Sinan is trying to build something just as great.

[long-pause]

As they walk out, Mehmet looks back at the great dome one last time. He thinks of the people who built it, a thousand years ago. Stonemasons like his father, and boys like him, carrying tools and sweeping dust. Their names are forgotten. But their work is still here, still standing, still beautiful. He thinks of what Sinan said. Do your best work, even on the stones that nobody will see.

[pause]

Outside, in the square, the pigeons are pecking at crumbs on the warm stones. Mehmet's father buys them each a cup of cool water from a water seller. They sit for a while on the steps of a fountain, in the shade, and watch the people come and go. Then they walk home slowly, through the old streets, talking about domes and arches all the way.

[long-pause]

{img:topkapi-palace-gate-of-salutation-twin-towers-guards-1550-morning}
At the very tip of the old city, on a hill above the sea, there lies the palace of the sultan. Today, we call it the Topkapı Palace. In 1550, people simply call it the New Palace. It was built by Sultan Mehmed the Conqueror, about eighty years before. And it is the place from which the whole great empire is ruled.

[long-pause]

The palace is not one big building, like the palaces of Europe. It is more like a series of gardens and courtyards, one behind the other, with small buildings, pavilions and kiosks in between. A high wall surrounds the whole palace. The further inside you go, the more private and the more sacred it becomes. Only very few people ever reach the innermost parts.

[pause]

The first courtyard is open to everyone. It is a big, park-like space, with trees and grass. Here, there is a bakery, a hospital, a mint where coins are made, and stores of firewood. People come here to deliver things, or to present a request to the government. Mehmet has been here once, with his father, to deliver some carved stones for a palace fountain.

[pause]

{img:topkapi-second-courtyard-cypress-avenues-gazelles-janissaries-silent}
From the first courtyard, a great gate leads into the second courtyard. The gate has two tall towers, with pointed roofs, like a castle in a fairy tale. Only people with special business may go through. Inside, the second courtyard is a beautiful garden, with paths lined by tall cypress trees. Gazelles and peacocks wander on the grass. And the most surprising thing about it is the silence.

[pause]

In the second courtyard, everyone must be quiet. Even when there are thousands of soldiers standing there, in rows, nobody speaks. People who work in the palace learn a special sign language, so they can communicate without words. Foreign visitors who came to the palace always wrote about this silence. They said it was more impressive than any noise.

[long-pause]

{img:palace-kitchens-chimneys-cooks-huge-copper-pots-steam-ottoman}
Along one side of the second courtyard, there are the palace kitchens. They are enormous. There are ten kitchens, each with its own domes and chimneys. Hundreds of cooks work here. Every day, they cook for thousands of people who live and work in the palace. There is a kitchen for sweets, a kitchen for the sultan, a kitchen for the soldiers, and many more. The smoke rises from the chimneys all day long.

[pause]

The cooks use huge copper pots, some of them big enough for a child to sit in. Every day, they cook rice, meat, vegetables, soups and sweet dishes. They use enormous amounts of food. Hundreds of sheep, thousands of chickens, mountains of rice and butter, honey and sugar. The palace is like a small city. And like a city, it needs a great deal of food.

[pause]

{img:imperial-council-hall-domed-viziers-meeting-golden-window-grille}
On the other side of the second courtyard, there is a small building with a tower. This is where the government of the empire meets, four days a week. The chief ministers, called viziers, sit on a long sofa and discuss the business of the empire. Wars, taxes, laws, and the problems of people from distant provinces. Above them, in the wall, there is a small window with a golden grille. Behind it, the sultan can sit and listen, without being seen.

[long-pause]

Beyond the second courtyard, there are the private parts of the palace, where the sultan lives with his family and servants. There are schools for the young men who will become the officials of the empire. There are beautiful pavilions, with tiled walls and views over the sea. And there are gardens full of flowers, especially tulips and roses. But ordinary people like Mehmet will never see these places. For them, the inner palace is a mystery, hidden behind high walls and silent gates.

[pause]

At night, the palace is guarded by soldiers who walk slowly along the walls, carrying lanterns. From the water, the boatmen can see their small lights moving in the darkness, high above the sea. In the gardens, the nightingales sing among the roses. And in the pavilions by the sea, the sultan sometimes sits late into the night, reading, writing poems or looking out over the dark water toward Asia.

[long-pause]

{img:sultan-suleiman-on-white-horse-friday-procession-janissaries-crowds}
Every Friday, the sultan rides out of the palace to pray at one of the great mosques of the city. This is the only time that most people in Istanbul ever see him. And so, on Friday mornings, crowds gather along the streets to watch him pass. One Friday, Mehmet and Fatma go with their father to watch.

[long-pause]

They stand in the crowd near the mosque, waiting. First come soldiers on foot, the Janissaries, in long blue robes and tall white felt hats, with a long piece of cloth hanging down the back. Then come officials of the palace, on horseback, in rich robes of silk. Then come the archers of the sultan's guard, with tall feathers on their helmets. Everything is very orderly and very quiet.

[pause]

And then, the sultan himself. He rides a white horse, with a saddle covered in gold and jewels. He wears a long robe of white silk, and a large white turban. He is about fifty-five years old. He is tall and thin, with a long neck, a thin face and a drooping moustache. He looks serious and tired. He does not smile. But as he passes, the people bow deeply, and some of them call out blessings.

[pause]

{img:people-handing-petitions-to-officials-procession-street-ottoman}
Along the way, some people hold up pieces of paper. They are petitions, written requests to the sultan. A widow asks for help. A farmer complains about an unfair tax. A man asks for justice in a dispute with his neighbour. Officials walk beside the sultan's horse, and collect the petitions. Later, they will be read, and many of them will be answered.

[pause]

Suleiman is called the Lawgiver by his own people. He has worked hard to make good laws for the whole empire. Laws about taxes, about trade, about land and about crimes. He wants the laws to be fair, and the same for everyone. Of course, he is also a powerful ruler, who leads great armies and makes war. But many of his subjects respect him, because they believe he is trying to be just.

[long-pause]

{img:hurrem-sultan-gardens-palace-portrait-calm-elegant-distance}
Suleiman has a wife, called Hürrem. She came to the palace many years ago, as a young girl from what is now Ukraine. She was very clever and lively, and the sultan fell deeply in love with her. He married her, which was very unusual for a sultan in those days. She is the most powerful woman in the empire. She has built a mosque, a school, a soup kitchen and a hospital for women, in the city.

[pause]

Suleiman and Hürrem write letters to each other when he is away at war. Some of these letters still exist today. They are full of love. Suleiman also writes poems. He writes under a different name, Muhibbi, which means the lover, or the beloved friend. He writes about love, about God, and about how short and precious life is.

[pause]

{img:sultan-writing-poetry-pavilion-garden-tulips-scroll-evening-light}
One of his poems has a line that people still quote in Turkey today. He writes that people think that wealth and power are the greatest things in the world. But in this world, there is nothing as precious as health. Everything else passes away, like the wind. It is a quiet, wise thought, for the most powerful man in the world.

[long-pause]

When the sultan has passed, the crowd slowly breaks up. Fatma is very excited. She has seen the sultan, with her own eyes. She wants to tell everyone at the fountain. Mehmet is thinking about something else. He is thinking that this tired-looking man on the white horse is the one who asked Sinan to build the great mosque. One day, when it is finished, the sultan will come and pray there. And somewhere in the walls, there will be stones that Mehmet helped to carve.

[pause]

Fatma tells everyone at the fountain the next morning, just as she planned. She describes the white horse, the golden saddle, the tall turban and the serious face. Each time she tells the story, the horse gets a little whiter and the turban a little taller. The other girls listen with open mouths. Nine, sitting at her window, hears every word, and smiles quietly to herself.

[long-pause]

{img:istanbul-neighbourhood-church-synagogue-mosque-near-each-other-1550}
Istanbul in 1550 is a city of many peoples. About half of the people are Muslims, mostly Turks. The other half are Christians and Jews. Greeks, Armenians, Jews, and also people from many other places. They speak many different languages. They go to different houses of prayer. And yet, they live together in the same city, mostly in peace.

[long-pause]

In the Ottoman Empire, Christians and Jews are allowed to keep their own faith. They have their own churches and synagogues, their own schools and their own leaders. They pay a special tax, and there are some rules that they must follow. For example, their houses may not be taller than those of Muslims, and they must wear clothes of certain colours. Life is not completely equal. But compared with many other countries at that time, it is quite tolerant.

[pause]

Mehmet's mahalle is mostly Muslim. But on the next street, there is an Armenian neighbourhood, with its own little church. And a little further, near the water, there is a Jewish neighbourhood. People from all these neighbourhoods meet every day, in the markets, in the streets and at work. They buy from each other, work together and sometimes become good friends.

[pause]

{img:jewish-doctor-1550-istanbul-tall-hat-visiting-family-medicine-bag}
The Jewish community of Istanbul has a special story. About sixty years ago, in 1492, the king and queen of Spain forced all the Jews to leave their country. Many of them had nowhere to go. But the Ottoman sultan of the time, Bayezid the Second, welcomed them to his empire. Thousands of Jewish families came to Istanbul. They brought their language, their skills and their knowledge with them.

[pause]

Many of these Spanish Jews are doctors, merchants and craftsmen. They brought the first printing press to Istanbul, and printed books in Hebrew. The doctor of Mehmet's family is one of them. He is called Doctor Moshe. He is a quiet, gentle man, with a long grey beard and a tall hat. When Nine has a bad cough in the winter, he comes to visit her. He gives her a syrup made of honey and herbs. And she always says that he is the best doctor in the whole city.

[long-pause]

{img:armenian-family-courtyard-easter-red-eggs-neighbours-visiting}
The family's Armenian neighbours are the family of Bedros, the merchant from the bazaar. They live just around the corner. At their great feast of Easter, they paint eggs red, and give some to Mehmet's family. At the end of Ramadan, Mehmet's family brings them plates of sweets. The children of both families play together in the street. And the mothers exchange recipes over the garden wall.

[pause]

The Greeks of Istanbul are the oldest inhabitants of the city. They were here long before the Ottomans came. Many of them are fishermen, sailors, merchants and craftsmen. Their leader is the Patriarch, the head of the Orthodox Church, who lives in a district called the Fener, on the Golden Horn. On their holy days, the Greek churches ring their bells, and their priests walk through the streets in processions, with candles and icons.

[pause]

{img:greek-fishermen-mending-nets-shore-golden-horn-evening-boats}
Of course, not everything is always peaceful. Sometimes there are arguments and quarrels between neighbours of different faiths, as there are between neighbours everywhere. Sometimes there are unfair rules, or officials who treat some people worse than others. But on the whole, the people of Istanbul are used to living with each other. They have done so for generations.

[long-pause]

In the evenings, from Mehmet's window, you can hear many sounds. The call to prayer from the minaret of the little mosque. The bells of the Armenian church. The singing of prayers from the synagogue, on Friday evenings. And the voices of mothers calling their children home, in Turkish, Greek, Armenian and Spanish. Nine says that it is like a garden, with many different flowers. Each one is different. But together, they make the garden beautiful.

[pause]

On the corner of their street, there is a little shop that belongs to an old Greek man, who sells olive oil, soap and candles. Everyone in the street buys from him. He knows exactly what each family needs, and he often lets poor families pay later. When he was ill last winter, the Muslim neighbours brought him soup every day, and the Armenian neighbours looked after his shop. When he was better, he gave every child in the street a little candle.

[long-pause]

{img:ottoman-kitchen-copper-pots-woman-cooking-pilaf-rice-steam-window}
The food of Istanbul is some of the best in the world. The Ottoman Empire is so big that food comes from everywhere. Rice and dates from Egypt. Wheat from the plains of Anatolia and the Balkans. Honey and butter from the Black Sea. Spices from India and Arabia. Fresh fish from the Bosphorus, and fruit and vegetables from the gardens around the city.

[long-pause]

The most important food of all is bread. Every mahalle has its own bakery, and people eat bread with every meal. The government controls the price and the weight of the bread very carefully, so that even the poorest people can afford it. Officials visit the bakeries to check the bread. If a baker cheats, by making his loaves too small, he is punished.

[pause]

In the street, there are sellers of all kinds of food. Men carry trays on their heads, piled high with round rings of bread, covered in sesame seeds. They are called simit. There are sellers of grilled meat on skewers, and of fried fish, and of stuffed mussels. There are sellers of helva, a sweet made of sesame or flour and sugar. And there are sellers of boza, a thick, slightly sour drink made from millet, which people drink in winter.

[pause]

{img:street-vendor-tray-on-head-sesame-bread-rings-children-buying}
In the evenings, Ayşe cooks dinner for the family. The most special dish is pilaf. It is rice, cooked in butter and broth, until every grain is separate and shining. Sometimes she adds chickpeas, or little pieces of meat, or pine nuts and currants. On ordinary days, the family eats simpler food. Bulgur wheat, lentil soup, vegetables cooked in olive oil, white cheese and yoghurt.

[pause]

Yoghurt is eaten with almost everything. It is made at home, from the milk of cows, sheep or goats. There is also a drink made from yoghurt and water, with a little salt, called ayran. It is very refreshing on hot summer days. And there are many kinds of stuffed vegetables. Peppers, aubergines, courgettes and vine leaves, filled with rice and herbs.

[long-pause]

{img:family-dinner-floor-round-tray-pilaf-bowls-lamp-evening-ottoman}
The family eats together, sitting on cushions on the floor, around a big round tray. Everyone eats from the same dishes, with spoons made of wood or horn, and with pieces of bread. Before eating, they wash their hands, and say a short prayer. After eating, they wash their hands again. It is polite to eat slowly and to talk quietly at the table.

[pause]

After dinner, there are sometimes sweets. Fruit, fresh or dried. Figs, apricots, grapes and cherries. Pudding made from rice and milk, sprinkled with cinnamon. Baklava, made of thin layers of pastry with nuts and honey, which is a very special treat for holidays. And sherbet, the sweet drink made from fruit juice, flowers and sugar, cooled with snow brought down from the mountains.

[pause]

{img:snow-carriers-mountain-ice-packed-straw-sherbet-seller-summer}
Yes, snow. In winter, snow is collected on the mountains near the city, and packed in straw in deep cellars. In summer, it is brought into the city and sold to cool drinks. A cool glass of cherry sherbet on a hot summer evening is one of the great pleasures of Istanbul.

[long-pause]

There is one drink that is not yet known in Mehmet's family. But it is coming. In a few years, in 1554, two men from Syria will open the first coffee houses in Istanbul. People will sit in them, drinking the dark, bitter drink from little cups, talking, playing games and listening to storytellers. Coffee will become so popular that it changes the life of the city forever. But tonight, in 1550, Mehmet's family drinks only water, and goes to bed early.

[pause]

Ayşe has a big wooden chest in the storeroom, where she keeps food for the winter. There are jars of pickled vegetables and dried beans, strings of dried peppers and aubergines, sacks of flour and bulgur, and pots of grape syrup. In the autumn, the whole family helps to prepare it all. They dry fruit on the roof in the sun, and cut vegetables in the courtyard, and Nine tells stories while they work.

[long-pause]

{img:ottoman-garden-tulips-roses-cypress-trees-marble-pool-kiosk-spring}
The people of Istanbul love gardens. Behind many houses, even small ones, there is a little garden, with a fig tree, a vine, a few rose bushes and some herbs. The rich have big gardens, with pools, fountains, fruit trees and wooden pavilions. And all around the city, along the Bosphorus and the Golden Horn, there are great gardens belonging to the sultan.

[long-pause]

For the Ottomans, a garden is a little piece of paradise. In the Quran, paradise is described as a beautiful garden, with flowing water, shady trees and fruit. So people try to make their gardens like that. They plant trees for shade. They make little channels and pools for water. And they plant flowers, many, many flowers.

[pause]

The favourite flowers of the Ottomans are roses, carnations, hyacinths and tulips. Especially tulips. The tulip grows wild in the mountains of Central Asia, where the ancestors of the Turks came from. The Ottomans love it so much that they grow hundreds of different kinds, with beautiful names. Morning star, rose of dawn, the one who makes hearts burn. They love tulips with long, thin, pointed petals, like little daggers.

[pause]

{img:single-ottoman-tulip-long-pointed-petals-red-glass-vase-window}
Tulips are everywhere in Istanbul. Not only in the gardens, but also in art. They are painted on tiles, woven into carpets, embroidered on clothes and carved in stone. Ayşe embroiders tulips on her silk work, with red and white thread. In the new mosque that Mehmet's father is building, there will be tiles with tulips, in blue, green and bright red.

[pause]

A few years from now, a traveller from Flanders will see the tulips in the gardens of Istanbul. He will take some bulbs home with him to Europe. And there, in the Netherlands, people will fall in love with tulips too. So much that, about eighty years later, tulip bulbs will be sold for more money than a house. But that is another story.

[long-pause]

{img:mahalle-garden-family-picnic-under-fig-tree-carpet-spread-spring}
In the spring, when the gardens are in flower, the people of Istanbul go out to enjoy them. On fine days, families go to the gardens and meadows outside the city, for picnics. They spread carpets on the grass under the trees. They bring food in baskets, and play music, and sing. The children run around and pick flowers. The old people sit in the shade and talk.

[pause]

One Friday in the spring, Mehmet's family goes to a place by the water, outside the walls of the city. It is called Kağıthane, the Sweet Waters of Europe. It is a green valley with a little river, where two streams flow into the Golden Horn. On spring days, hundreds of families come here, from all over the city. There are tents and carpets everywhere on the grass.

[pause]

{img:kagithane-sweet-waters-meadow-river-families-picnicking-caiques}
The family spreads its carpet under a big plane tree, beside the river. Ayşe has brought stuffed vine leaves, bread, cheese, cherries and a jar of sherbet. Nine sits on a cushion, wrapped in her shawl, and closes her eyes in the sun. Mehmet and Fatma run along the riverbank, watching the caiques go by. Their father lies on his back in the grass, with his hands behind his head. For once, he has nothing to do.

[long-pause]

In the afternoon, a man comes by with a little puppet theatre. It is called Karagöz. The puppets are flat figures made of coloured leather, and they are shown behind a white cloth, with a lamp behind them. The children sit in front of the cloth and watch the shadows move. Karagöz is a funny, simple man, who always gets into trouble. His friend Hacivat is clever and proud. They argue and play tricks on each other. Fatma laughs so much that she gets the hiccups.

[pause]

On the way home, as the sun is going down, Fatma falls asleep in her mother's arms in the caique. Mehmet trails his hand in the cool water and watches the city come closer. Nine says that it has been the best day of the whole spring. Their father agrees. He says that they must come again next year, when the tulips are in flower once more. And they do.

[long-pause]

{img:bosporus-strait-1550-green-hills-villages-wooden-houses-water-ships}
To the north of the city, there is the Bosphorus. It is a narrow strip of sea, about thirty kilometres long, between Europe and Asia. It connects the Black Sea in the north with the Sea of Marmara in the south. At its narrowest point, it is only about seven hundred metres wide. On one side is Europe, and on the other side is Asia. And you can see from one to the other.

[long-pause]

In 1550, the shores of the Bosphorus are green and quiet. There are small fishing villages, with wooden houses right by the water. There are gardens, orchards and woods of chestnut trees, oaks and pines. Here and there, there are the summer houses of rich families, and the gardens of the sultan. It is one of the most beautiful places in the world.

[pause]

In the summer, Nikolaos, the Greek friend from Galata, invites Mehmet's family for a day on the Bosphorus. He has a cousin who is a fisherman, with a boat. Early in the morning, they all climb into the boat. Mehmet, his father, his mother, Fatma, Nine, Nikolaos and his son Yannis. The cousin, an old fisherman with a brown, wrinkled face, rows them out onto the blue water.

[pause]

{img:fishing-boat-family-on-bosporus-morning-sparkling-water-dolphins}
The water of the Bosphorus is clear and blue, and it flows quite fast, from north to south. The boat moves along the European shore, past the villages and the gardens. The old fisherman knows every house, and every tree. He tells them stories about the people who live there. And then, suddenly, Fatma shouts and points. Dolphins! A group of dolphins is swimming beside the boat, jumping out of the water, shining in the sun.

[pause]

The Bosphorus is full of fish. Every year, great shoals of fish swim through it, from the Black Sea to the Mediterranean, and back again. Bonito, mackerel, bluefish, sardines. The fishermen catch them with nets, from boats and from wooden platforms along the shore. Fish is one of the most important foods of Istanbul. The fish markets are always busy.

[long-pause]

{img:old-castle-rumeli-hisari-walls-towers-bosporus-shore-afternoon}
Halfway up the Bosphorus, they pass a great castle, with tall walls and round towers. It is called Rumeli Hisarı, the castle of Europe. Sultan Mehmed the Conqueror built it in only four months, just before he took the city in 1453. On the other shore, on the Asian side, there is a smaller castle. Between them, the Bosphorus is at its narrowest. Now the castles are peaceful and quiet, and swallows nest in their walls.

[pause]

At midday, they stop at a little beach on the Asian shore. The fisherman catches some fish, and grills them on a fire on the sand. They eat them with bread, salad and lemon. The children swim in the shallow water. Nine sits under a tree and falls asleep. It is the first time she has been to Asia in thirty years, even though it is so close.

[pause]

{img:family-eating-grilled-fish-beach-asian-shore-fire-shade-tree}
In the afternoon, they row back toward the city. The wind has dropped, and the water is calm and smooth like glass. The sun is getting lower. And as they come around the last bend of the Bosphorus, the whole city appears in front of them. The domes, the minarets, the palace on its hill, the houses covering the slopes, all glowing in the golden light. Everyone in the boat goes quiet.

[long-pause]

Mehmet looks for the new mosque on the third hill. He can see it, with its walls rising and its scaffolding around it. It is not finished yet. But even now, it is already one of the biggest buildings in the city. He tries to imagine it finished, with its great dome and its four minarets. And he thinks that it will be the most beautiful sight in the whole city. He is right.

[pause]

The old fisherman tells them about the currents of the Bosphorus. On the surface, the water flows south, from the Black Sea. But deep down, there is another current, flowing the other way, to the north. The fishermen know this. Sometimes they lower a heavy basket deep into the water, and the deep current pulls their little boat north, against the wind. Mehmet thinks that it must be magic. The fisherman laughs and says it is just the sea, being clever.

[long-pause]

{img:ramadan-istanbul-1550-evening-mosque-lamps-lit-minarets-crowds}
In the autumn, the holy month of Ramadan comes. During Ramadan, Muslims do not eat or drink anything from sunrise until sunset. It is a month of prayer, of patience and of thinking about God and about other people. It is also a month of kindness, when people give food and money to the poor. And in Istanbul, it is a very special time of the year.

[long-pause]

Before sunrise, while it is still dark, a drummer walks through the streets of the mahalle. He beats a drum and calls out in a singing voice, to wake everyone up. Then the families get up and eat a meal before dawn, called sahur. Mehmet's mother serves bread, cheese, olives, eggs and lots of water. Everyone eats and drinks as much as they can. Then, when the first light appears in the sky, they stop.

[pause]

The days of Ramadan are long and quiet. People work, but more slowly than usual. They are hungry and thirsty, especially in the afternoon. Mehmet's father and the other workers on the building site work fewer hours. Mehmet is old enough to fast now. It is his first Ramadan of fasting the whole day. The first days are hard. His stomach rumbles, and his mouth is dry. But he is proud, and does not complain.

[pause]

{img:family-waiting-sunset-iftar-table-dates-bread-soup-window-orange-sky}
In the late afternoon, the whole city waits for sunset. The women prepare the evening meal. The smell of cooking fills the streets. People gather at their tables and wait. Everyone listens for the signal. And then, as the sun sets, the call to prayer sounds from all the minarets at once. It is time to eat.

[pause]

This meal is called iftar. Mehmet's family breaks the fast with a date and a glass of water, as the Prophet Muhammad did. Then they pray. And then they eat a delicious meal together. Soup, bread, pilaf, vegetables and meat. Rich families invite poor people to share their iftar. The mosques and the soup kitchens give food to anyone who comes. Nobody in the city should be hungry on a Ramadan evening.

[long-pause]

{img:special-ramadan-bread-pide-bakery-queue-evening-lamps}
During Ramadan, the bakeries make a special round, flat bread, called pide, sprinkled with black seeds. It is only baked during this month. Every evening, just before sunset, there is a long queue at the bakery. The bread is warm and soft, and smells wonderful. Fatma is always sent to fetch it. She carries it home carefully in a cloth, trying very hard not to take a bite before sunset.

[pause]

After iftar, the evenings of Ramadan are full of life. The great mosques are lit up with hundreds of oil lamps, glowing in the darkness. People walk through the streets, visiting friends and family. They go to the mosques for the special evening prayers. And in the squares, there are storytellers, musicians and shadow puppet theatres. The city stays awake late into the night.

[pause]

{img:mosque-courtyard-night-ramadan-lamps-glowing-people-strolling-peaceful}
One evening, Mehmet's father takes the whole family to the courtyard of a great mosque, to see the lights. The courtyard is full of people, walking slowly, talking quietly. The lamps hang in the arches, like golden stars. Inside the mosque, hundreds of men are praying, and the soft sound of their voices drifts out through the open doors. Fatma holds her grandmother's hand, and looks up at the lamps with shining eyes.

[long-pause]

Nine tells the children that Ramadan is the month when the gates of heaven are open. She says that it is a time to forgive people who have hurt you, and to ask forgiveness from those you have hurt. Mehmet thinks about this. He goes to Fatma, and says sorry for taking her red slippers and hiding them, the week before. Fatma forgives him. And then she says sorry too, for telling their mother about it. They both laugh.

[long-pause]

{img:eid-morning-istanbul-family-new-clothes-walking-to-mosque-sunrise}
At the end of Ramadan, there is a great feast. It lasts for three days. In Turkish, it is called Şeker Bayramı, the Sugar Feast, because of all the sweets that people eat. It is the happiest time of the whole year. Everyone has been waiting for it for weeks.

[long-pause]

On the morning of the feast, everyone gets up early and washes. They put on new clothes, or their very best ones. Mehmet's mother has made new clothes for the whole family. A new shirt and waistcoat for Mehmet. A new dress for Fatma, embroidered with little tulips. And Fatma finally gets to wear her red slippers with the pointed toes. She has been waiting for this day for months.

[pause]

The men go to the mosque for the special feast prayer, early in the morning. The mosques are so full that people pray in the courtyards and even in the streets. After the prayer, everyone greets each other. They shake hands, hug, and wish each other a happy feast. Old quarrels are forgotten. Everyone smiles.

[pause]

{img:children-kissing-grandmothers-hand-receiving-sweets-coins-ottoman-home}
Then the visits begin. During the three days of the feast, families visit each other. First, the young visit the old. Mehmet and Fatma go to Nine first. They kiss her hand and touch it to their foreheads, as a sign of respect. Nine gives them each a little coin and a handful of sweets, wrapped in a handkerchief. Then they visit their aunts and uncles, the neighbours and the old people of the mahalle.

[pause]

Everywhere they go, they are given sweets. Little candies made of sugar, baklava, helva, dried fruits and nuts. By the end of the first day, Fatma says that she never wants to see a sweet again. By the next morning, she has changed her mind.

[long-pause]

{img:feast-fair-swings-carousel-children-playing-square-istanbul-colourful}
In the open squares of the city, there are fairs for the children. There are wooden swings, and little carousels turned by hand. There are men selling toys, sweets and balloons made of animal bladders. There are jugglers, acrobats, wrestlers and puppet shows. The children spend their coins and run from one thing to the next. Mehmet and Fatma ride on the swings until they are dizzy.

[pause]

The neighbours of other faiths also come to visit. Bedros, the Armenian merchant, and his family come with their best wishes for the feast. Doctor Moshe brings a little box of sugared almonds for Nine. Nikolaos comes from Galata, with Yannis. Mehmet's mother serves them all sweets and sherbet. The house is full of people and laughter all day long.

[pause]

{img:courtyard-evening-feast-family-guests-lanterns-music-laughter}
In the evening of the first day, the family sits together in the courtyard, under the fig tree. Ayşe has hung up little lanterns in the branches. A neighbour plays a long-necked lute, called a saz, and sings old songs from Anatolia. Mehmet's father sings along, softly. He learned these songs as a boy, in his village. Nine listens with her eyes closed, nodding her head in time with the music.

[long-pause]

Late that night, when everyone has gone home, Mehmet lies on his mattress and listens to the sounds of the city. Somewhere, people are still singing. A dog barks. A boat bell rings down at the water. He thinks about the year that has passed. His first year of work. Meeting Sinan. The bazaar, the hammam, the Bosphorus. It has been a good year. And he falls asleep with the taste of honey still in his mouth.

[long-pause]

{img:ottoman-calligrapher-reed-pen-ink-paper-low-desk-window-light}
Istanbul is also a city of books, art and learning. In the mosques and schools, students study the Quran, law, mathematics, astronomy and medicine. In the palace, there are workshops where the finest artists of the empire make beautiful things. And all over the city, there are craftsmen who make books, tiles, carpets and textiles.

[long-pause]

The most respected art of all is calligraphy, the art of beautiful writing. For Muslims, the words of the Quran are holy. So writing them beautifully is a way of honouring God. Calligraphers train for many years, with a master. They write with pens made of reeds, cut at an angle. They make their own ink, from soot, gum and water. And they practise the same letters, again and again, thousands of times, until every line is perfect.

[pause]

The greatest calligraphers are as famous as poets and architects. Their work is written on the walls of mosques, on fountains, on gravestones and in books. In the new mosque of Sultan Suleiman, there will be beautiful inscriptions, written by the most famous calligrapher of the time, a man called Ahmed Karahisari. Mehmet has seen his assistants on the building site, carefully drawing the letters on large sheets of paper.

[pause]

{img:paper-marbling-ebru-artist-tray-colours-swirling-water-sheet}
Another lovely art is paper marbling, called ebru. The artist fills a shallow tray with water, thickened with a special plant. Then he sprinkles drops of coloured paint onto the water. The drops float and spread out. With a needle or a comb, the artist gently pulls the colours into swirls, waves and flowers. Then he lays a sheet of paper on top of the water, very carefully. When he lifts it up, the beautiful pattern is printed on the paper.

[pause]

Every pattern is different. It can never be made exactly the same way twice. Marbled paper is used for the covers of books, and for the borders around calligraphy. Fatma once watched an old ebru master in a little shop near the bazaar. She stood there for an hour, without moving. Afterwards, he gave her a small sheet of marbled paper, with a red tulip in the middle. She keeps it in her little box of treasures.

[long-pause]

{img:miniature-painters-workshop-palace-tiny-brushes-colourful-book-pages}
In the palace, there is a workshop of painters, who make small, detailed paintings for books. They are called miniatures. The painters use tiny brushes, some of them made with only a single hair from a cat. They paint with colours made from minerals, plants and gold. Their pictures show the battles and festivals of the sultan, the cities of the empire, and scenes from old stories and poems.

[pause]

One of these painters is a man called Matrakçı Nasuh. He has travelled with the sultan's army, and painted pictures of all the cities they passed through. His pictures are like maps, seen from above. You can see the streets, the houses, the mosques and the gardens of each city, all painted in bright colours. Some of his pictures of Istanbul still exist today. They show us how the city looked, almost five hundred years ago.

[pause]

{img:iznik-tiles-blue-turquoise-red-tulips-carnations-glazed-pattern}
And there are the tiles. In the town of Iznik, not far from Istanbul, potters make the most beautiful tiles in the world. They are white, with patterns painted in blue, turquoise and green. And now, around this time, the potters are discovering a new colour, a bright tomato red, which nobody has been able to make before. The tiles are decorated with tulips, carnations, roses and leaves. They will cover the walls of the great mosques and palaces of Istanbul.

[long-pause]

All these artists work slowly and patiently. They are not in a hurry. A single book can take years to make. A single tile panel can take months. They believe that beautiful things take time. And that beauty is a kind of prayer. Mehmet's father says the same thing about stone. A stone that is carved slowly and carefully will last for a thousand years. A stone that is carved in a hurry will crack in the first winter.

[pause]

There are also bookbinders in the city, who make the beautiful leather covers for books. They press patterns of flowers and leaves into the leather, and sometimes decorate it with gold. And there are booksellers, near the great mosques, with little shops full of handwritten books. Printed books are still rare in the Ottoman world. Most books are copied by hand, one by one, by patient scribes with reed pens. Each one is precious.

[long-pause]

{img:suleymaniye-mosque-completed-1557-great-dome-four-minarets-hill-sunlight}
The years pass. Mehmet grows up. His voice gets deeper, and his shoulders get broader. He learns to carve stone almost as well as his father. And every year, the great mosque on the third hill grows higher. The walls are finished. The half domes are built. And then, at last, the great dome itself rises over the city.

[long-pause]

In the year 1557, seven years after our story began, the Süleymaniye mosque is finished. Mehmet is now twenty years old. He is a real stonemason, a master of his trade. Fatma is sixteen, and she is going to be married soon, to a young carpenter from the mahalle. Nine has passed away peacefully, in her sleep, during the winter. The family misses her very much. But she lived to see the great dome rise over the city, and she was very proud.

[pause]

The mosque is opened with a great ceremony. The sultan comes, with all his viziers and officials. There is a famous story about this day. Sinan stands before the sultan, holding the golden key of the mosque. The sultan asks who should open the doors. And he says that Sinan should open them, because no one deserves it more. Sinan opens the great doors, and the sultan walks in.

[pause]

{img:suleymaniye-interior-vast-dome-windows-light-stained-glass-calm}
Inside, the mosque is breathtaking. The great dome floats high above, more than fifty metres up. Around it, there are many windows, letting in the light. Some of the windows are made of coloured glass, red, blue and green, like jewels. The walls are white and calm. Around the prayer niche, there are beautiful Iznik tiles, with tulips and carnations in blue and red. The calligraphy of Ahmed Karahisari runs around the walls, in gold and black.

[pause]

The mosque is not covered with decorations everywhere. It is simple, clear and calm. Sinan wanted the space itself to be beautiful, with its perfect shapes, its light and its quiet. When you stand inside it, you feel peaceful. The sound is wonderful too. Sinan has hidden clay jars in the dome, to help the sound travel. So the voice of the imam can be heard clearly in every corner, without shouting.

[long-pause]

{img:young-stonemason-and-father-standing-in-mosque-looking-up-proud-quiet}
On the first Friday after the opening, Mehmet and his father go to pray in the new mosque. They kneel on the carpet among the thousands of other men. Mehmet looks around him, at the walls and the columns and the windows. He knows many of these stones. He remembers carving some of them. Over there, at the bottom of that column, is a stone that he cut when he was fifteen. Nobody else knows that. But he knows. And God knows.

[pause]

Mehmet's father has tears in his eyes. He is getting old now. This will be his last great building. He puts his hand on his son's shoulder and squeezes it, without saying anything. He does not need to. Mehmet knows exactly what he means.

[pause]

{img:courtyard-suleymaniye-soup-kitchen-poor-people-eating-gardens-tombs}
Around the mosque, the other buildings are opening too. The schools fill with students. The hospital receives its first patients. And in the soup kitchen, the cooks begin to serve free food every day, to the poor, to students and to travellers. Bread and soup, and on Fridays, rice with meat. Hundreds of people are fed every day, for free. The great mosque is not only a place of prayer. It is a place of kindness too.

[long-pause]

Sinan will build many more buildings after the Süleymaniye. He will call it the work of his journeyman years, the time when he was still learning. His masterpiece, he will say, is another mosque, in the city of Edirne, which he finishes when he is about eighty years old. But for the people of Istanbul, the Süleymaniye will always be the most beloved. It still stands on its hill today, watching over the city, as it has done for more than four hundred and fifty years.

[pause]

In the evenings, the Süleymaniye is lit up by thousands of little oil lamps, hanging on long chains from the ceiling. From far away, people can see its windows glowing in the darkness. And the smoke of all those lamps rises up, as Sinan planned, into the little room above the entrance. There, it is collected as soot, and made into ink. With that ink, calligraphers write beautiful words, on paper, about God and about the light.

[long-pause]

{img:ottoman-empire-later-centuries-istanbul-skyline-new-mosques-ships-1700s}
Sultan Suleiman ruled for forty-six years, longer than any other Ottoman sultan. In 1566, when he was about seventy years old, he went with his army to Hungary, one last time. There, in his tent, he passed away peacefully. His body was brought back to Istanbul, and he was buried in the garden of his great mosque, next to his beloved Hürrem, who had passed away eight years before.

[long-pause]

After Suleiman, the Ottoman Empire went on for more than three hundred and fifty years. For a long time, it remained one of the greatest powers in the world. Istanbul kept growing, and new mosques, palaces and fountains were built. In the early seventeen hundreds, there was even a period called the Tulip Era, when the sultan and his court held tulip festivals in the palace gardens, with candles placed among the flowers and tortoises carrying little lamps on their backs.

[pause]

Coffee houses spread all over the city, as we said. They became places where men met to talk, read, play games and listen to music and stories. Later, newspapers were read aloud in them. And from Istanbul, coffee and the coffee house travelled to Europe, to Venice, London, Paris and Vienna. Without Istanbul, perhaps nobody in Europe would drink coffee today.

[pause]

{img:istanbul-1800s-galata-bridge-crowds-steamships-trams-old-photo-feel-later}
But slowly, the world changed. The countries of Europe became richer and more powerful, with their new factories and their great ships. The Ottoman Empire grew weaker, and began to lose its lands, one after another. In the eighteen hundreds, Istanbul changed too. The first bridges were built across the Golden Horn. Steamships replaced many of the old caiques. Trams and a little underground railway were built. Many people began to wear European clothes.

[pause]

In the First World War, the Ottoman Empire fought on the losing side. After the war, the old empire came to an end. In 1923, a new country was founded, the Republic of Turkey. Its leader was a general called Mustafa Kemal, later known as Atatürk. The new capital of Turkey was not Istanbul, but the city of Ankara, in the middle of Anatolia. After almost five hundred years, Istanbul was no longer the capital of an empire.

[long-pause]

{img:topkapi-palace-museum-visitors-courtyard-cypress-trees-later-today}
The palace of the sultans became a museum. Today, visitors can walk through its courtyards and gardens, and see the rooms where the sultans lived. They can see the great kitchens, with their huge copper pots, and the treasury full of jewels. The silent second courtyard, with its cypress trees, is now full of tourists from all over the world, talking in a hundred different languages.

[pause]

Hagia Sophia also changed. In 1934, it became a museum. The plaster was removed from many of the old Christian mosaics, and their gold shone again. People of every faith could visit it. Then, in 2020, it was turned back into a mosque. But visitors are still welcome, outside the times of prayer, to stand under the great dome and look up at the floating light.

[pause]

{img:sinan-tomb-small-simple-corner-garden-suleymaniye-today-quiet}
Sinan lived to be about ninety years old. He passed away in 1588. He was buried in a small, simple tomb, which he designed himself, in a corner just outside the walls of the Süleymaniye. It is a very modest tomb, for such a great man. Above it, there is a little poem carved in stone, about his life and his work. Visitors still come to stand there quietly, and say a prayer for the old architect.

[long-pause]

As for Mehmet and his family, they were ordinary people, and their names were not written in the history books. But perhaps the stones that Mehmet carved are still there, in the walls of the great mosque. Perhaps a family of stonemasons still lives somewhere in the city, descended from him. And perhaps, somewhere in an old wooden box, there is still a little sheet of marbled paper, with a red tulip in the middle.

[long-pause]

{img:istanbul-today-skyline-bosporus-bridge-mosques-modern-city-sunset}
Today, Istanbul is one of the biggest cities in the world. More than fifteen million people live here. It is no longer a capital, but it is still the largest and busiest city of Turkey. It has spread far beyond the old walls, over the hills on both sides of the Bosphorus, in Europe and in Asia. Great bridges now cross the Bosphorus. And trains run in a tunnel under the water, from one continent to the other.

[long-pause]

But the old city is still there. On the hills above the Golden Horn, the domes and minarets still rise against the sky, just as they did in 1550. The Süleymaniye still stands on the third hill, the most beautiful of them all. Hagia Sophia still stands on the first hill, and the Blue Mosque, built about sixty years after our story, stands opposite it. In the early morning, the call to prayer still drifts out over the city from hundreds of minarets.

[pause]

The Grand Bazaar still exists too. It has more than four thousand shops, and more than sixty covered streets. Some of them still sell the same things as in 1550. Carpets, jewellery, copper pots and leather slippers. The old stone halls are still there, in the middle, with their little domes. And the shopkeepers still sit in their shops, calling out to the people walking by, and offering them a glass of tea.

[pause]

{img:grand-bazaar-today-colourful-lamps-shops-tourists-vaulted-ceiling}
The boats still cross the water. Now they are ferries, with engines instead of oars, and they carry thousands of people every day, between Europe and Asia. Many people say the ferry is the most beautiful way to travel in Istanbul. You sit on the deck with a glass of tea and a simit, and watch the city go by. The seagulls follow the ferry, and people throw them pieces of bread.

[pause]

The cats are still there too. Istanbul is famous for its cats. Thousands of them live in the streets, and the people still feed them and look after them, just as they did five hundred years ago. You can see them sleeping on the steps of the mosques, in the doorways of shops, and on the warm stones in the sun.

[long-pause]

{img:ferry-crossing-bosporus-seagulls-people-tea-glasses-deck-today}
Many old traditions have survived. The hammams are still open, and people still go there to relax in the steam, under the little stars of light in the dome. During Ramadan, the drummers still walk through some neighbourhoods before dawn, waking people for their early meal. And at the Sugar Feast, children still kiss the hands of their grandparents, and receive sweets and a little money.

[pause]

In the spring, Istanbul is full of tulips. Every April, there is a great tulip festival. Millions of tulips are planted in the parks and gardens of the city, in every colour. People come to walk among them and take photographs. The tulip, the old flower of the Ottomans, has come home again.

[pause]

{img:tulip-festival-istanbul-park-april-millions-flowers-families-today}
And in the Süleymaniye mosque, people still come to pray, five times a day. Visitors come too, from all over the world, to see Sinan's great dome. They stand in the middle of the mosque and look up, and they go quiet, just as Mehmet did. In the garden behind the mosque, Sultan Suleiman and Hürrem rest in their tombs, under the cypress trees.

[long-pause]

From the terrace of the mosque, there is a wonderful view over the Golden Horn. On a clear evening, you can see the whole old city, the water, the bridges, the Galata Tower and the hills of Asia beyond the Bosphorus. The light turns golden, and then pink, and then blue. The lamps of the city come on, one by one. And from the minarets, the evening call to prayer begins.

[long-pause]

{img:istanbul-1550-evening-golden-horn-domes-silhouettes-sunset-caiques}
So we leave Istanbul tonight, in the year 1550, as the evening falls. The sun is setting behind the old city. The domes and the minarets stand dark against the orange sky. The water of the Golden Horn is turning gold, and then pink. The last caiques are crossing to Galata, their oars dipping softly in the water.

[long-pause]

Nine is sitting by the window, her eyes half closed, listening to the sounds of the street. And Fatma is putting her red slippers carefully back in their box, for the next special day.

Mehmet and his father are washing the stone dust from their hands at the fountain. The water is cool and clear. Above them, on the third hill, the half-built walls of the great mosque stand silent in the dusk. The cranes are still. The scaffolding is empty. The stones are waiting for tomorrow, when the hammers will ring again.

On the hills of both shores, little lights are burning in the windows of the villages. And over everything, the moon is rising, white and calm, over the domes of the sleeping city.
