TOPIC: Hunting and gathering food
===
IMG: reindeer-herd-river-crossing-autumn | a large herd of reindeer crossing a shallow river in autumn on the open tundra, low golden sun, hunters watching from a distant ridge
HEAD: Reindeer were one of the most important sources of food for Ice Age people in Europe.
BODY: In many regions of Ice Age Europe, reindeer were the main animal hunted. At some sites in France, reindeer bones make up more than ninety percent of all animal remains. For this reason, archaeologists sometimes call the late Ice Age the age of the reindeer. Reindeer migrated in large herds between summer and winter grazing areas. Hunters learned their routes and waited at river crossings and narrow valleys, where the animals could be approached. Reindeer provided meat, fat, marrow, hides for clothing and tents, sinew for thread, and antlers and bones for tools. Almost every part of the animal was used. Archaeologists study the age and season of death of hunted animals by examining their teeth. This reveals when people hunted and how they planned their year. Even so, diets varied by region. Elsewhere, people relied more on horses, bison, ibex or red deer, depending on the local landscape.
===
IMG: hunters-spear-thrower-atlatl-tundra | an Ice Age hunter crouching in tall grass with a spear-thrower and a long spear, aiming toward distant horses, wind in the grass
HEAD: Ice Age hunters used spears and a clever tool called the spear-thrower.
BODY: Hunters in the Ice Age used spears tipped with sharp points of flint, bone or antler. Later in the period, they also used a spear-thrower, a stick with a hook at one end that held the back of the spear. When thrown, the spear-thrower acted like an extension of the arm, giving the spear much greater speed and range. The oldest known spear-throwers in Europe are around seventeen to twenty thousand years old. Some were beautifully carved with animal figures, such as a famous example from France showing a young ibex. Archaeologists have tested replicas and found that they greatly increase hunting power. Even so, hunting large animals remained dangerous and uncertain. Hunters worked together, using knowledge of animal behaviour, the landscape and the wind. The bow and arrow came into wider use in Europe only near the end of the Ice Age, although some researchers suggest it was known earlier.
===
IMG: mammoth-herd-steppe-hunters-watching-distance | a small group of Ice Age hunters lying on a hill watching a herd of woolly mammoths grazing on the steppe below, cold clear morning
HEAD: Ice Age people sometimes hunted mammoths, but these huge animals were not their everyday food.
BODY: Woolly mammoths were about the size of modern African elephants and covered with long hair. Popular images often show Ice Age people constantly hunting them. Archaeologists believe the reality was more complex. Mammoths were dangerous and difficult to kill. Some sites show clear evidence of mammoth hunting, including spear points embedded in bones. At other sites, people may have used mammoths that died naturally, collecting their bones, tusks and meat. A single mammoth provided an enormous quantity of meat, fat, hide and ivory. Ivory was especially valued for making tools, beads and figurines. Even so, most groups relied more on smaller animals such as reindeer, horses and hares for everyday food. Researchers continue to debate how much human hunting contributed to the extinction of mammoths. Climate change and the loss of their grassland habitat at the end of the Ice Age were probably also important factors.
===
IMG: salmon-river-fishing-ice-age-nets | Ice Age people fishing for salmon in a clear cold river using spears and woven nets, stones in the water, mountains behind
HEAD: Fish, birds and small animals were also important parts of the Ice Age diet.
BODY: Ice Age people did not live on large animals alone. Rivers provided fish, especially salmon, which swam upstream in great numbers each year. Archaeologists have found fish bones at many sites and carved images of fish in art. Harpoons made of antler, with barbs along one side, were used to catch large fish. Researchers believe that nets and traps were also used, although these rarely survive. People hunted birds such as ptarmigan and waterfowl, and small animals such as arctic hares and foxes. Chemical studies of human bones measure the balance of elements, which shows the types of food people ate. These studies suggest that some groups ate a considerable amount of fish, especially near rivers and coasts. Even so, diets changed with the seasons. In spring and autumn, migrating animals and fish provided abundant food. In late winter, food could become scarce, and stored supplies were vital for survival.
===
IMG: meat-drying-racks-smoke-camp | strips of meat hanging on wooden racks over a smoky fire to dry at an Ice Age camp, people working nearby, autumn evening
HEAD: Ice Age people preserved meat by drying, smoking and freezing it for the winter.
BODY: Hunting could produce large amounts of meat at certain times of year, especially during animal migrations in autumn. To survive the winter, people needed to store food. Meat could be cut into thin strips and dried in the wind or over smoky fires. Smoking helped preserve it and kept insects away. In very cold regions, meat could simply be frozen in pits dug into the ground. Archaeologists have found storage pits at sites in Eastern Europe, near mammoth-bone dwellings. Fat was also extremely important. It provided energy and could be stored for long periods. Bones were often broken open to extract marrow, and boiled to remove grease. Researchers find many smashed bones at Ice Age sites, showing that nothing was wasted. Even so, storage required planning and cooperation. A successful autumn hunt and careful preparation could mean the difference between comfort and hunger in the months that followed.
===
IMG: gathering-berries-roots-summer-tundra | Ice Age women and children gathering berries and roots on a summer tundra covered with small flowers, baskets of woven grass, soft sunlight
HEAD: Plants such as berries, roots, seeds and leaves were also part of the Ice Age diet.
BODY: Although the Ice Age landscape was cold, it supported many edible plants. In summer, people could gather berries, such as cranberries and crowberries, as well as leaves, roots, seeds and nuts where trees grew. Plant foods provided vitamins and variety. Scientists have found traces of plant remains in the tartar on Ice Age teeth and in ancient hearths. At some sites, grinding stones show that seeds or roots were processed into flour. Historians note that the importance of plant foods was long underestimated, because bones survive much better than plants in the ground. Even so, plants were seasonal. In winter, people depended mainly on animal foods and stored supplies. Knowledge of where and when to find plants was passed down through generations. This knowledge was as valuable as hunting skill. Women, men and children probably all took part in gathering, though the exact division of work is difficult to determine.
===
IMG: storage-pit-frozen-ground-winter-food | a pit dug into frozen ground lined with stones and covered by a mammoth shoulder blade, near a snowy hut, used for storing meat
HEAD: At some Ice Age sites, archaeologists have found pits dug into the ground for storing food.
BODY: On the cold plains of Eastern Europe, the ground stayed frozen for much of the year. Ice Age people took advantage of this by digging storage pits near their dwellings. Food placed in these pits stayed frozen and fresh for long periods. At sites such as Mezhyrich, archaeologists found several such pits around the huts. Some were covered with large bones, such as mammoth shoulder blades, to protect the contents from animals. Researchers believe these pits held meat, fat and perhaps bones for fuel. This kind of storage suggests that people stayed in the same place for much of the winter, rather than moving constantly. Even so, interpretations differ, since the original contents have long since disappeared. What remains are the pits themselves and scattered bones. Yet they show careful planning for the future. Storing food allowed families to survive the longest and darkest part of the year, when hunting was most difficult.

TOPIC: Tools of stone, bone and antler
===
IMG: flint-blades-workshop-stones | long thin flint blades and cores laid out on a hide beside a fire, a stone hammer and antler tools, sharp edges catching the light
HEAD: Ice Age toolmakers produced long, thin flint blades with great skill.
BODY: Stone tools are the most common objects found at Ice Age sites, because they do not decay. Flint was the preferred material, since it breaks in predictable ways and produces very sharp edges. Toolmakers struck flint with hammers of stone, bone or antler to remove long, thin blades. These blades were then shaped into many different tools, such as knives, scrapers, drills and spear points. Archaeologists call this technique blade technology. They can refit flakes back together like a puzzle, showing exactly how a tool was made. Such studies reveal the skill and planning of Ice Age craftspeople. One of the finest examples is the Solutrean laurel leaf point, a thin, finely flaked blade made in France and Spain around twenty thousand years ago. Some were too delicate for practical use and may have had a special purpose. Even so, most tools were everyday objects, made quickly and used until they wore out.
===
IMG: antler-harpoon-carving-firelight | an Ice Age man carving a barbed harpoon from reindeer antler with a flint tool by firelight, shavings on his lap
HEAD: Bone and antler were carved into harpoons, needles, spear points and many other tools.
BODY: Besides stone, Ice Age people used bone, antler and ivory. These materials were strong and flexible, and could be carved into complex shapes. Reindeer antler was especially useful. To work it, craftspeople soaked it in water to soften it, then cut grooves with flint tools to remove long strips. From these strips, they carved harpoons with rows of barbs, spear points, needles and awls. Archaeologists find many such tools, especially from the later part of the Ice Age, a period known as the Magdalenian, after a rock shelter in France called La Madeleine. Some bone tools were decorated with engraved animals or patterns. Even so, many objects made of wood, leather and plant fibres have not survived. The tools that remain represent only part of what people made and used. Yet they show a sophisticated knowledge of materials and a strong sense of design, combining usefulness with beauty.
===
IMG: bone-needles-awls-display-museum | a museum display case with Ice Age bone needles, awls and small tools on dark fabric, soft focused light
HEAD: Museums display thousands of small Ice Age tools that show the details of daily work.
BODY: Many museums in Europe hold large collections of Ice Age tools. The National Museum of Prehistory in Les Eyzies, France, displays objects from the many sites of the Vézère valley. Visitors can see needles, awls, scrapers, harpoons and blades, each made by hand thousands of years ago. Archaeologists study these tools with microscopes to find traces of use. Wear marks reveal whether a tool was used for cutting meat, scraping hides, working wood or boring holes. Residues sometimes survive on tool edges, including traces of blood, plants or pigment. Historians note that such research has transformed understanding of Ice Age life. Tools once thought to be simple turn out to have specific purposes. Even so, many questions remain. The names people gave their tools and the stories attached to them are lost. Yet each object preserves a small moment of skilled work, carried out by real people in a very different world.
===
IMG: spear-thrower-carved-animal-ibex | a carved reindeer antler spear-thrower decorated with a small ibex figure, displayed on dark fabric with soft light
HEAD: Some Ice Age tools were decorated with carved animals, combining usefulness and art.
BODY: Among the finest objects of the Ice Age are decorated spear-throwers. One famous example, found at the cave of Le Mas d'Azil in France, shows a young ibex looking back over its shoulder. Others show horses, birds and other animals. These carvings were made on reindeer antler with great precision. Archaeologists date most of them to the later part of the Ice Age, around fifteen to seventeen thousand years ago. Researchers debate why such objects were decorated. Some suggest the images had a magical or spiritual purpose, perhaps to bring success in hunting. Others think they expressed the owner's identity or simply reflected pleasure in beautiful things. Even so, the carvings show careful observation of animals. The proportions and movements are often very accurate. This suggests that Ice Age people watched animals closely and knew them intimately. The decorated tools blur the line between everyday objects and works of art.
===
IMG: stone-lamp-burning-fat-cave | a small carved sandstone lamp burning animal fat with a juniper wick, glowing softly in a dark cave passage, painted wall faintly visible
HEAD: Ice Age people made lamps of stone that burned animal fat to light dark caves.
BODY: To see inside dark caves, Ice Age people used lamps. Some were simple hollow stones. Others were carefully carved, with a bowl and a handle. The most famous example, found in Lascaux cave in France, is made of red sandstone and has a decorated handle. The lamps burned animal fat, with a wick of plant material, such as juniper or moss. Scientists have analysed residues in such lamps and identified the fats and wicks used. Experiments with replica lamps show that they gave a soft, steady light, enough to see and paint by, for an hour or more. Torches of wood were also used, and their marks can still be seen on cave walls. Even so, light inside caves was always limited. The flickering glow made painted animals appear to move. Researchers suggest this effect may have been intentional, adding to the power of the images for people who saw them.
===
IMG: ochre-grinding-red-pigment-stone | hands grinding red ochre into powder on a flat stone, small piles of red and yellow pigment, a bone tube and brush nearby
HEAD: Red and yellow ochre, a natural pigment, was widely used during the Ice Age.
BODY: Ochre is a natural earth pigment containing iron oxides. It ranges in colour from yellow to deep red. Ice Age people used it widely. They ground it into powder on flat stones and mixed it with water, fat or other substances to make paint. Ochre was used in cave paintings, on decorated objects and in burials, where bodies were sometimes covered with red powder. Researchers believe it may also have been used to treat hides, protect skin from the sun or keep insects away. Black pigments were made from charcoal and manganese minerals. Archaeologists find lumps of ochre with scraped surfaces at many sites. Even so, the meaning of its use, especially in burials, is uncertain. Red may have symbolised life, blood or warmth. The use of ochre goes back much further than the Ice Age in Europe, to early human sites in Africa more than a hundred thousand years ago.

TOPIC: Winter in the Ice Age
===
IMG: blizzard-camp-tents-snow-night | a small camp of hide tents during a snowstorm at night, wind blowing snow, faint firelight glowing through the tent walls
HEAD: Winters during the Ice Age were long, dark and very cold, especially in northern regions.
BODY: At the height of the last Ice Age, winters in much of Europe were far colder than today. In Central Europe, winter temperatures may have averaged twenty degrees or more below freezing. Strong winds blew across the open plains, carrying dust and snow. Scientists reconstruct these conditions from evidence such as frozen soil patterns, wind-blown dust deposits called loess and fossil insects. During storms, people stayed inside their shelters, keeping fires burning and conserving energy. Archaeologists find that many sites in the coldest regions were used mainly in warmer seasons, suggesting that people moved south or into sheltered valleys in winter. Even so, some groups stayed in cold regions year-round, especially where mammoth-bone dwellings provided strong shelter. Survival required careful preparation. Fuel, food and clothing had to be ready before winter began. In the darkness of a storm, the glow of a fire inside a hide tent was a sign of life and safety.
===
IMG: short-winter-day-low-sun-snowfield | a pale low winter sun over a wide snowfield with a few people walking far away, long blue shadows, very quiet
HEAD: In winter, daylight was short, and much of the day's work had to be done in a few hours.
BODY: In northern Europe, winter days are short. During the Ice Age, the length of the days was the same as today, since it depends on the Earth's position, not on climate. In midwinter, daylight in northern Germany or Britain lasted only around seven or eight hours. People had to complete outdoor tasks, such as hunting, gathering fuel and fetching water, in these short hours. Snow covered the ground, and hunting could be difficult. Animals were often thin and scattered. Historians and archaeologists believe that winter was a time of reduced activity and careful use of stored resources. Even so, winter also brought some advantages. Frozen rivers and lakes could be crossed on foot. Animal tracks in the snow showed hunters where to go. Frozen ground preserved stored meat. People who knew the land could find food and fuel even in the coldest months, though survival always required skill and patience.
===
IMG: storytelling-around-fire-winter-night | a group of Ice Age people sitting close around a fire inside a large shelter at night, an older woman telling a story with gestures, children listening
HEAD: Long winter nights were probably filled with storytelling, songs and the making of tools and clothing.
BODY: With long hours of darkness, Ice Age people spent much time inside their shelters. Researchers believe this time was used for indoor work, such as repairing clothing, sewing, carving tools and making ornaments. It was probably also a time for storytelling and music. No Ice Age stories survive, since there was no writing. Yet anthropologists who have studied hunter-gatherer societies around the world note that storytelling often plays a central role during long evenings. Stories pass on knowledge about animals, landscapes, history and values. Musical instruments from the Ice Age, such as bone flutes, show that music was part of life. Even so, historians are careful not to describe these evenings in too much detail, since direct evidence is limited. What can be said is that people had time, firelight and skills. The carved objects and ornaments found at sites suggest that some of this time was spent creating things of beauty.
===
IMG: children-playing-snow-camp-winter | Ice Age children in fur clothing playing in the snow near a camp, sliding down a small slope, adults working by the tents
HEAD: Archaeologists have found traces of Ice Age children, including footprints and small objects that may have been toys.
BODY: Children were an important part of Ice Age communities. Evidence of their presence includes small footprints and handprints in caves, and child-sized tools at some sites. In the cave of Niaux in France and other caves, footprints show children walking with adults. At some sites, small figurines of clay or carved animals may have been toys, although their purpose is uncertain. Researchers have also found evidence of children helping with tasks, such as handling clay or making simple tools. Historians note that childhood in the Ice Age combined play and learning. Children played in the snow, much as children do today, although direct evidence of such games does not survive. Even so, life was dangerous for the young. Infant and child mortality was high. Yet children represented the future of the group. Their care, education and survival through winter were among the most important concerns of every Ice Age community.
===
IMG: frozen-river-ice-crossing-band-dusk | a small band of Ice Age people carefully crossing a frozen river on foot at dusk, carrying bundles, snow-covered banks and bare birch trees
HEAD: Frozen rivers and lakes made travel easier in winter, but also brought danger.
BODY: In the depths of winter, rivers and lakes froze solid. This allowed people to cross water easily and travel along frozen rivers as natural roads. Recent Arctic and subarctic peoples have used frozen rivers in this way for travel and hunting. Researchers believe Ice Age people did the same. Fishing through holes in the ice could also provide food. Even so, ice was dangerous. Thin ice could break, and falling into freezing water could be fatal within minutes. Knowledge of ice conditions, passed down through generations, was essential. Archaeologists have found sites located near rivers, which provided water, fish and routes for travel throughout the year. In spring, the melting ice brought floods and made travel difficult again. Historians describe the Ice Age year as a cycle shaped by freezing and thawing, by migrating animals and by changing light. People planned their lives around this cycle with great care.
===
IMG: northern-lights-ice-age-sky-camp | green northern lights shimmering over a snowy Ice Age camp of hide tents, stars bright, a person standing and looking up
HEAD: During the Ice Age, people in northern regions probably saw the northern lights more often than people in the same places today.
BODY: The northern lights, or aurora borealis, appear when particles from the Sun strike the Earth's upper atmosphere. They are usually seen in regions close to the magnetic pole. During the Ice Age, the magnetic pole was in a different position, and people in parts of Europe may have seen the aurora on clear winter nights. Scientists note, however, that the strength and location of the aurora change over time, so it is difficult to know exactly how often they appeared. Some researchers have suggested that certain patterns in cave art may represent the sky, although this is speculative. Even so, Ice Age people certainly watched the night sky closely. The Moon, stars and seasons were important for timing travel and hunting. Some engraved bones have marks that may record lunar cycles. Under the clear, dark skies of the Ice Age, with no artificial light, the stars would have been extraordinarily bright.
===
IMG: spring-thaw-tundra-flowers-birds-return | the tundra in early spring with melting snow patches, small purple and yellow flowers, flocks of geese flying north, a river swollen with meltwater
HEAD: The return of spring brought migrating animals, birds and new plants to the Ice Age landscape.
BODY: After the long winter, spring was a time of renewal. Snow melted, rivers rose and the first plants began to grow. Migrating birds, such as geese and ducks, returned from the south. Reindeer herds moved toward their summer grazing grounds, and their calves were born. Fish began to swim upstream. For Ice Age people, spring meant new sources of food after months of stored supplies. Archaeologists find evidence of seasonal camps used in spring, located along migration routes and near rivers. Studies of animal teeth from these sites confirm the season of hunting. Even so, spring could be a difficult time. Melting snow made the ground muddy and travel hard. Stored food might be nearly exhausted before new food became plentiful. Historians note that the transition from winter to spring was often the hungriest period of the year. Yet the lengthening days and returning birds marked the end of the darkest season.

TOPIC: Animals of the Ice Age
===
IMG: woolly-mammoth-close-snow-breath | a woolly mammoth standing in light snow, long brown hair, curved tusks, breath steaming in the cold air, calm and close
HEAD: The woolly mammoth was the best-known animal of the Ice Age.
BODY: Woolly mammoths were large relatives of modern elephants. They stood about three to three and a half metres tall at the shoulder and were covered with long, shaggy hair over a dense undercoat. They had small ears and short tails, which reduced heat loss. Their long, curved tusks could reach more than four metres. Mammoths grazed on grasses and herbs of the mammoth steppe. Scientists know a great deal about them because complete frozen bodies have been found in the permafrost of Siberia. Some still have hair, skin and stomach contents. One famous example is a baby mammoth named Lyuba, found in 2007 and remarkably well preserved. Even so, these frozen mammoths are rare. Most of what is known comes from bones, teeth and tusks. Ice Age people drew and carved mammoths often, showing their humped backs and long hair. These images confirm what scientists have learned from preserved remains.
===
IMG: woolly-rhinoceros-steppe-wind | a woolly rhinoceros grazing on a cold windy steppe, thick brown fur, two horns, low grey clouds
HEAD: The woolly rhinoceros lived on the cold steppes and was hunted and painted by Ice Age people.
BODY: The woolly rhinoceros was a large animal covered with thick fur. It had two horns on its nose, the front one sometimes more than a metre long. It grazed on grasses and lived across Europe and northern Asia. Scientists have found preserved woolly rhinoceroses in frozen ground in Siberia and in an oil seep in Poland. These finds show the animal's fur, skin and shape. Ice Age people painted woolly rhinoceroses in caves, such as Chauvet in France. The images show their large horns and powerful bodies. The animal became extinct around fourteen thousand years ago. Researchers have studied ancient DNA from woolly rhinoceros remains. Some studies suggest that its population remained fairly stable until shortly before its extinction, which may point to climate change as the main cause. Even so, scientists continue to debate the role of human hunting. The woolly rhinoceros remains one of the most striking animals of the Ice Age.
===
IMG: cave-bear-den-cave-hibernating | a large cave bear resting in the mouth of a limestone cave with snow outside, quiet and calm
HEAD: Cave bears were large bears that hibernated in caves across Ice Age Europe.
BODY: The cave bear was larger than most modern brown bears. Despite its size, studies of its teeth and bones suggest that it ate mainly plants. Cave bears used caves for hibernation through the winter. Many died there, and their bones accumulated over thousands of years. Some caves contain the remains of hundreds or even thousands of bears. Claw marks on cave walls show where bears scratched the rock. In some painted caves, such as Chauvet, cave bear skulls were found placed in particular positions, perhaps by people. Researchers debate whether this had a ritual meaning. Cave bears became extinct around twenty-four thousand years ago. Possible reasons include climate change, a decline in their plant foods and competition with people for caves. Even so, their remains are among the most common fossils in European caves. Visitors to some caves can still see their hibernation hollows and scratch marks on the walls.
===
IMG: cave-lion-pride-tundra-dusk | a small group of cave lions resting on a rocky rise in the tundra at dusk, pale fur, no mane, watching the horizon
HEAD: Cave lions were large predators that hunted on the Ice Age steppes.
BODY: Cave lions were close relatives of modern lions, but slightly larger. They lived across Europe, northern Asia and parts of North America. Cave paintings, especially at Chauvet in France, show cave lions in remarkable detail. The paintings suggest that the males had little or no mane, unlike modern African lions. In recent years, frozen cave lion cubs have been discovered in the permafrost of Siberia, with fur and whiskers still preserved. These finds confirmed some details of their appearance. Cave lions hunted large animals such as reindeer, horses and young bison. They became extinct around fourteen thousand years ago. Scientists think that the loss of their prey animals and changes in climate were important factors. Even so, cave lions were clearly important in the minds of Ice Age people. They appear in art and in carved figures, including the famous Lion Man from Germany, which combines a lion's head with a human body.
===
IMG: giant-deer-megaloceros-antlers | a giant deer with enormous wide antlers standing in a misty open woodland clearing at dawn
HEAD: The giant deer, sometimes called the Irish elk, had the largest antlers of any deer that ever lived.
BODY: The giant deer, known by its scientific name Megaloceros, was one of the most impressive animals of the Ice Age. Males had antlers that could measure more than three and a half metres from tip to tip. Despite its common name, the Irish elk, it was not an elk, and it lived across Europe and Asia, not only in Ireland. Many well-preserved skeletons have been found in Irish bogs, which explains the name. The giant deer lived in open woodland and grassland. It appears in some cave paintings in France. Scientists think that the enormous antlers were used to impress females and rival males. Growing them each year required a great deal of food and minerals. The giant deer disappeared from most of Europe at the end of the Ice Age. Even so, a population survived in western Siberia until around seven thousand seven hundred years ago, long after the ice had retreated.
===
IMG: wild-horses-steppe-wind-manes | a herd of stocky wild horses with dark manes running across a windy cold steppe, dust rising, grey sky
HEAD: Wild horses were common on the Ice Age steppes and were among the animals most often painted in caves.
BODY: Wild horses lived in large herds across the open grasslands of Ice Age Europe and Asia. They were smaller and stockier than most modern domestic horses, with short legs and thick coats. Horses were an important source of food for Ice Age people at many sites. They are also among the animals most frequently shown in cave art. In Lascaux and other caves, painted horses appear in many colours and poses. Some are shown with spotted coats, such as the famous dappled horses of Pech Merle in France. Scientists once thought these spots were imaginary, but ancient DNA research has shown that spotted horses did exist at that time. Even so, Ice Age horses were not tamed. The domestication of horses happened much later, around five and a half thousand years ago on the steppes of Central Asia. Wild horses continued to live in Europe for thousands of years after the Ice Age ended.
===
IMG: wolves-near-camp-early-dogs-dusk | two grey wolves watching an Ice Age camp from the edge of the tundra at dusk, a fire glowing among the tents
HEAD: Dogs were domesticated from wolves during the late Ice Age.
BODY: Wolves were common predators on the Ice Age steppes. They hunted the same animals as people, such as reindeer and horses. At some point during the late Ice Age, some wolves began to live closely with humans. Over generations, they became the first domestic dogs. Scientists still debate when and where this happened. Estimates range from about fifteen thousand to more than twenty thousand years ago. One of the oldest confirmed dogs was found buried with two people at Bonn-Oberkassel in Germany, dated to around fourteen thousand years ago. Studies of its bones show that it had been cared for during a serious illness, which suggests a close bond with people. Even so, the process of domestication was gradual and complex. Dogs may have helped people with hunting, guarding camps and carrying loads. The partnership between people and dogs that began in the Ice Age has continued ever since.
===
