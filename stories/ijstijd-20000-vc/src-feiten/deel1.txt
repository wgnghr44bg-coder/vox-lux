TOPIC: A frozen world, twenty thousand years ago
===
IMG: ice-age-europe-tundra-glacier-distant-mammoths | a vast cold tundra plain in Ice Age Europe with a white glacier on the far horizon and a small herd of woolly mammoths in the distance, low pale sun
HEAD: About twenty thousand years ago, the Earth was in the coldest part of the last Ice Age.
BODY: Scientists call this period the Last Glacial Maximum. It lasted roughly from twenty-six thousand to nineteen thousand years ago. Average temperatures around the world were several degrees lower than today. In northern Europe and North America, the winters were long and bitterly cold. Summers were short and cool. Huge sheets of ice covered large parts of the northern continents. Scientists know about this period from many kinds of evidence. Ice cores drilled from Greenland and Antarctica contain tiny bubbles of ancient air. Layers of sediment on the sea floor record changes in temperature. Pollen preserved in lake mud shows which plants grew in each region. Even so, the Ice Age was not one single frozen moment. The climate changed repeatedly over thousands of years, with colder and milder phases. Yet at its peak, around twenty thousand years ago, the world looked very different from the one that exists today.
===
IMG: glacier-edge-meltwater-river-cold-steppe | the edge of a massive ice sheet with meltwater streams flowing across a cold gravel plain, grey sky, no people
HEAD: Great ice sheets covered Scandinavia, much of Britain and northern North America.
BODY: At the height of the last Ice Age, an enormous ice sheet covered Scandinavia and spread across the Baltic Sea and northern Germany. Another covered most of Scotland, Ireland and northern England. In North America, ice stretched from the Arctic to the area of modern New York and Chicago. In places, the ice was more than three kilometres thick. Mountain ranges such as the Alps and the Pyrenees had their own large glaciers. Geologists can still trace the edges of these ice sheets today. They find ridges of rock and gravel, called moraines, left behind when the ice melted. Large boulders carried by the ice lie far from where they came from. Even so, much of Europe south of the ice was not covered. It was a cold, open land of grass and low shrubs. People and animals lived in these southern regions, especially in sheltered valleys in what are now France, Spain and Central Europe.
===
IMG: low-sea-level-land-bridge-doggerland-plain | a wide green and brown lowland plain with rivers and marshes where the North Sea is today, herds of deer, a distant coastline, soft light
HEAD: Because so much water was frozen in ice, sea levels were about a hundred and twenty metres lower than today.
BODY: The ice sheets held a huge amount of the world's water. As a result, sea levels fell dramatically. Coastlines lay far beyond where they are now. Britain was not an island but part of mainland Europe. A wide area of land, now called Doggerland, stretched across what is today the southern North Sea. In the far north, a land bridge called Beringia connected Siberia and Alaska. This bridge later helped people reach the Americas. Scientists know about these lost lands from the shape of the sea floor and from objects brought up by fishing boats. Trawlers in the North Sea have recovered mammoth bones, stone tools and even a carved antler point. Even so, much of the evidence of coastal life from this period now lies under the sea. Archaeologists believe many people may have lived along the old coasts, where food was plentiful. Their camps are now hidden beneath the waves.
===
IMG: mammoth-steppe-grassland-herds-reindeer-horses | a cold dry grassland stretching to the horizon with herds of reindeer and wild horses grazing, a few woolly mammoths further away, pale blue sky
HEAD: Much of Ice Age Europe and Asia was covered by a cold, dry grassland called the mammoth steppe.
BODY: South and east of the ice sheets lay a vast landscape of grasses, herbs and low shrubs. Scientists call it the mammoth steppe, after its most famous animal. It stretched from Spain across Europe and Asia to Alaska and Canada. The climate was cold and dry, with little snow in many areas. This allowed grass to grow and support large herds of animals. Woolly mammoths, woolly rhinoceroses, reindeer, wild horses, bison and musk oxen grazed there. Predators such as wolves, cave lions and cave hyenas hunted them. Scientists reconstruct this landscape from pollen, plant remains found in frozen soil and the stomach contents of preserved animals. Even so, the mammoth steppe was not identical everywhere. Some areas had more trees, especially in sheltered valleys and further south. Yet across thousands of kilometres, the land was open and rich in grazing animals, providing food for the people who lived there.
===
IMG: ice-age-people-band-walking-snowy-valley | a small group of Ice Age people dressed in fur clothing walking in a line through a snowy valley, carrying bundles and spears, a river beside them, low sun
HEAD: Ice Age people in Europe lived in small, mobile groups that moved with the seasons.
BODY: The people of Ice Age Europe did not live in permanent villages. They lived in small groups, often called bands by archaeologists. A band might include around twenty-five to fifty people, related by family and friendship. They moved across the landscape following animal herds and seasonal food sources. In summer, they might camp near rivers and open plains. In winter, they sought shelter in valleys, caves and protected hillsides. Archaeologists study these movements by examining campsites, animal bones and the sources of stone used for tools. Flint found at a camp sometimes came from a hundred kilometres or more away. Even so, groups did not wander aimlessly. They knew their territories well and returned to familiar places year after year. Their movements were planned around the seasons, the animals and the weather. Knowledge of the land, passed down over generations, was one of their most important tools for survival.
===
IMG: cro-magnon-family-campfire-night-stars | an Ice Age family sitting around a campfire at night under a starry sky, wrapped in furs, a hide tent behind them, warm firelight on their faces
HEAD: The people of the late Ice Age in Europe were modern humans, the same species as people today.
BODY: The people who lived in Europe twenty thousand years ago belonged to our own species, Homo sapiens. They are sometimes called Cro-Magnons, after a rock shelter in France where their remains were found in 1868. Their bodies and brains were essentially the same as those of people living today. They spoke languages, told stories, made art and cared for one another. Historians and archaeologists stress that they were not primitive in their thinking. They had to solve complex problems to survive in a harsh climate. Studies of skeletons show that many were tall and strongly built, with signs of an active life. Even so, their lives were very different from modern lives. They had no farms, no metal and no writing. Everything they used came from the land, the animals and their own skills. In the evening, families gathered around fires, as people have done for countless generations.

TOPIC: Who were the people of the Ice Age?
===
IMG: modern-humans-arrive-europe-forest-edge | a small group of early modern humans in hide clothing at the edge of a cold pine forest, looking out over a wide river valley, morning mist
HEAD: Modern humans arrived in Europe more than forty thousand years ago.
BODY: Homo sapiens first evolved in Africa, around three hundred thousand years ago. Over a long period, groups spread out of Africa into Asia and beyond. Modern humans reached Europe more than forty thousand years ago. Some of the earliest evidence comes from caves in Bulgaria and Italy, where human teeth and tools have been dated to around forty-five thousand years ago. Scientists use radiocarbon dating and the analysis of ancient DNA to study these early arrivals. DNA studies show that the first groups were later replaced or absorbed by other populations, which arrived in several waves. Even so, the details are still being researched. New discoveries regularly change the picture. By twenty thousand years ago, modern humans had lived in Europe for over twenty thousand years. They had adapted to the cold through clothing, shelter, fire and knowledge. Their descendants would eventually witness the end of the Ice Age.
===
IMG: neanderthal-memory-cave-shelter-mist | the entrance of a limestone cave on a misty hillside with a quiet old hearth inside, no people, soft grey light
HEAD: Before modern humans, Europe had been home to the Neanderthals for hundreds of thousands of years.
BODY: The Neanderthals were a different human species that lived in Europe and western Asia for a very long time. They were strong and well adapted to cold climates. They made stone tools, controlled fire, hunted large animals and buried some of their dead. Neanderthals disappeared around forty thousand years ago, not long after modern humans arrived. Scientists still debate why. Possible reasons include competition with modern humans, climate changes and small population sizes. Studies of ancient DNA show that Neanderthals and modern humans sometimes had children together. Most people living outside Africa today carry a small percentage of Neanderthal DNA. Even so, by the time of the Last Glacial Maximum, Neanderthals had been gone for about twenty thousand years. The people living in Europe at that time knew nothing of them. Yet caves once used by Neanderthals were sometimes later used by modern humans, as layers of ancient remains show.
===
IMG: ice-age-hunters-faces-fur-hoods-close | close portrait of two Ice Age hunters in fur hoods with weathered calm faces and red ochre marks, snowy background softly blurred
HEAD: Skeletons show that Ice Age people were often strong and healthy, though life was physically demanding.
BODY: Studies of skeletons from Ice Age Europe give a detailed picture of the people. Many adults were tall, sometimes taller than later farming populations. Their bones show strong muscle attachments, the result of a very active life of walking, carrying and hunting. Their teeth were often in good condition, since their diet contained little sugar. Archaeologists also find signs of injuries and illness. Some skeletons show healed broken bones, which suggests that others cared for the injured while they recovered. Scientists estimate life expectancy using these remains. Many children died young, as in most societies before modern medicine. Even so, those who survived childhood could live into their forties, fifties or longer. The people of the Ice Age were not short-lived savages. They were capable, skilled and resilient. Their health depended greatly on the availability of food, which changed with the seasons and with the movement of animals.
===
IMG: small-band-camp-river-terrace-tents | a small Ice Age camp of hide tents on a grassy river terrace, smoke rising from fires, people working outside, a wide river below, autumn light
HEAD: A typical Ice Age band was made up of several related families living and moving together.
BODY: Archaeologists believe that most people lived in groups of a few families, perhaps twenty-five to fifty people in total. Such groups were large enough to share work and protect each other, but small enough to find enough food. Studies of modern hunter-gatherer societies offer clues about how such bands may have worked, though historians are careful about making direct comparisons. In many such societies, decisions are made by discussion, and food is shared widely. Archaeological evidence from Ice Age campsites shows areas for different activities, such as cooking, tool making and processing hides. This suggests organised daily life. Even so, the details of social life are difficult to reconstruct. No written records exist, and much of what people did left no trace. Yet the evidence of shared hearths, cooperative hunting and careful burials points to communities in which people depended closely on one another for survival through the long winters.
===
IMG: bands-meeting-summer-gathering-river-valley | a large gathering of Ice Age people from several groups in a broad river valley in summer, many tents and fires, people talking and trading
HEAD: Different groups probably met at certain times of year to exchange goods, news and marriage partners.
BODY: Small bands could not survive in isolation forever. They needed contact with other groups to find marriage partners, share information and exchange goods. Archaeologists find evidence of such contacts in objects that travelled long distances. Shells from the Mediterranean and Atlantic coasts have been found at sites hundreds of kilometres inland. Special stones and amber also moved across great distances. Some large sites, with many hearths and objects, may have been places where several bands gathered, perhaps during seasons when food was plentiful. Researchers suggest that cave art sites may also have been meeting places. Even so, it is difficult to prove exactly how and when such gatherings took place. What is clear is that Ice Age people were connected across wide regions. Studies of ancient DNA support this, showing that populations across Europe were genetically related. These networks helped people survive during difficult times.
===
IMG: child-learning-flint-knapping-elder | an older Ice Age man showing a child how to shape a flint blade with a bone tool, sitting on a hide by a fire, flakes scattered around
HEAD: Children in the Ice Age learned essential skills by watching and helping adults.
BODY: There were no schools in the Ice Age. Children learned by observing and copying the adults around them. They learned how to make tools, prepare food, sew clothing and read the landscape. Archaeologists have found evidence of this learning process. At some sites, clusters of poorly made flint flakes lie next to expert work. Researchers interpret these as the attempts of apprentices, perhaps children, practising beside skilled toolmakers. Small handprints and footprints of children have also been found in caves, including in famous painted caves. Some prints show children walking with adults deep underground. Even so, much of children's lives is invisible in the archaeological record. Their toys, games and songs have mostly disappeared. Yet the evidence suggests that children were present in all parts of community life. Knowledge passed from generation to generation in this way was the foundation of survival through the Ice Age.

TOPIC: Shelters: rock shelters and mammoth-bone houses
===
IMG: rock-shelter-overhang-dordogne-valley-fire | a deep limestone rock overhang above a river valley with a small Ice Age camp beneath it, fires and hide windbreaks, autumn trees below
HEAD: Many Ice Age people in southwestern France lived under natural rock overhangs called rock shelters.
BODY: In the valleys of the Dordogne and Vézère rivers in southwestern France, limestone cliffs form natural overhangs. Archaeologists call these rock shelters, or abris in French. They offered protection from rain, snow and wind, and many faced south to catch the winter sun. People built fires beneath them and added walls of hides or wood to create sheltered living spaces. Excavations at sites such as the Abri Pataud and Laugerie-Haute have revealed thick layers of ancient occupation. These layers contain hearths, tools, animal bones and ornaments, showing that people returned to the same places over thousands of years. The region is so rich in Ice Age sites that it is sometimes called the valley of humanity. Even so, people did not usually live deep inside caves, which were dark and damp. They lived at cave entrances and under rock shelters, where there was light and fresh air.
===
IMG: cave-entrance-hearth-skins-drying-smoke | the wide entrance of a cave with a hearth, animal skins stretched on wooden frames to dry, smoke drifting out, snow outside
HEAD: Cave entrances offered shelter, but people rarely lived in the dark interiors of caves.
BODY: The popular image of cave people living deep inside caves is not accurate. Archaeologists find that most living areas were at cave entrances or under overhangs. Here, daylight was available, smoke could escape and the space was dry. Deep caves were cold, completely dark and often wet. People did enter them, sometimes for long distances, but mainly for special purposes, such as making art. At cave entrances, archaeologists find hearths, tools, animal bones and the remains of hides and other materials. Skins were stretched on frames to dry and were scraped and softened for clothing and tents. Even so, caves and rock shelters were not available everywhere. On the open plains of Central and Eastern Europe, there were few caves. People there built their own shelters from the materials around them. Some of these shelters were made from the bones of mammoths, the largest animals of the steppe.
===
IMG: mammoth-bone-hut-mezhyrich-snow | a round hut built of stacked mammoth jaw bones and long tusks, covered with hides, standing on a snowy plain beside a river, smoke rising
HEAD: On the plains of Ukraine, Ice Age people built round houses from mammoth bones.
BODY: At sites such as Mezhyrich in Ukraine, archaeologists have found the remains of remarkable dwellings built from mammoth bones. They date to around fifteen thousand years ago. The builders used large bones, such as jaws, skulls, leg bones and tusks, to make the walls and frame of round huts. The jaws were stacked in patterns, and tusks may have formed arches over entrances. The structures were probably covered with hides and turf for warmth. One hut at Mezhyrich contained bones from around a hundred and fifty mammoths. Archaeologists debate whether these bones came from animals hunted by the people or from mammoths that had died naturally. Most likely, both sources were used. Similar structures have been found at other sites, such as Kostenki in Russia. Even so, the huts show remarkable planning and effort. They provided sturdy shelters on open plains where wood was scarce, and some may have been used for many winters.
===
IMG: mammoth-bone-hut-interior-hearth-furs | the inside of a mammoth bone hut with a central hearth glowing, furs on the floor, tools and baskets around the walls, people resting
HEAD: Inside a mammoth-bone hut, a central hearth provided warmth and light through the winter.
BODY: The interior of a mammoth-bone dwelling was small, perhaps four to six metres across. In the centre was a hearth, where a fire burned. Since wood was scarce on the steppe, people often burned bones, which contain fat and burn well once a fire is hot. Archaeologists find burned bone in many Ice Age hearths. Around the hearth, the floor was probably covered with furs and hides for sleeping and sitting. Tools, food and clothing were stored near the walls. Storage pits dug near some huts held food, perhaps frozen meat for the winter. Historians and archaeologists reconstruct these interiors from the arrangement of objects found on excavated floors. Even so, much remains uncertain, since hides, wood and plant materials have long since decayed. Yet the evidence shows well-organised living spaces. Families shared a warm interior while snow and wind swept across the open plain outside, often for many months.
===
IMG: tent-hides-poles-reindeer-hunters-camp | a camp of conical tents made of reindeer hides over wooden poles on open tundra, reindeer antlers piled nearby, people working, late summer
HEAD: In many places, Ice Age people lived in tents made of animal hides stretched over wooden poles.
BODY: Where caves and mammoth bones were not available, people built tents. These were made of wooden poles covered with hides, especially reindeer and horse skins. Tents could be taken down and carried when the group moved. Archaeologists find traces of them as rings of stones that once held down the edges, or as patterns of post holes and hearths. Sites such as Pincevent in France, dated to around fourteen thousand years ago, show carefully arranged camps of reindeer hunters. Each tent had a hearth, and activities such as tool making took place in specific areas around it. Researchers have studied these camps in great detail, mapping the position of every flint flake and bone. Even so, the tents themselves have vanished. Their form is reconstructed from the remains and from comparisons with tents used by recent Arctic peoples. These comparisons suggest that hide tents could be surprisingly warm and practical.
===
IMG: hearth-stones-pit-embers-camp-evening | a carefully built hearth ringed with flat stones, glowing embers in a shallow pit, cooking stones nearby, a calm evening camp
HEAD: Hearths were the centre of daily life in Ice Age camps.
BODY: Fire was essential for survival during the Ice Age. It provided warmth, light and protection from predators. It made food easier to digest and allowed meat to be cooked and preserved by smoking. Ice Age hearths were often carefully built. Some were shallow pits lined with stones. Others were flat areas surrounded by rocks. Stones heated in the fire could be dropped into containers of water to boil it, a method known as stone boiling. Archaeologists find many such fire-cracked stones at Ice Age sites. They also study ash and charcoal to learn what fuels were burned. Wood was used where available, and bone and animal fat were used elsewhere. Even so, keeping a fire going required effort and planning. Fuel had to be gathered constantly. Around the hearth, people worked, ate, told stories and slept. Its glow was the centre of the home through the long, dark winter nights.
===
IMG: sleeping-furs-bedding-grass-shelter-night | a quiet Ice Age shelter at night with people sleeping under thick furs on bedding of dry grass, the hearth glowing low
HEAD: Ice Age people slept on bedding of grass and furs, close to the warmth of the fire.
BODY: Archaeologists have found evidence of bedding at several ancient sites. At Border Cave in South Africa, layers of grass bedding are much older than the European Ice Age, showing that people prepared comfortable sleeping places very early. In Ice Age Europe, people probably slept on layers of dry grass, moss or branches covered with thick furs. Furs of reindeer, wolves and other animals provided excellent insulation. Families likely slept close together for warmth. Some researchers suggest that ash from fires was spread under bedding to keep insects away, as found at some early sites. Even so, direct evidence of bedding in Ice Age Europe is rare, since plant materials decay quickly. Most reconstructions rely on comparisons and on small traces found during careful excavation. Sleep in winter was probably long, since nights lasted many hours. In the warmth of furs and firelight, people rested through the cold darkness until the late winter dawn.

TOPIC: Clothing and staying warm
===
IMG: sewing-bone-needle-sinew-firelight | a woman in fur clothing sewing a hide garment with a fine bone needle and sinew thread by firelight, small tools laid out on a hide
HEAD: The invention of the eyed bone needle allowed Ice Age people to sew close-fitting clothing.
BODY: One of the most important inventions of the Ice Age was the bone needle with an eye. The oldest known examples come from Denisova Cave in Siberia and are around forty thousand years old or more. In Europe, eyed needles became common after about twenty-five thousand years ago. They were carefully made from bone or ivory, ground smooth and pierced with a tiny hole. Thread was made from sinew, the strong tissue of animal tendons, or from plant fibres. With needles, people could sew tailored clothing that fitted the body closely. Such clothing kept heat in far better than loose wraps. Archaeologists consider the needle a major step in adapting to cold climates. Even so, the clothes themselves have not survived. Their form is reconstructed from the tools, from art and from burials. The small needles found at many sites show skilled, patient work carried out over countless winter evenings.
===
IMG: tailored-fur-parka-hood-trousers | an Ice Age man standing in snow wearing a tailored fur parka with a hood, fur trousers and boots, holding a spear, calm expression
HEAD: Ice Age clothing probably included hooded parkas, trousers, boots and mittens made of fur and hide.
BODY: Researchers believe that Ice Age people wore layered clothing similar to that of recent Arctic peoples. An outer layer of fur, such as reindeer or wolf, kept out wind and cold. An inner layer of softer hide could be worn next to the skin. Hooded parkas protected the head and neck, while trousers, boots and mittens protected the limbs. Reindeer fur is particularly warm, because each hair is hollow and traps air. Wolf and wolverine fur were later valued by Arctic peoples for hoods because they resist frost. Archaeologists find evidence of fur processing in the bones of animals with cut marks around the paws, showing that skins were removed carefully. Even so, no complete Ice Age garment has survived in Europe. The details of design remain uncertain. Yet the evidence of needles, scrapers and fur-bearing animals shows that clothing was carefully made. Without it, survival in the cold would have been impossible.
===
IMG: sungir-burial-beads-clothing-reconstruction | a museum reconstruction of Ice Age clothing decorated with thousands of small ivory beads, soft display lighting
HEAD: Burials at Sungir in Russia show clothing decorated with thousands of ivory beads.
BODY: Sungir is an archaeological site near the town of Vladimir in Russia, dated to around thirty-four thousand years ago. Excavations there uncovered several remarkable burials. An adult man was buried with around three thousand beads made of mammoth ivory. Two children were buried together with around ten thousand beads, as well as ivory spears and carved animals. The beads lay in rows across the bodies, showing that they had been sewn onto clothing, such as shirts, trousers and caps. Researchers have estimated that each bead took perhaps an hour to make. The burials therefore represent thousands of hours of work. Historians and archaeologists interpret this as a sign of great care and respect for the dead. Even so, the meaning of the burials is debated. The children may have held a special status. Yet the outlines of the beads show the shape of Ice Age clothing more clearly than any other find.
===
IMG: reindeer-hide-scraping-woman-camp | an Ice Age woman scraping a reindeer hide stretched on a wooden frame with a stone scraper, outside a tent, a child watching
HEAD: Preparing animal hides was a long and skilled task, done with stone scrapers.
BODY: Turning an animal skin into usable leather or fur took many steps. First, the skin was removed carefully from the animal. Then the inner side was scraped clean of fat and flesh using stone tools called scrapers. These are among the most common tools found at Ice Age sites. The hide was then dried, stretched and softened. Some methods used animal brains, fat or smoke to make the hide supple and waterproof. Red ochre, a natural iron pigment, may also have been rubbed into hides to help preserve them. Archaeologists study wear marks on stone scrapers under microscopes to identify their use on hides. Even so, the exact methods varied between groups and regions. The work required great skill and patience. A single garment could need several hides. Preparing enough skins for a family's clothing, bedding and tents was one of the most important tasks of the year, especially before winter.
===
IMG: fur-boots-mittens-snow-tracks | close view of fur-lined hide boots and mittens on a person walking through fresh snow, footprints behind, soft winter light
HEAD: Protecting the hands and feet from frostbite was essential for survival in the Ice Age.
BODY: In extreme cold, hands and feet lose heat quickly. Frostbite could cause permanent damage or death. Ice Age people therefore needed warm, well-made boots and mittens. Researchers believe they wore boots of hide with fur on the inside, possibly lined with dry grass for extra insulation, as recent Arctic peoples did. Some scientists have studied the bones of feet from Ice Age skeletons. They found that toe bones became more delicate around forty thousand years ago, which may indicate the regular use of supportive footwear. Even so, this interpretation is debated. Footprints preserved in some caves show bare feet, perhaps because people removed their boots in damp underground passages. Mittens and gloves are rarely shown in art, but they were almost certainly used. Keeping footwear and mittens dry was also important. Wet clothing loses its warmth, so items were dried carefully by the fire every evening.
===
IMG: fire-making-flint-pyrite-sparks | close view of hands striking a flint against a lump of pyrite to make sparks over dry tinder fungus, a dark background
HEAD: Ice Age people could make fire by striking flint against a mineral called pyrite.
BODY: Making fire was a vital skill. One method used in the Ice Age was striking a piece of flint against iron pyrite, a shiny mineral. The impact produced sparks, which could light dry tinder, such as fungus, grass or fine wood shavings. Archaeologists have found lumps of pyrite with grooves and wear marks at several Ice Age sites. Researchers have also studied flint tools with microscopic traces that match this use. Another method used friction, rubbing wood together, though this leaves less evidence. Even so, people probably tried to keep fires burning rather than lighting new ones constantly. Embers could be carried in containers from one camp to another. Historians note that the ability to make fire at will gave people great independence. They could survive in places where natural fires were rare. Fire also protected camps from predators and made long winter nights bearable.
===
IMG: family-sleeping-close-hearth-furs-night | an Ice Age family resting close together under furs beside a low fire inside a hide tent, a child asleep, warm amber light
HEAD: Shared warmth, food and care helped Ice Age families survive the long winters.
BODY: Survival in the Ice Age depended on cooperation. Clothing, shelter and fire were the products of shared work. Families and bands divided tasks such as hunting, gathering, preparing hides and caring for children. Archaeological evidence suggests that people looked after those who could not work. Some skeletons show long-term injuries or disabilities that would have required help from others. Historians and archaeologists interpret this as evidence of care and compassion. Even so, life was difficult. Food shortages, illness and accidents were common. Winters were dangerous for the very young and the very old. Yet the long survival of Ice Age populations across thousands of years shows remarkable resilience. Their knowledge of animals, plants and weather allowed them to live in conditions that would challenge any modern person. At the heart of this survival was the family, gathered around the fire, sharing warmth through the darkest months of the year.
