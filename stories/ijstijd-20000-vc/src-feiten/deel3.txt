TOPIC: Art in the caves
===
IMG: lascaux-painted-horses-lamplight | painted horses and aurochs on a cave wall in red, black and yellow, lit softly by a small stone lamp, deep shadows
HEAD: The cave of Lascaux in France contains some of the most famous Ice Age paintings, made about seventeen thousand years ago.
BODY: Lascaux is a cave in the Vézère valley in southwestern France. It was discovered in September 1940 by four teenagers, after a dog reportedly disappeared into a hole. Inside, they found walls covered with paintings of horses, aurochs, deer, bison and other animals. Scientists date the paintings to around seventeen thousand years ago. The artists used red, yellow and black pigments made from ochre, manganese and charcoal. Some animals are more than five metres long. The largest chamber is known as the Hall of the Bulls. Historians and archaeologists consider Lascaux one of the greatest achievements of Ice Age art. Even so, the cave proved fragile. After it opened to the public in 1948, the breath of many visitors caused damage and the growth of mould. It was closed to the public in 1963. Today, only a few scientists may enter. Visitors see accurate replicas built nearby instead.
===
IMG: chauvet-lions-charcoal-wall | a cave wall with a group of lions drawn in charcoal, shaded with smudging, natural rock curves giving depth, dim light
HEAD: The cave of Chauvet in France contains some of the oldest known cave paintings, made more than thirty thousand years ago.
BODY: Chauvet Cave is located in the Ardèche region of southern France. It was discovered in December 1994 by three cave explorers, led by Jean-Marie Chauvet. Inside, they found hundreds of paintings and engravings of animals. Radiocarbon dating showed that many were made around thirty-six thousand years ago, far older than scientists had expected for such sophisticated art. The paintings include lions, rhinoceroses, mammoths, horses, bears and owls. Many animals that were rarely painted elsewhere appear here. The artists used the natural shape of the rock to give the animals depth and movement. Some lions are shaded with smudged charcoal to suggest form. Historians note that Chauvet changed understanding of early art, showing that great skill existed very early. Even so, the cave was sealed by a rockfall thousands of years ago, which preserved it perfectly. It is closed to the public, but a replica called Chauvet Two opened nearby in 2015.
===
IMG: painter-blowing-ochre-handprint-wall | a person in the dark blowing red pigment through a hollow bone over their hand pressed against a cave wall, leaving a stencil, lamp light
HEAD: Ice Age artists made hand stencils by blowing pigment around their hands pressed against cave walls.
BODY: Among the most personal images of the Ice Age are hand stencils. They were made by placing a hand on the cave wall and blowing pigment around it, either from the mouth or through a hollow bone or reed. When the hand was removed, its outline remained. Hand stencils are found in many caves in Europe, such as Gargas in France and El Castillo in Spain. Some in El Castillo may be more than forty thousand years old. Researchers have studied the size of the hands to estimate the age and sex of the people who made them. The results suggest that both women and men, as well as children, made stencils. In some caves, fingers appear shortened, perhaps because they were bent or because of injury. Even so, the meaning of hand stencils remains uncertain. They may have been signatures, symbols or part of rituals. Yet they bring people very close to the individuals who once stood in those caves.
===
IMG: venus-figurine-carved-ivory-small | a small carved ivory female figurine from the Ice Age displayed on dark fabric, softly lit, worn smooth surface
HEAD: Small carved female figurines, often called Venus figurines, were made across Europe during the Ice Age.
BODY: Archaeologists have found more than two hundred small female figurines from Ice Age Europe. They were carved from ivory, stone, bone or modelled in clay. The best known is the Venus of Willendorf, found in Austria in 1908. It is made of limestone and is around thirty thousand years old. The oldest known example, the Venus of Hohle Fels from Germany, is carved from mammoth ivory and is around forty thousand years old. Many figurines emphasise the hips, belly and breasts, while faces are often not shown. The name Venus was given by early researchers and does not reflect the original meaning. Historians and archaeologists have proposed many interpretations. The figurines may represent fertility, motherhood, ancestors or simply women of the community. Even so, no single explanation is accepted. The figurines were made over a long period and across a wide area, so their meanings may have varied. They remain among the most discussed objects of the Ice Age.
===
IMG: lion-man-ivory-figurine-hohlenstein | an ivory figurine of a standing figure with a lion's head and human body, displayed in a museum case against dark background
HEAD: The Lion Man, carved from mammoth ivory around forty thousand years ago, is one of the oldest known figurative sculptures.
BODY: In 1939, archaeologists excavating a cave called Hohlenstein-Stadel in southern Germany found fragments of carved mammoth ivory. Decades later, the pieces were assembled into a figure about thirty-one centimetres tall. It shows a standing figure with a human body and the head of a cave lion. It is known as the Lion Man. Scientists date it to around forty thousand years ago. Experiments with replica tools suggest that carving it would have taken several hundred hours of work. Historians see the Lion Man as evidence of imagination and belief, since it shows a being that does not exist in nature. It may represent a spirit, a mythical figure or a person in a ritual costume. Even so, its exact meaning is unknown. The Lion Man is now displayed in the Ulm Museum in Germany. The caves of the Swabian Jura, where it was found, were listed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2017.
===
IMG: bone-flute-vulture-music-cave | a long thin bird bone flute with finger holes from the Ice Age lying on dark fabric, warm side light
HEAD: Flutes made from bird bones and mammoth ivory are among the oldest known musical instruments.
BODY: Music was part of life in the Ice Age. At Hohle Fels and other caves in southern Germany, archaeologists have found flutes made from the wing bones of birds, such as a griffon vulture and swans, and from mammoth ivory. The most complete one, made from a vulture bone, is about twenty-two centimetres long and has five finger holes. It has been dated to around forty thousand years ago. Researchers have made replicas and found that they can produce a range of clear notes. Making an ivory flute was especially difficult, since the ivory had to be split, hollowed and fitted back together. Historians note that these instruments suggest that music played an important role in social life, perhaps in gatherings, rituals or storytelling. Even so, no melody from the Ice Age can be known. Singing, drumming and other instruments made of wood or skin probably existed too, but they have not survived.
===
IMG: altamira-bison-ceiling-painting | a cave ceiling covered with painted bison in red and black, using natural bulges of the rock, soft lamplight from below
HEAD: The painted ceiling of Altamira in Spain shows a group of bison in remarkable colour and detail.
BODY: The cave of Altamira is located near the town of Santillana del Mar in northern Spain. Its paintings were discovered in 1879 by Marcelino Sanz de Sautuola and his young daughter María. The most famous chamber has a low ceiling covered with paintings of bison, shown in red and black. The artists used the natural bulges of the rock to give the animals a three-dimensional appearance. When the discovery was first announced, many experts refused to believe that such fine art could be prehistoric. Sautuola was accused of forgery. Only after similar paintings were found in France around 1900 was Altamira accepted as genuine. Scientists now date different parts of the art to between about thirty-six thousand and fifteen thousand years ago. Even so, the cave suffered from too many visitors and has been mostly closed for conservation. A detailed replica stands in the nearby museum, where visitors can see the painted ceiling.

TOPIC: Beliefs, burials and community
===
IMG: sungir-burial-ivory-beads-grave | an excavated Ice Age grave covered with rows of small ivory beads and red ochre, archaeologists' brushes nearby, soft light
HEAD: Ice Age people buried some of their dead with great care, using ochre, ornaments and objects.
BODY: Burials from the Ice Age show that people cared deeply for their dead. In many graves, bodies were covered with red ochre and accompanied by objects such as beads, tools and carved items. The burials at Sungir in Russia are among the richest. Others are known from sites such as Dolní Věstonice in the Czech Republic and Arene Candide in Italy. At Arene Candide, a young man known as the Young Prince was buried with a cap of shell beads and ochre, around twenty-eight thousand years ago. Historians and archaeologists see these burials as evidence of beliefs about death and perhaps an afterlife. Even so, only a small number of people seem to have been buried in this way. Most Ice Age burials are rare, and many people may have been treated differently after death. The careful burials that survive show love, respect and shared ritual. They reveal the emotional lives of people who lived thousands of years ago.
===
IMG: dolni-vestonice-clay-figurine-kiln | a small fired clay figurine from the Ice Age beside a simple hearth used as a kiln, glowing embers, an open landscape at dusk
HEAD: At Dolní Věstonice in the Czech Republic, Ice Age people made figurines of fired clay, long before pottery.
BODY: Dolní Věstonice is an important Ice Age site in Moravia, in the Czech Republic, dated to around twenty-nine thousand years ago. Archaeologists found evidence of mammoth hunters' dwellings, hearths and burials. They also found more than two thousand pieces of fired clay, including a famous female figurine known as the Venus of Dolní Věstonice. It is one of the oldest known objects made of fired clay. Clay figures of animals were also found. Some pieces seem to have exploded in the fire, perhaps deliberately. Researchers have suggested that this may have been part of a ritual. Fingerprints preserved on some of the clay show that people of different ages handled it, including children. Even so, people at Dolní Věstonice did not make pottery for cooking. Pottery came much later in Europe. The site also contained a triple burial of young people, laid out carefully with ochre and ornaments.
===
IMG: group-dance-firelight-cave-music | a group of Ice Age people dancing in a circle in a large cave chamber lit by fires and lamps, shadows on painted walls, a flute player
HEAD: Many researchers believe that painted caves were places for gatherings, rituals and music.
BODY: The purpose of cave art has been debated for more than a century. Early researchers suggested that it was art for its own sake. Others proposed that it was hunting magic, meant to bring success in the hunt. Later, some researchers argued that the images had religious or shamanic meanings, connected with spirits and other worlds. Recent studies of acoustics have found that paintings are often located in parts of caves where sound echoes strongly. This suggests that music or chanting may have taken place there. Footprints in some caves show groups of people walking together. Historians note that no single theory explains all cave art. Paintings were made over more than twenty thousand years and may have had many meanings. Even so, it is clear that caves were special places. Entering them required lamps, effort and courage. The art suggests that Ice Age people shared stories and beliefs that were important enough to record deep underground.
===
IMG: shell-beads-necklace-trade-routes | a necklace of small pierced sea shells and carved ivory beads from the Ice Age laid on a hide, soft light
HEAD: Ornaments such as shell beads and pierced teeth were worn and traded across long distances.
BODY: Ice Age people wore ornaments, such as necklaces and decorations sewn onto clothing. Common materials included pierced shells, animal teeth, ivory beads and carved stones. Shells from the Mediterranean and Atlantic coasts have been found at sites far inland, hundreds of kilometres from the sea. Researchers believe they were passed from group to group through exchange networks. Pierced teeth of animals such as foxes, deer and bears were also popular. Archaeologists study the wear on beads to learn how they were strung and worn. Historians suggest that ornaments signalled identity, belonging or status. They might show which group a person belonged to, or mark important life events. Even so, the meanings are difficult to recover. What is clear is that people valued beauty and personal decoration, just as people do today. Beads were among the most common objects people made, and they were often carefully repaired and reused.
===
IMG: healing-care-injured-elder-hearth | an older Ice Age man with a bandaged leg resting by a hearth while a younger woman brings him food, inside a hide shelter
HEAD: Some Ice Age skeletons show that injured or disabled people were cared for by others over long periods.
BODY: Archaeologists have found Ice Age skeletons with healed fractures and long-term disabilities. Some people survived serious injuries that would have left them unable to hunt or travel for months. Their survival suggests that others provided food, shelter and care. Similar evidence exists from earlier times, including among Neanderthals. Historians and archaeologists see this as a sign of strong social bonds. Caring for vulnerable members of the group would have required effort and resources. One well-known example is a man buried at Dolní Věstonice who had physical abnormalities, yet was buried with care. Even so, interpreting such evidence requires caution. Researchers cannot always tell how severe an injury was or how long a person needed help. Yet the pattern across many sites suggests that compassion was part of Ice Age life. The people of the Ice Age survived not only through skill and toughness, but also through looking after one another.
===
IMG: gathering-many-bands-feast-summer-river | a large summer gathering of Ice Age families by a wide river, people sharing roasted meat, children running, many tents, warm evening
HEAD: Large seasonal gatherings may have strengthened ties between Ice Age groups.
BODY: Some Ice Age sites are much larger than ordinary camps. They contain many hearths, large numbers of animal bones and a wide variety of objects. Researchers have suggested that such sites may have been places where several bands gathered, perhaps during seasons of plenty, such as the autumn reindeer migration. Similar gatherings are known among many recent hunter-gatherer peoples. They are occasions for feasting, trading, arranging marriages, settling disputes and performing ceremonies. Historians note that such meetings helped spread ideas and techniques across large regions. The similarity of art styles and tools over wide areas of Ice Age Europe may reflect these connections. Even so, it is difficult to prove that a particular site was a gathering place. Large sites might also result from repeated use by small groups over many years. Yet the idea of seasonal gatherings fits the evidence well. It suggests that Ice Age life combined small family groups with wider communities.

TOPIC: The end of the Ice Age
===
IMG: glaciers-melting-meltwater-lakes-spring | a retreating glacier with large blue meltwater lakes in front of it, streams flowing, green moss beginning to grow on bare rock
HEAD: After about nineteen thousand years ago, the climate slowly began to warm, and the great ice sheets began to melt.
BODY: The Last Glacial Maximum did not last forever. From about nineteen thousand years ago, changes in the Earth's orbit increased the amount of summer sunlight in the northern hemisphere. Temperatures began to rise, and the ice sheets slowly began to melt. The process took thousands of years and was not smooth. There were sudden warm phases and sudden cold returns. One cold return, known as the Younger Dryas, began around twelve thousand nine hundred years ago and lasted about twelve hundred years. Then temperatures rose rapidly again. Scientists date the official end of the last Ice Age to about eleven thousand seven hundred years ago, the start of the period called the Holocene. Ice cores from Greenland record these changes year by year. Even so, the effects were felt differently in each region. Melting ice created huge lakes and floods. Sea levels rose, and the landscape of Europe changed dramatically.
===
IMG: forests-spread-birch-pine-tundra-retreat | young birch and pine forests spreading across former tundra, red deer among the trees, soft green light, a river winding through
HEAD: As the climate warmed, forests spread across the open grasslands of Europe.
BODY: With rising temperatures and increasing rainfall, the landscape of Europe changed. Trees that had survived in sheltered southern areas spread northward. First came birch and pine, followed later by oak, elm and hazel. Pollen preserved in lake sediments allows scientists to trace this spread in detail. The open mammoth steppe gradually disappeared, replaced by forests and wetlands. Animals adapted to open, cold conditions lost their habitats. Reindeer moved north, following the retreating tundra. Forest animals, such as red deer, roe deer, wild boar and aurochs, became more common. Historians note that this change transformed human life. People adapted their hunting methods, using bows and arrows in forests. They gathered more plant foods, fish and shellfish. Even so, the change took many generations. Each generation would have seen only small changes. Yet over thousands of years, the world of the Ice Age hunters gave way to a very different landscape.
===
IMG: doggerland-flooding-coast-rising-sea | a low coastal landscape with the sea slowly flooding marshes and small islands, waves washing over grassland, a deserted camp
HEAD: Rising seas gradually flooded Doggerland and separated Britain from mainland Europe.
BODY: As the ice sheets melted, sea levels rose by more than a hundred metres over several thousand years. Low-lying lands were slowly flooded. Doggerland, the plain that once connected Britain with mainland Europe, shrank into a series of islands and marshes. Around eight thousand years ago, a huge underwater landslide off the coast of Norway, known as the Storegga Slide, caused a tsunami that struck the coasts of the North Sea. Researchers believe it may have flooded much of what remained of Doggerland. By about seven thousand years ago, Britain had become an island. Archaeologists and geologists study the drowned landscape using maps of the sea floor and samples taken by drilling. Even so, much remains unknown. People who lived in Doggerland had to move as their land disappeared. Their camps and hunting grounds now lie under the North Sea, where fishing boats still occasionally bring up their tools.
===
IMG: mammoths-last-herds-wrangel-island | a small group of woolly mammoths walking along the shore of a remote Arctic island, grey sea and low clouds
HEAD: Woolly mammoths disappeared from most of their range at the end of the Ice Age, but survived on a remote island much longer.
BODY: As forests replaced grasslands, woolly mammoths lost their habitat. Their populations declined across Europe, Asia and North America. Most had disappeared by about ten thousand years ago. Scientists debate the relative importance of climate change and human hunting in their extinction. Many think both played a role. However, small populations survived on isolated islands. On Saint Paul Island in Alaska, mammoths lived until about five and a half thousand years ago. On Wrangel Island, off the coast of northeastern Siberia, they survived until around four thousand years ago. This means that mammoths were still alive when the pyramids of Egypt were being built. Studies of ancient DNA from Wrangel Island mammoths show that the population had become very small and genetically limited. Even so, the reasons for their final disappearance remain uncertain. Their story shows how slowly the world of the Ice Age faded.
===
IMG: hunters-new-forests-red-deer-bow | a hunter with a wooden bow walking quietly through a young forest of birch and pine, red deer in the distance, morning light
HEAD: After the Ice Age, people in Europe adapted to forests, using bows, fishing and gathering more plant foods.
BODY: The period after the Ice Age in Europe is called the Mesolithic, or Middle Stone Age. People continued to live by hunting, fishing and gathering, but their way of life changed. In forests, they hunted red deer, roe deer and wild boar with bows and arrows tipped with small flint points, called microliths. They fished in rivers and lakes and gathered shellfish on the coasts. Some groups built dugout canoes and made wooden fish traps. One famous Mesolithic site in Britain is Star Carr in Yorkshire, where people lived about eleven thousand years ago. Archaeologists found antler headdresses and remains of wooden platforms there. Historians note that Mesolithic people were the direct descendants of Ice Age hunters in many regions. Even so, new groups also arrived and mixed with them. Ancient DNA shows several movements of people across Europe during this period. Their lives were shaped by the new forests and coasts.
===
IMG: first-farms-fields-near-east-later | a small early farming village of mud brick houses beside green fields of wheat in a warm valley, people working, soft evening light
HEAD: Farming began in the Near East around eleven thousand years ago, soon after the end of the Ice Age.
BODY: The warmer and more stable climate after the Ice Age created new possibilities. In the Near East, in a region sometimes called the Fertile Crescent, people began to cultivate wild cereals such as wheat and barley. They also began to domesticate animals such as sheep, goats and cattle. This development, known as the Neolithic Revolution, began around eleven thousand years ago. Farming spread gradually into Europe, reaching Greece around eight and a half thousand years ago and Britain around six thousand years ago. Ancient DNA studies show that farming spread partly through the movement of farming people from Anatolia into Europe. They mixed with the descendants of Ice Age hunters. Even so, hunting and gathering continued in many regions for a long time. Historians see the end of the Ice Age as the beginning of a new chapter in human history. Yet modern Europeans still carry part of the ancestry of the Ice Age hunters in their DNA.

TOPIC: How we know: archaeology and science
===
IMG: archaeologists-excavating-cave-floor-layers | archaeologists carefully excavating the floor of a cave entrance with trowels and brushes, layers of soil visible, string grid, lamps
HEAD: Archaeologists learn about Ice Age life by carefully excavating layers of soil at ancient sites.
BODY: Ice Age sites are usually buried beneath layers of soil, sand or rock that built up over thousands of years. Archaeologists excavate them slowly and carefully, layer by layer. Each layer represents a different period of time. Archaeologists record the exact position of every object, from stone tools to animal bones and pieces of charcoal. They use grids, measurements and photographs to create detailed maps. Soil samples are collected and sieved to find tiny objects, such as beads, seeds and fish bones. The study of how sites formed is called site formation analysis. It helps researchers understand which objects belong together. Historians note that excavation methods have improved greatly since the nineteenth century, when many sites were dug quickly and carelessly. Even so, excavation is destructive, since each layer is removed. For this reason, modern archaeologists often leave parts of sites untouched for future researchers, who may have better methods.
===
IMG: radiocarbon-lab-samples-scientist | a scientist in a laboratory preparing small samples of ancient bone and charcoal for dating, glass vials and precise instruments
HEAD: Radiocarbon dating allows scientists to determine the age of bones, charcoal and other organic remains.
BODY: Radiocarbon dating was developed in the late 1940s by the American chemist Willard Libby, who received the Nobel Prize for it in 1960. All living things absorb carbon, including a small amount of a radioactive form called carbon fourteen. After death, the carbon fourteen slowly decays at a known rate. By measuring how much remains, scientists can calculate how long ago the organism died. The method works for remains up to about fifty thousand years old, which covers the late Ice Age. Modern laboratories use machines called accelerator mass spectrometers, which need only tiny samples. This allows scientists to date even small pieces of charcoal from cave paintings. Even so, radiocarbon dates must be calibrated, because the amount of carbon fourteen in the atmosphere has varied over time. Researchers use tree rings, cave formations and other records to make these corrections. Calibrated dates have greatly improved the accuracy of Ice Age chronology.
===
IMG: ancient-dna-lab-clean-room-bone | a scientist in a white clean room suit drilling a tiny sample from an ancient bone in a sterile laboratory, soft blue light
HEAD: The study of ancient DNA has revealed how Ice Age populations moved and mixed across Europe.
BODY: In recent decades, scientists have learned to extract DNA from ancient bones and teeth. The work must be done in very clean laboratories, since modern DNA can easily contaminate samples. The Swedish geneticist Svante Pääbo, a pioneer of this field, received the Nobel Prize in 2022 for his work on ancient human DNA, including the Neanderthal genome. Ancient DNA studies have shown that the population of Ice Age Europe changed several times. Some groups disappeared during the coldest periods, while others expanded from refuges in southern Europe. Studies have also revealed details such as eye and skin colour. Some Ice Age Europeans appear to have had dark skin and blue eyes. Even so, ancient DNA research is complex, and results are sometimes revised as new samples are studied. Historians note that it has transformed understanding of prehistory. Combined with archaeology, it allows researchers to trace the movements of people across thousands of years.
===
IMG: permafrost-cliff-siberia-mammoth-tusk | a melting permafrost cliff on a Siberian riverbank with a large mammoth tusk emerging from the frozen soil, grey summer light
HEAD: Frozen ground in Siberia and Alaska preserves Ice Age animals in remarkable detail.
BODY: In the far north, the ground beneath the surface has remained frozen since the Ice Age. This frozen ground, called permafrost, acts like a natural freezer. It has preserved the bodies of Ice Age animals, including mammoths, woolly rhinoceroses, cave lion cubs, horses and wolves, sometimes with fur, skin and internal organs intact. Many finds are made when riverbanks erode in summer or when local people search for mammoth ivory. Scientists study these remains to learn about the animals' appearance, diet, health and genetics. In 2018, a well-preserved foal of an extinct horse species was found in a Siberian crater. Even so, warming temperatures are now causing permafrost to thaw more rapidly. This exposes more remains, but also causes them to decay quickly once uncovered. Scientists race to study and preserve them. The permafrost is one of the most important archives of Ice Age life, and it is changing quickly.
===
IMG: museum-mammoth-skeleton-hall-visitors | a tall woolly mammoth skeleton displayed in a quiet museum hall with soft lighting, a few visitors looking up
HEAD: Museums around the world display Ice Age skeletons, tools and art.
BODY: Many museums tell the story of the Ice Age. In France, the National Museum of Prehistory in Les Eyzies displays tools, art and remains from the Vézère valley. In Germany, the Blaubeuren museum shows finds from the Swabian caves, including ivory figurines and flutes. In Britain, the Natural History Museum in London displays mammoth and other Ice Age skeletons. In Russia, the Zoological Museum in Saint Petersburg holds famous frozen mammoths. Historians note that museums allow people to see objects that would otherwise remain in storage or in fragile sites. Many museums now use reconstructions, models and digital displays to show what Ice Age life may have looked like. Even so, reconstructions involve interpretation. Museums often explain which details are certain and which are uncertain. A visit to such a museum allows people to stand face to face with the tools, ornaments and art of people who lived tens of thousands of years ago.
===
IMG: replica-cave-lascaux-iv-visitors-quiet | visitors walking quietly through a faithful replica of a painted cave, soft lighting on painted animals, hushed atmosphere
HEAD: Replicas allow visitors to experience famous painted caves without damaging the originals.
BODY: Since the original caves are too fragile for regular visits, several detailed replicas have been built. Lascaux Two opened in 1983, recreating the main chambers of Lascaux. A larger and more complete replica, known as Lascaux Four, opened in 2016 near the town of Montignac. It reproduces almost the entire cave, using 3D scanning, sculpting and painting by skilled artists. The replica of Chauvet, called the Grotte Chauvet Two, opened in 2015 and reproduces the most important parts of the cave. Altamira also has a replica in its museum. Historians and archaeologists note that these replicas help protect the original sites while allowing the public to understand Ice Age art. Even so, some visitors miss the experience of the real caves. Yet the replicas are remarkably faithful. Lighting, temperature and sound are carefully designed. Walking through them, visitors can see the animals painted by Ice Age artists much as they first appeared.

TOPIC: The Ice Age today
===
IMG: glacier-today-alps-blue-ice-valley | a modern glacier in the Alps with blue crevasses and a wide U-shaped valley below, hikers small on a path, clear sky
HEAD: Glaciers and valleys shaped by the Ice Age can still be seen across Europe today.
BODY: The landscapes of Europe still show the effects of the last Ice Age. Mountain glaciers in the Alps, Scandinavia and Iceland are smaller remnants of the great ice of the past. Many valleys in mountain regions have a broad U shape, carved by glaciers. Lakes such as those of the Alps, Scotland and Scandinavia often fill hollows scraped out by ice. In northern Europe and North America, boulders carried by ice lie scattered across fields. Historians and geologists use these features to reconstruct the extent of the ice. Even so, today's glaciers are shrinking rapidly. Many Alpine glaciers have lost much of their volume in recent decades. Scientists measure these changes carefully. They help researchers understand both the past and the present climate. Visitors to mountain regions can still walk to glacier edges and see the polished rock and moraines left behind by ice that once covered much of the land.
===
IMG: valley-dordogne-today-cliffs-river-calm | the Vézère river valley in France today with limestone cliffs, green trees and a small village, calm summer evening light
HEAD: The Vézère valley in France, rich in Ice Age sites, is now a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
BODY: The Vézère valley in the Dordogne region of southwestern France contains one of the greatest concentrations of Ice Age sites in the world. It includes Lascaux, many decorated caves and dozens of rock shelters. In 1979, the prehistoric sites and decorated caves of the valley were listed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The village of Les Eyzies is often called the capital of prehistory. Its museum stands beneath a cliff where Ice Age people once lived. Today, the valley is a quiet landscape of limestone cliffs, woods, meadows and small villages along the river. Visitors come to see the caves, museums and rock shelters. Historians note that the valley's sites have been studied since the nineteenth century and have shaped understanding of the Ice Age. Even so, research continues, and new discoveries are still made. In the evening, the river flows quietly beneath the cliffs where people lived twenty thousand years ago.
===
IMG: reindeer-herders-tundra-today-arctic | a modern reindeer herd on the Arctic tundra with a herder in traditional clothing, snow-covered hills, low winter sun
HEAD: Reindeer still live on the Arctic tundra, where some peoples continue to herd them today.
BODY: Reindeer, once a central part of Ice Age life in Europe, now live mainly in the Arctic and subarctic regions of Scandinavia, Russia and North America, where they are known as caribou. Several Arctic peoples, such as the Sámi of northern Scandinavia and the Nenets of Siberia, herd reindeer as part of their traditional way of life. Their knowledge of cold climates, clothing and animal behaviour has helped researchers imagine how Ice Age people may have lived. Historians and anthropologists caution, however, that modern Arctic peoples are not living copies of Ice Age societies. They have their own long histories and cultures. Even so, their skills show what is possible in extreme cold. Fur clothing, hide tents and careful use of every part of the animal are practical solutions that people have developed again and again. The sight of a reindeer herd moving across the snowy tundra recalls the landscapes of twenty thousand years ago.
===
IMG: cave-entrance-today-quiet-forest-light | the entrance of a limestone cave today surrounded by green forest and ferns, sunlight filtering through leaves, quiet and peaceful
HEAD: Many Ice Age caves now lie quietly in forests and hillsides, protected for the future.
BODY: Across Europe, hundreds of caves and rock shelters once used by Ice Age people now lie in peaceful landscapes. Many are protected by law and closed to the public to preserve their fragile contents. Some have gates to prevent damage. Others are open for guided visits in small groups, with strict limits on numbers. Scientists monitor temperature, humidity and air quality inside painted caves to protect the art. Historians note that the protection of these sites is a responsibility shared by many countries. Even so, threats remain, including climate change, tourism and vandalism. New caves continue to be discovered. In 1991, the Cosquer Cave was found near Marseille, with an entrance now deep under the sea, since sea levels have risen since the Ice Age. Its paintings include seals and penguin-like great auks. Each new discovery adds to the picture of life during the Ice Age.

HEAD: The Ice Age shaped the land, the animals and the people of Europe in ways that are still visible today.
BODY: The last Ice Age lasted for tens of thousands of years. At its coldest, around twenty thousand years ago, ice covered much of the north, and people lived in a world of tundra, steppe and great herds. They survived through skill, cooperation and knowledge. They made needles, clothing, tools, lamps and shelters of hide and bone. They painted animals on cave walls, carved figures and played flutes. They cared for each other and buried their dead with ornaments. Historians, archaeologists and scientists continue to study their lives through excavation, dating and ancient DNA. Even so, much remains unknown. Their languages, stories and names are lost. Yet their descendants live on, and part of their ancestry survives in people today. The landscape, too, remembers them in its valleys, caves and lakes. On clear winter nights, snow still covers quiet valleys, and the stars shine as they did over the camps of the Ice Age.
