About twenty thousand years ago, the world was in the coldest part of the last Ice Age. Great sheets of ice covered the north, the seas were lower, and mammoths and reindeer grazed on wide frozen grasslands. Yet people lived there, in small family groups, through winters far colder than any today. This story looks at their shelters of hide and mammoth bone, their fur clothing and bone needles, their hunting and their long winter nights, the animals they knew, and the paintings they left deep inside caves. It ends with the slow melting of the ice, and the traces it left behind.

About twenty thousand years ago, the Earth was in the coldest part of the last Ice Age. Scientists call this period the Last Glacial Maximum. It lasted roughly from twenty-six thousand to nineteen thousand years ago. Average temperatures around the world were several degrees lower than today. In northern Europe and North America, the winters were long and bitterly cold. Summers were short and cool. Huge sheets of ice covered large parts of the northern continents. Scientists know about this period from many kinds of evidence. Ice cores drilled from Greenland and Antarctica contain tiny bubbles of ancient air. Layers of sediment on the sea floor record changes in temperature. Pollen preserved in lake mud shows which plants grew in each region. Even so, the Ice Age was not one single frozen moment. The climate changed repeatedly over thousands of years, with colder and milder phases. Yet at its peak, around twenty thousand years ago, the world looked very different from the one that exists today.

[long-pause]

{img:02-glacier-edge-meltwater-river-cold-steppe}

Great ice sheets covered Scandinavia, much of Britain and northern North America. At the height of the last Ice Age, an enormous ice sheet covered Scandinavia and spread across the Baltic Sea and northern Germany. Another covered most of Scotland, Ireland and northern England. In North America, ice stretched from the Arctic to the area of modern New York and Chicago. In places, the ice was more than three kilometres thick. Mountain ranges such as the Alps and the Pyrenees had their own large glaciers. Geologists can still trace the edges of these ice sheets today. They find ridges of rock and gravel, called moraines, left behind when the ice melted. Large boulders carried by the ice lie far from where they came from. Even so, much of Europe south of the ice was not covered. It was a cold, open land of grass and low shrubs. People and animals lived in these southern regions, especially in sheltered valleys in what are now France, Spain and Central Europe.

[long-pause]

{img:03-low-sea-level-land-bridge-doggerland-plain}

Because so much water was frozen in ice, sea levels were about a hundred and twenty metres lower than today. The ice sheets held a huge amount of the world's water. As a result, sea levels fell dramatically. Coastlines lay far beyond where they are now. Britain was not an island but part of mainland Europe. A wide area of land, now called Doggerland, stretched across what is today the southern North Sea. In the far north, a land bridge called Beringia connected Siberia and Alaska. This bridge later helped people reach the Americas. Scientists know about these lost lands from the shape of the sea floor and from objects brought up by fishing boats. Trawlers in the North Sea have recovered mammoth bones, stone tools and even a carved antler point. Even so, much of the evidence of coastal life from this period now lies under the sea. Archaeologists believe many people may have lived along the old coasts, where food was plentiful. Their camps are now hidden beneath the waves.

[long-pause]

{img:04-mammoth-steppe-grassland-herds-reindeer-horses}

Much of Ice Age Europe and Asia was covered by a cold, dry grassland called the mammoth steppe. South and east of the ice sheets lay a vast landscape of grasses, herbs and low shrubs. Scientists call it the mammoth steppe, after its most famous animal. It stretched from Spain across Europe and Asia to Alaska and Canada. The climate was cold and dry, with little snow in many areas. This allowed grass to grow and support large herds of animals. Woolly mammoths, woolly rhinoceroses, reindeer, wild horses, bison and musk oxen grazed there. Predators such as wolves, cave lions and cave hyenas hunted them. Scientists reconstruct this landscape from pollen, plant remains found in frozen soil and the stomach contents of preserved animals. Even so, the mammoth steppe was not identical everywhere. Some areas had more trees, especially in sheltered valleys and further south. Yet across thousands of kilometres, the land was open and rich in grazing animals, providing food for the people who lived there.

[long-pause]

{img:05-ice-age-people-band-walking-snowy-valley}

Ice Age people in Europe lived in small, mobile groups that moved with the seasons. The people of Ice Age Europe did not live in permanent villages. They lived in small groups, often called bands by archaeologists. A band might include around twenty-five to fifty people, related by family and friendship. They moved across the landscape following animal herds and seasonal food sources. In summer, they might camp near rivers and open plains. In winter, they sought shelter in valleys, caves and protected hillsides. Archaeologists study these movements by examining campsites, animal bones and the sources of stone used for tools. Flint found at a camp sometimes came from a hundred kilometres or more away. Even so, groups did not wander aimlessly. They knew their territories well and returned to familiar places year after year. Their movements were planned around the seasons, the animals and the weather. Knowledge of the land, passed down over generations, was one of their most important tools for survival.

[long-pause]

{img:06-cro-magnon-family-campfire-night-stars}

The people of the late Ice Age in Europe were modern humans, the same species as people today. The people who lived in Europe twenty thousand years ago belonged to our own species, Homo sapiens. They are sometimes called Cro-Magnons, after a rock shelter in France where their remains were found in 1868. Their bodies and brains were essentially the same as those of people living today. They spoke languages, told stories, made art and cared for one another. Historians and archaeologists stress that they were not primitive in their thinking. They had to solve complex problems to survive in a harsh climate. Studies of skeletons show that many were tall and strongly built, with signs of an active life. Even so, their lives were very different from modern lives. They had no farms, no metal and no writing. Everything they used came from the land, the animals and their own skills. In the evening, families gathered around fires, as people have done for countless generations.

[long-pause]

{img:07-modern-humans-arrive-europe-forest-edge}

Modern humans arrived in Europe more than forty thousand years ago. Homo sapiens first evolved in Africa, around three hundred thousand years ago. Over a long period, groups spread out of Africa into Asia and beyond. Modern humans reached Europe more than forty thousand years ago. Some of the earliest evidence comes from caves in Bulgaria and Italy, where human teeth and tools have been dated to around forty-five thousand years ago. Scientists use radiocarbon dating and the analysis of ancient DNA to study these early arrivals. DNA studies show that the first groups were later replaced or absorbed by other populations, which arrived in several waves. Even so, the details are still being researched. New discoveries regularly change the picture. By twenty thousand years ago, modern humans had lived in Europe for over twenty thousand years. They had adapted to the cold through clothing, shelter, fire and knowledge. Their descendants would eventually witness the end of the Ice Age.

[long-pause]

{img:08-neanderthal-memory-cave-shelter-mist}

Before modern humans, Europe had been home to the Neanderthals for hundreds of thousands of years. The Neanderthals were a different human species that lived in Europe and western Asia for a very long time. They were strong and well adapted to cold climates. They made stone tools, controlled fire, hunted large animals and buried some of their dead. Neanderthals disappeared around forty thousand years ago, not long after modern humans arrived. Scientists still debate why. Possible reasons include competition with modern humans, climate changes and small population sizes. Studies of ancient DNA show that Neanderthals and modern humans sometimes had children together. Most people living outside Africa today carry a small percentage of Neanderthal DNA. Even so, by the time of the Last Glacial Maximum, Neanderthals had been gone for about twenty thousand years. The people living in Europe at that time knew nothing of them. Yet caves once used by Neanderthals were sometimes later used by modern humans, as layers of ancient remains show.

[long-pause]

{img:09-ice-age-hunters-faces-fur-hoods-close}

Skeletons show that Ice Age people were often strong and healthy, though life was physically demanding. Studies of skeletons from Ice Age Europe give a detailed picture of the people. Many adults were tall, sometimes taller than later farming populations. Their bones show strong muscle attachments, the result of a very active life of walking, carrying and hunting. Their teeth were often in good condition, since their diet contained little sugar. Archaeologists also find signs of injuries and illness. Some skeletons show healed broken bones, which suggests that others cared for the injured while they recovered. Scientists estimate life expectancy using these remains. Many children died young, as in most societies before modern medicine. Even so, those who survived childhood could live into their forties, fifties or longer. The people of the Ice Age were not short-lived savages. They were capable, skilled and resilient. Their health depended greatly on the availability of food, which changed with the seasons and with the movement of animals.

[long-pause]

{img:10-small-band-camp-river-terrace-tents}

A typical Ice Age band was made up of several related families living and moving together. Archaeologists believe that most people lived in groups of a few families, perhaps twenty-five to fifty people in total. Such groups were large enough to share work and protect each other, but small enough to find enough food. Studies of modern hunter-gatherer societies offer clues about how such bands may have worked, though historians are careful about making direct comparisons. In many such societies, decisions are made by discussion, and food is shared widely. Archaeological evidence from Ice Age campsites shows areas for different activities, such as cooking, tool making and processing hides. This suggests organised daily life. Even so, the details of social life are difficult to reconstruct. No written records exist, and much of what people did left no trace. Yet the evidence of shared hearths, cooperative hunting and careful burials points to communities in which people depended closely on one another for survival through the long winters.

[long-pause]

{img:11-bands-meeting-summer-gathering-river-valley}

Different groups probably met at certain times of year to exchange goods, news and marriage partners. Small bands could not survive in isolation forever. They needed contact with other groups to find marriage partners, share information and exchange goods. Archaeologists find evidence of such contacts in objects that travelled long distances. Shells from the Mediterranean and Atlantic coasts have been found at sites hundreds of kilometres inland. Special stones and amber also moved across great distances. Some large sites, with many hearths and objects, may have been places where several bands gathered, perhaps during seasons when food was plentiful. Researchers suggest that cave art sites may also have been meeting places. Even so, it is difficult to prove exactly how and when such gatherings took place. What is clear is that Ice Age people were connected across wide regions. Studies of ancient DNA support this, showing that populations across Europe were genetically related. These networks helped people survive during difficult times.

[long-pause]

{img:12-child-learning-flint-knapping-elder}

Children in the Ice Age learned essential skills by watching and helping adults. There were no schools in the Ice Age. Children learned by observing and copying the adults around them. They learned how to make tools, prepare food, sew clothing and read the landscape. Archaeologists have found evidence of this learning process. At some sites, clusters of poorly made flint flakes lie next to expert work. Researchers interpret these as the attempts of apprentices, perhaps children, practising beside skilled toolmakers. Small handprints and footprints of children have also been found in caves, including in famous painted caves. Some prints show children walking with adults deep underground. Even so, much of children's lives is invisible in the archaeological record. Their toys, games and songs have mostly disappeared. Yet the evidence suggests that children were present in all parts of community life. Knowledge passed from generation to generation in this way was the foundation of survival through the Ice Age.

[long-pause]

{img:13-rock-shelter-overhang-dordogne-valley-fire}

Many Ice Age people in southwestern France lived under natural rock overhangs called rock shelters. In the valleys of the Dordogne and Vézère rivers in southwestern France, limestone cliffs form natural overhangs. Archaeologists call these rock shelters, or abris in French. They offered protection from rain, snow and wind, and many faced south to catch the winter sun. People built fires beneath them and added walls of hides or wood to create sheltered living spaces. Excavations at sites such as the Abri Pataud and Laugerie-Haute have revealed thick layers of ancient occupation. These layers contain hearths, tools, animal bones and ornaments, showing that people returned to the same places over thousands of years. The region is so rich in Ice Age sites that it is sometimes called the valley of humanity. Even so, people did not usually live deep inside caves, which were dark and damp. They lived at cave entrances and under rock shelters, where there was light and fresh air.

[long-pause]

{img:14-cave-entrance-hearth-skins-drying-smoke}

Cave entrances offered shelter, but people rarely lived in the dark interiors of caves. The popular image of cave people living deep inside caves is not accurate. Archaeologists find that most living areas were at cave entrances or under overhangs. Here, daylight was available, smoke could escape and the space was dry. Deep caves were cold, completely dark and often wet. People did enter them, sometimes for long distances, but mainly for special purposes, such as making art. At cave entrances, archaeologists find hearths, tools, animal bones and the remains of hides and other materials. Skins were stretched on frames to dry and were scraped and softened for clothing and tents. Even so, caves and rock shelters were not available everywhere. On the open plains of Central and Eastern Europe, there were few caves. People there built their own shelters from the materials around them. Some of these shelters were made from the bones of mammoths, the largest animals of the steppe.

[long-pause]

{img:15-mammoth-bone-hut-mezhyrich-snow}

On the plains of Ukraine, Ice Age people built round houses from mammoth bones. At sites such as Mezhyrich in Ukraine, archaeologists have found the remains of remarkable dwellings built from mammoth bones. They date to around fifteen thousand years ago. The builders used large bones, such as jaws, skulls, leg bones and tusks, to make the walls and frame of round huts. The jaws were stacked in patterns, and tusks may have formed arches over entrances. The structures were probably covered with hides and turf for warmth. One hut at Mezhyrich contained bones from around a hundred and fifty mammoths. Archaeologists debate whether these bones came from animals hunted by the people or from mammoths that had died naturally. Most likely, both sources were used. Similar structures have been found at other sites, such as Kostenki in Russia. Even so, the huts show remarkable planning and effort. They provided sturdy shelters on open plains where wood was scarce, and some may have been used for many winters.

[long-pause]

{img:16-mammoth-bone-hut-interior-hearth-furs}

Inside a mammoth-bone hut, a central hearth provided warmth and light through the winter. The interior of a mammoth-bone dwelling was small, perhaps four to six metres across. In the centre was a hearth, where a fire burned. Since wood was scarce on the steppe, people often burned bones, which contain fat and burn well once a fire is hot. Archaeologists find burned bone in many Ice Age hearths. Around the hearth, the floor was probably covered with furs and hides for sleeping and sitting. Tools, food and clothing were stored near the walls. Storage pits dug near some huts held food, perhaps frozen meat for the winter. Historians and archaeologists reconstruct these interiors from the arrangement of objects found on excavated floors. Even so, much remains uncertain, since hides, wood and plant materials have long since decayed. Yet the evidence shows well-organised living spaces. Families shared a warm interior while snow and wind swept across the open plain outside, often for many months.

[long-pause]

{img:17-tent-hides-poles-reindeer-hunters-camp}

In many places, Ice Age people lived in tents made of animal hides stretched over wooden poles. Where caves and mammoth bones were not available, people built tents. These were made of wooden poles covered with hides, especially reindeer and horse skins. Tents could be taken down and carried when the group moved. Archaeologists find traces of them as rings of stones that once held down the edges, or as patterns of post holes and hearths. Sites such as Pincevent in France, dated to around fourteen thousand years ago, show carefully arranged camps of reindeer hunters. Each tent had a hearth, and activities such as tool making took place in specific areas around it. Researchers have studied these camps in great detail, mapping the position of every flint flake and bone. Even so, the tents themselves have vanished. Their form is reconstructed from the remains and from comparisons with tents used by recent Arctic peoples. These comparisons suggest that hide tents could be surprisingly warm and practical.

[long-pause]

{img:18-hearth-stones-pit-embers-camp-evening}

Hearths were the centre of daily life in Ice Age camps. Fire was essential for survival during the Ice Age. It provided warmth, light and protection from predators. It made food easier to digest and allowed meat to be cooked and preserved by smoking. Ice Age hearths were often carefully built. Some were shallow pits lined with stones. Others were flat areas surrounded by rocks. Stones heated in the fire could be dropped into containers of water to boil it, a method known as stone boiling. Archaeologists find many such fire-cracked stones at Ice Age sites. They also study ash and charcoal to learn what fuels were burned. Wood was used where available, and bone and animal fat were used elsewhere. Even so, keeping a fire going required effort and planning. Fuel had to be gathered constantly. Around the hearth, people worked, ate, told stories and slept. Its glow was the centre of the home through the long, dark winter nights.

[long-pause]

{img:19-sleeping-furs-bedding-grass-shelter-night}

Ice Age people slept on bedding of grass and furs, close to the warmth of the fire. Archaeologists have found evidence of bedding at several ancient sites. At Border Cave in South Africa, layers of grass bedding are much older than the European Ice Age, showing that people prepared comfortable sleeping places very early. In Ice Age Europe, people probably slept on layers of dry grass, moss or branches covered with thick furs. Furs of reindeer, wolves and other animals provided excellent insulation. Families likely slept close together for warmth. Some researchers suggest that ash from fires was spread under bedding to keep insects away, as found at some early sites. Even so, direct evidence of bedding in Ice Age Europe is rare, since plant materials decay quickly. Most reconstructions rely on comparisons and on small traces found during careful excavation. Sleep in winter was probably long, since nights lasted many hours. In the warmth of furs and firelight, people rested through the cold darkness until the late winter dawn.

[long-pause]

{img:20-sewing-bone-needle-sinew-firelight}

The invention of the eyed bone needle allowed Ice Age people to sew close-fitting clothing. One of the most important inventions of the Ice Age was the bone needle with an eye. The oldest known examples come from Denisova Cave in Siberia and are around forty thousand years old or more. In Europe, eyed needles became common after about twenty-five thousand years ago. They were carefully made from bone or ivory, ground smooth and pierced with a tiny hole. Thread was made from sinew, the strong tissue of animal tendons, or from plant fibres. With needles, people could sew tailored clothing that fitted the body closely. Such clothing kept heat in far better than loose wraps. Archaeologists consider the needle a major step in adapting to cold climates. Even so, the clothes themselves have not survived. Their form is reconstructed from the tools, from art and from burials. The small needles found at many sites show skilled, patient work carried out over countless winter evenings.

[long-pause]

{img:21-tailored-fur-parka-hood-trousers}

Ice Age clothing probably included hooded parkas, trousers, boots and mittens made of fur and hide. Researchers believe that Ice Age people wore layered clothing similar to that of recent Arctic peoples. An outer layer of fur, such as reindeer or wolf, kept out wind and cold. An inner layer of softer hide could be worn next to the skin. Hooded parkas protected the head and neck, while trousers, boots and mittens protected the limbs. Reindeer fur is particularly warm, because each hair is hollow and traps air. Wolf and wolverine fur were later valued by Arctic peoples for hoods because they resist frost. Archaeologists find evidence of fur processing in the bones of animals with cut marks around the paws, showing that skins were removed carefully. Even so, no complete Ice Age garment has survived in Europe. The details of design remain uncertain. Yet the evidence of needles, scrapers and fur-bearing animals shows that clothing was carefully made. Without it, survival in the cold would have been impossible.

[long-pause]

{img:22-sungir-burial-beads-clothing-reconstruction}

Burials at Sungir in Russia show clothing decorated with thousands of ivory beads. Sungir is an archaeological site near the town of Vladimir in Russia, dated to around thirty-four thousand years ago. Excavations there uncovered several remarkable burials. An adult man was buried with around three thousand beads made of mammoth ivory. Two children were buried together with around ten thousand beads, as well as ivory spears and carved animals. The beads lay in rows across the bodies, showing that they had been sewn onto clothing, such as shirts, trousers and caps. Researchers have estimated that each bead took perhaps an hour to make. The burials therefore represent thousands of hours of work. Historians and archaeologists interpret this as a sign of great care and respect for the dead. Even so, the meaning of the burials is debated. The children may have held a special status. Yet the outlines of the beads show the shape of Ice Age clothing more clearly than any other find.

[long-pause]

{img:23-reindeer-hide-scraping-woman-camp}

Preparing animal hides was a long and skilled task, done with stone scrapers. Turning an animal skin into usable leather or fur took many steps. First, the skin was removed carefully from the animal. Then the inner side was scraped clean of fat and flesh using stone tools called scrapers. These are among the most common tools found at Ice Age sites. The hide was then dried, stretched and softened. Some methods used animal brains, fat or smoke to make the hide supple and waterproof. Red ochre, a natural iron pigment, may also have been rubbed into hides to help preserve them. Archaeologists study wear marks on stone scrapers under microscopes to identify their use on hides. Even so, the exact methods varied between groups and regions. The work required great skill and patience. A single garment could need several hides. Preparing enough skins for a family's clothing, bedding and tents was one of the most important tasks of the year, especially before winter.

[long-pause]

{img:24-fur-boots-mittens-snow-tracks}

Protecting the hands and feet from frostbite was essential for survival in the Ice Age. In extreme cold, hands and feet lose heat quickly. Frostbite could cause permanent damage or death. Ice Age people therefore needed warm, well-made boots and mittens. Researchers believe they wore boots of hide with fur on the inside, possibly lined with dry grass for extra insulation, as recent Arctic peoples did. Some scientists have studied the bones of feet from Ice Age skeletons. They found that toe bones became more delicate around forty thousand years ago, which may indicate the regular use of supportive footwear. Even so, this interpretation is debated. Footprints preserved in some caves show bare feet, perhaps because people removed their boots in damp underground passages. Mittens and gloves are rarely shown in art, but they were almost certainly used. Keeping footwear and mittens dry was also important. Wet clothing loses its warmth, so items were dried carefully by the fire every evening.

[long-pause]

{img:25-fire-making-flint-pyrite-sparks}

Ice Age people could make fire by striking flint against a mineral called pyrite. Making fire was a vital skill. One method used in the Ice Age was striking a piece of flint against iron pyrite, a shiny mineral. The impact produced sparks, which could light dry tinder, such as fungus, grass or fine wood shavings. Archaeologists have found lumps of pyrite with grooves and wear marks at several Ice Age sites. Researchers have also studied flint tools with microscopic traces that match this use. Another method used friction, rubbing wood together, though this leaves less evidence. Even so, people probably tried to keep fires burning rather than lighting new ones constantly. Embers could be carried in containers from one camp to another. Historians note that the ability to make fire at will gave people great independence. They could survive in places where natural fires were rare. Fire also protected camps from predators and made long winter nights bearable.

[long-pause]

{img:26-family-sleeping-close-hearth-furs-night}

Shared warmth, food and care helped Ice Age families survive the long winters. Survival in the Ice Age depended on cooperation. Clothing, shelter and fire were the products of shared work. Families and bands divided tasks such as hunting, gathering, preparing hides and caring for children. Archaeological evidence suggests that people looked after those who could not work. Some skeletons show long-term injuries or disabilities that would have required help from others. Historians and archaeologists interpret this as evidence of care and compassion. Even so, life was difficult. Food shortages, illness and accidents were common. Winters were dangerous for the very young and the very old. Yet the long survival of Ice Age populations across thousands of years shows remarkable resilience. Their knowledge of animals, plants and weather allowed them to live in conditions that would challenge any modern person. At the heart of this survival was the family, gathered around the fire, sharing warmth through the darkest months of the year.

[long-pause]

{img:27-reindeer-herd-river-crossing-autumn}

Reindeer were one of the most important sources of food for Ice Age people in Europe. In many regions of Ice Age Europe, reindeer were the main animal hunted. At some sites in France, reindeer bones make up more than ninety percent of all animal remains. For this reason, archaeologists sometimes call the late Ice Age the age of the reindeer. Reindeer migrated in large herds between summer and winter grazing areas. Hunters learned their routes and waited at river crossings and narrow valleys, where the animals could be approached. Reindeer provided meat, fat, marrow, hides for clothing and tents, sinew for thread, and antlers and bones for tools. Almost every part of the animal was used. Archaeologists study the age and season of death of hunted animals by examining their teeth. This reveals when people hunted and how they planned their year. Even so, diets varied by region. Elsewhere, people relied more on horses, bison, ibex or red deer, depending on the local landscape.

[long-pause]

{img:28-hunters-spear-thrower-atlatl-tundra}

Ice Age hunters used spears and a clever tool called the spear-thrower. Hunters in the Ice Age used spears tipped with sharp points of flint, bone or antler. Later in the period, they also used a spear-thrower, a stick with a hook at one end that held the back of the spear. When thrown, the spear-thrower acted like an extension of the arm, giving the spear much greater speed and range. The oldest known spear-throwers in Europe are around seventeen to twenty thousand years old. Some were beautifully carved with animal figures, such as a famous example from France showing a young ibex. Archaeologists have tested replicas and found that they greatly increase hunting power. Even so, hunting large animals remained dangerous and uncertain. Hunters worked together, using knowledge of animal behaviour, the landscape and the wind. The bow and arrow came into wider use in Europe only near the end of the Ice Age, although some researchers suggest it was known earlier.

[long-pause]

{img:29-mammoth-herd-steppe-hunters-watching-distance}

Ice Age people sometimes hunted mammoths, but these huge animals were not their everyday food. Woolly mammoths were about the size of modern African elephants and covered with long hair. Popular images often show Ice Age people constantly hunting them. Archaeologists believe the reality was more complex. Mammoths were dangerous and difficult to kill. Some sites show clear evidence of mammoth hunting, including spear points embedded in bones. At other sites, people may have used mammoths that died naturally, collecting their bones, tusks and meat. A single mammoth provided an enormous quantity of meat, fat, hide and ivory. Ivory was especially valued for making tools, beads and figurines. Even so, most groups relied more on smaller animals such as reindeer, horses and hares for everyday food. Researchers continue to debate how much human hunting contributed to the extinction of mammoths. Climate change and the loss of their grassland habitat at the end of the Ice Age were probably also important factors.

[long-pause]

{img:30-salmon-river-fishing-ice-age-nets}

Fish, birds and small animals were also important parts of the Ice Age diet. Ice Age people did not live on large animals alone. Rivers provided fish, especially salmon, which swam upstream in great numbers each year. Archaeologists have found fish bones at many sites and carved images of fish in art. Harpoons made of antler, with barbs along one side, were used to catch large fish. Researchers believe that nets and traps were also used, although these rarely survive. People hunted birds such as ptarmigan and waterfowl, and small animals such as arctic hares and foxes. Chemical studies of human bones measure the balance of elements, which shows the types of food people ate. These studies suggest that some groups ate a considerable amount of fish, especially near rivers and coasts. Even so, diets changed with the seasons. In spring and autumn, migrating animals and fish provided abundant food. In late winter, food could become scarce, and stored supplies were vital for survival.

[long-pause]

{img:31-meat-drying-racks-smoke-camp}

Ice Age people preserved meat by drying, smoking and freezing it for the winter. Hunting could produce large amounts of meat at certain times of year, especially during animal migrations in autumn. To survive the winter, people needed to store food. Meat could be cut into thin strips and dried in the wind or over smoky fires. Smoking helped preserve it and kept insects away. In very cold regions, meat could simply be frozen in pits dug into the ground. Archaeologists have found storage pits at sites in Eastern Europe, near mammoth-bone dwellings. Fat was also extremely important. It provided energy and could be stored for long periods. Bones were often broken open to extract marrow, and boiled to remove grease. Researchers find many smashed bones at Ice Age sites, showing that nothing was wasted. Even so, storage required planning and cooperation. A successful autumn hunt and careful preparation could mean the difference between comfort and hunger in the months that followed.

[long-pause]

{img:32-gathering-berries-roots-summer-tundra}

Plants such as berries, roots, seeds and leaves were also part of the Ice Age diet. Although the Ice Age landscape was cold, it supported many edible plants. In summer, people could gather berries, such as cranberries and crowberries, as well as leaves, roots, seeds and nuts where trees grew. Plant foods provided vitamins and variety. Scientists have found traces of plant remains in the tartar on Ice Age teeth and in ancient hearths. At some sites, grinding stones show that seeds or roots were processed into flour. Historians note that the importance of plant foods was long underestimated, because bones survive much better than plants in the ground. Even so, plants were seasonal. In winter, people depended mainly on animal foods and stored supplies. Knowledge of where and when to find plants was passed down through generations. This knowledge was as valuable as hunting skill. Women, men and children probably all took part in gathering, though the exact division of work is difficult to determine.

[long-pause]

{img:33-storage-pit-frozen-ground-winter-food}

At some Ice Age sites, archaeologists have found pits dug into the ground for storing food. On the cold plains of Eastern Europe, the ground stayed frozen for much of the year. Ice Age people took advantage of this by digging storage pits near their dwellings. Food placed in these pits stayed frozen and fresh for long periods. At sites such as Mezhyrich, archaeologists found several such pits around the huts. Some were covered with large bones, such as mammoth shoulder blades, to protect the contents from animals. Researchers believe these pits held meat, fat and perhaps bones for fuel. This kind of storage suggests that people stayed in the same place for much of the winter, rather than moving constantly. Even so, interpretations differ, since the original contents have long since disappeared. What remains are the pits themselves and scattered bones. Yet they show careful planning for the future. Storing food allowed families to survive the longest and darkest part of the year, when hunting was most difficult.

[long-pause]

{img:34-flint-blades-workshop-stones}

Ice Age toolmakers produced long, thin flint blades with great skill. Stone tools are the most common objects found at Ice Age sites, because they do not decay. Flint was the preferred material, since it breaks in predictable ways and produces very sharp edges. Toolmakers struck flint with hammers of stone, bone or antler to remove long, thin blades. These blades were then shaped into many different tools, such as knives, scrapers, drills and spear points. Archaeologists call this technique blade technology. They can refit flakes back together like a puzzle, showing exactly how a tool was made. Such studies reveal the skill and planning of Ice Age craftspeople. One of the finest examples is the Solutrean laurel leaf point, a thin, finely flaked blade made in France and Spain around twenty thousand years ago. Some were too delicate for practical use and may have had a special purpose. Even so, most tools were everyday objects, made quickly and used until they wore out.

[long-pause]

{img:35-antler-harpoon-carving-firelight}

Bone and antler were carved into harpoons, needles, spear points and many other tools. Besides stone, Ice Age people used bone, antler and ivory. These materials were strong and flexible, and could be carved into complex shapes. Reindeer antler was especially useful. To work it, craftspeople soaked it in water to soften it, then cut grooves with flint tools to remove long strips. From these strips, they carved harpoons with rows of barbs, spear points, needles and awls. Archaeologists find many such tools, especially from the later part of the Ice Age, a period known as the Magdalenian, after a rock shelter in France called La Madeleine. Some bone tools were decorated with engraved animals or patterns. Even so, many objects made of wood, leather and plant fibres have not survived. The tools that remain represent only part of what people made and used. Yet they show a sophisticated knowledge of materials and a strong sense of design, combining usefulness with beauty.

[long-pause]

{img:36-bone-needles-awls-display-museum}

Museums display thousands of small Ice Age tools that show the details of daily work. Many museums in Europe hold large collections of Ice Age tools. The National Museum of Prehistory in Les Eyzies, France, displays objects from the many sites of the Vézère valley. Visitors can see needles, awls, scrapers, harpoons and blades, each made by hand thousands of years ago. Archaeologists study these tools with microscopes to find traces of use. Wear marks reveal whether a tool was used for cutting meat, scraping hides, working wood or boring holes. Residues sometimes survive on tool edges, including traces of blood, plants or pigment. Historians note that such research has transformed understanding of Ice Age life. Tools once thought to be simple turn out to have specific purposes. Even so, many questions remain. The names people gave their tools and the stories attached to them are lost. Yet each object preserves a small moment of skilled work, carried out by real people in a very different world.

[long-pause]

{img:37-spear-thrower-carved-animal-ibex}

Some Ice Age tools were decorated with carved animals, combining usefulness and art. Among the finest objects of the Ice Age are decorated spear-throwers. One famous example, found at the cave of Le Mas d'Azil in France, shows a young ibex looking back over its shoulder. Others show horses, birds and other animals. These carvings were made on reindeer antler with great precision. Archaeologists date most of them to the later part of the Ice Age, around fifteen to seventeen thousand years ago. Researchers debate why such objects were decorated. Some suggest the images had a magical or spiritual purpose, perhaps to bring success in hunting. Others think they expressed the owner's identity or simply reflected pleasure in beautiful things. Even so, the carvings show careful observation of animals. The proportions and movements are often very accurate. This suggests that Ice Age people watched animals closely and knew them intimately. The decorated tools blur the line between everyday objects and works of art.

[long-pause]

{img:38-stone-lamp-burning-fat-cave}

Ice Age people made lamps of stone that burned animal fat to light dark caves. To see inside dark caves, Ice Age people used lamps. Some were simple hollow stones. Others were carefully carved, with a bowl and a handle. The most famous example, found in Lascaux cave in France, is made of red sandstone and has a decorated handle. The lamps burned animal fat, with a wick of plant material, such as juniper or moss. Scientists have analysed residues in such lamps and identified the fats and wicks used. Experiments with replica lamps show that they gave a soft, steady light, enough to see and paint by, for an hour or more. Torches of wood were also used, and their marks can still be seen on cave walls. Even so, light inside caves was always limited. The flickering glow made painted animals appear to move. Researchers suggest this effect may have been intentional, adding to the power of the images for people who saw them.

[long-pause]

{img:39-ochre-grinding-red-pigment-stone}

Red and yellow ochre, a natural pigment, was widely used during the Ice Age. Ochre is a natural earth pigment containing iron oxides. It ranges in colour from yellow to deep red. Ice Age people used it widely. They ground it into powder on flat stones and mixed it with water, fat or other substances to make paint. Ochre was used in cave paintings, on decorated objects and in burials, where bodies were sometimes covered with red powder. Researchers believe it may also have been used to treat hides, protect skin from the sun or keep insects away. Black pigments were made from charcoal and manganese minerals. Archaeologists find lumps of ochre with scraped surfaces at many sites. Even so, the meaning of its use, especially in burials, is uncertain. Red may have symbolised life, blood or warmth. The use of ochre goes back much further than the Ice Age in Europe, to early human sites in Africa more than a hundred thousand years ago.

[long-pause]

{img:40-blizzard-camp-tents-snow-night}

Winters during the Ice Age were long, dark and very cold, especially in northern regions. At the height of the last Ice Age, winters in much of Europe were far colder than today. In Central Europe, winter temperatures may have averaged twenty degrees or more below freezing. Strong winds blew across the open plains, carrying dust and snow. Scientists reconstruct these conditions from evidence such as frozen soil patterns, wind-blown dust deposits called loess and fossil insects. During storms, people stayed inside their shelters, keeping fires burning and conserving energy. Archaeologists find that many sites in the coldest regions were used mainly in warmer seasons, suggesting that people moved south or into sheltered valleys in winter. Even so, some groups stayed in cold regions year-round, especially where mammoth-bone dwellings provided strong shelter. Survival required careful preparation. Fuel, food and clothing had to be ready before winter began. In the darkness of a storm, the glow of a fire inside a hide tent was a sign of life and safety.

[long-pause]

{img:41-short-winter-day-low-sun-snowfield}

In winter, daylight was short, and much of the day's work had to be done in a few hours. In northern Europe, winter days are short. During the Ice Age, the length of the days was the same as today, since it depends on the Earth's position, not on climate. In midwinter, daylight in northern Germany or Britain lasted only around seven or eight hours. People had to complete outdoor tasks, such as hunting, gathering fuel and fetching water, in these short hours. Snow covered the ground, and hunting could be difficult. Animals were often thin and scattered. Historians and archaeologists believe that winter was a time of reduced activity and careful use of stored resources. Even so, winter also brought some advantages. Frozen rivers and lakes could be crossed on foot. Animal tracks in the snow showed hunters where to go. Frozen ground preserved stored meat. People who knew the land could find food and fuel even in the coldest months, though survival always required skill and patience.

[long-pause]

{img:42-storytelling-around-fire-winter-night}

Long winter nights were probably filled with storytelling, songs and the making of tools and clothing. With long hours of darkness, Ice Age people spent much time inside their shelters. Researchers believe this time was used for indoor work, such as repairing clothing, sewing, carving tools and making ornaments. It was probably also a time for storytelling and music. No Ice Age stories survive, since there was no writing. Yet anthropologists who have studied hunter-gatherer societies around the world note that storytelling often plays a central role during long evenings. Stories pass on knowledge about animals, landscapes, history and values. Musical instruments from the Ice Age, such as bone flutes, show that music was part of life. Even so, historians are careful not to describe these evenings in too much detail, since direct evidence is limited. What can be said is that people had time, firelight and skills. The carved objects and ornaments found at sites suggest that some of this time was spent creating things of beauty.

[long-pause]

{img:43-children-playing-snow-camp-winter}

Archaeologists have found traces of Ice Age children, including footprints and small objects that may have been toys. Children were an important part of Ice Age communities. Evidence of their presence includes small footprints and handprints in caves, and child-sized tools at some sites. In the cave of Niaux in France and other caves, footprints show children walking with adults. At some sites, small figurines of clay or carved animals may have been toys, although their purpose is uncertain. Researchers have also found evidence of children helping with tasks, such as handling clay or making simple tools. Historians note that childhood in the Ice Age combined play and learning. Children played in the snow, much as children do today, although direct evidence of such games does not survive. Even so, life was dangerous for the young. Infant and child mortality was high. Yet children represented the future of the group. Their care, education and survival through winter were among the most important concerns of every Ice Age community.

[long-pause]

{img:44-frozen-river-ice-crossing-band-dusk}

Frozen rivers and lakes made travel easier in winter, but also brought danger. In the depths of winter, rivers and lakes froze solid. This allowed people to cross water easily and travel along frozen rivers as natural roads. Recent Arctic and subarctic peoples have used frozen rivers in this way for travel and hunting. Researchers believe Ice Age people did the same. Fishing through holes in the ice could also provide food. Even so, ice was dangerous. Thin ice could break, and falling into freezing water could be fatal within minutes. Knowledge of ice conditions, passed down through generations, was essential. Archaeologists have found sites located near rivers, which provided water, fish and routes for travel throughout the year. In spring, the melting ice brought floods and made travel difficult again. Historians describe the Ice Age year as a cycle shaped by freezing and thawing, by migrating animals and by changing light. People planned their lives around this cycle with great care.

[long-pause]

{img:45-northern-lights-ice-age-sky-camp}

During the Ice Age, people in northern regions probably saw the northern lights more often than people in the same places today. The northern lights, or aurora borealis, appear when particles from the Sun strike the Earth's upper atmosphere. They are usually seen in regions close to the magnetic pole. During the Ice Age, the magnetic pole was in a different position, and people in parts of Europe may have seen the aurora on clear winter nights. Scientists note, however, that the strength and location of the aurora change over time, so it is difficult to know exactly how often they appeared. Some researchers have suggested that certain patterns in cave art may represent the sky, although this is speculative. Even so, Ice Age people certainly watched the night sky closely. The Moon, stars and seasons were important for timing travel and hunting. Some engraved bones have marks that may record lunar cycles. Under the clear, dark skies of the Ice Age, with no artificial light, the stars would have been extraordinarily bright.

[long-pause]

{img:46-spring-thaw-tundra-flowers-birds-return}

The return of spring brought migrating animals, birds and new plants to the Ice Age landscape. After the long winter, spring was a time of renewal. Snow melted, rivers rose and the first plants began to grow. Migrating birds, such as geese and ducks, returned from the south. Reindeer herds moved toward their summer grazing grounds, and their calves were born. Fish began to swim upstream. For Ice Age people, spring meant new sources of food after months of stored supplies. Archaeologists find evidence of seasonal camps used in spring, located along migration routes and near rivers. Studies of animal teeth from these sites confirm the season of hunting. Even so, spring could be a difficult time. Melting snow made the ground muddy and travel hard. Stored food might be nearly exhausted before new food became plentiful. Historians note that the transition from winter to spring was often the hungriest period of the year. Yet the lengthening days and returning birds marked the end of the darkest season.

[long-pause]

{img:47-woolly-mammoth-close-snow-breath}

The woolly mammoth was the best-known animal of the Ice Age. Woolly mammoths were large relatives of modern elephants. They stood about three to three and a half metres tall at the shoulder and were covered with long, shaggy hair over a dense undercoat. They had small ears and short tails, which reduced heat loss. Their long, curved tusks could reach more than four metres. Mammoths grazed on grasses and herbs of the mammoth steppe. Scientists know a great deal about them because complete frozen bodies have been found in the permafrost of Siberia. Some still have hair, skin and stomach contents. One famous example is a baby mammoth named Lyuba, found in 2007 and remarkably well preserved. Even so, these frozen mammoths are rare. Most of what is known comes from bones, teeth and tusks. Ice Age people drew and carved mammoths often, showing their humped backs and long hair. These images confirm what scientists have learned from preserved remains.

[long-pause]

{img:48-woolly-rhinoceros-steppe-wind}

The woolly rhinoceros lived on the cold steppes and was hunted and painted by Ice Age people. The woolly rhinoceros was a large animal covered with thick fur. It had two horns on its nose, the front one sometimes more than a metre long. It grazed on grasses and lived across Europe and northern Asia. Scientists have found preserved woolly rhinoceroses in frozen ground in Siberia and in an oil seep in Poland. These finds show the animal's fur, skin and shape. Ice Age people painted woolly rhinoceroses in caves, such as Chauvet in France. The images show their large horns and powerful bodies. The animal became extinct around fourteen thousand years ago. Researchers have studied ancient DNA from woolly rhinoceros remains. Some studies suggest that its population remained fairly stable until shortly before its extinction, which may point to climate change as the main cause. Even so, scientists continue to debate the role of human hunting. The woolly rhinoceros remains one of the most striking animals of the Ice Age.

[long-pause]

{img:49-cave-bear-den-cave-hibernating}

Cave bears were large bears that hibernated in caves across Ice Age Europe. The cave bear was larger than most modern brown bears. Despite its size, studies of its teeth and bones suggest that it ate mainly plants. Cave bears used caves for hibernation through the winter. Many died there, and their bones accumulated over thousands of years. Some caves contain the remains of hundreds or even thousands of bears. Claw marks on cave walls show where bears scratched the rock. In some painted caves, such as Chauvet, cave bear skulls were found placed in particular positions, perhaps by people. Researchers debate whether this had a ritual meaning. Cave bears became extinct around twenty-four thousand years ago. Possible reasons include climate change, a decline in their plant foods and competition with people for caves. Even so, their remains are among the most common fossils in European caves. Visitors to some caves can still see their hibernation hollows and scratch marks on the walls.

[long-pause]

{img:50-cave-lion-pride-tundra-dusk}

Cave lions were large predators that hunted on the Ice Age steppes. Cave lions were close relatives of modern lions, but slightly larger. They lived across Europe, northern Asia and parts of North America. Cave paintings, especially at Chauvet in France, show cave lions in remarkable detail. The paintings suggest that the males had little or no mane, unlike modern African lions. In recent years, frozen cave lion cubs have been discovered in the permafrost of Siberia, with fur and whiskers still preserved. These finds confirmed some details of their appearance. Cave lions hunted large animals such as reindeer, horses and young bison. They became extinct around fourteen thousand years ago. Scientists think that the loss of their prey animals and changes in climate were important factors. Even so, cave lions were clearly important in the minds of Ice Age people. They appear in art and in carved figures, including the famous Lion Man from Germany, which combines a lion's head with a human body.

[long-pause]

{img:51-giant-deer-megaloceros-antlers}

The giant deer, sometimes called the Irish elk, had the largest antlers of any deer that ever lived. The giant deer, known by its scientific name Megaloceros, was one of the most impressive animals of the Ice Age. Males had antlers that could measure more than three and a half metres from tip to tip. Despite its common name, the Irish elk, it was not an elk, and it lived across Europe and Asia, not only in Ireland. Many well-preserved skeletons have been found in Irish bogs, which explains the name. The giant deer lived in open woodland and grassland. It appears in some cave paintings in France. Scientists think that the enormous antlers were used to impress females and rival males. Growing them each year required a great deal of food and minerals. The giant deer disappeared from most of Europe at the end of the Ice Age. Even so, a population survived in western Siberia until around seven thousand seven hundred years ago, long after the ice had retreated.

[long-pause]

{img:52-wild-horses-steppe-wind-manes}

Wild horses were common on the Ice Age steppes and were among the animals most often painted in caves. Wild horses lived in large herds across the open grasslands of Ice Age Europe and Asia. They were smaller and stockier than most modern domestic horses, with short legs and thick coats. Horses were an important source of food for Ice Age people at many sites. They are also among the animals most frequently shown in cave art. In Lascaux and other caves, painted horses appear in many colours and poses. Some are shown with spotted coats, such as the famous dappled horses of Pech Merle in France. Scientists once thought these spots were imaginary, but ancient DNA research has shown that spotted horses did exist at that time. Even so, Ice Age horses were not tamed. The domestication of horses happened much later, around five and a half thousand years ago on the steppes of Central Asia. Wild horses continued to live in Europe for thousands of years after the Ice Age ended.

[long-pause]

{img:53-wolves-near-camp-early-dogs-dusk}

Dogs were domesticated from wolves during the late Ice Age. Wolves were common predators on the Ice Age steppes. They hunted the same animals as people, such as reindeer and horses. At some point during the late Ice Age, some wolves began to live closely with humans. Over generations, they became the first domestic dogs. Scientists still debate when and where this happened. Estimates range from about fifteen thousand to more than twenty thousand years ago. One of the oldest confirmed dogs was found buried with two people at Bonn-Oberkassel in Germany, dated to around fourteen thousand years ago. Studies of its bones show that it had been cared for during a serious illness, which suggests a close bond with people. Even so, the process of domestication was gradual and complex. Dogs may have helped people with hunting, guarding camps and carrying loads. The partnership between people and dogs that began in the Ice Age has continued ever since.

[long-pause]

{img:54-lascaux-painted-horses-lamplight}

The cave of Lascaux in France contains some of the most famous Ice Age paintings, made about seventeen thousand years ago. Lascaux is a cave in the Vézère valley in southwestern France. It was discovered in September 1940 by four teenagers, after a dog reportedly disappeared into a hole. Inside, they found walls covered with paintings of horses, aurochs, deer, bison and other animals. Scientists date the paintings to around seventeen thousand years ago. The artists used red, yellow and black pigments made from ochre, manganese and charcoal. Some animals are more than five metres long. The largest chamber is known as the Hall of the Bulls. Historians and archaeologists consider Lascaux one of the greatest achievements of Ice Age art. Even so, the cave proved fragile. After it opened to the public in 1948, the breath of many visitors caused damage and the growth of mould. It was closed to the public in 1963. Today, only a few scientists may enter. Visitors see accurate replicas built nearby instead.

[long-pause]

{img:55-chauvet-lions-charcoal-wall}

The cave of Chauvet in France contains some of the oldest known cave paintings, made more than thirty thousand years ago. Chauvet Cave is located in the Ardèche region of southern France. It was discovered in December 1994 by three cave explorers, led by Jean-Marie Chauvet. Inside, they found hundreds of paintings and engravings of animals. Radiocarbon dating showed that many were made around thirty-six thousand years ago, far older than scientists had expected for such sophisticated art. The paintings include lions, rhinoceroses, mammoths, horses, bears and owls. Many animals that were rarely painted elsewhere appear here. The artists used the natural shape of the rock to give the animals depth and movement. Some lions are shaded with smudged charcoal to suggest form. Historians note that Chauvet changed understanding of early art, showing that great skill existed very early. Even so, the cave was sealed by a rockfall thousands of years ago, which preserved it perfectly. It is closed to the public, but a replica called Chauvet Two opened nearby in 2015.

[long-pause]

{img:56-painter-blowing-ochre-handprint-wall}

Ice Age artists made hand stencils by blowing pigment around their hands pressed against cave walls. Among the most personal images of the Ice Age are hand stencils. They were made by placing a hand on the cave wall and blowing pigment around it, either from the mouth or through a hollow bone or reed. When the hand was removed, its outline remained. Hand stencils are found in many caves in Europe, such as Gargas in France and El Castillo in Spain. Some in El Castillo may be more than forty thousand years old. Researchers have studied the size of the hands to estimate the age and sex of the people who made them. The results suggest that both women and men, as well as children, made stencils. In some caves, fingers appear shortened, perhaps because they were bent or because of injury. Even so, the meaning of hand stencils remains uncertain. They may have been signatures, symbols or part of rituals. Yet they bring people very close to the individuals who once stood in those caves.

[long-pause]

{img:57-venus-figurine-carved-ivory-small}

Small carved female figurines, often called Venus figurines, were made across Europe during the Ice Age. Archaeologists have found more than two hundred small female figurines from Ice Age Europe. They were carved from ivory, stone, bone or modelled in clay. The best known is the Venus of Willendorf, found in Austria in 1908. It is made of limestone and is around thirty thousand years old. The oldest known example, the Venus of Hohle Fels from Germany, is carved from mammoth ivory and is around forty thousand years old. Many figurines emphasise the hips, belly and breasts, while faces are often not shown. The name Venus was given by early researchers and does not reflect the original meaning. Historians and archaeologists have proposed many interpretations. The figurines may represent fertility, motherhood, ancestors or simply women of the community. Even so, no single explanation is accepted. The figurines were made over a long period and across a wide area, so their meanings may have varied. They remain among the most discussed objects of the Ice Age.

[long-pause]

{img:58-lion-man-ivory-figurine-hohlenstein}

The Lion Man, carved from mammoth ivory around forty thousand years ago, is one of the oldest known figurative sculptures. In 1939, archaeologists excavating a cave called Hohlenstein-Stadel in southern Germany found fragments of carved mammoth ivory. Decades later, the pieces were assembled into a figure about thirty-one centimetres tall. It shows a standing figure with a human body and the head of a cave lion. It is known as the Lion Man. Scientists date it to around forty thousand years ago. Experiments with replica tools suggest that carving it would have taken several hundred hours of work. Historians see the Lion Man as evidence of imagination and belief, since it shows a being that does not exist in nature. It may represent a spirit, a mythical figure or a person in a ritual costume. Even so, its exact meaning is unknown. The Lion Man is now displayed in the Ulm Museum in Germany. The caves of the Swabian Jura, where it was found, were listed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2017.

[long-pause]

{img:59-bone-flute-vulture-music-cave}

Flutes made from bird bones and mammoth ivory are among the oldest known musical instruments. Music was part of life in the Ice Age. At Hohle Fels and other caves in southern Germany, archaeologists have found flutes made from the wing bones of birds, such as a griffon vulture and swans, and from mammoth ivory. The most complete one, made from a vulture bone, is about twenty-two centimetres long and has five finger holes. It has been dated to around forty thousand years ago. Researchers have made replicas and found that they can produce a range of clear notes. Making an ivory flute was especially difficult, since the ivory had to be split, hollowed and fitted back together. Historians note that these instruments suggest that music played an important role in social life, perhaps in gatherings, rituals or storytelling. Even so, no melody from the Ice Age can be known. Singing, drumming and other instruments made of wood or skin probably existed too, but they have not survived.

[long-pause]

{img:60-altamira-bison-ceiling-painting}

The painted ceiling of Altamira in Spain shows a group of bison in remarkable colour and detail. The cave of Altamira is located near the town of Santillana del Mar in northern Spain. Its paintings were discovered in 1879 by Marcelino Sanz de Sautuola and his young daughter María. The most famous chamber has a low ceiling covered with paintings of bison, shown in red and black. The artists used the natural bulges of the rock to give the animals a three-dimensional appearance. When the discovery was first announced, many experts refused to believe that such fine art could be prehistoric. Sautuola was accused of forgery. Only after similar paintings were found in France around 1900 was Altamira accepted as genuine. Scientists now date different parts of the art to between about thirty-six thousand and fifteen thousand years ago. Even so, the cave suffered from too many visitors and has been mostly closed for conservation. A detailed replica stands in the nearby museum, where visitors can see the painted ceiling.

[long-pause]

{img:61-sungir-burial-ivory-beads-grave}

Ice Age people buried some of their dead with great care, using ochre, ornaments and objects. Burials from the Ice Age show that people cared deeply for their dead. In many graves, bodies were covered with red ochre and accompanied by objects such as beads, tools and carved items. The burials at Sungir in Russia are among the richest. Others are known from sites such as Dolní Věstonice in the Czech Republic and Arene Candide in Italy. At Arene Candide, a young man known as the Young Prince was buried with a cap of shell beads and ochre, around twenty-eight thousand years ago. Historians and archaeologists see these burials as evidence of beliefs about death and perhaps an afterlife. Even so, only a small number of people seem to have been buried in this way. Most Ice Age burials are rare, and many people may have been treated differently after death. The careful burials that survive show love, respect and shared ritual. They reveal the emotional lives of people who lived thousands of years ago.

[long-pause]

{img:62-dolni-vestonice-clay-figurine-kiln}

At Dolní Věstonice in the Czech Republic, Ice Age people made figurines of fired clay, long before pottery. Dolní Věstonice is an important Ice Age site in Moravia, in the Czech Republic, dated to around twenty-nine thousand years ago. Archaeologists found evidence of mammoth hunters' dwellings, hearths and burials. They also found more than two thousand pieces of fired clay, including a famous female figurine known as the Venus of Dolní Věstonice. It is one of the oldest known objects made of fired clay. Clay figures of animals were also found. Some pieces seem to have exploded in the fire, perhaps deliberately. Researchers have suggested that this may have been part of a ritual. Fingerprints preserved on some of the clay show that people of different ages handled it, including children. Even so, people at Dolní Věstonice did not make pottery for cooking. Pottery came much later in Europe. The site also contained a triple burial of young people, laid out carefully with ochre and ornaments.

[long-pause]

{img:63-group-dance-firelight-cave-music}

Many researchers believe that painted caves were places for gatherings, rituals and music. The purpose of cave art has been debated for more than a century. Early researchers suggested that it was art for its own sake. Others proposed that it was hunting magic, meant to bring success in the hunt. Later, some researchers argued that the images had religious or shamanic meanings, connected with spirits and other worlds. Recent studies of acoustics have found that paintings are often located in parts of caves where sound echoes strongly. This suggests that music or chanting may have taken place there. Footprints in some caves show groups of people walking together. Historians note that no single theory explains all cave art. Paintings were made over more than twenty thousand years and may have had many meanings. Even so, it is clear that caves were special places. Entering them required lamps, effort and courage. The art suggests that Ice Age people shared stories and beliefs that were important enough to record deep underground.

[long-pause]

{img:64-shell-beads-necklace-trade-routes}

Ornaments such as shell beads and pierced teeth were worn and traded across long distances. Ice Age people wore ornaments, such as necklaces and decorations sewn onto clothing. Common materials included pierced shells, animal teeth, ivory beads and carved stones. Shells from the Mediterranean and Atlantic coasts have been found at sites far inland, hundreds of kilometres from the sea. Researchers believe they were passed from group to group through exchange networks. Pierced teeth of animals such as foxes, deer and bears were also popular. Archaeologists study the wear on beads to learn how they were strung and worn. Historians suggest that ornaments signalled identity, belonging or status. They might show which group a person belonged to, or mark important life events. Even so, the meanings are difficult to recover. What is clear is that people valued beauty and personal decoration, just as people do today. Beads were among the most common objects people made, and they were often carefully repaired and reused.

[long-pause]

{img:65-healing-care-injured-elder-hearth}

Some Ice Age skeletons show that injured or disabled people were cared for by others over long periods. Archaeologists have found Ice Age skeletons with healed fractures and long-term disabilities. Some people survived serious injuries that would have left them unable to hunt or travel for months. Their survival suggests that others provided food, shelter and care. Similar evidence exists from earlier times, including among Neanderthals. Historians and archaeologists see this as a sign of strong social bonds. Caring for vulnerable members of the group would have required effort and resources. One well-known example is a man buried at Dolní Věstonice who had physical abnormalities, yet was buried with care. Even so, interpreting such evidence requires caution. Researchers cannot always tell how severe an injury was or how long a person needed help. Yet the pattern across many sites suggests that compassion was part of Ice Age life. The people of the Ice Age survived not only through skill and toughness, but also through looking after one another.

[long-pause]

{img:66-gathering-many-bands-feast-summer-river}

Large seasonal gatherings may have strengthened ties between Ice Age groups. Some Ice Age sites are much larger than ordinary camps. They contain many hearths, large numbers of animal bones and a wide variety of objects. Researchers have suggested that such sites may have been places where several bands gathered, perhaps during seasons of plenty, such as the autumn reindeer migration. Similar gatherings are known among many recent hunter-gatherer peoples. They are occasions for feasting, trading, arranging marriages, settling disputes and performing ceremonies. Historians note that such meetings helped spread ideas and techniques across large regions. The similarity of art styles and tools over wide areas of Ice Age Europe may reflect these connections. Even so, it is difficult to prove that a particular site was a gathering place. Large sites might also result from repeated use by small groups over many years. Yet the idea of seasonal gatherings fits the evidence well. It suggests that Ice Age life combined small family groups with wider communities.

[long-pause]

{img:67-glaciers-melting-meltwater-lakes-spring}

After about nineteen thousand years ago, the climate slowly began to warm, and the great ice sheets began to melt. The Last Glacial Maximum did not last forever. From about nineteen thousand years ago, changes in the Earth's orbit increased the amount of summer sunlight in the northern hemisphere. Temperatures began to rise, and the ice sheets slowly began to melt. The process took thousands of years and was not smooth. There were sudden warm phases and sudden cold returns. One cold return, known as the Younger Dryas, began around twelve thousand nine hundred years ago and lasted about twelve hundred years. Then temperatures rose rapidly again. Scientists date the official end of the last Ice Age to about eleven thousand seven hundred years ago, the start of the period called the Holocene. Ice cores from Greenland record these changes year by year. Even so, the effects were felt differently in each region. Melting ice created huge lakes and floods. Sea levels rose, and the landscape of Europe changed dramatically.

[long-pause]

{img:68-forests-spread-birch-pine-tundra-retreat}

As the climate warmed, forests spread across the open grasslands of Europe. With rising temperatures and increasing rainfall, the landscape of Europe changed. Trees that had survived in sheltered southern areas spread northward. First came birch and pine, followed later by oak, elm and hazel. Pollen preserved in lake sediments allows scientists to trace this spread in detail. The open mammoth steppe gradually disappeared, replaced by forests and wetlands. Animals adapted to open, cold conditions lost their habitats. Reindeer moved north, following the retreating tundra. Forest animals, such as red deer, roe deer, wild boar and aurochs, became more common. Historians note that this change transformed human life. People adapted their hunting methods, using bows and arrows in forests. They gathered more plant foods, fish and shellfish. Even so, the change took many generations. Each generation would have seen only small changes. Yet over thousands of years, the world of the Ice Age hunters gave way to a very different landscape.

[long-pause]

{img:69-doggerland-flooding-coast-rising-sea}

Rising seas gradually flooded Doggerland and separated Britain from mainland Europe. As the ice sheets melted, sea levels rose by more than a hundred metres over several thousand years. Low-lying lands were slowly flooded. Doggerland, the plain that once connected Britain with mainland Europe, shrank into a series of islands and marshes. Around eight thousand years ago, a huge underwater landslide off the coast of Norway, known as the Storegga Slide, caused a tsunami that struck the coasts of the North Sea. Researchers believe it may have flooded much of what remained of Doggerland. By about seven thousand years ago, Britain had become an island. Archaeologists and geologists study the drowned landscape using maps of the sea floor and samples taken by drilling. Even so, much remains unknown. People who lived in Doggerland had to move as their land disappeared. Their camps and hunting grounds now lie under the North Sea, where fishing boats still occasionally bring up their tools.

[long-pause]

{img:70-mammoths-last-herds-wrangel-island}

Woolly mammoths disappeared from most of their range at the end of the Ice Age, but survived on a remote island much longer. As forests replaced grasslands, woolly mammoths lost their habitat. Their populations declined across Europe, Asia and North America. Most had disappeared by about ten thousand years ago. Scientists debate the relative importance of climate change and human hunting in their extinction. Many think both played a role. However, small populations survived on isolated islands. On Saint Paul Island in Alaska, mammoths lived until about five and a half thousand years ago. On Wrangel Island, off the coast of northeastern Siberia, they survived until around four thousand years ago. This means that mammoths were still alive when the pyramids of Egypt were being built. Studies of ancient DNA from Wrangel Island mammoths show that the population had become very small and genetically limited. Even so, the reasons for their final disappearance remain uncertain. Their story shows how slowly the world of the Ice Age faded.

[long-pause]

{img:71-hunters-new-forests-red-deer-bow}

After the Ice Age, people in Europe adapted to forests, using bows, fishing and gathering more plant foods. The period after the Ice Age in Europe is called the Mesolithic, or Middle Stone Age. People continued to live by hunting, fishing and gathering, but their way of life changed. In forests, they hunted red deer, roe deer and wild boar with bows and arrows tipped with small flint points, called microliths. They fished in rivers and lakes and gathered shellfish on the coasts. Some groups built dugout canoes and made wooden fish traps. One famous Mesolithic site in Britain is Star Carr in Yorkshire, where people lived about eleven thousand years ago. Archaeologists found antler headdresses and remains of wooden platforms there. Historians note that Mesolithic people were the direct descendants of Ice Age hunters in many regions. Even so, new groups also arrived and mixed with them. Ancient DNA shows several movements of people across Europe during this period. Their lives were shaped by the new forests and coasts.

[long-pause]

{img:72-first-farms-fields-near-east-later}

Farming began in the Near East around eleven thousand years ago, soon after the end of the Ice Age. The warmer and more stable climate after the Ice Age created new possibilities. In the Near East, in a region sometimes called the Fertile Crescent, people began to cultivate wild cereals such as wheat and barley. They also began to domesticate animals such as sheep, goats and cattle. This development, known as the Neolithic Revolution, began around eleven thousand years ago. Farming spread gradually into Europe, reaching Greece around eight and a half thousand years ago and Britain around six thousand years ago. Ancient DNA studies show that farming spread partly through the movement of farming people from Anatolia into Europe. They mixed with the descendants of Ice Age hunters. Even so, hunting and gathering continued in many regions for a long time. Historians see the end of the Ice Age as the beginning of a new chapter in human history. Yet modern Europeans still carry part of the ancestry of the Ice Age hunters in their DNA.

[long-pause]

{img:73-archaeologists-excavating-cave-floor-layers}

Archaeologists learn about Ice Age life by carefully excavating layers of soil at ancient sites. Ice Age sites are usually buried beneath layers of soil, sand or rock that built up over thousands of years. Archaeologists excavate them slowly and carefully, layer by layer. Each layer represents a different period of time. Archaeologists record the exact position of every object, from stone tools to animal bones and pieces of charcoal. They use grids, measurements and photographs to create detailed maps. Soil samples are collected and sieved to find tiny objects, such as beads, seeds and fish bones. The study of how sites formed is called site formation analysis. It helps researchers understand which objects belong together. Historians note that excavation methods have improved greatly since the nineteenth century, when many sites were dug quickly and carelessly. Even so, excavation is destructive, since each layer is removed. For this reason, modern archaeologists often leave parts of sites untouched for future researchers, who may have better methods.

[long-pause]

{img:74-radiocarbon-lab-samples-scientist}

Radiocarbon dating allows scientists to determine the age of bones, charcoal and other organic remains. Radiocarbon dating was developed in the late 1940s by the American chemist Willard Libby, who received the Nobel Prize for it in 1960. All living things absorb carbon, including a small amount of a radioactive form called carbon fourteen. After death, the carbon fourteen slowly decays at a known rate. By measuring how much remains, scientists can calculate how long ago the organism died. The method works for remains up to about fifty thousand years old, which covers the late Ice Age. Modern laboratories use machines called accelerator mass spectrometers, which need only tiny samples. This allows scientists to date even small pieces of charcoal from cave paintings. Even so, radiocarbon dates must be calibrated, because the amount of carbon fourteen in the atmosphere has varied over time. Researchers use tree rings, cave formations and other records to make these corrections. Calibrated dates have greatly improved the accuracy of Ice Age chronology.

[long-pause]

{img:75-ancient-dna-lab-clean-room-bone}

The study of ancient DNA has revealed how Ice Age populations moved and mixed across Europe. In recent decades, scientists have learned to extract DNA from ancient bones and teeth. The work must be done in very clean laboratories, since modern DNA can easily contaminate samples. The Swedish geneticist Svante Pääbo, a pioneer of this field, received the Nobel Prize in 2022 for his work on ancient human DNA, including the Neanderthal genome. Ancient DNA studies have shown that the population of Ice Age Europe changed several times. Some groups disappeared during the coldest periods, while others expanded from refuges in southern Europe. Studies have also revealed details such as eye and skin colour. Some Ice Age Europeans appear to have had dark skin and blue eyes. Even so, ancient DNA research is complex, and results are sometimes revised as new samples are studied. Historians note that it has transformed understanding of prehistory. Combined with archaeology, it allows researchers to trace the movements of people across thousands of years.

[long-pause]

{img:76-permafrost-cliff-siberia-mammoth-tusk}

Frozen ground in Siberia and Alaska preserves Ice Age animals in remarkable detail. In the far north, the ground beneath the surface has remained frozen since the Ice Age. This frozen ground, called permafrost, acts like a natural freezer. It has preserved the bodies of Ice Age animals, including mammoths, woolly rhinoceroses, cave lion cubs, horses and wolves, sometimes with fur, skin and internal organs intact. Many finds are made when riverbanks erode in summer or when local people search for mammoth ivory. Scientists study these remains to learn about the animals' appearance, diet, health and genetics. In 2018, a well-preserved foal of an extinct horse species was found in a Siberian crater. Even so, warming temperatures are now causing permafrost to thaw more rapidly. This exposes more remains, but also causes them to decay quickly once uncovered. Scientists race to study and preserve them. The permafrost is one of the most important archives of Ice Age life, and it is changing quickly.

[long-pause]

{img:77-museum-mammoth-skeleton-hall-visitors}

Museums around the world display Ice Age skeletons, tools and art. Many museums tell the story of the Ice Age. In France, the National Museum of Prehistory in Les Eyzies displays tools, art and remains from the Vézère valley. In Germany, the Blaubeuren museum shows finds from the Swabian caves, including ivory figurines and flutes. In Britain, the Natural History Museum in London displays mammoth and other Ice Age skeletons. In Russia, the Zoological Museum in Saint Petersburg holds famous frozen mammoths. Historians note that museums allow people to see objects that would otherwise remain in storage or in fragile sites. Many museums now use reconstructions, models and digital displays to show what Ice Age life may have looked like. Even so, reconstructions involve interpretation. Museums often explain which details are certain and which are uncertain. A visit to such a museum allows people to stand face to face with the tools, ornaments and art of people who lived tens of thousands of years ago.

[long-pause]

{img:78-replica-cave-lascaux-iv-visitors-quiet}

Replicas allow visitors to experience famous painted caves without damaging the originals. Since the original caves are too fragile for regular visits, several detailed replicas have been built. Lascaux Two opened in 1983, recreating the main chambers of Lascaux. A larger and more complete replica, known as Lascaux Four, opened in 2016 near the town of Montignac. It reproduces almost the entire cave, using 3D scanning, sculpting and painting by skilled artists. The replica of Chauvet, called the Grotte Chauvet Two, opened in 2015 and reproduces the most important parts of the cave. Altamira also has a replica in its museum. Historians and archaeologists note that these replicas help protect the original sites while allowing the public to understand Ice Age art. Even so, some visitors miss the experience of the real caves. Yet the replicas are remarkably faithful. Lighting, temperature and sound are carefully designed. Walking through them, visitors can see the animals painted by Ice Age artists much as they first appeared.

[long-pause]

{img:79-glacier-today-alps-blue-ice-valley}

Glaciers and valleys shaped by the Ice Age can still be seen across Europe today. The landscapes of Europe still show the effects of the last Ice Age. Mountain glaciers in the Alps, Scandinavia and Iceland are smaller remnants of the great ice of the past. Many valleys in mountain regions have a broad U shape, carved by glaciers. Lakes such as those of the Alps, Scotland and Scandinavia often fill hollows scraped out by ice. In northern Europe and North America, boulders carried by ice lie scattered across fields. Historians and geologists use these features to reconstruct the extent of the ice. Even so, today's glaciers are shrinking rapidly. Many Alpine glaciers have lost much of their volume in recent decades. Scientists measure these changes carefully. They help researchers understand both the past and the present climate. Visitors to mountain regions can still walk to glacier edges and see the polished rock and moraines left behind by ice that once covered much of the land.

[long-pause]

{img:80-valley-dordogne-today-cliffs-river-calm}

The Vézère valley in France, rich in Ice Age sites, is now a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The Vézère valley in the Dordogne region of southwestern France contains one of the greatest concentrations of Ice Age sites in the world. It includes Lascaux, many decorated caves and dozens of rock shelters. In 1979, the prehistoric sites and decorated caves of the valley were listed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The village of Les Eyzies is often called the capital of prehistory. Its museum stands beneath a cliff where Ice Age people once lived. Today, the valley is a quiet landscape of limestone cliffs, woods, meadows and small villages along the river. Visitors come to see the caves, museums and rock shelters. Historians note that the valley's sites have been studied since the nineteenth century and have shaped understanding of the Ice Age. Even so, research continues, and new discoveries are still made. In the evening, the river flows quietly beneath the cliffs where people lived twenty thousand years ago.

[long-pause]

{img:81-reindeer-herders-tundra-today-arctic}

Reindeer still live on the Arctic tundra, where some peoples continue to herd them today. Reindeer, once a central part of Ice Age life in Europe, now live mainly in the Arctic and subarctic regions of Scandinavia, Russia and North America, where they are known as caribou. Several Arctic peoples, such as the Sámi of northern Scandinavia and the Nenets of Siberia, herd reindeer as part of their traditional way of life. Their knowledge of cold climates, clothing and animal behaviour has helped researchers imagine how Ice Age people may have lived. Historians and anthropologists caution, however, that modern Arctic peoples are not living copies of Ice Age societies. They have their own long histories and cultures. Even so, their skills show what is possible in extreme cold. Fur clothing, hide tents and careful use of every part of the animal are practical solutions that people have developed again and again. The sight of a reindeer herd moving across the snowy tundra recalls the landscapes of twenty thousand years ago.

[long-pause]

{img:82-cave-entrance-today-quiet-forest-light}

Many Ice Age caves now lie quietly in forests and hillsides, protected for the future. Across Europe, hundreds of caves and rock shelters once used by Ice Age people now lie in peaceful landscapes. Many are protected by law and closed to the public to preserve their fragile contents. Some have gates to prevent damage. Others are open for guided visits in small groups, with strict limits on numbers. Scientists monitor temperature, humidity and air quality inside painted caves to protect the art. Historians note that the protection of these sites is a responsibility shared by many countries. Even so, threats remain, including climate change, tourism and vandalism. New caves continue to be discovered. In 1991, the Cosquer Cave was found near Marseille, with an entrance now deep under the sea, since sea levels have risen since the Ice Age. Its paintings include seals and penguin-like great auks. Each new discovery adds to the picture of life during the Ice Age.

[long-pause]

{img:83-starry-sky-snowy-valley-night-calm}

The Ice Age shaped the land, the animals and the people of Europe in ways that are still visible today. The last Ice Age lasted for tens of thousands of years. At its coldest, around twenty thousand years ago, ice covered much of the north, and people lived in a world of tundra, steppe and great herds. They survived through skill, cooperation and knowledge. They made needles, clothing, tools, lamps and shelters of hide and bone. They painted animals on cave walls, carved figures and played flutes. They cared for each other and buried their dead with ornaments. Historians, archaeologists and scientists continue to study their lives through excavation, dating and ancient DNA. Even so, much remains unknown. Their languages, stories and names are lost. Yet their descendants live on, and part of their ancestry survives in people today. The landscape, too, remembers them in its valleys, caves and lakes. On clear winter nights, snow still covers quiet valleys, and the stars shine as they did over the camps of the Ice Age.
