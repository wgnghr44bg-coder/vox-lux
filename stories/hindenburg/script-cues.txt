Tonight we are going almost ninety years back, to a wide green airfield on the edge of Frankfurt, in Germany, where an enormous silver airship is floating just above the grass.

[long-pause]

It is the evening of the third of May, 1937, and the air is mild and still.

[pause]

The airship is called the Hindenburg. She is the largest thing that has ever flown, longer than two football pitches laid end to end, and she hangs there in the soft light as if she were made of nothing at all.

[pause]

If you enjoy quiet stories like this one, you are welcome to subscribe to the channel. And now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

The airfield is called Rhein-Main, and it lies among pine woods and fields, a few miles south of the city. Beyond the trees, the roofs of Frankfurt glow a little in the late sun, and somewhere far off a church clock is striking.

[pause]

On the grass, small groups of people are walking towards the ship. Men in dark overcoats and hats. Women in long coats with fur collars, holding their gloves. A porter pushing a cart with leather suitcases, each one tied with a strap.

[pause]

Children walk between their parents, looking up. Most of them have never seen anything so large, and they do not quite believe it.

[long-pause]

Around the ship stand the men of the ground crew, a hundred or more of them, in grey work clothes and flat caps. They hold the long mooring ropes in their hands, and they stand quite still, as they have been taught to do.

[pause]

The airship rests a few metres above the ground, held gently in place. When the wind moves, she turns a little, very slowly, like a great silver fish in a calm pond.

[pause]

From where the passengers stand, it is hard to see where she ends. The nose is far away to one side, and the tail is far away on the other. The huge fins at the back rise higher than a house.

[long-pause]

{img:silver-hull-close-detail}
Close up, the skin of the ship is not metal at all. It is cotton cloth, stretched tight over a framework and painted with a silver coating, and in the evening light it has a soft, pearly shine.

[pause]

Along the side, in tall black letters, is a single word: Hindenburg. The letters are so big that a person standing underneath can read only part of one at a time.

[pause]

Below the middle of the ship, there is a small, neat gondola, hardly larger than a railway carriage, and it looks very tiny under all that silver. That is where the passengers will walk in.

[long-pause]

Beside the field there is a low white building with a wide veranda, where the passengers have been waiting since the afternoon. Inside, the staff of the airline are checking tickets and weighing luggage, and writing everything down in tall green books.

[pause]

A clerk calls out a name. A woman in a grey travelling coat stands up and smiles, and goes to the desk.

[pause]

Nobody is in a hurry. The airship will not leave until the evening, and no one here has ever been late for an airship. It would not wait for them, but it has never needed to.

[long-pause]

This is how people travelled across the Atlantic Ocean in the spring of 1937. Not on a loud, small aeroplane that could only cross the sea with great difficulty, but on a floating hotel, high above the waves, with beds and a dining room and a quiet lounge.

[pause]

It takes about two and a half days to reach America. The ship will fly west over the sea, past Ireland, over Newfoundland, down the coast of Canada and New England, and over the city of New York, until it comes to a small, flat place in New Jersey called Lakehurst.

[pause]

The passengers on the ground tonight will be there on Thursday morning, if all goes well.

[long-pause]

{img:passengers-walking-to-ship}
But before we go on board, let us step back a little, and see how this ship came to be here.

[pause]

Because the Hindenburg did not appear from nowhere. She was the work of many years and many hands, in a small town on the shore of a lake, a few hundred miles to the south. And in that town, there is a story that begins long before her first flight, with an old count who had one very stubborn idea.

[pause]

Let us go there first, and then we will come back to this field in Frankfurt, in time for the evening.

[long-pause]

{img:lake-constance-friedrichshafen}
The town is called Friedrichshafen, and it lies on the northern shore of Lake Constance, where Germany, Switzerland and Austria meet around a wide, quiet sheet of water.

[pause]

On a clear morning, the lake is pale blue and almost still. Little white steamers cross it from harbour to harbour, and fishing boats rest near the reeds. Behind the water, far to the south, the snowy line of the Alps stands up against the sky.

[pause]

It is a gentle place, with orchards on the hillsides and church towers with round green roofs. And for about forty years, it has also been the home of the airships.

[long-pause]

The idea began with a count. His name was Ferdinand von Zeppelin, and he was born in 1838, in a town on this same lake. As a young man he was a soldier, and he travelled widely. He even went to America, where he saw a balloon rise above the fields of Minnesota.

[pause]

He never forgot it. Years later, when most men would have been thinking about retirement, he began to draw a different kind of aircraft. Not a round balloon that drifts wherever the wind blows, but a long one with a rigid frame inside, with engines and rudders, that could travel in a chosen direction.

[pause]

Many people thought it was a foolish dream. The count was nearly sixty, and he put almost all his own money into it.

[long-pause]

{img:first-zeppelin-over-the-lake}
On a warm summer evening in July of 1900, his first airship rose from a floating shed on the lake. It was long and thin, like a cigar made of cloth, and it travelled for a few miles before settling back on the water.

[pause]

The people who watched it from the shore were silent at first. And then they began to cheer.

[pause]

It was not a very good airship yet. But it flew, and that was the beginning. In the years that followed, the count built bigger and better ones, and his name became so well known that the word zeppelin came to mean any such ship, anywhere in the world.

[long-pause]

The count died in 1917, and he did not see what came next. But the work of the company he started carried on, and it was soon in the hands of a man who knew the airships better than anyone.

[pause]

His name was Hugo Eckener. He had once been a newspaper writer and a student of the human mind, a big, calm man with a short grey beard and a very steady manner. He had been won over to the zeppelins by the count himself, and in time he became their best captain and the head of the company.

[pause]

Eckener believed that airships were not weapons or toys. He believed they could carry ordinary people across the world, comfortably and safely, and he spent his life trying to prove it.

[long-pause]

{img:graf-zeppelin-over-the-alps}
In 1928, the company finished a new airship, named Graf Zeppelin in honour of the old count. She was bigger and more comfortable than anything before her, and Eckener flew her across the Atlantic, and then, in 1929, right around the whole world.

[pause]

It took twenty-one days. The ship flew over Germany and Russia, over the vast, empty forests of Siberia, across the Pacific to Tokyo and Los Angeles, and on to New York and home again.

[pause]

Crowds gathered wherever she appeared. People stood on rooftops and in fields and waved their hats. In the years afterwards, the Graf Zeppelin carried thousands of passengers over the oceans, and in all those years, not one passenger was ever hurt.

[long-pause]

That record was the pride of the whole company. It is the reason the airships were trusted. A mother could put her child on board, and a banker could send his letters, and everyone could be sure that Eckener's captains would take them safely there and back.

[pause]

{img:graf-zeppelin-saloon-passengers}
The Graf Zeppelin was a lovely ship, but she was not very large. She had room for only about twenty passengers, who slept in small cabins and ate at one long table, and there was never a spare cabin when the sailings were full.

[pause]

The company had grown by now, and the passengers were asking for more. People wanted to fly to America in their hundreds, not their dozens. They wanted a bigger ship, with more space and more comfort, that could make the crossing again and again, every week.

[long-pause]

So, in the early 1930s, in the drawing office at Friedrichshafen, the designers sat down to plan a new airship, bigger than anything the world had ever seen.

[pause]

The chief designer was a man named Ludwig Dürr, who had worked with the old count himself, and who had drawn the plans for almost every Zeppelin since the first. He was quiet and exact, and he knew the fine points of every girder.

[pause]

They worked in a long room with high windows, at wide wooden tables, with rulers and compasses and thousands of sheets of paper. Outside, the lake glittered in the afternoon light, and every so often one of them would look up and watch the gulls.

[long-pause]

The new ship would be given the number LZ 129. The letters stood for Luftschiffbau Zeppelin, which means Zeppelin Airship Construction, and the number meant that she was the hundred and twenty-ninth airship the company had designed.

[pause]

She would be nearly two hundred and fifty metres long, and she would carry fifty passengers in cabins like those on an ocean liner, with a dining room and a lounge and a long promenade with big windows. She would cross the sea in half the time of the fastest ship.

[pause]

It was an enormous plan. And it needed an enormous hall to build her in.

[long-pause]

{img:giant-construction-hall-exterior}
Beside the lake, a little way from the harbour, stood the great construction hall of the Zeppelin company.

[pause]

It was one of the largest buildings in Germany. It was longer than two hundred metres and as tall as a cathedral, with a curved roof, and from the outside it looked like an enormous barn made of iron and glass. Its doors were so big that a whole house could have been rolled through them.

[pause]

Inside, there was room for an airship to be built from the first girder to the last rivet, safe from the wind and the rain.

[long-pause]

The first thing to appear was the skeleton.

[pause]

It was made of a metal called duralumin, an alloy of aluminium that is as strong as steel but far lighter. Workers made it into long, thin girders, each one shaped like a narrow triangle, with tiny round holes drilled all along its sides to make it lighter still. A person could lift one of them with two hands.

[pause]

These girders were riveted together to form great rings, as wide as a street and as tall as a three-storey house. One by one, the rings were lifted into place, standing upright in a long line, like the ribs of a whale.

[long-pause]

Between the rings ran long girders from nose to tail, and they were braced with fine steel wires, which were tightened and tuned by hand. The men who did this work moved along the frame on narrow catwalks, high above the floor, and their hammers rang softly in the huge, echoing hall.

[pause]

{img:workers-riveting-girders}
It took many months. The workers brought their lunches in tin boxes and ate them sitting on the girders with their legs hanging down into the empty space. Some of them had worked on the Graf Zeppelin, and some of them had even worked on the first airships of the old count.

[pause]

They knew every part of the work, and they took great care, because they knew that the ship would one day carry real people, and that it must be right.

[long-pause]

{img:gas-cell-being-hung-in-frame}
Inside the frame, the gas cells were hung.

[pause]

These were enormous bags, sixteen in all, and each one was as large as a small house. They were made of cotton, lined with a thin layer of gelatine to make them airtight, and they hung in the frame like soft, pale balloons, each in its own section.

[pause]

When the ship was finished, they would be filled with a gas lighter than air, and together they would hold about two hundred thousand cubic metres of it. That is enough to fill eighty Olympic swimming pools.

[long-pause]

There was a question about which gas to use, and it was an important one.

[pause]

The safest gas for an airship is helium. It is light and does not burn at all. But in those years, almost all the helium in the world came from a few wells in the United States, and the American government did not want to sell it to Germany.

[pause]

So the designers had to turn to the other light gas, hydrogen, which was cheap and easy to make, and which had been used in airships for thirty years. Hydrogen burns easily, but the Zeppelin men knew how to handle it. They had flown it for millions of miles, and they were careful with it. Their ships were built with that care in mind, and no one on board would be allowed to light a flame.

[pause]

Still, the designers left their plans open. Eckener hoped that one day he would be able to buy helium, and the ship was built so that it could be changed over to use it.

[long-pause]

{img:covering-the-frame-with-cloth}
When the frame and the gas cells were in place, the covering was added.

[pause]

Seamstresses, working in a large, bright room, cut and stitched sheets of strong cotton, and the sheets were laid over the frame and pulled tight. Then the cloth was painted with several coats of a special varnish, mixed with aluminium powder, which made it stiff as a drum and shining silver. It reflected the sunlight, so that the gas inside would stay cool.

[pause]

Painters then marked the great letters along the sides. The company took care that every letter should be perfectly straight, and they measured them with long strings.

[long-pause]

At the sides of the ship, four small gondolas were fixed to the hull, each one holding an engine.

[pause]

They were diesel engines, built by the Daimler-Benz company, and each one was as big as a small car. Each drove a huge wooden propeller, almost six metres across, which turned slowly and steadily, with a deep, humming sound.

[pause]

Diesel fuel does not burn as easily as petrol, and that was another reason the designers chose it. The four engines together could push the ship through the air at about eighty miles an hour.

[long-pause]

{img:name-painted-on-the-hull}
And then there was the name.

[pause]

The new ship was to be named after the old president of Germany, Paul von Hindenburg, who had died in 1934. He had been a field marshal in the First World War and a president afterwards, and he was very well known to ordinary people. They thought of him as a tall, calm, white-moustached grandfather figure. It seemed a fitting name for a very large, very steady ship.

[pause]

So she became the Hindenburg.

[long-pause]

{img:hindenburg-leaving-hall-morning}
In the early spring of 1936, the great doors of the hall slid open, and the ship came out into the morning.

[pause]

She was pulled out slowly by hundreds of men, who held her ropes, and she floated just above the ground. The crowd that gathered along the shore had never seen anything so big. People said they could not understand how it could rise at all.

[pause]

On the fourth of March, 1936, with the sun on the lake and the Alps behind her, the Hindenburg made her first flight.

[long-pause]

{img:first-flight-over-lake-constance}
For the first test flights, the Hindenburg stayed close to home.

[pause]

She rose from the field at Friedrichshafen with her engines humming, turned slowly over the lake, and flew out above the orchards and the little villages, while farmers stopped their work in the fields and looked up. Then she returned the same evening and settled gently in front of the hall.

[pause]

The engineers walked around her with notebooks, measuring and noting down every small thing. Eckener went up himself, and he came down pleased.

[long-pause]

The ship was working beautifully. She was steadier than the old Graf Zeppelin, and quieter, and she answered the rudders smoothly. She seemed to be a ship that wanted to fly.

[pause]

{img:test-flight-above-german-countryside}
During the spring, she made several longer trips across Germany, with guests on board, and she was seen over the roofs of Munich and Hamburg and the Rhine valley. In every town, people hurried out of their houses and stood in the streets, with their hands over their eyes, watching the great silver shape pass overhead.

[pause]

It must have felt like seeing a cloud that had decided to go somewhere.

[long-pause]

In May of 1936, she made her first crossing of the Atlantic.

[pause]

She left Frankfurt in the evening, with fifty-one passengers, and flew west over the sea for more than two days. Ships far below sent greetings by wireless as she passed, and on the third morning she reached the coast of America and drifted over the quiet fields of New Jersey, to the long hangar at Lakehurst.

[pause]

A crowd was waiting at the fence. They had come to see the largest flying machine in the world, and for a while they said nothing at all.

[long-pause]

{img:new-york-skyline-airship-1936}
She went on to fly over New York, at about the height of the rooftops of the tallest buildings.

[pause]

Office workers went to their windows and leaned out. Taxi drivers stopped in the middle of the streets. Some of them ran onto the pavement, and a few of them took off their hats. The newspapers said that the whole city had stopped, just for a minute, to look up.

[pause]

The Hindenburg was famous from then on, in America as well as in Germany.

[long-pause]

{img:rio-de-janeiro-sugarloaf-airship}
That summer, she also began to fly to South America.

[pause]

Her route to Rio de Janeiro took her south, over the coast of Portugal and the island of Madeira, and out across the warm blue Atlantic, where flying fish skimmed over the water. She arrived in the early morning, with the sun rising behind Sugarloaf Mountain, and settled on the airfield outside the city, in front of a crowd of people who had come to see her.

[pause]

On the ship, there was coffee and fresh rolls, and the passengers stood at the windows to look at the green mountains and the bay.

[long-pause]

In August, she was seen over Berlin, flying slowly above the great stadium during the opening of the Olympic Games, while a hundred thousand people looked up from the seats.

[pause]

These were years when Germany was under the rule of the Nazi party, and the government of the day liked to show the airship off as a symbol of the nation's cleverness. Eckener himself did not like politics and did not like the new government, and he often said so. For him, the ship belonged to the passengers and to the crew, and to the idea of flying peacefully from one land to another.

[long-pause]

{img:max-schmeling-aboard-lounge}
Many famous people travelled in her that summer.

[pause]

The boxer Max Schmeling flew home on the Hindenburg in June, after winning a famous fight in New York, and he was welcomed home with great excitement. Business people, diplomats and writers, scientists and film stars, all of them wanted to try the new way of crossing the sea.

[pause]

A ticket cost about four hundred dollars one way, which was a great deal of money in those years. But the seats were always full.

[long-pause]

By the end of the season, the Hindenburg had made ten round trips to the United States and seven to Brazil.

[pause]

She had carried nearly three thousand passengers, and flown over three hundred thousand kilometres, without any serious trouble. The pilots knew her well, and the crew had learned all her habits. Her captain, a calm, solid man named Max Pruss, had flown on airships for many years, and he trusted her completely.

[pause]

When the last flight of the season was over, in October, she returned to her hangar to rest for the winter.

[long-pause]

{img:winter-hangar-airship-work}
During the winter, the engineers made some changes.

[pause]

The passengers had said that fifty places were not enough. People were being turned away. So the company decided to make more room. Along the lower deck, a row of new cabins was built, which made it possible to carry seventy-two passengers instead of fifty.

[pause]

The little aluminium piano, which had been on board in the first season, was taken out, to save weight. Every kilogram counted when there were so many more passengers and so much more luggage.

[pause]

And so, in the spring of 1937, the Hindenburg was ready to begin her second season, with a new schedule, more cabins, and more people than ever waiting to fly.

[long-pause]

{img:gas-cells-rising-in-the-keel-light}
Before we go on, it may be pleasant to understand how a ship like this stays in the air at all.

[pause]

The idea is very old and very simple. Hydrogen is the lightest gas there is, and it is lighter than air, so a large bag filled with it wants to rise, in the same way that a cork wants to rise in a bucket of water. The Hindenburg was a very large bag, or really sixteen bags together, and the push from all that gas was enough to lift the heavy frame, the engines, the passengers, the food, the fuel and the luggage, and to hold them quietly in the sky.

[long-pause]

An airship does not need to move fast to stay up. That is why she is so different from an aeroplane. The aeroplane must keep rushing forward, or she will fall. The airship floats, even when she is standing still.

[pause]

So when the pilots slow the engines, she does not sink. She simply hangs there, as a ship hangs on the sea, and the engines do no more than push her gently from place to place. From the windows, it feels as though the ship is standing still and the world is slowly gliding past.

[long-pause]

The pilots have to look after the balance of the ship all the time. If she is too heavy, she sinks. If she is too light, she rises. If her nose is too high or too low, the cargo and the passengers can feel it.

[pause]

To keep her steady, they use water. All along the ship there are tanks, filled with water that serves as ballast, and the pilots can let some of it out when they want to rise, or hold it when they want to stay low. There are also moving weights, and the elevator wheels in the control car, which tilt the nose up and down in small, careful steps.

[pause]

{img:ballast-water-tanks-in-the-keel}
The sun has an effect, too. When it shines on the silver skin, the gas inside warms and expands, and the ship rises a little. In the evening, when the air cools, the gas cools, and she sinks. The pilots know these habits very well, and they move to meet them before the passengers notice anything at all.

[long-pause]

There are valves on top of the gas cells, which let the gas out if the pressure becomes too high. When the ship rises, the gas expands, and some of it must be released, or the cells would swell and burst. Each valve is opened by a cord that runs down to the control car.

[pause]

The pilots use these valves sparingly. Every bit of hydrogen they let out is a little less lift for the rest of the trip. They watch the gauges, and calculate the weight of everything on board, from the last drop of fuel to the last loaf of bread, so that on landing day the ship will come in at exactly the right weight.

[pause]

It is a delicate kind of work, like looking after a very large, very patient animal. The pilots speak about the ship almost as a person. She is tender in the morning, they say, or she is heavy in the rain, or she is happy in the cool of the evening.

[long-pause]

What the passengers feel is only peace.

[pause]

There is no bumping, no engine roar, and almost no sense of motion. They can sit in a chair with a cup of tea and the cup does not tremble. They can walk down the long corridor without holding the rail. Sometimes they forget altogether that they are flying.

[pause]

{img:teacup-resting-still-on-lounge-table}
It is perhaps the secret of the airships' charm. They did not tear through the sky. They went over the world like a thought, gentle and almost silent, and for those who rode in them, the whole earth seemed to turn slowly beneath a window.

[long-pause]

{img:airship-over-the-bay-of-biscay-at-dawn}
The Hindenburg did not only fly to the north. Her other great route went south, to Brazil, and it was a very different sort of journey.

[pause]

She left from Frankfurt, or sometimes from Friedrichshafen, and flew across France and down the coast of Spain and Portugal. By the first evening, she was over the sea, with the lights of Lisbon fading behind. The water turned from grey-green to a deep and shining blue, and the air grew softer and warmer.

[long-pause]

On the second morning, the passengers saw the Canary Islands rising out of the water, with their steep brown mountains and white villages. Then, the tropics began.

[pause]

The windows were opened wide. The passengers took off their jackets and sat in the sun on the promenade in their shirtsleeves. The sea was a bright turquoise, with long, slow swells, and sometimes small groups of flying fish rose from the waves and skimmed away over the surface, glittering in the light.

[pause]

A few passengers fell asleep in their chairs, with their hats over their faces.

[long-pause]

{img:cape-verde-islands-warm-sea-and-ship-shadow}
They passed close to the islands of Cape Verde, where the volcanic rock rose out of the sea in dark cones, and where white surf broke on black beaches. Small fishing boats with brown sails bobbed in the bays.

[pause]

Then there was nothing but the ocean for a day and a night. The air grew heavy and warm. In the cabins, the stewards opened the little vents, and the evening came down red and gold across the sea.

[long-pause]

Somewhere in the middle of the Atlantic, the ship crossed the equator.

[pause]

It was a custom on old sailing ships to hold a little ceremony when crossing the line, and the airship men kept it up, in a gentle way. A steward dressed up as the old sea-king Neptune, with a long beard made of cotton, and walked solemnly through the lounge, and the passengers who had never crossed before were given a certificate with a stamp.

[pause]

There was laughter, and glasses of cool lemonade, and a few songs. Even the serious businessmen joined in.

[long-pause]

{img:brazilian-coast-first-sight-from-promenade}
On the fourth morning, the coast of Brazil appeared as a thin green line.

[pause]

It grew into a wide curve of white sand and tall palms, with red-roofed towns and blue hills behind. The passengers crowded to the windows. The air coming in smelled of flowers and warm earth.

[pause]

The ship came down the coast, past the small port of Recife, and on towards Rio de Janeiro. Fishermen in their little rafts, called jangadas, looked up from the water and waved their hats.

[long-pause]

{img:rio-sugarloaf-and-bay-from-the-airship}
Rio was the loveliest sight of all.

[pause]

The city lay along the shore in a great ring of beaches, below mountains covered with forest, and the bay was dotted with green islands. Sugarloaf Mountain stood at the entrance, like a huge smooth stone, and the statue on the high peak above the town raised its arms to the sky.

[pause]

The ship made a slow, wide circle over the water, so that everyone could see. Then she turned inland to the airfield, and the long journey was over.

[long-pause]

Many passengers remembered the Brazil flights as the happiest of all.

[pause]

There was no cold wind, no dark ocean, and no worries about storms. There was only the long blue light, the warm air, and the feeling of being carried gently across half the world. They talked about it for years, and they kept their postcards from Rio in the drawer beside their bed.

[pause]

And yet it was the northern route, the road to New York and Lakehurst, that had the greater fame. It was more direct, it was busier, and it ended in the great American city, where thousands of people waited each time to see the silver ship arrive.

[pause]

It was along that road that she made her last journey.

[long-pause]

{img:passengers-checking-in-hall}
Let us return to the field at Frankfurt, on that mild May evening.

[pause]

In the waiting room, the clerks are calling the passengers one by one. There are thirty-six of them tonight, which is far fewer than the ship can carry. In the early part of the season, the flights from Germany are never full, and so there is plenty of room for everyone to spread out.

[pause]

Among them are businessmen and engineers, a few families, a retired couple going to visit a daughter, and a travelling entertainer with his equipment. Some of them are German, and some are American. A few of them are returning home to Mexico, to Canada, to Ohio, after many months away.

[long-pause]

Their luggage has been weighed and labelled and sent ahead. The weight matters very much on an airship, and each passenger is allowed only a small amount. So the suitcases are tidy and small, and people have packed with great care, choosing one good suit, one evening dress, and one pair of walking shoes.

[pause]

A young man who is travelling with a camera has paid a little extra to bring it. A mother has packed some wooden toys for her children, wrapped in a woollen scarf.

[long-pause]

{img:matches-and-lighters-handed-over}
Before they walk to the ship, there is one rule that everyone is told about.

[pause]

At a small table by the door, an officer of the airline asks each passenger, politely, to hand over any matches and lighters. They are kept in a box, and returned when the journey is over. Nobody argues. A man pats his coat and finds a lighter he had forgotten, and passes it over with an apologetic smile.

[pause]

It is a quiet little ceremony, repeated at every departure. The gas that lifts the ship is the kind that burns, and the airline is careful. Smoking is allowed in only one room on the whole ship, and that room has its own special door.

[long-pause]

{img:walking-across-the-grass-to-gondola}
Then, the passengers walk out across the grass.

[pause]

The air has turned a little cooler, and the sun is low behind the pine trees. On the field, the ship seems even bigger than before, with the light of the evening glowing on her silver side. The people walk slowly, in twos and threes, and some of them stop to look up.

[pause]

A boy in short trousers tugs his father's sleeve and asks how such a thing can stay in the sky. His father answers that it is full of a very light gas, and that it is like a ship on the sea, only the sea is made of air. The boy nods, but he does not look convinced.

[long-pause]

At the foot of the ship, a short staircase leads up into the gondola.

[pause]

The steps are narrow and carpeted, and a steward in a white jacket stands at the top, welcoming everyone by name. He takes their coats and their hats, and shows them the way along a corridor, then up a spiral staircase to the main deck. Everywhere is clean, and there is a faint smell of fresh paint and warm wood.

[pause]

The passengers can hardly believe how quiet it is inside. After the open field, it feels like stepping into the entrance hall of a small hotel.

[long-pause]

{img:relatives-waving-on-the-field}
On the field, friends and relatives gather behind a low rope to wave goodbye. There are not many tonight, but they have brought handkerchiefs, and some are holding up small flags.

[pause]

A few of the crew have family nearby, and a small boy is lifted on to his mother's shoulders, to see his father at one of the windows.

[pause]

Photographers are busy among the crowd. They take pictures of the ship, and the passengers, and the ground crew in their grey coats, and then they pack their cameras into leather cases and wait.

[long-pause]

Inside, the officers walk through the ship one last time, checking that everything is in its place. The mail has been loaded, the food is stored in the galley, and the water tanks are full. The last sacks of letters are carried on board, each one stamped with a special airship postmark.

[pause]

A little after eight o'clock, a bell rings, and a voice calls out an order over the field. The ground crew tighten their grip on the ropes.

[long-pause]

{img:engines-starting-propellers-turning}
One by one, the four engines start. First there is a cough, and then a deep, steady hum that grows until it fills the whole airfield. The propellers swing slowly, and then faster, and the grass beneath them bends flat.

[pause]

Then the ship is released.

[pause]

The ground crew let go of the ropes and step back. And, with no jolt and no noise except for the gentle sound of the engines, the Hindenburg begins to rise.

[pause]

The passengers at the windows see the field become smaller, quietly and without effort, as if the ground itself were sinking away.

[long-pause]

{img:spiral-staircase-to-passenger-deck}
Let us follow the passengers up the stairs.

[pause]

The passenger rooms are not in the gondola at all. They are inside the body of the ship itself, in the middle of the hull, on two decks, one above the other. The upper deck is called A deck, and the lower one is called B deck. It is as if a small ocean liner had been built inside a silver whale.

[pause]

At the top of the staircase, there is a wide corridor with a soft carpet, and a faint scent of flowers.

[long-pause]

Down the middle of A deck runs the corridor, and on each side of it are the cabins.

[pause]

Each cabin is small and neat, like a cabin on a night train. There are two narrow beds, one above the other, with clean white sheets and a warm blanket. There is a little folding table, a wardrobe with a mirror on the door, and a basin that folds out of the wall, with hot and cold running water.

[pause]

There are no windows in the cabins. But a little lamp glows by every pillow, and the walls are painted in soft colours, cream and pale green and grey.

[long-pause]

{img:passenger-unpacking-in-cabin}
The passengers open their doors and step inside, one after another.

[pause]

A woman puts her hat on the shelf and sits for a moment on the lower bed, trying the mattress. A man hangs up his overcoat and takes out a book from his bag. Somewhere along the corridor, a child laughs, and a steward answers in a low voice.

[pause]

Everything on board is made of the lightest materials. The beds are aluminium, and the walls are thin, and even the furniture has been designed to weigh as little as possible. But nothing looks cheap. Everything has been chosen with care, like the fittings in a good hotel.

[long-pause]

On the left side of the corridor is the dining room.

[pause]

It is a long, bright room with a row of tall windows that slope outwards at the bottom, so that the passengers can look down at the world as they eat. The tables are covered with white linen, and each one has a small vase with a flower in it. The chairs are made of aluminium, covered in soft cloth, and the china on the table is edged with a thin line of gold, and marked with the ship's name.

[pause]

On the walls, the designers have hung pictures of lands the airship has flown over, painted lightly in pale colours.

[long-pause]

{img:passenger-lounge-evening-light}
On the right side of the corridor is the lounge.

[pause]

It has soft chairs, small round tables, and curtains of pale silk. The windows are as big as those in the dining room, and in the evening, the light that comes through them is rosy and gold. In the first season, there was a little piano here, covered in pigskin, but now there is just a clear space, where people can sit and talk.

[pause]

Behind the lounge is a writing room, with desks and writing paper printed with the picture of the ship, where passengers can write letters and postcards. The mail is collected before they land.

[long-pause]

{img:promenade-windows-looking-down}
Along both sides of the ship, outside the dining room and the lounge, there is a long, narrow promenade.

[pause]

It is a kind of corridor with a sloping row of big windows, and some of them can be opened. The passengers can walk up and down, or sit on folding chairs, and watch the land go by below. In the evening the light slides along the walls, and the air from the open windows is cool and smells of fields.

[pause]

There is always someone standing here. Some people stay for an hour at a time, hardly speaking, just watching the world pass beneath them.

[long-pause]

Everywhere, the stewards move quietly about.

[pause]

They wear white jackets, and they carry trays with cups and glasses, and they know every passenger by name. They have been trained in some of the finest hotels in Germany, and they treat every guest as if he or she were the only one.

[pause]

They never hurry, but they are always there. If a passenger rings the little bell in his cabin, a steward arrives within a minute, and asks, in a soft voice, what he may bring.

[long-pause]

{img:evening-view-from-promenade-over-germany}
Now the ship has risen, and the passengers go to the windows, one by one.

[pause]

Below them, Germany is spread out in the evening: the green and brown squares of the fields, the winding silver line of the river, the small white villages, and the church spires casting long shadows. A train moves slowly along a track, trailing a little thread of steam, and from this height it looks like a toy.

[pause]

Most of the passengers have flown before, but there are always a few who have not, and they stand with their faces to the glass, and say nothing at all.

[long-pause]

{img:b-deck-stairs-down-from-lounge}
Below the lounge and the dining room is B deck, which is smaller, but just as interesting.

[pause]

You reach it by a narrow staircase from A deck, and on the way down, you can feel the faint, steady hum of the engines through the handrail. It is a little warmer here, and the air smells of coffee and fresh bread.

[pause]

Most of the long deck is taken up by the rooms that keep the ship running. But there are also three places that the passengers love.

[long-pause]

{img:smoking-room-airlock-door}
The first is the smoking room.

[pause]

It is the only place on the whole ship where a person may light a cigarette, and it is guarded in an interesting way. The air inside is kept at a slightly higher pressure than the air outside. This way, if any light gas were to leak, it would be pushed away from the door and could never get in.

[pause]

To go in, you have to pass through a small airlock with two doors, and a steward stands inside and keeps watch. In the middle of the room, there is a single electric lighter, fixed to the wall, and each smoker takes turns to use it.

[long-pause]

{img:smoking-room-bar-guests-talking}
Inside, the room is cosy. The chairs are covered in leather, the walls are pale, and there is a small bar in one corner where a steward pours wine and beer. A few men sit with newspapers and cigars, talking in low voices about business, politics and the weather.

[pause]

Through the round windows, the evening sky is deep gold. The sound of the engines is faint, like a distant river.

[pause]

There is a feeling of calm in the room, and a sort of friendly formality. Nobody raises his voice. And when the cigars are finished, the men stand, fold their papers, and make their way up the stairs.

[long-pause]

The second place is the kitchen.

[pause]

It is not a large room, but it is a marvel of its kind. Everything in it is run by electricity, with no flame anywhere, so the ovens and the hot plates are all electric. It was designed to be as light as possible, and everything is made of aluminium, polished until it shines.

[pause]

The chief cook and his helpers work in white jackets and tall hats, preparing three meals a day for more than a hundred people. They have a cold room for meat and fish, a bakery for bread, and a long counter where the plates are lined up.

[long-pause]

{img:cook-preparing-dishes-aluminium-kitchen}
The food is very good. The passengers eat soups and roasts and fresh fish, and they have fruit and cheese and cakes, and the cook takes great pride in each one.

[pause]

On the first evening of a crossing, there is often a rich soup first, then a fish from the Rhine, then a roast with vegetables, and finally a sweet dessert and coffee. The wines are German, from the Rhine and the Moselle valleys, and are poured into thin crystal glasses.

[pause]

Nothing is wasted, and nothing is missing. Even a hundred miles from land, the table might be set for a wedding.

[long-pause]

The third place is a surprise.

[pause]

It is a small shower, with tiled walls and a little curtain. There is no other airship in the world with a shower on board. It uses water carefully, since every drop must be carried, but for a few minutes a passenger can stand under warm water, high above the sea.

[pause]

People talk about it for weeks afterwards. It seems to them the most extraordinary thing of the whole flight. To wash in the sky, with nothing but a thin wall between you and the clouds.

[long-pause]

{img:crew-mess-room-tea}
Beside the passengers' rooms, there is also a mess room for the officers and the crew.

[pause]

Here the men sit at the end of a shift, with big mugs of tea and plates of bread and sausage. They talk about the weather, their families, and the wind that is expected over the sea. A radio operator brings in a slip of paper, reads it aloud, and goes away again.

[pause]

The men are friendly, and quiet, and used to working together. Many of them have flown on airships for ten or fifteen years, and some for even longer. They have a name for each of the little noises the ship makes, and they know them all.

[long-pause]

{img:laundry-and-store-rooms-b-deck}
Behind the mess room, a narrow door leads to the stores.

[pause]

Here are the crates of vegetables, the sacks of flour, the cases of wine, and the blocks of ice. Here, too, are the folded sheets and the clean towels, in tidy piles. Everything has a place, and everything is counted, because a ship that flies for days must carry all it needs.

[pause]

It is a place of orderly quiet, with the light turned low. In an hour or two, the stewards will come to lay the tables for dinner, and the whole ship will fill with the soft clink of plates.

[long-pause]

{img:control-car-captain-and-helmsmen}
In the front of the ship, beneath the nose, is the control car. This is where the airship is steered.

[pause]

It is a long, narrow room with big windows at the front, hung under the hull like a small ship's bridge. Along the wall are charts and clocks and maps, and at the front, two steersmen stand at two large wheels. One turns the rudder left and right. The other tilts the elevators, which make the nose go up and down.

[pause]

The steersmen do not talk much. Their eyes are on the compass and the clouds, and their hands move the wheels by tiny amounts.

[long-pause]

{img:captain-pruss-at-chart-table}
The commander tonight is Captain Max Pruss.

[pause]

He is a solid man in his middle forties, with a quiet manner. He first flew on airships many years ago, and he has commanded the Hindenburg on many of her flights. He has made her crossings to America and to Brazil, again and again, and he knows her as a rider knows his horse.

[pause]

Beside him stand two officers. One is Albert Sammt, a lean and thoughtful man who has trained for years under the old captains. The other is Anton Wittemann. They take turns on watch, four hours on, and four hours off, through every day and night of the flight.

[long-pause]

There is one more captain on board tonight.

[pause]

His name is Ernst Lehmann, and he is one of the most experienced airship men in the whole world. He flew zeppelins in the First World War, and he has since commanded the Graf Zeppelin and the first flights of the Hindenburg. Now he is a director of the company, and he travels as an observer, to see how things are going on the new route.

[pause]

He does not give orders. He sits quietly at the chart table, drinking coffee, and watching with interest as the younger men do their work. Everyone on board treats him with great respect.

[long-pause]

{img:navigator-with-sextant-and-maps}
In a small room behind the control car, the navigator works over his charts.

[pause]

There are no stars to see yet, and no shore to follow once the land is behind them. So he must work out where the ship is from the speed of the wind, the direction of the compass, and the readings of the clock. Every hour, he marks a small cross on the map, and the crosses make a line, which slowly walks across the ocean.

[pause]

On the journey, the navigator also receives weather reports from the radio room, so that he can steer around storms and find the strongest tailwinds, though tonight the wind will be against them.

[long-pause]

{img:radio-room-operators-at-work}
Behind the navigator's room is the radio room.

[pause]

Two or three operators sit in front of a bank of dials, with headphones over their ears. They tap messages in the dots and dashes of the Morse code, and they listen for the replies that come in from ships and stations across the sea.

[pause]

Every few hours, a report goes out to the ground: where the ship is, how fast she is flying, and whether all is well. The messages go into a ledger. Passengers can also send telegrams, and many of them do, to tell their families they are safe and happy.

[long-pause]

Now we go into the ship's backbone.

[pause]

Along the bottom of the hull runs a long, narrow walkway, called the keel. It is lit by small yellow lamps, and it stretches all the way from nose to tail, over two hundred metres. It is just wide enough for one person to walk, and a rope handrail runs along it. On both sides, far above the walker's head, the gas cells rise into the dark like pale grey clouds.

[pause]

The keel is the ship's main street. The crew use it to carry water, mail and tools, and in the evening, a few of the stewards walk along it with trays for the men at their posts.

[long-pause]

{img:engine-mechanic-in-gondola-wind}
Out at the sides, the engines are in their small gondolas, and they cannot be reached from inside the hull except by a ladder through the open air.

[pause]

Each gondola has a mechanic, who sits on a little seat among the pipes and dials, and listens to the engine with his whole body. The roar of it is great, and it shakes his seat, but the mechanics have learned to love it. When the machine is running well, they say, it sounds like a very large cat purring.

[pause]

They go out in turn, hour after hour, with a thermos of coffee and a rag in their back pocket.

[long-pause]

There are also men whose work is only to look after the ship's body. They are called riggers, and they climb up through the ship, high among the cells, and check that all the wires are tight, the fabric is sound and the valves are working. They carry a lamp, and a small tool bag, and they move about with great ease.

[pause]

One of them walks along a high catwalk with one hand on a girder. He stops now and then to look at a gas cell, and puts out his hand to touch the cloth, almost as one might stroke an animal.

[long-pause]

{img:cabin-boy-werner-franz-young}
And there are the youngest members of the crew, who are the cabin boys.

[pause]

One of them is a boy of fourteen named Werner Franz. He is one of the youngest people on board, and still new to the work. He is small and quick, with short fair hair and a bright face. His work is to carry coffee to the officers, and to fetch things from the stores, and to help the stewards in the dining room.

[pause]

Werner is very proud of his job. He wears a smart little uniform, with polished buttons, and he has been told to walk quickly but never run.

[pause]

He hurries along the corridor with a tray, smiling at a passenger who is coming out of her cabin.

[long-pause]

{img:rhine-valley-at-dusk-from-above}
As the evening deepens, the ship follows the river Rhine to the north.

[pause]

She is not flying high. The pilots keep her low, about as high as a tall church tower, so the passengers can see the towns and castles below. The Rhine winds beneath them like a ribbon of dull silver, with barges moving slowly down it, and little white lights coming on along the banks.

[pause]

On the hillsides are vineyards in neat rows, and old castles with round towers. In the villages, people stand at their windows. A man in a field takes off his hat and waves it, and the passengers wave back through the open windows of the promenade.

[long-pause]

At Cologne, the cathedral rises out of the evening haze, with its two tall spires, black against the sky.

[pause]

The ship passes close enough for the passengers to see the carvings on the towers. The streets are filling with lamps. Trams run along the river like bright beads on a string, and on the bridges, people stop to look up.

[pause]

The captain orders a gentle turn, so that all the passengers on both sides can see the city. Then the great shape of the cathedral slides slowly behind them, and the flat country of the Netherlands begins.

[long-pause]

{img:dutch-canals-and-windmills-evening}
The land below is very flat and very green. Long straight canals shine in the last light, with small boats on them, and white windmills turn slowly on the edges of the fields.

[pause]

Cows stand in the meadows in the golden light. A girl on a bicycle looks up and nearly falls off.

[pause]

The passengers watch for a long time, saying very little. It is the last of the green country they will see for a long while, and they do not want to miss a single field.

[long-pause]

Meanwhile, the stewards have laid the tables, and a soft bell calls the passengers to dinner.

[pause]

They come into the dining room in twos and threes, in dark suits and evening dresses. The room is warm and bright. The waiters bow and draw out the chairs, the glasses glint, and a little vase of spring flowers on each table nods with the gentle movement of the ship.

[pause]

At first, nobody talks much. Strangers sit side by side, looking at their menus, and then someone makes a remark about the view, and someone answers, and soon the whole room is full of low, friendly voices.

[long-pause]

{img:dinner-conversation-around-the-table}
They talk about the places they are going to and the places they have come from. A businessman tells a story about his first journey to America, by steamship, which took eight days and which he spent being seasick. An older woman describes the little village where she was born. A young engineer asks the others, politely, what they think of the new aeroplanes.

[pause]

Outside the windows, the sky is turning from gold to pink, and then to a deep blue.

[pause]

It is the kind of evening in which everything feels slow, and kind, and a long way from ordinary life.

[long-pause]

{img:lights-of-the-dutch-coast-at-night}
After dinner, the passengers walk out onto the promenade once more.

[pause]

The sun has gone down, and the land is a dark carpet, with a few scattered lights. At last the coast appears, as a pale line of surf, and beyond it lies the black shining sea. The ship begins to move out over the water, and the lights of the shore grow smaller and fewer.

[pause]

There is a short, hushed moment as the last of them disappears. Then one by one, the passengers turn from the windows, and go quietly back inside.

[long-pause]

In the lounge, a few people sit with cards or with coffee. Someone is writing a letter at a little desk. A couple play a quiet game of chess, with the board on their knees, while a steward moves among them with a tray of small glasses.

[pause]

In the smoking room, the evening's last cigars are being finished, and the steward is tidying the ashtrays.

[pause]

Gradually the voices grow fewer. The lamps are turned down, one by one, and a pleasant tiredness settles over everyone.

[long-pause]

{img:cabin-at-night-turned-down-bed}
The passengers go along the corridor to their cabins.

[pause]

The beds have been turned down by the stewards. The sheets are cool and crisp, the pillows are soft, and the small lamps shine on the walls. Outside the doors, a few pairs of shoes have been left in the corridor to be cleaned.

[pause]

One by one, the lights go out. And in the dark, the hum of the four engines grows into the whole sound of the night. It is a deep, steady note, like a slow breath, and it runs under everything.

[long-pause]

Outside, the ship slips on through the darkness.

[pause]

Her silver sides glow faintly under the stars, and a small light burns at the tip of each fin. Far below her lies the black water, with a ghostly line of white where the waves are breaking, and she moves on above it, quiet and almost weightless.

[pause]

On board, the watch officers stand in the control car, looking forward into the night. A steward pours coffee. The compass needle holds steady.

[pause]

And in the dark cabins, thirty-six passengers sleep, carried across the sea by a ship made of air.

[long-pause]

{img:sunrise-over-the-atlantic-from-window}
It is Tuesday, the fourth of May, and the sun is coming up over the Atlantic.

[pause]

The first light creeps along the promenade windows, soft and pink, and then turns to gold. The sea below is a wide grey-blue plain, with long, slow swells moving across it, and no land in sight in any direction. A few thin clouds lie below the ship, and the shadow of the airship slides over them, a long, dark shape that follows her all the way.

[pause]

An early passenger is already standing at the window, wrapped in a woollen coat, holding a cup of hot coffee in both hands.

[long-pause]

{img:breakfast-in-the-dining-room-morning}
Soon the dining room fills for breakfast.

[pause]

The stewards bring fresh rolls, butter, honey and jam, soft cheese, boiled eggs and thin slices of ham. There is hot coffee and tea, and a jug of cream. The windows are bright, and the table linen is white against the blue of the sea.

[pause]

Everyone sleeps well in an airship, the crew say, because there is no rocking, and the engines make a steady sound. Most of the passengers have woken refreshed, and they sit over their breakfast in no great hurry.

[long-pause]

Far below, there is a ship.

[pause]

It is a small dark steamer, like a toy in a bath, trailing a thin thread of smoke. The passengers crowd to the windows to see it. They can make out the tiny figures on its deck, who have stopped to look up at the great silver shape passing overhead.

[pause]

On the promenade, a few passengers wave. After a minute, a small white speck, which may be a handkerchief, waves back from the ship. Then the steamer slips away astern, and the sea is empty again.

[long-pause]

{img:passengers-reading-and-writing-letters}
The day passes in a gentle way.

[pause]

Some of the passengers sit on the promenade with books on their knees. Some write postcards and letters at the little desks in the writing room, which will be carried ashore at the end of the journey. A few of them just sit and look out, with their hands in their laps, and do nothing at all, which is a pleasure in itself.

[pause]

A man plays patience on a small table, laying down the cards with a soft snap. A woman knits a scarf in the lounge. The only sounds are the low drone of the engines and the occasional creak of the ship's frame.

[long-pause]

There are a few children on board, and they have found the ship a perfect playground.

[pause]

They run along the promenade, as quietly as they can, and press their noses to the slanting glass. They count the waves, and look for whales, and ask the stewards a thousand questions. The stewards answer every one with patience, and one of them shows a small boy how the windows are opened and how to feel the cold air coming in.

[pause]

Their mothers watch from the lounge and smile. It is a good place for a child, they say, because there is nowhere dangerous to fall.

[long-pause]

{img:small-group-touring-the-keel-walkway}
In the afternoon, an officer takes a small group of passengers on a tour of the inside of the ship.

[pause]

They climb a narrow ladder and step onto the keel walkway, and walk along it in single file, in the half-dark, with the huge gas cells rising on both sides like soft pale hills. The officer explains how the ship is built, and what each part does. The passengers listen with their heads tipped back. They have never been inside anything this big and this quiet.

[pause]

At the end of the walkway, they look out from a small hatch at the tail, and see the long wake of air behind them, and the sea stretching away.

[long-pause]

{img:afternoon-tea-with-cake-in-lounge}
At four o'clock, tea is served in the lounge.

[pause]

The stewards carry in silver pots, and plates of thin sandwiches and small cakes, and the passengers sit in the soft chairs, with the afternoon sun spilling across the carpet. Someone asks for a second cup, and the steward brings it with a bow.

[pause]

The talk is slow and pleasant. They speak about the weather in different countries, about their children, about books they have read. Two strangers discover that they were born in the same small town, forty miles apart, and they spend half an hour comparing names.

[long-pause]

Through the afternoon, a steward goes quietly from cabin to cabin with telegrams.

[pause]

Some are replies to messages the passengers sent from the ship: a message from a wife who says the children are well, a note from a business partner, a greeting from a friend who will be waiting at the airfield. Each message is handwritten on a thin sheet of paper, and the passengers read them in silence, then fold them away in their pockets.

[pause]

It is a strange thing, they say to each other, to receive a letter in the middle of the sky.

[long-pause]

{img:evening-sea-with-long-shadows-from-ship}
By evening the light changes. The sea turns bronze, then rose, and then a deep violet, and long shadows lie on the water.

[pause]

The ship's captain has been watching the weather with care. A strong wind from the west is blowing against them, and it is slowing the ship down. Each hour, they fall a little farther behind the timetable that was planned. It is nothing to worry about, but the officers speak about it in low voices.

[pause]

The passengers, who are not worried at all, go in to dinner, where the little table lamps are lit and the glasses are shining. And once again, the evening is warm and quiet, high above the cold dark sea.

[long-pause]

{img:stars-over-the-atlantic-from-promenade}
The second night is clear, and the sky is full of stars.

[pause]

A few of the passengers, who cannot sleep, put on their coats and go out to the promenade. They open one of the windows a little way and stand in the cool air, looking up. There is no moon yet. The stars are thick and sharp, and the Milky Way lies across the sky like a slow white river.

[pause]

Nobody speaks. They can hear only the engines, and the soft rush of air along the hull. Below them the sea is entirely dark, and it seems that the ship is floating among the stars.

[long-pause]

{img:night-watch-in-control-car}
In the control car, the night watch is on duty.

[pause]

The lights are low, so that the officers can see out into the darkness. One of them keeps his eyes on the compass, and another looks out at the clouds ahead. Once every few minutes, the steersman makes a small movement on the wheel and then comes back to centre.

[pause]

The men do not talk much, but when they do, they use the old words of the sea and sky. They speak of the swell, the bearing, the barometer. A steward comes with a pot of strong coffee and a plate of bread and sausage, and the officers eat standing up.

[long-pause]

{img:baker-at-work-before-dawn}
In the galley, long before the passengers wake, a baker is already at work.

[pause]

He has been up since three o'clock, rolling out dough under a hanging lamp, and shaping rolls and loaves with floury hands. The electric oven glows. The little room is warm and smells wonderful, and he hums to himself, a tune from home.

[pause]

When the first batch is ready, he lays the rolls on a tray to cool, and the warm smell drifts up through the ship, into the corridors, and under the doors of the sleeping cabins.

[long-pause]

Wednesday, the fifth of May, begins grey and soft.

[pause]

There is low cloud over the sea, and the sun is a pale circle behind it. Sometimes the ship dips into a thin bank of mist, and for a moment everything outside the windows is white. Then she emerges again, into a pearly light, and the sea is back, wide and slow.

[pause]

The wind from the west is still against them. The captain has taken the ship a little south to look for better air, but it is slow work, and the officers watch the clocks.

[long-pause]

{img:radio-weather-report-handed-to-captain}
In the radio room, a report arrives from the American coast.

[pause]

It says that the weather is unsettled near New York and New Jersey, with thunderstorms expected on Thursday. The operator copies it down and hands the paper to the captain, who reads it carefully, folds it and slips it into his pocket.

[pause]

He has landed in storms before, and he knows what to do. If the weather is bad at Lakehurst when they arrive, he will wait, and he will keep the ship in the air until it is safe to come down. There is no need to hurry. An airship can float for as long as she has fuel, and they have plenty.

[long-pause]

In the middle of the afternoon, a dark line appears on the horizon.

[pause]

It grows and takes shape, and it is the coast of Newfoundland, rough and rocky, with grey cliffs and pale patches of old snow. The passengers hurry to the windows. A few small fishing boats are working in the bays, and some tiny houses sit in the folds of the hills. A thread of smoke rises from one chimney.

[pause]

It is the first land that anyone has seen for more than a day. The passengers watch it as it slowly passes, with a feeling that is part relief and part surprise.

[long-pause]

{img:fishing-village-seen-from-above}
A little fishing village lies in a cove, with its boats drawn up on the shore and its wooden houses painted red and white.

[pause]

The people of the village have come out of their doors. They stand along the beach and on the hillside, looking up, and some of them wave. A boy runs along the shore beside the airship for a little way, as fast as he can, and then stops, out of breath, and watches her go.

[pause]

The passengers wave back, and call out, though nobody on the ground can hear them. A woman laughs, and wipes her eyes.

[long-pause]

{img:nova-scotia-evening-farmland-from-above}
In the evening, the ship follows the coast of Canada southwards.

[pause]

Nova Scotia lies below, with its dark spruce woods, its small lakes and its white farms. The sun goes down behind the hills, and the lights of the villages come on, one at a time. For a long while, the coastline runs beside the ship like a dark thread, with the sea gleaming on the other side.

[pause]

The last night of the crossing has begun. Tomorrow, if all goes well, they will arrive at Lakehurst. Many of the passengers go to bed early, to be fresh for the morning, and their cases are already packed.

[long-pause]

But a few remain in the lounge, in the light of the small lamps, to say goodbye to a journey that they have enjoyed.

[pause]

Two men who met at breakfast on Tuesday sit over a last glass of wine and exchange business cards. A young woman writes a few final lines to her mother. An elderly man stands at the window with his hand on the glass, as if he could feel the night air through it.

[pause]

It has been, they agree, a most peaceful voyage. Nobody wishes it to be over just yet.

[long-pause]

{img:thursday-morning-new-england-coast}
On Thursday morning, the sixth of May, the coast of New England comes slowly out of the haze.

[pause]

It is a long, low line of beaches and dunes, with small white lighthouses and dark pine woods behind them. The sea is pale green near the shore, and a few sailing boats are out, with their sails like scraps of paper. The passengers rise early and crowd to the windows.

[pause]

They have been in the air for nearly three days now, and this is the first time they have seen America.

[long-pause]

In the course of the morning, the ship passes over Boston.

[pause]

The old streets and church spires lie spread out beneath her, with the harbour full of ships, and the gold dome of the State House shining in the sun. People come out of the shops and the offices, and stand on the pavements. In the parks, children point.

[pause]

The Hindenburg turns in a wide, easy circle, and the passengers see the city from every side. A steward comes along the promenade with a tray of coffee, and says in his soft voice that the ship is about half a day behind her timetable, but that all is well.

[long-pause]

{img:last-breakfast-and-packing-cases}
In the cabins, the passengers are packing.

[pause]

They fold their clothes and tuck them into the suitcases, with the soaps and brushes from the little basins, and close the lids with a click. The beds are stripped. A woman smooths the last wrinkle from the blanket, and then stands in the cabin door for a moment, to look back.

[pause]

Down the corridor, the stewards are carrying the cases to the baggage room, ready to be taken ashore. They work quickly and quietly, as they have done on many other mornings.

[long-pause]

{img:last-lunch-in-dining-room}
At midday, lunch is served.

[pause]

The dining room is full, and the talk is cheerful. A man at one table has told his neighbours about the farm he is going to visit in Pennsylvania. At another, two children are drawing the airship on the back of their menu cards. The steward presents each of them with a small souvenir, a card with a picture of the ship, and asks them to sign it.

[pause]

It is a happy room, and everyone feels it. They have become a small community in three days, with friendly nods and shared jokes.

[long-pause]

Along the promenade, people are exchanging addresses.

[pause]

They write on the backs of cards and pieces of notepaper, in pencil, leaning against the sloping window. They promise to write, and to visit, and to meet again in a year. Some of them mean it, and some will not. But for now it feels very real.

[pause]

A man from Chicago shakes hands with a woman from Mexico City. A pair of friends laugh over something that has been funny since the first dinner. A little boy has made a paper aeroplane from a menu, and he asks a steward whether he may fly it out of the window, and the steward says, with great seriousness, that it is better to keep it for the ground.

[long-pause]

{img:captain-looking-at-weather-map}
In the control car, the mood is calm but attentive.

[pause]

The captain bends over the chart table with a pencil. The reports from the airfield at Lakehurst have been coming in all morning, and they speak of storms. There are heavy clouds to the west, and a line of thunder moving across New Jersey. If the ship arrived at the airfield now, she would have to meet the ground crew in the middle of rain and gusting wind.

[pause]

Captain Pruss has been in this position before. He does not seem worried. He reads the reports once more, and says that they will fly on to New York, and see what the sky does.

[long-pause]

Far off to the west, a line of dark clouds is building up.

[pause]

They are tall and flat-topped, with white edges in the sunlight, and from time to time, the passengers can see a small flicker at their base, which is lightning, miles away. Between the storms and the ship, the sky is clear and gently blue.

[pause]

The sea below is calm. The ship has plenty of fuel, and she can wait. She can fly wherever the pilots wish, in any direction, and she can keep flying till the weather clears.

[long-pause]

{img:long-island-sound-and-beaches-from-above}
They follow the coast south, over the long beaches of Cape Cod, and the sheltered waters of Long Island Sound.

[pause]

Summer houses stand on the dunes, with their shutters closed for the spring. A few boats pull at their moorings in the small harbours. On the shore, some people on a beach look up, and a man on a pier lifts his hat.

[pause]

Then, in the distance, a faint grey shape rises from the haze, small at first and then growing taller. It is New York.

[long-pause]

{img:new-york-skyline-from-the-harbour-afternoon}
In the afternoon, the Hindenburg arrives over New York.

[pause]

The city rises out of the haze like a forest of grey and silver towers, each one standing a little apart from the others, with the rivers shining on both sides. Ferries cross the harbour. Tugs push barges. Far down in the bay stands a small green figure with a raised torch, which is the Statue of Liberty.

[pause]

The ship passes over the water at a stately pace, and the passengers are silent for a while. They have seen pictures of it many times, but pictures are different from the real thing.

[long-pause]

She flies in over Manhattan, low enough to look across at the upper floors of the tallest buildings.

[pause]

The Empire State Building stands in the middle, with its narrow tower at the top, and the airship glides past it like a visitor in a slow parade. The windows of the offices are full of faces. People have run out onto the ledges and rooftops. In the streets below, traffic has slowed down, and the pavements are crowded with people leaning back to look up.

[pause]

For a few minutes, the whole city seems to be watching.

[long-pause]

{img:crowds-in-times-square-looking-up}
In Times Square, the crowds stand still in the afternoon light.

[pause]

Women in hats and men in dark suits tilt their heads back. A policeman pushes his cap to the back of his head. A newspaper seller forgets to call out his papers. Delivery boys, taxi drivers and shop girls all gather together to see the largest flying thing in the world slide quietly overhead.

[pause]

The sound of her engines is soft and deep, and it carries all the way down between the buildings, like the humming of a gigantic bee.

[long-pause]

On board, the passengers lean out of the open windows of the promenade.

[pause]

They can see people waving on the rooftops, and they wave back. Some of them hold up handkerchiefs or hats. A man takes photographs with a small folding camera, one after another, while his wife laughs and tells him to put it down and look.

[pause]

It is a moment that many will remember for the rest of their lives. The great grey city below, the warm spring sun, the sense of being, for a few minutes, at the centre of the world's attention.

[long-pause]

{img:airship-over-brooklyn-bridge-and-river}
She turns over the East River, and passes above the great bridges, which look like fine lace from here, with the water gleaming below.

[pause]

Brooklyn lies to the east, with its low rooftops and its steeples. Smoke rises from factory chimneys. Ships are tied up along the docks. On the shore, a group of children stand with their arms raised, as if they were trying to touch the airship.

[pause]

Then the ship turns slowly southward, away from the towers, and the buildings begin to grow smaller.

[long-pause]

{img:new-jersey-farmland-from-above}
Now there are fields below her again.

[pause]

New Jersey spreads out beyond the city, green and brown and flat, with small white towns and long straight roads. The first buds are opening on the trees. Smoke rises from farmhouses. The storm clouds hang low and dark in the west, a long way off, with the sun shining through a gap in them.

[pause]

The captain walks slowly through the ship and tells the passengers, one group at a time, that the landing must wait a little while.

[long-pause]

He says it simply and kindly. There are thunderstorms near Lakehurst, and the winds are changeable. It would not be safe to land just now, so he will take the ship out over the coast, where the air is calm, and wait for the weather to clear. It may take an hour or two.

[pause]

The passengers are not troubled. They have time, and they are comfortable. Some of them are even pleased, since it means a little longer in the air. A steward brings tea and little cakes to the lounge, and the afternoon goes on.

[pause]

Out of the windows, the long coast of New Jersey slides by, with its white beaches and its low, wet-looking dunes.

[long-pause]

{img:jersey-shore-beach-from-airship}
The ship turns east, and the long beaches of the New Jersey coast slide beneath her.

[pause]

The sand is pale and wide. Rows of wooden boardwalks and shuttered hotels line the shore, and a few strollers walk along the waterline in their spring coats. Little waves roll in, one after another, with a thin line of white foam. It is a peaceful sight, and the passengers settle by the windows to watch it.

[pause]

They fly slowly past the towns of the shore, over the great piers of Atlantic City, with the wooden rollercoasters and the ferris wheel standing still in the afternoon, and out over the open water once more.

[long-pause]

The afternoon passes gently.

[pause]

In the lounge, tea is poured again. A few of the passengers take out their cards, while others sit and talk, or doze quietly with their heads resting against the cushions. The light through the windows is warm and low.

[pause]

Now and again, a steward appears at the door and tells them that the radio has brought news from the ground. The storms are still moving over the airfield. The captain is waiting. No one is in a hurry.

[long-pause]

{img:captain-and-officers-discussing-landing}
In the control car, Captain Pruss talks with his officers.

[pause]

They have the reports from Lakehurst in front of them, and they speak quietly and carefully. Lightning is still seen to the west, but the line of storms is moving off, towards the sea. The wind on the ground is light. The rain is nearly over. If the clouds keep going, the field may be ready to receive them soon.

[pause]

Pruss thinks for a moment. He is not a man to rush. He says they will wait a little longer, and then they will go in.

[long-pause]

{img:clouds-clearing-after-the-storm-sunset}
Gradually, the dark clouds drift away.

[pause]

The sun comes out below them, low and golden, and the whole country lights up, wet and shining. The roofs of the farmhouses glitter, and the puddles in the fields are bright as mirrors. The sky in the east turns a pale and tender blue.

[pause]

At about six o'clock, a message comes in from the airfield. The weather is improving. The landing can begin. And aboard the ship, the order is given to turn.

[long-pause]

Slowly, the great silver body swings around, and the engines change their note.

[pause]

The ship leaves the coast and heads inland, across the pine woods and sandy tracks of southern New Jersey. Below her, the ground is dark and green with trees. Small lakes and cranberry bogs shine in the light, and here and there a farm road runs straight through the forest.

[pause]

The passengers are told to gather their belongings, and a steward goes from cabin to cabin to check that everyone is ready. They put on their hats and coats, and many of them walk out to the promenade once more, to watch.

[long-pause]

{img:lakehurst-naval-air-station-hangar-evening}
Down on the ground, at the Naval Air Station at Lakehurst, the evening is waiting.

[pause]

The airfield is a wide flat expanse of sand and grass, surrounded by pine trees, with a single enormous hangar at one end. It is so big that clouds have been known to form inside its roof. The hangar's great doors stand open, and a patch of golden light lies across the grass.

[pause]

The air smells of wet earth and pine needles after the rain. Birds are singing in the trees.

[long-pause]

{img:ground-crew-of-navy-sailors-ready}
Out on the grass, the ground crew is ready.

[pause]

There are nearly a hundred men, most of them sailors of the United States Navy, in dark blue working clothes, with some local men beside them. They stand in lines, waiting, with their hands in their pockets. They have done this many times, and they know the work. They will catch the ropes, steady the ship, and walk her slowly in to the mooring mast.

[pause]

Their officer, Commander Charles Rosendahl, stands at the edge of the field in his uniform, looking up at the sky. He has been watching for the airship all afternoon.

[long-pause]

{img:families-waiting-behind-the-fence}
Behind a fence at the side of the field, a crowd of friends and relatives has gathered.

[pause]

They have been there since the morning. Some of them have come from New York and Philadelphia, by train and by car, and they are dressed in their best, with flowers and umbrellas. There are mothers and fathers, sisters, brothers, and a few small children. They talk quietly, and look at the sky, and every few minutes someone says that they can see her.

[pause]

A newspaper reporter is writing in a notebook. A radio announcer, who has come to describe the landing for a station in Chicago, tests his microphone and looks at the clouds, and a photographer sets up a heavy camera on a tripod.

[long-pause]

{img:airship-approaching-lakehurst-low-golden-light}
At last she appears.

[pause]

A small silver shape grows above the trees, glowing in the evening sun, and slowly it becomes larger. A murmur runs along the fence. People lift their hands to shade their eyes, and the children point and cry out. There is no mistaking her. She comes in steadily, high and calm, with her nose pointing towards the hangar.

[pause]

For a long time she seems hardly to move, as if she were hanging motionless in the air. Then she begins to turn.

[long-pause]

{img:ship-making-slow-turn-above-the-field}
She makes a wide turn over the field and circles, with her engines humming softly, as the captain looks the ground over once more.

[pause]

The ground crew shift their feet. The reporter has stopped writing. The sun is low behind the trees, and the silver sides of the ship glow warmly in the clear air. A pale line of mist is rising from the grass at the edge of the field.

[pause]

Then the engines change their note, and she begins her final approach.

[long-pause]

{img:ship-slowing-above-the-field-evening-light}
The time is a little after seven o'clock. The wind has dropped, and the evening is warm and still.

[pause]

The Hindenburg comes in slowly, with her nose raised, and her engines turning gently. The captain is watching the ground. The officers call out the height in a low voice, and the steersmen make their small, careful corrections. Water is let out from the tanks, to make the ship lighter, and it falls in a bright rain onto the grass.

[pause]

On the promenade, the passengers stand at the open windows, looking down at the people waving, a few hundred feet below.

[long-pause]

{img:landing-lines-falling-to-the-ground-crew}
At twenty minutes past seven, the landing ropes are let down.

[pause]

They fall, long and heavy, over the field, and the ground crew run forward to catch them. The sailors take hold of the ropes, and begin to steady the ship. The great silver shape hangs just over their heads, perfectly calm. A man on the ground raises his hand, and calls up to the crew in German.

[pause]

In the lounge, a steward has come to say that they will be on the ground in only a few minutes. A woman puts on her hat.

[long-pause]

{img:evening-sky-over-the-lakehurst-field}
And then something changes at the back of the ship.

[pause]

It is quick, and quiet, and at first it is hard to say what it is. Some of the people on the field see a faint glow, high up near the tail. Others notice nothing at all, and look the other way. Then there is a sudden, soft sound, like a great sigh of wind.

[pause]

In a few seconds, everyone understands what is happening. The gas inside the ship has caught fire. The tail begins to sink, and the ship starts to settle towards the ground.

[long-pause]

{img:ground-crew-hurrying-across-the-grass}
It takes only about half a minute.

[pause]

In that short time, the men on the field do what they can. The sailors do not run away. They hurry towards the ship, and some of them hold out their arms. The passengers and crew, as the ship comes down, step or jump from the windows and doorways close to the ground, and many of them are helped away across the grass by the men who rush forward to meet them.

[pause]

It is a time of great confusion and great kindness. Strangers help strangers. A sailor takes a child by the hand. Someone calls out a name, and someone answers.

[long-pause]

{img:quiet-field-with-hangar-at-dusk}
Then it is over, and the field is very quiet.

[pause]

Of the ninety-seven people on board, sixty-two came safely through. Thirty-five did not, and one man of the ground crew also lost his life. They were passengers and crew members, and they had been going home, or going to work, or looking forward to an ordinary evening.

[pause]

The people in the crowd stand silent along the fence. Some of them are weeping. A voice from the radio announcer, who has been describing the scene, trembles and falls silent, and then goes on, with difficulty.

[long-pause]

{img:navy-medical-staff-with-stretchers-in-twilight}
Soon the doctors and the nurses arrive from the naval hospital and from nearby towns.

[pause]

Ambulances and cars come along the sandy road, and the injured are carried gently away, and the Navy men put up lamps on the field. The people from the airship are taken to hospitals in Lakehurst and in the towns around. Doctors and nurses work through the whole night, quietly and kindly.

[pause]

Among those who walked away from the field was a boy of fourteen. He was the cabin boy, Werner Franz, who was near the ship's kitchen when the first sound came. A tank of water broke open over his head, and wetted him through, and when he saw a gap in the side, he ran out through it and onto the grass. He was not hurt.

[long-pause]

{img:lamps-on-the-field-at-night}
The night comes down slowly.

[pause]

The sky turns dark blue, and the first stars appear above the pines. Lamps burn along the edge of the field. The Navy men stay at their posts, and the officers speak in low voices. A cold breeze blows over the grass, smelling of wet earth and smoke.

[pause]

Far away, in homes across America and Germany, the telephones begin to ring, and the news goes out over the wires and over the radio. By morning, the whole world knows.

[long-pause]

{img:newspaper-presses-spring-1937}
In the morning, the story is on the front page of every newspaper in the world.

[pause]

The presses have been running all night. Boys on street corners call out the headlines, and men in hats stand in line to buy a copy. In cafés and kitchens, people read the news aloud. Some of them cry, and some of them sit in silence, with the paper folded on their knees.

[pause]

In Germany, a day of mourning is declared. In America, flags are lowered. Letters and telegrams of sympathy arrive from many countries, from people who had never seen an airship.

[long-pause]

{img:radio-recording-played-in-living-room}
The radio announcer's voice is played again and again.

[pause]

He had been recording the landing for a Chicago station, and he went on speaking through what he saw, with a voice that shook, and many people heard the recording in their living rooms the next day. A film camera had also been running on the field, and the pictures were shown in cinemas across the country, to audiences who sat very still.

[pause]

It was one of the first times that the world had heard and seen a great event so close to the moment it happened. Many people who were there said afterwards that it was the sound that stayed with them the longest.

[long-pause]

In the days after, investigators arrived in Lakehurst from Washington and from Germany.

[pause]

Hugo Eckener himself came, and he stood with them on the grass at the place where the ship had settled. He was quiet, and grave. For weeks, commissions of inquiry met in America and in Germany, and heard the surviving officers, riggers and sailors tell what they had seen.

[pause]

They measured the ground, and studied the photographs, and looked at the film again and again, a frame at a time.

[long-pause]

{img:wooden-office-with-papers-and-maps-inquiry}
The question that everyone asked was: what had happened?

[pause]

The inquiry never found one single answer that everybody agreed on. The most widely accepted explanation is that a small leak of hydrogen had gathered near the tail, and that the build-up of static electricity on the ship's skin, after her flight through the stormy air, produced a spark. Some thought there had been a fault in a wire or a gas valve. Others have suggested that the paint on the skin made the fire spread faster.

[pause]

Many people have studied it since, in laboratories and with computers, and they still do not entirely agree. What is certain is that it was not anyone's wish, and that the crew did everything they could.

[long-pause]

Eckener said that the cause lay in the gas.

[pause]

If the ship had been filled with helium, he said, nothing could have happened. He had always hoped to fly with helium, and it was not made available. Looking back, many people agree with him. The hydrogen was a risk that the designers had known about, and had tried to manage with great care, but the care had not been enough on that evening.

[pause]

He lived until 1954, and he never stopped believing that airships were safe and useful in the right conditions. But he was a realist. He knew that the public trust, built up over thirty years, had been broken in half a minute.

[long-pause]

{img:flowers-and-letters-on-a-table-spring-1937}
In the weeks that followed, a great many letters were written.

[pause]

Families sent flowers to the hospitals. Sailors of the Navy wrote to the relatives of those they had helped. People who had flown on earlier voyages wrote to the company, to say how comfortable they had been, and how much they had enjoyed the journey.

[pause]

Those who had died were brought home with respect, and they were laid to rest by their families, in Germany and in America and in other lands. In many towns, the church bells rang for them, and people stood quietly in the streets.

[long-pause]

{img:graf-zeppelin-resting-in-hangar-1937}
After that evening, the era of the great passenger airships came quietly to an end.

[pause]

The Graf Zeppelin, the old and faithful ship, made her last flights in the weeks that followed, and was then retired and tied up in her hangar at Frankfurt. A sister ship of the Hindenburg was begun, but she was never used for passengers. In 1940, the two old airships were taken apart and their metal was put to other uses.

[pause]

The skies were soon taken over by aeroplanes, which grew bigger and faster and safer every year. Within a few years, people were crossing the Atlantic in a single day, instead of in two and a half.

[long-pause]

But for the few years that they flew, the airships had been something unique.

[pause]

Nothing since has quite matched them. A modern aeroplane takes you across the sea in a sealed tube, with your seat belt on, high above the clouds, with a small window. The Hindenburg took you across at the height of a church tower, with a bed and a table and a window you could open, and let you feel the sea air on your face.

[pause]

Those who flew on her never forgot it. They spoke of the quiet, of the wide windows, of the slow, golden light, and of the feeling that the ship was not in a hurry.

[long-pause]

{img:captain-pruss-older-in-quiet-garden}
The people who were on board went on with their lives.

[pause]

Captain Pruss was badly hurt, but he recovered, and he lived for many years more, in Germany. Many of the passengers who came through returned to their homes, and picked up their old jobs, and raised their children. Some of them kept a hat, or a suitcase, or a postcard from the journey.

[pause]

The cabin boy, Werner Franz, grew up, and had a family, and he never forgot the ship. He lived until 2014, when he died at the age of ninety-two, the last of the crew. In his later years he told his story to many people, so that it would not be lost.

[long-pause]

Today, at Lakehurst, a simple memorial marks the place.

[pause]

In the grass, near the edge of the old field, a simple brass marker is set in the ground at the place where the ship came down, among low trees. In the early morning, the light is soft and gold, and the only sound is the wind in the pines.

[pause]

Visitors who come here walk slowly. Some of them leave a flower. Others stand silent for a while, then turn and walk back to the car, and drive away along the quiet sandy road.

[long-pause]

{img:giant-hangar-no-one-at-lakehurst-today}
Not far away, the old hangar is still standing.

[pause]

It is called Hangar One, and it was built in 1921, long before the Hindenburg, to shelter the first great airships of the American Navy. It is still one of the largest buildings of its kind in the world, with a curving steel roof and enormous doors, and it is now a national landmark.

[pause]

A great stillness fills it. Light falls in long bars from the high windows, and the dust floats in the air as slowly as a small airship would.

[long-pause]

{img:zeppelin-museum-friedrichshafen-harbour-today}
If you travel to Friedrichshafen today, you can still walk along the shore of Lake Constance, where it all began.

[pause]

The lake is as blue as it ever was, with small white ferries crossing it, and the Alps standing pale on the far side. Beside the harbour, a long, low building made of glass and stone is home to the Zeppelin Museum, which tells the story of the airships from the old count's first drawings to the last flights.

[pause]

On a sunny morning, children come in with their teachers, and walk quietly up the steps.

[long-pause]

Inside, there is a part of the Hindenburg that you can walk through.

[pause]

It is a full-size copy of a section of the ship's passenger area, built from the old plans, with a piece of the metal frame, a row of cabins, and a stretch of the long promenade with its sloping windows. Visitors can step into a little cabin, sit on a narrow bed and look at the fold-away basin. They can stand at the windows and imagine the sea moving below.

[pause]

They are often surprised by how small and how gentle everything is. The rooms feel like those of a quiet little hotel.

[long-pause]

{img:old-photographs-and-objects-in-museum-case}
In the glass cases nearby are the small things that remain.

[pause]

There are menus printed with the ship's name, and silver spoons, and cups with a thin line of gold. There are postcards that were written on board, and old tickets, and luggage labels. Each one is labelled in a neat hand.

[pause]

Near the end of the exhibition, a long wall of photographs shows the faces of the crew and the passengers, smiling, in their best clothes, on the grass in front of the ship.

[long-pause]

And above the lake, there is something even more remarkable.

[pause]

On a summer day, you can sometimes see a small silver airship drifting slowly over the water, its engines humming softly. She is called a Zeppelin NT, and she was built in Friedrichshafen, in the same place, by a company that has kept the old name.

[pause]

She is much smaller than the Hindenburg, only a dozen passengers, and she is filled with helium, which cannot burn. She flies slowly over the vineyards and the rooftops of the town, and people look up from their gardens and smile, just as they did ninety years ago.

[long-pause]

{img:frankfurt-airport-forest-edge-today}
In Frankfurt, the airfield where the ship once rose has become one of the busiest airports in Europe.

[pause]

Aeroplanes take off there by the hundred, and the old fields have been covered with runways and terminals. But a little way from the noise, among the pines, there are still quiet paths where you can walk. The airships once rose from this ground, long ago, on warm spring evenings.

[long-pause]

{img:empire-state-building-mooring-mast-detail}
In New York, at the top of the Empire State Building, a thin mast still points to the sky.

[pause]

In the end it never worked well, because the winds at that height are strong and gusty. There is a particular quiet in the stories of those who flew on the airships.

They did not remember the speed, or the noise, or the danger. They remembered the slowness. They remembered how a coastline would rise out of the haze and take an hour to pass, how a church tower would stay in the window long enough for them to read the clock on its face, and how a river would wind away beneath them like a thread of silver.

They remembered the colour of the sea at evening, and the way the clouds seemed to lie below them like a soft white floor.

When we think of the Hindenburg, it is perhaps right to remember her this way.

Not only for the last half-minute of her life, but for all the many days that she flew without trouble. For the people who looked up and waved.
