Tonight we are going almost ninety years back, to a wide green airfield on the edge of Frankfurt, in Germany, where an enormous silver airship is floating just above the grass.

--

It is the evening of the third of May, 1937, and the air is mild and still.

The airship is called the Hindenburg. She is the largest thing that has ever flown, longer than two football pitches laid end to end, and she hangs there in the soft light as if she were made of nothing at all.

If you enjoy quiet stories like this one, you are welcome to subscribe to the channel. And now, let us begin.

--

The airfield is called Rhein-Main, and it lies among pine woods and fields, a few miles south of the city. Beyond the trees, the roofs of Frankfurt glow a little in the late sun, and somewhere far off a church clock is striking.

On the grass, small groups of people are walking towards the ship. Men in dark overcoats and hats. Women in long coats with fur collars, holding their gloves. A porter pushing a cart with leather suitcases, each one tied with a strap.

Children walk between their parents, looking up. Most of them have never seen anything so large, and they do not quite believe it.

--

Around the ship stand the men of the ground crew, a hundred or more of them, in grey work clothes and flat caps. They hold the long mooring ropes in their hands, and they stand quite still, as they have been taught to do.

The airship rests a few metres above the ground, held gently in place. When the wind moves, she turns a little, very slowly, like a great silver fish in a calm pond.

From where the passengers stand, it is hard to see where she ends. The nose is far away to one side, and the tail is far away on the other. The huge fins at the back rise higher than a house.

--

{img:silver-hull-close-detail}
Close up, the skin of the ship is not metal at all. It is cotton cloth, stretched tight over a framework and painted with a silver coating, and in the evening light it has a soft, pearly shine.

Along the side, in tall black letters, is a single word: Hindenburg. The letters are so big that a person standing underneath can read only part of one at a time.

Below the middle of the ship, there is a small, neat gondola, hardly larger than a railway carriage, and it looks very tiny under all that silver. That is where the passengers will walk in.

--

Beside the field there is a low white building with a wide veranda, where the passengers have been waiting since the afternoon. Inside, the staff of the airline are checking tickets and weighing luggage, and writing everything down in tall green books.

A clerk calls out a name. A woman in a grey travelling coat stands up and smiles, and goes to the desk.

Nobody is in a hurry. The airship will not leave until the evening, and no one here has ever been late for an airship. It would not wait for them, but it has never needed to.

--

This is how people travelled across the Atlantic Ocean in the spring of 1937. Not on a loud, small aeroplane that could only cross the sea with great difficulty, but on a floating hotel, high above the waves, with beds and a dining room and a quiet lounge.

It takes about two and a half days to reach America. The ship will fly west over the sea, past Ireland, over Newfoundland, down the coast of Canada and New England, and over the city of New York, until it comes to a small, flat place in New Jersey called Lakehurst.

The passengers on the ground tonight will be there on Thursday morning, if all goes well.

--

But before we go on board, let us step back a little, and see how this ship came to be here.

Because the Hindenburg did not appear from nowhere. She was the work of many years and many hands, in a small town on the shore of a lake, a few hundred miles to the south. And in that town, there is a story that begins long before her first flight, with an old count who had one very stubborn idea.

Let us go there first, and then we will come back to this field in Frankfurt, in time for the evening.

But keep one thing in mind as we go. The people walking across the grass tonight are about to make a journey that the whole world will remember. Not one of them knows it yet.
