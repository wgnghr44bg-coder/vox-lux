There is a particular quiet in the stories of those who flew on the airships.

They did not remember the speed, or the noise, or the danger. They remembered the slowness. They remembered how a coastline would rise out of the haze and take an hour to pass, how a church tower would stay in the window long enough for them to read the clock on its face, and how a river would wind away beneath them like a thread of silver.

They remembered the colour of the sea at evening, and the way the clouds seemed to lie below them like a soft white floor.

--

{img:passenger-with-cup-at-promenade-window-sunset}
They remembered the small moments, too.

A cup of coffee on the promenade, with the steam rising in the cool air. A friendly word with a steward. A child asleep in his mother's arms in the lounge, with a blanket over his knees. The click of the cards, and the low talk at the tables. The moment when the lights came on in the dining room and the whole ship seemed to glow, like a lantern floating over the dark water.

It was a kind of travelling that has almost vanished, a journey that was also a place to be.

--

And the people who worked on board remembered it too.

The stewards talked afterwards about the sound of the engines, which they heard in their sleep for years. The cooks remembered the smell of fresh bread in the galley at dawn. The riggers remembered the high, soft light among the gas cells, and the way a ship that large could be so gentle.

Those who worked on her spoke of her with a simple, quiet pride, the way a person speaks about a house where he grew up.

--

{img:old-photograph-album-open-on-table}
In many homes in Germany and America, there are still old photographs of the ship.

They are small, grey and white, with curled corners. In one, a group of passengers stands on the grass in front of the great silver hull, squinting a little in the sun. In another, a child is pressing her face to a window. In another, a young steward in a white jacket holds a tray and smiles at the camera.

The people in these pictures look at us across the years, calm and a little shy, and they are always about to step on board.

--

{img:hindenburg-distant-silver-in-evening-sky}
When we think of the Hindenburg, it is perhaps right to remember her this way.

Not only for the last half-minute of her life, but for all the many days that she flew without trouble. For the people who looked up and waved. For the nights she carried her passengers over the sea, softly and without hurry, while they slept. For the thoughtful men and women who built her, flew her, and looked after her.

She was, for a short time, the most graceful machine in the world.
