In the morning, the story is on the front page of every newspaper in the world.

The presses have been running all night. Boys on street corners call out the headlines, and men in hats stand in line to buy a copy. In cafés and kitchens, people read the news aloud. Some of them cry, and some of them sit in silence, with the paper folded on their knees.

In Germany, a day of mourning is declared. In America, flags are lowered. Letters and telegrams of sympathy arrive from many countries, from people who had never seen an airship.

--

{img:radio-recording-played-in-living-room}
The radio announcer's voice is played again and again.

He had been recording the landing for a Chicago station, and he went on speaking through what he saw, with a voice that shook, and many people heard the recording in their living rooms the next day. A film camera had also been running on the field, and the pictures were shown in cinemas across the country, to audiences who sat very still.

It was one of the first times that the world had heard and seen a great event so close to the moment it happened. Many people who were there said afterwards that it was the sound that stayed with them the longest.

--

In the days after, investigators arrived in Lakehurst from Washington and from Germany.

Hugo Eckener himself came, and he stood with them on the grass at the place where the ship had settled. He was quiet, and grave. For weeks, commissions of inquiry met in America and in Germany, and heard the surviving officers, riggers and sailors tell what they had seen.

They measured the ground, and studied the photographs, and looked at the film again and again, a frame at a time.

--

{img:wooden-office-with-papers-and-maps-inquiry}
The question that everyone asked was: what had happened?

The inquiry never found one single answer that everybody agreed on. The most widely accepted explanation is that a small leak of hydrogen had gathered near the tail, and that the build-up of static electricity on the ship's skin, after her flight through the stormy air, produced a spark. Some thought there had been a fault in a wire or a gas valve. Others have suggested that the paint on the skin made the fire spread faster.

Many people have studied it since, in laboratories and with computers, and they still do not entirely agree. What is certain is that it was not anyone's wish, and that the crew did everything they could.

--

Eckener said that the cause lay in the gas.

If the ship had been filled with helium, he said, nothing could have happened. He had always hoped to fly with helium, and it was not made available. Looking back, many people agree with him. The hydrogen was a risk that the designers had known about, and had tried to manage with great care, but the care had not been enough on that evening.

He lived until 1954, and he never stopped believing that airships were safe and useful in the right conditions. But he was a realist. He knew that the public trust, built up over thirty years, had been broken in half a minute.

--

{img:flowers-and-letters-on-a-table-spring-1937}
In the weeks that followed, a great many letters were written.

Families sent flowers to the hospitals. Sailors of the Navy wrote to the relatives of those they had helped. People who had flown on earlier voyages wrote to the company, to say how comfortable they had been, and how much they had enjoyed the journey.

Those who had died were brought home with respect, and they were laid to rest by their families, in Germany and in America and in other lands. In many towns, the church bells rang for them, and people stood quietly in the streets.
