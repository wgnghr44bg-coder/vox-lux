After that evening, the era of the great passenger airships came quietly to an end.

The Graf Zeppelin, the old and faithful ship, made her last flights in the weeks that followed, and was then retired and tied up in her hangar at Frankfurt. A sister ship of the Hindenburg was begun, but she was never used for passengers. In 1940, the two old airships were taken apart and their metal was put to other uses.

The skies were soon taken over by aeroplanes, which grew bigger and faster and safer every year. Within a few years, people were crossing the Atlantic in a single day, instead of in two and a half.

--

But for the few years that they flew, the airships had been something unique.

Nothing since has quite matched them. A modern aeroplane takes you across the sea in a sealed tube, with your seat belt on, high above the clouds, with a small window. The Hindenburg took you across at the height of a church tower, with a bed and a table and a window you could open, and let you feel the sea air on your face.

Those who flew on her never forgot it. They spoke of the quiet, of the wide windows, of the slow, golden light, and of the feeling that the ship was not in a hurry.

--

{img:captain-pruss-older-in-quiet-garden}
The people who were on board went on with their lives.

Captain Pruss was badly hurt, but he recovered, and he lived for many years more, in Germany. Many of the passengers who came through returned to their homes, and picked up their old jobs, and raised their children. Some of them kept a hat, or a suitcase, or a postcard from the journey.

The cabin boy, Werner Franz, grew up, and had a family, and he never forgot the ship. He lived until 2014, when he died at the age of ninety-two, the last of the crew. In his later years he told his story to many people, so that it would not be lost.

--

Today, at Lakehurst, a simple memorial marks the place.

In the grass, near the edge of the old field, a simple brass marker is set in the ground at the place where the ship came down, among low trees. In the early morning, the light is soft and gold, and the only sound is the wind in the pines.

Visitors who come here walk slowly. Some of them leave a flower. Others stand silent for a while, then turn and walk back to the car, and drive away along the quiet sandy road.

--

{img:giant-hangar-no-one-at-lakehurst-today}
Not far away, the old hangar is still standing.

It is called Hangar One, and it was built in 1921, long before the Hindenburg, to shelter the first great airships of the American Navy. It is still one of the largest buildings of its kind in the world, with a curving steel roof and enormous doors, and it is now a national landmark.

A great stillness fills it. Light falls in long bars from the high windows, and the dust floats in the air as slowly as a small airship would.
