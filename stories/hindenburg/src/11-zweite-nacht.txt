The second night is clear, and the sky is full of stars.

A few of the passengers, who cannot sleep, put on their coats and go out to the promenade. They open one of the windows a little way and stand in the cool air, looking up. There is no moon yet. The stars are thick and sharp, and the Milky Way lies across the sky like a slow white river.

Nobody speaks. They can hear only the engines, and the soft rush of air along the hull. Below them the sea is entirely dark, and it seems that the ship is floating among the stars.

--

{img:night-watch-in-control-car}
In the control car, the night watch is on duty.

The lights are low, so that the officers can see out into the darkness. One of them keeps his eyes on the compass, and another looks out at the clouds ahead. Once every few minutes, the steersman makes a small movement on the wheel and then comes back to centre.

The men do not talk much, but when they do, they use the old words of the sea and sky. They speak of the swell, the bearing, the barometer. A steward comes with a pot of strong coffee and a plate of bread and sausage, and the officers eat standing up.

--

{img:baker-at-work-before-dawn}
In the galley, long before the passengers wake, a baker is already at work.

He has been up since three o'clock, rolling out dough under a hanging lamp, and shaping rolls and loaves with floury hands. The electric oven glows. The little room is warm and smells wonderful, and he hums to himself, a tune from home.

When the first batch is ready, he lays the rolls on a tray to cool, and the warm smell drifts up through the ship, into the corridors, and under the doors of the sleeping cabins.

--

Wednesday, the fifth of May, begins grey and soft.

There is low cloud over the sea, and the sun is a pale circle behind it. Sometimes the ship dips into a thin bank of mist, and for a moment everything outside the windows is white. Then she emerges again, into a pearly light, and the sea is back, wide and slow.

The wind from the west is still against them. The captain has taken the ship a little south to look for better air, but it is slow work, and the officers watch the clocks.

--

{img:radio-weather-report-handed-to-captain}
In the radio room, a report arrives from the American coast.

It says that the weather is unsettled near New York and New Jersey, with thunderstorms expected on Thursday. The operator copies it down and hands the paper to the captain, who reads it carefully, folds it and slips it into his pocket.

He has landed in storms before, and he knows what to do. If the weather is bad at Lakehurst when they arrive, he will wait, and he will keep the ship in the air until it is safe to come down. There is no need to hurry. An airship can float for as long as she has fuel, and they have plenty.

--

In the middle of the afternoon, a dark line appears on the horizon.

It grows and takes shape, and it is the coast of Newfoundland, rough and rocky, with grey cliffs and pale patches of old snow. The passengers hurry to the windows. A few small fishing boats are working in the bays, and some tiny houses sit in the folds of the hills. A thread of smoke rises from one chimney.

It is the first land that anyone has seen for more than a day. The passengers watch it as it slowly passes, with a feeling that is part relief and part surprise.

--

{img:fishing-village-seen-from-above}
A little fishing village lies in a cove, with its boats drawn up on the shore and its wooden houses painted red and white.

The people of the village have come out of their doors. They stand along the beach and on the hillside, looking up, and some of them wave. A boy runs along the shore beside the airship for a little way, as fast as he can, and then stops, out of breath, and watches her go.

The passengers wave back, and call out, though nobody on the ground can hear them. A woman laughs, and wipes her eyes.

--

{img:nova-scotia-evening-farmland-from-above}
In the evening, the ship follows the coast of Canada southwards.

Nova Scotia lies below, with its dark spruce woods, its small lakes and its white farms. The sun goes down behind the hills, and the lights of the villages come on, one at a time. For a long while, the coastline runs beside the ship like a dark thread, with the sea gleaming on the other side.

The last night of the crossing has begun. Tomorrow, if all goes well, they will arrive at Lakehurst. Many of the passengers go to bed early, to be fresh for the morning, and their cases are already packed.

--

But a few remain in the lounge, in the light of the small lamps, to say goodbye to a journey that they have enjoyed.

Two men who met at breakfast on Tuesday sit over a last glass of wine and exchange business cards. A young woman writes a few final lines to her mother. An elderly man stands at the window with his hand on the glass, as if he could feel the night air through it.

It has been, they agree, a most peaceful voyage. Nobody wishes it to be over just yet.
