Tonight we travel about seven hundred years back in time. We go to a city built on water, in the north of Italy. The year is 1300. And the city is called Venice.

[long-pause]

It is early morning, and a soft mist lies over the water. The sky in the east is pale pink and gold. Out of the mist rise church towers and rooftops, as if they were floating on the sea. There are no roads here, only canals of green water winding between the houses. Small boats glide along them, pushed by men standing at the back with long oars.

[pause]

A bell rings from a tall brick tower, and then another bell answers. Seagulls cry over the water. A fisherman rows slowly home with a basket full of shining fish. A woman opens a wooden shutter and hangs a sheet out to air. On a small bridge of stone, a cat sits washing its face in the first sunlight. And everywhere, there is the soft sound of water lapping against old walls.

[pause]

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

{img:venice-grand-canal-1300-merchant-houses-boats-morning-light}
Venice in 1300 is one of the richest cities in the whole world. Its ships sail all over the Mediterranean Sea and beyond. They bring back spices and silk from the East, and they carry wool and glass and silver. Merchants from many lands come to Venice to buy and sell. The city is full of churches and palaces, markets and workshops. And it is all built on a group of small islands in a shallow lagoon.

[pause]

Tonight we will spend some time in this city on the water. We will meet a family of glassmakers who live on a little island called Murano. We will watch them make glass in their glowing furnace. We will go with the mother to the great market at Rialto. We will ride in the boats along the canals, and visit the golden church of San Marco. We will see the great shipyard, where ships are built faster than anywhere else in the world.

[pause]

{img:murano-island-1300-glass-furnace-chimneys-smoke-lagoon-morning}
We will also meet a famous traveller called Marco Polo, who has just come home from China. We will watch the great festival where the ruler of Venice marries the sea. And we will wait with the family through a long winter, for a ship that is late to come home.

[long-pause]

So let us begin on the quiet lagoon, on a spring morning in the year 1300. The mist is slowly lifting from the water. The sun is warming the old brick walls. And from the little island of Murano, thin lines of smoke rise into the sky from the glass furnaces.

[pause]

{img:small-boat-rowing-across-lagoon-murano-to-venice-dawn-family}
A small wooden boat is crossing the lagoon from Murano toward the city. A man is rowing it, standing at the back, with slow and steady strokes. A woman sits in the middle with a basket on her lap. Beside her are two children, a boy and a girl, looking out at the water. The boy trails his fingers in the cool lagoon. The girl points at a heron standing in the shallows. And ahead of them, the towers and domes of Venice glow golden in the morning sun.

[pause]

In the city, the first shops are opening their shutters. A baker carries a long board of fresh bread on his shoulder, calling out to the neighbours. A woman lowers a basket on a rope from her window, and a boy below puts a few eggs into it. She pulls the basket up again, and drops a coin down to him in return. In Venice, people have found many clever ways to live together in a small space.

[pause]

The family in the boat is going to the city to sell glass. They have done this journey many times before. But the children still love it. They love the wide open water and the fresh salty air. They love the moment when the city appears out of the mist, as if by magic. And they love the bustle and noise of the market, so different from their quiet island.

[long-pause]

{img:venetian-lagoon-shallow-water-reed-islands-birds-salt-marsh-morning}
Venice lies in a lagoon. A lagoon is a large area of shallow water, protected from the open sea by long, thin islands of sand. The Venetian lagoon is about fifty kilometres long. It is full of little islands, mudflats and marshes. And in the middle of it lies the city.

[pause]

The water in the lagoon is not deep. In many places, a grown man could stand on the bottom with his head above the water. But there are deeper channels too, winding through the shallows like hidden roads. The fishermen and the boatmen know every one of these channels. Strangers who do not know them can easily get stuck in the mud.

[long-pause]

Twice a day, the tide comes in from the sea, and twice a day it goes out again. When the tide is high, the water covers the mudflats, and the lagoon looks like a great silver lake. When the tide is low, the mudflats appear, brown and shining. Then the birds come down to feed. There are herons and egrets, ducks and little wading birds. They walk slowly over the mud, looking for worms and tiny crabs.

[pause]

{img:herons-egrets-feeding-mudflats-low-tide-lagoon-golden-light}
The tide is very important for Venice. It washes the canals clean twice a day, carrying the dirty water out to sea. Without the tides, the canals would become still and smelly. The Venetians watch the tides carefully. They know that sometimes, in winter, the tide comes in much higher than usual. Then the water floods the streets and the squares. We will come back to that later.

[long-pause]

People have lived in the lagoon for a very long time. The first people were fishermen and salt-makers. They built little huts of wood and reeds on the islands. They caught fish and eels, crabs and clams in the shallow water. They made salt by letting seawater dry in shallow ponds in the sun. Salt was very valuable in those days. It was used to keep meat and fish from going bad. Venice grew rich, at first, partly from salt.

[pause]

{img:salt-pans-lagoon-workers-raking-white-salt-sun-shallow-ponds}
Even in 1300, many people in the lagoon still live by fishing. Every morning before dawn, the fishermen row out in their small flat boats. They put out nets and traps made of reeds. They come back in the morning with their catch, and sell it at the fish market. Their wives mend the nets in the afternoons, sitting outside their houses in the sun.

[long-pause]

Out on the islands, there are vegetable gardens and vineyards, and fields where cows graze. There are monasteries too, quiet places where monks live and pray. Their bells ring out across the water at all hours of the day. On some islands, there are just a few houses and a little church. On others, like Murano, there are busy villages full of workshops.

[pause]

{img:lagoon-island-monastery-cypress-trees-bell-tower-evening-calm}
In the evenings, the lagoon is very beautiful. The sun sets behind the hills of the mainland, far to the west. The water turns pink, then orange, then deep purple. The boats come home, one by one, their oars dipping slowly. The bells ring for evening prayer from all the islands at once. And then the stars come out, and their light shines on the still water, so that there seem to be stars above and below.

[pause]

In spring, the lagoon is full of life. Birds build their nests in the reeds and on the little islands. Ducks swim past with long lines of fluffy ducklings behind them. Fish come into the shallow water to lay their eggs. The fishermen catch eels in long traps made of woven reeds. And the children of the islands search the mudflats for clams and little crabs at low tide.

[pause]

The people of the lagoon have their own boats, made for the shallow water. They are flat at the bottom, so they can float in very little water. The boatmen push them along with long poles, or row them with a single oar, standing up. They glide silently across the shallows, where bigger boats cannot go. It is a peaceful way to travel, slow and quiet.

[long-pause]

{img:early-venice-wooden-huts-reed-islands-refugees-boats-ancient}
How did a great city come to be built on the water? The story goes back about eight hundred years before our story, to the time when the Roman Empire was falling apart.

[pause]

In those days, armies from the north came into Italy. People in the towns on the mainland were frightened. So some of them fled to the islands in the lagoon. The shallow water and the mud protected them. Armies on horseback could not follow them there. And big ships could not sail into the shallow lagoon without knowing the hidden channels.

[long-pause]

At first, the people lived simply, in houses of wood and reeds. They fished and made salt, and they traded along the rivers and the coast. Slowly, over many years, more people came. The little villages on the islands grew bigger. People began to build with brick and stone. They built churches and houses and little bridges between the islands. And slowly, the villages joined together into one city.

[pause]

{img:workers-driving-wooden-piles-into-mud-building-foundations-venice}
Building on the soft mud of the lagoon is not easy. So the Venetians found a clever way to do it. They took long wooden poles, made from the trunks of oak and larch trees. They drove these poles deep into the mud, side by side, thousands of them. On top of the poles, they laid flat planks of wood. Then they laid stones on top of the planks. And on top of the stones, they built their houses and churches.

[long-pause]

The wooden poles are under the water and the mud, where there is no air. So they do not rot. Instead, over the years, they become as hard as stone. Many of the poles that hold up Venice today were put there hundreds of years ago. The whole city stands on a hidden forest of wooden poles, upside down under the water. The wood for these poles came from the forests in the mountains, floated down the rivers on rafts.

[pause]

{img:timber-rafts-floating-down-river-from-mountain-forests-to-lagoon}
The early Venetians chose a leader, whom they called the doge. The word doge comes from the Latin word for leader. The doge was chosen by the people, not born into the job like a king. The first doges lived simply, among the people. Later, the doges became grander. But they were always chosen, never simply born to rule.

[long-pause]

There is a famous old story about Saint Mark, who wrote one of the books of the Bible. His body lay in a tomb in the city of Alexandria, in Egypt. About four hundred and seventy years before our story, two Venetian merchants brought his body back to Venice. They hid it in a basket under some pork, so the guards would not look inside. From then on, Saint Mark was the protector of Venice. His symbol, a lion with wings, appears everywhere in the city.

[pause]

{img:winged-lion-saint-mark-stone-carving-column-sunlight-venice}
The Venetians are proud of their unusual city. They say it is a gift from God, built where nobody else would build. They call it the Most Serene Republic. And they look after it with great care. Every Venetian knows that the city lives with the water, and must always respect it. The sea brings them their wealth. But the sea could also take everything away.

[pause]

The early Venetians were very good at trade. They had little land for farming, so they had to buy most of their food. They sold salt and fish, and they carried goods for other people along the rivers and coasts. Little by little, their boats went further and further. They sailed down the coast of the Adriatic Sea, and then out into the wider Mediterranean.

[pause]

Every old family in Venice has its own story about how its ancestors came to the lagoon. Nonna Bettina tells the children that their family came from a town on the mainland, long ago. They came in a little boat, she says, with a goat, a cooking pot and a holy picture. The children do not know if it is true. But they love the story, and they ask for it again and again.

[pause]

Over the years, the Venetians also learned to make their islands bigger. They filled in shallow places with earth and stones and rubbish. They built walls of wood and stone along the edges of the canals. Slowly, the little islands joined together, with bridges and narrow streets between them. Today, the city stands on more than a hundred small islands. But from above, it looks like one great island, shaped a little like a fish.

[long-pause]

{img:murano-glassmaker-family-1300-workshop-doorway-father-mother-children}
Let us meet a family who lives on the island of Murano, just across the water from Venice.

[pause]

The father is called Matteo. He is a glassmaker, like his father and his grandfather before him. He is about forty years old, with strong arms and a short dark beard. His hands are covered with small marks from the heat of the furnace. He is a quiet man who does not talk very much. But when he is making glass, his face lights up with joy.

[pause]

The mother is called Lucia. She runs the house and keeps the accounts of the workshop. She sells the glass to merchants who come to Murano to buy. She is good with numbers, and very good at bargaining. The merchants say that Lucia drives a hard bargain. She takes that as a compliment.

[long-pause]

They have two children. The son is called Nicolò, and he is thirteen. He has just started to learn the glassmaker's craft from his father. He is eager and quick, but sometimes a bit too impatient. The daughter is called Chiara, and she is eleven. She has bright eyes and a lot of questions. She loves to watch the glass being made, and to help her mother at the market.

[pause]

{img:glassmaker-father-teaching-son-blowpipe-furnace-glow-workshop}
The grandmother lives with them too. She is Matteo's mother, and her name is Bettina. She is old and small, with white hair tucked under a linen cap. She spends her days sitting by the window, sewing and watching the canal. She knows everything that happens on Murano. Nobody can pass by without Nonna Bettina noticing. In Italian, nonna means grandmother.

[long-pause]

There is one more member of the family, though he is not at home. He is Matteo's younger brother, called Zorzi. Zorzi is a sailor on the great merchant galleys of Venice. Each year, he sails with the galleys to Alexandria in Egypt, or to Constantinople. He is away for many months at a time. When he comes home, he brings presents and stories from faraway lands. The children love their uncle Zorzi very much.

[pause]

{img:sailor-uncle-telling-stories-children-courtyard-gifts-spices-evening}
This spring, Zorzi has sailed with the galleys to Alexandria again. Before he left, he promised to bring back something special for each of the children. He would not tell them what. You will see, he said with a wink, when I come home before Christmas. Since then, Chiara has been guessing every day. Perhaps a parrot? Perhaps a little monkey? Perhaps a bag of sugar? Nicolò pretends not to care. But he guesses too, secretly.

[long-pause]

The family's house is next to the glass workshop, beside a small canal. It is built of red brick, with a roof of curved clay tiles. The front door opens onto the canal, where the family's boat is tied up. There is a small courtyard behind the house, with a well and a fig tree. On warm evenings, the family eats supper in the courtyard, under the fig tree.

[pause]

{img:family-supper-courtyard-fig-tree-well-lamps-murano-evening}
Tonight, the family is eating fish soup with bread. Nonna Bettina says a short prayer first. Then everyone talks at once, as Italian families do. Nicolò tells about the glass bead he made today, which cracked. Chiara tells about a funny merchant who wore a hat with a feather. Lucia tells about the price of firewood, which has gone up again. Matteo just listens and smiles. Above them, the swallows are diving and swooping in the evening sky.

[pause]

Every morning, before the work begins, the family goes to the little church on Murano for early mass. The church is old and quiet, with a floor of coloured stones. The priest knows every family on the island. After mass, the women stand outside and chat for a few minutes. The men walk quickly back to their furnaces. And the children run ahead, racing each other along the canal.

[pause]

The family also has a cat, a fat ginger cat called Rosso, which means red. Rosso sleeps all day in the warmest place he can find. In winter, that is beside the furnace in the workshop. In summer, that is on the sunny windowsill. In the evenings, he wakes up and goes hunting for mice in the storeroom. He is very good at it. Nonna Bettina says he is the best worker in the family.

[long-pause]

{img:murano-glass-workshop-furnace-glowing-orange-glassblowers-working}
Murano is the island of glass. Almost every family here has something to do with glassmaking.

[pause]

Glass has been made in Venice for hundreds of years. But the glass furnaces burn very hot, day and night. In a city built of wood and close-packed houses, that is dangerous. A fire could spread quickly from house to house. So, about ten years before our story, the rulers of Venice made a new law. All the glass furnaces had to move out of the city, to the island of Murano. Murano is close to Venice, but separate, with water all around it.

[long-pause]

There was another reason too. Venetian glass is very special, and very valuable. The glassmakers know secrets that nobody else in Europe knows. They know how to make glass very clear and pure. They know how to colour it red and blue and green. The rulers of Venice want to keep these secrets safe. So the glassmakers must stay on Murano. They are honoured and well paid. But they are not allowed to leave Venice and work anywhere else.

[pause]

{img:glassblower-blowing-bubble-on-pipe-molten-glass-orange-glow-close}
Let us go inside Matteo's workshop. It is a big room with a high roof and wide doors, open to let out the heat. In the middle stands the furnace, a round dome of brick with openings in its sides. Inside, a fire of wood burns fiercely. In the heart of the fire there are clay pots, full of melted glass. The glass glows bright orange, like liquid sunshine.

[long-pause]

Matteo takes a long iron pipe and dips the end of it into one of the pots. He turns it slowly, and a lump of glowing glass sticks to the end. Then he lifts it out and blows gently into the other end of the pipe. The lump of glass swells into a little round bubble. He turns the pipe all the time, so the glass stays even. He shapes it with wooden tools and iron tongs. Slowly, a beautiful cup takes shape.

[pause]

{img:glassmaker-shaping-cup-tongs-wooden-tool-bench-workshop-warm-light}
Glass is made from sand and ashes. The sand comes from the riverbanks. The ashes come from special plants that grow in salty places, in Egypt and Syria. Uncle Zorzi's galleys bring them back to Venice in big sacks. When the sand and ashes are heated together, very hot, they melt and turn into glass. The glassmakers add a little of this or that, to change the colour. A pinch of one thing makes blue glass. A pinch of another makes green.

[long-pause]

Nicolò is learning the craft from his father. At first, he does the simple jobs. He sweeps the floor and carries the firewood. He keeps the fire burning at the right heat. Then he learns to gather glass on the end of the pipe. Then he learns to blow small, simple things, like beads and little bottles. It will take him many years to become a master. But he is patient, mostly, and he loves the work.

[pause]

{img:boy-apprentice-making-glass-beads-small-flame-workshop-father-watching}
Today, Nicolò is trying to make a small glass bead, blue with a white swirl. He has tried many times. The beads keep cracking, or coming out lopsided. He sighs and scowls. Matteo comes over and watches. Slower, he says quietly. Let the glass tell you what it wants. Nicolò tries again, more slowly this time. And the bead comes out round and smooth and perfect. He holds it up to the light, and it glows blue. Matteo smiles and pats his shoulder. That one, he says, is for your mother.

[pause]

The furnace must never go out. If it goes cold, the clay pots inside can crack, and the melted glass is spoiled. So someone must always keep the fire burning, day and night. Matteo and his workers take turns. At night, a young worker sits by the furnace and feeds it with wood. He dozes a little in the warmth, but he wakes up every time the fire needs more wood.

[pause]

When a piece of glass is finished, it cannot cool down too quickly, or it will crack. So the glassmakers put it into a special cooler oven, beside the furnace. There it cools slowly, over many hours. In the morning, Lucia takes the finished pieces out and checks each one carefully. She holds them up to the light, looking for little bubbles or cracks. The best pieces go to the merchants. The pieces with small faults are sold more cheaply at the market.

[long-pause]

{img:venetian-house-1300-canal-front-water-door-boat-moored-brick}
Let us look more closely at the family's house, and at how people live in Venice.

[pause]

In Venice, most houses have two front doors. One door opens onto the canal, at the water's edge. People arrive by boat and step straight into the house. The other door opens onto a narrow street or a little square, for people arriving on foot. The canal door is the grand one, and the street door is the everyday one.

[long-pause]

On the ground floor of the family's house, there is a big room used for storage. Here, there are crates of finished glass, wrapped in straw. There are sacks of sand and ashes, and piles of firewood. Because the ground floor is close to the water, it sometimes gets damp. So nobody sleeps or lives there.

[pause]

{img:venetian-house-upper-room-wooden-beams-chest-bed-window-canal-light}
The family lives on the upper floor, up a steep wooden staircase. There is a large main room, with windows looking out over the canal. The windows have little panes of glass, made in Matteo's workshop. That is unusual, because most houses in 1300 have only wooden shutters. But this is a glassmaker's house. The light comes in through the glass and makes patterns on the floor.

[long-pause]

There is a fireplace in the main room, with a big chimney. Venetian chimneys are special. They are wide at the top, like an upside-down bell or a funnel. That is to stop sparks from flying out and setting other roofs alight. All over the city, you can see these funny bell-shaped chimneys on the rooftops.

[pause]

{img:venetian-rooftops-bell-shaped-chimneys-terracotta-tiles-sunset}
The furniture is simple but well made. There is a big wooden table and some benches. There are wooden chests, painted with flowers and birds, where clothes and linen are kept. There is a big bed for Matteo and Lucia, with curtains around it to keep out the draughts. The children sleep in a smaller room next door. Nonna Bettina has a little room of her own, near the fireplace, because she feels the cold.

[long-pause]

On the roof, there is a little wooden platform called an altana. Many Venetian houses have one. It is a place to sit in the fresh air, above the crowded streets and canals. The women of Venice use it to dry their washing and to bleach their hair in the sun. In the evenings, families sit up there and enjoy the cool breeze from the lagoon.

[pause]

{img:wooden-roof-terrace-altana-washing-drying-women-sun-lagoon-view}
Chiara loves the altana. From up there, she can see the whole of Murano. She can see the smoke rising from the furnaces, and the boats going up and down the canals. She can see across the water to the towers and domes of Venice. On clear days, she can even see the mountains far away to the north, with snow on their tops. She often sits up there with Nonna Bettina in the late afternoon. They watch the boats, and Nonna tells her stories about the old days.

[pause]

Water for drinking is a problem in Venice, because the lagoon water is salty. So the Venetians collect rainwater. In every campo there is a clever well. Under the square, there is a big hidden basin, filled with sand. When it rains, the water runs into the basin through little openings in the stones. The sand cleans the water. And the clean water collects in the middle, where people can draw it up from the well.

[pause]

In the kitchen, there is a big fireplace with an iron pot hanging over it. Lucia cooks soups and stews there, and bakes flat bread on a hot stone. The family eats a lot of fish, because it is fresh and cheap in Venice. They eat beans and rice, onions and cabbages from the islands. On Sundays, there might be a little meat. And there is always bread and a little wine with every meal.

[pause]

Nonna Bettina has a little garden on the altana, in wooden boxes and clay pots. She grows herbs there, like basil and rosemary and sage. She grows a few flowers too, red geraniums and little white daisies. Every evening, she waters them carefully from a jug. The smell of the herbs drifts down into the house. And Lucia picks a few leaves for the soup.

[long-pause]

{img:murano-morning-lucia-loading-boat-glass-crates-canal-children}
Let us follow Lucia and Chiara through one busy day, as they take glass to sell in the city.

[pause]

They get up at dawn. Lucia packs a basket with bread and cheese and a little bottle of wine for lunch. Matteo and Nicolò carry crates of glass down to the boat. The glass is wrapped carefully in straw, so it will not break. There are cups and bottles and little bowls, and a few strings of coloured beads. Lucia checks every crate and counts the pieces. Then she and Chiara climb into the boat.

[long-pause]

A neighbour called Piero rows them across the lagoon. He is an old boatman with a cheerful face and a loud voice. He stands at the back of the boat and pushes with a single long oar. The boat glides smoothly over the water. Piero sings as he rows, an old song about a fisherman and a mermaid. Chiara knows the words and sings along. Lucia shakes her head and smiles.

[pause]

{img:boatman-rowing-standing-single-oar-singing-lagoon-woman-girl-crates}
As they come closer to the city, the water gets busier. There are boats everywhere. There are boats full of vegetables from the islands, and boats full of firewood from the mainland. There are fishing boats, and boats carrying water in big barrels. There are elegant boats carrying rich people, with cushions and curtains. Everyone is shouting greetings and warnings to each other. Piero steers between them all without a single bump.

[long-pause]

They row into a canal and stop at a little landing near a merchant's house. The merchant is a big man with a fur-trimmed robe and a gold ring. He buys glass from Lucia every month and sells it to traders who take it to faraway lands. He looks at each piece carefully, holding it up to the light. Then he names a price. Lucia shakes her head and names a higher price. They argue politely for a while. In the end, they agree on a price somewhere in the middle. Both of them look satisfied.

[pause]

{img:merchant-inspecting-glass-cup-to-light-woman-bargaining-canal-house}
Chiara watches closely. She is learning how to bargain from her mother. Never take the first price, Lucia tells her afterwards. And never be rude. A merchant must leave happy, so he comes back next month. Chiara nods seriously. She thinks she will be a very good bargainer one day.

[long-pause]

With the money from the glass, they go shopping at the market. They buy things that the family needs. There is flour for bread, and olive oil, and a few onions. There is a new needle for Nonna Bettina, and some thread. And Lucia buys a little bag of something special: a handful of raisins, for a treat on Sunday.

[pause]

{img:mother-daughter-walking-narrow-venetian-street-baskets-afternoon-light}
At midday, they sit on the steps of a little church and eat their bread and cheese. Pigeons come and peck at the crumbs around their feet. Chiara throws them a few extra crumbs, when her mother is not looking. The sun is warm on their faces. The bells of the church ring above them, so loudly that Chiara puts her hands over her ears. Then they walk back to the boat, where Piero is waiting, and they row home across the shining lagoon.

[pause]

On the way home, Piero rows past the island of San Michele, where there is a little monastery. The monks are working in their garden, among the vegetables and the fruit trees. One of them waves to the boat, and Chiara waves back. The bells of the monastery ring for the afternoon prayer. The sound drifts across the water, soft and slow.

[pause]

When they reach Murano, Matteo is waiting at the landing. He helps them out of the boat and asks how the selling went. Very well, says Lucia, and she shows him the money. Matteo smiles. Chiara tells him about the merchant and the bargaining, waving her arms. Matteo laughs and says she will soon be selling the glass herself. Chiara thinks that is a very good idea.

[long-pause]

{img:rialto-wooden-bridge-1300-grand-canal-market-crowds-boats-morning}
The heart of trade in Venice is a place called the Rialto. It lies in the middle of the city, beside the Grand Canal. The Grand Canal is the biggest canal in Venice. It winds through the city like a great letter S.

[pause]

In 1300, there is only one bridge across the Grand Canal, and it is here at the Rialto. It is a bridge made of wood, resting on boats. Later it will be rebuilt as a wooden bridge that can open in the middle, to let tall ships pass. And much later still, it will be rebuilt in stone. But in our story, it is a simple wooden bridge, always crowded with people.

[long-pause]

Around the bridge there is a great market. It is one of the busiest markets in the whole world. There are stalls selling fruit and vegetables, fresh from the islands of the lagoon. There are stalls selling meat and cheese and eggs. And there is a big fish market, where the fishermen sell their catch every morning. The smell of the fish market is very strong. But the fish are fresh and good.

[pause]

{img:rialto-fish-market-stalls-fishermen-baskets-eels-crabs-morning}
The Rialto is also where the rich merchants meet. They stand in groups under the arcades, talking business. They buy and sell cargoes of pepper and silk, cotton and wool. They lend money to each other for voyages. They talk about the news from faraway ports: the price of pepper in Alexandria, or the weather in Constantinople. A ship's cargo can be bought and sold here many times, before it even arrives in Venice.

[long-pause]

There are money changers at the Rialto too. They sit at little tables with scales and piles of coins. Merchants come from many lands, with many different kinds of money. The money changers weigh the coins and change them for Venetian coins. The most famous Venetian coin is a gold coin called the ducat. It is trusted all over the world, because its gold is always pure.

[pause]

{img:money-changer-table-scales-gold-ducats-coins-merchants-arcade}
In a little church near the Rialto, there is an old inscription carved in stone. It asks merchants to be honest. Around this temple, it says, let the merchant's law be just, his weights true, and his contracts fair. The Venetians are proud of their honest trade. Of course, not every merchant is always honest. But most of them try to be, because trust is good for business.

[long-pause]

Chiara loves the Rialto market. There is so much to see. There are merchants from Germany with long blond beards and fur hats. There are traders from Greece and Turkey, from Egypt and Syria. There are Jewish merchants and Armenian merchants. She hears languages she has never heard before. She sees silks of every colour, and spices piled in little hills, red and yellow and brown.

[pause]

{img:spice-stall-rialto-pepper-cinnamon-saffron-piles-girl-looking}
One day, a spice seller lets Chiara smell his spices. He holds out a little pot of cinnamon, and then one of cloves, and then one of pepper. The pepper makes her sneeze, and the spice seller laughs. These come from very far away, he tells her. From India, and from islands even further east. They have travelled for many months to reach you. Chiara thinks of her uncle Zorzi, on his galley. She wonders if he is buying spices too, at this very moment, in Alexandria.

[pause]

Around the market, there are little taverns where people stop to eat and drink. They serve small plates of food, like fried fish, boiled eggs and pieces of bread with sardines. Merchants stand at the counters, eating quickly and talking business. Boatmen come in for a quick glass of wine between jobs. The taverns are noisy and cheerful, full of talk and laughter from morning till night.

[pause]

At the end of the day, the market slowly empties. The stall holders pack up their goods and sweep the ground. The fishermen wash down their stalls with buckets of lagoon water. Cats come out to look for scraps of fish. The merchants go home to their palaces along the Grand Canal. And the boatmen take the last customers home, as the sun goes down.

[long-pause]

{img:venetian-boats-1300-canal-gondola-early-form-boatman-oar-houses}
In Venice, boats are like carts and carriages in other cities. Everyone travels by boat, all the time.

[pause]

There are many kinds of boats in Venice. There are big flat barges for carrying heavy goods, like stone and wood and barrels. There are small fishing boats, with nets piled in the bottom. There are fast little boats for carrying messages. And there are the gondolas, long thin boats for carrying people.

[long-pause]

In 1300, the gondola looks a little different from the gondolas of today. It is not yet painted all black. Rich families paint their gondolas in bright colours, with gold decorations and silk cushions. Some have a little cabin in the middle, with curtains, to keep out the sun and the rain. The boatman stands at the back and rows with one long oar. He leans forward with each stroke, pushing the boat through the water.

[pause]

{img:colourful-medieval-gondola-silk-cushions-cabin-curtains-noble-family}
Rowing a boat standing up, with only one oar, is a special skill. The oar rests in a carved wooden holder on the side of the boat. The boatman pushes and turns the oar with each stroke, so the boat goes straight. It takes years to learn. Venetian boys start to learn when they are very young. Nicolò can already row the family's boat quite well. Chiara is learning too, though she sometimes goes round in circles.

[long-pause]

There are no bridges over most of the canals yet in 1300. There are a few bridges, made of wood. But to cross most canals, people use a ferry called a traghetto. It is a boat that goes back and forth across the canal, all day long. You step into it on one side, pay a small coin, and step out on the other side. The traghetto boatmen are proud of their work. They belong to a guild, a kind of club, with its own rules and its own saint.

[pause]

{img:traghetto-ferry-boat-crossing-canal-passengers-standing-boatman}
Along the canals, there are little landing places with wooden posts in the water. Boats tie up to the posts while people go into houses or shops. The posts are often painted in stripes, in the colours of the family who owns the house. Seaweed and little crabs grow on the bottoms of the posts, where the water laps against them.

[long-pause]

The boatmen of Venice are famous for their singing. They sing as they row, to keep the rhythm and to pass the time. They sing old songs about love and the sea, about saints and the lagoon. Sometimes, one boatman sings a line, and another boatman answers him from a different canal. The songs echo off the walls of the houses, across the water.

[pause]

{img:evening-canal-boatman-singing-lantern-boat-reflections-calm-water}
In the evening, Matteo sometimes takes the family out in the boat. They row slowly along the canals of Murano, as the sun goes down. Nicolò rows, and the others sit and enjoy the cool air. They wave to neighbours sitting outside their houses. They listen to the songs of the other boatmen. The water is still and golden. And the lanterns begin to glow in the windows, one by one, reflected in the canal.

[pause]

Building a boat is a special craft in Venice. There are little boatyards all over the city, called squeri. In a squero, the boat builders work in the open air, beside a canal. They use wood of eight different kinds, each for a different part of the boat. The boats are built by hand, slowly and carefully, without any plans on paper. The builders carry the plans in their heads, learned from their fathers.

[pause]

On Murano, every family has a boat, just as families in other places have a cart or a horse. Children learn to row as soon as they are strong enough. They learn which canals are deep and which are shallow. They learn how to pass other boats without bumping. And they learn the boatmen's calls, which warn other boats at the corners of the canals. Every Venetian child knows these calls by heart.

[long-pause]

{img:doge-venice-1300-ceremonial-robes-corno-cap-palace-hall-councillors}
Venice is not ruled by a king. It is a republic, which means it is ruled by its own people, or at least by some of them. At the head of the republic is the doge.

[pause]

The doge is chosen for life. He is usually an old and wise nobleman, who has served Venice for many years. In 1300, the doge is a man called Pietro Gradenigo. He wears a long robe of gold cloth and a special cap. The cap is shaped a little like a soft horn, standing up at the back. It is called the corno, and only the doge may wear it.

[long-pause]

But the doge does not have all the power. The Venetians do not want any one man to become too powerful. So the doge must share his power with councils of noblemen. The most important is the Great Council. About this time, the Great Council grows much bigger. It now has more than a thousand members, all from the old noble families of Venice. They meet in a great hall in the doge's palace.

[pause]

{img:great-council-hall-venice-noblemen-benches-voting-urns-candlelight}
The way the Venetians choose a new doge is very complicated. It is done on purpose, so that nobody can cheat. First, a group of councillors is chosen by drawing little balls from an urn. Then that group chooses another group. Then some of those are chosen by lot again. This happens ten times, back and forth. At the end, a group of forty-one men chooses the new doge. It takes many days.

[long-pause]

There is a charming custom in this choosing. At the very start, a young councillor goes out into the square. He must bring back the first boy he meets, who is between eight and ten years old. This boy will draw the little balls out of the urn during the voting. He is called the ballot boy. Because he is a child, people believe he will be honest and fair. Later, he will have a special place in the doge's household.

[pause]

{img:young-ballot-boy-drawing-ball-from-urn-council-watching-palace}
Chiara has heard about the ballot boy from Nonna Bettina. She wishes very much that girls could be chosen too. If I were the ballot boy, she says, I would be the most honest one ever. Nonna Bettina laughs. I am sure you would, she says. But perhaps it is better to sell glass at the Rialto. It pays more.

[long-pause]

The doge lives in a great palace next to the church of San Marco. In 1300, the palace is older and simpler than the beautiful pink-and-white palace that visitors see today. But it is still very grand. There are halls for the councils and courts for the judges. There are rooms for the doge and his family. And there are prisons too, for those who have broken the law.

[pause]

{img:doge-palace-1300-waterfront-boats-arriving-officials-morning}
The doge has many duties. He leads the great religious ceremonies of the city. He welcomes ambassadors from foreign lands. He signs the laws that the councils have agreed. But he must always be careful. There are many rules about what he may and may not do. He may not open letters from foreign rulers on his own. He may not leave Venice without permission. In some ways, the doge is the most honoured man in Venice. And in other ways, he is the most watched.

[pause]

The ordinary people of Venice do not have a vote in the Great Council. But they have their own ways of taking part in the life of the city. They belong to guilds, groups of people who do the same work. There is a guild of glassmakers, a guild of boatmen, and a guild of bakers. The guilds have their own rules, their own meetings and their own saints. They look after their members when they are ill or old.

[pause]

There are also religious brotherhoods, called scuole. Ordinary people join them to help others. They give food to the poor, care for the sick, and help widows and orphans. They hold processions on feast days, with candles and banners. Matteo belongs to one of these brotherhoods. Once a month, he goes to a meeting and gives a little money for the poor. Lucia says it is the most important money he spends.

[pause]

Every year, the doge must also make many public appearances. He walks in processions through the city on feast days, under a golden umbrella. Behind him come the councillors in their red robes, and the priests with their candles. In front of him walk musicians with silver trumpets. The people of Venice crowd the streets to watch. And the doge greets them, raising his hand, as the procession moves slowly toward San Marco.

[long-pause]

{img:basilica-san-marco-1300-domes-golden-mosaics-facade-square-morning}
At the heart of Venice lies a great open square, called the Piazza San Marco. And at one end of the square stands the most famous church in Venice. It is the Basilica of San Marco, the church of Saint Mark.

[pause]

The church is very different from the churches in other parts of Italy. It has five round domes on its roof, like a church in Constantinople. Its front is decorated with columns of coloured marble, brought from all over the Mediterranean. Above the doors, there are arches filled with pictures made of tiny pieces of glass and gold. These pictures are called mosaics. They glitter in the sunshine.

[long-pause]

Inside, the church is dim and quiet and golden. The walls and the domes are covered with mosaics. There are pictures of saints and angels, of stories from the Bible. There is the story of Noah and his ark, and the story of the creation of the world. The background of every picture is made of tiny squares of gold glass. When candles are lit, the whole church shimmers and glows, like the inside of a jewel box.

[pause]

{img:interior-san-marco-golden-mosaics-domes-candles-worshippers-dim}
Many of the tiny pieces of gold glass were made on Murano. Matteo's father helped to make some of them, long ago. He made the gold glass by putting a thin layer of real gold leaf between two layers of clear glass. Then the glass was cut into tiny squares, smaller than a fingernail. Artists pressed them into wet plaster, one by one, to make the pictures. It took many years.

[long-pause]

Above the main door of the church stand four great horses of bronze. They are covered in gold, and they shine in the sun. They are very old, more than a thousand years old even then. The Venetians brought them back from Constantinople about a hundred years before our story. Nobody is quite sure who made them. They look as if they are about to step forward, with their heads held high.

[pause]

{img:four-bronze-horses-san-marco-gilded-sunlight-pigeons-loggia}
On Sundays and holy days, the family from Murano sometimes comes to San Marco for mass. They row across the lagoon in their best clothes. Nonna Bettina wears her best linen cap. Lucia wears a dress of blue wool, and a little necklace of glass beads. The children are scrubbed clean and told to behave.

[long-pause]

Inside the church, Chiara looks up at the golden domes with her mouth open. She tries to count the angels, but there are too many. She finds the little picture of Noah's ark, with all the animals going inside two by two. She looks for the pieces of gold glass her grandfather made. She cannot tell which ones they are. But Matteo tells her they are up there, somewhere, shining.

[pause]

{img:family-standing-piazza-san-marco-after-mass-pigeons-bell-tower-sun}
After mass, the family walks out into the great square. It is full of people in their Sunday clothes. There are pigeons everywhere, pecking at crumbs. Children run and play between the columns. The great bell tower stands tall above everything. Its bells are ringing, so loudly that the whole square seems to shake. Matteo buys the children a little sweet cake each, from a man with a tray. And they sit on the steps in the sunshine and eat them slowly.

[pause]

The Piazza San Marco is not only a place for church. It is also a place for markets and fairs. On certain days, there are stalls selling food and cloth and many other things. There are jugglers and acrobats, and men with performing animals. There are storytellers, who tell old tales to anyone who will listen. And there are always people walking up and down, just to see and be seen.

[pause]

Near the water, there are two tall columns of stone. On top of one of them stands the winged lion of Saint Mark. On top of the other stands Saint Theodore, the older protector of Venice, standing on a crocodile. Visitors arriving by sea step ashore between these two columns. It is the grand entrance to the city. Chiara likes the crocodile best. She has never seen a real one, but Uncle Zorzi says they live in the river Nile.

[long-pause]

{img:arsenal-venice-shipyard-1300-galleys-being-built-workers-walls}
At the eastern end of Venice, behind high brick walls, there is a very special place. It is called the Arsenal. It is the great shipyard of the Republic of Venice. And in 1300, it is one of the biggest workshops in the whole world.

[pause]

The Arsenal is where Venice builds its galleys. These are the long ships with oars and sails that carry Venetian goods across the sea. Behind its walls, there are long sheds and docks, workshops and storehouses. Hundreds of workers work there every day. They are called the arsenalotti, and they are proud of their skill.

[long-pause]

The workers of the Arsenal build ships in a very clever way. Each group of workers has its own special job. Some make the frames of the ships. Some make the planks. Some make the oars, and others make the ropes and the sails. Some make the barrels for water and wine. Then all the parts are brought together, and the ship is put together quickly, like a puzzle.

[pause]

{img:arsenal-rope-walk-long-building-workers-twisting-hemp-rope}
There is a very long building in the Arsenal, where ropes are made. It is so long that you can hardly see from one end to the other. Workers walk backward slowly along it, twisting fibres of hemp into rope. The ropes must be very long and very strong. A big galley needs many kilometres of rope.

[long-pause]

The workers of the Arsenal have special privileges. They are well paid, and they are respected in the city. They guard the doge on special days. They row the doge's golden ship at the great festivals. When a new doge is chosen, they carry him around the Piazza San Marco on their shoulders. And every day at the end of work, each worker is given a little wine to drink.

[pause]

{img:shipwrights-shaping-timber-adzes-galley-frame-workshop-sawdust}
The great poet Dante visited Venice about twenty years after our story. He saw the Arsenal, and he wrote about it in his famous poem. He described the workers boiling sticky tar in great pots, in winter, to fix the ships. He described them hammering and sawing and making oars and ropes and sails. It was such a busy, noisy, smoky place that it stayed in his memory.

[long-pause]

Nicolò dreams of seeing the Arsenal. Only the workers are allowed inside its walls. But one day, his uncle Zorzi took him to the great gate and let him peep in. He saw a forest of masts and the bones of half-built ships. He heard the hammering and the shouting. He smelled the hot tar and the fresh wood. He has never forgotten it.

[pause]

{img:boy-and-sailor-uncle-looking-through-arsenal-gate-masts-beyond}
At the gate of the Arsenal, there is a stone lion, the lion of Saint Mark. Around it, there are always people coming and going. Workers carry tools and timber. Boats bring loads of wood from the forests of the mainland. Sailors come to look at their new ships. And over everything, there is the sound of hammering, all day long, from dawn until the evening bell.

[pause]

The Arsenal has its own bakery too, which makes ship's biscuit for the galleys. It is a hard, dry bread that keeps for many months at sea. The bakers make thousands of biscuits every week. They are stored in big dry rooms, ready for the next voyage. The smell of baking drifts over the walls of the Arsenal, mixing with the smell of hot tar and fresh wood.

[pause]

At the end of the working day, a great bell rings at the Arsenal. The workers put down their tools and pour out through the gate. They walk home through the narrow streets in groups, talking and laughing. Their wives are waiting with supper. Their children run to meet them at the doors. And the Arsenal falls quiet for the night, its half-built ships standing silent in the dark.

[pause]

Nicolò sometimes imagines working at the Arsenal instead of in the glass workshop. He imagines building great ships and sailing away on them, like his uncle. But then he thinks of the glowing furnace, and the glass bubble swelling on the end of his pipe. He thinks of the beautiful colours, and the joy of making something new. And he decides that glass is what he loves best after all.

[long-pause]

{img:merchant-galleys-convoy-leaving-venice-spring-sails-oars-lagoon}
Venice is rich because of trade. And the most important trade of all is with the East.

[pause]

Every year, in spring and summer, fleets of merchant galleys leave Venice. They sail together in groups, called convoys, for safety. Some convoys sail to Constantinople, the great city between Europe and Asia. Some sail to Alexandria in Egypt. Some sail to the ports of Syria and Lebanon. Some even sail west, past Spain, to the ports of England and Flanders.

[long-pause]

The galleys sail with both oars and sails. When the wind is good, they raise their sails. When the wind drops, or when they enter a harbour, the rowers take up their oars. There are more than a hundred rowers on a big galley. They sit on wooden benches, three to a bench. They are free men, paid for their work. Many of them also carry a little cargo of their own to sell.

[pause]

{img:galley-rowers-benches-oars-sea-sunshine-sail-above-calm}
In Alexandria, the Venetians buy spices and silk, cotton and sugar. The spices come from India and from islands even further east. They have travelled for months, on ships and on camels, to reach Egypt. There is pepper and cinnamon, nutmeg and cloves, and ginger. In Europe, these spices are very expensive. Only the rich can afford them. They make food taste wonderful, and they are used in medicines too.

[long-pause]

In return, the Venetians sell wool and cloth, metal and wood. And they sell glass from Murano, which is famous everywhere. The merchants of Alexandria love Venetian glass beads and cups. So Matteo's glass sometimes travels all the way to Egypt, on the same galleys as Uncle Zorzi.

[pause]

{img:alexandria-harbour-1300-venetian-galley-unloading-spices-camels}
Zorzi has told the children all about Alexandria. It is a great city, he says, with a famous old lighthouse at the entrance to the harbour. There are markets full of things you have never imagined. There are camels and palm trees and people in long white robes. The sun is so hot that you must rest in the shade at midday. And the evenings are warm and full of the smell of spices.

[long-pause]

When the galleys come home, there is great excitement in Venice. People crowd along the waterfront to watch them arrive. The merchants hurry to the Rialto to buy and sell the new cargoes. The families of the sailors wait at the quays, looking for their loved ones. Bells ring, and people cheer. It is one of the happiest days of the year.

[pause]

{img:galleys-returning-crowds-waterfront-families-waving-autumn-light}
This year, the convoy to Alexandria is expected home in the autumn, in October or November. Lucia counts the weeks on her fingers. Chiara asks every day if it is time yet. Nonna Bettina says a prayer for the sailors every evening. And Matteo, who does not say much, stands at the canal door sometimes in the evening, looking out toward the open sea.

[pause]

Before the galleys leave, the families of the sailors come to the waterfront to say goodbye. Wives hug their husbands, and children cling to their fathers' legs. The priests bless the ships and the sailors. Then the oars dip into the water, and the galleys move slowly out into the lagoon. The families wave until the ships are only little dots on the horizon. Then they walk home slowly, already counting the days.

[pause]

Zorzi is not a rower. He is a sailor who works the sails and the ropes, and he helps to load and unload the cargo. He has a small chest of his own on board, with a few things to trade. Each year, he buys a little cotton or a few spices in Alexandria. He sells them in Venice for a small profit. It is not a fortune, but it helps. And one day, he hopes to buy a share in a ship of his own.

[long-pause]

{img:marco-polo-older-man-venice-house-telling-stories-listeners-1300}
In the year 1300, there is a man living in Venice who has seen more of the world than almost anyone. His name is Marco Polo.

[pause]

About twenty-five years before our story, Marco Polo left Venice with his father and his uncle. They were merchants, and they travelled east, across Persia and the great deserts of Asia. They travelled for more than three years. And at last they reached China, the land of the great khan, Kublai Khan.

[long-pause]

Marco Polo stayed in China for about seventeen years. He worked for the great khan, travelling all over his empire. He saw wonderful things that no Venetian had ever seen. He saw great cities with millions of people. He saw paper money, and black stones that burned like wood. He saw palaces with golden roofs and gardens full of strange animals. He saw a postal service with fast horses, and great canals full of boats.

[pause]

{img:marco-polo-caravan-crossing-asian-desert-camels-sunset-long-journey}
Then, about five years before our story, Marco Polo came home to Venice at last. He was about forty years old. His family hardly recognised him. They say he arrived in ragged clothes, after so many years away. His relatives did not believe it was really him. So he invited them to a great feast. At the end of the feast, he cut open the seams of his ragged coats. Out poured jewels, rubies and emeralds and pearls, onto the table. Then everyone believed him.

[long-pause]

A little later, Marco Polo was taken prisoner during a war between Venice and Genoa. In prison, he met a writer called Rustichello. To pass the time, Marco told Rustichello all about his travels. Rustichello wrote it all down. That became a famous book, called The Travels of Marco Polo. It was copied by hand many times and read all over Europe. Many people did not believe it. They said it was full of tall tales.

[pause]

{img:prison-room-two-men-writing-book-candle-marco-polo-rustichello}
Now Marco Polo is home again, living in his family's house near the Rialto. Many people come to visit him, to hear his stories. Some come to laugh at him and call him a liar. People give him a nickname, Il Milione, which means the Million. Some say it is because he talks about millions of everything. Others say it is because his family is very rich.

[long-pause]

One day in the summer, Uncle Zorzi's friend takes Nicolò and Chiara to hear Marco Polo speak. He is sitting in the courtyard of his house, with a group of people around him. He is a man of about forty-five, with a grey beard and a calm face. He speaks slowly and clearly. He tells them about the great city of Hangzhou, with its lakes and its twelve thousand bridges. He tells them about the great khan's summer palace, made of bamboo.

[pause]

{img:children-listening-wide-eyes-marco-polo-courtyard-afternoon-shade}
Chiara listens with her mouth open. Twelve thousand bridges! Venice has only a few. She asks Marco Polo a question. Is it true about the black stones that burn? Marco Polo smiles at her. It is true, he says. The Chinese dig them out of the ground and burn them in their stoves. They keep the houses warm all winter. Chiara thinks about this all the way home. She is not sure if she believes it. But she thinks Marco Polo is the most interesting man she has ever met.

[pause]

Today, of course, we know that most of what Marco Polo said was true. The black stones were coal. His book helped many people in Europe to dream about faraway lands. Two hundred years later, a young sailor called Christopher Columbus read it too. He made notes in the margins of his copy. And it helped to inspire him to sail west, across the ocean.

[pause]

Marco Polo also brought home some objects from the East. People say he had a little silver tablet from the great khan, which allowed him to travel safely. He had pieces of silk and jewels, and perhaps some strange things too. Some say he brought back the first noodles to Italy, but that is probably only a story. Italians were already eating pasta before he came home.

[pause]

In the evening after the visit, Chiara tells her family everything. She tells them about the twelve thousand bridges and the black stones and the paper money. Nonna Bettina shakes her head. Paper money, she says. Who would want money made of paper? It would blow away in the wind. Everyone laughs. But Matteo says quietly that Marco Polo is a wise man. And that the world is much bigger than any of us know.

[long-pause]

{img:venetian-children-1300-playing-campo-square-hoop-ball-well}
What is life like for children in Venice in 1300?

[pause]

Most children do not go to school. They learn at home, from their parents and grandparents. Boys learn their father's craft, as Nicolò is learning glassmaking. Girls learn to cook and sew and run a household. Some girls learn their mother's trade too, like weaving or selling. Chiara is learning to keep accounts and to bargain, from Lucia.

[long-pause]

But some children do learn to read and write and count. There are small schools in Venice, run by teachers in their own houses. The sons of merchants go there to learn arithmetic and letters, because they will need them in business. Some daughters learn to read too, especially in richer families. Lucia can read and write a little, and she is teaching Chiara.

[pause]

{img:mother-teaching-daughter-letters-wax-tablet-window-light-venice}
Every evening, after supper, Lucia and Chiara sit at the table with a wax tablet. A wax tablet is a small board of wood, covered with a thin layer of wax. You write on it with a pointed stick. When you are finished, you can smooth the wax and start again. Lucia writes numbers and words, and Chiara copies them. Chiara likes the numbers best. She can already add up a column of numbers faster than her brother.

[long-pause]

The children of Venice also have plenty of time to play. In every neighbourhood, there is a little square called a campo. In the middle of each campo there is a well, with a stone top carved with flowers and saints. The children play in the campo, while their mothers fetch water from the well and talk. They play with balls and hoops, and they play tag and hide and seek. They jump over the little canals, and sometimes they fall in.

[pause]

{img:boys-fishing-from-bridge-canal-lines-laughing-sunny-afternoon}
The boys love to fish from the bridges and the landing places. They use lines of string with little hooks, and bits of bread as bait. They catch small fish and crabs, and proudly bring them home. In summer, they swim in the canals, though their mothers tell them not to. The water is cool on hot days, and the boys splash and shout.

[long-pause]

The children also love the festivals of Venice. There are many of them, all through the year. There are processions with candles and banners. There are boat races on the Grand Canal. There are fairs with jugglers and acrobats and musicians. And there are special foods, like sweet cakes and roasted chestnuts.

[pause]

{img:children-watching-boat-race-grand-canal-crowds-flags-cheering}
Chiara's best friend is a girl called Isabetta, who lives next door. Her father is a glassmaker too. The two girls play together almost every day. They sit on the altana and make up stories. They make necklaces from broken bits of coloured glass that they find in the workshops. They watch the boats and make up names for all the people in them. In the evenings, they wave to each other from their windows, until their mothers call them to bed.

[pause]

Children in Venice also have their own small jobs. Chiara fetches water from the well every morning, in two copper buckets. She feeds the chickens that live in a coop on the roof. She helps her grandmother with the sewing and the mending. And on market days, she minds the little stall of glass beads that Lucia sets up in the campo. She is very serious about it, and very proud.

[pause]

Every evening, before bed, the children say their prayers with Nonna Bettina. They kneel beside her bed and say the old prayers in Latin. Then Nonna Bettina makes the sign of the cross on their foreheads with her thumb. She tells them a short story, or sings them an old lullaby from the lagoon. And then they go to bed, in the little room next door, and listen to the water lapping against the house until they fall asleep.

[pause]

In winter, when the weather is cold, the children play indoors. They play games with little bones or pebbles, throwing them and catching them on the backs of their hands. They play a game like draughts on a board scratched into the floor. Nonna Bettina teaches them old riddles and rhymes. And sometimes, Matteo makes them little glass marbles in the workshop, and they roll them across the wooden floor.

[long-pause]

{img:festa-della-sensa-doge-golden-ship-bucintoro-lagoon-crowds-boats}
Every year, in spring, Venice holds its most famous festival. It is called the Sensa, and it falls on Ascension Day, forty days after Easter. On this day, the doge of Venice marries the sea.

[pause]

It sounds very strange, but it is a beautiful ceremony. Venice owes everything to the sea. The sea protects the city, and it brings ships and trade and wealth. So once a year, the Venetians thank the sea, and promise to look after it. And the doge does this by marrying it, with a golden ring.

[long-pause]

On the morning of the festival, the doge goes on board a magnificent ship. It is called the Bucintoro. It is a great galley, painted red and gold, with a golden lion at the front. There is a covered deck with cushions and curtains of red velvet. The workers of the Arsenal row it, with great golden oars. Behind it come hundreds and hundreds of other boats, decorated with flowers and flags.

[pause]

{img:hundreds-decorated-boats-following-golden-ship-lagoon-spring-flags}
The whole city comes out to watch. People crowd along the waterfront and on every bridge and roof. They wave flags and cheer. Musicians play on the boats. Church bells ring all over the city. It is the happiest day of the year in Venice.

[long-pause]

The Bucintoro rows slowly out across the lagoon, to the place where the lagoon meets the open sea. There, everything becomes quiet. The bishop blesses the water with holy water. Then the doge stands up at the side of the ship. He takes a golden ring from his finger. He holds it up for everyone to see. Then he says the old words: We marry you, O sea, as a sign of our true and lasting rule. And he throws the ring into the water.

[pause]

{img:doge-throwing-gold-ring-into-sea-bucintoro-side-quiet-moment}
The ring sinks down into the blue water and disappears. A great cheer goes up from all the boats. Trumpets sound, and drums beat. The bells of Venice ring out across the lagoon. The sea has been married once again.

[long-pause]

This year, the whole family from Murano goes out in their boat to watch. Nicolò rows, and Matteo helps. Lucia and Nonna Bettina sit in the middle, in their best clothes. Chiara sits at the front, so she can see everything. They follow the great golden ship across the lagoon, among hundreds of other boats.

[pause]

{img:family-small-boat-watching-festival-crowds-boats-lagoon-sunshine}
When the doge throws the ring, Chiara stands up to see better. The boat wobbles, and Lucia grabs her dress. Sit down, she says, or you will marry the sea too! Everyone laughs. Afterwards, there is a great fair in the Piazza San Marco. It lasts for many days. There are stalls selling everything you can imagine. Matteo even sells some of his glass there, and does very good business. And in the evening, the family rows home across the lagoon, tired and happy, as the sun goes down.

[pause]

During the fair, there are many stalls selling things from all over the world. There are carpets from Persia and silks from China. There are spices from India and furs from Russia. There are fine cloths from Flanders and swords from Germany. People come from far away to buy and sell. The square is so crowded that you can hardly move. And the noise of people talking in many languages fills the air.

[pause]

Chiara is allowed to spend one small coin at the fair. She walks from stall to stall for a long time, thinking carefully. In the end, she buys a little ribbon of blue silk, for her hair. She ties it on at once and looks at herself in a piece of polished glass. Isabetta says it looks lovely. Chiara decides that it is the best coin she has ever spent.

[long-pause]

{img:venice-autumn-1300-grape-harvest-island-vineyard-baskets-lagoon}
Summer turns into autumn. The days get shorter, and the evenings get cooler. On the islands of the lagoon, it is time for the grape harvest.

[pause]

Many families in the lagoon have small vineyards on the islands. In autumn, everyone helps to pick the grapes. They carry them in big baskets to the wine presses. The juice runs out into barrels, purple and sweet. The children are allowed to eat as many grapes as they like. They come home with purple fingers and purple faces.

[long-pause]

On Murano, the glass furnaces burn on as always. In autumn, the merchants come to buy glass for the winter trade. Lucia is very busy. She sells cups and bottles, beads and bowls. She writes everything down carefully in her account book. It has been a good year for the workshop. Matteo has made some of his most beautiful glass ever. There is a set of cups in pale blue glass, with thin white threads swirling through them.

[pause]

{img:pale-blue-venetian-glass-cups-white-threads-workshop-table-light}
Nicolò has made progress too. He can now make good glass beads, round and smooth, in many colours. He has started to blow small bottles. Some of them are still a bit lopsided. But some of them are good enough to sell. When the first one is sold, he is so proud that he cannot stop smiling all day.

[long-pause]

In the evenings, the family sits by the fire, because the nights are getting cold. Nonna Bettina knits and tells stories. Chiara practises her letters on the wax tablet. Nicolò carves a little boat out of wood. Lucia does her accounts. And Matteo sits and stares into the fire, thinking. He is thinking about his brother Zorzi. The galleys should be home soon.

[pause]

{img:family-evening-fireside-grandmother-knitting-girl-wax-tablet-boy-carving}
In October, news arrives from the east. A fast boat has come into Venice with letters. The convoy of galleys has left Alexandria, the letters say. They are on their way home. Everyone in the family is very happy. Chiara jumps up and down. Nicolò grins. Lucia says a little prayer of thanks. And Matteo smiles, for the first time in days.

[long-pause]

So they wait. October goes by, and November begins. The leaves fall from the few trees on Murano. The rain comes, and the wind blows cold from the north. Every day, Chiara runs to the altana to look out to sea. Every evening, she comes down and shakes her head. Not yet, she says.

[pause]

{img:girl-on-rooftop-terrace-looking-out-to-sea-grey-november-sky-wind}
Other families are waiting too. All over Venice, there are families of sailors, waiting for their men to come home. The women gather at the wells and talk about it. The men talk about it at the Rialto. When will the galleys come? Will they be home before Christmas? Nobody knows. And the November days grow shorter and darker, one after another.

[pause]

In autumn, the first chestnuts arrive at the markets from the hills of the mainland. Men roast them over little fires in the campi and sell them in paper cones. The smell of roasting chestnuts drifts through the streets. The children love them. They burn their fingers peeling them, and then they blow on them and eat them hot. It is one of the best things about autumn.

[pause]

The evenings are getting darker now, and the family spends more time indoors. Lucia lights the oil lamps earlier. The fire in the fireplace is lit every evening. The family gathers around it after supper, wrapped in warm wool. Rosso the cat curls up on Nonna Bettina's lap. And the sound of the rain on the roof tiles makes everyone feel snug and safe.

[pause]

On the first of November, the family goes to the little church of Murano for the feast of All Saints. The next day is All Souls, when families remember those who have passed away. They light candles for Matteo's father and for Lucia's parents. They leave flowers at their resting places. Nonna Bettina tells the children a little story about each one. It is a quiet and gentle day, full of memories.

[long-pause]

{img:stormy-adriatic-sea-grey-waves-distant-galleys-november-rain}
By the end of November, the galleys are late. They should have been home by now.

[pause]

At first, nobody is very worried. Galleys are often late. The winds are not always good, and the ships must sometimes wait in harbours along the way. But as the days go by, people begin to worry more. The weather has been bad this autumn. There have been storms on the sea. And there has been no news.

[long-pause]

In the family's house on Murano, nobody talks about it very much. But everyone is thinking about it. Lucia counts the days on her fingers, again and again. Nonna Bettina lights a candle every evening in front of a little picture of the Madonna. Matteo works long hours in the workshop, as if work could make the time go faster. Nicolò is quieter than usual.

[pause]

{img:grandmother-lighting-candle-madonna-icon-evening-quiet-prayer}
Chiara goes up to the altana every day. She looks out across the lagoon toward the open sea. She watches every sail that appears on the horizon. Is that them? No, it is only a fishing boat. Is that them? No, it is a ship from the mainland. Every evening, she comes down slowly, and does not say anything.

[long-pause]

One day, a merchant at the Rialto brings news. He has heard from a ship that came in from the south. The galleys were seen in a harbour on the coast of Greece, two weeks ago. They were sheltering from a storm. They were all safe, at that time. Lucia hurries home across the lagoon to tell the family. Everyone feels a little better. But two weeks is a long time. And there might have been more storms since then.

[pause]

{img:woman-hurrying-home-boat-news-grey-lagoon-afternoon-hope}
In December, the doge orders special prayers in all the churches of Venice for the galleys. The bells ring every evening at the same time. People stop what they are doing and say a prayer. In the Basilica of San Marco, many candles are lit. Families of the sailors come to kneel and pray together.

[long-pause]

The family from Murano rows across to San Marco one evening for the prayers. The great church is full of people, and full of candles. The golden mosaics glow and glitter in the soft light. The priests sing the old prayers in Latin. Chiara kneels between her mother and her grandmother. She looks up at the mosaics of the saints and the angels. She asks them, very quietly, to bring her uncle home safely.

[pause]

{img:candlelit-basilica-families-kneeling-praying-golden-mosaics-december}
On the way home, the boat crosses the dark lagoon. There is no moon, and the stars are hidden by clouds. A cold wind blows from the sea. Matteo rows slowly, without speaking. Nicolò holds the lantern at the front of the boat. Chiara sits close to her grandmother, wrapped in a shawl. Nonna Bettina holds her hand. He will come home, the old woman says quietly. The sea has always given him back before. And it will give him back again.

[pause]

The other families of sailors on Murano visit each other often during these weeks. They share news and rumours, and they comfort each other. The women bring little gifts of food to each other's houses. The children play together, a little more quietly than usual. Everyone is waiting for the same thing. And somehow, waiting together makes it a little easier.

[pause]

Nicolò makes something special during these weeks. He works on it secretly in the evenings, when the workshop is quiet. It is a small glass bowl, blue with white swirls, the best thing he has ever made. He wants to give it to his uncle when he comes home. Every evening, he works on it a little more. And every evening, he says to himself: he will come home, and I will give him this bowl.

[long-pause]

{img:acqua-alta-flooded-venetian-square-water-rising-winter-evening-1300}
In the middle of December, something happens that the Venetians call acqua alta. That means high water.

[pause]

Sometimes, in winter, a strong wind blows up the Adriatic Sea from the south. It pushes the sea water up into the lagoon. At the same time, there is a high tide. Together, the wind and the tide make the water rise much higher than usual. It comes up over the edges of the canals. It flows into the streets and the squares. It comes into the ground floors of the houses.

[long-pause]

On this December day, the wind begins to blow from the south in the morning. It is a warm, damp wind, called the sirocco. The old boatmen look at the sky and shake their heads. High water tonight, they say. Better get ready. All over Venice and Murano, people start to move things upstairs from their ground floors.

[pause]

{img:family-carrying-crates-upstairs-ground-floor-preparing-for-flood-lamplight}
In the family's house, everyone helps. Matteo and Nicolò carry the crates of glass up the stairs, one by one. They carry the sacks of sand and ashes too. Lucia and Chiara carry the firewood and the baskets. Nonna Bettina sits at the top of the stairs and tells them where to put everything. By the afternoon, the ground floor is almost empty.

[long-pause]

In the evening, the water begins to rise. It comes up slowly over the edge of the canal, and spreads across the little street. It creeps under the front door and into the ground floor. It rises a little more, and a little more. The family watches from the upstairs window. The street outside has become part of the canal. Boats are floating where people usually walk.

[pause]

{img:view-from-window-flooded-street-boats-floating-lanterns-night}
Out in the workshop, the furnace is a worry. If the water reaches the fire, it could crack the clay pots full of melted glass. Matteo goes out with Nicolò, wading through the water in his boots. They build a little wall of sandbags around the furnace. The water rises up to the bags and stops. It goes no higher. The furnace stays dry, and the fire keeps burning. Matteo and Nicolò come back inside, soaked but smiling.

[long-pause]

The water stays high for a few hours. Then, slowly, as the tide turns, it begins to go down again. By midnight, it has gone back into the canals. It leaves behind mud and puddles and a strong smell of the sea. But nobody has been hurt, and nothing important has been damaged. The family goes to bed, tired but relieved.

[pause]

{img:morning-after-flood-people-sweeping-mud-street-sunshine-venice}
The next morning, everyone on Murano is out with brooms and buckets. They sweep the mud out of their houses and wash the floors. Neighbours help each other, and they joke about the high water. It happens every few winters, they say. Venice has lived with the water for eight hundred years. It will live with it for eight hundred more. And by the afternoon, the sun is shining, and the streets of Murano are clean again.

[pause]

Some of the old people on Murano remember much worse floods than this one. Nonna Bettina remembers one when she was a girl, when the water came up to the first floor of the houses. People had to row their boats through the church. The fish swam in the campo, she says, and we caught them from the windows. The children do not know if they should believe her. But they love the story.

[pause]

That night, after the water has gone down, Chiara lies awake in bed. She listens to the wind outside, which is calmer now. She thinks about the galleys, out at sea in this same wind. She hopes the storm has not reached them. She holds the little blue bead that Nicolò made, which her mother gave her to keep. And at last, she falls asleep.

[long-pause]

{img:galleys-arriving-venice-december-dusk-crowds-waterfront-torches-joy}
Three days before Christmas, in the afternoon, the bells of San Marco begin to ring. Not the usual bells for prayer, but loud and joyful bells, ringing and ringing. And then the bells of all the churches of Venice join in, one after another.

[pause]

On Murano, Chiara is in the courtyard, fetching water from the well. She hears the bells and stops. She listens. Then she drops the bucket and runs up the stairs to the altana. She looks out across the lagoon. And there, coming slowly in from the open sea, are the galleys. Their sails are grey and patched, and their oars are dipping slowly. One, two, three, four, five. All of them.

[long-pause]

Chiara screams so loudly that the whole street can hear her. They are home! The galleys are home! Lucia runs out of the house. Matteo runs out of the workshop. Nicolò drops a bead he was making, and it breaks on the floor, and he does not even notice. Nonna Bettina sits down on a chair and cries with happiness.

[pause]

{img:family-rowing-fast-across-lagoon-toward-arriving-galleys-excitement}
They all pile into the boat, and Matteo rows across the lagoon as fast as he can. The waterfront near San Marco is crowded with people. Everyone has come to welcome the galleys home. Families are calling out names. People are laughing and crying and hugging each other. The galleys come in to the quay, one by one, and the sailors climb down.

[long-pause]

Chiara stands on tiptoe and looks at every face. Where is he? Where is he? And then she sees him. Uncle Zorzi, thin and brown and bearded, with a sack over his shoulder. He sees them at the same moment, and he waves and shouts. The children run to him through the crowd. He drops his sack and picks them both up at once, one in each arm.

[pause]

{img:sailor-uncle-hugging-niece-nephew-quay-crowd-lanterns-dusk}
That evening, the whole family eats together around the fire on Murano. Zorzi tells them about the voyage. There were terrible storms, he says, and they had to shelter in a harbour in Greece for many weeks. They ran short of food and water. But the captain was wise, and nobody was lost. He thanks God that he is home.

[long-pause]

Then he opens his sack. For Nonna Bettina, there is a soft shawl of Egyptian cotton. For Lucia, a little bag of saffron, the most precious spice of all. For Matteo, a sack of the special ashes for making glass, the very best kind. For Nicolò, a little knife with a handle of carved bone. And for Chiara, there is a small wooden box. She opens it slowly. Inside, there is a little bird made of glass. It is not Venetian glass. It is Egyptian glass, green and gold, made in Alexandria.

[pause]

{img:girl-holding-small-green-gold-glass-bird-firelight-family-smiling}
Chiara holds the little bird up to the firelight. It glows green and gold. It is the most beautiful thing she has ever seen. She will keep it all her life. And on Christmas Eve, the whole family rows across to San Marco for the midnight mass. The great church is full of candles and music. The golden mosaics shine. And Chiara stands between her uncle and her grandmother, holding the little glass bird in her pocket, and sings the Christmas songs as loudly as she can.

[pause]

Nicolò gives his uncle the blue glass bowl with the white swirls. Zorzi holds it up to the firelight and turns it slowly. Did you make this, he asks? Nicolò nods, blushing. Zorzi is quiet for a moment. Then he says it is the most beautiful bowl he has ever seen, in all his travels. He will keep it on the shelf in his little house, he says. And every time he comes home from the sea, he will look at it first.

[pause]

On Christmas Day, the family has a special meal. There is roast capon, a kind of chicken, stuffed with bread and herbs. There is a little saffron rice, golden and fragrant, made with Uncle Zorzi's saffron. There are sweet cakes with honey and almonds and raisins. Everyone eats until they are full. Then they sit by the fire and sing old Christmas songs, while the bells of Murano ring out across the lagoon.

[long-pause]

{img:venice-1400s-later-gothic-palaces-grand-canal-golden-age}
In the centuries after our story, Venice grew even richer and more beautiful. It became one of the greatest cities in Europe.

[pause]

The Venetians built magnificent palaces along the Grand Canal, with pointed windows and marble walls. They rebuilt the doge's palace in pink and white stone. They built new churches and filled them with paintings by great artists. Painters like Bellini, Titian and Tintoretto made Venice famous for its art. The city was full of music, books and festivals.

[long-pause]

Murano went on making glass, better and better. About a hundred and fifty years after our story, a glassmaker called Angelo Barovier discovered how to make perfectly clear glass. It was called cristallo, because it was as clear as crystal. Venetian glass became famous all over the world. Kings and queens bought Venetian mirrors and chandeliers. And Murano is still making glass today, more than seven hundred years later.

[pause]

{img:murano-glassmaker-1500s-clear-cristallo-goblet-elegant-workshop}
The Rialto bridge was rebuilt in stone, about three hundred years after our story. It is a single great arch of white stone, with shops along the top. It is still there today, one of the most famous bridges in the world. Many more bridges were built over the canals too. Today, there are more than four hundred bridges in Venice.

[long-pause]

But slowly, the trade of Venice began to decline. About two hundred years after our story, sailors from Portugal found a new way to India, sailing around Africa. Spices could now come to Europe without passing through Egypt and Venice. And the great new lands across the Atlantic were reached from Spain and Portugal, not from Venice. Little by little, Venice became less rich and less powerful.

[pause]

{img:carnival-venice-1700s-masks-costumes-piazza-san-marco-lanterns}
But Venice became famous for other things. Its carnival became the most famous in Europe. People came from everywhere to wear masks and costumes and dance in the squares. Its theatres and its music were famous too. A composer called Vivaldi wrote beautiful music there, and taught music to the girls of an orphanage. Venice became a city that people visited for pleasure and for beauty.

[long-pause]

The Republic of Venice lasted for more than a thousand years. It came to an end in 1797, when the French general Napoleon came with his army. The last doge took off his corno cap and handed it to his servant. I shall not need this any more, he said. After that, Venice became part of other countries, and later part of Italy.

[pause]

{img:old-venice-quiet-canal-1800s-boatman-evening-faded-palace}
But the city itself stayed. Its palaces and churches, its canals and bridges, its squares and its bell towers. Through all the changes, the Venetians went on living in their city on the water. They went on rowing their boats and fishing in the lagoon. They went on making glass on Murano. And every year, on Ascension Day, they went on remembering the old marriage with the sea.

[pause]

Through all these centuries, the families of Murano went on making glass. Fathers taught sons, and sometimes daughters too. The secrets of the craft were passed down from one generation to the next. Some families have been making glass on Murano for more than five hundred years. Their names are still written above the doors of their workshops today.

[pause]

The lagoon changed slowly too. Some old islands disappeared under the water, and new ones were made. The rivers that flowed into the lagoon were moved, so they would not fill it with mud. The Venetians worked very hard to keep their lagoon healthy. They knew that without it, their city could not survive.

[long-pause]

{img:venice-today-grand-canal-gondolas-vaporetto-palaces-sunny-today}
Today, Venice is one of the most visited cities in the world. Every year, millions of people come to see it.

[pause]

They walk across the stone Rialto bridge and through the busy markets. They visit the golden Basilica of San Marco, and look up at the mosaics. They sit in the cafés of the Piazza San Marco and listen to the musicians. They ride in black gondolas along the canals, as the gondoliers sing. And they get lost in the narrow streets, which is part of the fun.

[long-pause]

The gondolas are all black now. About four hundred years ago, a law was made that all gondolas should be black, to stop people showing off. Today there are only a few hundred gondolas left. Most people travel by water buses, called vaporetti, which chug along the Grand Canal. But the gondoliers still stand at the back of their boats and row with one oar, as they did seven hundred years ago.

[pause]

{img:murano-today-glass-workshop-visitors-watching-glassblower-furnace}
Murano is still the island of glass. There are dozens of glass workshops there today. Visitors can watch the glassblowers at work, in front of their glowing furnaces. The glassmakers still dip their long pipes into the melted glass, and blow, and turn, and shape. In the shops, there are glass vases and bowls, beads and chandeliers, in every colour you can imagine. There is a glass museum too, where you can see glass made hundreds of years ago.

[long-pause]

The Rialto market is still there, beside the Grand Canal. Every morning, the fishermen bring their catch to the fish market. The stalls are full of fish and crabs, prawns and squid. In the vegetable market, there are artichokes and tomatoes from the islands of the lagoon. Venetian families still shop there, just as Lucia and Chiara did.

[pause]

{img:rialto-market-today-fish-stalls-venetian-shoppers-morning-light}
The festival of the Sensa still takes place every year. The mayor of Venice now plays the part of the doge. He goes out to sea in a decorated boat, and throws a golden ring into the water. And acqua alta still comes in the winter. Today, there are great barriers out at sea that can rise up to keep the high water out of the lagoon.

[long-pause]

Venice faces many challenges today. The water is slowly rising, and the city is slowly sinking. There are so many visitors that some Venetians feel crowded out of their own city. But the people of Venice love their city very much. They work hard to protect it, as their ancestors did.

[pause]

{img:venetian-family-today-altana-roof-terrace-evening-lagoon-view}
In the evenings, when the day visitors have gone home, Venice becomes quiet again. The canals are still, and the lamps are reflected in the water. Old people sit outside their houses and talk with their neighbours. Children play in the little campi, around the old stone wells. Families sit on their altane and watch the sun set over the lagoon. And the bells of the churches ring out across the water, just as they did seven hundred years ago.

[pause]

In the quieter parts of Venice, far from the crowds, ordinary life goes on. Women hang their washing on lines strung between the houses, high above the narrow canals. Old men play cards in little bars. Boats deliver fruit and vegetables to the shops. Rubbish is collected by boat, and the post is delivered by boat. Even the ambulances and fire engines in Venice are boats.

[pause]

The festivals of Venice are still celebrated today. Every September, there is a great boat race on the Grand Canal, called the Regata Storica. Before the race, there is a procession of historic boats, with rowers dressed in old costumes. The boats are decorated with gold and silk, like the boats of long ago. Thousands of people watch from the banks and the bridges, cheering their favourite teams.

[long-pause]

{img:murano-evening-1300-sunset-lagoon-furnace-glow-boats-calm}
So we leave Venice tonight, in the year 1300, as the evening falls.

[pause]

The sun is setting behind the mountains of the mainland, far to the west. The lagoon turns gold, then pink, then a deep soft blue. The boats are coming home across the still water, one by one. A fisherman pulls his boat up to a landing and ties it to a striped post. The seagulls settle on the rooftops for the night. And from Murano, the glass furnaces glow orange in the dusk.

[long-pause]

In the family's house, the lamps are being lit. Lucia is closing her account book and putting it away in the chest. Matteo is coming in from the workshop, wiping the soot from his hands. Nicolò is sitting by the window, holding up a little blue bead to the last of the light. Nonna Bettina is folding her knitting and putting it in her basket.

[pause]

{img:venetian-family-evening-room-lamps-glass-window-grandmother-children}
Uncle Zorzi is home, sitting by the fire with his boots off. He is telling Nicolò about the lighthouse of Alexandria, for the third time tonight. Nicolò does not mind. He could listen to his uncle's stories forever.

[long-pause]

Up on the altana, Chiara is sitting with her friend Isabetta. They are wrapped in shawls against the evening chill. Chiara is holding her little glass bird from Egypt. She holds it up to the sky, so the last light shines through it. Green and gold. Isabetta says it is the most beautiful thing she has ever seen. Chiara agrees. They sit together quietly and watch the stars come out over the lagoon.

[pause]

{img:two-girls-rooftop-terrace-dusk-glass-bird-held-to-sky-lagoon-stars}
Across the water, the towers and domes of Venice are dark against the evening sky. The bells of San Marco ring for evening prayer. Then the bells of all the churches answer, near and far, across the lagoon. The sound drifts softly over the water. And then it fades, and everything is quiet.

[long-pause]

Down in the courtyard, Rosso the cat is sitting by the well, washing his face with his paw. A late swallow swoops low over the roof and disappears. In the workshop, the night worker is feeding the furnace with wood. Its warm orange glow spills out through the open door onto the canal. And the water shines softly in the dark, gold and blue.

[pause]

The tide is coming in, gently, filling the canals. The water laps softly against the old brick walls and the wooden posts. A boatman somewhere is singing an old song, slowly, as he rows home in the dark. The lamps in the windows are reflected in the still water. And the whole city on the water settles down to sleep, under the quiet stars.
