In Venice, boats are like carts and carriages in other cities. Everyone travels by boat, all the time.

There are many kinds of boats in Venice. There are big flat barges for carrying heavy goods, like stone and wood and barrels. There are small fishing boats, with nets piled in the bottom. There are fast little boats for carrying messages. And there are the gondolas, long thin boats for carrying people.

--

In 1300, the gondola looks a little different from the gondolas of today. It is not yet painted all black. Rich families paint their gondolas in bright colours, with gold decorations and silk cushions. Some have a little cabin in the middle, with curtains, to keep out the sun and the rain. The boatman stands at the back and rows with one long oar. He leans forward with each stroke, pushing the boat through the water.

{img:colourful-medieval-gondola-silk-cushions-cabin-curtains-noble-family}
Rowing a boat standing up, with only one oar, is a special skill. The oar rests in a carved wooden holder on the side of the boat. The boatman pushes and turns the oar with each stroke, so the boat goes straight. It takes years to learn. Venetian boys start to learn when they are very young. Nicolò can already row the family's boat quite well. Chiara is learning too, though she sometimes goes round in circles.

--

There are no bridges over most of the canals yet in 1300. There are a few bridges, made of wood. But to cross most canals, people use a ferry called a traghetto. It is a boat that goes back and forth across the canal, all day long. You step into it on one side, pay a small coin, and step out on the other side. The traghetto boatmen are proud of their work. They belong to a guild, a kind of club, with its own rules and its own saint.

{img:traghetto-ferry-boat-crossing-canal-passengers-standing-boatman}
Along the canals, there are little landing places with wooden posts in the water. Boats tie up to the posts while people go into houses or shops. The posts are often painted in stripes, in the colours of the family who owns the house. Seaweed and little crabs grow on the bottoms of the posts, where the water laps against them.

--

The boatmen of Venice are famous for their singing. They sing as they row, to keep the rhythm and to pass the time. They sing old songs about love and the sea, about saints and the lagoon. Sometimes, one boatman sings a line, and another boatman answers him from a different canal. The songs echo off the walls of the houses, across the water.

{img:evening-canal-boatman-singing-lantern-boat-reflections-calm-water}
In the evening, Matteo sometimes takes the family out in the boat. They row slowly along the canals of Murano, as the sun goes down. Nicolò rows, and the others sit and enjoy the cool air. They wave to neighbours sitting outside their houses. They listen to the songs of the other boatmen. The water is still and golden. And the lanterns begin to glow in the windows, one by one, reflected in the canal.

Building a boat is a special craft in Venice. There are little boatyards all over the city, called squeri. In a squero, the boat builders work in the open air, beside a canal. They use wood of eight different kinds, each for a different part of the boat. The boats are built by hand, slowly and carefully, without any plans on paper. The builders carry the plans in their heads, learned from their fathers.

On Murano, every family has a boat, just as families in other places have a cart or a horse. Children learn to row as soon as they are strong enough. They learn which canals are deep and which are shallow. They learn how to pass other boats without bumping. And they learn the boatmen's calls, which warn other boats at the corners of the canals. Every Venetian child knows these calls by heart.
