The heart of trade in Venice is a place called the Rialto. It lies in the middle of the city, beside the Grand Canal. The Grand Canal is the biggest canal in Venice. It winds through the city like a great letter S.

In 1300, there is only one bridge across the Grand Canal, and it is here at the Rialto. It is a bridge made of wood, resting on boats. Later it will be rebuilt as a wooden bridge that can open in the middle, to let tall ships pass. And much later still, it will be rebuilt in stone. But in our story, it is a simple wooden bridge, always crowded with people.

--

Around the bridge there is a great market. It is one of the busiest markets in the whole world. There are stalls selling fruit and vegetables, fresh from the islands of the lagoon. There are stalls selling meat and cheese and eggs. And there is a big fish market, where the fishermen sell their catch every morning. The smell of the fish market is very strong. But the fish are fresh and good.

{img:rialto-fish-market-stalls-fishermen-baskets-eels-crabs-morning}
The Rialto is also where the rich merchants meet. They stand in groups under the arcades, talking business. They buy and sell cargoes of pepper and silk, cotton and wool. They lend money to each other for voyages. They talk about the news from faraway ports: the price of pepper in Alexandria, or the weather in Constantinople. A ship's cargo can be bought and sold here many times, before it even arrives in Venice.

--

There are money changers at the Rialto too. They sit at little tables with scales and piles of coins. Merchants come from many lands, with many different kinds of money. The money changers weigh the coins and change them for Venetian coins. The most famous Venetian coin is a gold coin called the ducat. It is trusted all over the world, because its gold is always pure.

{img:money-changer-table-scales-gold-ducats-coins-merchants-arcade}
In a little church near the Rialto, there is an old inscription carved in stone. It asks merchants to be honest. Around this temple, it says, let the merchant's law be just, his weights true, and his contracts fair. The Venetians are proud of their honest trade. Of course, not every merchant is always honest. But most of them try to be, because trust is good for business.

--

Chiara loves the Rialto market. There is so much to see. There are merchants from Germany with long blond beards and fur hats. There are traders from Greece and Turkey, from Egypt and Syria. There are Jewish merchants and Armenian merchants. She hears languages she has never heard before. She sees silks of every colour, and spices piled in little hills, red and yellow and brown.

{img:spice-stall-rialto-pepper-cinnamon-saffron-piles-girl-looking}
One day, a spice seller lets Chiara smell his spices. He holds out a little pot of cinnamon, and then one of cloves, and then one of pepper. The pepper makes her sneeze, and the spice seller laughs. These come from very far away, he tells her. From India, and from islands even further east. They have travelled for many months to reach you. Chiara thinks of her uncle Zorzi, on his galley. She wonders if he is buying spices too, at this very moment, in Alexandria.

Around the market, there are little taverns where people stop to eat and drink. They serve small plates of food, like fried fish, boiled eggs and pieces of bread with sardines. Merchants stand at the counters, eating quickly and talking business. Boatmen come in for a quick glass of wine between jobs. The taverns are noisy and cheerful, full of talk and laughter from morning till night.

At the end of the day, the market slowly empties. The stall holders pack up their goods and sweep the ground. The fishermen wash down their stalls with buckets of lagoon water. Cats come out to look for scraps of fish. The merchants go home to their palaces along the Grand Canal. And the boatmen take the last customers home, as the sun goes down.
