In the year 1300, there is a man living in Venice who has seen more of the world than almost anyone. His name is Marco Polo.

About twenty-five years before our story, Marco Polo left Venice with his father and his uncle. They were merchants, and they travelled east, across Persia and the great deserts of Asia. They travelled for more than three years. And at last they reached China, the land of the great khan, Kublai Khan.

--

Marco Polo stayed in China for about seventeen years. He worked for the great khan, travelling all over his empire. He saw wonderful things that no Venetian had ever seen. He saw great cities with millions of people. He saw paper money, and black stones that burned like wood. He saw palaces with golden roofs and gardens full of strange animals. He saw a postal service with fast horses, and great canals full of boats.

{img:marco-polo-caravan-crossing-asian-desert-camels-sunset-long-journey}
Then, about five years before our story, Marco Polo came home to Venice at last. He was about forty years old. His family hardly recognised him. They say he arrived in ragged clothes, after so many years away. His relatives did not believe it was really him. So he invited them to a great feast. At the end of the feast, he cut open the seams of his ragged coats. Out poured jewels, rubies and emeralds and pearls, onto the table. Then everyone believed him.

--

A little later, Marco Polo was taken prisoner during a war between Venice and Genoa. In prison, he met a writer called Rustichello. To pass the time, Marco told Rustichello all about his travels. Rustichello wrote it all down. That became a famous book, called The Travels of Marco Polo. It was copied by hand many times and read all over Europe. Many people did not believe it. They said it was full of tall tales.

{img:prison-room-two-men-writing-book-candle-marco-polo-rustichello}
Now Marco Polo is home again, living in his family's house near the Rialto. Many people come to visit him, to hear his stories. Some come to laugh at him and call him a liar. People give him a nickname, Il Milione, which means the Million. Some say it is because he talks about millions of everything. Others say it is because his family is very rich.

--

One day in the summer, Uncle Zorzi's friend takes Nicolò and Chiara to hear Marco Polo speak. He is sitting in the courtyard of his house, with a group of people around him. He is a man of about forty-five, with a grey beard and a calm face. He speaks slowly and clearly. He tells them about the great city of Hangzhou, with its lakes and its twelve thousand bridges. He tells them about the great khan's summer palace, made of bamboo.

{img:children-listening-wide-eyes-marco-polo-courtyard-afternoon-shade}
Chiara listens with her mouth open. Twelve thousand bridges! Venice has only a few. She asks Marco Polo a question. Is it true about the black stones that burn? Marco Polo smiles at her. It is true, he says. The Chinese dig them out of the ground and burn them in their stoves. They keep the houses warm all winter. Chiara thinks about this all the way home. She is not sure if she believes it. But she thinks Marco Polo is the most interesting man she has ever met.

Today, of course, we know that most of what Marco Polo said was true. The black stones were coal. His book helped many people in Europe to dream about faraway lands. Two hundred years later, a young sailor called Christopher Columbus read it too. He made notes in the margins of his copy. And it helped to inspire him to sail west, across the ocean.

Marco Polo also brought home some objects from the East. People say he had a little silver tablet from the great khan, which allowed him to travel safely. He had pieces of silk and jewels, and perhaps some strange things too. Some say he brought back the first noodles to Italy, but that is probably only a story. Italians were already eating pasta before he came home.

In the evening after the visit, Chiara tells her family everything. She tells them about the twelve thousand bridges and the black stones and the paper money. Nonna Bettina shakes her head. Paper money, she says. Who would want money made of paper? It would blow away in the wind. Everyone laughs. But Matteo says quietly that Marco Polo is a wise man. And that the world is much bigger than any of us know.
