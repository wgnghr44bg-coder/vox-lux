Today, Venice is one of the most visited cities in the world. Every year, millions of people come to see it.

They walk across the stone Rialto bridge and through the busy markets. They visit the golden Basilica of San Marco, and look up at the mosaics. They sit in the cafés of the Piazza San Marco and listen to the musicians. They ride in black gondolas along the canals, as the gondoliers sing. And they get lost in the narrow streets, which is part of the fun.

--

The gondolas are all black now. About four hundred years ago, a law was made that all gondolas should be black, to stop people showing off. Today there are only a few hundred gondolas left. Most people travel by water buses, called vaporetti, which chug along the Grand Canal. But the gondoliers still stand at the back of their boats and row with one oar, as they did seven hundred years ago.

{img:murano-today-glass-workshop-visitors-watching-glassblower-furnace}
Murano is still the island of glass. There are dozens of glass workshops there today. Visitors can watch the glassblowers at work, in front of their glowing furnaces. The glassmakers still dip their long pipes into the melted glass, and blow, and turn, and shape. In the shops, there are glass vases and bowls, beads and chandeliers, in every colour you can imagine. There is a glass museum too, where you can see glass made hundreds of years ago.

--

The Rialto market is still there, beside the Grand Canal. Every morning, the fishermen bring their catch to the fish market. The stalls are full of fish and crabs, prawns and squid. In the vegetable market, there are artichokes and tomatoes from the islands of the lagoon. Venetian families still shop there, just as Lucia and Chiara did.

{img:rialto-market-today-fish-stalls-venetian-shoppers-morning-light}
The festival of the Sensa still takes place every year. The mayor of Venice now plays the part of the doge. He goes out to sea in a decorated boat, and throws a golden ring into the water. And acqua alta still comes in the winter. Today, there are great barriers out at sea that can rise up to keep the high water out of the lagoon.

--

Venice faces many challenges today. The water is slowly rising, and the city is slowly sinking. There are so many visitors that some Venetians feel crowded out of their own city. But the people of Venice love their city very much. They work hard to protect it, as their ancestors did.

{img:venetian-family-today-altana-roof-terrace-evening-lagoon-view}
In the evenings, when the day visitors have gone home, Venice becomes quiet again. The canals are still, and the lamps are reflected in the water. Old people sit outside their houses and talk with their neighbours. Children play in the little campi, around the old stone wells. Families sit on their altane and watch the sun set over the lagoon. And the bells of the churches ring out across the water, just as they did seven hundred years ago.

In the quieter parts of Venice, far from the crowds, ordinary life goes on. Women hang their washing on lines strung between the houses, high above the narrow canals. Old men play cards in little bars. Boats deliver fruit and vegetables to the shops. Rubbish is collected by boat, and the post is delivered by boat. Even the ambulances and fire engines in Venice are boats.

The festivals of Venice are still celebrated today. Every September, there is a great boat race on the Grand Canal, called the Regata Storica. Before the race, there is a procession of historic boats, with rowers dressed in old costumes. The boats are decorated with gold and silk, like the boats of long ago. Thousands of people watch from the banks and the bridges, cheering their favourite teams.
