Venice is rich because of trade. And the most important trade of all is with the East.

Every year, in spring and summer, fleets of merchant galleys leave Venice. They sail together in groups, called convoys, for safety. Some convoys sail to Constantinople, the great city between Europe and Asia. Some sail to Alexandria in Egypt. Some sail to the ports of Syria and Lebanon. Some even sail west, past Spain, to the ports of England and Flanders.

--

The galleys sail with both oars and sails. When the wind is good, they raise their sails. When the wind drops, or when they enter a harbour, the rowers take up their oars. There are more than a hundred rowers on a big galley. They sit on wooden benches, three to a bench. They are free men, paid for their work. Many of them also carry a little cargo of their own to sell.

{img:galley-rowers-benches-oars-sea-sunshine-sail-above-calm}
In Alexandria, the Venetians buy spices and silk, cotton and sugar. The spices come from India and from islands even further east. They have travelled for months, on ships and on camels, to reach Egypt. There is pepper and cinnamon, nutmeg and cloves, and ginger. In Europe, these spices are very expensive. Only the rich can afford them. They make food taste wonderful, and they are used in medicines too.

--

In return, the Venetians sell wool and cloth, metal and wood. And they sell glass from Murano, which is famous everywhere. The merchants of Alexandria love Venetian glass beads and cups. So Matteo's glass sometimes travels all the way to Egypt, on the same galleys as Uncle Zorzi.

{img:alexandria-harbour-1300-venetian-galley-unloading-spices-camels}
Zorzi has told the children all about Alexandria. It is a great city, he says, with a famous old lighthouse at the entrance to the harbour. There are markets full of things you have never imagined. There are camels and palm trees and people in long white robes. The sun is so hot that you must rest in the shade at midday. And the evenings are warm and full of the smell of spices.

--

When the galleys come home, there is great excitement in Venice. People crowd along the waterfront to watch them arrive. The merchants hurry to the Rialto to buy and sell the new cargoes. The families of the sailors wait at the quays, looking for their loved ones. Bells ring, and people cheer. It is one of the happiest days of the year.

{img:galleys-returning-crowds-waterfront-families-waving-autumn-light}
This year, the convoy to Alexandria is expected home in the autumn, in October or November. Lucia counts the weeks on her fingers. Chiara asks every day if it is time yet. Nonna Bettina says a prayer for the sailors every evening. And Matteo, who does not say much, stands at the canal door sometimes in the evening, looking out toward the open sea.

Before the galleys leave, the families of the sailors come to the waterfront to say goodbye. Wives hug their husbands, and children cling to their fathers' legs. The priests bless the ships and the sailors. Then the oars dip into the water, and the galleys move slowly out into the lagoon. The families wave until the ships are only little dots on the horizon. Then they walk home slowly, already counting the days.

Zorzi is not a rower. He is a sailor who works the sails and the ropes, and he helps to load and unload the cargo. He has a small chest of his own on board, with a few things to trade. Each year, he buys a little cotton or a few spices in Alexandria. He sells them in Venice for a small profit. It is not a fortune, but it helps. And one day, he hopes to buy a share in a ship of his own.
