In the middle of December, something happens that the Venetians call acqua alta. That means high water.

Sometimes, in winter, a strong wind blows up the Adriatic Sea from the south. It pushes the sea water up into the lagoon. At the same time, there is a high tide. Together, the wind and the tide make the water rise much higher than usual. It comes up over the edges of the canals. It flows into the streets and the squares. It comes into the ground floors of the houses.

--

On this December day, the wind begins to blow from the south in the morning. It is a warm, damp wind, called the sirocco. The old boatmen look at the sky and shake their heads. High water tonight, they say. Better get ready. All over Venice and Murano, people start to move things upstairs from their ground floors.

{img:family-carrying-crates-upstairs-ground-floor-preparing-for-flood-lamplight}
In the family's house, everyone helps. Matteo and Nicolò carry the crates of glass up the stairs, one by one. They carry the sacks of sand and ashes too. Lucia and Chiara carry the firewood and the baskets. Nonna Bettina sits at the top of the stairs and tells them where to put everything. By the afternoon, the ground floor is almost empty.

--

In the evening, the water begins to rise. It comes up slowly over the edge of the canal, and spreads across the little street. It creeps under the front door and into the ground floor. It rises a little more, and a little more. The family watches from the upstairs window. The street outside has become part of the canal. Boats are floating where people usually walk.

{img:view-from-window-flooded-street-boats-floating-lanterns-night}
Out in the workshop, the furnace is a worry. If the water reaches the fire, it could crack the clay pots full of melted glass. Matteo goes out with Nicolò, wading through the water in his boots. They build a little wall of sandbags around the furnace. The water rises up to the bags and stops. It goes no higher. The furnace stays dry, and the fire keeps burning. Matteo and Nicolò come back inside, soaked but smiling.

--

The water stays high for a few hours. Then, slowly, as the tide turns, it begins to go down again. By midnight, it has gone back into the canals. It leaves behind mud and puddles and a strong smell of the sea. But nobody has been hurt, and nothing important has been damaged. The family goes to bed, tired but relieved.

{img:morning-after-flood-people-sweeping-mud-street-sunshine-venice}
The next morning, everyone on Murano is out with brooms and buckets. They sweep the mud out of their houses and wash the floors. Neighbours help each other, and they joke about the high water. It happens every few winters, they say. Venice has lived with the water for eight hundred years. It will live with it for eight hundred more. And by the afternoon, the sun is shining, and the streets of Murano are clean again.

Some of the old people on Murano remember much worse floods than this one. Nonna Bettina remembers one when she was a girl, when the water came up to the first floor of the houses. People had to row their boats through the church. The fish swam in the campo, she says, and we caught them from the windows. The children do not know if they should believe her. But they love the story.

That night, after the water has gone down, Chiara lies awake in bed. She listens to the wind outside, which is calmer now. She thinks about the galleys, out at sea in this same wind. She hopes the storm has not reached them. She holds the little blue bead that Nicolò made, which her mother gave her to keep. And at last, she falls asleep.
