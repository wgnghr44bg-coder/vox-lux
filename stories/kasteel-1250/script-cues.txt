Tonight we are going nearly eight hundred years back, to a great castle of red stone in the middle of England, in the year 1250.

[long-pause]

It stands on a low rise in the green Warwickshire countryside, surrounded by a wide lake that the builders made themselves, with tall towers and thick walls, and smoke rising from its kitchens. Inside live a lord and a lady, their children, their knights and their servants, more than a hundred people under one roof.

[pause]

The castle is called Kenilworth. And tonight, we will spend a little time inside it, and see how its people lived.

[pause]

If you like quiet journeys into the past, you are welcome to subscribe to the channel, so that the next one is easy to find.

[long-pause]

Let us imagine England, in the middle of the thirteenth century.

[pause]

It is a green and quiet land, much emptier than today. Perhaps four or five million people live in the whole of England, most of them in small villages, surrounded by great open fields, divided into long strips, and by meadows, and woods, and commons, where the animals graze. There are only a few towns, and they are small. London, the largest, has perhaps fifty thousand people.

[long-pause]

The roads are muddy tracks, through the fields and the forests. People travel on foot, or on horseback, or in heavy wooden carts. A journey from one side of England to the other takes weeks.

[pause]

In the middle of the country, in the county of Warwickshire, there is the great forest of Arden, with its oaks and its glades and its deer. And on the edge of the forest, beside two little streams, there is the castle of Kenilworth.

[pause]

{img:forest-of-arden-oaks-glade-deer-morning-light}
In 1250, it is one of the largest and strongest castles in England. And it is also a home.

[long-pause]

What is a castle?

[pause]

A castle is a fortified home: the house of a great lord, built to be defended. It is a fortress, and a palace, and the centre of a great estate, all at once. From his castle, the lord rules his lands, and the villages around, and the people who live in them. He collects his rents, and holds his courts, and keeps his treasure, and his horses, and his armour.

[pause]

{img:castle-great-hall-lord-holding-court-villagers-waiting}
The first castles in England were built by the Normans, after William the Conqueror invaded England, in 1066, nearly two hundred years before. At first, they were simple: a great mound of earth, called a motte, with a wooden tower on top, and a courtyard below it, surrounded by a ditch and a wooden fence.

[long-pause]

But over the years, the castles were rebuilt in stone, larger and stronger, with high walls and towers, and great gatehouses, and deep moats.

[pause]

By 1250, there are hundreds of castles in England, and in Wales, and in Scotland, and Ireland, and France. Some belong to the King. Some to the great barons, the earls, the most powerful lords of the land. Some to bishops. Some are small, just a tower and a wall. Some, like Kenilworth, are enormous.

[pause]

And every castle is full of life. Because a castle is not just stone. It is people: the lord, and the lady, and their children, and their knights and squires, and the steward, and the clerks, and the cooks, and the bakers, and the brewers, and the grooms, and the huntsmen, and the falconers, and the washerwomen, and the carpenters, and the smiths, and the guards, and the priests, and the guests, and the dogs, and the horses.

[long-pause]

In 1250, Kenilworth belongs to the King of England, Henry the Third. But he has given it to his younger sister, Eleanor, and her husband, one of the most famous men in England: Simon de Montfort, the Earl of Leicester.

[pause]

Their story is one of the great stories of the Middle Ages. And their castle will see, in a few years, some of the most dramatic events in English history.

[pause]

{img:castle-gate-morning-carts-arriving-guards-calm}
But tonight, in the year 1250, the castle is at peace. And we are going inside, to see how its people live, from the lord and lady at their high table, to the boy who turns the spit in the kitchen.

[long-pause]

{img:great-mere-lake-around-castle-reflections-reeds-swans}
The first thing that every visitor notices about Kenilworth is the water.

[pause]

The castle stands on a low hill of red sandstone. And all around it, on the south and the west, there is a great lake, which the people call the mere. It is about a mile long, and wide, and deep, with reeds along its edges, and swans and ducks and herons, and fish swimming in its clear water.

[long-pause]

The lake is not natural. It was made by the builders of the castle, by building great dams, of earth, across the valleys of the two little streams that meet near the castle, so that the water rose up, and spread out, and filled the valleys, all around the castle hill.

[pause]

One of the dams is also the main road into the castle: a long, wide causeway, across the water, leading to a great gatehouse, with towers. It is called the Tiltyard, because later, knights will hold jousting contests on it, riding along its length.

[pause]

So the castle is almost an island. An enemy cannot reach the walls from the south or the west, because of the water. And the water is too deep, and too wide, for siege towers, or for tunnels under the walls. Kenilworth is almost impossible to attack.

[long-pause]

The castle was first built more than a hundred years before, in about 1120, by a man called Geoffrey de Clinton, who was the treasurer of King Henry the First.

[pause]

He built a great square tower, a keep, of red sandstone, on the top of the hill, with walls more than four metres thick. It still stands today. And he founded a priory, a monastery of canons, in the valley nearby, which still gives its name to the town of Kenilworth.

[pause]

{img:massive-square-keep-red-sandstone-thick-walls-corner-towers}
Later, the castle passed to the kings of England. And King John, who reigned from 1199 to 1216, spent a great deal of money on it, more than on almost any other castle in England. He built the outer walls, with their towers, and made the great lake, by building the dams. He loved Kenilworth, and often stayed there.

[long-pause]

After King John died, his son, Henry the Third, became king, as a boy of nine. And in 1244, King Henry gave Kenilworth to his sister Eleanor, and later to her husband, Simon de Montfort.

[pause]

Simon and Eleanor love Kenilworth too. It becomes their favourite home, and their stronghold. They improve it, and make it even stronger, and more comfortable.

[pause]

{img:red-stone-castle-reflected-still-water-summer-evening}
In the evening, when the sun is low, the red sandstone of the castle glows, a deep, warm, rosy colour, and the towers are reflected in the still water of the mere, upside down, among the clouds. It is one of the most beautiful sights in England.

[long-pause]

Let us imagine that we are riding toward Kenilworth, on a summer day, in the year 1250.

[pause]

We come along a track through the fields and the woods, from the town of Warwick, a few miles away. We pass through the little village of Kenilworth, with its houses of timber and thatch, and its church, and the great priory, with its tall church, and its monks, or rather canons, in their black cloaks.

[pause]

And then, as we come over a little rise, we see the castle, across the water: the great keep, standing high above everything, with its four corner towers, and the long walls around it, and the gatehouses, and the towers, and the smoke from the kitchens, and the banners flying from the top of the keep, with the arms of Simon de Montfort: a white lion, standing on its hind legs, with a forked tail, on a red shield.

[long-pause]

We ride along the causeway, across the water, with the lake shining on either side, and the swans gliding past.

[pause]

At the end of the causeway, we come to the gatehouse. The great wooden gates are open, in the daytime, in times of peace. The portcullis, a heavy iron grille, hangs above our heads, ready to be dropped down in a moment, if danger comes. The guards, in their padded coats and iron helmets, look at us, and ask us our business.

[pause]

{img:gatehouse-portcullis-raised-guards-visitors-entering-sunlight}
And then we ride through, into the outer courtyard of the castle, the outer bailey. And we are inside Kenilworth.

[long-pause]

{img:simon-de-montfort-earl-tall-dark-knight-surcoat-lion-portrait}
Who are the lord and lady of Kenilworth?

[pause]

Simon de Montfort is about forty-two years old in 1250. He was born in France, around the year 1208, the youngest son of a famous and fierce French nobleman, also called Simon de Montfort, who led a cruel crusade in the south of France, and was killed when Simon was still a boy.

[long-pause]

The family had a claim, through Simon's grandmother, to the great English earldom of Leicester. Simon's older brother, Amaury, was the heir. But Amaury gave his claim to Simon. And so, in 1230, at the age of about twenty-two, Simon came to England, with almost nothing, a young French knight, speaking French and Latin, but hardly a word of English, to win back his inheritance.

[pause]

He was tall, and handsome, and clever, and charming, and very proud, and very religious, and very ambitious. The young King Henry liked him at once, and gave him the earldom of Leicester, and made him one of his closest friends.

[pause]

Simon is a serious man. He reads a great deal, and talks with learned churchmen, and is a close friend of Robert Grosseteste, the great Bishop of Lincoln, one of the cleverest men in England, and of the Franciscan friar Adam Marsh, who writes him long letters of advice, telling him to control his temper, and to be gentle with his wife.

[long-pause]

He prays a great deal. He often gets up in the night to pray. He wears a hair shirt, under his fine clothes, a rough garment of goat's hair, which scratches the skin, as a penance. He eats and drinks little. And he has a fierce temper, and does not forgive easily.

[pause]

He is also a great soldier, perhaps the best in England. He has fought in France, and in the Holy Land, on crusade. And in 1250, he is far away, much of the time, in Gascony, in the south west of France, which belongs to the King of England, governing it for King Henry, and fighting the rebellious lords there.

[pause]

{img:countess-eleanor-noblewoman-fine-gown-veil-castle-chamber}
His wife is Eleanor, the youngest sister of King Henry. She is about thirty-five, in 1250.

[long-pause]

Eleanor was born in 1215, the youngest child of King John, and his queen, Isabella of Angoulême. When she was only nine, she was married to a great English lord, William Marshal, the Earl of Pembroke, the son of the most famous knight in England. But William died, when she was sixteen.

[pause]

Heartbroken, the young widow swore a solemn vow, before the Archbishop of Canterbury, that she would never marry again, and would live a chaste and holy life, for the rest of her days.

[pause]

{img:young-widow-kneeling-before-archbishop-vow-cathedral-candles}
But then she met Simon de Montfort. And they fell in love. And in January 1238, they were married, quietly, secretly almost, in the King's own little chapel, at the palace of Westminster, with the King himself giving his sister away.

[pause]

There was a great scandal. Because of her vow, many people said the marriage was wrong. The King's brother, Richard, was furious, because he had not been asked. Simon had to go to Rome, to ask the Pope to approve the marriage. But in the end, it was accepted.

[long-pause]

Simon and Eleanor are a devoted couple. They are both proud, and strong-willed, and quick-tempered, and they quarrel sometimes. But they love each other deeply, and they stand together, through everything.

[pause]

Eleanor is a great lady. She lives in splendour, as a king's daughter should. She loves fine clothes, and jewels, and furs, and silk, and spends a great deal of money, and is often in debt. She runs her great household with energy, and authority. She manages the estates, and the money, when Simon is away. She is clever, and determined, and brave.

[pause]

And they have a family. By 1250, they have four or five sons: Henry, who is about twelve, and Simon, about ten, and Amaury, and Guy, and perhaps already little Richard, the youngest. And two years later, in 1252, a daughter will be born, Eleanor, called after her mother.

[long-pause]

The King, Henry the Third, is their brother and their friend. But he is a difficult king.

[pause]

He is gentle, and kind, and very religious, and he loves beautiful things: churches, and paintings, and gold, and jewels. He is rebuilding Westminster Abbey, in London, in the beautiful new Gothic style, as a tribute to Saint Edward the Confessor, whom he loves. But he is not a good ruler. He changes his mind often, and spends too much money, and listens to foreign favourites, and quarrels with his barons.

[pause]

{img:king-henry-third-gentle-crowned-man-westminster-abbey-building}
And he quarrels, sometimes, with Simon, his proud and difficult brother-in-law. They will be friends, and then enemies, and then friends again. And in the end, their quarrel will change the history of England.

[pause]

But that is still in the future. In 1250, Kenilworth is at peace, and its life goes on, day after day, in the rhythm of the seasons.

[long-pause]

{img:castle-inner-courtyard-great-hall-keep-kitchens-busy-morning}
Let us walk through the castle of Kenilworth, and see what it is like inside.

[pause]

The castle has two great courtyards, called baileys. The outer bailey is a large open space, inside the outer walls, with grass, and paths, and many buildings, of timber and stone, leaning against the walls: stables for the horses, and barns, and storehouses, and workshops, and houses for some of the servants, and pens for animals, and a forge, and a well.

[long-pause]

The outer bailey is busy all day long. Horses are led in and out of the stables, and groomed, and fed. Carts arrive, with wood, and hay, and grain, and barrels of wine. The smith hammers at his anvil, mending horseshoes, and armour, and tools. The carpenters saw wood. Children run about. Dogs bark. Chickens scratch in the dust.

[pause]

The outer walls are thick, and high, with a walkway along the top, behind the battlements, the crenellations, the up-and-down tops of the walls, where archers can stand and shoot, and hide behind the stone. At intervals, there are towers, sticking out from the walls, so that the defenders can shoot along the face of the walls.

[pause]

In the walls, there are narrow slits, called arrow loops, for the archers. They are narrow on the outside, and wide on the inside, so that an archer inside can see and shoot in many directions, but an enemy outside can hardly hit him.

[long-pause]

Then, through another gate, there is the inner bailey, at the top of the hill, with the most important buildings.

[pause]

There is the great keep, the oldest part of the castle, a huge square tower, of red sandstone, with walls four metres thick. In 1250, it is used for storing things, and as a last refuge, if the castle is attacked, and perhaps for some private rooms. But the lord and lady no longer live in it. It is too dark, and gloomy, and uncomfortable, with its tiny windows.

[pause]

{img:keep-interior-dim-thick-walls-stores-barrels-small-windows}
Instead, they live in newer buildings, around the inner courtyard, which are more comfortable, with larger windows, and fireplaces.

[long-pause]

The most important of these is the great hall.

[pause]

It is a long, tall building, with a high roof of timber, and large windows, and a great fireplace, or a hearth in the middle of the floor, with smoke rising up to a hole in the roof. At one end, there is a raised platform, called the dais, where the lord and lady sit, at the high table, with their family, and their most important guests. Along the sides, there are long trestle tables, and benches, where everyone else sits.

[pause]

{img:great-hall-timber-roof-dais-high-table-trestles-tapestries}
The great hall is the heart of the castle. Here, everyone eats together, at the great meals of the day. Here, the lord holds his court, and receives visitors, and does justice. Here, there are feasts, and music, and dancing, and storytelling. And at night, some of the servants sleep here, on the floor, wrapped in their cloaks, by the fire.

[long-pause]

Beside the great hall, at the end behind the dais, are the private rooms of the lord and lady, called the chamber, or the solar.

[pause]

These are the most comfortable rooms in the castle. The walls are plastered, and painted, with patterns, and pictures, or hung with tapestries, and painted cloths, to keep out the draughts. There is a fireplace, with a chimney. The windows have wooden shutters, and in the best rooms, a little glass, which is very expensive. There is a great bed, with curtains all around it, and feather mattresses, and linen sheets, and furs.

[pause]

Here, the lord and lady sleep, and dress, and talk privately with their friends and advisers, and do their accounts, and pray, and rest.

[long-pause]

At the other end of the great hall, there is a passage, with three doors. One leads to the buttery, where the drinks are kept, in barrels, wine and ale. One leads to the pantry, where the bread is kept. And the third leads, along a covered passage, to the kitchen.

[pause]

The kitchen is a separate building, of stone, because of the danger of fire. It has huge fireplaces, and ovens, and is always hot, and smoky, and busy.

[pause]

{img:castle-chapel-small-stone-altar-candles-painted-saints}
And there is the chapel, a small, beautiful stone building, with painted walls, and a little altar, and candles, where the castle's priest, the chaplain, says Mass every day for the lord and lady and their household.

[pause]

There are towers, too, with rooms for guests, and for the household officers, and for the children, and for the ladies who serve the countess. There are wardrobes, rooms for storing clothes and cloth and furs and spices and precious things. And there are latrines, called garderobes, little rooms built out from the walls, with a stone seat, and a shaft that drops down into the moat, or the lake, below.

[long-pause]

Everything in the castle is designed for two things: to keep out enemies, and to keep a great household comfortable, and well fed, and in order. And it is the household that we shall meet next.

[long-pause]

{img:castle-guard-night-watch-wall-walk-lantern-stars-mere}
Even in times of peace, a castle is always watched.

[pause]

There is a small garrison at Kenilworth: a constable, a trusted knight, who is in charge of the castle and its defence when the lord is away. And under him, a group of soldiers, sergeants, and watchmen, and archers, and crossbowmen, and the porter, who keeps the gates.

[long-pause]

The porter lives in the gatehouse, in a little room beside the gate, and sees everyone who comes in and goes out. He knows everyone in the household, and asks strangers their business. At night, he closes the great gates, and bars them, with heavy wooden beams, and lowers the portcullis, and keeps the keys.

[pause]

On the walls, the watchmen walk, all night long, along the wall walks, behind the battlements, from tower to tower, with lanterns, looking out over the dark water of the mere, and the fields, and the woods, and listening.

[pause]

{img:watchman-blowing-horn-tower-top-dawn-mist-calm}
They call out the hours of the night, and blow their horns, or pipes, at certain times, to show that they are awake, and that all is well. If they see or hear anything strange, they raise the alarm.

[long-pause]

A great castle must always be ready for a siege.

[pause]

So there are stores, in the keep, and in the cellars: great quantities of grain, and salted meat, and dried fish, and barrels of wine and ale, and firewood, and arrows, and crossbow bolts, and stones, for throwing down from the walls. And there is a well, inside the castle, deep, with fresh water, which will not run dry, even in a long siege.

[pause]

{img:keep-cellar-stores-barrels-grain-sacks-arrows-bundles}
The walls are high and thick, and there are towers, and arrow loops, and the great gatehouse, with its portcullis, and its murder holes, openings in the ceiling of the gate passage, through which things can be dropped on attackers. And above all, at Kenilworth, there is the water of the mere, which keeps enemies far from the walls.

[long-pause]

But in 1250, the guards' life is mostly quiet.

[pause]

They stand at the gate, in the sunshine, and talk to the carters, and the villagers, and the messengers. They practise with their bows and crossbows, at targets, in the outer bailey. They clean their weapons, and their helmets. They play merels, scratched into the stone of the window sills, and dice, in the guardroom, when the constable is not looking.

[pause]

{img:guardroom-soldiers-playing-merels-dice-stone-bench-lamp}
And at night, they walk the walls, under the stars, and look out over the quiet water, and the dark woods, and the few lights of the village, and think of their homes, and their families, and wait for the dawn.

[long-pause]

{img:great-household-gathered-courtyard-officers-servants-livery}
A great lord and lady, in 1250, never live alone. They live surrounded by their household: a great company of people, perhaps a hundred, or a hundred and fifty, or more, who serve them, and travel with them, and live with them, and eat at their table.

[pause]

We know a great deal about the household of Eleanor de Montfort, because one of her household accounts survives, from fifteen years later, in 1265. It is a long roll of parchment, kept by her clerks, day by day, recording everything that was bought, and eaten, and drunk, and spent, and who came to dinner, and how many horses were in the stables, each night.

[long-pause]

The household is run by the steward, an important man, a knight, or a gentleman, who is in charge of everything: the servants, and the food, and the money, and the accounts, and the order of the household. He is like the manager of a great hotel, and a great business, at the same time.

[pause]

Under him, there are other officers, each with his own department.

[pause]

The marshal is in charge of the stables, and the horses, and the grooms, and the carts, and the journeys. The chamberlain looks after the lord's and lady's private rooms, and their clothes, and their beds, and their treasure. The wardrober looks after the wardrobe, the store of cloth, and clothes, and spices, and wax, and precious things.

[long-pause]

The pantler is in charge of the bread, in the pantry. The butler, whose name comes from the bottles, is in charge of the wine and the ale, in the buttery. The cook, or several cooks, in charge of the kitchen. The baker, and the brewer, and the larderer, who looks after the meat, and the poulterer, who looks after the birds.

[pause]

There are clerks, who write the letters, and keep the accounts, in Latin, on parchment. There is the almoner, who gives out food and money to the poor, at the castle gate, every day, in the lord's name. There is the chaplain, who says Mass, and his clerk.

[pause]

{img:almoner-giving-bread-to-poor-castle-gate-morning}
And there are many lesser servants: grooms, and porters, and messengers, and carters, and huntsmen, and falconers, and washerwomen, and pages, and kitchen boys, and boys who turn the spits, and men who carry water, and wood, and clean the latrines.

[long-pause]

Most of the servants in a great household, in 1250, are men and boys.

[pause]

But a great lady has her own women: her ladies, who are well-born, and keep her company, and help her dress, and sew with her, and talk with her, and travel with her. And her maids, who do the work of her chamber. And the nurses, who look after the youngest children. And the laundresses, who wash the linen.

[pause]

{img:ladies-in-waiting-sewing-embroidery-chamber-window-light}
Eleanor's household also includes knights, who serve Simon, and ride with him, and fight for him, and are part of his company. And squires, young men of good families, who are learning to be knights. And pages, boys of good families, sent to the castle to be brought up, and learn manners, and serve at table.

[long-pause]

All the members of the household wear the lord's livery: clothes of the same colour, given to them by the lord, every year, at Christmas, or Whitsun, so that everyone can see whose household they belong to. The knights get fine cloth, and fur. The servants get plainer cloth.

[pause]

They are given their food, every day, at the lord's table, and a place to sleep, and wages, small for the servants, larger for the officers. And they are given gifts, and favours, and protection.

[pause]

In return, they owe the lord and lady their loyalty, and their service. A household is like a great family. Some servants stay with the same lord all their lives, and their children after them.

[long-pause]

Eleanor's household account of 1265 tells us many little things.

[pause]

It tells us that, on an ordinary day, the household might eat a whole ox, or several sheep, and many chickens, and hundreds of eggs, and thousands of herrings, in Lent. That it drank many gallons of wine, and hundreds of gallons of ale, every week. That it burned many pounds of wax candles, and tallow candles. That the countess bought cloth, and furs, and spices, and almonds, and figs, and raisins, and rice, and sugar.

[pause]

{img:medieval-household-roll-parchment-latin-entries-close-calm}
It tells us that there were often forty or fifty horses in the stables, and sometimes many more, when guests came. That messengers were sent, all over England, with letters. That the countess gave alms to the poor, and money to friars, and paid for the shoeing of horses, and the mending of carts.

[long-pause]

And it tells us, quietly, about the life of a great lady, day after day: who came to dinner, and how many people ate in the hall, and what they ate, and where the household travelled.

[pause]

It is one of the most detailed records of daily life that survives from medieval England. And thanks to it, we can imagine, very clearly, the life of the castle of Kenilworth, and its great, busy, hungry household.

[long-pause]

{img:castle-dawn-mist-over-mere-towers-bell-ringing}
Let us spend an ordinary day at Kenilworth, in the summer of 1250.

[pause]

The day begins at dawn, or before. In the chapel, a bell rings. The castle wakes up, slowly, in the grey light, with the mist lying on the water of the mere, and the birds singing in the woods across the lake.

[long-pause]

The servants are the first to get up. They have slept on straw mattresses, or on the floor, in the hall, or in the kitchen, or in the stables, or in little rooms in the towers. They roll up their bedding, and put it away, and wash their faces and hands in cold water, from a basin, or from the well.

[pause]

The fires are lit, or brought back to life, from the embers, in the kitchen, and the hall, and the chambers. The bakers are already at work, in the bakehouse, kneading the dough, and lighting the ovens. The grooms are feeding and watering the horses. The cowherd is milking the cows.

[pause]

The guards on the walls change watch. The porter opens the great gates, and lowers the drawbridge, if there is one, and the castle opens to the world, for another day.

[long-pause]

In their chamber, the lord and lady wake, in their great curtained bed.

[pause]

Their servants bring them warm water, to wash. They dress, with help: in linen undershirts, and long woollen tunics, and over them, surcoats, long sleeveless gowns, of fine wool, or silk, in bright colours, red, and blue, and green. The countess wears a veil, and a wimple, a linen cloth wrapped around her neck and chin, as married ladies do. The earl wears a belt, with a purse, and a knife.

[pause]

{img:noble-lady-dressing-wimple-veil-maids-helping-chamber-morning}
Then they go to the chapel, to hear Mass, said by their chaplain, with their family, and the members of the household who are not working.

[long-pause]

Breakfast, in 1250, is not a proper meal. Most people eat a little bread, and drink a little ale, or wine, when they get up, standing up, or as they work. Children, and the old, and the sick, and the workers who do heavy labour, are given something more.

[pause]

Then the day's work begins.

[pause]

The earl, when he is at home, meets with his steward, and his officers, and hears reports on his lands, and his money, and his household. He receives visitors, and messengers, and reads letters, or has them read to him, and dictates answers to his clerks. He hears the complaints of his tenants, and settles disputes. Or he goes out, riding, to inspect his lands, or to hunt.

[long-pause]

The countess, too, is busy. She meets with the steward, and the wardrober, and checks the accounts, and orders what is needed: food, and wine, and cloth, and candles. She sees to the children, and their lessons. She receives visitors, and writes letters. She gives orders to her ladies, and her servants.

[pause]

And in the quieter hours, she sits with her ladies, in her chamber, by the window, sewing, and embroidering, and talking, and listening to someone read aloud, from a book, a romance, or the life of a saint, in French.

[pause]

{img:ladies-listening-reader-romance-book-chamber-afternoon-light}
Because the language of the castle, in 1250, is French. The lords and ladies of England speak French, as their ancestors did, who came from Normandy with William the Conqueror. The clerks write in Latin. And the servants, and the villagers, speak English. Many people in the castle speak two languages, or three.

[long-pause]

At about ten or eleven o'clock in the morning, a horn is blown, or a bell is rung, and everyone goes to the great hall, for dinner, the main meal of the day.

[pause]

We shall go to dinner, in a moment. It is a long, grand affair, which lasts for an hour or two.

[pause]

After dinner, the afternoon. The work of the castle goes on. The lord and the young men may go hunting, or hawking, or practise with their weapons, in the courtyard. The ladies may walk in the garden, or ride, or play games, or sew. The children have their lessons, or play.

[long-pause]

In the late afternoon, there is supper, a lighter meal, at about five o'clock, in the hall, or, for the lord and lady, in their chamber.

[pause]

And then the evening. In summer, the evenings are long and light. People walk by the lake, and watch the sun go down over the water, and the swallows swooping over the mere. In winter, they gather around the fires, in the hall, and the chamber, and tell stories, and sing, and play games, like chess, and dice, and listen to music, played on the harp, or the fiddle, or the lute.

[pause]

{img:evening-hall-fire-harp-player-storytelling-household-gathered}
And then, as darkness falls, the gates are closed, and the drawbridge raised, and the guards set for the night. The lights go out, one by one. The household lies down to sleep. And the castle is quiet, except for the guards walking on the walls, and the water lapping against the stones, and the owls calling in the woods.

[long-pause]

{img:castle-chamber-winter-evening-fireplace-candles-shutters-closed}
What is it like, to live in a castle, in the winter of 1250?

[pause]

It is cold. Stone walls, four metres thick, stay cold all winter long. The wind blows through the cracks around the shutters, and under the doors, and down the chimneys. The floors are of stone, or of beaten earth, or of wooden boards, covered with rushes, or straw, or rush mats, which are changed now and then, and sprinkled with sweet-smelling herbs, like lavender, and meadowsweet, and mint.

[long-pause]

The only warmth comes from the fires.

[pause]

In the great hall, there is a great fire, which burns all day, in winter, and is banked down at night. In the best chambers, there are fireplaces, with chimneys, which are quite new in 1250, and a great improvement over an open hearth in the middle of the floor, because the smoke goes up the chimney, instead of filling the room.

[pause]

{img:great-hall-central-hearth-smoke-rising-louvre-roof-winter}
The fires burn wood, great logs, from the forest. Huge quantities of firewood are needed, every winter, and the villagers must cart it to the castle, as part of their service to the lord. In some places, charcoal is burned, in braziers, iron baskets on legs, which can be carried from room to room.

[long-pause]

To keep warm, people wear many layers of clothes: linen shirts, and woollen tunics, and surcoats, and cloaks, lined with fur. The lords and ladies wear rich furs: squirrel, and ermine, white with little black tails, and sable, and miniver, the grey and white fur of a Baltic squirrel. Eleanor's accounts show her buying great quantities of furs. Ordinary people wear sheepskin, and rabbit, and cat.

[pause]

They hang tapestries, and painted cloths, on the walls, to keep out the draughts. And they sleep in great beds, with curtains all around, drawn tight at night, to keep in the warmth, under heavy blankets, and furs, and quilts, and often share their beds, with their family, or their servants, for warmth.

[pause]

There are warming pans, too, filled with hot coals, to warm the beds, before people get in.

[long-pause]

And then there is light.

[pause]

In the winter, the days are short. The sun rises late, and sets early, at about four o'clock in the afternoon. And after dark, the castle must be lit.

[pause]

{img:candle-maker-dipping-tallow-wax-candles-workshop}
The best light comes from candles of beeswax, which burn brightly, and cleanly, with a sweet smell. But beeswax is very expensive. It is used in the chapel, and in the chambers of the lord and lady. Eleanor's accounts record great quantities of wax, bought for candles, many pounds every week.

[pause]

The rest of the castle uses tallow candles, made of animal fat, which are cheaper, but smoky, and smelly, and do not give as much light. And rushlights: the stems of rushes, peeled, and dipped in fat, which burn for a little while, with a small flame. And oil lamps, little bowls of oil, with a wick floating in them.

[long-pause]

So, on a winter evening, in the castle, the great hall is lit by the fire, and by torches, and candles, on iron stands, and hanging from the roof, in great iron circles, called candelabra. The light flickers, and shadows move on the walls, and on the tapestries.

[pause]

In the chamber, the lady and her women sit close to the fire, and to the candles, sewing, and talking, and listening to music. The darkness outside is deep, and complete, with no lights anywhere, except the stars, and the moon, and perhaps a few candles in the village, across the water.

[pause]

{img:ladies-sewing-close-to-fire-candles-chamber-winter-night-warm}
And then, after a while, everyone goes to bed, early, to save the candles, and to keep warm. And they sleep a long time, in the winter, nine or ten hours, and wake before dawn, in the darkness, and wait for the light to come back.

[long-pause]

{img:great-hall-dinner-high-table-trestle-tables-servants-dishes}
When the horn sounds for dinner, the household gathers in the great hall.

[pause]

The trestle tables are set up, along the walls, long boards laid on wooden stands, and covered with white linen cloths. The benches are pulled up. At the end of the hall, on the dais, the high table is laid, with the finest cloth, and the best cups and dishes, and a great salt cellar, of silver, in the middle.

[long-pause]

Everyone has a place, according to their rank.

[pause]

At the high table sit the lord and lady, in the middle, and beside them, their most important guests: a bishop, perhaps, or an abbot, or another earl, or a knight of high rank, and their older children. Below the dais, at the long tables, sit the knights, and the officers of the household, and the clerks, and the squires, and then the lesser servants, further down the hall. Where you sit tells everyone how important you are.

[pause]

Before the meal, servants come round with basins, and jugs of warm water, sometimes scented with herbs, and towels, and everyone washes their hands. This is important, because they will eat with their fingers.

[long-pause]

Then the chaplain says grace, a prayer of blessing, in Latin. And the meal begins.

[pause]

The servants come in procession, from the kitchen, carrying the dishes, held high, led by the steward, or the marshal of the hall, with his white rod of office. Sometimes, on feast days, the procession is announced by trumpets.

[pause]

{img:procession-servants-carrying-dishes-steward-white-rod-hall}
At the high table, there are special servants. The carver, a young squire of good family, kneels before the lord, and carves the meat, with great skill, and courtesy, and serves it to the lord and lady. The cupbearer, another young man, serves the wine, kneeling. These are honourable jobs, and the sons of great families do them, as part of their training.

[long-pause]

There are no plates, as we know them. Instead, each person has a trencher: a thick slice of stale bread, four days old, cut square, on which the food is put. The trencher soaks up the juices and the sauces. At the end of the meal, the trenchers, full of gravy, are not eaten by the diners, but are collected, and given to the poor, at the castle gate, or to the dogs.

[pause]

People share. Two people eat from one dish, and drink from one cup. A young man is expected to cut the meat for the lady beside him, and offer her the best pieces.

[pause]

Everyone has their own knife, which they carry on their belt, and use to cut their food, and spear it. There are spoons, for soups and sauces. But there are no forks. Forks will not be used in England for another three hundred years. People eat with their fingers, carefully, using only three fingers of the right hand, and wiping them on the tablecloth, or a napkin, between dishes.

[long-pause]

What do they eat?

[pause]

A great dinner has two or three courses, and each course has many dishes, all put on the table at once, so that people can choose what they like.

[pause]

{img:roast-meats-pies-birds-dishes-high-table-feast-colour}
There is meat: beef, and mutton, and pork, and venison, from the forest, roasted, or boiled, or baked in pies. There are birds: chickens, and capons, and geese, and ducks, and pigeons, and partridges, and herons, and swans, and peacocks, on great feast days. There are stews, and pottages, thick soups of meat and vegetables and herbs. There are sauces, of herbs, and vinegar, and mustard, and spices.

[long-pause]

The spices are very important. They come from far away, from India, and the Spice Islands, along the trade routes, through the Middle East, and Venice, and are very expensive: pepper, and ginger, and cinnamon, and cloves, and nutmeg, and saffron, which colours food a beautiful golden yellow. Great lords use them generously, to show how rich they are. Eleanor's accounts show her buying pounds of pepper, and ginger, and cinnamon, and saffron.

[pause]

There is sugar too, which is very rare and costly, and is kept with the spices, and used like a spice, a little at a time. And almonds, which are ground and soaked to make almond milk, used in many dishes, especially on fast days.

[pause]

And always, there is bread: fine white bread, called manchet, for the high table, and coarser brown bread, for the lower tables. And to drink, wine, from Gascony, in the south of France, for the lord and the knights, and ale, brewed in the castle, for everyone else.

[long-pause]

On many days of the year, people are not allowed to eat meat. On Fridays, and often on Wednesdays and Saturdays, and all through Lent, the forty days before Easter, and on the eves of great feasts. These are fast days. On these days, they eat fish.

[pause]

Fresh fish from the mere, and the castle's fish ponds: pike, and bream, and perch, and eels. And salted and dried fish, brought from the sea: herrings, by the thousand, and cod, and stockfish, dried so hard that it must be beaten with a hammer and soaked for days. On a fast day, the household of a great lady might eat thousands of herrings.

[pause]

{img:fish-day-table-pike-eels-herrings-bread-lent}
At the end of the meal, there might be fruit, apples and pears, from the orchard, and cheese, and wafers, thin, crisp little cakes, made with honey, and spiced wine, called hippocras.

[long-pause]

Then the tables are cleared, and the cloths removed, and grace is said, and everyone washes their hands again.

[pause]

And the leftovers, all the food that was not eaten, and the trenchers, soaked in gravy, are gathered up, and given to the almoner, who takes them to the gate, where the poor of the village are waiting.

[long-pause]

{img:castle-kitchen-huge-fireplaces-spits-cauldrons-cooks-steam}
To feed a household of a hundred or more people, twice a day, every day, the kitchen of Kenilworth must be a busy place.

[pause]

The kitchen is a large building of stone, separate from the hall, or joined to it by a covered passage, so that if it catches fire, as kitchens often do, the fire will not spread. It has a high roof, with openings to let out the smoke and the heat, and great fireplaces, big enough for a man to stand in, and ovens, and a well, or a water supply.

[long-pause]

It is always hot, and smoky, and noisy, and crowded, from before dawn until after supper.

[pause]

The master cook is in charge, an important man, with a great wooden spoon, which he uses to taste the food, and to keep the kitchen boys in order. Under him, there are other cooks, and helpers, and scullions, who wash the pots and pans, and boys who fetch water, and wood, and do all the hardest and dirtiest jobs.

[pause]

In the great fireplaces, meat is roasted on spits, long iron rods, turned slowly in front of the fire, by boys, who sit beside the fire, for hours, turning, turning, in the terrible heat, sometimes protected by a screen of wet straw. Later, in some kitchens, dogs will turn the spits, running in a wheel.

[long-pause]

Great iron and bronze cauldrons hang over the fires, on chains, and hooks, boiling meat, and stews, and soups. Sometimes, several things are cooked in one cauldron, at the same time: a piece of meat in the water, and puddings tied in cloths, and eggs, and vegetables in earthenware jars, all together.

[pause]

In the ovens, bread is baked, and pies. Medieval pies are not like ours. The pastry, called the coffin, is thick and hard, and is made to hold the filling while it cooks, and to carry it, and keep it. Often, it is not eaten at all.

[pause]

{img:pastry-cook-making-large-pies-coffins-oven-paddle}
There are tables, for chopping, and pounding, and mixing. Great mortars and pestles, of stone or bronze, for grinding spices, and almonds, and herbs, and breadcrumbs, to make sauces. Sieves, and strainers, and ladles, and knives, and cleavers, and skimmers, and graters.

[long-pause]

Near the kitchen, there are other rooms.

[pause]

The larder, where meat is kept, salted, or hanging, in the cool. The scullery, for washing. The pastry, where pies and pastries are made. The bakehouse, with its great ovens. The brewhouse, where ale is brewed, from barley malt and water, in great vats. The dairy, where milk is made into butter and cheese.

[pause]

{img:brewhouse-vats-malt-steam-brewer-stirring-wooden-paddle}
Outside, there is a garden, with herbs, and vegetables: parsley, and sage, and mint, and thyme, and fennel, and onions, and leeks, and garlic, and cabbages, and peas, and beans. And there are pens for live animals: pigs, and sheep, and hens, and geese, waiting to be cooked. And dovecotes, full of pigeons.

[long-pause]

Where does all the food come from?

[pause]

Most of it comes from the lord's own lands, the manors, all over England, which send their produce to the castle: grain, and cattle, and sheep, and pigs, and hens, and eggs, and cheese. Other things are bought at markets, and fairs: wine, and fish, and spices, and salt, and cloth. The countess's accounts show her clerks buying things at the market in Warwick, and at the great fairs, and in London.

[pause]

In the autumn, many animals are killed, because there is not enough food to keep them all through the winter. Their meat is salted, or smoked, and stored, to last until the spring.

[long-pause]

The cooks of 1250 do not have recipe books, mostly. They learn by watching, and doing, from older cooks. But a few years later, cookery books will begin to be written, in French and English, with recipes for the great dishes of the time.

[pause]

Blancmange, for example, which is not a pudding, but a dish of chicken, and rice, and almond milk, and sugar, all white. And frumenty, a thick porridge of wheat boiled in milk, with eggs and saffron, which is served with venison. And mortrews, a smooth, thick dish of pounded meat, or fish, with breadcrumbs and spices.

[pause]

{img:cook-tasting-pot-wooden-spoon-steam-kitchen-evening}
At the end of the day, when supper is over, and the last dishes have been washed, the kitchen is quiet at last. The fires are banked down, covered with ashes, to keep them alive until the morning. And the kitchen boys lie down to sleep, on the warm floor, by the fire.

[long-pause]

{img:squires-training-courtyard-wooden-swords-quintain-horses}
In the outer courtyard of Kenilworth, in the afternoon, the young men are training.

[pause]

There are knights, grown men, who serve the earl, and live in his household. And there are squires, young men of fifteen or sixteen or eighteen, who are learning to be knights. And pages, boys of seven or eight, or older, the sons of noble families, who have been sent to the castle to be brought up, and to learn the ways of the nobility.

[long-pause]

A boy of a noble family, in 1250, does not usually grow up at home. At about seven, he is sent to live in the household of a great lord, often a relative, or a friend of his family, as a page.

[pause]

As a page, he learns good manners, and courtesy: how to serve at table, and carve meat, and pour wine, and speak to ladies, and to lords, and to bishops, and how to behave in the hall, and in church. He learns to ride, and to look after horses, and dogs, and hawks. He learns to sing, and dance, and play chess, and perhaps a little reading, in French, and Latin.

[pause]

And he learns to fight, with little wooden swords, and shields, and practises wrestling, and running, and jumping, and swimming.

[long-pause]

At about fourteen, he becomes a squire. Now, he serves a knight, as his assistant.

[pause]

He looks after his knight's horses, and armour, and weapons. He helps him to put on his armour, which is very heavy, and complicated, and cannot be done alone. He rides with him, and carries his shield, and his lance. He goes with him to tournaments, and to war, and looks after him if he is wounded.

[pause]

{img:squire-helping-knight-into-chain-mail-hauberk-armoury}
And he trains, every day, for hours: riding, and fighting on horseback, with a lance, and on foot, with a sword, and a shield, and a mace, and an axe.

[long-pause]

In 1250, a knight's armour is made of mail: thousands of little iron rings, linked together, like a heavy, flexible cloth.

[pause]

A knight wears a long shirt of mail, called a hauberk, down to his knees, with long sleeves, and mittens of mail, and a hood of mail, over his head. And mail stockings, called chausses, on his legs. Under the mail, he wears a thick padded coat, of linen and wool, stuffed with cotton, or rags, called a gambeson, to soften the blows. Over the mail, he wears a long cloth surcoat, without sleeves, with his coat of arms on it, so that people can see who he is.

[pause]

On his head, over the mail hood, he wears a helmet of iron: in 1250, often a great helm, a flat-topped cylinder of iron, covering his whole head, with narrow slits to see through, and little holes to breathe through. It is hot, and heavy, and hard to see out of. But it protects him well.

[long-pause]

The most important thing for a knight is his horse.

[pause]

A knight has several horses. A great warhorse, called a destrier, big, and strong, and trained for battle, very expensive, which he rides only in war, and tournaments. A riding horse, a palfrey, gentle and comfortable, for travelling. And packhorses, for his baggage. The squires look after them all.

[pause]

{img:destrier-warhorse-groom-brushing-stable-sunlight}
The young men practise riding at the quintain: a wooden post, with a crossbar that turns, with a shield at one end, and a heavy bag of sand at the other. The rider gallops at it, and strikes the shield with his lance. If he is not quick enough, the crossbar swings round, and the sandbag hits him on the back of the head, and knocks him off his horse, and everyone laughs.

[long-pause]

At about twenty-one, if he has done well, the squire is made a knight.

[pause]

There is a ceremony, often at a great feast, like Whitsun, or Christmas, or a royal wedding. The night before, the young man takes a bath, to make himself clean, and spends the night in the chapel, praying, watching over his armour, which lies on the altar. In the morning, he hears Mass.

[pause]

Then, in front of the household, and the guests, his lord, or another knight, gives him his sword, and his spurs, and dubs him, with a blow on the neck, or the shoulder, with the hand, or the flat of the sword, and says: be a good knight. And then there is a feast.

[long-pause]

Knights, in the stories of the time, are supposed to be brave, and loyal, and generous, and courteous, and to protect the weak, and serve God, and their lord, and honour ladies. This is called chivalry.

[pause]

Of course, real knights are not always like that. Many are rough, and violent, and greedy. But the ideals are real, and many young men try to live up to them. And they listen, in the evenings, in the hall, to the stories of the knights of King Arthur, and of Roland, and of the great heroes, and dream of being like them.

[pause]

{img:minstrel-reciting-arthur-romance-hall-young-knights-listening}
Simon de Montfort himself is admired, all over Europe, as a model of a knight: brave, and pious, and skilled, and honourable. Young men come from far away to serve in his household, and learn from him.

[long-pause]

{img:castle-blacksmith-forge-anvil-hammer-sparks-courtyard}
A great castle is like a little town, with its own craftsmen, who keep everything working.

[pause]

The most important is the smith. His forge stands in the outer courtyard, with a great hearth, and bellows, worked by a boy, and an anvil, and hammers, and tongs. All day long, the sound of his hammer rings across the courtyard.

[long-pause]

He makes and mends everything of iron: horseshoes, and nails, for the castle's many horses. Hinges, and locks, and keys, and bolts, for the doors and the gates. Tools, for the carpenters, and the masons, and the gardeners. Pots, and spits, and hooks, and chains, for the kitchen. Ploughshares, and sickles, and scythes, for the farms.

[pause]

And sometimes, he mends armour, and weapons, for the knights, although the finest armour, the mail shirts and the helmets, is made by specialist armourers, in the towns, and is very expensive. A mail shirt is made of perhaps thirty thousand little iron rings, each one riveted shut, by hand. It takes months to make.

[pause]

{img:armourer-linking-mail-rings-workbench-careful-light}
There is the farrier, who shoes the horses, and looks after their feet, and treats them when they are sick. There is the saddler, who makes and mends saddles, and bridles, and harness. There is the bowyer, who makes bows, and the fletcher, who makes arrows, with goose feathers.

[long-pause]

There are the carpenters, who make and mend everything of wood: the roofs, and the floors, and the doors, and the shutters, and the tables, and the benches, and the beds, and the chests, and the carts, and the barrels, and the scaffolding for the masons.

[pause]

And there are the masons, who look after the stone walls, and repair them, and build new buildings. A castle is never finished. There is always something being built, or repaired, or improved.

[pause]

{img:masons-repairing-castle-wall-scaffold-ropes-pulley-summer}
There is the plumber, whose name comes from plumbum, the Latin word for lead, who works with lead: making the lead sheets for the roofs, and the pipes for water, and the gutters.

[long-pause]

There is the glazier, who makes the windows, when there is glass, which is still rare and precious in 1250. He sets little pieces of glass, greenish and full of bubbles, into strips of lead, to make windows. Most windows in the castle have no glass at all, only wooden shutters, or oiled cloth, or thin sheets of horn, which let in a little light.

[pause]

There are the tailors, and the furriers, who make the clothes of the household, from the cloth and the furs bought by the wardrober. And the laundresses, who wash the linen, the sheets, and the tablecloths, and the shirts, in great tubs, with water from the well, and lye, made from wood ash, and spread them on the grass, or on hedges, to dry in the sun.

[pause]

{img:laundresses-washing-linen-tubs-drying-on-hedges-sunny}
Without all these people, and their skills, and their hard work, the great castle could not live, and the lord and lady could not live in it. They are the quiet, busy heart of Kenilworth.

[long-pause]

{img:noble-children-playing-castle-garden-ball-hoop-summer}
What is life like for the children of Kenilworth, in 1250?

[pause]

The children of the earl and countess, Henry, and Simon, and Amaury, and Guy, and the youngest, are brought up in the castle, with their nurses, and their tutors, and the other children of the household.

[long-pause]

When they are babies, they are looked after by a nurse, a wet nurse, who feeds them, because great ladies do not usually feed their own babies. The babies are wrapped tightly in long strips of linen, called swaddling, so that they cannot move their arms and legs, because people believe this will help them grow straight. They sleep in wooden cradles, which can be rocked.

[pause]

When they are a little older, they play.

[pause]

They play with toys: little wooden horses, and knights, made of tin or wood, and dolls, and balls, and hoops, and tops, and windmills on sticks, and whistles. They play games: hide and seek, and blind man's buff, and leapfrog, and hopscotch. They play in the gardens, and the courtyards, and along the shore of the mere, and they fish, and swim, in the summer, and skate on the ice, in winter, with skates made of bones.

[long-pause]

The boys of noble families learn to ride as soon as they can sit on a pony. They learn to use a little bow, and arrows, and to throw a spear, and to fight with wooden swords.

[pause]

And they learn to read. The sons of Simon de Montfort are educated very carefully, because their father is a learned man, and believes in education. They learn to read and write in Latin, and French. Simon even sends his older sons to be taught by his friend, Robert Grosseteste, the great bishop of Lincoln, in his household, one of the greatest scholars of the age.

[pause]

{img:boys-learning-latin-tutor-book-wax-tablets-castle-room}
Grosseteste writes to Simon, praising his sons, and saying that they are doing well in their lessons, and in good manners.

[long-pause]

Girls are brought up mostly by their mothers, and the ladies of the household.

[pause]

They learn to sew, and embroider, and spin, and weave. They learn to run a household, and to manage servants, and accounts. They learn good manners, and how to dress, and how to behave at table, and in the hall. Many learn to read, in French, and some in Latin, so that they can read their prayer books. They learn to ride, and to hawk, and to dance, and to sing, and to play the harp.

[pause]

{img:young-girl-learning-embroidery-mother-ladies-window}
The little daughter of Simon and Eleanor, also called Eleanor, will be born in 1252. She will grow up to marry the Prince of Wales, Llywelyn ap Gruffudd, and become Princess of Wales.

[long-pause]

The children of the servants grow up in the castle too.

[pause]

They help their parents, in the kitchen, and the stables, and the laundry, and the gardens, from a young age. They fetch water, and wood, and feed the chickens, and scare the birds away from the crops, and turn the spits, and run errands. They play in the courtyards, when they can, with the other children.

[pause]

Childhood, in 1250, is shorter than today. At twelve or fourteen, a child is almost an adult, and works like an adult. Girls of noble families are often married at fourteen or fifteen, and boys a little older. But the young are loved, and cared for, and many letters, and poems, and stories, from the Middle Ages, show how much parents loved their children, and grieved when they were ill.

[long-pause]

In the evening, in the chamber of the countess, the children gather around their mother, by the fire, and listen to stories: stories from the Bible, and the lives of the saints, and the old romances, of knights and dragons and enchanted forests, and fairies, and the great deeds of their own ancestors.

[pause]

And they say their prayers, and are put to bed, in their little beds, in their own chamber, with their nurses, and the curtains drawn around them, against the cold.

[pause]

{img:children-asleep-curtained-bed-nurse-candle-castle-night}
Outside, the wind blows across the mere, and the guards walk on the walls. But the children of Kenilworth sleep, warm and safe, behind the thick red walls of the castle.

[long-pause]

{img:castle-chapel-mass-priest-altar-candles-family-kneeling}
Religion is part of every hour of life in the castle, in 1250.

[pause]

Every morning, the lord and lady hear Mass, in the chapel, with their household. They pray at night, and in the morning, and before and after meals. They give alms to the poor, every day. They keep the fast days, and the feast days. They go on pilgrimages, to the shrines of the saints.

[long-pause]

The year is shaped by the great feasts of the Church. And the greatest of all, in the castle, is Christmas.

[pause]

Christmas, in 1250, is not one day, but twelve days, from Christmas Day, on the twenty-fifth of December, to Twelfth Night, on the sixth of January. Before it, there are the four weeks of Advent, a time of fasting, and preparation, with no meat, and quiet.

[pause]

In the days before Christmas, the castle is busy. The servants bring in great loads of holly, and ivy, and bay, and green branches, from the woods, to decorate the hall, and the chapel. A great log, the Yule log, is dragged in, to burn in the hall fire, for the twelve days. The cooks prepare enormous quantities of food. Guests arrive, with their servants, and their horses, from all over the country.

[long-pause]

On Christmas Eve, everyone goes to the chapel, for midnight Mass, by candlelight.

[pause]

And then, on Christmas Day, the feast begins. The hall is full of people, and light, and noise, and the smell of roasting meat. There is a great dinner: roast boar, with its head carried in on a great platter, decorated with rosemary and bay, with an apple in its mouth, while everyone sings. And roast geese, and swans, and capons, and venison, and pies, and frumenty, and wine, and ale, and sweet spiced cakes.

[pause]

{img:boars-head-carried-in-platter-feast-hall-singing-candles}
The lord gives gifts to his household: new clothes, the livery for the year, and money, and presents. And the poor of the village are invited to the castle, and given food, and drink, and alms.

[long-pause]

During the twelve days, there is no work, except what is necessary. There is feasting, and music, and dancing, and singing of carols, round dances, with everyone holding hands, in a ring, singing. There are games, and riddles, and plays, and mummers, people in masks and costumes, who come to the hall and perform, and dance, and act out stories, without speaking.

[pause]

On Twelfth Night, there is the last great feast. A bean is baked into a cake, and whoever finds the bean in his slice becomes the King of the Bean, and rules over the feast, and everyone must obey his silly commands.

[pause]

{img:mummers-masks-costumes-dancing-great-hall-firelight}
Then Christmas is over, and the guests go home, and the hall is cleaned, and the green branches are taken down and burned, and the castle goes back to its ordinary life, in the cold, dark days of January.

[long-pause]

There are other great feasts, through the year.

[pause]

Candlemas, at the beginning of February, when candles are blessed in the chapel. Easter, the greatest feast of the Church, after the long fast of Lent, with eggs, which were forbidden in Lent, and new clothes, and feasting. Whitsun, in early summer, when knights are often made, and there are tournaments. Midsummer, with bonfires, on the night of Saint John. Lammas, at the beginning of August, the feast of the first bread from the new harvest. Michaelmas, at the end of September, when rents are paid, and accounts are made up, and geese are eaten.

[pause]

And the feast days of the saints, many of them, each with its own customs, and its own special foods, and prayers.

[long-pause]

Simon de Montfort is a very religious man. He goes to Mass often, and prays at night. He is a friend of the friars, the Franciscans and the Dominicans, who are new in England in these years, and who preach to the people, and live in poverty. He gives money to churches, and hospitals, and to the poor.

[pause]

And he has a great devotion to the pilgrimage. Before he married, he went on crusade, to the Holy Land, to Jerusalem. And all his life, he will remember it.

[pause]

{img:knight-praying-alone-chapel-night-candle-hair-shirt}
At Kenilworth, in the evening, after supper, when the household has gone to bed, he sometimes goes to the chapel alone, and kneels there, for a long time, in the candlelight, praying, while the castle sleeps around him.

[long-pause]

{img:minstrel-harp-fiddle-playing-great-hall-evening-listeners}
In the long evenings, in the castle, there is music, and stories, and games.

[pause]

A great household has its own minstrels: musicians, who play for the lord and lady, at dinner, and in the evenings, and at feasts. And wandering minstrels come to the castle too, travelling from one great house to another, with their instruments on their backs, hoping for a meal, and a bed, and a gift of money.

[long-pause]

The instruments of 1250 are many.

[pause]

The harp, small, and light, held on the knee, or against the shoulder, with strings of gut, or metal. The fiddle, called the vielle, played with a bow, held against the chest, or the shoulder. The psaltery, a flat wooden box with strings, plucked with the fingers, or a quill. The lute, newly arrived from the Arab lands, through Spain. Pipes, and flutes, and recorders. Bagpipes. Little drums, called tabors, played with one hand, while the other plays a pipe. Bells, and cymbals, and trumpets, for great occasions.

[pause]

{img:medieval-instruments-vielle-psaltery-harp-table-chamber}
The minstrels play dances, for the lords and ladies to dance to: round dances, and carols, with everyone holding hands, in a circle, or a long line, singing as they dance. And they sing songs, love songs, and ballads, in French, and sometimes in English.

[long-pause]

And they tell stories, long stories, in verse, sung or chanted to the music of the harp: the romances.

[pause]

The stories of King Arthur, and his queen, Guinevere, and the knights of the Round Table, Lancelot, and Gawain, and Tristan and Isolde, and the quest for the Holy Grail. The stories of Charlemagne, and his knights, Roland and Oliver. The stories of Alexander the Great, and of Troy, and of the crusades. Some of these stories take many evenings to tell, a little each night.

[pause]

One of the most famous songs of the time is in English, and is still sung today: Sumer is icumen in, lhude sing cuccu. Summer is coming in, loudly sing, cuckoo. It was written down, in about 1250, at the abbey of Reading. It is one of the oldest songs in English that we can still sing.

[long-pause]

There are games too.

[pause]

Chess is the great game of the nobility. The pieces are carved from ivory, or bone, or wood, or walrus tusk: the king, and the queen, and the bishops, and the knights, and the rooks, and the pawns. Lords and ladies play chess together, in the chamber, by the fire, for hours. Knowing how to play chess well is a mark of good breeding.

[pause]

{img:lord-and-lady-playing-chess-ivory-pieces-fireside-chamber}
There are other board games: tables, which is like backgammon, played with dice, and counters. And merels, a game of lines and counters, like noughts and crosses, but more complicated, which is often scratched into stone benches, or window sills, so that guards, and servants, can play it while they wait.

[long-pause]

People play dice, too, and gamble, although the Church disapproves. Playing cards will not come to Europe for more than a hundred years. But they play riddles, and word games, and blind man's buff, and hot cockles, a game where one person kneels with their eyes covered, and the others hit them, gently, on the hand, and they must guess who did it.

[pause]

And on summer evenings, they play outside, in the courtyards, and the meadows: bowls, and quoits, and ball games, and archery contests.

[pause]

{img:summer-evening-meadow-bowls-archery-household-relaxing-castle}
And sometimes, there are visitors who bring entertainment: jugglers, and acrobats, and tumblers, and men with dancing bears, and performing dogs, and fools, and jesters, who tell jokes, and make everyone laugh, even the stern earl.

[long-pause]

{img:hunting-party-riding-out-castle-hounds-horns-autumn-forest}
The favourite pastime of the lords and ladies of 1250 is hunting.

[pause]

Around Kenilworth, there is the great forest of Arden, and the lord's own hunting park, called the Chase, an area of woods and open land, surrounded by a fence or a bank, where deer are kept for hunting. Nobody else may hunt there, on pain of heavy fines.

[long-pause]

A great hunt is a grand occasion. The lord, and his guests, and the young knights and squires, ride out from the castle in the early morning, with the huntsmen, and the dogs, and the horns.

[pause]

The dogs are very important, and much loved. There are greyhounds, tall and fast, which chase the deer by sight. And scenting hounds, which follow the deer by smell, and bay loudly when they find the trail. And big, strong dogs, mastiffs, and alaunts, for hunting the wild boar. The dogs live in kennels, in the castle, looked after by the keepers of the hounds, and are fed on bread, and meat.

[pause]

The huntsmen go ahead, in the early morning, to find where the deer are. Then the hounds are released, and follow the scent, and the riders follow the hounds, galloping through the woods, and across the open land, blowing their horns, with the hounds baying, in a great noise and excitement.

[long-pause]

Hunting is not only a sport. It is also a way of getting meat, venison, for the castle table, which is the finest meat of all, reserved for the nobility. And it is training for war, teaching young men to ride hard, through rough country, and to be brave, and quick.

[pause]

Ladies hunt too. They ride with the men, or watch from a stand, in a clearing, while the deer are driven past, and shoot at them with bows and arrows.

[pause]

{img:noble-lady-on-horse-falcon-on-glove-meadow-river-morning}
But the favourite hunting of ladies is hawking: hunting with falcons and hawks.

[long-pause]

Hawking is one of the great passions of the medieval nobility.

[pause]

The hawks and falcons are caught wild, as young birds, or taken from their nests, and trained, with great patience, by the falconers, to hunt, and to come back to the falconer's glove. It takes months, and great skill.

[pause]

{img:falconer-training-hawk-lure-mews-castle-quiet-afternoon}
The birds are kept in a special building, called the mews, and are treated like precious treasures. They wear little leather hoods over their heads, to keep them calm, and little bells on their legs, so that they can be found, and leather straps, called jesses, on their legs, which the falconer holds.

[long-pause]

Later, a famous book of hunting will even give a list of which bird suits each rank of person. A king flies a gyrfalcon, the largest and most beautiful of the falcons, from the far north. An earl flies a peregrine falcon, the fastest bird in the world, which dives on its prey from high in the sky. A lady flies a merlin, a small, quick falcon. A yeoman flies a goshawk. A priest flies a sparrowhawk.

[pause]

The lords and ladies carry their hawks on their gloved left hands, everywhere: when they ride, when they visit, even sometimes in church, or at dinner. A fine hawk is a sign of rank, and a great gift.

[pause]

When they go hawking, they ride out into the open country, along the rivers, and the meadows, and the marshes, around the mere, where there are ducks, and herons, and partridges. When a bird is flushed, the falconer takes off the hawk's hood, and throws it into the air, and it flies up, and up, and dives on its prey. Then it is called back, with a lure, a bundle of feathers on a string, and given a reward of meat.

[long-pause]

Simon de Montfort loves hunting, and hawking, and so do his sons. And Eleanor's accounts record the care of the hawks, and the dogs, and the horses, and the payments to the falconers, and the huntsmen.

[pause]

There is also fishing, in the mere, and the ponds, with nets, and lines. The mere is full of fish, which are an important part of the castle's food, especially on fast days. The castle has its own fishermen, with little boats.

[pause]

{img:small-boat-fishermen-nets-mere-castle-reflection-dawn}
In the evening, the hunters come home, tired, and muddy, and happy, with the deer slung across a horse, and the hounds trotting behind, and the horns blowing, to tell the castle that they are coming. And that night, in the hall, there is venison for dinner, and long stories of the day's hunt.

[long-pause]

{img:manor-village-open-fields-strips-ploughing-oxen-castle-distance}
A castle like Kenilworth does not stand alone. All around it, there are the lands that feed it, and the people who work them.

[pause]

In 1250, most of England is divided into manors. A manor is an estate, belonging to a lord, with a village, and fields, and meadows, and woods. The lord owns the land. The villagers live on it, and farm it, and in return, they work for the lord, and pay him rents, in money, and in produce.

[long-pause]

Around Kenilworth, there are villages, with houses of timber, and wattle and daub, woven sticks covered with mud and straw, and thatched roofs. Each house has a little garden, called a croft, with vegetables, and herbs, and fruit trees, and a pig, and some hens.

[pause]

Around the village, there are the great open fields, two or three of them, each divided into long, narrow strips. Each family has strips scattered in each field, some good land, some bad. One field is left fallow, unplanted, each year, to rest, and the animals graze on it, and manure it.

[pause]

The villagers grow wheat, and barley, and oats, and rye, and peas, and beans. They keep cattle, and oxen, for ploughing, and sheep, for wool, and pigs, which run in the woods, eating acorns. There are meadows, by the streams, where hay is cut in June, for the animals in winter.

[long-pause]

Many of the villagers are serfs, also called villeins, who are not free. They belong to the manor. They cannot leave it without the lord's permission, or marry their daughters without paying a fee, and they must work on the lord's own land, called the demesne, for several days a week, ploughing, and sowing, and harvesting, and carting, before they can work on their own strips.

[pause]

Others are free tenants, who pay rent in money, and owe less work.

[pause]

{img:reeve-supervising-villeins-lord-demesne-field-morning}
The lord's steward, and his bailiff, and the reeve, a villager chosen by the others, manage the manor, and make sure the work is done, and the rents are paid, and keep accounts, written on parchment, by a clerk, and sent to the castle, every year.

[long-pause]

The castle needs a great deal from its manors.

[pause]

Grain, for bread and ale. Cattle, and sheep, and pigs, for meat. Hay, and oats, for the horses. Wood, for the fires, and for building. Eggs, and cheese, and butter. Carts, and men, to carry things. And money, from the rents, to buy wine, and spices, and cloth, and to pay the servants.

[pause]

{img:manor-court-hall-villagers-steward-fines-clerk-writing}
The lord also holds courts, in the manors, and at the castle, called manor courts, where disputes between villagers are settled, and fines are paid, for breaking the customs of the manor: for letting a pig into a neighbour's corn, or for brewing bad ale, or for not doing your work on the lord's land, or for a quarrel.

[long-pause]

The people of the castle and the people of the village are close neighbours, and depend on each other.

[pause]

Many of the castle's servants come from the villages. The villagers sell their eggs, and chickens, and vegetables, to the castle. They come to the castle gate, for alms, in hard times. They come to the castle courts, for justice. They watch the lord ride out, to hunt, and the lady pass by, on her palfrey, with her ladies.

[pause]

And on great feast days, at Christmas, and Easter, and Whitsun, the villagers are sometimes invited to the castle, to eat in the hall, and share in the feast.

[long-pause]

In the village of Kenilworth, there is also the great priory, of the Augustinian canons, founded by Geoffrey de Clinton, a hundred and thirty years before. The canons are priests, who live together, like monks, and pray, and serve the churches around.

[pause]

The priory is rich, and has its own lands, and its own tenants. Its church is large and beautiful, and the bells ring out across the fields, at the hours of prayer, morning and evening, so that the villagers, working in the fields, know the time of day.

[pause]

{img:priory-church-bells-fields-villagers-pausing-evening-light}
And when the bells ring for evening prayers, the villagers stop their work, and stand, for a moment, in the fields, and say a prayer. And then they go home, along the lanes, with their tools on their shoulders, to their supper, and their families, and their fires, in the shadow of the great red castle.

[long-pause]

{img:noble-household-travelling-road-carts-horses-baggage-train}
A great lord and lady, in 1250, do not stay in one place all year.

[pause]

They have many castles, and manors, all over England, and sometimes in France too. Simon and Eleanor have Kenilworth, and Leicester, and Odiham, in Hampshire, which is Eleanor's own castle, and many manors. And so, every few weeks, or months, the whole household packs up, and moves, from one place to another.

[long-pause]

There are good reasons for this.

[pause]

The lord needs to visit his lands, to see that they are well run, and to hold his courts, and to be seen by his tenants. The food of one manor runs out, after a few weeks of feeding a great household, so the household moves on, to another, where the barns are full. And after a few weeks, a castle becomes dirty, and needs to be cleaned, especially the latrines.

[pause]

{img:servants-packing-chests-tapestries-bedding-castle-chamber}
And so, the great household is always on the move. Eleanor's accounts show her household moving often, from one castle to another, staying a few weeks in each place.

[long-pause]

Moving a great household is a huge undertaking.

[pause]

Everything must be packed: the beds, and the bedding, and the curtains, and the tapestries, and the clothes, and the furs, and the silver and gold plate, and the chapel things, the altar cloths and the chalice and the books, and the kitchen pots and pans, and the accounts, and the money, in locked chests, and the spices, and the wine.

[pause]

Everything is loaded into great carts, with four wheels, pulled by teams of horses, and onto packhorses. Dozens of carts and horses. The marshal of the household is in charge, and organizes everything.

[long-pause]

The journey is slow. A great household might travel fifteen or twenty miles a day, along the muddy roads, through the fields and the forests, and across the rivers, by fords, and bridges, and ferries.

[pause]

The lord and the knights ride on their horses. The countess rides a palfrey, a gentle horse, or travels in a wagon, a heavy, covered carriage, with cushions, and curtains, painted and decorated, which is very uncomfortable on the rough roads. The children ride, or travel with their nurses. The servants walk, or ride in the carts.

[pause]

{img:covered-wagon-carriage-lady-curtains-road-forest-riders}
Ahead of the household, the harbingers ride, to the next stopping place, to make sure that there is food, and lodging, and stabling for the horses.

[long-pause]

At night, they stay at a castle, or a manor, belonging to the lord, or a friend, or at a monastery, where travellers are always welcome, or at an inn, in a town.

[pause]

On the way, people come out of the villages to watch the great household pass by: the banners, and the horses, and the knights, and the carts, and the lady in her carriage, and the hounds, and the hawks on their perches, and the servants, and the cooks, and the long line of baggage, stretching back along the road for half a mile.

[pause]

{img:villagers-watching-procession-pass-banners-knights-lane}
The beggars come too, and the almoner gives them money, and bread, from the lord.

[long-pause]

And when the household arrives at the next castle, everything is unpacked, and set up again: the beds, and the tapestries, and the chapel, and the kitchen. And within a day or two, the castle is full of life again, and the household goes on, as if it had never moved.

[pause]

In winter, the travelling is harder, because the roads are deep in mud, or frozen, or flooded. So the household often stays longer in one place, in winter. And Kenilworth, with its great hall, and its comfortable chambers, and its lake full of fish, and its forest full of deer, is one of the favourite places to spend the winter.

[long-pause]

{img:tournament-field-knights-banners-pavilions-spectators-stands}
One of the great excitements of a knight's life, in 1250, is the tournament.

[pause]

A tournament, in this time, is not the elegant jousting contest of later centuries, with two knights riding at each other along a wooden barrier. In 1250, a tournament is more like a mock battle, a great rough game, fought over a wide area of open countryside, between two teams of knights, sometimes hundreds on each side.

[long-pause]

The knights gather, from all over the country, and sometimes from abroad, at an agreed place, in the open fields, between two towns. They set up their tents, and pavilions, of coloured cloth, with their banners flying. Crowds come to watch: ladies, and townspeople, and villagers, and merchants, selling food, and drink, and horses, and armour.

[pause]

On the day of the tournament, the two teams line up, facing each other, across the fields, in their armour, on their great warhorses, with their coats of arms on their shields, and their surcoats, and their horses' trappings, in bright colours.

[pause]

{img:knights-lined-up-colourful-surcoats-shields-morning-field}
At a signal, the two sides charge at each other, with their lances, and then fight with swords, and maces, on horseback, and on foot, all over the fields, through the hedges, and the woods, and the villages, for hours, until evening.

[long-pause]

The aim is not to kill, but to capture. A knight who is captured, or unhorsed, must pay a ransom to the knight who captured him, or give him his horse and his armour. So a skilled knight can become rich, by winning tournaments. The most famous knight of all, William Marshal, Eleanor's first father-in-law, was said to have captured five hundred knights in tournaments, in his youth, and become rich and famous that way.

[pause]

But tournaments are dangerous. Knights are often hurt, and sometimes lose their lives, in the confusion, and the crowd.

[pause]

The Church disapproves of tournaments, and forbids them, but nobody takes much notice. The kings of England sometimes forbid them too, because they are afraid that the great lords will use them as an excuse to gather their armies, and plot against the king. King Henry the Third often forbids them. But the young knights love them, and hold them anyway.

[long-pause]

In the evening, after the tournament, there is a great feast, and the heralds announce the names of the knights who have fought best, and prizes are given, sometimes by the ladies.

[pause]

The heralds are very important at tournaments. They are experts on coats of arms, and can recognize every knight by his shield, even when his face is hidden by his helmet. They keep lists of the coats of arms of all the knights in England, called rolls of arms. Some of these rolls, from the thirteenth century, still survive, with little painted shields, in rows, and the names of the knights beside them.

[pause]

{img:roll-of-arms-painted-shields-rows-parchment-herald}
Coats of arms began in the twelfth century, as a way of recognizing knights in battle, and at tournaments, when their faces were covered by their helmets. By 1250, every noble family has its own coat of arms, which is passed down from father to son, and is painted on shields, and banners, and surcoats, and seals, and windows, and furniture. Simon de Montfort's arms, as we have seen, are a white lion on a red shield, with a forked tail.

[long-pause]

At Kenilworth itself, a few years later, in 1279, there will be a famous tournament, called the Round Table, held by a great lord, Roger Mortimer, in imitation of the knights of King Arthur. A hundred knights, and a hundred ladies, will come, and there will be jousting, and feasting, and dancing, for several days, in the great castle, in honour of the old stories of Camelot.

[pause]

{img:castle-feast-hall-knights-ladies-dancing-round-table-candles}
Because the stories of King Arthur, and his knights of the Round Table, are the most popular stories of the age. Everyone knows them, and loves them, and every knight wants to be like Lancelot, or Gawain, or Percival.

[long-pause]

{img:barons-council-oxford-1258-great-hall-debate-king-seated}
The peace of Kenilworth, in 1250, does not last.

[pause]

In the years that follow, King Henry the Third quarrels more and more with his barons. He spends too much money, on foreign wars, and on his favourites, his relatives from France, and on his great plans. He asks for more and more taxes. He does not listen to the advice of his barons. And many people in England feel that he is ruling badly.

[long-pause]

In 1258, the barons meet at Oxford, and force the King to agree to a set of reforms, called the Provisions of Oxford. The King must rule with the advice of a council of barons, and a parliament must meet three times a year, to discuss the affairs of the kingdom.

[pause]

The leader of the reformers, more and more, is Simon de Montfort, the King's own brother-in-law.

[pause]

Simon believes, deeply, that the King must rule according to the law, and with the consent of the great men of the kingdom, and that he must not do whatever he likes. He swears an oath to keep the Provisions, and he will not break it, whatever happens. He is a man of strong principles, and a terrible stubbornness.

[long-pause]

The King breaks his promises, and the quarrel grows, year by year, until, in 1264, it becomes a civil war: the King and his supporters, against Simon and the barons who support reform. This is called the Second Barons' War.

[pause]

In May 1264, at the town of Lewes, in Sussex, Simon's army defeats the King's army, and captures the King, and his son, Prince Edward. For more than a year, Simon de Montfort rules England, in the King's name.

[pause]

{img:parliament-westminster-1265-knights-burgesses-gathered-hall}
And in January 1265, he calls a parliament, at Westminster, which is one of the most famous in English history. For the first time, he invites not only the great barons, and the bishops, but also two knights from every county, and two citizens, called burgesses, from many of the towns, to come and speak for their communities. Many historians see this as the beginning of the House of Commons, and of the English Parliament as we know it.

[long-pause]

During this time, Eleanor is at Odiham, and then at Dover, and Kenilworth is one of the great strongholds of the Montfort family, held for them by their son, the younger Simon.

[pause]

But in the summer of 1265, Prince Edward escapes from captivity, and gathers an army, and turns against Simon.

[pause]

{img:summer-evening-castle-towers-mere-quiet-anxious-1265}
On the fourth of August, 1265, at Evesham, in Worcestershire, Prince Edward's army meets Simon's army, in a summer storm, with thunder, and darkness, in the middle of the day. We will not watch the battle. It is enough to know that Simon de Montfort, the Earl of Leicester, lost his life there, with his eldest son, Henry, and many of his loyal knights.

[long-pause]

Simon was about fifty-seven years old.

[pause]

The common people of England had loved him. They saw him as their champion, who had stood up for the law, and for the rights of the people, against a bad king. After his death, many people believed that he was a saint, and went on pilgrimage to the place where he fell, at Evesham, and told stories of miracles there.

[pause]

Today, at Evesham, there is a simple memorial stone, in the ruins of the abbey, where his remains were buried. And in the Houses of Parliament, in London, there is a statue of him, among the great men who shaped the English Parliament.

[long-pause]

Eleanor was at Dover when she heard the news. She was grief-stricken. She dressed in mourning for the rest of her life.

[pause]

Soon afterward, she left England, and went to France, to a convent of Dominican nuns, at Montargis, south of Paris, which Simon's family had founded. And she lived there, quietly, for ten more years, and passed away there, in 1275, at the age of about sixty.

[pause]

{img:french-convent-garden-cloister-nun-widow-quiet-afternoon}
But at Kenilworth, the story was not over yet. Because the castle, the great red fortress on its lake, refused to surrender.

[long-pause]

{img:kenilworth-surrounded-distant-camps-tents-across-mere-1266}
After Evesham, most of Simon de Montfort's supporters surrendered, or fled. But not the men of Kenilworth.

[pause]

The castle was held by a group of Simon's loyal followers, knights and soldiers, who had lost their lands, and had nothing left to lose. They refused to surrender. And so, in June 1266, King Henry himself came to Kenilworth, with a great army, to besiege it.

[long-pause]

It was the longest siege in English history, before the end of the Middle Ages. It lasted for six months, from June until December, 1266.

[pause]

We will watch it gently, and from a distance, as if we were standing on a hill above the mere, far away, looking down at the castle on its island, and the great camp of the King's army, spread out around it, with its tents and banners.

[pause]

The King's army brought great siege engines: huge catapults, called trebuchets, which threw heavy stones at the walls. And siege towers, tall wooden towers on wheels. But the great mere protected the castle. The siege towers could not reach the walls across the water. The King even sent for barges, all the way from Chester, to try to cross the lake. But the defenders were too strong.

[long-pause]

The defenders, too, had their own engines, inside the castle, and threw stones back, and it is said that sometimes the stones crashed together in the air, between the walls and the camp.

[pause]

There is a story that, at one point, the defenders opened the gates, and left them open, all day, as if to say: come in, if you dare. And nobody did.

[pause]

{img:castle-gates-open-defiant-quiet-afternoon-causeway-water}
Inside the castle, the defenders held out, month after month, through the summer, and the autumn. But slowly, their food ran out. And illness came, in the crowded castle.

[long-pause]

Meanwhile, outside, the King and his advisers were looking for a way to end the war, and bring peace to England.

[pause]

In October 1266, a group of bishops and barons, meeting in the King's camp, at Kenilworth, drew up a peace settlement, which became known as the Dictum of Kenilworth. It said that the rebels could have their lands back, if they paid a fine, a large sum of money, depending on how deeply they had been involved in the rebellion. It was a way of forgiving, and of healing the wounds of the war.

[pause]

{img:bishops-writing-peace-terms-tent-parchment-seals-autumn}
At first, the defenders of Kenilworth refused. But by December, they were starving, and many were ill. And at last, on the fourteenth of December, 1266, they agreed to surrender, on the terms of the Dictum, and were allowed to leave the castle freely, with their horses and their arms.

[long-pause]

They walked out of the castle, along the causeway, across the water, weak, and thin, and tired, in the cold winter air. And the King's men went in.

[pause]

The great siege was over. And with it, the Barons' War.

[pause]

The King gave Kenilworth to his younger son, Edmund, the Earl of Lancaster. And from then on, for hundreds of years, it would belong to the great House of Lancaster.

[long-pause]

Simon de Montfort lost his life, and his cause seemed to have failed. But his ideas did not die.

[pause]

The young Prince Edward, who had defeated him at Evesham, became King Edward the First, in 1272. And he, too, called parliaments, with knights from the counties, and burgesses from the towns, as Simon had done. Over the centuries, Parliament grew stronger and stronger, until, in the end, it became the true ruler of England.

[pause]

{img:parliament-old-hall-westminster-later-centuries-members-seated}
So, in a way, the quarrels, and the ideas, of the proud earl of Kenilworth, helped to shape the government of England, and of many other countries, all over the world, to this day.

[long-pause]

{img:john-of-gaunt-great-hall-tall-windows-kenilworth-fourteenth-century}
After the siege, Kenilworth was repaired, and became one of the greatest castles of the House of Lancaster.

[pause]

At the end of the fourteenth century, about a hundred and twenty years after the siege, it belonged to one of the richest and most powerful men in England, John of Gaunt, Duke of Lancaster, the son of King Edward the Third.

[long-pause]

John of Gaunt turned Kenilworth from a fortress into a palace. He built a magnificent new great hall, on the site of the old one, of red sandstone, with huge, tall windows, filled with tracery, delicate patterns of stone, and glass, and great fireplaces, and a high roof. It was one of the finest halls in England, a place of feasting, and splendour.

[pause]

His son became King Henry the Fourth. And Kenilworth became a royal castle, and the kings of England stayed there often, and loved it, and added to it. King Henry the Fifth built a little banqueting house, a summer house, on the far side of the mere, called the Pleasance in the Marsh, where he could go by boat, across the water, to eat, and rest, and enjoy the view.

[pause]

{img:boat-crossing-mere-to-summer-pleasure-house-evening-lanterns}
And then, in the sixteenth century, Kenilworth was given by Queen Elizabeth the First to her favourite, Robert Dudley, the Earl of Leicester, the same title that Simon de Montfort had held, three hundred years before.

[long-pause]

Robert Dudley spent a fortune on Kenilworth. He built new buildings, with enormous windows, full of glass, so that, at night, with all the candles lit, the castle shone across the lake like a lantern. He built a new gatehouse. And he made a beautiful garden, in the Elizabethan style, with flower beds, and fountains, and an aviary, full of birds, and a terrace, from which to look at the view.

[pause]

And in July 1575, Queen Elizabeth came to stay with him, at Kenilworth, for nineteen days.

[pause]

It was one of the most famous royal visits in English history. Dudley entertained the Queen with feasts, and plays, and music, and fireworks over the lake, and hunting, and bear-baiting, and pageants on the water, with a floating island, and the Lady of the Lake, and a mermaid, and a dolphin, with musicians hidden inside it.

[pause]

Some people believe that a young boy from a nearby town, Stratford-upon-Avon, about eleven years old, may have come to watch some of the entertainments. His name was William Shakespeare. But we do not know for certain.

[long-pause]

Then, in the seventeenth century, England had another civil war, between King Charles the First and Parliament.

[pause]

After the war, in 1649, Parliament ordered that Kenilworth should be slighted: damaged, so that it could never again be used as a fortress. The soldiers blew up one side of the great keep, and broke down the walls, and the dams were opened, and the water of the great mere was drained away, so that the castle stood no longer on an island, but in green fields.

[pause]

{img:drained-mere-meadows-ruined-castle-seventeenth-century-quiet}
Some of the buildings, like Dudley's gatehouse, and the stables, were turned into houses, and lived in. But the rest of the castle slowly became a ruin.

[long-pause]

In the nineteenth century, the ruins became famous again, through a novel, Kenilworth, by Sir Walter Scott, published in 1821, about Robert Dudley, and Queen Elizabeth, and the great visit of 1575. Thousands of people came to see the ruins, and they became one of the most visited places in England.

[pause]

Artists painted them, like J. M. W. Turner. Writers wrote about them, like Charles Dickens. And people came by train, and later by car, to picnic in the meadows, and walk among the great red walls.

[pause]

{img:victorian-visitors-picnic-castle-ruins-meadow-parasols}
And so the castle of Simon and Eleanor, after nearly nine hundred years, is still there.

[long-pause]

{img:kenilworth-castle-ruins-red-sandstone-green-lawns-today}
Today, Kenilworth Castle stands on its low hill, in the green Warwickshire countryside, a great ruin of red sandstone, looked after by English Heritage.

[pause]

The little town of Kenilworth has grown up around it, with houses, and shops, and pubs, and a school, and the ruins of the old priory, in a park, near the church. But the castle still rises above everything, as it did in 1250.

[long-pause]

You come to the castle along a quiet road, and walk in through the gatehouse of Robert Dudley, which is still standing, with its little turrets, and its rooms furnished as they were in later centuries, when it was a house.

[pause]

And then you are in the outer courtyard, on the green grass, and before you, the great red walls of the castle rise up: the keep, with one side blown away, standing like a great broken tooth against the sky. The tall windows of John of Gaunt's great hall, empty, with the sky shining through them. And Dudley's tower, with its huge windows, rising high above the walls.

[pause]

You can walk all around the walls, and climb up into some of the towers, by new wooden staircases, and look out, over the fields, where the great mere used to be, and imagine the water, shining all around, and the swans, and the boats.

[long-pause]

In the keep, the oldest part of the castle, built by Geoffrey de Clinton, more than nine hundred years ago, you can still see the thick walls, and the little windows, and the marks where the floors once were.

[pause]

In the great hall of John of Gaunt, you can stand on the grass, where the floor once was, and look up at the tall windows, and the great fireplaces, high up in the walls, and imagine the feasts, the long tables, and the music, and the firelight.

[pause]

{img:great-hall-ruin-tall-tracery-windows-sky-grass-floor}
And in Dudley's building, a new staircase, with viewing platforms, takes you up, to the level of the floors where Queen Elizabeth's rooms once were, so that you can stand where she stood, and look out through her windows, over the garden, and the countryside.

[long-pause]

The Elizabethan garden has been recreated, by English Heritage, as it was in 1575, using a detailed description written by a man who saw it, at the time of the Queen's visit.

[pause]

It is a beautiful, formal garden, with paths of sand, and low hedges, and flower beds, with flowers that grew in Elizabethan times, and four tall obelisks, and a white marble fountain, with figures of the sea, and a great wooden aviary, painted, full of brightly coloured birds, singing, and a terrace, from which you can look down on the garden, as the Queen did.

[pause]

In the stables, which were built by Dudley, there is a café, and an exhibition about the history of the castle, from Geoffrey de Clinton, to the siege of 1266, to the Queen's visit, to the present day.

[long-pause]

And the land where the great mere once lay is now green meadows, with cows, and sheep, grazing, and paths for walking, and a little stream running through.

[pause]

In the winter, after heavy rain, the meadows sometimes flood, and for a few days, the water spreads out again, around the castle, and the red walls are reflected in it once more, as they were in the days of Simon and Eleanor.

[pause]

{img:flooded-meadows-castle-reflection-winter-sunset-still}
Along the old causeway, the dam of King John, now a road and a path, people walk their dogs, and look across at the castle, where, eight hundred years ago, the King's army camped, during the long siege.

[long-pause]

In the summer, there are events at the castle: knights in armour, jousting, and falconry displays, with falcons and hawks swooping over the grass, and medieval music, and plays, and children dressed as knights and princesses, running about on the lawns, with wooden swords.

[pause]

And in the quiet evenings, when the visitors have gone, and the sun is low, the red sandstone of the castle glows, deep and warm, as it did in 1250, and the swallows swoop over the meadows, where the lake used to be, and the rooks call from the trees.

[pause]

It is very peaceful.

[long-pause]

So we leave the castle of Kenilworth for tonight, with the red walls glowing in the evening light, and the meadows quiet where the great lake once lay.

[pause]

The lord and lady at their high table, the cook with his great wooden spoon, the squires at the quintain, and the children asleep behind the curtains of their bed, nearly eight hundred years ago.

[pause]
