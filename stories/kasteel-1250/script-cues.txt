Tonight we are going nearly eight hundred years back, to a great castle of red stone in the middle of England, in the year 1250.

[long-pause]

The castle stands on a low hill in green Warwickshire. All around it lies a wide lake, made by its builders. It has tall towers and thick walls, and smoke rises from its kitchens. Inside live a lord and a lady with their children. Their knights and servants live there too. More than a hundred people share this one home.

[pause]

The castle is called Kenilworth. Tonight we will spend a little time inside it, and see how its people lived.

[pause]

If you like quiet journeys into the past, you are welcome to subscribe to the channel. Then the next one is easy to find.

[long-pause]

Let us picture England in the middle of the thirteenth century.

[pause]

It is a green and quiet land, much emptier than today. Perhaps four or five million people live in all of England. Most of them live in small villages, with big open fields around them. There are only a few towns, and they are small. Even London has only about fifty thousand people.

[long-pause]

The roads are muddy tracks through the fields and forests. People walk, or ride a horse, or sit in a heavy wooden cart. A trip across England takes weeks.

[pause]

In the middle of the country lies the great forest of Arden, with its oaks and its deer. And at the edge of the forest, beside two little streams, stands Kenilworth.

[pause]

{img:forest-of-arden-oaks-glade-deer-morning-light}
In 1250, it is one of the largest and strongest castles in England. But it is also a home.

[long-pause]

So what is a castle, really?

[pause]

A castle is a home that can be defended. It is the house of a great lord. It is a fortress and a palace at the same time. From his castle, the lord rules his lands and the villages around them. He collects his rents there, and he judges quarrels there, and he keeps his horses and his armour safe.

[pause]

{img:castle-great-hall-lord-holding-court-villagers-waiting}
The first castles in England were built by the Normans, after William the Conqueror came across the sea in 1066. At first they were simple. There was a big mound of earth with a wooden tower on top. Below it was a yard, with a ditch and a wooden fence around it.

[long-pause]

Over the years, the castles were rebuilt in stone. They grew bigger and stronger, with high walls and strong gates.

[pause]

By 1250 there are hundreds of castles. Some belong to the King. Some belong to the barons, the most powerful lords in the land. Some are just one tower and a wall. Some, like Kenilworth, are enormous.

[pause]

And every castle is full of life. A castle is not just stone. It is people. There is the lord and the lady, and their knights and their cooks. There are grooms for the horses and guards on the walls. And there are the dogs, who go everywhere.

[long-pause]

In 1250, Kenilworth belongs to the King of England, Henry the Third. But he has given it to his younger sister, Eleanor, and to her husband. Her husband is one of the most famous men in England. His name is Simon de Montfort, and he is the Earl of Leicester.

[pause]

Their story is one of the great stories of the Middle Ages. And in a few years, their castle will face a test that no one can yet imagine.

[pause]

{img:castle-gate-morning-carts-arriving-guards-calm}
But tonight, in the year 1250, the castle is at peace. So let us go inside. We will meet everyone, from the lord and lady at their high table to the boy who turns the meat over the kitchen fire.

[long-pause]

{img:great-mere-lake-around-castle-reflections-reeds-swans}
The first thing every visitor notices about Kenilworth is the water.

[pause]

The castle stands on a low hill of red sandstone. On two sides there is a great lake, which people call the mere. It is about a mile long, wide and deep. There are reeds along its edges, and swans and herons, and fish in the clear water.

[long-pause]

The lake is not natural. The builders made it themselves. They built big banks of earth across two little valleys, so the streams could not run away. The water rose and spread out, until it filled the valleys all around the castle hill.

[pause]

One of these banks is also the main road into the castle. It is a long, wide path across the water, with a strong gatehouse at the end. Later, knights will hold jousting contests on it, riding at each other along its length.

[pause]

So the castle is almost an island. No enemy can reach the walls from the water side. The lake is too deep for siege towers, and too wet for digging tunnels. The builders made Kenilworth very hard to attack. One day, that will matter more than anyone can guess.

[long-pause]

The castle was first built about a hundred and thirty years before, by a man called Geoffrey de Clinton. He looked after the money of King Henry the First.

[pause]

Geoffrey built a great square tower on top of the hill. This kind of tower is called a keep. Its walls are more than four metres thick, and it still stands today. He also founded a little monastery in the valley nearby.

[pause]

{img:massive-square-keep-red-sandstone-thick-walls-corner-towers}
Later, the castle passed to the kings of England. King John loved Kenilworth, and he spent more money on it than on almost any other castle. He built the outer walls with their towers. And he made the great lake.

[long-pause]

When King John died, his son Henry became king. Henry was only nine years old. Many years later, in 1244, King Henry gave Kenilworth to his sister Eleanor, and then to her husband Simon.

[pause]

Simon and Eleanor love the place. It becomes their favourite home. They make it stronger and more comfortable, year after year. Perhaps, somewhere deep down, Simon already feels that he may need a strong place one day.

[pause]

{img:red-stone-castle-reflected-still-water-summer-evening}
In the evening, when the sun is low, the red stone glows a warm, rosy colour. The towers are mirrored in the still water, upside down among the clouds. It is one of the loveliest sights in England.

[long-pause]

Let us imagine we are riding toward Kenilworth on a summer day in 1250.

[pause]

We follow a track through fields and woods, from the town of Warwick. We pass the village of Kenilworth, with its timber houses and thatched roofs. We pass the monastery, where the canons walk in their black cloaks. Canons are a kind of monk.

[pause]

Then we come over a little hill, and there it is, across the water. The great keep stands high above everything. Smoke drifts from the kitchens. And on top of the keep flies a red banner with a white lion. It is the sign of Simon de Montfort.

[long-pause]

We ride along the road across the water. The lake shines on both sides, and the swans glide past.

[pause]

At the end we reach the gatehouse. In peaceful times the big wooden gates stand open during the day. Above our heads hangs the portcullis, a heavy iron gate. It can drop down in a moment if danger comes. The guards wear padded coats and iron helmets. They look at us, and ask us why we have come.

[pause]

{img:gatehouse-portcullis-raised-guards-visitors-entering-sunlight}
Then they wave us through, into the big outer yard of the castle. We are inside Kenilworth.

[long-pause]

Inside the gate, a groom takes our horses. A servant brings us a cup of ale, because we have come a long way. All around us, people are busy, and nobody pays us much attention. That is how it is in a big castle. Visitors come and go every day.

[pause]

We look up at the walls, so high and so thick. It is hard to believe that anything could ever threaten a place like this. And yet the builders thought of everything. They knew that peace does not always last.

[long-pause]

{img:simon-de-montfort-earl-tall-dark-knight-surcoat-lion-portrait}
So who are the lord and lady of Kenilworth?

[pause]

In 1250, Simon de Montfort is about forty-two years old. He was born in France. He was the youngest son of a famous and fierce French lord, who was also called Simon. His father died in a war when Simon was still a boy.

[long-pause]

Simon's family had a claim to a great English title, the Earl of Leicester. His older brother gave that claim to him. So when Simon was about twenty-two, he came to England. He had almost nothing. He spoke French and Latin, but hardly any English. He came to win back what his family had lost.

[pause]

He was tall and handsome. He was clever and charming, but also very proud. The young King Henry liked him at once. He made Simon the Earl of Leicester, and one of his closest friends.

[pause]

Simon is a serious man. He reads a lot and talks with wise churchmen. One of his friends, a friar called Adam Marsh, writes him long letters. In them he tells Simon to control his temper, and to be gentle with his wife.

[long-pause]

Simon prays a great deal. He often gets up in the night to pray. Under his fine clothes he wears a rough shirt of goat hair that scratches the skin. He wears it to make himself humble. He eats and drinks very little. But he has a fierce temper, and he does not forgive easily.

[pause]

He is also a great soldier, perhaps the best in England. He has fought in France and in the Holy Land. In 1250 he is often far away, in the south of France. There he rules a region called Gascony for the King, and fights the lords who rebel.

[pause]

{img:countess-eleanor-noblewoman-fine-gown-veil-castle-chamber}
His wife is Eleanor, the youngest sister of King Henry. In 1250 she is about thirty-five.

[long-pause]

Eleanor is a daughter of King John. When she was only nine, she was married to a great English lord. But he died when she was sixteen.

[pause]

The young widow was heartbroken. In front of the Archbishop of Canterbury, she made a holy promise. She would never marry again, and she would live a quiet, holy life.

[pause]

{img:young-widow-kneeling-before-archbishop-vow-cathedral-candles}
But then she met Simon de Montfort. And they fell in love. In 1238 they married, almost in secret, in the King's own little chapel in London. The King himself gave his sister away.

[pause]

It caused a great scandal. Many people said the marriage was wrong, because of her promise. The King's brother was furious. Simon had to travel all the way to Rome, to ask the Pope to accept the marriage. In the end, the Pope said yes.

[long-pause]

Simon and Eleanor are very close. They are both proud and quick to anger, and sometimes they argue. But they love each other deeply, and they stand together through everything.

[pause]

Eleanor lives like the king's daughter she is. She loves fine clothes, furs and jewels. She spends a lot of money, and she is often in debt. But she runs the great household with energy. When Simon is away, she takes care of the lands and the money. She is clever, and she is brave.

[pause]

By 1250 they have several sons. The eldest, Henry, is about twelve. Simon is about ten. Then come Amaury, Guy and little Richard. In two years a daughter will be born. She will be called Eleanor, like her mother.

[long-pause]

The King, Henry the Third, is their brother and their friend. But he is a difficult king.

[pause]

He is gentle and kind, and very religious. He loves beautiful things, like churches and gold. He is rebuilding Westminster Abbey in London, in a beautiful new style. But he is not a good ruler. He changes his mind often. He spends too much money, and he listens to the wrong friends.

[pause]

{img:king-henry-third-gentle-crowned-man-westminster-abbey-building}
And sometimes he quarrels with Simon, his proud brother-in-law. They are friends, then enemies, then friends again. In the end, their quarrel will change the history of England. But how, and when, no one at Kenilworth can yet say.

[pause]

For now, in 1250, the castle is at peace. Its life goes on, day after day, with the turning of the seasons.

[long-pause]

{img:castle-inner-courtyard-great-hall-keep-kitchens-busy-morning}
Let us walk through Kenilworth and see what it is like inside.

[pause]

The castle has two big yards, called baileys. The outer bailey is a large open space inside the outer walls. Along the walls lean many smaller buildings. There are stables for the horses, barns, workshops and a forge. There is also a well.

[long-pause]

The outer bailey is busy all day. Horses are led in and out and brushed and fed. Carts arrive with wood, hay and barrels of wine. The smith hammers at his anvil, mending horseshoes and armour. Children run about, and the dogs bark at everything.

[pause]

The outer walls are thick and high. On top there is a narrow walkway behind the battlements. Battlements are the up-and-down stones at the top of a wall. Archers can stand between them to shoot, and hide behind them when they need to.

[pause]

In the walls there are narrow slits, called arrow loops. They are thin on the outside and wide on the inside. An archer inside can see and shoot in many directions. But an enemy outside can hardly hit him.

[long-pause]

Through another gate lies the inner bailey, at the top of the hill. Here stand the most important buildings.

[pause]

Here is the great keep, the oldest part of the castle. It is a huge square tower with walls four metres thick. By 1250, the lord and lady no longer live in it. It is too dark and gloomy, with tiny windows. Now it is used for storing food and weapons. And it is the last safe place, if the castle is ever attacked.

[pause]

{img:keep-interior-dim-thick-walls-stores-barrels-small-windows}
The family now lives in newer buildings around the inner yard. These have bigger windows and proper fireplaces.

[long-pause]

The most important of these is the great hall.

[pause]

It is a long, tall room with a high wooden roof. At one end there is a raised floor, called the dais. Here the lord and lady sit at the high table, with their family and their best guests. Everyone else sits on benches at long tables down the sides.

[pause]

{img:great-hall-timber-roof-dais-high-table-trestles-tapestries}
The great hall is the heart of the castle. Here everyone eats together. Here the lord welcomes visitors and settles quarrels. Here there are feasts, music and stories. And at night, some of the servants sleep here on the floor, wrapped in their cloaks by the fire.

[long-pause]

Behind the high table are the private rooms of the lord and lady.

[pause]

These are the most comfortable rooms in the castle. The walls are painted, or hung with tapestries to keep out the cold air. There is a fireplace with a chimney. The best windows even have a little glass, which is very costly. There is a great bed with curtains all around it, and feather mattresses and furs.

[pause]

Here the lord and lady sleep and dress. Here they talk in private, and count their money, and pray.

[long-pause]

At the other end of the hall there are three doors. One leads to the buttery, where the wine and ale are kept in barrels. One leads to the pantry, where the bread is kept. The third leads along a covered path to the kitchen.

[pause]

The kitchen is a separate stone building, because fire is always a danger. It has huge fireplaces and ovens. It is always hot, smoky and busy.

[pause]

{img:castle-chapel-small-stone-altar-candles-painted-saints}
There is also a chapel, a small and beautiful stone room with painted walls and candles. Every day the castle priest says Mass here for the family.

[pause]

There are towers with rooms for guests and for the children. There are storerooms for cloth, spices and other precious things. And there are toilets, called garderobes. They are little rooms built out from the walls, with a stone seat and a long drop down into the water below.

[long-pause]

Everything in the castle has two jobs. It must keep a big household warm and well fed. And it must keep enemies out. For now, only the first job matters. But the walls are ready, just in case.

[long-pause]

If we climb the stairs to the top of the walls, we can see everything. The castle yards lie below us like a little town. Beyond the walls lies the shining lake, and beyond the lake, the green fields and the dark line of the forest.

[pause]

On a clear day you can see for miles. That is the point. From up here, the guards would see any danger long before it arrived. They would see the dust of riders on the road, or the smoke of a camp in the woods. In 1250 they see only farmers and carts. But they keep looking all the same.

[long-pause]

{img:castle-guard-night-watch-wall-walk-lantern-stars-mere}
Even in times of peace, a castle is always watched.

[pause]

There is a small group of soldiers at Kenilworth. They are led by the constable, a knight the lord trusts. When the lord is away, the constable is in charge of the castle. Under him serve watchmen, archers and the porter, who keeps the gate.

[long-pause]

The porter lives in a little room in the gatehouse. He sees everyone who comes in and goes out. He knows every face in the household, and he asks strangers their business. At night he closes the great gates. He bars them with heavy wooden beams. Then he lowers the portcullis, and keeps the keys.

[pause]

On the walls, the watchmen walk all night with their lanterns. They go from tower to tower. They look out over the dark water and the sleeping fields. And they listen.

[pause]

{img:watchman-blowing-horn-tower-top-dawn-mist-calm}
At certain times they blow a horn, to show they are awake and all is well. If they see or hear anything strange, they must raise the alarm. Most nights, nothing happens at all. But a watchman can never be sure which night will be different.

[long-pause]

A great castle must always be ready for a siege. A siege is when an enemy surrounds a castle and waits, to starve the people inside.

[pause]

So there are big stores in the keep and the cellars. There is grain and salted meat, dried fish and barrels of ale. There are arrows by the bundle, and stones to throw down from the walls. And inside the castle there is a deep well, which will not run dry.

[pause]

{img:keep-cellar-stores-barrels-grain-sacks-arrows-bundles}
The gatehouse has its own tricks. In the ceiling of the gate passage there are holes, so that things can be dropped on attackers. But the best defence of Kenilworth is the water of the mere. It keeps any enemy far from the walls.

[long-pause]

In 1250, though, the guards have a quiet life.

[pause]

They stand at the gate in the sunshine and chat with the carters and the villagers. They practise with their bows at targets in the yard. They polish their helmets. And when the constable is not looking, they play little board games, scratched into the stone of the window sills.

[pause]

{img:guardroom-soldiers-playing-merels-dice-stone-bench-lamp}
At night they walk the walls under the stars. They look out over the quiet water, the dark woods and the few lights of the village. They think of their families, and wait for the dawn. None of them knows that, some years from now, these same walls will be tested as never before.

[long-pause]

{img:great-household-gathered-courtyard-officers-servants-livery}
A great lord and lady in 1250 never live alone. Around them lives their household. That is a big company of people, a hundred or more, who serve them. They travel with them, live with them and eat at their table.

[pause]

We know a lot about the household of Eleanor de Montfort. One of her account books has survived, from fifteen years later. It is a long roll of parchment, kept by her clerks day by day. It tells what was bought and eaten, who came to dinner, and how many horses stood in the stables each night.

[long-pause]

The household is run by the steward. He is an important man, often a knight. He is in charge of the servants, the food and the money. You could say he is the manager of a great hotel and a great business at the same time.

[pause]

Under him work other officers, each with his own job.

[pause]

The marshal looks after the stables, the horses and the journeys. The chamberlain looks after the private rooms of the lord and lady, their clothes and their treasure. Another officer keeps the store of cloth, spices and candles.

[long-pause]

The pantler is in charge of the bread. The butler is in charge of the wine and the ale. His name comes from the old word for bottle. Then there are the cooks, the baker and the brewer.

[pause]

There are clerks, who write the letters and keep the accounts in Latin. There is the almoner, who gives food and money to the poor at the gate every day. And there is the chaplain, the castle priest.

[pause]

{img:almoner-giving-bread-to-poor-castle-gate-morning}
And there are many lesser servants. There are grooms and messengers, huntsmen and washerwomen. There are kitchen boys, and men who carry water and wood all day long.

[long-pause]

Most of the servants in a great household are men and boys.

[pause]

But a great lady has her own women. Her ladies come from good families. They keep her company, help her dress and sew with her. There are maids who clean her rooms, and nurses who care for the youngest children.

[pause]

{img:ladies-in-waiting-sewing-embroidery-chamber-window-light}
The household also has knights, who ride and fight with Simon. There are squires, young men who are learning to be knights. And there are pages. These are boys from good families, sent to the castle to learn manners and to serve at table.

[long-pause]

Everyone in the household wears the lord's colours. Each year the lord gives them new clothes in the same colour, so everyone can see whose people they are. The knights get fine cloth and fur. The servants get plainer cloth.

[pause]

They get their food every day, a place to sleep, and a small wage. In return they owe the lord and lady their loyalty. A household is like a big family. Some servants stay with the same lord all their lives. Their children often serve after them.

[pause]

But loyalty is easy in peaceful times. One day, these same people will have to choose whom they stand by. And not all of them will choose the same way.

[long-pause]

Eleanor's account book tells us many little things.

[pause]

On an ordinary day, the household might eat a whole ox, or several sheep, and hundreds of eggs. In Lent, they eat thousands of herrings. Every week they drink hundreds of gallons of ale. And the countess buys cloth, furs and spices, almonds and figs, and even sugar.

[pause]

{img:medieval-household-roll-parchment-latin-entries-close-calm}
The book tells us that there were often forty or fifty horses in the stables. When guests came, there were many more. Messengers rode all over England with letters. And the countess gave money to the poor, and paid for the mending of carts.

[long-pause]

Most of all, the book tells us quietly about the life of a great lady, day after day. Who came to dinner, how many ate in the hall, and where the household travelled.

[pause]

It is one of the best records of daily life from medieval England. Thanks to it, we can picture Kenilworth very clearly, with its big, busy and very hungry household.

[long-pause]

Life in the household has its own rules. Everyone knows their place, and everyone knows their job. A good steward keeps the peace between the cooks and the grooms, between the clerks and the knights.

[pause]

Sometimes there are quarrels. Two servants argue about who should carry the water. A young squire is too proud to help in the stables. Then the steward steps in, calmly and firmly. A household of a hundred people is like a ship at sea. It only works if everyone pulls together.

[long-pause]

{img:castle-dawn-mist-over-mere-towers-bell-ringing}
Let us spend an ordinary day at Kenilworth, in the summer of 1250.

[pause]

The day begins at dawn, or even before. In the chapel a bell rings. The castle wakes slowly in the grey light. Mist lies on the water, and the birds sing in the woods across the lake.

[long-pause]

The servants get up first. They have slept on straw in the hall, in the kitchen, or in the stables. They roll up their bedding and wash their faces in cold water.

[pause]

The fires are woken up from last night's embers. The bakers are already kneading dough and lighting the ovens. The grooms feed the horses. Someone milks the cows.

[pause]

The guards on the walls change places. The porter opens the great gates. The castle opens to the world for another day.

[long-pause]

In their room, the lord and lady wake in their great curtained bed.

[pause]

Servants bring warm water so they can wash. They dress with help. First comes a linen shirt, then a long woollen tunic. Over that goes a long gown without sleeves, in bright red, blue or green. The countess wears a veil, and a linen cloth wrapped around her neck and chin, as married ladies do. The earl wears a belt with a purse and a knife.

[pause]

{img:noble-lady-dressing-wimple-veil-maids-helping-chamber-morning}
Then they go to the chapel to hear Mass, with their family and those servants who are free.

[long-pause]

Breakfast in 1250 is not a real meal. Most people just eat a bit of bread with a little ale, often standing up. Children, old people and the sick get something more.

[pause]

Then the day's work begins.

[pause]

When the earl is at home, he meets with his steward. He hears how his lands and money are doing. He reads letters, or has them read to him, and tells his clerks what to answer. He settles quarrels between his tenants. Or he rides out to look at his lands, or to hunt.

[pause]

But often there is a letter that makes him frown. Letters from the King's court, about money and power and old quarrels. Simon reads them carefully. And sometimes, afterwards, he stands at the window for a long time.

[long-pause]

The countess is busy too. She checks the accounts with the steward and orders what is needed. She looks after the children and their lessons. She writes letters and welcomes visitors.

[pause]

In quieter hours, she sits with her ladies by the window. They sew and talk. Someone reads aloud from a book, a story of knights or the life of a saint.

[pause]

{img:ladies-listening-reader-romance-book-chamber-afternoon-light}
The book is in French. In 1250, the lords and ladies of England speak French. Their families came from Normandy long ago. The clerks write in Latin. The servants and the villagers speak English. So many people in the castle speak two or three languages.

[long-pause]

At about ten or eleven in the morning, a horn is blown. Everyone goes to the great hall for dinner, the main meal of the day. It is a long, grand meal, and we will join it soon.

[pause]

After dinner, the castle works again. The lord and the young men may go hunting, or practise with their weapons in the yard. The ladies may walk in the garden, or ride, or play games. The children have their lessons, or play.

[long-pause]

In the late afternoon there is supper, a lighter meal, at about five o'clock.

[pause]

Then comes the evening. In summer the evenings are long and light. People walk by the lake and watch the swallows dip over the water. In winter they gather by the fires. They tell stories, play chess, and listen to a harp or a fiddle.

[pause]

{img:evening-hall-fire-harp-player-storytelling-household-gathered}
As darkness falls, the gates are closed and the guards are set for the night. The lights go out one by one. The household lies down to sleep. Soon there is only the sound of the guards on the walls, the water against the stones, and the owls in the woods.

[long-pause]

Late at night, the steward makes one last walk through the castle. He checks that the fires in the kitchen are covered. He checks that the doors of the storerooms are locked. He says goodnight to the porter at the gate.

[pause]

Then he climbs to his own small room and lies down. Tomorrow will be another long day. And somewhere in England, far from this quiet castle, men are already whispering about the King, and about the barons, and about Simon.

[long-pause]

{img:castle-chamber-winter-evening-fireplace-candles-shutters-closed}
What is it like to live in a castle in the winter of 1250?

[pause]

The answer is simple: it is cold. Thick stone walls stay cold all winter long. The wind finds every crack around the shutters and under the doors. The floors are stone or wood. They are covered with rushes or straw, with sweet herbs like lavender and mint scattered on top, to make the rooms smell fresh.

[long-pause]

The only warmth comes from the fires.

[pause]

In the great hall, a big fire burns all day in winter. In the best rooms there are fireplaces with chimneys. Chimneys are quite new in 1250. They are a great improvement, because the smoke goes up and out, instead of filling the room.

[pause]

{img:great-hall-central-hearth-smoke-rising-louvre-roof-winter}
The fires burn great logs from the forest. Every winter, the castle needs mountains of firewood. The villagers must bring it by cart, as part of their work for the lord. If the wood runs out in a hard winter, everyone will feel it.

[long-pause]

To keep warm, people wear many layers. There is a linen shirt, a woollen tunic, a gown, and a cloak lined with fur. The lord and lady wear rich furs. One of the finest is ermine, which is white with little black tails. Eleanor's accounts show her buying a great deal of fur. Ordinary people wear sheepskin or rabbit.

[pause]

Tapestries hang on the walls to keep out the cold air. People sleep in big beds with curtains drawn tight, under heavy blankets and furs. Often they share a bed, for the warmth. And sometimes a pan full of hot coals is slid into the bed before anyone gets in.

[long-pause]

Then there is light.

[pause]

In winter the days are short. The sun sets at about four in the afternoon. After that, the castle must be lit.

[pause]

{img:candle-maker-dipping-tallow-wax-candles-workshop}
The best candles are made of beeswax. They burn bright and clean, with a sweet smell. But beeswax costs a lot. It is used in the chapel and in the rooms of the lord and lady.

[pause]

The rest of the castle uses candles made of animal fat. They are cheaper, but smoky and smelly. There are also rushlights, made from rush stems dipped in fat. They burn only a little while, with a small flame.

[long-pause]

So on a winter evening, the great hall is lit by the fire, by torches and by candles. The light flickers, and shadows move across the walls.

[pause]

In her room, the lady and her women sit close to the fire. They sew and talk and listen to music. Outside, the dark is deep and complete. There are no lights at all, except the stars, the moon, and a few candles in the village across the water.

[pause]

{img:ladies-sewing-close-to-fire-candles-chamber-winter-night-warm}
Everyone goes to bed early, to save candles and to stay warm. In winter they sleep a long time. Then they wake in the dark before dawn, and wait for the light to come back. On those long nights, the lady sometimes lies awake and thinks of Simon, far away in France. She wonders where he is sleeping tonight, and whether he is safe.

[long-pause]

{img:great-hall-dinner-high-table-trestle-tables-servants-dishes}
When the horn sounds for dinner, the whole household gathers in the great hall.

[pause]

The tables are set up along the walls. They are long boards on wooden stands, covered with white linen. At the end of the hall, on the raised floor, the high table is laid with the finest cloth and the best cups. In the middle stands a big silver salt dish.

[long-pause]

Everyone has a place, and the place tells you how important you are.

[pause]

At the high table sit the lord and lady in the middle. Beside them sit their most important guests, perhaps a bishop or another earl. Below sit the knights and the officers. Further down the hall sit the lesser servants.

[pause]

Before the meal, servants bring bowls of warm water and towels. Everyone washes their hands. That matters, because they will eat with their fingers.

[long-pause]

Then the chaplain says a short prayer in Latin, and the meal begins.

[pause]

The servants come in from the kitchen in a line, holding the dishes high. They are led by an officer with a white staff. On feast days, trumpets announce them.

[pause]

{img:procession-servants-carrying-dishes-steward-white-rod-hall}
At the high table, a young squire kneels and carves the meat for the lord and lady. Another young man pours the wine, also kneeling. These are honourable jobs. The sons of great families do them, as part of learning to be knights.

[long-pause]

There are no plates as we know them. Instead, each person has a thick slice of old, hard bread, called a trencher. The food is put on top of it, and the bread soaks up the sauce. After the meal, the trenchers are not eaten by the diners. They are given to the poor at the gate, or to the dogs.

[pause]

People share. Two people eat from one dish and drink from one cup. A young man is expected to cut the meat for the lady beside him, and to offer her the best pieces.

[pause]

Everyone carries their own knife. There are spoons for soup. But there are no forks. Forks will not reach England for another three hundred years. So people eat neatly with three fingers, and wipe them on the tablecloth between dishes.

[long-pause]

What do they eat?

[pause]

A great dinner has two or three courses. Each course has many dishes, all on the table at once, so people can choose.

[pause]

{img:roast-meats-pies-birds-dishes-high-table-feast-colour}
There is roast beef, mutton and pork. There is deer from the forest, and meat baked in pies. There are chickens, geese and pigeons. On great feast days there might even be a swan or a peacock. There are thick soups, and sauces of herbs and mustard.

[long-pause]

Spices are very important. They come from very far away, from India and beyond, and they cost a fortune. There is pepper and ginger, cinnamon and saffron. Saffron turns food a lovely golden yellow. Great lords use spices freely, to show how rich they are.

[pause]

Sugar is rare too, and is used like a spice, a little at a time. Almonds are ground and soaked to make almond milk, which goes into many dishes.

[pause]

And there is always bread. Fine white bread for the high table, and darker bread for the lower tables. The lord and his knights drink wine from the south of France. Everyone else drinks ale brewed in the castle.

[long-pause]

On many days of the year, people may not eat meat. On Fridays, and during the forty days before Easter, they eat fish instead.

[pause]

There is fresh fish from the lake and the castle ponds, like pike and eels. And there is salted fish from the sea, above all herrings, by the thousand. Some dried fish is so hard that it must be beaten with a hammer and soaked for days before it can be cooked.

[pause]

{img:fish-day-table-pike-eels-herrings-bread-lent}
At the end of the meal there might be apples and pears, cheese, and thin, crisp little cakes made with honey.

[long-pause]

Then the tables are cleared and the cloths are taken away. Everyone washes their hands again.

[pause]

All the leftover food is gathered up and given to the almoner. He carries it to the gate, where the poor of the village are waiting. In a good year, there is plenty for them. But everyone knows that years are not always good.

[long-pause]

After dinner, the lord sometimes stays at the high table a little longer. He talks with his guests about the news from London and from France. Who is in favour with the King? Who has fallen out of favour? Which lord has been given new lands, and which lord feels cheated?

[pause]

The servants clearing the tables hear bits of these talks. They do not understand all of it. But they notice when their lord's voice grows quiet and serious. And they notice when the guests look at each other and say nothing at all.

[long-pause]

{img:castle-kitchen-huge-fireplaces-spits-cauldrons-cooks-steam}
To feed more than a hundred people twice a day, every day, the kitchen of Kenilworth must be a very busy place.

[pause]

The kitchen is a big stone building of its own. Kitchens often catch fire, so it stands apart from the hall. It has a high roof with openings to let out the smoke. Its fireplaces are so big a man can stand inside them.

[long-pause]

It is always hot, smoky, noisy and crowded, from before dawn until after supper.

[pause]

The master cook is in charge. He carries a big wooden spoon. He uses it to taste the food, and to keep the kitchen boys in line. Under him work other cooks and helpers. There are boys who wash the pots, and boys who fetch water and wood.

[pause]

Meat is roasted on long iron rods in front of the fire. Boys sit beside the fire for hours, turning the rods slowly, in terrible heat. It is the hardest job in the castle. Every kitchen boy dreams of the day he will not have to do it any more.

[long-pause]

Big iron pots hang over the fires on chains, full of bubbling stew. Sometimes many things cook in one pot at once. A piece of meat might sit in the water next to a pudding tied in a cloth, with eggs beside it.

[pause]

In the ovens, bread and pies are baked. Pies then were not like ours. The pastry is thick and hard. It is made to hold the filling while it cooks, and often it is not eaten at all.

[pause]

{img:pastry-cook-making-large-pies-coffins-oven-paddle}
There are big tables for chopping. There are heavy stone bowls for grinding spices and almonds. There are sieves, ladles and sharp knives everywhere.

[long-pause]

Around the kitchen there are other rooms.

[pause]

There is the larder, where meat is kept cool, salted or hanging. There is the bakehouse with its big ovens. There is the brewhouse, where ale is made from barley in great tubs. And there is the dairy, where milk becomes butter and cheese.

[pause]

{img:brewhouse-vats-malt-steam-brewer-stirring-wooden-paddle}
Outside lies a garden with herbs and vegetables, like parsley, sage, onions and cabbages. There are pens for pigs, sheep and hens, waiting to be cooked. And there is a dovecote full of pigeons.

[long-pause]

Where does all the food come from?

[pause]

Most of it comes from the lord's own farms all over England. They send grain, cattle and cheese to the castle. Other things are bought at markets and fairs, like wine, fish, salt and spices.

[pause]

In autumn, many animals are killed, because there is not enough food to keep them all through the winter. Their meat is salted or smoked, so it lasts until spring. The cooks count the barrels carefully. A castle that runs out of food is a castle in danger.

[long-pause]

The cooks of 1250 mostly do not have recipe books. They learn by watching older cooks. But a little later, the first cookery books will be written.

[pause]

One famous dish is blancmange. It is not a pudding, as you might think. It is chicken and rice with almond milk and sugar, all white. Another is frumenty, a thick wheat porridge cooked in milk, often served with deer meat.

[pause]

{img:cook-tasting-pot-wooden-spoon-steam-kitchen-evening}
At the end of the day, when the last dish is washed, the kitchen is quiet at last. The fires are covered with ash, to keep them alive until morning. And the kitchen boys lie down to sleep on the warm floor, close to the fire.

[long-pause]

The busiest day of all is the day before a great feast. Then the kitchen works through the night. Extra cooks are hired from the village. Extra fires are lit. Hundreds of pies are baked and dozens of birds are roasted.

[pause]

The master cook hardly sleeps. If anything goes wrong, it will be his name that is spoken in the hall. A burnt dish or a cold sauce could cost him his place. So he tastes everything twice, and keeps the boys running until the last dish is carried out.

[long-pause]

{img:squires-training-courtyard-wooden-swords-quintain-horses}
In the outer yard of Kenilworth, in the afternoon, the young men are training.

[pause]

There are knights, grown men who serve the earl. There are squires, young men of about sixteen who are learning to become knights. And there are pages. They are boys of seven or eight from noble families, sent to the castle to be raised here.

[long-pause]

In 1250, a boy from a noble family does not usually grow up at home. At about seven, he goes to live with a great lord, often a relative or a friend of the family. There he becomes a page.

[pause]

A page learns good manners. He learns how to serve at table, and how to speak to lords and ladies. He learns to ride, and to care for horses, dogs and hawks. He learns to sing and dance, and to play chess. He may learn a little reading too.

[pause]

And he learns to fight. He practises with a small wooden sword and shield. He wrestles, runs and swims.

[long-pause]

At about fourteen, he becomes a squire. Now he serves one knight as his helper.

[pause]

He looks after the knight's horses and weapons. He helps him put on his armour, which is heavy and impossible to put on alone. He rides beside him and carries his shield. He goes with him to tournaments, and to war.

[pause]

{img:squire-helping-knight-into-chain-mail-hauberk-armoury}
And every day he trains for hours. He learns to fight on horseback with a long lance, and on foot with a sword.

[long-pause]

In 1250, a knight's armour is made of mail. Mail is thousands of little iron rings, linked together like a heavy, bendy cloth.

[pause]

A knight wears a long mail shirt down to his knees. He has mail on his hands, his head and his legs. Under the mail he wears a thick padded coat, to soften the blows. Over the mail he wears a long cloth coat with his coat of arms on it. That way, people can see who he is.

[pause]

On his head he wears an iron helmet. It is often shaped like a flat-topped pot, with narrow slits for his eyes. It is hot and heavy, and hard to see out of. But it keeps him safe.

[long-pause]

The most important thing a knight owns is his horse.

[pause]

He has a great warhorse, big and strong and trained for battle. It costs a fortune, and he only rides it in war and in tournaments. For travelling he has a calm riding horse. And there are packhorses for his luggage.

[pause]

{img:destrier-warhorse-groom-brushing-stable-sunlight}
The young men practise at the quintain. It is a wooden post with a turning arm. On one end hangs a shield. On the other end hangs a heavy bag of sand. The rider gallops at it and hits the shield with his lance. If he is too slow, the arm swings round. The sandbag hits him on the back of the head and knocks him off his horse. And everyone laughs.

[long-pause]

At about twenty-one, if he has done well, the squire becomes a knight.

[pause]

The night before, he takes a bath, to make himself clean. Then he spends the whole night alone in the chapel, praying, with his armour lying on the altar. In the morning he hears Mass.

[pause]

Then, in front of everyone, his lord gives him his sword and his spurs. He taps him on the shoulder and says: be a good knight. And then there is a feast.

[long-pause]

In the stories of the time, knights are supposed to be brave and loyal. They should protect the weak, serve God, and honour ladies. This is called chivalry.

[pause]

Real knights are not always like that. Some are rough and greedy. But many young men truly try. In the evenings they listen to stories of King Arthur and his knights, and dream of being heroes too.

[pause]

{img:minstrel-reciting-arthur-romance-hall-young-knights-listening}
Simon de Montfort is admired all over Europe as a model knight. Young men come from far away to serve him. They do not yet know that one day, serving Simon will mean choosing a side against the King himself.

[long-pause]

In the evening, after training, the squires sit together in the yard. They rub their sore arms and laugh about who fell off at the quintain. They clean their knights' armour with sand and a little oil, so it will not rust.

[pause]

And they talk about the future. Each one hopes to be made a knight one day. Each one dreams of a great tournament where everyone will see how brave he is. Not one of them is thinking about real war. Not yet.

[long-pause]

{img:castle-blacksmith-forge-anvil-hammer-sparks-courtyard}
A great castle is like a little town. It has its own craftsmen, who keep everything working.

[pause]

The most important one is the smith. His forge stands in the outer yard. A boy works the bellows to make the fire roar. All day long, the ring of the hammer sounds across the yard.

[long-pause]

The smith makes and mends everything of iron. Horseshoes and nails for all the horses. Hinges, locks and keys for the doors. Tools for the gardeners, and pots and chains for the kitchen.

[pause]

Sometimes he mends a knight's armour. But the best armour is made by special craftsmen in the towns. One mail shirt is made of perhaps thirty thousand little rings. Each ring is closed by hand. It takes months to make one.

[pause]

{img:armourer-linking-mail-rings-workbench-careful-light}
There are other craftsmen too. One man shoes the horses and treats them when they are sick. Another makes saddles. One makes bows, and another makes arrows with goose feathers. In peaceful times, they make only a few arrows each week. If war ever comes, they will have to make thousands.

[long-pause]

The carpenters make and mend everything of wood. Roofs and floors, doors and shutters, tables and beds, carts and barrels.

[pause]

The masons look after the stone walls. A castle is never finished. There is always a wall to repair, or a new tower to build.

[pause]

{img:masons-repairing-castle-wall-scaffold-ropes-pulley-summer}
There is even a plumber. His name comes from the Latin word for lead. He makes lead sheets for the roofs and lead pipes for water.

[long-pause]

There is the glazier, who makes windows. But glass is still rare and costly in 1250. It is greenish and full of little bubbles. Most windows in the castle have no glass at all. They only have wooden shutters, or thin sheets of horn that let in a little light.

[pause]

There are tailors and fur workers, who make the clothes of the household. And there are the washerwomen. They wash the sheets and shirts in big tubs. Instead of soap they often use water mixed with wood ash. Then they spread the linen on the grass and the hedges to dry in the sun.

[pause]

{img:laundresses-washing-linen-tubs-drying-on-hedges-sunny}
Without these people and their hard work, the castle could not live. They are the quiet, busy heart of Kenilworth.

[long-pause]

{img:noble-children-playing-castle-garden-ball-hoop-summer}
What is life like for the children of Kenilworth in 1250?

[pause]

The sons of the earl and countess grow up in the castle. They have nurses and teachers, and other children of the household to play with.

[long-pause]

When they are babies, a nurse feeds them, because great ladies usually do not. The babies are wrapped tightly in long strips of linen, so they cannot move their arms and legs. People believe this helps them grow straight. They sleep in wooden cradles that can be rocked.

[pause]

When they are older, they play.

[pause]

They have little wooden horses and knights, dolls, balls and spinning tops. They play hide and seek and leapfrog. In summer they fish and swim along the shore of the lake. In winter they skate on the ice, with skates made of bone.

[long-pause]

The boys learn to ride as soon as they can sit on a pony. They learn to shoot a small bow and to fight with wooden swords.

[pause]

And they learn to read. Simon is a learned man and he believes in good schooling. His sons learn Latin and French. He even sends the older boys to his friend, the great Bishop of Lincoln, one of the wisest men of the age.

[pause]

{img:boys-learning-latin-tutor-book-wax-tablets-castle-room}
The bishop writes back to Simon. He says the boys are doing well, both in their lessons and in their manners. Simon is proud. He does not know yet what hard roads his sons will have to travel.

[long-pause]

Girls are raised mostly by their mothers and the ladies of the household.

[pause]

They learn to sew and to weave. They learn to run a household and keep accounts. Many learn to read in French, so they can read their prayer books. They learn to ride, to dance, and to play the harp.

[pause]

{img:young-girl-learning-embroidery-mother-ladies-window}
Simon and Eleanor's daughter will be born in 1252. When she grows up, she will marry the Prince of Wales, and become Princess of Wales herself.

[long-pause]

The children of the servants grow up in the castle too.

[pause]

From a young age, they help their parents in the kitchen, the stables and the gardens. They fetch water, feed the chickens and run errands. When they can, they play in the yards with the other children.

[pause]

Childhood in 1250 is shorter than today. At twelve or fourteen, a child is almost grown up. But children are deeply loved. Old letters and poems show how much parents cared for them, and how they worried when a child was ill.

[long-pause]

In the evening, the children gather around their mother by the fire. They listen to stories from the Bible and stories of saints. They hear old tales of knights and dragons and magic forests.

[pause]

Then they say their prayers. They are put to bed in their own room, with the curtains drawn against the cold.

[pause]

{img:children-asleep-curtained-bed-nurse-candle-castle-night}
Outside, the wind blows across the lake, and the guards walk on the walls. But the children of Kenilworth sleep warm and safe behind the thick red walls.

[long-pause]

Sometimes the boys are allowed to watch the knights train in the yard. They sit on a wall, swinging their legs, and cheer when someone is knocked off his horse. Then they run off to play knights themselves, with sticks for swords and a bucket for a helmet.

[pause]

Their father watches them from a window now and then, and smiles. He was once a boy like them, in a castle in France. He knows how fast these years go by. And he wants his sons to be ready for whatever life may bring.

[long-pause]

{img:castle-chapel-mass-priest-altar-candles-family-kneeling}
In 1250, faith is part of every hour of life in the castle.

[pause]

Every morning, the lord and lady hear Mass in the chapel. They pray at night and before meals. They give to the poor every day. They keep the fast days and the feast days.

[long-pause]

The year is shaped by the great feasts of the Church. And the greatest of all is Christmas.

[pause]

Christmas in 1250 is not one day but twelve. It lasts from the twenty-fifth of December to the sixth of January. Before it come four quiet weeks, without meat, to get ready.

[pause]

In the days before Christmas, the castle is busy. Servants bring in holly, ivy and green branches from the woods, to decorate the hall. A huge log is dragged in, to burn in the hall fire all twelve days. The cooks prepare mountains of food. And guests arrive from all over the country.

[long-pause]

On Christmas Eve, everyone goes to the chapel for Mass at midnight, by candlelight.

[pause]

On Christmas Day the feast begins. The hall is full of light and noise and the smell of roasting meat. A boar's head is carried in on a great dish, with an apple in its mouth, while everyone sings. There are roast geese, pies, wine and sweet spiced cakes.

[pause]

{img:boars-head-carried-in-platter-feast-hall-singing-candles}
The lord gives gifts to his household. Everyone gets new clothes for the year, and some money. And the poor of the village are invited in, and given food and drink.

[long-pause]

For twelve days, nobody works more than they must. There is music, dancing and singing. People dance in a ring, holding hands. There are riddles and games. Mummers come in, people in masks and costumes, who dance and act out stories without saying a word.

[pause]

On the last night, a bean is baked into a cake. Whoever finds the bean becomes the King of the Bean. For one evening, everyone must obey his silly orders.

[pause]

{img:mummers-masks-costumes-dancing-great-hall-firelight}
Then Christmas is over. The guests go home and the green branches are burned. The castle goes back to its ordinary life, in the cold, dark days of January.

[long-pause]

There are other feasts through the year.

[pause]

At Easter, after the long fast, people eat eggs again and wear new clothes. In early summer there is Whitsun, when young men are often made knights. At midsummer, bonfires burn in the night. And at the end of September, the rents are paid and everyone eats goose.

[long-pause]

Simon de Montfort is a very religious man. He is a friend of the friars, who are new in England. Friars are travelling preachers who live in poverty, and preach to ordinary people. Simon gives money to churches, hospitals and the poor.

[pause]

When he was younger, he went all the way to Jerusalem on a crusade. He has never forgotten it.

[pause]

{img:knight-praying-alone-chapel-night-candle-hair-shirt}
Sometimes, late in the evening, when everyone has gone to bed, he goes to the chapel alone. He kneels there for a long time in the candlelight. What does a man like Simon pray for, so late at night? Perhaps for his family. Perhaps for England. Perhaps for the strength to do what he believes is right, whatever it may cost him.

[long-pause]

{img:minstrel-harp-fiddle-playing-great-hall-evening-listeners}
In the long evenings, the castle is full of music, stories and games.

[pause]

A great household has its own minstrels. Minstrels are musicians who play at dinner, in the evenings and at feasts. Travelling minstrels come too. They walk from one great house to the next, with their instruments on their backs. They hope for a meal, a bed and a little money.

[long-pause]

There are many instruments in 1250.

[pause]

There is the small harp, held on the knee. There is a kind of fiddle, played with a bow. There is the lute, newly arrived from Arab lands through Spain. There are pipes and flutes, and small drums. One man can play a pipe with one hand and a drum with the other.

[pause]

{img:medieval-instruments-vielle-psaltery-harp-table-chamber}
The minstrels play dances for the lords and ladies. Everyone holds hands in a circle or a long line, and sings as they dance. The minstrels also sing love songs and ballads, mostly in French, sometimes in English.

[long-pause]

And they tell stories. These are long stories, sung or chanted to the sound of the harp.

[pause]

There are the stories of King Arthur and his knights. Lancelot, and Gawain, and the search for the Holy Grail. There are stories of the great king Charlemagne, and of the old war at Troy. Some of these stories take many evenings to tell. Each night the minstrel stops at an exciting moment. Then everyone has to wait until tomorrow to hear what happens next.

[pause]

One famous song of this time is in English, and people still sing it today. It begins: Sumer is icumen in, lhude sing cuccu. That means: summer is coming in, loudly sing, cuckoo. It is one of the oldest English songs we can still sing.

[long-pause]

There are games too.

[pause]

Chess is the great game of the nobles. The pieces are carved from ivory, bone or wood. Lords and ladies play together for hours, by the fire. Playing chess well shows that you were raised well.

[pause]

{img:lord-and-lady-playing-chess-ivory-pieces-fireside-chamber}
There is a game like backgammon, played with dice. And there is a simpler game with lines and counters. The guards scratch its board into stone benches and window sills, so they can play while they wait.

[long-pause]

People play dice too, and bet on them, though the Church does not like it. Playing cards will not come to Europe for another hundred years. But there are riddles and word games and blind man's buff.

[pause]

On summer evenings, people play outside. They roll bowls on the grass and hold archery contests in the meadow.

[pause]

{img:summer-evening-meadow-bowls-archery-household-relaxing-castle}
And sometimes visitors bring their own fun. There are jugglers and acrobats, and men with dancing bears. There are jesters who tell jokes, and make everyone laugh. Even the serious earl smiles at them now and then.

[long-pause]

{img:hunting-party-riding-out-castle-hounds-horns-autumn-forest}
The favourite pastime of the lords and ladies of 1250 is hunting.

[pause]

Around Kenilworth lies the great forest of Arden. The lord also has his own hunting park. It is a stretch of woods and open land with a fence around it, where deer are kept. Nobody else may hunt there. Anyone caught doing so must pay a heavy fine.

[long-pause]

A great hunt is a big event. Early in the morning, the lord and his guests ride out, with the young knights and squires. The huntsmen come too, with the dogs and the horns.

[pause]

The dogs are much loved. There are tall, fast greyhounds, which chase the deer by sight. There are hounds that follow the deer by smell, and bark loudly when they find the trail. And there are big, strong dogs for hunting wild boar.

[pause]

The huntsmen go ahead first, to find where the deer are. Then the hounds are let loose. They follow the scent, and the riders follow the hounds. They gallop through the woods, blowing their horns, with the hounds barking all around. Will the deer escape this time? Often it does.

[long-pause]

Hunting is not only a sport. It also brings deer meat to the castle table, the finest meat of all. And it is training for war. It teaches young men to ride hard through rough country, and to be quick and brave.

[pause]

Ladies hunt too. Sometimes they ride with the men. Sometimes they wait in a clearing while the deer are driven past, and shoot at them with a bow.

[pause]

{img:noble-lady-on-horse-falcon-on-glove-meadow-river-morning}
But the favourite hunting of the ladies is hunting with birds. This is called hawking.

[long-pause]

Hawking is a great passion of the nobles.

[pause]

The hawks and falcons are caught when they are young. The falconers train them with great patience, to hunt, and to come back to the glove. It takes months.

[pause]

{img:falconer-training-hawk-lure-mews-castle-quiet-afternoon}
The birds live in a special building and are treated like treasure. They wear little leather hoods to keep them calm. They have little bells on their legs, so they can be found if they fly too far.

[long-pause]

Later, a famous book will say which bird suits which person. A king flies the biggest and most beautiful falcon of all. An earl flies a peregrine, the fastest bird in the world. A lady flies a merlin, a small and quick falcon.

[pause]

The lords and ladies carry their birds on a thick glove on their left hand. They take them almost everywhere, even sometimes to dinner. A fine hawk shows how important you are, and it makes a great gift.

[pause]

When they go hawking, they ride out along the rivers and the marshes around the lake. When a duck or a heron flies up, the falconer takes off the hood and throws the hawk into the air. It climbs high, and then it dives. Afterwards it is called back with a bundle of feathers on a string, and gets a piece of meat as a reward.

[long-pause]

Simon de Montfort loves hunting and hawking, and so do his sons. Eleanor's accounts show what they spent on the care of the hawks, the dogs and the horses.

[pause]

There is also fishing in the lake and the ponds. The castle has its own fishermen with little boats and nets. The fish are very important, especially on days without meat.

[pause]

{img:small-boat-fishermen-nets-mere-castle-reflection-dawn}
In the evening, the hunters come home tired, muddy and happy. A deer is slung across a horse, and the hounds trot behind. The horns blow, to tell the castle they are coming. That night there is deer meat for dinner, and long stories about the day.

[long-pause]

Not every hunt is a success. Sometimes the hounds lose the scent in a stream. Sometimes a hawk flies off and does not come back. Then the falconer must search the woods for hours, listening for the little bell on its leg.

[pause]

A lost hawk is a real worry. A good bird takes months to train, and it is worth as much as a fine horse. When the falconer finally finds it, perched in a tall oak, he holds up a piece of meat and calls softly. And after a long, tense moment, the hawk drops down onto his glove.

[long-pause]

{img:manor-village-open-fields-strips-ploughing-oxen-castle-distance}
A castle like Kenilworth does not stand alone. All around it lie the lands that feed it, and the people who work them.

[pause]

In 1250, most of England is divided into manors. A manor is an estate with a village, fields and woods. The lord owns the land. The villagers live on it and farm it. In return they work for the lord, and pay him rent.

[long-pause]

Around Kenilworth there are villages with timber houses and thatched roofs. The walls are made of woven sticks, covered with mud and straw. Each house has a little garden with vegetables, a pig and some hens.

[pause]

Around each village lie two or three big open fields. Each field is divided into long, narrow strips. Every family has strips in each field, some good land and some poor. Each year one field is left empty to rest. The animals graze there and make the soil rich again.

[pause]

The villagers grow wheat, barley and beans. They keep oxen for ploughing, sheep for wool, and pigs that eat acorns in the woods. In June they cut hay in the meadows, to feed the animals through the winter. If the hay harvest fails, it will be a hard winter for people and animals alike.

[long-pause]

Many villagers are not free. They are called serfs. They belong to the manor. They may not leave without the lord's permission. And for several days each week, they must work on the lord's own fields before they can work on their own.

[pause]

Other villagers are free. They pay rent in money, and owe less work.

[pause]

{img:reeve-supervising-villeins-lord-demesne-field-morning}
The work is watched over by the reeve. He is a villager, chosen by the others, who makes sure the work gets done. Each year, the accounts of the manor are written down by a clerk and sent to the castle.

[long-pause]

The castle needs a great deal from its manors.

[pause]

It needs grain for bread and ale, and animals for meat. It needs hay and oats for the horses, and wood for the fires. And it needs money from the rents, to buy wine, spices and cloth.

[pause]

{img:manor-court-hall-villagers-steward-fines-clerk-writing}
The lord also holds courts in the manors. Here, quarrels between villagers are settled and small fines are paid. Perhaps someone's pig got into a neighbour's corn. Perhaps someone brewed bad ale, or did not come to work on the lord's land.

[long-pause]

The people of the castle and the village depend on each other.

[pause]

Many castle servants come from the villages. The villagers sell their eggs and vegetables to the castle. In hard times, they come to the castle gate for help. They watch the lord ride out to hunt, and the lady pass by on her horse.

[pause]

And on great feast days, the villagers are sometimes invited into the hall, to share in the feast.

[long-pause]

In the village of Kenilworth there is also a rich monastery. The canons who live there are priests who live together like monks. They pray and serve the churches around.

[pause]

The monastery church is large and beautiful. Its bells ring across the fields at the hours of prayer, morning and evening. That way, the villagers in the fields know what time it is.

[pause]

{img:priory-church-bells-fields-villagers-pausing-evening-light}
When the evening bell rings, the villagers stop their work for a moment, and say a prayer. Then they walk home along the lanes, with their tools on their shoulders. They go home to their supper and their fires, in the shadow of the great red castle. They trust that the castle will protect them. They cannot know yet that one day, the castle itself will need protecting.

[long-pause]

Every year, the villagers watch the sky. Rain at the wrong time can spoil the harvest. A wet summer can mean too little grain, and too little grain means a hungry winter. The older people still remember bad years, when the bread ran out before spring.

[pause]

So when the harvest is good, the whole village celebrates. The last cart of corn is decorated with flowers and brought home with singing. And everyone hopes next year will be just as kind.

[long-pause]

{img:noble-household-travelling-road-carts-horses-baggage-train}
A great lord and lady in 1250 do not stay in one place all year.

[pause]

They own many castles and farms all over England. Simon and Eleanor have Kenilworth, Leicester, and Eleanor's own castle at Odiham, in the south. So every few weeks or months, the whole household packs up and moves.

[long-pause]

There are good reasons for this.

[pause]

The lord must visit his lands, to see that they are run well, and to be seen by his people. And a big household eats a lot. After a few weeks, the food of one place runs out. So they move on, to a place where the barns are still full. Besides, after a few weeks, a castle gets dirty, and needs a good clean.

[pause]

{img:servants-packing-chests-tapestries-bedding-castle-chamber}
So the great household is always on the move. Eleanor's accounts show them moving often, staying a few weeks in each place.

[long-pause]

Moving a great household is a huge task.

[pause]

Everything must be packed. The beds and the curtains, the tapestries and the clothes. The silver plates, the things from the chapel, and the kitchen pots. And the money, in locked chests.

[pause]

It is all loaded onto big carts pulled by horses, and onto packhorses. There are dozens of them. The marshal is in charge, and he plans everything.

[long-pause]

The journey is slow. The household travels perhaps fifteen or twenty miles a day, along muddy roads. They cross rivers at shallow places, or on bridges and ferries.

[pause]

The lord and the knights ride. The countess rides a gentle horse, or travels in a covered wagon with cushions and curtains. It looks grand, but it bumps terribly on the rough roads. The servants walk, or ride in the carts.

[pause]

{img:covered-wagon-carriage-lady-curtains-road-forest-riders}
Riders go ahead to the next stop, to make sure there is food and a place to sleep. On these roads there are robbers in the woods, so nobody travels alone if they can help it.

[long-pause]

At night they stay at one of the lord's castles, or with a friend, or at a monastery. Monasteries always welcome travellers.

[pause]

People come out of the villages to watch the great household go by. There are banners and knights, the lady in her wagon, the hounds and the hawks, and a long line of carts. Sometimes it stretches back along the road for half a mile.

[pause]

{img:villagers-watching-procession-pass-banners-knights-lane}
The beggars come too, and the almoner gives them bread and coins from the lord.

[long-pause]

When the household reaches the next castle, everything is unpacked and set up again. Within a day or two, the castle is full of life, as if they had always been there.

[pause]

In winter, travelling is harder. The roads are deep in mud, or frozen, or flooded. So the household often stays longer in one place. And Kenilworth, with its warm hall, its lake full of fish and its forest full of deer, is one of their favourite places to spend the winter.

[long-pause]

Sometimes on the road, a messenger comes galloping up with a letter for the earl. Then the whole line of carts stops, and everyone waits. The earl reads the letter in silence. Then he gives a few short orders, and they move on.

[pause]

Nobody dares ask what was in the letter. But the knights ride a little closer to the carts after that. And the guards look a little more carefully at the woods along the road.

[long-pause]

{img:tournament-field-knights-banners-pavilions-spectators-stands}
One of the most exciting things in a knight's life in 1250 is the tournament.

[pause]

A tournament in this time is not the neat jousting of later years, with two knights riding at each other along a fence. In 1250, a tournament is more like a pretend battle. It is a big, rough game, played across wide open country, between two teams of knights. Sometimes there are hundreds on each side.

[long-pause]

The knights gather from all over the country at an agreed place, in the fields between two towns. They put up tents of bright cloth, with their banners flying. Crowds come to watch. There are ladies and townspeople, and merchants selling food, drink and horses.

[pause]

On the day itself, the two teams line up across the fields. They sit on their great warhorses, in their armour, with their coats of arms on their shields.

[pause]

{img:knights-lined-up-colourful-surcoats-shields-morning-field}
At a signal, the two sides charge. They fight with lances, then with swords. The fight spreads across the fields, through the hedges and the woods, for hours, until evening.

[long-pause]

The aim is not to kill, but to capture. A knight who is captured must pay money to the knight who caught him, or give up his horse and armour. So a skilled knight can grow rich from tournaments. The most famous knight of all, William Marshal, was the father of Eleanor's first husband. It was said he captured five hundred knights in his young days.

[pause]

But tournaments are dangerous. Knights are often hurt in the crush and the confusion.

[pause]

The Church does not like tournaments, and often forbids them. King Henry forbids them too. He fears that the great lords will use them as an excuse to gather their men and plot against him. Perhaps he is right to worry. But the young knights love tournaments, and hold them anyway.

[long-pause]

In the evening there is a great feast. The heralds call out the names of the knights who fought best, and the ladies sometimes hand out the prizes.

[pause]

The heralds know every coat of arms. They can name every knight by his shield, even when his face is hidden by his helmet. They keep long lists of coats of arms, painted in rows. Some of these lists still survive today.

[pause]

{img:roll-of-arms-painted-shields-rows-parchment-herald}
Coats of arms began as a way to recognise knights in their helmets. By 1250, every noble family has its own. It passes from father to son, and it is painted on shields, banners and windows. Simon's arms, as we saw, are a white lion on a red shield.

[long-pause]

At Kenilworth itself, some years later, there will be a famous tournament called the Round Table. It is held in honour of King Arthur's knights. A hundred knights and a hundred ladies will come, for days of jousting, feasting and dancing.

[pause]

{img:castle-feast-hall-knights-ladies-dancing-round-table-candles}
Because the stories of King Arthur are the most loved stories of the age. Every young knight wants to be like Lancelot or Gawain. And in those stories, there is always a moment when a knight must choose between his king and what he believes is right. The young men of Kenilworth listen to that part very closely.

[long-pause]

Simon himself does not care much for tournaments. He thinks they are a waste of good knights. He would rather his men trained for real war. But his sons and the young knights of his household love them, and sometimes he lets them go.

[pause]

When they come home, they tell stories of every charge and every capture, louder and louder as the evening goes on. Their mother listens with a smile. And their father listens with a little frown, thinking of other battles that might one day not be a game.

[long-pause]

{img:barons-council-oxford-1258-great-hall-debate-king-seated}
The peace of Kenilworth in 1250 does not last.

[pause]

In the years that follow, King Henry quarrels more and more with his barons. He spends too much money on foreign wars and on his friends from France. He asks for more and more taxes. He does not listen to advice. Many people feel that he is ruling badly. And slowly, the trouble grows.

[long-pause]

In 1258, the barons meet at Oxford. They force the King to accept a set of new rules. From now on, the King must rule with the advice of a council. And a parliament, a great meeting of the kingdom, must come together three times a year.

[pause]

More and more, the leader of these reformers is Simon de Montfort, the King's own brother-in-law.

[pause]

Simon believes deeply that a king must obey the law, like everyone else. He swears an oath to keep the new rules, and he will not break it, whatever happens. He is a man of strong ideas, and very, very stubborn.

[long-pause]

The King breaks his promises. The quarrel grows, year by year. In 1264, it turns into a war between the King and the barons who follow Simon.

[pause]

In May 1264, near the town of Lewes, Simon's army defeats the King's army. The King and his son, Prince Edward, are captured. For more than a year, Simon rules England in the King's name.

[pause]

{img:parliament-westminster-1265-knights-burgesses-gathered-hall}
In January 1265, Simon calls a parliament at Westminster. It becomes one of the most famous in English history. For the first time, he invites not only the great lords and bishops. He also invites two knights from every county, and two ordinary citizens from many of the towns. Many historians see this as the very beginning of the English Parliament as we know it.

[long-pause]

All this time, Kenilworth is one of the great strongholds of the Montfort family. Simon's son, also called Simon, holds it for him.

[pause]

But in the summer of 1265, Prince Edward escapes. He gathers an army and turns against Simon. And now everything that Simon has built hangs in the balance.

[pause]

{img:summer-evening-castle-towers-mere-quiet-anxious-1265}
On the fourth of August 1265, near the town of Evesham, the two armies meet. A summer storm darkens the sky, with thunder in the middle of the day. We will not watch the battle. It is enough to know that Simon de Montfort lost his life there, together with his eldest son Henry, and many of his loyal knights.

[long-pause]

Simon was about fifty-seven years old.

[pause]

The ordinary people of England had loved him. They saw him as a man who stood up for the law against a bad king. After his death, many people believed he was a saint. They travelled to Evesham to pray where he fell.

[pause]

Today, at Evesham, there is a simple stone in the ruins of the old abbey, where he was buried. And in the Houses of Parliament in London, there is a statue of him.

[long-pause]

Eleanor was at Dover when the news reached her. Her grief was deep. She wore mourning clothes for the rest of her life.

[pause]

Soon afterwards she left England for France. She went to live in a convent of nuns south of Paris, which Simon's family had founded. She lived there quietly for ten more years, and died there, at the age of about sixty.

[pause]

{img:french-convent-garden-cloister-nun-widow-quiet-afternoon}
But at Kenilworth, the story was not over yet. Because the great red castle on its lake refused to give up.

[long-pause]

{img:kenilworth-surrounded-distant-camps-tents-across-mere-1266}
After Evesham, most of Simon's followers gave up or fled. But not the men of Kenilworth.

[pause]

The castle was held by a group of Simon's loyal knights and soldiers. They had lost their lands, and they had nothing left to lose. They refused to surrender. So in June 1266, King Henry himself came to Kenilworth with a great army, to lay siege to it.

[long-pause]

It became the longest siege in English history during the Middle Ages. It lasted six months, from June until December.

[pause]

We will watch it gently, from far away. Imagine we are standing on a hill above the lake. Below us lies the castle on its island. Around it lies the King's great camp, with its tents and banners.

[pause]

The King's army brought huge catapults that threw heavy stones at the walls. They brought tall wooden towers on wheels. But now the old builders' cleverness paid off. The towers could not cross the water. The King even had boats brought from far away, to try to cross the lake. But the defenders held firm.

[long-pause]

The defenders had their own catapults inside the castle, and threw stones back. It is said that sometimes the stones crashed together in the air.

[pause]

There is a story that one day, the defenders opened the gates and left them open all day. It was as if they were saying: come in, if you dare. Nobody did.

[pause]

{img:castle-gates-open-defiant-quiet-afternoon-causeway-water}
Month after month, the defenders held out, through summer and autumn. But slowly, their food began to run out. The great storerooms grew emptier each week. And in the crowded castle, people began to fall ill. How long could they last?

[long-pause]

Outside, meanwhile, the King's advisers were looking for a way to end the war and bring peace.

[pause]

In October, a group of bishops and lords met in the King's camp at Kenilworth. They wrote a peace plan, which became known as the Dictum of Kenilworth. It said the rebels could get their lands back, if they paid a fine. The worse their part in the rebellion, the bigger the fine. It was a way to forgive, and to heal the country.

[pause]

{img:bishops-writing-peace-terms-tent-parchment-seals-autumn}
At first, the defenders said no. But by December they were starving, and many were sick. At last, on the fourteenth of December, they agreed to surrender. They were allowed to leave freely, with their horses and their weapons.

[long-pause]

They walked out of the castle and along the road across the water. They were weak, thin and tired, in the cold winter air. And the King's men went in.

[pause]

The great siege was over. And with it, the war.

[pause]

The King gave Kenilworth to his younger son, Edmund. For hundreds of years after that, it belonged to his family, the House of Lancaster.

[long-pause]

Simon de Montfort had lost his life, and his cause seemed to have failed. But his ideas did not die.

[pause]

The young Prince Edward, who beat him at Evesham, became King Edward the First in 1272. And he too called parliaments, with knights from the counties and citizens from the towns, just as Simon had done. Over the centuries, Parliament grew stronger and stronger.

[pause]

{img:parliament-old-hall-westminster-later-centuries-members-seated}
So in a way, the ideas of the proud earl of Kenilworth helped to shape the government of England, and of many countries far away, right up to our own day.

[long-pause]

{img:john-of-gaunt-great-hall-tall-windows-kenilworth-fourteenth-century}
After the siege, Kenilworth was repaired. It became one of the greatest castles of the House of Lancaster.

[pause]

About a hundred and twenty years after the siege, it belonged to one of the richest men in England, John of Gaunt. He was the son of King Edward the Third.

[long-pause]

John of Gaunt turned Kenilworth from a fortress into a palace. He built a splendid new great hall of red stone. It had huge, tall windows filled with delicate stone patterns and glass. It was one of the finest halls in England, a place for feasts and music.

[pause]

His son became King Henry the Fourth. So Kenilworth became a royal castle, and the kings of England loved to stay there. King Henry the Fifth built a little summer house on the far side of the lake. He went there by boat, to eat and rest and enjoy the view.

[pause]

{img:boat-crossing-mere-to-summer-pleasure-house-evening-lanterns}
Much later, Queen Elizabeth the First gave Kenilworth to her favourite, Robert Dudley. She made him Earl of Leicester, the same title Simon de Montfort had held three hundred years before.

[long-pause]

Robert Dudley spent a fortune on Kenilworth. He built new rooms with enormous windows full of glass. At night, with all the candles lit, the castle shone across the lake like a lantern. He built a new gatehouse, and a beautiful garden with fountains and a big cage full of birds.

[pause]

In July 1575, Queen Elizabeth came to stay with him for nineteen days.

[pause]

It was one of the most famous royal visits in English history. There were feasts, plays and fireworks over the lake. There were shows on the water, with a floating island and a mermaid. There was even a huge dolphin with musicians hidden inside it.

[pause]

Some people think that a boy of eleven from the nearby town of Stratford came to watch. His name was William Shakespeare. But nobody knows for sure.

[long-pause]

Then, in the seventeenth century, England had another civil war, this time between King Charles the First and Parliament.

[pause]

After that war, Parliament ordered that Kenilworth should be damaged on purpose, so it could never be used as a fortress again. Soldiers blew up one side of the great keep and broke down the walls. They opened the dams. The water of the great lake drained away. The castle no longer stood on an island, but in green fields.

[pause]

{img:drained-mere-meadows-ruined-castle-seventeenth-century-quiet}
Some buildings, like Dudley's gatehouse, were turned into houses. The rest slowly became a ruin.

[long-pause]

In 1821, the ruins became famous again, thanks to a novel called Kenilworth by Sir Walter Scott. It told the story of Robert Dudley and the Queen's great visit. Thousands of people came to see the ruins.

[pause]

Painters painted them, and writers wrote about them. People came by train, and later by car, to picnic in the meadows and walk among the red walls.

[pause]

{img:victorian-visitors-picnic-castle-ruins-meadow-parasols}
And so the castle of Simon and Eleanor, after nearly nine hundred years, is still there.

[long-pause]

{img:kenilworth-castle-ruins-red-sandstone-green-lawns-today}
Today, Kenilworth Castle still stands on its low hill in the green Warwickshire countryside. It is a great ruin of red stone, cared for by English Heritage.

[pause]

The little town of Kenilworth has grown up around it, with houses, shops and pubs. But the castle still rises above everything, as it did in 1250.

[long-pause]

You walk in through Robert Dudley's gatehouse, which still stands, with its little turrets.

[pause]

Then you are in the outer yard, on the green grass. In front of you rise the great red walls. There is the keep, with one side blown away, standing like a broken tooth against the sky. There are the tall, empty windows of John of Gaunt's great hall, with the sky shining through them.

[pause]

You can walk along the walls and climb some of the towers on new wooden stairs. From there you look out over the fields where the great lake used to be. You can imagine the water shining all around, and the swans, and the boats.

[long-pause]

In the keep, the oldest part, you can still see the thick walls and the little windows. You can see the marks where the floors once were.

[pause]

In John of Gaunt's great hall, you stand on the grass where the floor used to be. You look up at the tall windows and the fireplaces high in the walls. And you can imagine the long tables, the music and the firelight.

[pause]

{img:great-hall-ruin-tall-tracery-windows-sky-grass-floor}
In Dudley's tower, new stairs take you up to where the Queen's rooms once were. You can stand where she stood, and look out through her windows.

[long-pause]

The Elizabethan garden has been made again, just as it was in 1575. That was possible because a man who saw it wrote a careful description at the time.

[pause]

It is a neat and lovely garden. There are sandy paths, low hedges and flowers from that time. There is a white marble fountain, and a big painted cage full of singing birds.

[pause]

In the old stables there is a café, and an exhibition that tells the castle's whole story.

[long-pause]

The land where the lake once lay is now green meadow, with cows and sheep and paths for walking.

[pause]

In winter, after heavy rain, the meadows sometimes flood. For a few days the water spreads around the castle again. And once more, the red walls are mirrored in it, as in the days of Simon and Eleanor.

[pause]

{img:flooded-meadows-castle-reflection-winter-sunset-still}
Along King John's old dam, people walk their dogs. They look across at the castle, where eight hundred years ago the King's army camped through that long siege.

[long-pause]

In summer there are knights in armour at the castle, and shows with falcons swooping over the grass. Children dressed as knights and princesses run across the lawns with wooden swords.

[pause]

And in the quiet evenings, when the visitors have gone, the red stone glows deep and warm, as it did in 1250. The swallows dip over the meadows where the lake used to be.

[pause]

It is very peaceful.

[long-pause]

So we leave the castle of Kenilworth for tonight, with the red walls glowing in the evening light, and the meadows quiet where the great lake once lay.

[pause]

We leave the lord and lady at their high table, the cook with his big wooden spoon, the squires at the quintain, and the children asleep behind the curtains of their bed, nearly eight hundred years ago.

[pause]

{img:kenilworth-dusk-silhouette-towers-meadow-mist}
If you enjoyed this evening in the castle, you are very welcome to subscribe to the channel, and to come back for another quiet story.

[pause]

There should be another quiet story waiting on the screen now, if you are still awake.

[pause]

Sleep well, and good night.
