In the outer courtyard of Kenilworth, in the afternoon, the young men are training.

There are knights, grown men, who serve the earl, and live in his household. And there are squires, young men of fifteen or sixteen or eighteen, who are learning to be knights. And pages, boys of seven or eight, or older, the sons of noble families, who have been sent to the castle to be brought up, and to learn the ways of the nobility.

--

A boy of a noble family, in 1250, does not usually grow up at home. At about seven, he is sent to live in the household of a great lord, often a relative, or a friend of his family, as a page.

As a page, he learns good manners, and courtesy: how to serve at table, and carve meat, and pour wine, and speak to ladies, and to lords, and to bishops, and how to behave in the hall, and in church. He learns to ride, and to look after horses, and dogs, and hawks. He learns to sing, and dance, and play chess, and perhaps a little reading, in French, and Latin.

And he learns to fight, with little wooden swords, and shields, and practises wrestling, and running, and jumping, and swimming.

--

At about fourteen, he becomes a squire. Now, he serves a knight, as his assistant.

He looks after his knight's horses, and armour, and weapons. He helps him to put on his armour, which is very heavy, and complicated, and cannot be done alone. He rides with him, and carries his shield, and his lance. He goes with him to tournaments, and to war, and looks after him if he is wounded.

{img:squire-helping-knight-into-chain-mail-hauberk-armoury}
And he trains, every day, for hours: riding, and fighting on horseback, with a lance, and on foot, with a sword, and a shield, and a mace, and an axe.

--

In 1250, a knight's armour is made of mail: thousands of little iron rings, linked together, like a heavy, flexible cloth.

A knight wears a long shirt of mail, called a hauberk, down to his knees, with long sleeves, and mittens of mail, and a hood of mail, over his head. And mail stockings, called chausses, on his legs. Under the mail, he wears a thick padded coat, of linen and wool, stuffed with cotton, or rags, called a gambeson, to soften the blows. Over the mail, he wears a long cloth surcoat, without sleeves, with his coat of arms on it, so that people can see who he is.

On his head, over the mail hood, he wears a helmet of iron: in 1250, often a great helm, a flat-topped cylinder of iron, covering his whole head, with narrow slits to see through, and little holes to breathe through. It is hot, and heavy, and hard to see out of. But it protects him well.

--

The most important thing for a knight is his horse.

A knight has several horses. A great warhorse, called a destrier, big, and strong, and trained for battle, very expensive, which he rides only in war, and tournaments. A riding horse, a palfrey, gentle and comfortable, for travelling. And packhorses, for his baggage. The squires look after them all.

{img:destrier-warhorse-groom-brushing-stable-sunlight}
The young men practise riding at the quintain: a wooden post, with a crossbar that turns, with a shield at one end, and a heavy bag of sand at the other. The rider gallops at it, and strikes the shield with his lance. If he is not quick enough, the crossbar swings round, and the sandbag hits him on the back of the head, and knocks him off his horse, and everyone laughs.

--

At about twenty-one, if he has done well, the squire is made a knight.

There is a ceremony, often at a great feast, like Whitsun, or Christmas, or a royal wedding. The night before, the young man takes a bath, to make himself clean, and spends the night in the chapel, praying, watching over his armour, which lies on the altar. In the morning, he hears Mass.

Then, in front of the household, and the guests, his lord, or another knight, gives him his sword, and his spurs, and dubs him, with a blow on the neck, or the shoulder, with the hand, or the flat of the sword, and says: be a good knight. And then there is a feast.

--

Knights, in the stories of the time, are supposed to be brave, and loyal, and generous, and courteous, and to protect the weak, and serve God, and their lord, and honour ladies. This is called chivalry.

Of course, real knights are not always like that. Many are rough, and violent, and greedy. But the ideals are real, and many young men try to live up to them. And they listen, in the evenings, in the hall, to the stories of the knights of King Arthur, and of Roland, and of the great heroes, and dream of being like them.

{img:minstrel-reciting-arthur-romance-hall-young-knights-listening}
Simon de Montfort himself is admired, all over Europe, as a model of a knight: brave, and pious, and skilled, and honourable. Young men come from far away to serve in his household, and learn from him.
