The first thing that every visitor notices about Kenilworth is the water.

The castle stands on a low hill of red sandstone. And all around it, on the south and the west, there is a great lake, which the people call the mere. It is about a mile long, and wide, and deep, with reeds along its edges, and swans and ducks and herons, and fish swimming in its clear water.

--

The lake is not natural. It was made by the builders of the castle, by building great dams, of earth, across the valleys of the two little streams that meet near the castle, so that the water rose up, and spread out, and filled the valleys, all around the castle hill.

One of the dams is also the main road into the castle: a long, wide causeway, across the water, leading to a great gatehouse, with towers. It is called the Tiltyard, because later, knights will hold jousting contests on it, riding along its length.

So the castle is almost an island. An enemy cannot reach the walls from the south or the west, because of the water. And the water is too deep, and too wide, for siege towers, or for tunnels under the walls. Kenilworth is almost impossible to attack.

--

The castle was first built more than a hundred years before, in about 1120, by a man called Geoffrey de Clinton, who was the treasurer of King Henry the First.

He built a great square tower, a keep, of red sandstone, on the top of the hill, with walls more than four metres thick. It still stands today. And he founded a priory, a monastery of canons, in the valley nearby, which still gives its name to the town of Kenilworth.

{img:massive-square-keep-red-sandstone-thick-walls-corner-towers}
Later, the castle passed to the kings of England. And King John, who reigned from 1199 to 1216, spent a great deal of money on it, more than on almost any other castle in England. He built the outer walls, with their towers, and made the great lake, by building the dams. He loved Kenilworth, and often stayed there.

--

After King John died, his son, Henry the Third, became king, as a boy of nine. And in 1244, King Henry gave Kenilworth to his sister Eleanor, and later to her husband, Simon de Montfort.

Simon and Eleanor love Kenilworth too. It becomes their favourite home, and their stronghold. They improve it, and make it even stronger, and more comfortable.

{img:red-stone-castle-reflected-still-water-summer-evening}
In the evening, when the sun is low, the red sandstone of the castle glows, a deep, warm, rosy colour, and the towers are reflected in the still water of the mere, upside down, among the clouds. It is one of the most beautiful sights in England.

--

Let us imagine that we are riding toward Kenilworth, on a summer day, in the year 1250.

We come along a track through the fields and the woods, from the town of Warwick, a few miles away. We pass through the little village of Kenilworth, with its houses of timber and thatch, and its church, and the great priory, with its tall church, and its monks, or rather canons, in their black cloaks.

And then, as we come over a little rise, we see the castle, across the water: the great keep, standing high above everything, with its four corner towers, and the long walls around it, and the gatehouses, and the towers, and the smoke from the kitchens, and the banners flying from the top of the keep, with the arms of Simon de Montfort: a white lion, standing on its hind legs, with a forked tail, on a red shield.

--

We ride along the causeway, across the water, with the lake shining on either side, and the swans gliding past.

At the end of the causeway, we come to the gatehouse. The great wooden gates are open, in the daytime, in times of peace. The portcullis, a heavy iron grille, hangs above our heads, ready to be dropped down in a moment, if danger comes. The guards, in their padded coats and iron helmets, look at us, and ask us our business.

{img:gatehouse-portcullis-raised-guards-visitors-entering-sunlight}
And then we ride through, into the outer courtyard of the castle, the outer bailey. And we are inside Kenilworth.
