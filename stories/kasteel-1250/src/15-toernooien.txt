One of the most exciting things in a knight's life in 1250 is the tournament.

A tournament in this time is not the neat jousting of later years, with two knights riding at each other along a fence. In 1250, a tournament is more like a pretend battle. It is a big, rough game, played across wide open country, between two teams of knights. Sometimes there are hundreds on each side.

--

The knights gather from all over the country at an agreed place, in the fields between two towns. They put up tents of bright cloth, with their banners flying. Crowds come to watch. There are ladies and townspeople, and merchants selling food, drink and horses.

On the day itself, the two teams line up across the fields. They sit on their great warhorses, in their armour, with their coats of arms on their shields.

{img:knights-lined-up-colourful-surcoats-shields-morning-field}
At a signal, the two sides charge. They fight with lances, then with swords. The fight spreads across the fields, through the hedges and the woods, for hours, until evening.

--

The aim is not to kill, but to capture. A knight who is captured must pay money to the knight who caught him, or give up his horse and armour. So a skilled knight can grow rich from tournaments. The most famous knight of all, William Marshal, was the father of Eleanor's first husband. It was said he captured five hundred knights in his young days.

But tournaments are dangerous. Knights are often hurt in the crush and the confusion.

The Church does not like tournaments, and often forbids them. King Henry forbids them too. He fears that the great lords will use them as an excuse to gather their men and plot against him. Perhaps he is right to worry. But the young knights love tournaments, and hold them anyway.

--

In the evening there is a great feast. The heralds call out the names of the knights who fought best, and the ladies sometimes hand out the prizes.

The heralds know every coat of arms. They can name every knight by his shield, even when his face is hidden by his helmet. They keep long lists of coats of arms, painted in rows. Some of these lists still survive today.

{img:roll-of-arms-painted-shields-rows-parchment-herald}
Coats of arms began as a way to recognise knights in their helmets. By 1250, every noble family has its own. It passes from father to son, and it is painted on shields, banners and windows. Simon's arms, as we saw, are a white lion on a red shield.

--

At Kenilworth itself, some years later, there will be a famous tournament called the Round Table. It is held in honour of King Arthur's knights. A hundred knights and a hundred ladies will come, for days of jousting, feasting and dancing.

{img:castle-feast-hall-knights-ladies-dancing-round-table-candles}
Because the stories of King Arthur are the most loved stories of the age. Every young knight wants to be like Lancelot or Gawain. And in those stories, there is always a moment when a knight must choose between his king and what he believes is right. The young men of Kenilworth listen to that part very closely.

--

Simon himself does not care much for tournaments. He thinks they are a waste of good knights. He would rather his men trained for real war. But his sons and the young knights of his household love them, and sometimes he lets them go.

When they come home, they tell stories of every charge and every capture, louder and louder as the evening goes on. Their mother listens with a smile. And their father listens with a little frown, thinking of other battles that might one day not be a game.
