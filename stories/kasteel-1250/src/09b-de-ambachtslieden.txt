A great castle is like a little town, with its own craftsmen, who keep everything working.

The most important is the smith. His forge stands in the outer courtyard, with a great hearth, and bellows, worked by a boy, and an anvil, and hammers, and tongs. All day long, the sound of his hammer rings across the courtyard.

--

He makes and mends everything of iron: horseshoes, and nails, for the castle's many horses. Hinges, and locks, and keys, and bolts, for the doors and the gates. Tools, for the carpenters, and the masons, and the gardeners. Pots, and spits, and hooks, and chains, for the kitchen. Ploughshares, and sickles, and scythes, for the farms.

And sometimes, he mends armour, and weapons, for the knights, although the finest armour, the mail shirts and the helmets, is made by specialist armourers, in the towns, and is very expensive. A mail shirt is made of perhaps thirty thousand little iron rings, each one riveted shut, by hand. It takes months to make.

{img:armourer-linking-mail-rings-workbench-careful-light}
There is the farrier, who shoes the horses, and looks after their feet, and treats them when they are sick. There is the saddler, who makes and mends saddles, and bridles, and harness. There is the bowyer, who makes bows, and the fletcher, who makes arrows, with goose feathers.

--

There are the carpenters, who make and mend everything of wood: the roofs, and the floors, and the doors, and the shutters, and the tables, and the benches, and the beds, and the chests, and the carts, and the barrels, and the scaffolding for the masons.

And there are the masons, who look after the stone walls, and repair them, and build new buildings. A castle is never finished. There is always something being built, or repaired, or improved.

{img:masons-repairing-castle-wall-scaffold-ropes-pulley-summer}
There is the plumber, whose name comes from plumbum, the Latin word for lead, who works with lead: making the lead sheets for the roofs, and the pipes for water, and the gutters.

--

There is the glazier, who makes the windows, when there is glass, which is still rare and precious in 1250. He sets little pieces of glass, greenish and full of bubbles, into strips of lead, to make windows. Most windows in the castle have no glass at all, only wooden shutters, or oiled cloth, or thin sheets of horn, which let in a little light.

There are the tailors, and the furriers, who make the clothes of the household, from the cloth and the furs bought by the wardrober. And the laundresses, who wash the linen, the sheets, and the tablecloths, and the shirts, in great tubs, with water from the well, and lye, made from wood ash, and spread them on the grass, or on hedges, to dry in the sun.

{img:laundresses-washing-linen-tubs-drying-on-hedges-sunny}
Without all these people, and their skills, and their hard work, the great castle could not live, and the lord and lady could not live in it. They are the quiet, busy heart of Kenilworth.
