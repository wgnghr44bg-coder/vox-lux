A castle like Kenilworth does not stand alone. All around it lie the lands that feed it, and the people who work them.

In 1250, most of England is divided into manors. A manor is an estate with a village, fields and woods. The lord owns the land. The villagers live on it and farm it. In return they work for the lord, and pay him rent.

--

Around Kenilworth there are villages with timber houses and thatched roofs. The walls are made of woven sticks, covered with mud and straw. Each house has a little garden with vegetables, a pig and some hens.

Around each village lie two or three big open fields. Each field is divided into long, narrow strips. Every family has strips in each field, some good land and some poor. Each year one field is left empty to rest. The animals graze there and make the soil rich again.

The villagers grow wheat, barley and beans. They keep oxen for ploughing, sheep for wool, and pigs that eat acorns in the woods. In June they cut hay in the meadows, to feed the animals through the winter. If the hay harvest fails, it will be a hard winter for people and animals alike.

--

Many villagers are not free. They are called serfs. They belong to the manor. They may not leave without the lord's permission. And for several days each week, they must work on the lord's own fields before they can work on their own.

Other villagers are free. They pay rent in money, and owe less work.

{img:reeve-supervising-villeins-lord-demesne-field-morning}
The work is watched over by the reeve. He is a villager, chosen by the others, who makes sure the work gets done. Each year, the accounts of the manor are written down by a clerk and sent to the castle.

--

The castle needs a great deal from its manors.

It needs grain for bread and ale, and animals for meat. It needs hay and oats for the horses, and wood for the fires. And it needs money from the rents, to buy wine, spices and cloth.

{img:manor-court-hall-villagers-steward-fines-clerk-writing}
The lord also holds courts in the manors. Here, quarrels between villagers are settled and small fines are paid. Perhaps someone's pig got into a neighbour's corn. Perhaps someone brewed bad ale, or did not come to work on the lord's land.

--

The people of the castle and the village depend on each other.

Many castle servants come from the villages. The villagers sell their eggs and vegetables to the castle. In hard times, they come to the castle gate for help. They watch the lord ride out to hunt, and the lady pass by on her horse.

And on great feast days, the villagers are sometimes invited into the hall, to share in the feast.

--

In the village of Kenilworth there is also a rich monastery. The canons who live there are priests who live together like monks. They pray and serve the churches around.

The monastery church is large and beautiful. Its bells ring across the fields at the hours of prayer, morning and evening. That way, the villagers in the fields know what time it is.

{img:priory-church-bells-fields-villagers-pausing-evening-light}
When the evening bell rings, the villagers stop their work for a moment, and say a prayer. Then they walk home along the lanes, with their tools on their shoulders. They go home to their supper and their fires, in the shadow of the great red castle. They trust that the castle will protect them. They cannot know yet that one day, the castle itself will need protecting.

--

Every year, the villagers watch the sky. Rain at the wrong time can spoil the harvest. A wet summer can mean too little grain, and too little grain means a hungry winter. The older people still remember bad years, when the bread ran out before spring.

So when the harvest is good, the whole village celebrates. The last cart of corn is decorated with flowers and brought home with singing. And everyone hopes next year will be just as kind.
