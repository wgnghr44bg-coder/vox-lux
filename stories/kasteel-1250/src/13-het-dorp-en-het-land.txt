A castle like Kenilworth does not stand alone. All around it, there are the lands that feed it, and the people who work them.

In 1250, most of England is divided into manors. A manor is an estate, belonging to a lord, with a village, and fields, and meadows, and woods. The lord owns the land. The villagers live on it, and farm it, and in return, they work for the lord, and pay him rents, in money, and in produce.

--

Around Kenilworth, there are villages, with houses of timber, and wattle and daub, woven sticks covered with mud and straw, and thatched roofs. Each house has a little garden, called a croft, with vegetables, and herbs, and fruit trees, and a pig, and some hens.

Around the village, there are the great open fields, two or three of them, each divided into long, narrow strips. Each family has strips scattered in each field, some good land, some bad. One field is left fallow, unplanted, each year, to rest, and the animals graze on it, and manure it.

The villagers grow wheat, and barley, and oats, and rye, and peas, and beans. They keep cattle, and oxen, for ploughing, and sheep, for wool, and pigs, which run in the woods, eating acorns. There are meadows, by the streams, where hay is cut in June, for the animals in winter.

--

Many of the villagers are serfs, also called villeins, who are not free. They belong to the manor. They cannot leave it without the lord's permission, or marry their daughters without paying a fee, and they must work on the lord's own land, called the demesne, for several days a week, ploughing, and sowing, and harvesting, and carting, before they can work on their own strips.

Others are free tenants, who pay rent in money, and owe less work.

{img:reeve-supervising-villeins-lord-demesne-field-morning}
The lord's steward, and his bailiff, and the reeve, a villager chosen by the others, manage the manor, and make sure the work is done, and the rents are paid, and keep accounts, written on parchment, by a clerk, and sent to the castle, every year.

--

The castle needs a great deal from its manors.

Grain, for bread and ale. Cattle, and sheep, and pigs, for meat. Hay, and oats, for the horses. Wood, for the fires, and for building. Eggs, and cheese, and butter. Carts, and men, to carry things. And money, from the rents, to buy wine, and spices, and cloth, and to pay the servants.

{img:manor-court-hall-villagers-steward-fines-clerk-writing}
The lord also holds courts, in the manors, and at the castle, called manor courts, where disputes between villagers are settled, and fines are paid, for breaking the customs of the manor: for letting a pig into a neighbour's corn, or for brewing bad ale, or for not doing your work on the lord's land, or for a quarrel.

--

The people of the castle and the people of the village are close neighbours, and depend on each other.

Many of the castle's servants come from the villages. The villagers sell their eggs, and chickens, and vegetables, to the castle. They come to the castle gate, for alms, in hard times. They come to the castle courts, for justice. They watch the lord ride out, to hunt, and the lady pass by, on her palfrey, with her ladies.

And on great feast days, at Christmas, and Easter, and Whitsun, the villagers are sometimes invited to the castle, to eat in the hall, and share in the feast.

--

In the village of Kenilworth, there is also the great priory, of the Augustinian canons, founded by Geoffrey de Clinton, a hundred and thirty years before. The canons are priests, who live together, like monks, and pray, and serve the churches around.

The priory is rich, and has its own lands, and its own tenants. Its church is large and beautiful, and the bells ring out across the fields, at the hours of prayer, morning and evening, so that the villagers, working in the fields, know the time of day.

{img:priory-church-bells-fields-villagers-pausing-evening-light}
And when the bells ring for evening prayers, the villagers stop their work, and stand, for a moment, in the fields, and say a prayer. And then they go home, along the lanes, with their tools on their shoulders, to their supper, and their families, and their fires, in the shadow of the great red castle.
