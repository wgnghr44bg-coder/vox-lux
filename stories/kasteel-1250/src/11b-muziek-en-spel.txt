In the long evenings, in the castle, there is music, and stories, and games.

A great household has its own minstrels: musicians, who play for the lord and lady, at dinner, and in the evenings, and at feasts. And wandering minstrels come to the castle too, travelling from one great house to another, with their instruments on their backs, hoping for a meal, and a bed, and a gift of money.

--

The instruments of 1250 are many.

The harp, small, and light, held on the knee, or against the shoulder, with strings of gut, or metal. The fiddle, called the vielle, played with a bow, held against the chest, or the shoulder. The psaltery, a flat wooden box with strings, plucked with the fingers, or a quill. The lute, newly arrived from the Arab lands, through Spain. Pipes, and flutes, and recorders. Bagpipes. Little drums, called tabors, played with one hand, while the other plays a pipe. Bells, and cymbals, and trumpets, for great occasions.

{img:medieval-instruments-vielle-psaltery-harp-table-chamber}
The minstrels play dances, for the lords and ladies to dance to: round dances, and carols, with everyone holding hands, in a circle, or a long line, singing as they dance. And they sing songs, love songs, and ballads, in French, and sometimes in English.

--

And they tell stories, long stories, in verse, sung or chanted to the music of the harp: the romances.

The stories of King Arthur, and his queen, Guinevere, and the knights of the Round Table, Lancelot, and Gawain, and Tristan and Isolde, and the quest for the Holy Grail. The stories of Charlemagne, and his knights, Roland and Oliver. The stories of Alexander the Great, and of Troy, and of the crusades. Some of these stories take many evenings to tell, a little each night.

One of the most famous songs of the time is in English, and is still sung today: Sumer is icumen in, lhude sing cuccu. Summer is coming in, loudly sing, cuckoo. It was written down, in about 1250, at the abbey of Reading. It is one of the oldest songs in English that we can still sing.

--

There are games too.

Chess is the great game of the nobility. The pieces are carved from ivory, or bone, or wood, or walrus tusk: the king, and the queen, and the bishops, and the knights, and the rooks, and the pawns. Lords and ladies play chess together, in the chamber, by the fire, for hours. Knowing how to play chess well is a mark of good breeding.

{img:lord-and-lady-playing-chess-ivory-pieces-fireside-chamber}
There are other board games: tables, which is like backgammon, played with dice, and counters. And merels, a game of lines and counters, like noughts and crosses, but more complicated, which is often scratched into stone benches, or window sills, so that guards, and servants, can play it while they wait.

--

People play dice, too, and gamble, although the Church disapproves. Playing cards will not come to Europe for more than a hundred years. But they play riddles, and word games, and blind man's buff, and hot cockles, a game where one person kneels with their eyes covered, and the others hit them, gently, on the hand, and they must guess who did it.

And on summer evenings, they play outside, in the courtyards, and the meadows: bowls, and quoits, and ball games, and archery contests.

{img:summer-evening-meadow-bowls-archery-household-relaxing-castle}
And sometimes, there are visitors who bring entertainment: jugglers, and acrobats, and tumblers, and men with dancing bears, and performing dogs, and fools, and jesters, who tell jokes, and make everyone laugh, even the stern earl.
