A great lord and lady in 1250 do not stay in one place all year.

They own many castles and farms all over England. Simon and Eleanor have Kenilworth, Leicester, and Eleanor's own castle at Odiham, in the south. So every few weeks or months, the whole household packs up and moves.

--

There are good reasons for this.

The lord must visit his lands, to see that they are run well, and to be seen by his people. And a big household eats a lot. After a few weeks, the food of one place runs out. So they move on, to a place where the barns are still full. Besides, after a few weeks, a castle gets dirty, and needs a good clean.

{img:servants-packing-chests-tapestries-bedding-castle-chamber}
So the great household is always on the move. Eleanor's accounts show them moving often, staying a few weeks in each place.

--

Moving a great household is a huge task.

Everything must be packed. The beds and the curtains, the tapestries and the clothes. The silver plates, the things from the chapel, and the kitchen pots. And the money, in locked chests.

It is all loaded onto big carts pulled by horses, and onto packhorses. There are dozens of them. The marshal is in charge, and he plans everything.

--

The journey is slow. The household travels perhaps fifteen or twenty miles a day, along muddy roads. They cross rivers at shallow places, or on bridges and ferries.

The lord and the knights ride. The countess rides a gentle horse, or travels in a covered wagon with cushions and curtains. It looks grand, but it bumps terribly on the rough roads. The servants walk, or ride in the carts.

{img:covered-wagon-carriage-lady-curtains-road-forest-riders}
Riders go ahead to the next stop, to make sure there is food and a place to sleep. On these roads there are robbers in the woods, so nobody travels alone if they can help it.

--

At night they stay at one of the lord's castles, or with a friend, or at a monastery. Monasteries always welcome travellers.

People come out of the villages to watch the great household go by. There are banners and knights, the lady in her wagon, the hounds and the hawks, and a long line of carts. Sometimes it stretches back along the road for half a mile.

{img:villagers-watching-procession-pass-banners-knights-lane}
The beggars come too, and the almoner gives them bread and coins from the lord.

--

When the household reaches the next castle, everything is unpacked and set up again. Within a day or two, the castle is full of life, as if they had always been there.

In winter, travelling is harder. The roads are deep in mud, or frozen, or flooded. So the household often stays longer in one place. And Kenilworth, with its warm hall, its lake full of fish and its forest full of deer, is one of their favourite places to spend the winter.

--

Sometimes on the road, a messenger comes galloping up with a letter for the earl. Then the whole line of carts stops, and everyone waits. The earl reads the letter in silence. Then he gives a few short orders, and they move on.

Nobody dares ask what was in the letter. But the knights ride a little closer to the carts after that. And the guards look a little more carefully at the woods along the road.
