A great lord and lady, in 1250, do not stay in one place all year.

They have many castles, and manors, all over England, and sometimes in France too. Simon and Eleanor have Kenilworth, and Leicester, and Odiham, in Hampshire, which is Eleanor's own castle, and many manors. And so, every few weeks, or months, the whole household packs up, and moves, from one place to another.

--

There are good reasons for this.

The lord needs to visit his lands, to see that they are well run, and to hold his courts, and to be seen by his tenants. The food of one manor runs out, after a few weeks of feeding a great household, so the household moves on, to another, where the barns are full. And after a few weeks, a castle becomes dirty, and needs to be cleaned, especially the latrines.

{img:servants-packing-chests-tapestries-bedding-castle-chamber}
And so, the great household is always on the move. Eleanor's accounts show her household moving often, from one castle to another, staying a few weeks in each place.

--

Moving a great household is a huge undertaking.

Everything must be packed: the beds, and the bedding, and the curtains, and the tapestries, and the clothes, and the furs, and the silver and gold plate, and the chapel things, the altar cloths and the chalice and the books, and the kitchen pots and pans, and the accounts, and the money, in locked chests, and the spices, and the wine.

Everything is loaded into great carts, with four wheels, pulled by teams of horses, and onto packhorses. Dozens of carts and horses. The marshal of the household is in charge, and organizes everything.

--

The journey is slow. A great household might travel fifteen or twenty miles a day, along the muddy roads, through the fields and the forests, and across the rivers, by fords, and bridges, and ferries.

The lord and the knights ride on their horses. The countess rides a palfrey, a gentle horse, or travels in a wagon, a heavy, covered carriage, with cushions, and curtains, painted and decorated, which is very uncomfortable on the rough roads. The children ride, or travel with their nurses. The servants walk, or ride in the carts.

{img:covered-wagon-carriage-lady-curtains-road-forest-riders}
Ahead of the household, the harbingers ride, to the next stopping place, to make sure that there is food, and lodging, and stabling for the horses.

--

At night, they stay at a castle, or a manor, belonging to the lord, or a friend, or at a monastery, where travellers are always welcome, or at an inn, in a town.

On the way, people come out of the villages to watch the great household pass by: the banners, and the horses, and the knights, and the carts, and the lady in her carriage, and the hounds, and the hawks on their perches, and the servants, and the cooks, and the long line of baggage, stretching back along the road for half a mile.

{img:villagers-watching-procession-pass-banners-knights-lane}
The beggars come too, and the almoner gives them money, and bread, from the lord.

--

And when the household arrives at the next castle, everything is unpacked, and set up again: the beds, and the tapestries, and the chapel, and the kitchen. And within a day or two, the castle is full of life again, and the household goes on, as if it had never moved.

In winter, the travelling is harder, because the roads are deep in mud, or frozen, or flooded. So the household often stays longer in one place, in winter. And Kenilworth, with its great hall, and its comfortable chambers, and its lake full of fish, and its forest full of deer, is one of the favourite places to spend the winter.
