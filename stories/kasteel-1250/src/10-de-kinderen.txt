What is life like for the children of Kenilworth, in 1250?

The children of the earl and countess, Henry, and Simon, and Amaury, and Guy, and the youngest, are brought up in the castle, with their nurses, and their tutors, and the other children of the household.

--

When they are babies, they are looked after by a nurse, a wet nurse, who feeds them, because great ladies do not usually feed their own babies. The babies are wrapped tightly in long strips of linen, called swaddling, so that they cannot move their arms and legs, because people believe this will help them grow straight. They sleep in wooden cradles, which can be rocked.

When they are a little older, they play.

They play with toys: little wooden horses, and knights, made of tin or wood, and dolls, and balls, and hoops, and tops, and windmills on sticks, and whistles. They play games: hide and seek, and blind man's buff, and leapfrog, and hopscotch. They play in the gardens, and the courtyards, and along the shore of the mere, and they fish, and swim, in the summer, and skate on the ice, in winter, with skates made of bones.

--

The boys of noble families learn to ride as soon as they can sit on a pony. They learn to use a little bow, and arrows, and to throw a spear, and to fight with wooden swords.

And they learn to read. The sons of Simon de Montfort are educated very carefully, because their father is a learned man, and believes in education. They learn to read and write in Latin, and French. Simon even sends his older sons to be taught by his friend, Robert Grosseteste, the great bishop of Lincoln, in his household, one of the greatest scholars of the age.

{img:boys-learning-latin-tutor-book-wax-tablets-castle-room}
Grosseteste writes to Simon, praising his sons, and saying that they are doing well in their lessons, and in good manners.

--

Girls are brought up mostly by their mothers, and the ladies of the household.

They learn to sew, and embroider, and spin, and weave. They learn to run a household, and to manage servants, and accounts. They learn good manners, and how to dress, and how to behave at table, and in the hall. Many learn to read, in French, and some in Latin, so that they can read their prayer books. They learn to ride, and to hawk, and to dance, and to sing, and to play the harp.

{img:young-girl-learning-embroidery-mother-ladies-window}
The little daughter of Simon and Eleanor, also called Eleanor, will be born in 1252. She will grow up to marry the Prince of Wales, Llywelyn ap Gruffudd, and become Princess of Wales.

--

The children of the servants grow up in the castle too.

They help their parents, in the kitchen, and the stables, and the laundry, and the gardens, from a young age. They fetch water, and wood, and feed the chickens, and scare the birds away from the crops, and turn the spits, and run errands. They play in the courtyards, when they can, with the other children.

Childhood, in 1250, is shorter than today. At twelve or fourteen, a child is almost an adult, and works like an adult. Girls of noble families are often married at fourteen or fifteen, and boys a little older. But the young are loved, and cared for, and many letters, and poems, and stories, from the Middle Ages, show how much parents loved their children, and grieved when they were ill.

--

In the evening, in the chamber of the countess, the children gather around their mother, by the fire, and listen to stories: stories from the Bible, and the lives of the saints, and the old romances, of knights and dragons and enchanted forests, and fairies, and the great deeds of their own ancestors.

And they say their prayers, and are put to bed, in their little beds, in their own chamber, with their nurses, and the curtains drawn around them, against the cold.

{img:children-asleep-curtained-bed-nurse-candle-castle-night}
Outside, the wind blows across the mere, and the guards walk on the walls. But the children of Kenilworth sleep, warm and safe, behind the thick red walls of the castle.
