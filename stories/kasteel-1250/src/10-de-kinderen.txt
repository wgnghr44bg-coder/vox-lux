What is life like for the children of Kenilworth in 1250?

The sons of the earl and countess grow up in the castle. They have nurses and teachers, and other children of the household to play with.

--

When they are babies, a nurse feeds them, because great ladies usually do not. The babies are wrapped tightly in long strips of linen, so they cannot move their arms and legs. People believe this helps them grow straight. They sleep in wooden cradles that can be rocked.

When they are older, they play.

They have little wooden horses and knights, dolls, balls and spinning tops. They play hide and seek and leapfrog. In summer they fish and swim along the shore of the lake. In winter they skate on the ice, with skates made of bone.

--

The boys learn to ride as soon as they can sit on a pony. They learn to shoot a small bow and to fight with wooden swords.

And they learn to read. Simon is a learned man and he believes in good schooling. His sons learn Latin and French. He even sends the older boys to his friend, the great Bishop of Lincoln, one of the wisest men of the age.

{img:boys-learning-latin-tutor-book-wax-tablets-castle-room}
The bishop writes back to Simon. He says the boys are doing well, both in their lessons and in their manners. Simon is proud. He does not know yet what hard roads his sons will have to travel.

--

Girls are raised mostly by their mothers and the ladies of the household.

They learn to sew and to weave. They learn to run a household and keep accounts. Many learn to read in French, so they can read their prayer books. They learn to ride, to dance, and to play the harp.

{img:young-girl-learning-embroidery-mother-ladies-window}
Simon and Eleanor's daughter will be born in 1252. When she grows up, she will marry the Prince of Wales, and become Princess of Wales herself.

--

The children of the servants grow up in the castle too.

From a young age, they help their parents in the kitchen, the stables and the gardens. They fetch water, feed the chickens and run errands. When they can, they play in the yards with the other children.

Childhood in 1250 is shorter than today. At twelve or fourteen, a child is almost grown up. But children are deeply loved. Old letters and poems show how much parents cared for them, and how they worried when a child was ill.

--

In the evening, the children gather around their mother by the fire. They listen to stories from the Bible and stories of saints. They hear old tales of knights and dragons and magic forests.

Then they say their prayers. They are put to bed in their own room, with the curtains drawn against the cold.

{img:children-asleep-curtained-bed-nurse-candle-castle-night}
Outside, the wind blows across the lake, and the guards walk on the walls. But the children of Kenilworth sleep warm and safe behind the thick red walls.

--

Sometimes the boys are allowed to watch the knights train in the yard. They sit on a wall, swinging their legs, and cheer when someone is knocked off his horse. Then they run off to play knights themselves, with sticks for swords and a bucket for a helmet.

Their father watches them from a window now and then, and smiles. He was once a boy like them, in a castle in France. He knows how fast these years go by. And he wants his sons to be ready for whatever life may bring.
