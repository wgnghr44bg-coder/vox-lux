Let us walk through Kenilworth and see what it is like inside.

The castle has two big yards, called baileys. The outer bailey is a large open space inside the outer walls. Along the walls lean many smaller buildings. There are stables for the horses, barns, workshops and a forge. There is also a well.

--

The outer bailey is busy all day. Horses are led in and out and brushed and fed. Carts arrive with wood, hay and barrels of wine. The smith hammers at his anvil, mending horseshoes and armour. Children run about, and the dogs bark at everything.

The outer walls are thick and high. On top there is a narrow walkway behind the battlements. Battlements are the up-and-down stones at the top of a wall. Archers can stand between them to shoot, and hide behind them when they need to.

In the walls there are narrow slits, called arrow loops. They are thin on the outside and wide on the inside. An archer inside can see and shoot in many directions. But an enemy outside can hardly hit him.

--

Through another gate lies the inner bailey, at the top of the hill. Here stand the most important buildings.

Here is the great keep, the oldest part of the castle. It is a huge square tower with walls four metres thick. By 1250, the lord and lady no longer live in it. It is too dark and gloomy, with tiny windows. Now it is used for storing food and weapons. And it is the last safe place, if the castle is ever attacked.

{img:keep-interior-dim-thick-walls-stores-barrels-small-windows}
The family now lives in newer buildings around the inner yard. These have bigger windows and proper fireplaces.

--

The most important of these is the great hall.

It is a long, tall room with a high wooden roof. At one end there is a raised floor, called the dais. Here the lord and lady sit at the high table, with their family and their best guests. Everyone else sits on benches at long tables down the sides.

{img:great-hall-timber-roof-dais-high-table-trestles-tapestries}
The great hall is the heart of the castle. Here everyone eats together. Here the lord welcomes visitors and settles quarrels. Here there are feasts, music and stories. And at night, some of the servants sleep here on the floor, wrapped in their cloaks by the fire.

--

Behind the high table are the private rooms of the lord and lady.

These are the most comfortable rooms in the castle. The walls are painted, or hung with tapestries to keep out the cold air. There is a fireplace with a chimney. The best windows even have a little glass, which is very costly. There is a great bed with curtains all around it, and feather mattresses and furs.

Here the lord and lady sleep and dress. Here they talk in private, and count their money, and pray.

--

At the other end of the hall there are three doors. One leads to the buttery, where the wine and ale are kept in barrels. One leads to the pantry, where the bread is kept. The third leads along a covered path to the kitchen.

The kitchen is a separate stone building, because fire is always a danger. It has huge fireplaces and ovens. It is always hot, smoky and busy.

{img:castle-chapel-small-stone-altar-candles-painted-saints}
There is also a chapel, a small and beautiful stone room with painted walls and candles. Every day the castle priest says Mass here for the family.

There are towers with rooms for guests and for the children. There are storerooms for cloth, spices and other precious things. And there are toilets, called garderobes. They are little rooms built out from the walls, with a stone seat and a long drop down into the water below.

--

Everything in the castle has two jobs. It must keep a big household warm and well fed. And it must keep enemies out. For now, only the first job matters. But the walls are ready, just in case.

--

If we climb the stairs to the top of the walls, we can see everything. The castle yards lie below us like a little town. Beyond the walls lies the shining lake, and beyond the lake, the green fields and the dark line of the forest.

On a clear day you can see for miles. That is the point. From up here, the guards would see any danger long before it arrived. They would see the dust of riders on the road, or the smoke of a camp in the woods. In 1250 they see only farmers and carts. But they keep looking all the same.
