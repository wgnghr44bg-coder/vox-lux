Let us walk through the castle of Kenilworth, and see what it is like inside.

The castle has two great courtyards, called baileys. The outer bailey is a large open space, inside the outer walls, with grass, and paths, and many buildings, of timber and stone, leaning against the walls: stables for the horses, and barns, and storehouses, and workshops, and houses for some of the servants, and pens for animals, and a forge, and a well.

--

The outer bailey is busy all day long. Horses are led in and out of the stables, and groomed, and fed. Carts arrive, with wood, and hay, and grain, and barrels of wine. The smith hammers at his anvil, mending horseshoes, and armour, and tools. The carpenters saw wood. Children run about. Dogs bark. Chickens scratch in the dust.

The outer walls are thick, and high, with a walkway along the top, behind the battlements, the crenellations, the up-and-down tops of the walls, where archers can stand and shoot, and hide behind the stone. At intervals, there are towers, sticking out from the walls, so that the defenders can shoot along the face of the walls.

In the walls, there are narrow slits, called arrow loops, for the archers. They are narrow on the outside, and wide on the inside, so that an archer inside can see and shoot in many directions, but an enemy outside can hardly hit him.

--

Then, through another gate, there is the inner bailey, at the top of the hill, with the most important buildings.

There is the great keep, the oldest part of the castle, a huge square tower, of red sandstone, with walls four metres thick. In 1250, it is used for storing things, and as a last refuge, if the castle is attacked, and perhaps for some private rooms. But the lord and lady no longer live in it. It is too dark, and gloomy, and uncomfortable, with its tiny windows.

{img:keep-interior-dim-thick-walls-stores-barrels-small-windows}
Instead, they live in newer buildings, around the inner courtyard, which are more comfortable, with larger windows, and fireplaces.

--

The most important of these is the great hall.

It is a long, tall building, with a high roof of timber, and large windows, and a great fireplace, or a hearth in the middle of the floor, with smoke rising up to a hole in the roof. At one end, there is a raised platform, called the dais, where the lord and lady sit, at the high table, with their family, and their most important guests. Along the sides, there are long trestle tables, and benches, where everyone else sits.

{img:great-hall-timber-roof-dais-high-table-trestles-tapestries}
The great hall is the heart of the castle. Here, everyone eats together, at the great meals of the day. Here, the lord holds his court, and receives visitors, and does justice. Here, there are feasts, and music, and dancing, and storytelling. And at night, some of the servants sleep here, on the floor, wrapped in their cloaks, by the fire.

--

Beside the great hall, at the end behind the dais, are the private rooms of the lord and lady, called the chamber, or the solar.

These are the most comfortable rooms in the castle. The walls are plastered, and painted, with patterns, and pictures, or hung with tapestries, and painted cloths, to keep out the draughts. There is a fireplace, with a chimney. The windows have wooden shutters, and in the best rooms, a little glass, which is very expensive. There is a great bed, with curtains all around it, and feather mattresses, and linen sheets, and furs.

Here, the lord and lady sleep, and dress, and talk privately with their friends and advisers, and do their accounts, and pray, and rest.

--

At the other end of the great hall, there is a passage, with three doors. One leads to the buttery, where the drinks are kept, in barrels, wine and ale. One leads to the pantry, where the bread is kept. And the third leads, along a covered passage, to the kitchen.

The kitchen is a separate building, of stone, because of the danger of fire. It has huge fireplaces, and ovens, and is always hot, and smoky, and busy.

{img:castle-chapel-small-stone-altar-candles-painted-saints}
And there is the chapel, a small, beautiful stone building, with painted walls, and a little altar, and candles, where the castle's priest, the chaplain, says Mass every day for the lord and lady and their household.

There are towers, too, with rooms for guests, and for the household officers, and for the children, and for the ladies who serve the countess. There are wardrobes, rooms for storing clothes and cloth and furs and spices and precious things. And there are latrines, called garderobes, little rooms built out from the walls, with a stone seat, and a shaft that drops down into the moat, or the lake, below.

--

Everything in the castle is designed for two things: to keep out enemies, and to keep a great household comfortable, and well fed, and in order. And it is the household that we shall meet next.
