After Evesham, most of Simon's followers gave up or fled. But not the men of Kenilworth.

The castle was held by a group of Simon's loyal knights and soldiers. They had lost their lands, and they had nothing left to lose. They refused to surrender. So in June 1266, King Henry himself came to Kenilworth with a great army, to lay siege to it.

--

It became the longest siege in English history during the Middle Ages. It lasted six months, from June until December.

We will watch it gently, from far away. Imagine we are standing on a hill above the lake. Below us lies the castle on its island. Around it lies the King's great camp, with its tents and banners.

The King's army brought huge catapults that threw heavy stones at the walls. They brought tall wooden towers on wheels. But now the old builders' cleverness paid off. The towers could not cross the water. The King even had boats brought from far away, to try to cross the lake. But the defenders held firm.

--

The defenders had their own catapults inside the castle, and threw stones back. It is said that sometimes the stones crashed together in the air.

There is a story that one day, the defenders opened the gates and left them open all day. It was as if they were saying: come in, if you dare. Nobody did.

{img:castle-gates-open-defiant-quiet-afternoon-causeway-water}
Month after month, the defenders held out, through summer and autumn. But slowly, their food began to run out. The great storerooms grew emptier each week. And in the crowded castle, people began to fall ill. How long could they last?

--

Outside, meanwhile, the King's advisers were looking for a way to end the war and bring peace.

In October, a group of bishops and lords met in the King's camp at Kenilworth. They wrote a peace plan, which became known as the Dictum of Kenilworth. It said the rebels could get their lands back, if they paid a fine. The worse their part in the rebellion, the bigger the fine. It was a way to forgive, and to heal the country.

{img:bishops-writing-peace-terms-tent-parchment-seals-autumn}
At first, the defenders said no. But by December they were starving, and many were sick. At last, on the fourteenth of December, they agreed to surrender. They were allowed to leave freely, with their horses and their weapons.

--

They walked out of the castle and along the road across the water. They were weak, thin and tired, in the cold winter air. And the King's men went in.

The great siege was over. And with it, the war.

The King gave Kenilworth to his younger son, Edmund. For hundreds of years after that, it belonged to his family, the House of Lancaster.

--

Simon de Montfort had lost his life, and his cause seemed to have failed. But his ideas did not die.

The young Prince Edward, who beat him at Evesham, became King Edward the First in 1272. And he too called parliaments, with knights from the counties and citizens from the towns, just as Simon had done. Over the centuries, Parliament grew stronger and stronger.

{img:parliament-old-hall-westminster-later-centuries-members-seated}
So in a way, the ideas of the proud earl of Kenilworth helped to shape the government of England, and of many countries far away, right up to our own day.
