After Evesham, most of Simon de Montfort's supporters surrendered, or fled. But not the men of Kenilworth.

The castle was held by a group of Simon's loyal followers, knights and soldiers, who had lost their lands, and had nothing left to lose. They refused to surrender. And so, in June 1266, King Henry himself came to Kenilworth, with a great army, to besiege it.

--

It was the longest siege in English history, before the end of the Middle Ages. It lasted for six months, from June until December, 1266.

We will watch it gently, and from a distance, as if we were standing on a hill above the mere, far away, looking down at the castle on its island, and the great camp of the King's army, spread out around it, with its tents and banners.

The King's army brought great siege engines: huge catapults, called trebuchets, which threw heavy stones at the walls. And siege towers, tall wooden towers on wheels. But the great mere protected the castle. The siege towers could not reach the walls across the water. The King even sent for barges, all the way from Chester, to try to cross the lake. But the defenders were too strong.

--

The defenders, too, had their own engines, inside the castle, and threw stones back, and it is said that sometimes the stones crashed together in the air, between the walls and the camp.

There is a story that, at one point, the defenders opened the gates, and left them open, all day, as if to say: come in, if you dare. And nobody did.

{img:castle-gates-open-defiant-quiet-afternoon-causeway-water}
Inside the castle, the defenders held out, month after month, through the summer, and the autumn. But slowly, their food ran out. And illness came, in the crowded castle.

--

Meanwhile, outside, the King and his advisers were looking for a way to end the war, and bring peace to England.

In October 1266, a group of bishops and barons, meeting in the King's camp, at Kenilworth, drew up a peace settlement, which became known as the Dictum of Kenilworth. It said that the rebels could have their lands back, if they paid a fine, a large sum of money, depending on how deeply they had been involved in the rebellion. It was a way of forgiving, and of healing the wounds of the war.

{img:bishops-writing-peace-terms-tent-parchment-seals-autumn}
At first, the defenders of Kenilworth refused. But by December, they were starving, and many were ill. And at last, on the fourteenth of December, 1266, they agreed to surrender, on the terms of the Dictum, and were allowed to leave the castle freely, with their horses and their arms.

--

They walked out of the castle, along the causeway, across the water, weak, and thin, and tired, in the cold winter air. And the King's men went in.

The great siege was over. And with it, the Barons' War.

The King gave Kenilworth to his younger son, Edmund, the Earl of Lancaster. And from then on, for hundreds of years, it would belong to the great House of Lancaster.

--

Simon de Montfort lost his life, and his cause seemed to have failed. But his ideas did not die.

The young Prince Edward, who had defeated him at Evesham, became King Edward the First, in 1272. And he, too, called parliaments, with knights from the counties, and burgesses from the towns, as Simon had done. Over the centuries, Parliament grew stronger and stronger, until, in the end, it became the true ruler of England.

{img:parliament-old-hall-westminster-later-centuries-members-seated}
So, in a way, the quarrels, and the ideas, of the proud earl of Kenilworth, helped to shape the government of England, and of many other countries, all over the world, to this day.
