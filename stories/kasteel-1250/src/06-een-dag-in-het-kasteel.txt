Let us spend an ordinary day at Kenilworth, in the summer of 1250.

The day begins at dawn, or before. In the chapel, a bell rings. The castle wakes up, slowly, in the grey light, with the mist lying on the water of the mere, and the birds singing in the woods across the lake.

--

The servants are the first to get up. They have slept on straw mattresses, or on the floor, in the hall, or in the kitchen, or in the stables, or in little rooms in the towers. They roll up their bedding, and put it away, and wash their faces and hands in cold water, from a basin, or from the well.

The fires are lit, or brought back to life, from the embers, in the kitchen, and the hall, and the chambers. The bakers are already at work, in the bakehouse, kneading the dough, and lighting the ovens. The grooms are feeding and watering the horses. The cowherd is milking the cows.

The guards on the walls change watch. The porter opens the great gates, and lowers the drawbridge, if there is one, and the castle opens to the world, for another day.

--

In their chamber, the lord and lady wake, in their great curtained bed.

Their servants bring them warm water, to wash. They dress, with help: in linen undershirts, and long woollen tunics, and over them, surcoats, long sleeveless gowns, of fine wool, or silk, in bright colours, red, and blue, and green. The countess wears a veil, and a wimple, a linen cloth wrapped around her neck and chin, as married ladies do. The earl wears a belt, with a purse, and a knife.

{img:noble-lady-dressing-wimple-veil-maids-helping-chamber-morning}
Then they go to the chapel, to hear Mass, said by their chaplain, with their family, and the members of the household who are not working.

--

Breakfast, in 1250, is not a proper meal. Most people eat a little bread, and drink a little ale, or wine, when they get up, standing up, or as they work. Children, and the old, and the sick, and the workers who do heavy labour, are given something more.

Then the day's work begins.

The earl, when he is at home, meets with his steward, and his officers, and hears reports on his lands, and his money, and his household. He receives visitors, and messengers, and reads letters, or has them read to him, and dictates answers to his clerks. He hears the complaints of his tenants, and settles disputes. Or he goes out, riding, to inspect his lands, or to hunt.

--

The countess, too, is busy. She meets with the steward, and the wardrober, and checks the accounts, and orders what is needed: food, and wine, and cloth, and candles. She sees to the children, and their lessons. She receives visitors, and writes letters. She gives orders to her ladies, and her servants.

And in the quieter hours, she sits with her ladies, in her chamber, by the window, sewing, and embroidering, and talking, and listening to someone read aloud, from a book, a romance, or the life of a saint, in French.

{img:ladies-listening-reader-romance-book-chamber-afternoon-light}
Because the language of the castle, in 1250, is French. The lords and ladies of England speak French, as their ancestors did, who came from Normandy with William the Conqueror. The clerks write in Latin. And the servants, and the villagers, speak English. Many people in the castle speak two languages, or three.

--

At about ten or eleven o'clock in the morning, a horn is blown, or a bell is rung, and everyone goes to the great hall, for dinner, the main meal of the day.

We shall go to dinner, in a moment. It is a long, grand affair, which lasts for an hour or two.

After dinner, the afternoon. The work of the castle goes on. The lord and the young men may go hunting, or hawking, or practise with their weapons, in the courtyard. The ladies may walk in the garden, or ride, or play games, or sew. The children have their lessons, or play.

--

In the late afternoon, there is supper, a lighter meal, at about five o'clock, in the hall, or, for the lord and lady, in their chamber.

And then the evening. In summer, the evenings are long and light. People walk by the lake, and watch the sun go down over the water, and the swallows swooping over the mere. In winter, they gather around the fires, in the hall, and the chamber, and tell stories, and sing, and play games, like chess, and dice, and listen to music, played on the harp, or the fiddle, or the lute.

{img:evening-hall-fire-harp-player-storytelling-household-gathered}
And then, as darkness falls, the gates are closed, and the drawbridge raised, and the guards set for the night. The lights go out, one by one. The household lies down to sleep. And the castle is quiet, except for the guards walking on the walls, and the water lapping against the stones, and the owls calling in the woods.
