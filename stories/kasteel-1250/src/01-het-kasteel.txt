Tonight we are going nearly eight hundred years back, to a great castle of red stone in the middle of England, in the year 1250.

--

It stands on a low rise in the green Warwickshire countryside, surrounded by a wide lake that the builders made themselves, with tall towers and thick walls, and smoke rising from its kitchens. Inside live a lord and a lady, their children, their knights and their servants, more than a hundred people under one roof.

The castle is called Kenilworth. And tonight, we will spend a little time inside it, and see how its people lived.

If you like quiet journeys into the past, you are welcome to subscribe to the channel, so that the next one is easy to find.

--

Let us imagine England, in the middle of the thirteenth century.

It is a green and quiet land, much emptier than today. Perhaps four or five million people live in the whole of England, most of them in small villages, surrounded by great open fields, divided into long strips, and by meadows, and woods, and commons, where the animals graze. There are only a few towns, and they are small. London, the largest, has perhaps fifty thousand people.

--

The roads are muddy tracks, through the fields and the forests. People travel on foot, or on horseback, or in heavy wooden carts. A journey from one side of England to the other takes weeks.

In the middle of the country, in the county of Warwickshire, there is the great forest of Arden, with its oaks and its glades and its deer. And on the edge of the forest, beside two little streams, there is the castle of Kenilworth.

{img:forest-of-arden-oaks-glade-deer-morning-light}
In 1250, it is one of the largest and strongest castles in England. And it is also a home.

--

What is a castle?

A castle is a fortified home: the house of a great lord, built to be defended. It is a fortress, and a palace, and the centre of a great estate, all at once. From his castle, the lord rules his lands, and the villages around, and the people who live in them. He collects his rents, and holds his courts, and keeps his treasure, and his horses, and his armour.

{img:castle-great-hall-lord-holding-court-villagers-waiting}
The first castles in England were built by the Normans, after William the Conqueror invaded England, in 1066, nearly two hundred years before. At first, they were simple: a great mound of earth, called a motte, with a wooden tower on top, and a courtyard below it, surrounded by a ditch and a wooden fence.

--

But over the years, the castles were rebuilt in stone, larger and stronger, with high walls and towers, and great gatehouses, and deep moats.

By 1250, there are hundreds of castles in England, and in Wales, and in Scotland, and Ireland, and France. Some belong to the King. Some to the great barons, the earls, the most powerful lords of the land. Some to bishops. Some are small, just a tower and a wall. Some, like Kenilworth, are enormous.

And every castle is full of life. Because a castle is not just stone. It is people: the lord, and the lady, and their children, and their knights and squires, and the steward, and the clerks, and the cooks, and the bakers, and the brewers, and the grooms, and the huntsmen, and the falconers, and the washerwomen, and the carpenters, and the smiths, and the guards, and the priests, and the guests, and the dogs, and the horses.

--

In 1250, Kenilworth belongs to the King of England, Henry the Third. But he has given it to his younger sister, Eleanor, and her husband, one of the most famous men in England: Simon de Montfort, the Earl of Leicester.

Their story is one of the great stories of the Middle Ages. And their castle will see, in a few years, some of the most dramatic events in English history.

{img:castle-gate-morning-carts-arriving-guards-calm}
But tonight, in the year 1250, the castle is at peace. And we are going inside, to see how its people live, from the lord and lady at their high table, to the boy who turns the spit in the kitchen.
