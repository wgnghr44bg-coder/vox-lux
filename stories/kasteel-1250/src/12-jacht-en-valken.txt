The favourite pastime of the lords and ladies of 1250 is hunting.

Around Kenilworth, there is the great forest of Arden, and the lord's own hunting park, called the Chase, an area of woods and open land, surrounded by a fence or a bank, where deer are kept for hunting. Nobody else may hunt there, on pain of heavy fines.

--

A great hunt is a grand occasion. The lord, and his guests, and the young knights and squires, ride out from the castle in the early morning, with the huntsmen, and the dogs, and the horns.

The dogs are very important, and much loved. There are greyhounds, tall and fast, which chase the deer by sight. And scenting hounds, which follow the deer by smell, and bay loudly when they find the trail. And big, strong dogs, mastiffs, and alaunts, for hunting the wild boar. The dogs live in kennels, in the castle, looked after by the keepers of the hounds, and are fed on bread, and meat.

The huntsmen go ahead, in the early morning, to find where the deer are. Then the hounds are released, and follow the scent, and the riders follow the hounds, galloping through the woods, and across the open land, blowing their horns, with the hounds baying, in a great noise and excitement.

--

Hunting is not only a sport. It is also a way of getting meat, venison, for the castle table, which is the finest meat of all, reserved for the nobility. And it is training for war, teaching young men to ride hard, through rough country, and to be brave, and quick.

Ladies hunt too. They ride with the men, or watch from a stand, in a clearing, while the deer are driven past, and shoot at them with bows and arrows.

{img:noble-lady-on-horse-falcon-on-glove-meadow-river-morning}
But the favourite hunting of ladies is hawking: hunting with falcons and hawks.

--

Hawking is one of the great passions of the medieval nobility.

The hawks and falcons are caught wild, as young birds, or taken from their nests, and trained, with great patience, by the falconers, to hunt, and to come back to the falconer's glove. It takes months, and great skill.

{img:falconer-training-hawk-lure-mews-castle-quiet-afternoon}
The birds are kept in a special building, called the mews, and are treated like precious treasures. They wear little leather hoods over their heads, to keep them calm, and little bells on their legs, so that they can be found, and leather straps, called jesses, on their legs, which the falconer holds.

--

Later, a famous book of hunting will even give a list of which bird suits each rank of person. A king flies a gyrfalcon, the largest and most beautiful of the falcons, from the far north. An earl flies a peregrine falcon, the fastest bird in the world, which dives on its prey from high in the sky. A lady flies a merlin, a small, quick falcon. A yeoman flies a goshawk. A priest flies a sparrowhawk.

The lords and ladies carry their hawks on their gloved left hands, everywhere: when they ride, when they visit, even sometimes in church, or at dinner. A fine hawk is a sign of rank, and a great gift.

When they go hawking, they ride out into the open country, along the rivers, and the meadows, and the marshes, around the mere, where there are ducks, and herons, and partridges. When a bird is flushed, the falconer takes off the hawk's hood, and throws it into the air, and it flies up, and up, and dives on its prey. Then it is called back, with a lure, a bundle of feathers on a string, and given a reward of meat.

--

Simon de Montfort loves hunting, and hawking, and so do his sons. And Eleanor's accounts record the care of the hawks, and the dogs, and the horses, and the payments to the falconers, and the huntsmen.

There is also fishing, in the mere, and the ponds, with nets, and lines. The mere is full of fish, which are an important part of the castle's food, especially on fast days. The castle has its own fishermen, with little boats.

{img:small-boat-fishermen-nets-mere-castle-reflection-dawn}
In the evening, the hunters come home, tired, and muddy, and happy, with the deer slung across a horse, and the hounds trotting behind, and the horns blowing, to tell the castle that they are coming. And that night, in the hall, there is venison for dinner, and long stories of the day's hunt.
