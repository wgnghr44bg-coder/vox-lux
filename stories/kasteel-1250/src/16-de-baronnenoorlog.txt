The peace of Kenilworth in 1250 does not last.

In the years that follow, King Henry quarrels more and more with his barons. He spends too much money on foreign wars and on his friends from France. He asks for more and more taxes. He does not listen to advice. Many people feel that he is ruling badly. And slowly, the trouble grows.

--

In 1258, the barons meet at Oxford. They force the King to accept a set of new rules. From now on, the King must rule with the advice of a council. And a parliament, a great meeting of the kingdom, must come together three times a year.

More and more, the leader of these reformers is Simon de Montfort, the King's own brother-in-law.

Simon believes deeply that a king must obey the law, like everyone else. He swears an oath to keep the new rules, and he will not break it, whatever happens. He is a man of strong ideas, and very, very stubborn.

--

The King breaks his promises. The quarrel grows, year by year. In 1264, it turns into a war between the King and the barons who follow Simon.

In May 1264, near the town of Lewes, Simon's army defeats the King's army. The King and his son, Prince Edward, are captured. For more than a year, Simon rules England in the King's name.

{img:parliament-westminster-1265-knights-burgesses-gathered-hall}
In January 1265, Simon calls a parliament at Westminster. It becomes one of the most famous in English history. For the first time, he invites not only the great lords and bishops. He also invites two knights from every county, and two ordinary citizens from many of the towns. Many historians see this as the very beginning of the English Parliament as we know it.

--

All this time, Kenilworth is one of the great strongholds of the Montfort family. Simon's son, also called Simon, holds it for him.

But in the summer of 1265, Prince Edward escapes. He gathers an army and turns against Simon. And now everything that Simon has built hangs in the balance.

{img:summer-evening-castle-towers-mere-quiet-anxious-1265}
On the fourth of August 1265, near the town of Evesham, the two armies meet. A summer storm darkens the sky, with thunder in the middle of the day. We will not watch the battle. It is enough to know that Simon de Montfort lost his life there, together with his eldest son Henry, and many of his loyal knights.

--

Simon was about fifty-seven years old.

The ordinary people of England had loved him. They saw him as a man who stood up for the law against a bad king. After his death, many people believed he was a saint. They travelled to Evesham to pray where he fell.

Today, at Evesham, there is a simple stone in the ruins of the old abbey, where he was buried. And in the Houses of Parliament in London, there is a statue of him.

--

Eleanor was at Dover when the news reached her. Her grief was deep. She wore mourning clothes for the rest of her life.

Soon afterwards she left England for France. She went to live in a convent of nuns south of Paris, which Simon's family had founded. She lived there quietly for ten more years, and died there, at the age of about sixty.

{img:french-convent-garden-cloister-nun-widow-quiet-afternoon}
But at Kenilworth, the story was not over yet. Because the great red castle on its lake refused to give up.
