The peace of Kenilworth, in 1250, does not last.

In the years that follow, King Henry the Third quarrels more and more with his barons. He spends too much money, on foreign wars, and on his favourites, his relatives from France, and on his great plans. He asks for more and more taxes. He does not listen to the advice of his barons. And many people in England feel that he is ruling badly.

--

In 1258, the barons meet at Oxford, and force the King to agree to a set of reforms, called the Provisions of Oxford. The King must rule with the advice of a council of barons, and a parliament must meet three times a year, to discuss the affairs of the kingdom.

The leader of the reformers, more and more, is Simon de Montfort, the King's own brother-in-law.

Simon believes, deeply, that the King must rule according to the law, and with the consent of the great men of the kingdom, and that he must not do whatever he likes. He swears an oath to keep the Provisions, and he will not break it, whatever happens. He is a man of strong principles, and a terrible stubbornness.

--

The King breaks his promises, and the quarrel grows, year by year, until, in 1264, it becomes a civil war: the King and his supporters, against Simon and the barons who support reform. This is called the Second Barons' War.

In May 1264, at the town of Lewes, in Sussex, Simon's army defeats the King's army, and captures the King, and his son, Prince Edward. For more than a year, Simon de Montfort rules England, in the King's name.

{img:parliament-westminster-1265-knights-burgesses-gathered-hall}
And in January 1265, he calls a parliament, at Westminster, which is one of the most famous in English history. For the first time, he invites not only the great barons, and the bishops, but also two knights from every county, and two citizens, called burgesses, from many of the towns, to come and speak for their communities. Many historians see this as the beginning of the House of Commons, and of the English Parliament as we know it.

--

During this time, Eleanor is at Odiham, and then at Dover, and Kenilworth is one of the great strongholds of the Montfort family, held for them by their son, the younger Simon.

But in the summer of 1265, Prince Edward escapes from captivity, and gathers an army, and turns against Simon.

{img:summer-evening-castle-towers-mere-quiet-anxious-1265}
On the fourth of August, 1265, at Evesham, in Worcestershire, Prince Edward's army meets Simon's army, in a summer storm, with thunder, and darkness, in the middle of the day. We will not watch the battle. It is enough to know that Simon de Montfort, the Earl of Leicester, lost his life there, with his eldest son, Henry, and many of his loyal knights.

--

Simon was about fifty-seven years old.

The common people of England had loved him. They saw him as their champion, who had stood up for the law, and for the rights of the people, against a bad king. After his death, many people believed that he was a saint, and went on pilgrimage to the place where he fell, at Evesham, and told stories of miracles there.

Today, at Evesham, there is a simple memorial stone, in the ruins of the abbey, where his remains were buried. And in the Houses of Parliament, in London, there is a statue of him, among the great men who shaped the English Parliament.

--

Eleanor was at Dover when she heard the news. She was grief-stricken. She dressed in mourning for the rest of her life.

Soon afterward, she left England, and went to France, to a convent of Dominican nuns, at Montargis, south of Paris, which Simon's family had founded. And she lived there, quietly, for ten more years, and passed away there, in 1275, at the age of about sixty.

{img:french-convent-garden-cloister-nun-widow-quiet-afternoon}
But at Kenilworth, the story was not over yet. Because the castle, the great red fortress on its lake, refused to surrender.
