When the horn sounds for dinner, the household gathers in the great hall.

The trestle tables are set up, along the walls, long boards laid on wooden stands, and covered with white linen cloths. The benches are pulled up. At the end of the hall, on the dais, the high table is laid, with the finest cloth, and the best cups and dishes, and a great salt cellar, of silver, in the middle.

--

Everyone has a place, according to their rank.

At the high table sit the lord and lady, in the middle, and beside them, their most important guests: a bishop, perhaps, or an abbot, or another earl, or a knight of high rank, and their older children. Below the dais, at the long tables, sit the knights, and the officers of the household, and the clerks, and the squires, and then the lesser servants, further down the hall. Where you sit tells everyone how important you are.

Before the meal, servants come round with basins, and jugs of warm water, sometimes scented with herbs, and towels, and everyone washes their hands. This is important, because they will eat with their fingers.

--

Then the chaplain says grace, a prayer of blessing, in Latin. And the meal begins.

The servants come in procession, from the kitchen, carrying the dishes, held high, led by the steward, or the marshal of the hall, with his white rod of office. Sometimes, on feast days, the procession is announced by trumpets.

{img:procession-servants-carrying-dishes-steward-white-rod-hall}
At the high table, there are special servants. The carver, a young squire of good family, kneels before the lord, and carves the meat, with great skill, and courtesy, and serves it to the lord and lady. The cupbearer, another young man, serves the wine, kneeling. These are honourable jobs, and the sons of great families do them, as part of their training.

--

There are no plates, as we know them. Instead, each person has a trencher: a thick slice of stale bread, four days old, cut square, on which the food is put. The trencher soaks up the juices and the sauces. At the end of the meal, the trenchers, full of gravy, are not eaten by the diners, but are collected, and given to the poor, at the castle gate, or to the dogs.

People share. Two people eat from one dish, and drink from one cup. A young man is expected to cut the meat for the lady beside him, and offer her the best pieces.

Everyone has their own knife, which they carry on their belt, and use to cut their food, and spear it. There are spoons, for soups and sauces. But there are no forks. Forks will not be used in England for another three hundred years. People eat with their fingers, carefully, using only three fingers of the right hand, and wiping them on the tablecloth, or a napkin, between dishes.

--

What do they eat?

A great dinner has two or three courses, and each course has many dishes, all put on the table at once, so that people can choose what they like.

{img:roast-meats-pies-birds-dishes-high-table-feast-colour}
There is meat: beef, and mutton, and pork, and venison, from the forest, roasted, or boiled, or baked in pies. There are birds: chickens, and capons, and geese, and ducks, and pigeons, and partridges, and herons, and swans, and peacocks, on great feast days. There are stews, and pottages, thick soups of meat and vegetables and herbs. There are sauces, of herbs, and vinegar, and mustard, and spices.

--

The spices are very important. They come from far away, from India, and the Spice Islands, along the trade routes, through the Middle East, and Venice, and are very expensive: pepper, and ginger, and cinnamon, and cloves, and nutmeg, and saffron, which colours food a beautiful golden yellow. Great lords use them generously, to show how rich they are. Eleanor's accounts show her buying pounds of pepper, and ginger, and cinnamon, and saffron.

There is sugar too, which is very rare and costly, and is kept with the spices, and used like a spice, a little at a time. And almonds, which are ground and soaked to make almond milk, used in many dishes, especially on fast days.

And always, there is bread: fine white bread, called manchet, for the high table, and coarser brown bread, for the lower tables. And to drink, wine, from Gascony, in the south of France, for the lord and the knights, and ale, brewed in the castle, for everyone else.

--

On many days of the year, people are not allowed to eat meat. On Fridays, and often on Wednesdays and Saturdays, and all through Lent, the forty days before Easter, and on the eves of great feasts. These are fast days. On these days, they eat fish.

Fresh fish from the mere, and the castle's fish ponds: pike, and bream, and perch, and eels. And salted and dried fish, brought from the sea: herrings, by the thousand, and cod, and stockfish, dried so hard that it must be beaten with a hammer and soaked for days. On a fast day, the household of a great lady might eat thousands of herrings.

{img:fish-day-table-pike-eels-herrings-bread-lent}
At the end of the meal, there might be fruit, apples and pears, from the orchard, and cheese, and wafers, thin, crisp little cakes, made with honey, and spiced wine, called hippocras.

--

Then the tables are cleared, and the cloths removed, and grace is said, and everyone washes their hands again.

And the leftovers, all the food that was not eaten, and the trenchers, soaked in gravy, are gathered up, and given to the almoner, who takes them to the gate, where the poor of the village are waiting.
