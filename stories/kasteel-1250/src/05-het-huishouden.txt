A great lord and lady, in 1250, never live alone. They live surrounded by their household: a great company of people, perhaps a hundred, or a hundred and fifty, or more, who serve them, and travel with them, and live with them, and eat at their table.

We know a great deal about the household of Eleanor de Montfort, because one of her household accounts survives, from fifteen years later, in 1265. It is a long roll of parchment, kept by her clerks, day by day, recording everything that was bought, and eaten, and drunk, and spent, and who came to dinner, and how many horses were in the stables, each night.

--

The household is run by the steward, an important man, a knight, or a gentleman, who is in charge of everything: the servants, and the food, and the money, and the accounts, and the order of the household. He is like the manager of a great hotel, and a great business, at the same time.

Under him, there are other officers, each with his own department.

The marshal is in charge of the stables, and the horses, and the grooms, and the carts, and the journeys. The chamberlain looks after the lord's and lady's private rooms, and their clothes, and their beds, and their treasure. The wardrober looks after the wardrobe, the store of cloth, and clothes, and spices, and wax, and precious things.

--

The pantler is in charge of the bread, in the pantry. The butler, whose name comes from the bottles, is in charge of the wine and the ale, in the buttery. The cook, or several cooks, in charge of the kitchen. The baker, and the brewer, and the larderer, who looks after the meat, and the poulterer, who looks after the birds.

There are clerks, who write the letters, and keep the accounts, in Latin, on parchment. There is the almoner, who gives out food and money to the poor, at the castle gate, every day, in the lord's name. There is the chaplain, who says Mass, and his clerk.

{img:almoner-giving-bread-to-poor-castle-gate-morning}
And there are many lesser servants: grooms, and porters, and messengers, and carters, and huntsmen, and falconers, and washerwomen, and pages, and kitchen boys, and boys who turn the spits, and men who carry water, and wood, and clean the latrines.

--

Most of the servants in a great household, in 1250, are men and boys.

But a great lady has her own women: her ladies, who are well-born, and keep her company, and help her dress, and sew with her, and talk with her, and travel with her. And her maids, who do the work of her chamber. And the nurses, who look after the youngest children. And the laundresses, who wash the linen.

{img:ladies-in-waiting-sewing-embroidery-chamber-window-light}
Eleanor's household also includes knights, who serve Simon, and ride with him, and fight for him, and are part of his company. And squires, young men of good families, who are learning to be knights. And pages, boys of good families, sent to the castle to be brought up, and learn manners, and serve at table.

--

All the members of the household wear the lord's livery: clothes of the same colour, given to them by the lord, every year, at Christmas, or Whitsun, so that everyone can see whose household they belong to. The knights get fine cloth, and fur. The servants get plainer cloth.

They are given their food, every day, at the lord's table, and a place to sleep, and wages, small for the servants, larger for the officers. And they are given gifts, and favours, and protection.

In return, they owe the lord and lady their loyalty, and their service. A household is like a great family. Some servants stay with the same lord all their lives, and their children after them.

--

Eleanor's household account of 1265 tells us many little things.

It tells us that, on an ordinary day, the household might eat a whole ox, or several sheep, and many chickens, and hundreds of eggs, and thousands of herrings, in Lent. That it drank many gallons of wine, and hundreds of gallons of ale, every week. That it burned many pounds of wax candles, and tallow candles. That the countess bought cloth, and furs, and spices, and almonds, and figs, and raisins, and rice, and sugar.

{img:medieval-household-roll-parchment-latin-entries-close-calm}
It tells us that there were often forty or fifty horses in the stables, and sometimes many more, when guests came. That messengers were sent, all over England, with letters. That the countess gave alms to the poor, and money to friars, and paid for the shoeing of horses, and the mending of carts.

--

And it tells us, quietly, about the life of a great lady, day after day: who came to dinner, and how many people ate in the hall, and what they ate, and where the household travelled.

It is one of the most detailed records of daily life that survives from medieval England. And thanks to it, we can imagine, very clearly, the life of the castle of Kenilworth, and its great, busy, hungry household.
