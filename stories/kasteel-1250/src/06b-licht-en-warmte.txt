What is it like, to live in a castle, in the winter of 1250?

It is cold. Stone walls, four metres thick, stay cold all winter long. The wind blows through the cracks around the shutters, and under the doors, and down the chimneys. The floors are of stone, or of beaten earth, or of wooden boards, covered with rushes, or straw, or rush mats, which are changed now and then, and sprinkled with sweet-smelling herbs, like lavender, and meadowsweet, and mint.

--

The only warmth comes from the fires.

In the great hall, there is a great fire, which burns all day, in winter, and is banked down at night. In the best chambers, there are fireplaces, with chimneys, which are quite new in 1250, and a great improvement over an open hearth in the middle of the floor, because the smoke goes up the chimney, instead of filling the room.

{img:great-hall-central-hearth-smoke-rising-louvre-roof-winter}
The fires burn wood, great logs, from the forest. Huge quantities of firewood are needed, every winter, and the villagers must cart it to the castle, as part of their service to the lord. In some places, charcoal is burned, in braziers, iron baskets on legs, which can be carried from room to room.

--

To keep warm, people wear many layers of clothes: linen shirts, and woollen tunics, and surcoats, and cloaks, lined with fur. The lords and ladies wear rich furs: squirrel, and ermine, white with little black tails, and sable, and miniver, the grey and white fur of a Baltic squirrel. Eleanor's accounts show her buying great quantities of furs. Ordinary people wear sheepskin, and rabbit, and cat.

They hang tapestries, and painted cloths, on the walls, to keep out the draughts. And they sleep in great beds, with curtains all around, drawn tight at night, to keep in the warmth, under heavy blankets, and furs, and quilts, and often share their beds, with their family, or their servants, for warmth.

There are warming pans, too, filled with hot coals, to warm the beds, before people get in.

--

And then there is light.

In the winter, the days are short. The sun rises late, and sets early, at about four o'clock in the afternoon. And after dark, the castle must be lit.

{img:candle-maker-dipping-tallow-wax-candles-workshop}
The best light comes from candles of beeswax, which burn brightly, and cleanly, with a sweet smell. But beeswax is very expensive. It is used in the chapel, and in the chambers of the lord and lady. Eleanor's accounts record great quantities of wax, bought for candles, many pounds every week.

The rest of the castle uses tallow candles, made of animal fat, which are cheaper, but smoky, and smelly, and do not give as much light. And rushlights: the stems of rushes, peeled, and dipped in fat, which burn for a little while, with a small flame. And oil lamps, little bowls of oil, with a wick floating in them.

--

So, on a winter evening, in the castle, the great hall is lit by the fire, and by torches, and candles, on iron stands, and hanging from the roof, in great iron circles, called candelabra. The light flickers, and shadows move on the walls, and on the tapestries.

In the chamber, the lady and her women sit close to the fire, and to the candles, sewing, and talking, and listening to music. The darkness outside is deep, and complete, with no lights anywhere, except the stars, and the moon, and perhaps a few candles in the village, across the water.

{img:ladies-sewing-close-to-fire-candles-chamber-winter-night-warm}
And then, after a while, everyone goes to bed, early, to save the candles, and to keep warm. And they sleep a long time, in the winter, nine or ten hours, and wake before dawn, in the darkness, and wait for the light to come back.
