At the end of May 1520, the king and queen and the whole court gather at Dover. Dover is on the south coast of England. From there they will cross the sea to France.

Dover is a busy little port under great white cliffs. An old castle stands on the hill above. Here the sea between England and France is narrowest. It is only about thirty-three kilometres across. On a clear day you can see France from the cliffs, a thin grey line on the horizon.

--

The harbour is full of ships of every size. They must carry thousands of people across the sea. And horses and food and tents and furniture and tapestries. The king's own ship has flags and banners in green and white. Some people say its sails are made of gold cloth.

Then a surprise happens. Just before Henry leaves, a visitor arrives by sea. It is the young emperor Charles, the ruler of Spain and the Netherlands and Germany. He is Catherine's nephew. The two rulers meet at Dover. They ride together to Canterbury and spend a few days there. Catherine is overjoyed to meet her nephew for the first time. But the visit has a hidden reason. Charles is worried. He does not want England and France to become too friendly.

{img:ships-crossing-channel-calm-sea-summer-morning-sails-france-coast}
On the last day of May, the king's ships sail across the calm blue summer sea. They sail to Calais. Calais is the last English town in France. England has held it for nearly two hundred years. It is a busy walled town by the sea, with English soldiers and English traders.

--

From Calais the court rides in a long procession across the flat green land. They ride to the little town of Guînes, near the border. A golden palace and hundreds of tents are waiting. And on the seventh of June, the two kings will meet in the valley. The place will be remembered as the Field of the Cloth of Gold.

--

{img:calais-walled-town-harbour-english-flags-1520-merchants-quay}
Calais in 1520 is a little piece of England in France. Its people speak English. They pay taxes to the English king. All English wool sold to Europe must pass through it. England will lose it in the end, almost forty years later. The queen then will be Mary, the little girl who is four now. It is said she spoke of Calais with great sadness. When I die, she said, you will find Calais written on my heart.
