At the end of May 1520, the king, and the queen, and the whole court, gather at Dover, on the south coast of England, to cross the Channel to France.

Dover is a busy little port, under the great white chalk cliffs, with the old castle, on the hill above, guarding the narrowest crossing of the sea, between England and France: only about thirty-three kilometres. On a clear day, from the cliffs, you can see the coast of France, a thin grey line, on the horizon.

--

The harbour is full of ships: dozens of them, of every size, to carry the king, and the court, and the thousands of people, and the horses, and the baggage, and the food, and the tents, and the furniture, and the tapestries, and the cloth of gold, across the sea. The king's own ship is decorated with flags, and banners, and streamers, in green and white, the Tudor colours, and with sails of cloth of gold, some people say.

Before Henry leaves, the Emperor Charles the Fifth, Catherine's nephew, the young ruler of Spain, and the Netherlands, and Germany, comes, by sea, to visit him, unexpectedly, and they meet at Dover, and ride together to Canterbury, and spend a few days together, at the archbishop's palace, and Catherine is overjoyed to see her nephew, for the first time. Because the emperor, too, wants to be Henry's friend, and does not want England and France to become too friendly.

{img:ships-crossing-channel-calm-sea-summer-morning-sails-france-coast}
Then, on the thirty-first of May, the king's fleet sails, across the calm, blue summer sea, to Calais, the last English town in France, which England has held for nearly two hundred years: a busy, walled town, by the sea, with English soldiers, and English merchants, who trade in wool, and English churches, and English streets.

--

From Calais, the court rides, in a great procession, across the flat green countryside, to the little town of Guînes, near the border, where the golden palace, and the tents, are waiting. And on the seventh of June, the two kings meet, in the valley, between Guînes and Ardres, on the Field of the Cloth of Gold.

--

{img:calais-walled-town-harbour-english-flags-1520-merchants-quay}
Calais, in 1520, is a little piece of England in France. Its people speak English, and pay taxes to the English king, and send members to the English Parliament. The great wool merchants of England, the Staplers, have their headquarters there, and all English wool sold to Europe must pass through it. England will lose it, at last, in 1558, under Queen Mary, who said, it is told, that when she died, they would find the word Calais written on her heart.
