In Tudor England, every man must be an archer.

The law says that every Englishman, between the ages of seventeen and sixty, must own a longbow, and arrows, and practise archery, regularly, on Sundays, and holy days, after church, at the butts: earth mounds, with targets, set up on the village green, or in a field, near every village, and town. Fathers must buy bows for their sons, from the age of seven, and teach them to shoot.

--

The longbow is the great English weapon, which won the famous battles of the Hundred Years' War, a century before: Crécy, and Poitiers, and Agincourt. It is made of a single piece of yew wood, as tall as a man, or taller, and needs great strength to draw: the archers on the Mary Rose had bows which needed a pull of fifty or seventy kilograms, and their skeletons show that their left arms, and shoulders, were bigger, and stronger, than their right, from years of practice.

A good archer can shoot ten or twelve arrows a minute, and hit a target at two hundred metres.

{img:longbow-yew-arrows-goose-feathers-quiver-close-tudor-archer}
King Henry himself is a fine archer, and loves to shoot, with his guard, and his friends, and makes bets on the results. He encourages archery all over the country, and bans other games, like bowls, and tennis, and cards, and dice, for ordinary people, so that they will practise archery instead. But the people play them anyway.

--

On Sunday afternoons, in summer, after church, the men and boys of the village gather at the butts, and shoot, in turns, and the women, and children, watch, and cheer, and bring food, and ale. There are competitions, and prizes: a silver arrow, or a new bow, or a pig.

But by 1520, guns are beginning to replace bows, in war, and the young men are less interested in archery than their grandfathers were, which worries the king, and his council. In a few decades, the longbow will disappear from war. But archery will remain a sport, and a pastime, in England, until today.
