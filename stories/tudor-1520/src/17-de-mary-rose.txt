In 1520, one of the finest ships in Henry's navy is the Mary Rose.

She was built at Portsmouth, in 1510 and 1511, one of the first ships built for the king's new navy. She is a great carrack, a large warship, about forty metres long, with four masts, and high castles, at the front, and the back, and rows of gun ports, along her sides, with heavy guns, of bronze, and iron, a new idea, which made her one of the most powerful warships in the world.

--

She was probably named after the king's favourite sister, Mary, and the Tudor rose, the symbol of the family. Henry loved her. An admiral wrote to him that she was the noblest ship of sail, of any great ship, at this hour, that I trow be in Christendom.

She served for thirty-four years, in Henry's wars, against France, and Scotland. And in the summer of 1520, she was one of the ships that guarded the Channel, while the king crossed to France, for the Field of the Cloth of Gold.

Life on board the Mary Rose, for her crew of about four hundred, sailors, and gunners, and soldiers, and archers, was crowded, and hard, and cramped, below decks, with the guns, and the stores, and the barrels of food, and beer, and the animals.

--

In July 1545, twenty-five years after our story, during a battle with the French fleet, in the Solent, near Portsmouth, the Mary Rose sank, in front of the king, who was watching from the shore, at Southsea Castle. We will not describe it. Few of her crew survived.

She lay at the bottom of the Solent, in the mud, for four hundred and thirty-seven years. The mud preserved half of her hull, and thousands of things inside her: the belongings of the crew, and the ship's equipment, and stores.

{img:mary-rose-museum-preserved-hull-galleries-soft-light-visitors}
In 1982, after years of work by divers, and archaeologists, she was lifted out of the sea, in a great cradle, watched, on television, by millions of people, all over the world. And today, she is in a museum, in Portsmouth, in a special building, where you can see the great wooden hull, and the guns, and more than nineteen thousand objects found inside her.

--

And those objects tell us, wonderfully, about the everyday lives of ordinary Tudor people: the sailors, and the archers, and the gunners, and the carpenter, and the cook, and the barber-surgeon.

There are their wooden bowls, and plates, and spoons, with their initials carved on them. Their combs, for removing lice. Their shoes, and their clothes, and their leather jerkins. Their dice, and their backgammon boards, and their little pocket sundials, and their rosaries, and their whistles, and their musical instruments: a fiddle, and pipes, and a tabor drum. The cook's great cauldrons, and the bones of the beef, and the pork, and the fish, they ate. The barber-surgeon's chest, with his medicines, and his instruments, and his razors. The carpenter's tools. The archers' longbows, and thousands of arrows.

{img:mary-rose-objects-wooden-bowls-combs-shoes-whistle-dice-museum}
And the bones of the ship's dog, a little terrier, which lived on board, and caught rats, and which the crew called, the archaeologists believe, Hatch, because he was found near a hatch. His skeleton is in the museum too.
