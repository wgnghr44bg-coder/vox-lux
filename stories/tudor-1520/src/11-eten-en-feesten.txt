What do the English eat in 1520?

It depends a lot on who you are. The rich eat huge amounts of meat. The poor eat mostly bread and thick soup, with some cheese and a little bacon.

--

At the king's court there are two big meals a day. Dinner is at about ten or eleven in the morning. Supper is at about four or five in the afternoon. Hundreds of people eat at court every day. And the king pays for all of it.

A great feast has two or three courses. Each course has many dishes, all put on the table at once. People choose what they like. There is beef and lamb and pork. There is deer from the king's parks. There are birds of every kind. Chickens and geese and ducks, pheasants and quails. There are even swans and peacocks. A roast peacock is sometimes served with its feathers put back on, just for show. And there are pies with golden decorated crusts.

{img:tudor-kitchen-huge-fireplace-spits-cooks-boys-hampton-court}
On Fridays there is no meat, only fish. The same goes for the weeks before Easter, and many other fast days. There is salmon and trout and carp from the ponds. There are eels and herrings, oysters and crabs. The Church even counts seals and porpoises as fish.

--

The rich love sugar and spices. Pepper, ginger, cinnamon and cloves come from far away in Asia. They are very costly. The rich put sugar in almost everything, even in meat and fish.

At the end of a great feast comes a course of sweet things. It is eaten in a separate room, or in a little summer house in the garden. There is marzipan and gingerbread. There are sugared fruits and jellies and spiced wine. And there are sugar sculptures. They are made of sugar and marzipan, in the shape of castles and ships and animals. They are painted and covered in gold, to amaze the guests.

{img:marzipan-sugar-subtlety-castle-ship-banquet-table-candles-gold}
The rich drink wine from France and Spain. Everyone else drinks ale, a thick sweet beer made without hops. It does not keep long. Beer made with hops is just starting to come over from Flanders. Old-fashioned English people do not like it at all.

--

The poor eat dark bread made of rye or barley. They eat thick stew of peas or beans or oats. Onions and cabbage and herbs from the garden go in the pot. It cooks over the fire all day. There is cheese and butter and eggs, and a little bacon from the family pig. Fresh meat is a rare treat, for festivals.

Potatoes and tomatoes and tea and coffee and chocolate are not yet known in England. They will come in the years ahead, from the New World and from the East. And they will change the way everyone eats.
