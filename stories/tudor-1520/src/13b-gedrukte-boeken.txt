In 1520, books are changing the world.

About seventy years ago, a German goldsmith called Gutenberg found a new way to make books. He made little metal letters that could be moved around. You put them together into words and lines and pages. You put ink on them. Then you press them onto paper in a big wooden press. Now you can make hundreds of copies of a book, fast and cheap. Before, every book had to be copied by hand, one by one.

--

The first printer in England was William Caxton. He was a trader who learned printing in Flanders. He set up his press in London about forty years ago. He printed about a hundred books, many of them in English. He printed The Canterbury Tales, a famous book of stories in English verse. And he printed the stories of King Arthur.

After him, other printers came. Caxton's helper moved the press to Fleet Street. For five hundred years after that, Fleet Street was the street of printers and newspapers.

{img:st-pauls-churchyard-bookstalls-books-readers-browsing-1520}
By 1520, there are bookshops all around Saint Paul's churchyard. They sell prayer books and sermons and Latin grammar for schoolboys. They sell little yearly books with the dates of festivals and the moon, and advice about farming and health. And they sell stories and songs and jokes.

--

More and more people can read. Perhaps one man in ten in the country, and many more in London. Some women can read too. New schools are being started in the towns. One famous school in London was started by a friend of Erasmus and More.

A few years from now, a young scholar called William Tyndale will print the New Testament in English. He wants a boy who drives the plough to be able to read the Bible. But his book will be forbidden. It will be smuggled into England hidden in bales of cloth. People will read it in secret. If they are caught, they will be in great trouble. And yet his words will live on in the English language for five hundred years. Let there be light. The salt of the earth. Fight the good fight.

--

{img:candlelit-room-family-reading-printed-book-aloud-tudor-evening}
In the evenings, in houses all over England, families sit by the fire. Someone reads aloud from a printed book. It might be a prayer book, or a story, or a song. The others listen while they sew and mend. And the children slowly fall asleep.
