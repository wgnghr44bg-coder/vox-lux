Life in Tudor England is often short. Illness is never far away.

There are no modern medicines and no vaccines. Doctors know very little about why people get sick. They believe what the old Greeks believed. The body has four fluids, and illness comes when they are out of balance. So doctors let out some blood. Or they give medicines made of herbs and minerals and strange things.

--

The most feared illness of Henry's England is the sweating sickness. It is a strange disease. It first came in 1485 and returned several times. Then it vanished and was never seen again. Nobody knows what it really was.

It struck suddenly, often in summer. And it was very quick. People said you could be merry at dinner and gone by supper. It seemed to strike the rich and the young and the strong more than the poor. Henry was terrified of it. Whenever it came, he fled from London. He moved from house to house in the country, with only a few servants. He took his own medicines and prayed. And every summer, the court watches and waits, and hopes it will not come back.

{img:herb-garden-tudor-apothecary-gathering-lavender-sage-rosemary}
Most ordinary people never see a doctor. Doctors cost too much. Instead they use remedies from the herb garden. The women of the family know a lot about plants. Sage for a sore throat. Lavender for a headache. Rosemary for memory. Honey for wounds.

--

Henry himself is very interested in medicine. He writes his own recipes for creams and medicines. He gives them to his friends and servants. He makes some for his own sore leg. A few of his recipes still survive in an old book in London.

And two years before our story, Henry set up a college for doctors in London. It makes sure doctors are properly trained. It still exists today.

--

{img:barber-surgeon-shop-striped-pole-basin-tools-london-street}
In the towns there are also barber surgeons. They cut hair and shave beards. They also pull teeth and set broken bones and treat wounds. Outside their shops hangs a pole with red and white stripes. The red is for blood and the white is for bandages. You can still see such poles outside barber shops today.
