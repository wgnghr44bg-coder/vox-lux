Let us walk through London, in the spring of 1520.

The city lies along the north bank of the Thames, inside its old walls, about two kilometres long, and one kilometre deep, with seven gates: Ludgate, and Newgate, and Aldersgate, and Cripplegate, and Moorgate, and Bishopsgate, and Aldgate. Outside the walls, the suburbs are spreading, along the roads, and along the river, to the west, toward Westminster.

--

The only bridge across the Thames is London Bridge: one of the wonders of Europe. It is built of stone, with nineteen arches, and on top of it, there are houses, and shops, three, four, five storeys high, on both sides of a narrow street, so that you could walk across the bridge without seeing the river at all. In the middle, there is a chapel, of Saint Thomas Becket. And at the southern end, there is a great gatehouse, with a drawbridge.

The water rushes so fast between the narrow arches of the bridge, at high and low tide, that it is dangerous to row through. People say that London Bridge was made for wise men to go over, and fools to go under. So many people get out of their boats, before the bridge, and walk around, and get back in, on the other side.

{img:watermen-boats-thames-passengers-crying-oars-westward-ho}
The river is the main road of London. There are thousands of boats, and the watermen, who row passengers up and down, and across, the river, calling out, eastward ho, and westward ho, to find customers. There are ships, from all over Europe, at the wharves, below the bridge, unloading wine from France, and spices from Venice, and cloth from Flanders, and timber, and furs, from the Baltic, and loading English wool, and cloth.

--

The streets of London are narrow, and crowded, and noisy, and dirty. The houses are of timber, and plaster, with overhanging upper storeys, which jut out over the street, so that the houses on either side almost touch, at the top, and the street below is dark, even at midday.

In the middle of the streets, there is a channel, the kennel, where the rain water, and the waste, flows away, toward the river. People throw their rubbish out of the windows. Pigs, and dogs, and chickens, wander in the streets. And the smell, in summer, is terrible.

{img:narrow-london-street-overhanging-timber-houses-signs-crowds}
But London is also a rich, and beautiful, and exciting city. There are more than a hundred parish churches, with their towers, and spires, and bells, ringing all day. There are the great halls of the guilds, the companies of the merchants, and craftsmen: the goldsmiths, and the mercers, and the grocers, and the drapers, and the fishmongers. There are fine houses of rich merchants, with gardens. There are the great monasteries, and friaries, with their churches, and their cloisters, and their gardens.

--

And above everything, on the hill, at the western end of the city, there is Saint Paul's Cathedral, the old Gothic cathedral, enormous, longer than any cathedral in England, with its great spire, the tallest in England, about a hundred and fifty metres high, which can be seen for many kilometres around.

The middle of Saint Paul's, the long nave, is used as a meeting place, and a market, and a short cut, called Paul's Walk, where people come to walk up and down, and gossip, and do business, and hire servants, and lawyers, and hear the news, all day long. And in the churchyard, outside, there are the booksellers, with their shops, selling the new printed books.
