The greatest sport of the Tudor court is the joust.

Two knights, in full armour, on great horses, covered with rich cloths, in their colours, charge at each other, along a wooden barrier, called the tilt, which keeps the horses apart, and try to strike each other with long wooden lances, on the helmet, or the shield, or the breastplate, and break the lance, or knock the other knight out of the saddle.

--

The lances are made of light wood, and designed to break easily, in a shower of splinters, which makes a great noise, and a great show, and the crowd cheers. Points are given for breaking a lance, and for striking the helmet, and for unhorsing an opponent.

The armour of the jousters is made especially for jousting: very heavy, and thick, on the left side, which faces the opponent, with a great helmet, with only a narrow slit to see through. Henry's own armour, made by the best armourers, from Germany, and Italy, and in his own workshops, at Greenwich, can still be seen today, in the Tower of London, and it shows how tall, and strong, and slim, he was, as a young man.

{img:henry-viii-armour-display-tower-of-london-tall-polished-later}
Henry loves jousting more than anything. He jousts at every great feast, and celebration, and wins, often, because he is strong, and skilled, and brave, and because, perhaps, some of his opponents are careful not to beat the king. At the Field of the Cloth of Gold, the two kings, Henry and Francis, jousted every day, and both did very well, although they did not joust against each other.

--

A tournament is a great spectacle. There are galleries, for the king, and the queen, and the ladies, and the courtiers, hung with tapestries, and cloth of gold. There are heralds, in their coats of arms, and trumpeters. There are pavilions, for the knights, to arm themselves. And there are crowds of ordinary people, around the lists, watching.

The knights come in, in procession, with their squires, and pages, and servants, all dressed in their colours, sometimes in costumes, as characters from the old stories of chivalry: knights of King Arthur, or wild men, or pilgrims. Each knight carries a token, a ribbon, or a sleeve, from a lady, tied to his helmet, or his lance.

{img:ladies-gallery-tournament-ribbons-tokens-knights-saluting}
And at the end of the day, the prizes are given out, by the queen, or the ladies, and there is a great feast, and dancing, late into the night.

--

Jousting is dangerous. Knights are often hurt. In 1524, Henry himself forgot to close the visor of his helmet, and was struck on the head by a lance, and was lucky not to be badly hurt. And in 1536, sixteen years after our story, he will have a terrible fall, in a joust, at Greenwich, and be unconscious for two hours, and his leg will be badly injured, and he will never joust again. Some historians think that this fall changed him, and made him the angry, suspicious, unpredictable king of his later years.

But in 1520, he is young, and strong, and jousts in the summer sunshine, and wins, and the crowds cheer, and the queen smiles.
