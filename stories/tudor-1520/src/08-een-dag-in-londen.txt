Let us spend an ordinary day in London with an apprentice. He is a boy of sixteen, learning to be a goldsmith. He works in Cheapside, the great market street of London.

He lives in his master's house, above the shop. He sleeps in the attic with the other boys. He will serve his master for seven years. He gets no pay, only food and clothes and a bed. In return, his master teaches him the craft.

--

He gets up at dawn, at about five, when the church bells ring. He takes down the wooden shutters at the front of the shop. The lower shutter folds down to make a counter. On it he lays out silver cups and spoons and rings and chains.

Breakfast is bread and weak beer. Then the work begins. The master and his workers sit at their benches by the window, for the light. They hammer and engrave and polish silver and gold. The boy helps and learns. He sweeps the floor and runs errands. And he stands at the front calling out to people passing by. What do you lack? What do you lack?

{img:goldsmith-workshop-bench-silver-cup-engraving-window-light}
Cheapside is the widest and grandest street in London. It is lined with goldsmiths and silk sellers and spice sellers. In the middle of the street there is a tall stone cross. There is also a big fountain where water carriers fill their tall wooden tubs.

--

At about eleven, the household eats dinner, the main meal of the day. They all sit at the master's table. The master and his wife, their children, the workers, the apprentices and the servants. There is thick soup of vegetables and grain. There is bread, and meat, or fish on Fridays. And there is beer.

In the afternoon the work goes on until evening. At about seven or eight, the shop is closed and the shutters go up.

{img:apprentices-playing-football-fields-outside-city-walls-evening}
After supper, the apprentices are free for a little while. They go out to the fields outside the city walls. They play football, a wild rough game with a pig's bladder for a ball. Or they practise archery, as every Englishman must by law. Or they wrestle. Or they go to the inns, although they are not supposed to.

--

The London apprentices are famous for being rowdy. When there is trouble, they gather in hundreds and fight. Three years ago, on the first of May, there was a big riot. The apprentices attacked the houses of foreign traders. Afterwards many of them were in great danger. They could have been hanged. Then Queen Catherine herself knelt before the king. She begged him to show mercy. And many of the boys were set free.

At nine o'clock, the curfew bell rings from a church in Cheapside. The apprentices must be home. The city gates are shut. And the night watchmen walk the dark streets with lanterns and bells, calling out the hours.
