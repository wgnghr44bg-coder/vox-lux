Let us spend an ordinary day in London, in 1520, with an apprentice, a boy of sixteen, learning to be a goldsmith, in Cheapside, the great market street of London, where the goldsmiths have their shops.

He lives in his master's house, as all apprentices do, above the shop, and sleeps in the attic, or under the counter, in the shop, with the other apprentices. He will serve his master for seven years, without pay, only food, and clothes, and a bed, and in return, his master will teach him the craft.

--

He gets up at dawn, at about five o'clock in summer, when the church bells ring. He takes down the wooden shutters, from the front of the shop, and the lower shutter becomes a counter, on which the goods are displayed: silver cups, and spoons, and rings, and chains, and brooches.

Breakfast is bread, and ale, or a little cheese. And then the work begins. The master, and the journeymen, work at the back of the shop, at their benches, by the window, for the light, hammering, and engraving, and polishing silver, and gold. The apprentice helps, and learns, and sweeps the floor, and runs errands, and stands at the front of the shop, calling out to the people passing: what do you lack? What do you lack?

{img:goldsmith-workshop-bench-silver-cup-engraving-window-light}
Cheapside is the widest and grandest street in London, lined with the shops of the goldsmiths, and the mercers, who sell silk, and velvet, and the haberdashers, and the grocers, who sell spices, and sugar, and dried fruit. In the middle of the street, there is the Eleanor Cross, a tall stone monument, and the Great Conduit, a fountain, where people fetch water, in great wooden tankards, carried by water carriers.

--

At about eleven o'clock, the household eats dinner, the main meal of the day, at the master's table: the master, and his wife, and their children, and the journeymen, and the apprentices, and the servants, all together. Pottage, a thick soup, of vegetables, and grain, and bread, and meat, beef, or mutton, or on Fridays, and in Lent, fish. And ale.

In the afternoon, the work goes on, until the evening. Then the shop is closed, at about seven, or eight, and the shutters put up.

{img:apprentices-playing-football-fields-outside-city-walls-evening}
In the evening, after supper, the apprentices are free, for a little while. They go out, into the streets, and the fields, outside the city walls, at Moorfields, and Finsbury, and play football, a wild, rough game, with a pig's bladder, or practise archery, at the butts, as every Englishman must, by law. Or wrestle. Or watch the bear baiting, across the river, in Southwark. Or go to the taverns, although they are not supposed to.

--

The apprentices of London are famous for being rowdy. When there is trouble in the city, they gather, in hundreds, shouting clubs, clubs, and fight. Three years before, in 1517, on the first of May, there was a great riot, called Evil May Day, when the apprentices attacked the houses of foreign merchants. And afterwards, the queen, Catherine, herself, knelt before the king, and begged for mercy for them, and many were pardoned.

And at nine o'clock, the curfew bell rings, from the church of Saint Mary-le-Bow, in Cheapside, and the apprentices must be home, and the city gates are shut, and the watchmen walk the dark streets, with their lanterns, and their bells, calling out the hours.
