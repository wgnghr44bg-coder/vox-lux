The greatest feast of the Tudor year is Christmas: not one day, but twelve, from Christmas Day, to Twelfth Night, on the sixth of January.

At court, Christmas is a time of feasting, and music, and masques, and plays, and dancing, and games, every day, and every night. The king, and the queen, and the whole court, spend it at Greenwich, or Richmond, or Eltham, and the halls are decorated with holly, and ivy, and bay, and rosemary, and a great Yule log burns in the hearth.

--

A Lord of Misrule is chosen, a courtier, or a servant, who is in charge of the entertainments, and the games, and the jokes, for the twelve days, and everyone must obey his silly commands, even the king. There are plays, and pageants, and disguisings, and mummers. On Twelfth Night, there is a great cake, with a bean inside, and whoever finds the bean in his slice becomes the King of the Bean, for the night.

The food is splendid: roast beef, and venison, and boar's head, carried in on a great dish, with an apple in its mouth, while everyone sings the Boar's Head Carol. And roast goose, and swan, and peacock. And great Christmas pies, filled with every kind of meat, and dried fruits, and spices, the ancestors of the mince pies that the English still eat at Christmas. And plum pottage, a thick, spicy porridge, of meat, and dried fruit, which, over the centuries, will turn into the Christmas pudding.

{img:boars-head-carried-feast-trumpets-singers-tudor-hall-christmas}
On New Year's Day, the king, and the courtiers, give each other gifts. The king gives gold, and silver cups, and plates, to his courtiers, and they give him gifts, of jewels, and books, and clothes, and horses, and hawks, and sweets. Every gift is written down, on a great roll of parchment, the New Year's gift roll, some of which still survive.

--

In the villages, too, Christmas is the great feast of the year. The farm work stops, for twelve days. The lord of the manor often invites his tenants, and the villagers, to a great Christmas dinner, in his hall. The young people go from house to house, singing carols, and wassailing: carrying a great bowl of hot, spiced ale, with roasted apples, floating in it, and wishing everyone good health, and good luck for the new year, and being given food, and drink, and coins, in return.

{img:wassailers-singing-snowy-village-door-bowl-lantern-night}
And on Plough Monday, after Twelfth Night, the ploughmen drag a plough through the village, decorated with ribbons, and ask for money, and then, the next day, the work of the farming year begins again.
