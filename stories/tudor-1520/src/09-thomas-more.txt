One of the most remarkable men in England, in 1520, is Thomas More.

He is forty-two years old: a lawyer, and a judge, and a scholar, and a writer, and a member of the king's council. He is a friend of the king, who likes to visit him, at his house, and walk with him, in his garden, with his arm around his neck, and talk about astronomy, and theology, and poetry.

--

More is famous all over Europe for a little book, which he wrote, in Latin, four years before, in 1516, called Utopia. It describes an imaginary island, in the New World, called Utopia, which means no place, where everyone lives in peace, and equality, and nobody owns anything privately, and everyone works six hours a day, and spends the rest of the time learning, and the people choose their own rulers, and practise many different religions, and tolerate each other, and gold is so little valued that they use it to make chamber pots, and chains for prisoners.

It is a clever, funny, serious book, which makes people think about the problems of their own country: the poverty, and the injustice, and the greed of the rich, and the enclosure of the common land, for sheep. In Utopia, More writes, sheep, which used to be so meek, and tame, have become so greedy that they eat up men.

{img:scholar-writing-latin-book-study-books-candle-window-tudor}
The word utopia has been used ever since, for a perfect imaginary society.

--

More is a warm, witty, kind, and very religious man. He is a close friend of the great Dutch scholar, Erasmus of Rotterdam, the most famous writer in Europe, who stays with him, in London, and describes him in a letter: his complexion is fair, his hair is dark brown, his eyes greyish blue, his expression cheerful, and friendly. He is always smiling, and joking.

More loves his family, and his home. A few years later, he will build a new house, at Chelsea, a village by the Thames, west of London, with a large garden, running down to the river, with an orchard, and a gallery, and a chapel, and a library. He will live there with his second wife, Alice, and his children, and his son-in-law, and his grandchildren, and his servants, and his pets: a monkey, and dogs, and rabbits, and birds, and a fox, and a weasel.

{img:holbein-style-family-portrait-room-tudor-household-reading-calm}
He believes that girls should be as well educated as boys, which is unusual in his time, and he teaches his daughters Latin, and Greek, and philosophy, and astronomy. His eldest daughter, Margaret, becomes one of the most learned women in England, and corresponds with Erasmus, in Latin.

--

A few years later, the German painter Hans Holbein will come to England, and stay with More, and paint his portrait, and a great picture of the whole More family, together, at Chelsea: the first large family group portrait in English art. The painting is lost, but Holbein's drawing for it survives, and shows them all, reading, and talking, and the little dog, and the monkey, climbing up the dress of Lady Alice.

More's story, later, will be a sad one, and we will come back to it. But in 1520, he is happy, and respected, and the king's friend.
