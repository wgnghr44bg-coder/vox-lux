Tonight we go about five hundred years back. We go to England, in the spring and summer of the year 1520.

[long-pause]

A young king sits on the throne. He is tall and golden-haired. He is strong and clever and full of music and laughter. His court shines with silk and gold. His palaces stand along the river Thames. His chief minister is building the finest house in England. And this summer, the king will sail to France. There he will meet the king of France, in the grandest meeting of kings Europe has ever seen.

[pause]

The king is Henry the Eighth. He is twenty-eight years old. Tonight we will spend a little time in his England. It is long before the troubles that come later. These are the years when everything still seems golden. But even now, there is one worry in the palace. The king has no son.

[pause]

If you like these slow journeys into the past, you are welcome to subscribe to the channel, so that the next one is easy to find.

[long-pause]

England in 1520 is a small country. Perhaps two and a half million people live there. That is fewer than in one big city today.

[pause]

Most of them live in the countryside. They live in small villages of wooden houses with straw roofs. Each village has a church, a green and a manor house. Around it lie fields and meadows and woods. The people are farmers and shepherds and craftsmen. They grow wheat and barley, oats and peas and beans. And above all, they keep sheep.

[long-pause]

England is the land of sheep. English wool is the finest in Europe. It is sold all over the continent, as wool and as cloth. It makes England rich. Rich wool traders build big churches in their towns, as thanks to God. In Parliament, the most important judge sits on a big cushion stuffed with wool. It is called the Woolsack. It reminds everyone where England's money comes from.

[pause]

{img:sheep-grazing-cotswold-hills-wool-church-tower-golden-stone}
There are a few towns, mostly small. Each has a weekly market and a few streets of shops and inns. There are a few cities, like Norwich and Bristol and York. Each has maybe ten thousand people. And there is London.

[long-pause]

London has perhaps fifty or sixty thousand people, and it is growing fast. It is busy and noisy and dirty and exciting. It lies along the north bank of the Thames, inside old Roman walls. It has a hundred churches. It has the great church of Saint Paul's, with the tallest spire in England. It has the Tower of London, the old royal castle. And it has London Bridge, covered with houses and shops.

[pause]

{img:old-st-pauls-cathedral-tall-spire-london-roofs-thames-1520}
West of the city, along the river, stand the palaces. They belong to the king and the bishops and the great lords. Their gardens run down to the water. The king and his court travel up and down the Thames in big painted boats. The rowers wear the king's colours, green and white.

[long-pause]

{img:young-henry-viii-1520-tall-athletic-red-gold-hair-beard-doublet}
In 1520, King Henry the Eighth is twenty-eight years old. He has been king for eleven years.

[pause]

He is tall, about one metre eighty-eight. That is very tall for his time. He is strong and broad and handsome. He has red-gold hair, a short beard and small bright eyes. A visitor from Venice wrote that he was the most handsome prince he had ever seen.

[long-pause]

He was never meant to be king. He was the second son of King Henry the Seventh. His father won the crown in a battle in 1485, at the end of a long civil war. Henry's older brother Arthur was supposed to be king. But Arthur passed away at fifteen. So Henry, at eleven, was suddenly next in line. When his father passed away, Henry became king at seventeen. The whole country was happy.

[pause]

The young king was everything his father was not. His father had been gloomy and careful. Henry was cheerful and generous and full of energy.

[pause]

{img:young-king-hunting-horseback-hounds-forest-greenwich-park}
He loves sport. He can hunt all day and wear out eight or ten horses. He loves jousting, where two riders charge at each other with long poles. He plays tennis and wrestles and dances. He is one of the best archers in England.

[long-pause]

But he is also clever and well taught. He speaks French and Latin, and a bit of Spanish and Italian. He reads books about God and even writes them. The year after our story, he will write a book to defend the Church. The Pope will thank him with a title. Defender of the Faith. British coins still carry the letters F D for it today.

[pause]

Most of all, he loves music. He plays the lute, which is like a round guitar. He plays the organ and the flute, and he sings well. He even writes his own songs. The best known is called Pastime with Good Company. It is about the joys of being young, with friends and hunting and dancing.

[pause]

{img:king-playing-lute-chamber-musicians-courtiers-listening-evening}
He owns more than seventy flutes and dozens of lutes. And he hires the best musicians in Europe to play at his court.

[long-pause]

In 1520 Henry is still young and golden and loved. He has been married for eleven years to Catherine of Aragon. He has a daughter, Mary, who is four. But he does not yet have a son. A king without a son worries about the future. Who will rule after him? Will the old wars come back? That worry is small for now. In the years to come, it will grow and grow. And it will change everything.

[pause]

But not yet.

[long-pause]

{img:greenwich-palace-placentia-thames-riverfront-towers-1520-barges}
Of all his palaces, King Henry likes Greenwich best.

[pause]

It stands on the south bank of the Thames, a little way down the river from London. It has a long front of red brick and stone, facing the water. There are towers and big windows, and a landing place for the royal boats. Behind it lies a large park that climbs up a hill. There are deer and woods. And on top of the hill stands a small tower with a wonderful view of the river and of London far away.

[long-pause]

Henry was born at Greenwich. He married Catherine there. His daughter Mary was born there too. He loves it. It is close to London, but it feels like the country. There is the river, the park, the hunting and the fresh air.

[pause]

At Greenwich, Henry has built a long field for jousting, with towers for people to watch from. He has his own armour workshop, with armour makers brought from Germany. There are tennis courts and bowling lanes. There are stables, and dogs for hunting, and a house for the hawks.

[pause]

{img:greenwich-tiltyard-towers-spectators-jousting-sunny-afternoon}
From the palace windows, the king can watch the ships go up and down the Thames. Trading ships come from all over Europe. And there are ships of his own navy. He loves his ships. He often visits the new shipyards nearby, to see them being built.

[long-pause]

The palace is gone now. It was pulled down about a hundred and fifty years later. In its place stand big white buildings with domes, by the river.

[pause]

But the park is still there, with its old trees and its deer and its hill. There is an old dead oak tree, held up by props. People call it Queen Elizabeth's Oak. The story says that Henry once danced under it. And that his daughter Elizabeth played there as a child.

[long-pause]

{img:greenwich-park-hill-view-river-thames-deer-old-trees-evening}
On summer evenings, the king and queen sometimes walk up the hill with their friends and the little princess. They sit under the great trees. Below them lie the palace and the shining river. Boats are going home to London. Far away, the spires of the city rise out of the haze. And in the woods, the deer are calling as the sun goes down.

[long-pause]

{img:catherine-of-aragon-queen-1520-gable-hood-gown-dignified-kind}
The queen of England in 1520 is Catherine of Aragon.

[pause]

She is thirty-four, six years older than Henry. She is the youngest daughter of the king and queen of Spain. They are the same king and queen who once sent Columbus across the ocean, to the New World.

[long-pause]

Catherine came to England when she was fifteen. She came to marry Henry's older brother, Arthur. But Arthur passed away just five months after the wedding. Catherine was left alone in a strange country. She was young and poor and lonely. For seven years, her father and Henry's father argued about money. Nobody knew what would happen to her.

[pause]

Then Henry became king. One of the first things he did was marry her. He said he loved her. They were crowned together in Westminster Abbey, and the people cheered in the streets.

[pause]

{img:young-royal-couple-coronation-westminster-abbey-1509-crowds}
Catherine is small and pretty, with long red-gold hair and blue eyes. She is clever and well taught. She is kind and brave and very religious. Once, while Henry was away at war, she ruled England for him. The Scots invaded from the north. Catherine organised the army herself. It is said she even rode north in armour to speak to the soldiers. And the English won.

[long-pause]

The English people love her. She gives to the poor and visits the sick.

[pause]

But her life has been full of sorrow. In eleven years she has been expecting a baby six times. But most of her babies did not live. Once a son was born. The whole country cheered with bonfires and bells. But the little boy lived only fifty-two days.

[pause]

{img:queen-catherine-with-little-princess-mary-garden-greenwich-spring}
Only one child has lived. That is Mary, who is four. She is a pretty, clever girl with red-gold hair. She already plays music and dances. Her father adores her. He carries her around in his arms and shows her to visitors.

[long-pause]

In 1520, Henry and Catherine still seem a loving couple. He wears her colours at the jousts. They dance and hunt and pray together. But Henry wants a son. And Catherine is thirty-four now. The doctors begin to doubt she will have more children. Nobody says it out loud. But at court, people are starting to wonder.

[pause]

Little Mary will one day be queen herself. And her half-sister Elizabeth, not yet born, will be one of the greatest queens in English history. But in 1520, nobody can imagine that.

[long-pause]

{img:cardinal-wolsey-scarlet-robes-hat-large-man-desk-letters-hampton}
After the king, the most powerful man in England is Thomas Wolsey. He is a cardinal, one of the highest leaders of the Church. He is also the king's chief minister.

[pause]

He is about forty-seven. He is a big, heavy man with a strong, clever face. He always wears the red robes and the red hat of a cardinal.

[long-pause]

He was not born a lord. His father was a butcher in a small town called Ipswich. But Thomas was very clever. He went to Oxford University at fifteen. He became a priest. Then he became a helper to the old king, and then to the young Henry.

[pause]

He worked harder than anyone. He liked the dull work of running a country. The young king did not. So Henry gave him more and more power. By 1520, Wolsey runs almost everything. He runs the government and the courts and the Church. Visitors from abroad say he is the real ruler of England. But all his power comes from one man. If the king ever turns against him, he will lose everything.

[pause]

{img:wolsey-procession-london-street-gentlemen-silver-crosses-mule}
He loves to show off. When he rides through London, he sits on a mule covered in red velvet. Two big silver crosses are carried in front of him. Dozens of gentlemen walk before and behind. They call out, make way, make way for my Lord Cardinal. He holds an orange to his nose. It is hollow and filled with a sponge soaked in vinegar and spices. He thinks it protects him from the smells and the sickness of the crowd.

[long-pause]

His household is huge, about five hundred people. There are lords and knights and priests, musicians and cooks and servants. His income is huge too.

[pause]

But he also does good things. He sets up new courts where poor people can get justice cheaply, even against the rich. He tries to stop rich landowners from fencing off common fields for their sheep. He starts a great college at Oxford. Today it is called Christ Church, one of the most beautiful colleges there. And he starts a school in his home town.

[pause]

{img:christ-church-oxford-tom-quad-college-golden-stone-later}
And in 1520 he is busy planning the biggest event of the age. A meeting between King Henry and King Francis of France. It will be called the Field of the Cloth of Gold. If it goes well, there may be peace. If it goes badly, there may be war.

[pause]

But first, let us visit his great new house on the Thames. It is called Hampton Court.

[long-pause]

{img:hampton-court-palace-red-brick-gatehouse-chimneys-1520-thames}
About twenty kilometres up the Thames, west of London, Cardinal Wolsey is building the finest house in England. It is called Hampton Court.

[pause]

He began it six years ago. By 1520 it is a great palace of warm red brick. It has big courtyards, tall gatehouses and towers. And on the roofs stand hundreds of tall twisted chimneys. Each one is different.

[long-pause]

You arrive by boat, past meadows and willows. You land at the water gate. You walk up through the gardens to the great gate, with its towers and painted stone shields.

[pause]

Inside is a big courtyard of red brick. Around it are about forty sets of rooms for guests. Each has its own fireplace and its own small toilet. Through another gate lie the great rooms. There is the hall, the chapel, the long galleries and the cardinal's own rooms.

[pause]

{img:tudor-long-gallery-tapestries-linenfold-panelling-windows-light}
The rooms are full of wonderful things. Wolsey has bought more than six hundred tapestries from Flanders. These are big woven pictures that hang on the walls. They show stories from the Bible and from history, in rich colours with gold thread. There are carpets from Turkey and Persia. He once ordered sixty in one go. There are beds with silk curtains. And there are gold and silver plates and cups by the hundred.

[long-pause]

There are gardens in the new style, with patterns of low hedges filled with flowers. There are paths and fountains and painted wooden posts. There are orchards and a park for hunting deer.

[pause]

And there is water. Fresh, clean water comes from springs five kilometres away. It runs through lead pipes, even under the river, to the palace. It goes to the kitchens and the fountains. It is even said to flush the toilets.

[pause]

{img:tudor-knot-garden-box-hedges-herbs-flowers-fountain-hampton}
Visitors from all over Europe are amazed. The king himself comes to stay. He looks around and admires it all. Perhaps he admires it a little too much. A servant should not have a finer house than his king. Wolsey notices. And he begins to worry.

[long-pause]

A few years later, Wolsey will lose the king's favour. He will give Hampton Court to the king as a gift. Henry will make it even bigger. He will add a great hall with a beautiful wooden roof. He will add huge kitchens that can feed more than a thousand people. And he will add a tennis court that is still used today.

[pause]

Hampton Court is still there by the river. It is one of the most beautiful palaces in England.

[long-pause]

{img:tudor-garden-raised-beds-herbs-flowers-rails-painted-posts-sunny}
The Tudors love gardens.

[pause]

The great houses have pleasure gardens in the new style from France and Italy. They are called knot gardens. Low clipped hedges make patterns like knots. The spaces between them are filled with flowers and herbs or coloured sand. They look best from the windows above. There are paths of gravel and grass. There are fountains and sundials. And there are shady seats under roses and honeysuckle.

[long-pause]

Henry likes tall wooden posts in his gardens. They are painted green and white, the Tudor colours. On top sit carved golden animals, like lions and dragons and greyhounds. Some gardens also have a small man-made hill. A path winds up to a little summer house on top. From there you can see the gardens and the river.

[pause]

The flowers are old English flowers, and some new ones. There are roses, red and white, the signs of the Tudor family. There are pinks that smell of cloves. There are violets and primroses and lilies and lavender.

[pause]

{img:tudor-orchard-apple-pear-cherry-trees-blossom-bees-skeps-spring}
Every house has a garden, even the smallest cottage. People grow cabbages and onions, peas and beans and lettuce. They grow herbs for cooking and medicine. They also spread herbs on the floors, to make the house smell sweet. And they grow fruit trees, apples and pears and plums and cherries. Many keep bees in round baskets of straw.

[long-pause]

Henry loves fruit. He has new kinds planted from France. His fruit grower plants great orchards in Kent. That is the start of Kent's fame as the garden of England.

[pause]

On summer evenings, the ladies and gentlemen of the court walk in the gardens. They pick flowers and herbs. They sit in the shade and play music and talk. They smell the roses and the lavender. And swallows swoop low over the river.

[long-pause]

{img:still-room-woman-distilling-rosewater-herbs-bottles-tudor-house}
In the great houses, the lady has a special room called a still room. Here she and her maids make medicines and perfumes from the garden. They make rose water and lavender water. They make syrups and creams, sugared flowers and fruit jam for the winter. They write their recipes down in household books. Mothers hand these books to their daughters, for many generations.

[long-pause]

{img:tudor-court-presence-chamber-throne-canopy-courtiers-silk-velvet}
The king's court is the heart of England. It is the centre of power and fashion and music. It is also full of ambition and gossip.

[pause]

It is not in one place. The king has many palaces. There is Greenwich by the river, where he was born. There is Richmond to the west. There is Westminster, next to the abbey. There is Windsor, and more. The whole court moves from one palace to another through the year. It moves with the seasons and the hunting. And it moves when a palace gets too dirty and smelly and needs a good clean.

[long-pause]

The court is huge. Perhaps a thousand or fifteen hundred people. There are lords and ladies and knights. There are priests and doctors and musicians. There are cooks and grooms and hunters, guards and washerwomen.

[pause]

The king's rooms are in a row, one after another. Each room is more private than the last. First comes the Great Hall, where everyone eats. Then the guard room, where the king's guards stand in red and gold. Then the room with the throne, where the king meets visitors. Then the king's private rooms, where only his closest friends may go. And last, his bedroom.

[pause]

{img:yeomen-of-the-guard-red-gold-uniforms-halberds-palace-doorway}
The closer you get to the king, the more power you have. His close friends help him dress. They play cards with him and ride with him. They can talk to him alone, every day. So they are some of the most powerful men in England. Everyone else is trying to get through those doors. And everyone watches who goes in, and who is sent away.

[long-pause]

Life at court is one long round of fun. There is hunting by day. There are feasts and dancing and music and plays at night.

[pause]

A new kind of party has come from Italy. It is called a masque. The king and his friends dress up in wild costumes and masks. They might be foreign princes, or knights, or wild men of the woods. They burst into the hall without warning and dance with the ladies. Then they take off their masks. And everyone pretends to be surprised that it is the king.

[pause]

{img:masque-court-costumes-masks-dancers-candlelit-hall-surprise}
The courtiers play cards and dice and chess, sometimes for a lot of money. They play tennis, a new and fashionable game. It is played indoors, in a special hall with walls and a sloping roof. They write poems and love letters. They sing and gossip and flirt. And all the time, they try to win the king's favour.

[long-pause]

Every morning, the king goes to church in his chapel. The best choir in England sings for him. Every day he does a little work. He reads the letters and reports that Wolsey sends him. And then he goes hunting.

[long-pause]

{img:london-bridge-1520-houses-shops-arches-river-boats-chapel}
Let us walk through London in the spring of 1520.

[pause]

The city lies along the north bank of the Thames, inside its old walls. It is about two kilometres long and one kilometre wide. Seven gates lead in and out. Outside the walls, new houses spread along the roads and along the river to the west.

[long-pause]

There is only one bridge across the Thames. It is London Bridge, one of the wonders of Europe. It is made of stone and has nineteen arches. On top of it stand houses and shops, four or five floors high. They line both sides of a narrow street. You can walk all the way across without seeing the river. In the middle stands a small chapel.

[pause]

Under the bridge, the water rushes very fast between the narrow arches. Rowing through is dangerous. People have a saying about it. London Bridge was made for wise men to go over, and for fools to go under. So many people get out of their boat before the bridge. They walk around and get back in on the other side.

[pause]

{img:watermen-boats-thames-passengers-crying-oars-westward-ho}
The river is London's main road. Thousands of small boats go up and down. The boatmen call out to find passengers. Eastward ho, they shout, and westward ho. Below the bridge, big ships tie up from all over Europe. They bring wine from France and spices from Venice and cloth from Flanders. And they take English wool away.

[long-pause]

The streets are narrow, crowded, noisy and dirty. The houses are made of wood and plaster. The upper floors stick out over the street. On each side they almost touch at the top. So the street below is dark, even at midday.

[pause]

Down the middle of each street runs a little gutter. Rain and dirty water flow along it to the river. People throw their rubbish out of the windows. Pigs and dogs and chickens wander about. In summer, the smell is terrible.

[pause]

{img:narrow-london-street-overhanging-timber-houses-signs-crowds}
But London is also rich and beautiful and exciting. There are more than a hundred churches, and their bells ring all day. There are the grand halls of the trade groups. The goldsmiths have one, and the fishmongers, and the cloth sellers. There are fine houses of rich traders, with gardens. And there are big monasteries with quiet cloisters.

[long-pause]

And above everything, on the hill at the west end of the city, stands Saint Paul's. It is a huge old church. Its spire is the tallest in England, about a hundred and fifty metres high. You can see it from far away.

[pause]

The long middle of the church is used as a meeting place. People walk up and down there. They gossip and do business. They hire servants and hear the news. And outside, in the churchyard, booksellers have their shops. They sell the new printed books.

[long-pause]

{img:london-apprentice-shop-opening-shutters-dawn-street-cheapside}
Let us spend an ordinary day in London with an apprentice. He is a boy of sixteen, learning to be a goldsmith. He works in Cheapside, the great market street of London.

[pause]

He lives in his master's house, above the shop. He sleeps in the attic with the other boys. He will serve his master for seven years. He gets no pay, only food and clothes and a bed. In return, his master teaches him the craft.

[long-pause]

He gets up at dawn, at about five, when the church bells ring. He takes down the wooden shutters at the front of the shop. The lower shutter folds down to make a counter. On it he lays out silver cups and spoons and rings and chains.

[pause]

Breakfast is bread and weak beer. Then the work begins. The master and his workers sit at their benches by the window, for the light. They hammer and engrave and polish silver and gold. The boy helps and learns. He sweeps the floor and runs errands. And he stands at the front calling out to people passing by. What do you lack? What do you lack?

[pause]

{img:goldsmith-workshop-bench-silver-cup-engraving-window-light}
Cheapside is the widest and grandest street in London. It is lined with goldsmiths and silk sellers and spice sellers. In the middle of the street there is a tall stone cross. There is also a big fountain where water carriers fill their tall wooden tubs.

[long-pause]

At about eleven, the household eats dinner, the main meal of the day. They all sit at the master's table. The master and his wife, their children, the workers, the apprentices and the servants. There is thick soup of vegetables and grain. There is bread, and meat, or fish on Fridays. And there is beer.

[pause]

In the afternoon the work goes on until evening. At about seven or eight, the shop is closed and the shutters go up.

[pause]

{img:apprentices-playing-football-fields-outside-city-walls-evening}
After supper, the apprentices are free for a little while. They go out to the fields outside the city walls. They play football, a wild rough game with a pig's bladder for a ball. Or they practise archery, as every Englishman must by law. Or they wrestle. Or they go to the inns, although they are not supposed to.

[long-pause]

The London apprentices are famous for being rowdy. When there is trouble, they gather in hundreds and fight. Three years ago, on the first of May, there was a big riot. The apprentices attacked the houses of foreign traders. Afterwards many of them were in great danger. They could have been hanged. Then Queen Catherine herself knelt before the king. She begged him to show mercy. And many of the boys were set free.

[pause]

At nine o'clock, the curfew bell rings from a church in Cheapside. The apprentices must be home. The city gates are shut. And the night watchmen walk the dark streets with lanterns and bells, calling out the hours.

[long-pause]

{img:tudor-doctor-physician-gown-urine-flask-herbs-apothecary-shop}
Life in Tudor England is often short. Illness is never far away.

[pause]

There are no modern medicines and no vaccines. Doctors know very little about why people get sick. They believe what the old Greeks believed. The body has four fluids, and illness comes when they are out of balance. So doctors let out some blood. Or they give medicines made of herbs and minerals and strange things.

[long-pause]

The most feared illness of Henry's England is the sweating sickness. It is a strange disease. It first came in 1485 and returned several times. Then it vanished and was never seen again. Nobody knows what it really was.

[pause]

It struck suddenly, often in summer. And it was very quick. People said you could be merry at dinner and gone by supper. It seemed to strike the rich and the young and the strong more than the poor. Henry was terrified of it. Whenever it came, he fled from London. He moved from house to house in the country, with only a few servants. He took his own medicines and prayed. And every summer, the court watches and waits, and hopes it will not come back.

[pause]

{img:herb-garden-tudor-apothecary-gathering-lavender-sage-rosemary}
Most ordinary people never see a doctor. Doctors cost too much. Instead they use remedies from the herb garden. The women of the family know a lot about plants. Sage for a sore throat. Lavender for a headache. Rosemary for memory. Honey for wounds.

[long-pause]

Henry himself is very interested in medicine. He writes his own recipes for creams and medicines. He gives them to his friends and servants. He makes some for his own sore leg. A few of his recipes still survive in an old book in London.

[pause]

And two years before our story, Henry set up a college for doctors in London. It makes sure doctors are properly trained. It still exists today.

[long-pause]

{img:barber-surgeon-shop-striped-pole-basin-tools-london-street}
In the towns there are also barber surgeons. They cut hair and shave beards. They also pull teeth and set broken bones and treat wounds. Outside their shops hangs a pole with red and white stripes. The red is for blood and the white is for bandages. You can still see such poles outside barber shops today.

[long-pause]

{img:thomas-more-house-chelsea-family-gathered-garden-thames-1520}
One of the most remarkable men in England in 1520 is Thomas More.

[pause]

He is forty-two years old. He is a lawyer and a judge, a scholar and a writer. He sits on the king's council. And he is the king's friend. Henry likes to visit him at home. They walk together in the garden, with the king's arm around his neck. They talk about the stars and about God and about poetry.

[long-pause]

More is famous all over Europe for a little book. It is called Utopia. It is about a made-up island in the New World. The name means no place. On this island, everyone lives in peace. Nobody owns things for himself alone. Everyone works six hours a day and spends the rest of the time learning. The people choose their own rulers. And gold is worth so little there that they use it to make chains for prisoners.

[pause]

It is a clever and funny book. But it is also serious. It makes people think about the problems of their own country. The poor are poor, and the rich are greedy. Farmers are pushed off the land to make room for sheep. In Utopia, More writes that the sheep have become so hungry they eat up men.

[pause]

{img:scholar-writing-latin-book-study-books-candle-window-tudor}
Ever since, people have used the word utopia for a perfect made-up world.

[long-pause]

More is warm and funny and kind. He is also very religious. His good friend Erasmus describes him in a letter. His hair is dark brown, Erasmus writes, and his eyes are grey-blue. He is always smiling and joking.

[pause]

More loves his family and his home. In a few years he will build a new house at Chelsea, a village by the Thames. It will have a big garden running down to the river. He will live there with his wife Alice and his children and grandchildren. And with many pets. A monkey, dogs, rabbits, birds, a fox and a weasel.

[pause]

{img:holbein-style-family-portrait-room-tudor-household-reading-calm}
He believes girls should learn as much as boys. That is unusual in his time. He teaches his daughters Latin and Greek and the stars. His eldest daughter Margaret becomes one of the most learned women in England. She even writes letters to Erasmus, in Latin.

[long-pause]

A few years later, a German painter called Hans Holbein will come to stay with More. He will draw the whole More family together at Chelsea. It is the first big family portrait in English art. The painting is lost. But the drawing survives. It shows them reading and talking. There is a little dog. And the monkey is climbing up Lady Alice's dress.

[pause]

More's story will later turn sad. He will have to choose between his king and what he believes is right. We will come back to that. But in 1520 he is happy and respected. And he is still the king's friend.

[long-pause]

{img:erasmus-scholar-fur-coat-writing-desk-books-cambridge-room}
One of the most famous visitors to Tudor England is a Dutch scholar. His name is Erasmus, from Rotterdam.

[pause]

In 1520 he is the most famous writer in Europe. He was the son of a priest. He became a monk and then a scholar. He travelled all over Europe, writing books in Latin and making friends everywhere. He is witty and wise and gentle. He thinks people should read the Bible and the great old writers of Greece and Rome. He thinks they should live simple, good lives. And he thinks the Church needs to change. But slowly and peacefully, with learning and kindness.

[long-pause]

He has visited England several times, and he loves it. He is a close friend of Thomas More. In More's house he wrote his most famous book. It is called In Praise of Folly. It is a funny book that pokes fun at the silly things people do. Even priests and princes. He gave it to More with a joke. The Latin word for folly sounds just like More's name.

[pause]

He also taught Greek at Cambridge University for a few years. There is still a tower there named after him. In his letters he complained about the cold and the bad beer and the cost of everything. But he also wrote that English girls had a charming custom. They kissed visitors hello and goodbye.

[pause]

{img:queens-college-cambridge-old-court-tower-river-cam-spring}
A few years ago, he printed the New Testament in its first language, Greek. Scholars all over Europe used it. It helped others to put the Bible into English and German, so that ordinary people could read it.

[long-pause]

Erasmus wants peace. Peace between the kings of Europe, and peace inside the Church. He is delighted by the coming meeting of the kings of England and France. Maybe now there will be peace. But in the years that follow, Europe will be torn apart by quarrels about faith, and by wars. And Erasmus, who wanted everyone to be calm and reasonable, will be attacked by both sides.

[pause]

He passed away in 1536, in Switzerland. Today a big European student programme is named after him. It lets millions of young people study in other countries. It is called Erasmus.

[long-pause]

{img:erasmus-letter-writing-candle-many-letters-friends-europe-desk}
Erasmus wrote thousands of letters to his friends all over Europe. They are warm and witty and full of news and ideas. Many still survive. In one of them he describes the home of Thomas More. He calls it a school of Christian life. Everyone there read and studied, he says, even the children and the servants. Nobody was ever lazy. Nobody ever quarrelled. And everyone was cheerful.

[long-pause]

{img:tudor-village-thatched-cottages-green-church-maypole-spring}
Most English people in 1520 do not live in London. They live in villages in the countryside.

[pause]

Let us visit a village in the south of England, in spring. It lies in a valley by a little river. It has a stone church with a square tower. It has a manor house where the lord lives. And it has about thirty houses along a lane and around a green. Geese eat the grass on the green. Children play there. And in May, a tall maypole stands in the middle.

[long-pause]

The houses are made of wood. The walls are woven sticks covered with clay and straw, painted white. The roofs are thick straw. Most houses have one or two rooms on the ground floor, with a fire in the middle. Some have a loft above for sleeping. The richer farmers have more rooms. Some even have a chimney and glass in the windows. That is new and very fashionable.

[pause]

Around the village lie the big open fields. They are split into long narrow strips. Each family farms a few strips. There are meadows by the river for hay. There is the common, where everyone can let their animals graze. And there are woods for firewood and for the pigs.

[pause]

{img:ploughing-oxen-open-field-strips-tudor-farmers-spring-sky}
The villagers work hard all year, with the seasons. They plough and sow in spring and autumn. They make hay in June. The harvest comes in August and September. Then everyone works in the fields from dawn to dusk. Men and women and children cut the grain with curved knives. They tie it into bundles and carry it to the barns. In winter they beat the grain out of the straw, in the barns.

[long-pause]

The sheep must be watched and moved. In early summer they are washed in the river and their wool is cut off. The cows must be milked twice a day. And there are pigs and hens and geese and bees.

[pause]

The village has its craftsmen too. There is the blacksmith at his fire. There is the carpenter, and the miller at the mill by the river. There is the man who makes straw roofs. And there is the alewife, who brews beer and sells it from her house. A bunch of green leaves on a pole outside her door shows that she has beer for sale.

[pause]

{img:village-alewife-house-bush-sign-door-villagers-ale-evening}
And there is the parson, the village priest. He lives in a house by the church. He holds the service every Sunday. He baptises the babies and marries the young people and buries the dead. He visits the sick. And often he teaches a few children to read.

[long-pause]

But for many villagers, life is slowly getting harder. Some landowners are putting hedges and fences around the common fields. They turn them into big fields for sheep. Sheep make more money than grain, and they need fewer workers. So farmers who lived on the land for generations are pushed out. They must find work somewhere else. Some end up as beggars on the roads. Every family in the village wonders who will be next.

[pause]

This is what Thomas More meant when he wrote that the sheep were eating men.

[long-pause]

{img:tudor-children-playing-hoop-spinning-top-village-lane-summer}
What is life like for children in Tudor England?

[pause]

Many babies and small children do not survive, because of illness. So parents treasure the children they have. They worry about them and pray for them. Babies are wrapped tightly in long strips of cloth. They sleep in wooden cradles by the fire.

[long-pause]

Children play with toys. There are wooden dolls and hobby horses, sticks with a horse's head on top. There are spinning tops, hoops, balls and marbles. There are tiny metal toys, little knights and horses and plates for a doll's house. Some of these have been found in the mud of the Thames in London. And children play games we still know. Blind man's buff, hide and seek, leapfrog and hopscotch.

[pause]

From a young age, children work too. In the country they help in the fields and with the animals. They chase birds away from the new seeds. They look after the geese and the pigs. They gather wood and fetch water. Girls help their mothers with spinning and cooking and the little ones.

[pause]

{img:tudor-grammar-school-boys-hornbook-schoolmaster-bench-latin}
Some boys go to school. In the small village school, they learn to read from a hornbook. It is a little wooden board with a handle. On it is a sheet of paper with the alphabet and a prayer. A thin clear sheet made of animal horn covers it, to keep it clean. Later some boys go to a grammar school in town. There they learn Latin from dawn to dusk, six days a week. The teacher is strict, and he keeps a stick close by.

[long-pause]

At about fourteen, many boys leave home. They go to the towns as apprentices for seven years. Or they become servants in richer houses. Girls often go into service too, from about twelve. They learn to run a house and save money for when they marry.

[pause]

The children of lords are taught at home by tutors. They learn Latin and French, music and dancing and riding and good manners. Often they are sent to live in another great house, or at court. There they serve as pages and learn the ways of the world.

[pause]

{img:princess-mary-child-virginals-lesson-tutor-palace-room-light}
Little Princess Mary is four in 1520. She already plays a small keyboard called the virginals. She is learning Latin and French. She will be one of the best taught princesses in Europe. Her mother asked a Spanish scholar to write a book about how Mary should learn. The book says girls should study and read just as well as boys.

[long-pause]

{img:tudor-banquet-great-hall-roast-meats-pies-subtlety-sugar-sculpture}
What do the English eat in 1520?

[pause]

It depends a lot on who you are. The rich eat huge amounts of meat. The poor eat mostly bread and thick soup, with some cheese and a little bacon.

[long-pause]

At the king's court there are two big meals a day. Dinner is at about ten or eleven in the morning. Supper is at about four or five in the afternoon. Hundreds of people eat at court every day. And the king pays for all of it.

[pause]

A great feast has two or three courses. Each course has many dishes, all put on the table at once. People choose what they like. There is beef and lamb and pork. There is deer from the king's parks. There are birds of every kind. Chickens and geese and ducks, pheasants and quails. There are even swans and peacocks. A roast peacock is sometimes served with its feathers put back on, just for show. And there are pies with golden decorated crusts.

[pause]

{img:tudor-kitchen-huge-fireplace-spits-cooks-boys-hampton-court}
On Fridays there is no meat, only fish. The same goes for the weeks before Easter, and many other fast days. There is salmon and trout and carp from the ponds. There are eels and herrings, oysters and crabs. The Church even counts seals and porpoises as fish.

[long-pause]

The rich love sugar and spices. Pepper, ginger, cinnamon and cloves come from far away in Asia. They are very costly. The rich put sugar in almost everything, even in meat and fish.

[pause]

At the end of a great feast comes a course of sweet things. It is eaten in a separate room, or in a little summer house in the garden. There is marzipan and gingerbread. There are sugared fruits and jellies and spiced wine. And there are sugar sculptures. They are made of sugar and marzipan, in the shape of castles and ships and animals. They are painted and covered in gold, to amaze the guests.

[pause]

{img:marzipan-sugar-subtlety-castle-ship-banquet-table-candles-gold}
The rich drink wine from France and Spain. Everyone else drinks ale, a thick sweet beer made without hops. It does not keep long. Beer made with hops is just starting to come over from Flanders. Old-fashioned English people do not like it at all.

[long-pause]

The poor eat dark bread made of rye or barley. They eat thick stew of peas or beans or oats. Onions and cabbage and herbs from the garden go in the pot. It cooks over the fire all day. There is cheese and butter and eggs, and a little bacon from the family pig. Fresh meat is a rare treat, for festivals.

[pause]

Potatoes and tomatoes and tea and coffee and chocolate are not yet known in England. They will come in the years ahead, from the New World and from the East. And they will change the way everyone eats.

[long-pause]

{img:tudor-christmas-great-hall-yule-log-holly-ivy-feast-candles}
The greatest feast of the Tudor year is Christmas. And it is not one day, but twelve. It lasts from Christmas Day to Twelfth Night, on the sixth of January.

[pause]

At court, Christmas means feasting and music and plays and dancing, every day and every night. The halls are decorated with holly and ivy and rosemary. And a huge log burns in the fireplace.

[long-pause]

A Lord of Misrule is chosen. He is a courtier or even a servant. For twelve days he is in charge of all the games and jokes. Everyone must obey his silly orders, even the king. On Twelfth Night there is a big cake with a bean hidden inside. Whoever finds the bean in their slice becomes King of the Bean for the night.

[pause]

The food is splendid. A boar's head is carried in on a great dish, with an apple in its mouth. Everyone sings a special song as it comes in. There is roast goose and swan. There are big Christmas pies full of meat and dried fruit and spices. Over the centuries, these will turn into the small mince pies the English still eat at Christmas. And there is a thick spicy porridge with dried fruit. Slowly, it will become the Christmas pudding.

[pause]

{img:boars-head-carried-feast-trumpets-singers-tudor-hall-christmas}
On New Year's Day, the king and the courtiers give each other gifts. The king gives gold and silver cups. The courtiers give him jewels and books, horses and hawks and sweets. Every gift is written down on a long roll of parchment. Some of these rolls still survive.

[long-pause]

In the villages, Christmas is the great feast too. The farm work stops for twelve days. The lord often invites the villagers to a big Christmas dinner in his hall. The young people go from house to house singing. They carry a big bowl of hot spiced ale with roasted apples floating in it. They wish everyone good health for the new year. And in return they get food and drink and a few coins.

[pause]

{img:wassailers-singing-snowy-village-door-bowl-lantern-night}
After Twelfth Night comes Plough Monday. The ploughmen drag a plough through the village, decorated with ribbons. They ask for coins. And the next day, the farming year begins again.

[long-pause]

{img:tudor-gentleman-doublet-slashed-sleeves-codpiece-flat-cap-gown}
The English of 1520 love fine clothes. And Henry's court is one of the most colourful in Europe.

[pause]

A gentleman wears a shirt of fine white linen. Over it he wears a doublet, a tight padded jacket of silk or velvet. The sleeves often have little cuts in them. Through the cuts, a different colour puffs out underneath. On his legs he wears tight stockings. And over everything he wears a gown. That is a long loose coat with wide sleeves and a big fur collar.

[long-pause]

On his head he wears a flat velvet cap with a feather. His shoes are wide and square at the toes. And he wears jewels everywhere. Rings on his fingers, gold chains around his neck, jewelled buttons.

[pause]

The king dresses more grandly than anyone. He wears cloth woven with real gold and silver thread. He wears purple velvet and white fur. Rubies and diamonds and pearls are sewn all over his clothes. A visitor from Venice once saw him at a feast. He wrote that the king shone like the sun.

[pause]

{img:tudor-lady-gable-hood-square-neckline-gown-sleeves-velvet-jewels}
A lady wears a long gown with a square neckline. The sleeves are long and wide, turned back to show a rich lining of fur or silk. The skirt falls to the ground. On her head she wears a hood shaped like the roof of a house. A black veil hangs down behind and covers all her hair. Later a smaller rounded hood will become the fashion, from France.

[long-pause]

There are laws about who may wear what. Only the king and his family may wear purple silk. Only the highest lords may wear cloth of gold. Ordinary people may not wear silk at all, or fine fur. Rabbit fur is fine. The laws are meant to keep everyone in their place. Just by looking, you can tell how important someone is.

[pause]

Ordinary people wear wool and linen in plain colours. Reddish brown, grey, blue and green. A countryman wears a shirt, a loose tunic with a belt, and a woollen cap. A countrywoman wears a long woollen gown with an apron. She covers her hair with a close linen cap.

[pause]

{img:tudor-countrywoman-coif-apron-wool-gown-basket-eggs-lane}
And everyone, rich or poor, owns only a few clothes. Cloth is costly, and every piece is sewn by hand. Clothes are mended and remade and handed down. People even leave them to their children in their wills. A fine gown might be worn for many years. Then it is cut down into a smaller gown for a daughter. And at last it becomes a cushion cover.

[long-pause]

{img:bristol-harbour-1497-small-ship-matthew-cabot-departure-dawn}
In 1520, the world is getting bigger.

[pause]

Twenty-eight years ago, Columbus sailed west for Spain. He found the islands of the Caribbean. He thought they were near Asia. Five years later, an Italian sailor called John Cabot set out from Bristol, in England. He had a small ship called the Matthew and eighteen men. He sailed for Henry's father. He crossed the ocean further north. And he landed somewhere on the coast of what is now Canada.

[long-pause]

Nobody knew if they would ever come back. The ocean was huge and empty. But they did come back, with amazing news. The sea there was so full of fish that you could catch them with a basket. Soon fishermen from England and France and Portugal were crossing the ocean every summer. They came home with ships full of salted fish.

[pause]

In 1519, a Portuguese captain called Magellan set out with five ships. He wanted to sail all the way around the world. In 1520 he is crossing the Pacific Ocean, the first European ever to do so. Nobody knows how big it is. The food runs out, and the water goes bad. Only one of his five ships will make it home, three years later. It is the first ship ever to sail around the world.

[pause]

{img:world-map-1520-new-world-outline-sea-monsters-ships-parchment}
And far away in Mexico, a Spanish adventurer has reached the great city of the Aztecs. News of its wealth and gold is reaching Europe. In 1520 a German artist, Albrecht Dürer, sees some Aztec treasures in Brussels. He writes that he has never seen anything so beautiful.

[long-pause]

The news reaches England slowly. It comes through traders and visitors, printed books and maps. In his book Utopia, Thomas More imagines a sailor who found a new island across the ocean.

[pause]

Strange new things begin to arrive in England in these years. Turkeys and tobacco. Later potatoes and tomatoes and chocolate. Parrots and monkeys. And stories of new lands and peoples, far across the sea.

[pause]

{img:merchant-showing-parrot-curiosities-new-world-tudor-house-wonder}
In 1520, England is still a small country on the edge of Europe. Its navy is small. But in the years ahead, under Henry's daughter Elizabeth, English sailors will cross the oceans. The first English towns will be founded in America. And England will start to look out across the sea, to the whole world.

[long-pause]

{img:english-parish-church-1520-painted-walls-rood-screen-candles-mass}
In 1520, England is a Catholic country. It has been for nearly a thousand years. It is part of the great Church of western Europe, led by the Pope in Rome.

[pause]

Nobody in England can imagine what will happen next. Within twenty years everything will change. The monasteries will be closed. The Pope will no longer lead the English Church. The bright pictures will be taken out of the churches. But all that is still in the future.

[long-pause]

In 1520, the village churches of England are full of colour.

[pause]

The walls are covered with paintings. They show the saints and Christ and the end of the world. Most people cannot read. So they learn the stories of their faith by looking at the pictures. The windows are full of coloured glass, glowing red and blue and gold. There are painted statues of the saints with candles burning in front of them.

[pause]

{img:rood-screen-carved-painted-saints-candles-parish-church-dim-light}
Across the church stands a big carved wooden screen. It is painted and covered with gold. It divides the people from the altar. On top of it stands a great carved cross.

[pause]

The service is in Latin, which most people do not understand. But they know the important moments. A bell rings. The priest lifts the holy bread high above his head. And everyone kneels and prays.

[long-pause]

The year is full of holy days and processions. In early summer there is a great feast day. The whole town walks through the streets behind the holy bread. There are banners and candles. In some towns, the craftsmen put on plays on wagons in the street. In one long day, from dawn to dusk, they tell the whole story of the Bible. From the making of the world to its end.

[pause]

{img:mystery-play-wagon-street-york-guild-actors-crowd-summer}
There are about eight hundred monasteries and convents all over England. They have great churches and much land. They run schools and hospitals. They feed the poor and give travellers a bed.

[pause]

And there are pilgrimages. People walk to holy places to pray. The greatest is at Canterbury, the shrine of Saint Thomas. It is covered with gold and jewels. Another is at Walsingham. King Henry himself once walked the last mile to Walsingham barefoot. He went there to pray for a son.

[long-pause]

But in Germany, three years ago, a monk called Martin Luther began to question the Church. He asked hard questions about its teaching and its power. His books are being printed and copied. Some are already being read in secret in England. Young scholars at Cambridge meet in an inn called the White Horse to talk about them. They must be careful. Reading these books is dangerous.

[pause]

Next year Henry will write his book against Luther. He will burn Luther's books in London. He does not know that a few years later, he himself will break away from Rome.

[long-pause]

{img:printing-press-london-1520-workshop-compositors-type-paper-sheets}
In 1520, books are changing the world.

[pause]

About seventy years ago, a German goldsmith called Gutenberg found a new way to make books. He made little metal letters that could be moved around. You put them together into words and lines and pages. You put ink on them. Then you press them onto paper in a big wooden press. Now you can make hundreds of copies of a book, fast and cheap. Before, every book had to be copied by hand, one by one.

[long-pause]

The first printer in England was William Caxton. He was a trader who learned printing in Flanders. He set up his press in London about forty years ago. He printed about a hundred books, many of them in English. He printed The Canterbury Tales, a famous book of stories in English verse. And he printed the stories of King Arthur.

[pause]

After him, other printers came. Caxton's helper moved the press to Fleet Street. For five hundred years after that, Fleet Street was the street of printers and newspapers.

[pause]

{img:st-pauls-churchyard-bookstalls-books-readers-browsing-1520}
By 1520, there are bookshops all around Saint Paul's churchyard. They sell prayer books and sermons and Latin grammar for schoolboys. They sell little yearly books with the dates of festivals and the moon, and advice about farming and health. And they sell stories and songs and jokes.

[long-pause]

More and more people can read. Perhaps one man in ten in the country, and many more in London. Some women can read too. New schools are being started in the towns. One famous school in London was started by a friend of Erasmus and More.

[pause]

A few years from now, a young scholar called William Tyndale will print the New Testament in English. He wants a boy who drives the plough to be able to read the Bible. But his book will be forbidden. It will be smuggled into England hidden in bales of cloth. People will read it in secret. If they are caught, they will be in great trouble. And yet his words will live on in the English language for five hundred years. Let there be light. The salt of the earth. Fight the good fight.

[long-pause]

{img:candlelit-room-family-reading-printed-book-aloud-tudor-evening}
In the evenings, in houses all over England, families sit by the fire. Someone reads aloud from a printed book. It might be a prayer book, or a story, or a song. The others listen while they sew and mend. And the children slowly fall asleep.

[long-pause]

{img:dover-harbour-1520-royal-fleet-ships-flags-white-cliffs-castle}
At the end of May 1520, the king and queen and the whole court gather at Dover. Dover is on the south coast of England. From there they will cross the sea to France.

[pause]

Dover is a busy little port under great white cliffs. An old castle stands on the hill above. Here the sea between England and France is narrowest. It is only about thirty-three kilometres across. On a clear day you can see France from the cliffs, a thin grey line on the horizon.

[long-pause]

The harbour is full of ships of every size. They must carry thousands of people across the sea. And horses and food and tents and furniture and tapestries. The king's own ship has flags and banners in green and white. Some people say its sails are made of gold cloth.

[pause]

Then a surprise happens. Just before Henry leaves, a visitor arrives by sea. It is the young emperor Charles, the ruler of Spain and the Netherlands and Germany. He is Catherine's nephew. The two rulers meet at Dover. They ride together to Canterbury and spend a few days there. Catherine is overjoyed to meet her nephew for the first time. But the visit has a hidden reason. Charles is worried. He does not want England and France to become too friendly.

[pause]

{img:ships-crossing-channel-calm-sea-summer-morning-sails-france-coast}
On the last day of May, the king's ships sail across the calm blue summer sea. They sail to Calais. Calais is the last English town in France. England has held it for nearly two hundred years. It is a busy walled town by the sea, with English soldiers and English traders.

[long-pause]

From Calais the court rides in a long procession across the flat green land. They ride to the little town of Guînes, near the border. A golden palace and hundreds of tents are waiting. And on the seventh of June, the two kings will meet in the valley. The place will be remembered as the Field of the Cloth of Gold.

[long-pause]

{img:calais-walled-town-harbour-english-flags-1520-merchants-quay}
Calais in 1520 is a little piece of England in France. Its people speak English. They pay taxes to the English king. All English wool sold to Europe must pass through it. England will lose it in the end, almost forty years later. The queen then will be Mary, the little girl who is four now. It is said she spoke of Calais with great sadness. When I die, she said, you will find Calais written on my heart.

[long-pause]

{img:field-of-cloth-of-gold-1520-tents-golden-pavilions-plain-guines}
In the summer of 1520, the grandest event of the age takes place. King Henry of England meets King Francis of France. The place will be called the Field of the Cloth of Gold.

[pause]

It is Cardinal Wolsey's idea. England and France have been enemies for hundreds of years. Wolsey wants peace between them, and between all the kingdoms of Europe. So he arranges for the two young kings to meet in person. They will meet as friends. They will feast and joust and make music together for more than two weeks. But will two proud young kings really get along?

[long-pause]

The meeting is in the north of France, near Calais. It is in a valley on the border, between an English town and a French town.

[pause]

Both kings want to outshine the other. Each brings his whole court. Henry brings about six thousand people across the sea. Francis brings just as many. Lords and ladies, knights and bishops, musicians and cooks, and thousands of horses.

[pause]

More than two thousand English workmen build Henry a palace in just a few weeks. It is a big square building around a courtyard. Each side is about a hundred metres long. The bottom is brick. The top is wood and canvas, painted to look like stone. It has so many real glass windows that people call it a house of glass. Inside are great halls hung with tapestries and cloth of gold. There is a chapel with an altar of gold and jewels.

[long-pause]

In front of the palace stand two fountains. They do not flow with water but with wine. Red wine and white wine. Anyone can drink, for free, from silver cups. On top of one fountain stands a statue of the old god of wine. The words on it say, in French, be merry, all who will.

[pause]

All around stand thousands of tents in every colour. Many are covered in cloth of gold. They glitter in the summer sun. The whole valley seems to be made of gold. That is how the place got its name.

[pause]

{img:wine-fountain-statue-bacchus-silver-cups-crowd-summer-day}
The French king's great tent is covered in gold cloth outside. Inside it is lined with blue velvet and golden lilies, the sign of France. Its ceiling is painted with stars. But on the first night a strong wind blows. And many of the French tents fall down.

[long-pause]

On the seventh of June, the two kings meet for the first time.

[pause]

They ride toward each other slowly, from opposite sides of the valley. They sit on big horses, dressed in gold and silver and jewels. Their lords follow behind. Trumpets sound and cannons fire. Everyone is watching. Each side is a little nervous. What if something goes wrong? In the middle of the valley, the kings stop and look at each other. Then they ride forward. Still on horseback, they hug. They take off their hats and hug again.

[pause]

{img:two-kings-on-horseback-embracing-valley-golden-light-nobles-watching}
Then they get down and walk arm in arm into a golden tent. They talk privately with Wolsey. When they come out, they are smiling. And the crowds cheer.

[pause]

For the next seventeen days, there are feasts and jousts and dances. There is music and wrestling and archery. There are church services and gifts between the two courts.

[long-pause]

{img:king-francis-first-tall-long-nose-dark-hair-velvet-portrait}
King Francis of France is twenty-five, three years younger than Henry. He is tall, even taller than Henry. He is very charming, with dark hair and a long nose. He loves art and beauty and glory.

[pause]

He invited the great Italian artist Leonardo da Vinci to live in France. Leonardo spent his last years in a small house near the king's castle. He passed away there the year before. Francis owns Leonardo's painting of the Mona Lisa. It is still in France today, in the Louvre in Paris.

[long-pause]

Henry and Francis compete in everything. In looks and clothes and palaces and horses. In jousting and power and glory. Each one wants to be the greatest king in Europe.

[pause]

There is a famous story about their meeting. One day after a feast, Henry was in high spirits. He challenged Francis to a wrestling match. The two kings wrestled in front of their courts. And Francis, it is said, threw Henry to the ground. For a moment, everyone held their breath. Henry was furious. He wanted to try again. But the courtiers quickly pulled them apart. And the two queens talked them into going in to dinner. The English writers of the time do not mention this story. But the French ones do.

[pause]

{img:two-queens-catherine-claude-pavilion-ladies-gowns-conversation}
Another day, Francis rode over very early, without guards. He walked into Henry's bedroom and woke him up. Brother, he said, I am your prisoner. Henry was delighted. He gave Francis a precious jewelled collar. Francis gave him a bracelet worth even more. It was a sign of trust and friendship.

[long-pause]

The two queens met too. Catherine and Claude dined and danced together with their ladies. They admired each other's clothes and chatted.

[pause]

On the last day there was a great church service in the open air. Wolsey led it, on a big stage in front of both courts. The choirs of both kings sang together. At the end, a firework in the shape of a dragon flew across the sky over the crowds.

[pause]

{img:open-air-mass-platform-choirs-two-courts-banners-summer-morning}
Then the two kings said goodbye. They promised to be friends forever. And they went home.

[long-pause]

Two years later, England and France were at war again.

[pause]

The meeting cost both kings a fortune. And it changed almost nothing. But it was remembered for centuries as one of the most splendid moments of the age. Two young kings in a golden summer. A valley full of golden tents and fountains of wine. And the kings hugging on horseback, promising peace.

[pause]

A big painting of the event still hangs at Hampton Court. It is full of tiny people and tents and horses. You can see the palace and the wine fountain and the kings. And even a dragon, flying in the sky.

[long-pause]

{img:tudor-joust-tiltyard-wooden-barrier-knights-lances-armour-crowds}
The greatest sport of the Tudor court is the joust.

[pause]

Two knights in full armour ride big horses dressed in their colours. They charge at each other along a wooden fence. The fence keeps the horses apart. Each knight tries to hit the other with a long wooden pole called a lance. If he hits the helmet or the shield hard enough, the lance breaks. Or he might even knock the other knight off his horse.

[long-pause]

The lances are made of light wood. They are meant to break easily, in a shower of splinters. It makes a great noise and a great show, and the crowd cheers. Points are given for breaking a lance and for hitting the helmet.

[pause]

Jousting armour is very heavy and thick, especially on the left side. The helmet has only a narrow slit to see through. Henry's own armour can still be seen today, in the Tower of London. It shows how tall and strong and slim he was as a young man.

[pause]

{img:henry-viii-armour-display-tower-of-london-tall-polished-later}
Henry loves jousting more than anything. He jousts at every big feast. And he often wins. He is strong and brave and skilled. But perhaps some of his opponents are careful not to beat the king. At the Field of the Cloth of Gold, both kings jousted every day. But they never jousted against each other. That might have been too risky.

[long-pause]

A tournament is a great show. There are covered stands for the king and queen and the ladies, hung with rich cloth. There are heralds and trumpeters. There are tents where the knights put on their armour. And crowds of ordinary people stand around the field to watch.

[pause]

The knights ride in one by one with their helpers, all dressed in their colours. Sometimes they dress up as heroes from the old stories, like the knights of King Arthur. Each knight wears a ribbon from a lady, tied to his helmet or his lance.

[pause]

{img:ladies-gallery-tournament-ribbons-tokens-knights-saluting}
At the end of the day, the queen and the ladies give out the prizes. Then there is a big feast and dancing late into the night.

[long-pause]

Jousting is dangerous. Knights often get hurt. A few years from now, Henry will forget to close the front of his helmet. A lance will strike his head. He will be lucky not to be badly hurt. And sixteen years from now, he will have a terrible fall. He will lie still for two hours, and his leg will be badly hurt. He will never joust again. Some historians think that fall changed him. After it, he became an angry and suspicious king.

[pause]

But in 1520 he is young and strong. He jousts in the summer sun and he wins. The crowds cheer. And the queen smiles.

[long-pause]

{img:tower-of-london-white-tower-walls-moat-1520-thames-boats}
At the east end of London, by the river, stands the Tower of London.

[pause]

It is an old royal castle. It was built by William the Conqueror more than four hundred and fifty years before. In the middle stands a big square tower of white stone, called the White Tower. Walls and towers and a moat surround it. In 1520, the Tower is many things at once. It is a royal palace and a store for weapons. It keeps the king's treasure. It is where England's coins are made. It is a prison for important prisoners. And it is also a zoo.

[long-pause]

For nearly three hundred years, the kings of England have kept wild animals at the Tower. They live in a building near the main gate called the Lion Tower. There are lions and leopards and other animals. They were gifts to the kings from foreign rulers.

[pause]

Once there was a polar bear. It was allowed to swim and fish in the Thames on a long chain. Once there was an elephant, a gift from the king of France. It had its own house. And there were many lions. People believed that when a king passed away, his lion would die too.

[pause]

{img:lion-tower-menagerie-lions-cages-visitors-looking-tudor}
Londoners can visit the animals for a small fee. Or they can bring a cat or a dog to feed to the lions. It is one of the great sights of London. Every visitor wants to see it.

[long-pause]

The animals stayed at the Tower for more than six hundred years. Then about two hundred years ago, the last ones were moved to the new London Zoo.

[pause]

Today at the Tower there are wire sculptures of the animals that once lived there. A lion, a polar bear, an elephant and some monkeys. And there are the ravens. There are always at least six of them. An old legend says that if the ravens ever leave the Tower, the kingdom will fall. So a special guard looks after them. He is called the Ravenmaster. Their wings are trimmed a little, so they cannot fly far.

[pause]

{img:ravens-tower-green-yeoman-warder-ravenmaster-feeding-morning}
The guards of the Tower, called Beefeaters, still live there with their families, inside the walls. Every night at ten o'clock they lock the gates in a small old ceremony. They have done this every single night, without fail, for more than seven hundred years.

[long-pause]

{img:tudor-musicians-lute-viol-recorders-sackbut-gallery-hall-candles}
Music is everywhere in Henry's England.

[pause]

At court there are dozens of musicians. There are trumpeters and drummers for the great ceremonies and the jousts. There are loud wind players for music outside. There are lute players and harp players and flute players for quiet music indoors. And there is the royal choir of men and boys. They sing every day in the chapel, in many voices at once.

[long-pause]

The king himself is a fine musician. More than thirty songs and pieces are said to be by him. They are in an old music book from his court, which is now kept in London.

[pause]

His most famous song is Pastime with Good Company. Its words say he loves good company, and will until he dies. Hunting and singing and dancing, that is what his heart is set on.

[pause]

{img:king-singing-with-courtiers-chamber-music-book-lute-evening}
There is an old story that Henry wrote the song Greensleeves. But that is almost certainly not true. Greensleeves was written later, in the time of his daughter Elizabeth.

[long-pause]

And there is dancing. Everyone at court must dance well. Dancing teachers show the courtiers the latest dances from France and Italy. Some dances are slow and graceful, like a gentle walk. In those, the ladies show off their fine gowns. Other dances are fast, with leaps and kicks and jumps. In those, the young men show off how strong they are. And Henry, a great dancer, jumps higher than anyone.

[pause]

There is music outside the court too. In the churches, with organs and choirs. In the streets, where singers sell printed songs. In the inns, with fiddlers. And in the villages, with bagpipes, and with one man playing a pipe and a little drum together.

[pause]

{img:may-day-village-green-maypole-dancers-ribbons-pipe-tabor-spring}
On May Day, early in the morning, the young people go out into the woods. They gather white hawthorn blossom and green branches to decorate their houses. They bring back a tall tree for the maypole. They set it up on the green with ribbons and flowers. Then they dance around it all day to the pipes and drums. They choose a May Queen and a May King. And they eat and drink and play games until night.

[long-pause]

Even the king goes out on May morning. One year, Henry and Catherine and the whole court rode out into the woods near Greenwich. Two hundred of the king's archers met them there. They were dressed in green, like Robin Hood and his merry men. They invited the king and queen to breakfast in the forest. They ate in little huts made of green branches and flowers, while birds sang.

[pause]

So the music of Tudor England is everywhere. In the court and the church, in the street and on the village green. And the young king loves all of it.

[long-pause]

{img:village-archery-butts-sunday-men-longbows-targets-green-churchyard}
In Tudor England, every man must be an archer.

[pause]

The law says so. Every Englishman between seventeen and sixty must own a longbow and arrows. He must practise on Sundays and holy days, after church. Every village has a place for this. There are mounds of earth with targets on them, on the green or in a field. Fathers must buy bows for their sons from the age of seven. And they must teach them to shoot.

[long-pause]

The longbow is the great English weapon. It won famous battles against France, about a hundred years before. It is made of one piece of yew wood, as tall as a man. It takes great strength to pull. Years later, the bones of English archers were found in an old shipwreck. Their left arms and shoulders were bigger than their right. That came from years and years of practice.

[pause]

A good archer can shoot ten or twelve arrows a minute. And he can hit a target two hundred metres away.

[pause]

{img:longbow-yew-arrows-goose-feathers-quiver-close-tudor-archer}
King Henry is a fine archer himself. He loves to shoot with his friends and his guards, and he makes bets on who will win. He wants everyone in the country to practise. So he forbids other games for ordinary people. Bowls and cards and dice are not allowed. But the people play them anyway.

[long-pause]

On summer Sundays, after church, the men and boys gather at the targets. They shoot in turns. The women and children watch and cheer and bring food and beer. There are contests with prizes. The winner might get a silver arrow, or a new bow, or even a pig.

[pause]

But by 1520, guns are starting to take the place of bows in war. The young men care less about archery than their grandfathers did. That worries the king. Without good archers, how will England defend itself? In a few years, the longbow will disappear from war. But archery will stay a sport in England until today.

[long-pause]

{img:mary-rose-warship-sails-portsmouth-harbour-1520-tudor-flags}
In 1520, one of the finest ships in Henry's navy is the Mary Rose.

[pause]

She was built at Portsmouth about ten years ago. She was one of the first ships of the king's new navy. She is a big warship, about forty metres long, with four masts. She has tall wooden castles at the front and the back. And along her sides there are rows of little doors for heavy guns. That was a new idea. It made her one of the strongest warships in the world.

[long-pause]

She was probably named after the king's favourite sister, Mary, and the Tudor rose. Henry loved her. One of his admirals wrote that she was the finest ship in all of Christian Europe.

[pause]

She served for thirty-four years in Henry's wars. In the summer of 1520 she helped guard the sea while the king crossed to France.

[pause]

About four hundred men lived on board. Sailors and gunners, soldiers and archers. Below deck it was crowded and dark. The men slept among the guns and the barrels of food and beer.

[long-pause]

Twenty-five years after our story, there was a sea battle near Portsmouth. The Mary Rose sank, while the king watched from the shore. We will not describe it. Few of her crew survived.

[pause]

She lay in the mud at the bottom of the sea for more than four hundred years. The mud kept half of her wooden hull safe. And it kept thousands of things inside her safe too. Nobody knew if she would ever be found.

[pause]

{img:mary-rose-museum-preserved-hull-galleries-soft-light-visitors}
Divers searched for years. At last they found her. In 1982 she was lifted out of the sea in a big steel cradle. Millions of people watched on television. Would the old wood hold? It did. Today she is in a museum in Portsmouth. You can see her great wooden hull and her guns. And you can see more than nineteen thousand things found inside her.

[long-pause]

Those things tell us a lot about ordinary Tudor people. About the sailors and the archers, the cook and the carpenter.

[pause]

There are wooden bowls and spoons with the owners' letters carved on them. There are combs and shoes and leather jackets. There are dice and board games and little pocket sundials. There are whistles and a fiddle and pipes and a drum. There are the cook's big pots, and the bones of the meat and fish the men ate. There is the ship's doctor's chest with his medicines and razors. There are the carpenter's tools. And there are longbows and thousands of arrows.

[pause]

{img:mary-rose-objects-wooden-bowls-combs-shoes-whistle-dice-museum}
And there are the bones of the ship's dog. He was a little terrier who lived on board and caught rats. The archaeologists named him Hatch, because they found him near a hatch, a small door in the deck. His skeleton is in the museum too.

[long-pause]

{img:tudor-palace-autumn-evening-quiet-courtyard-fallen-leaves-later}
The golden summer of 1520 did not last.

[pause]

In the years that followed, Henry wanted a son more and more. Catherine was getting older and could have no more children. And a few years later, Henry fell in love with one of the queen's ladies. Her name was Anne Boleyn. She was clever and witty, with dark eyes. She had grown up at the French court. And she did not want to be just his sweetheart. She wanted to be his wife.

[long-pause]

Henry asked the Pope to end his marriage to Catherine. He said it had never been a true marriage, because she had first been his brother's wife. The Pope said no. Partly because Catherine's nephew, the emperor, did not want it.

[pause]

So in the end, Henry broke away from the Pope and the Church of Rome. He made himself the head of the Church in England. And he married Anne. That was the start of a great change in England's faith, one that would last forever.

[pause]

{img:empty-monastery-cloister-ruins-roofless-church-1540s-quiet-later}
We will not follow the whole story tonight. It is enough to say that Henry's later years were troubled and often sad. He married six times in all. Cardinal Wolsey lost the king's favour and passed away soon after. Thomas More would not accept the king as head of the Church. He lost his life for it, calm and brave to the end. He said he was the king's good servant, but God's first. The monasteries were closed. Their lands were sold, and many of their buildings fell into ruin.

[long-pause]

Catherine was sent away from court. She lived quietly in the country, still calling herself queen. She passed away a few years later. Even then, she still loved Henry. In her last letter she wrote, my eyes long for you above all things.

[pause]

Henry grew old and heavy and ill. He had a wound on his leg that never healed. He became angry and suspicious. He passed away in 1547, at fifty-five.

[pause]

{img:three-tudor-children-portraits-edward-mary-elizabeth-gallery-later}
All three of his children became rulers of England, one after the other. First his son Edward, a clever serious boy. He passed away at fifteen. Then Mary, Catherine's daughter, the little girl of 1520. She became Queen Mary. And then Elizabeth, the daughter of Anne Boleyn. She ruled for forty-five years. Her time is called England's golden age, the age of Shakespeare.

[long-pause]

But in the summer of 1520, none of this has happened yet. The king is young, and his queen is beside him. His little daughter is playing in the gardens of Greenwich. The golden tents shine in the valley in France. The wine fountains are flowing. And everything still seems possible.

[long-pause]

{img:hampton-court-today-red-brick-gatehouse-visitors-sunny}
Today you can still visit many places of Tudor England. You can walk where Henry and Catherine and Wolsey and More walked, five hundred years ago.

[pause]

Hampton Court still stands by the Thames. It still has its red brick and its great gates and its twisted chimneys. It has the Great Hall with its beautiful carved wooden roof. And it has the huge Tudor kitchens. On some days, cooks in Tudor clothes still roast meat there on big spits, in front of the giant fireplaces.

[long-pause]

In the palace hangs the big painting of the Field of the Cloth of Gold. It is full of tiny people and golden tents and the wine fountain, and the dragon in the sky. There is also the old tennis court, still used today. It is one of the oldest tennis courts in the world.

[pause]

There are the gardens, with their patterns of low hedges. And there is a famous maze of tall hedges, the oldest in Britain. Families still get happily lost in it on summer afternoons. And there is a great grape vine, more than two hundred and fifty years old, that still grows grapes.

[pause]

In London there is the Tower, with Henry's tall armour in the White Tower. There are the crown jewels and the ravens. And there are the Beefeaters in their red and gold uniforms, as they have worn for five hundred years.

[long-pause]

At Greenwich, Henry's palace is gone. But there are beautiful old buildings by the river. And the park where he hunted still climbs up the hill, with a wonderful view of London.

[pause]

In Portsmouth you can see the Mary Rose in her museum. In Westminster Abbey you can see the graves of Henry's parents, in a chapel with a stone ceiling like lace. And the graves of his daughters Mary and Elizabeth. In the cathedral of Peterborough is the grave of Catherine of Aragon. Every year people leave pomegranates there, her symbol, and flowers. A banner hangs above with her name. Katharine, Queen of England.

[pause]

{img:catherine-of-aragon-grave-peterborough-pomegranates-flowers-banner}
And all over England there are still Tudor houses and inns. They have black wooden beams and white walls, crooked roofs and tall chimneys. People still live in them today.

[long-pause]

So we leave Tudor England tonight, on a summer evening in 1520. The golden tents are shining in the valley in France. The young king is laughing with his friends. And lute music drifts across the water at Greenwich.

[pause]

The apprentice in Cheapside is closing his shutters. The villagers are dancing round the maypole. The cardinal is in his red brick palace. And the little princess is asleep, with all of her future still before her.

[pause]

{img:thames-greenwich-evening-river-calm-old-buildings-golden}
If you enjoyed this evening in Tudor England, you are very welcome to subscribe to the channel, and to come back for another quiet story.

[pause]
