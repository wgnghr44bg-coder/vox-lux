Tonight we are going about five hundred years back, to England, in the spring and summer of the year 1520.

[long-pause]

A young king sits on the throne: tall, golden-haired, athletic, clever, and full of music and laughter. His court glitters with silk and gold, his palaces line the river Thames, and his chief minister is building the most splendid house in England. And in the summer of this year, the king will cross the sea to France, for the grandest meeting of kings that Europe has ever seen.

[pause]

The king is Henry the Eighth. He is twenty-eight years old. And tonight, we will spend a little time in his England, long before the troubles that will come later, in the years when everything still seemed golden.

[pause]

If you like these slow journeys into the past, you are welcome to subscribe to the channel, so that the next one is easy to find.

[long-pause]

England, in the year 1520, is a small country, by modern standards. Perhaps two and a half million people live in the whole of England and Wales: fewer than live in a single large city today.

[pause]

Most of them live in the countryside, in small villages, of timber houses, with thatched roofs, around a church, and a green, and a manor house, surrounded by fields, and meadows, and woods, and commons. They are farmers, and shepherds, and labourers, and craftsmen. They grow wheat, and barley, and rye, and oats, and peas, and beans. And above all, they keep sheep.

[long-pause]

England is the land of sheep. The wool of English sheep is the finest in Europe, and is sold, as wool, and as cloth, to Flanders, and Italy, and all over Europe. It is the great source of England's wealth. Rich wool merchants build great churches, in the wool towns of the Cotswolds, and East Anglia, with tall towers, and big windows, as thanks to God for their wealth. In the House of Lords, the Lord Chancellor sits on a great cushion, stuffed with wool, called the Woolsack, as a reminder of where England's wealth comes from.

[pause]

{img:sheep-grazing-cotswold-hills-wool-church-tower-golden-stone}
There are a few towns, mostly small: market towns, with a weekly market, and a few streets of shops, and inns, and houses. And a few cities: Norwich, and Bristol, and York, and Exeter, with perhaps ten thousand people each. And there is London.

[long-pause]

London, in 1520, has perhaps fifty or sixty thousand people, and is growing fast. It is one of the great cities of northern Europe: a crowded, busy, noisy, dirty, exciting city, along the north bank of the Thames, inside its old Roman walls, with a hundred churches, and a great cathedral, Saint Paul's, with the tallest spire in England, and the Tower of London, the old royal fortress, and London Bridge, covered with houses, and shops, and the river, full of boats.

[pause]

{img:old-st-pauls-cathedral-tall-spire-london-roofs-thames-1520}
And along the river, west of the city, there are the palaces: of the king, and the bishops, and the great lords, with their gardens running down to the water, and their boats, and their landing stages, where the king, and his court, travel up and down the Thames, in great painted barges, rowed by oarsmen, in the king's colours, green and white.

[long-pause]

{img:young-henry-viii-1520-tall-athletic-red-gold-hair-beard-doublet}
In the year 1520, King Henry the Eighth is twenty-eight years old, and has been king for eleven years.

[pause]

He is tall, about one metre eighty-eight, which is very tall for his time, and strong, and broad-shouldered, and handsome, with reddish-gold hair, and a short beard, and small, bright, intelligent eyes. A Venetian ambassador, a few years before, wrote that he was the handsomest prince he had ever seen, and that his skin was very fair, and glowing, like that of a pretty woman.

[long-pause]

He was not supposed to be king. He was the second son of King Henry the Seventh, the first Tudor king, who won the crown at the battle of Bosworth, in 1485, at the end of the long civil wars, called the Wars of the Roses. Henry's elder brother, Arthur, was the heir. But Arthur passed away, in 1502, at the age of fifteen. And so Henry, at eleven, became the heir. And when his father passed away, in 1509, he became king, at seventeen, to the joy of the whole country.

[pause]

The young king was everything his gloomy, careful, old father was not: young, handsome, generous, cheerful, and full of energy.

[pause]

{img:young-king-hunting-horseback-hounds-forest-greenwich-park}
He loves sport: hunting, all day, tiring out eight or ten horses, and jousting, and tennis, and archery, and wrestling, and dancing. He is one of the best jousters in England, and one of the best archers, and can draw a bow as well as any archer in his guard.

[long-pause]

But he is also clever, and very well educated. He speaks French, and Latin, and some Spanish, and Italian. He reads books of theology, and writes them: in 1521, the year after our story, he will write a book, defending the Catholic Church against Martin Luther, and the Pope will give him the title Defender of the Faith, which the kings and queens of England still carry today, and which still appears, as the letters F D, on British coins.

[pause]

He loves music more than anything. He plays the lute, and the organ, and the virginals, a kind of small harpsichord, and the recorder, and the flute, and sings, well. He composes music himself: songs, and pieces for instruments. The most famous is a song called Pastime with Good Company, about the joys of youth, and friendship, and hunting, and singing, and dancing.

[pause]

{img:king-playing-lute-chamber-musicians-courtiers-listening-evening}
He has more than seventy recorders, and flutes, in his collection, and dozens of lutes, and keyboard instruments, and employs the best musicians in Europe, at his court.

[long-pause]

In 1520, Henry is still young, and golden, and popular, and full of hope. He has been married for eleven years, to Catherine of Aragon. He has a daughter, Mary, who is four. He does not yet have a son. And that, in the years to come, will change everything.

[pause]

But not yet.

[long-pause]

{img:greenwich-palace-placentia-thames-riverfront-towers-1520-barges}
Of all his palaces, King Henry's favourite is Greenwich.

[pause]

It stands on the south bank of the Thames, about eight kilometres downstream from London, where the river makes a great loop, with a long front of red brick, and stone, facing the water, with towers, and bay windows, and a landing stage, where the royal barges come and go. Behind it, there is a great park, rising up the hill, with deer, and woods, and a little castle, a tower, on the top of the hill, with a wonderful view, over the river, and London, in the distance.

[long-pause]

Henry was born at Greenwich, in 1491. He married Catherine there, in 1509. His daughter Mary was born there, in 1516, and later, his daughter Elizabeth, in 1533. He loves it. It is close to London, but in the country, with the river, and the park, and the hunting, and the fresh air.

[pause]

At Greenwich, Henry has built a tiltyard, for jousting, with towers, for the spectators, and an armoury, where his own armourers, brought from Germany, make his armour. There are tennis courts, and bowling alleys, and a cockpit, and stables, and kennels, and mews, for the hawks.

[pause]

{img:greenwich-tiltyard-towers-spectators-jousting-sunny-afternoon}
And from the windows of the palace, the king can watch the ships, sailing up and down the Thames, to and from London, and the sea: merchant ships, from all over Europe, and the ships of his own navy, which he loves, and visits, and inspects, at the new royal dockyards, nearby, at Deptford, and Woolwich.

[long-pause]

The palace is gone now. It was pulled down in the seventeenth century, and replaced by the great buildings of the Royal Hospital for Seamen, designed by Christopher Wren, which are now the Old Royal Naval College, with its domes, and colonnades, by the river.

[pause]

But the park is still there, with its old trees, and its deer, and the hill, with its view. And in the park, there is an old oak tree, now dead, and propped up, called Queen Elizabeth's Oak, under which, the story says, Henry and Anne Boleyn danced, and their daughter, Elizabeth, played as a child.

[long-pause]

{img:greenwich-park-hill-view-river-thames-deer-old-trees-evening}
In the evenings, at Greenwich, in summer, the king and queen would sometimes walk up the hill in the park, with their friends, and the little princess, and sit under the great trees, and look down at the palace, and the river, shining in the evening light, with the boats going home to London, and the towers, and the spires, of the city, far away, in the haze, and listen to the deer calling, in the woods, as the sun went down.

[long-pause]

{img:catherine-of-aragon-queen-1520-gable-hood-gown-dignified-kind}
The queen of England, in 1520, is Catherine of Aragon.

[pause]

She is thirty-four years old, six years older than Henry. She is the youngest daughter of the most famous royal couple in Europe: Ferdinand and Isabella, the king and queen of Spain, who, twenty-eight years before, sent Christopher Columbus across the Atlantic, and found the New World.

[long-pause]

She came to England as a girl of fifteen, in 1501, to marry Henry's elder brother, Arthur. But Arthur passed away, five months after the wedding. And Catherine was left in England, a young widow, poor, and lonely, and uncertain, for seven years, while the two kings, her father, and Henry's father, argued about her dowry.

[pause]

Then, in 1509, when Henry became king, at seventeen, one of the first things he did was to marry her. He said that he loved her. They were crowned together, in Westminster Abbey, in a great ceremony, with the people cheering in the streets.

[pause]

{img:young-royal-couple-coronation-westminster-abbey-1509-crowds}
Catherine is small, and pretty, with long red-gold hair, and blue eyes, and fair skin. She is intelligent, and well educated, in Latin, and theology, and music, and dancing, and needlework. She is very religious, and kind, and dignified, and brave. Once, when Henry was away at war, in France, in 1513, she ruled England as regent, and, when the Scots invaded, she organized the army, and even rode north, in armour, it is said, to speak to the troops. And the English won a great victory, at Flodden.

[long-pause]

The English people love her. She gives generously to the poor, and visits the sick, and goes on pilgrimages, on foot.

[pause]

But her life has been full of sorrow. She has had six pregnancies, in eleven years. But most of her babies were lost, before or soon after birth. A son, Henry, was born in 1511, and the whole country celebrated, with bonfires, and bells, and a great tournament. But he lived only fifty-two days.

[pause]

{img:queen-catherine-with-little-princess-mary-garden-greenwich-spring}
Only one child has lived: a daughter, Mary, born in 1516, who is four in 1520: a pretty, clever little girl, with red-gold hair, who plays the virginals, already, and dances, and whom her father adores, and carries around in his arms, and shows off to the ambassadors.

[long-pause]

In 1520, Henry and Catherine are still a loving couple, in public. He wears her colours, at the tournaments, and they dance together, and hunt together, and pray together. But Henry wants a son, an heir, to secure the Tudor dynasty. And Catherine is thirty-four, and the doctors are beginning to doubt that she will have any more children.

[pause]

The Princess Mary will one day be queen herself, as Mary the First. And her half sister, Elizabeth, not yet born, will be the greatest queen in English history. But in 1520, nobody can imagine that.

[long-pause]

{img:cardinal-wolsey-scarlet-robes-hat-large-man-desk-letters-hampton}
The most powerful man in England, after the king, in 1520, is Thomas Wolsey, Cardinal, Archbishop of York, and Lord Chancellor.

[pause]

He is about forty-seven years old: a large, heavy man, with a strong, clever face, always dressed in the scarlet robes, and the scarlet hat, of a cardinal of the Church.

[long-pause]

He was not born a nobleman. His father was a butcher, or a cattle dealer, in the town of Ipswich, in Suffolk. But he was brilliantly clever, and went to Oxford University, at fifteen, and became a priest, and then a chaplain to the old king, Henry the Seventh, and then to the young Henry the Eighth.

[pause]

He worked harder than anyone. He liked to do the boring work of government, which the young king did not want to do, and so the king gave him more and more power, until, by 1520, Wolsey is running almost everything: the government, and the law courts, and the Church, and foreign policy. Foreign ambassadors say that he is the real ruler of England, and that he is seven times more powerful than the Pope.

[pause]

{img:wolsey-procession-london-street-gentlemen-silver-crosses-mule}
He loves splendour. When he goes through London, to Westminster, he rides on a mule, covered with crimson velvet, with gilded stirrups, with two great silver crosses carried before him, and two silver pillars, and a great silver mace, and dozens of gentlemen, and servants, walking in front, and behind, crying: make way, make way for my Lord Cardinal. He holds an orange, with the inside taken out, filled with a sponge, soaked in vinegar, and spices, to his nose, to protect himself from the smells, and the diseases, of the crowd.

[long-pause]

His household is enormous: about five hundred people, including nobles, and knights, and priests, and musicians, and cooks, and servants. His income is enormous too, from all his offices, and lands, and churches.

[pause]

But he also does good things. He sets up new courts, where poor people can bring their cases, and get justice cheaply, against the rich. He tries to stop landowners from enclosing the common fields, to make sheep pastures, and throwing poor farmers off the land. He founds a great college, at Oxford, Cardinal College, which later becomes Christ Church, one of the most beautiful colleges of Oxford University. And a school, in his home town of Ipswich.

[pause]

{img:christ-church-oxford-tom-quad-college-golden-stone-later}
And in 1520, he is busy planning the greatest diplomatic event of the age: a meeting between King Henry of England, and King Francis the First of France, which will be called the Field of the Cloth of Gold.

[pause]

But first, let us visit his great new house, on the river Thames: Hampton Court.

[long-pause]

{img:hampton-court-palace-red-brick-gatehouse-chimneys-1520-thames}
About twenty kilometres up the river Thames, west of London, in the green countryside, Cardinal Wolsey is building the most splendid house in England: Hampton Court.

[pause]

He began it in 1514, on the site of an old manor house, and by 1520, it is a great palace, of warm red brick, with stone trim, around great courtyards, with tall gatehouses, and towers, and turrets, and battlements, and hundreds of tall, twisted, decorated chimneys, rising from the roofs, each one different.

[long-pause]

You come to it along the river, by barge, from London, past the meadows, and the willows, and the villages, and land at the water gate, and walk up, through gardens, to the great gatehouse, the Great Gate, with its towers, and its arch, and its coats of arms, carved in stone, and painted.

[pause]

Inside, there is the Base Court, a great courtyard, of red brick, with the rooms for guests: about forty sets of lodgings, each with its own rooms, and fireplaces, and garderobes, for Wolsey's guests, and their servants. Then, through another gatehouse, there is the Clock Court, and the great rooms of the palace: the hall, and the chapel, and the long galleries, and the cardinal's own private rooms.

[pause]

{img:tudor-long-gallery-tapestries-linenfold-panelling-windows-light}
The rooms are filled with wonderful things. Wolsey has bought more than six hundred tapestries, from Flanders, which hang on the walls, showing stories from the Bible, and from history, and mythology, in rich colours, with gold and silver thread. There are carpets from Turkey, and Persia, which he loves, and has sent for, from Venice: sixty of them, in one order. There are beds, with curtains of silk, and cloth of gold. There are silver and gold plates, and cups, and candlesticks, by the hundred.

[long-pause]

There are gardens, laid out in the new style, with knots, patterns of low hedges, of box, and herbs, filled with flowers, and coloured sand, and paths, and fountains, and arbours, and painted wooden posts, with the coats of arms of the cardinal, and orchards, and a park, for hunting deer.

[pause]

And there is water: fresh, clean water, brought in lead pipes, from springs, five kilometres away, across the river, under the Thames, to the palace, for the kitchens, and the fountains, and even, it is said, for flushing the latrines.

[pause]

{img:tudor-knot-garden-box-hedges-herbs-flowers-fountain-hampton}
Visitors, and ambassadors, from all over Europe, are amazed. And the king himself comes to stay, and admires it, perhaps a little too much.

[long-pause]

A few years later, when Wolsey falls from favour, he will give Hampton Court to the king, as a gift. And Henry will make it even greater, with a new great hall, with a magnificent wooden roof, and huge kitchens, which can feed a court of more than a thousand people, and a real tennis court, where he will play, and which is still used today.

[pause]

And today, Hampton Court is still there, by the river, with its red brick, and its chimneys, and its gardens, one of the most beautiful palaces in England.

[long-pause]

{img:tudor-garden-raised-beds-herbs-flowers-rails-painted-posts-sunny}
The Tudors love gardens.

[pause]

The great houses, and palaces, have pleasure gardens, laid out in the new fashion, from France, and Italy: knot gardens, with patterns of low clipped hedges, of box, or lavender, or rosemary, or hyssop, forming knots, and interlacing shapes, filled with flowers, and herbs, or coloured gravel, and sand, best seen from the windows of the house above. With paths, of gravel, and grass, between them, and fountains, and sundials, and arbours, covered with roses, and honeysuckle, and vines, where people can sit in the shade.

[long-pause]

At Hampton Court, and later at Whitehall, Henry will put up tall wooden posts, painted green and white, the Tudor colours, with carved and gilded heraldic beasts on top: lions, and dragons, and greyhounds, and harts, holding the royal coats of arms. And there are mounts: little artificial hills, with a path spiralling up to a little banqueting house, on the top, from which you can see the gardens, and the river, and the countryside around.

[pause]

The flowers are those that have grown in England for centuries, and some new ones: roses, red and white, the symbols of the Tudor family. Gillyflowers, which we call pinks, which smell of cloves. Violets, and primroses, and cowslips, and lilies, and marigolds, and lavender, and sweet william, and columbine, and hollyhocks.

[pause]

{img:tudor-orchard-apple-pear-cherry-trees-blossom-bees-skeps-spring}
Every house, even the smallest cottage, has a garden: for vegetables, and herbs, and fruit, and bees. Cabbages, and onions, and leeks, and garlic, and peas, and beans, and parsnips, and turnips, and lettuce. Herbs for cooking, and medicine, and for strewing on the floors, to make the house smell sweet: sage, and thyme, and parsley, and mint, and rosemary, and lavender, and rue, and tansy, and fennel. And fruit trees: apples, and pears, and plums, and cherries, and quinces, and medlars.

[long-pause]

Henry loves fruit, and has new kinds planted, from France, and Flanders. His fruiterer, Richard Harris, will plant great orchards, of cherries, and apples, and pears, in Kent, in 1533, the beginning of Kent's fame as the garden of England.

[pause]

And in the summer evenings, the ladies, and gentlemen, of the court, walk in the gardens, and pick flowers, and herbs, and sit in the arbours, and play music, and talk, and smell the roses, and the lavender, as the swallows swoop over the river.

[long-pause]

{img:still-room-woman-distilling-rosewater-herbs-bottles-tudor-house}
In the great houses, the lady of the house has a still room, where she, and her maids, make medicines, and perfumes, and sweet waters, from the flowers, and herbs, of the garden: rose water, and lavender water, and syrups, and cordials, and ointments, and candied flowers, and preserves of fruit, for the winter. Many of their recipes were written down, in household books, which were handed down, from mother to daughter, for generations.

[long-pause]

{img:tudor-court-presence-chamber-throne-canopy-courtiers-silk-velvet}
The king's court, in 1520, is the centre of England: of power, and fashion, and music, and ambition, and gossip.

[pause]

It is not in one place. The king has many palaces: Greenwich, by the river, east of London, where he was born, and which he loves. Richmond, to the west, built by his father. Westminster, beside the abbey, and the parliament. Eltham, Windsor, and others. And the whole court moves from one to another, through the year, with the seasons, and the hunting, and when one palace becomes too dirty, and smelly, and needs to be cleaned.

[long-pause]

The court is huge: perhaps a thousand, or fifteen hundred people, in the king's household, and the queen's household, and the households of the great lords, who come to court with their own servants. There are lords, and ladies, and knights, and gentlemen, and pages, and clerks, and chaplains, and doctors, and musicians, and cooks, and grooms, and huntsmen, and falconers, and guards, and porters, and laundresses, and many more.

[pause]

The king's rooms are arranged in a row, one after another, each more private than the last. First, the Great Hall, where everyone eats, and anyone respectably dressed can come in. Then the Guard Chamber, where the king's guards, the Yeomen of the Guard, in their red and gold uniforms, stand with their halberds. Then the Presence Chamber, where the king sits, on his throne, under a canopy of cloth of gold, and receives ambassadors, and courtiers. Then the Privy Chamber, the king's private rooms, where only his closest friends and servants may go. And beyond that, the bedchamber.

[pause]

{img:yeomen-of-the-guard-red-gold-uniforms-halberds-palace-doorway}
The closer you can get to the king, the more powerful you are. The gentlemen of the Privy Chamber, the king's friends, who help him dress, and keep him company, and play cards with him, and ride with him, are some of the most powerful men in England, because they can talk to him, privately, every day.

[long-pause]

Life at court is a constant round of entertainments: hunting, and hawking, in the day, and banquets, and dancing, and music, and plays, and masques, in the evening.

[pause]

A masque is a kind of entertainment, new from Italy, in which the king, and his friends, dress up, in fantastic costumes, and masks, as foreign princes, or wild men, or knights, or gods, and burst into the hall, unexpectedly, and dance with the ladies, and then take off their masks, and everyone pretends to be surprised that it is the king.

[pause]

{img:masque-court-costumes-masks-dancers-candlelit-hall-surprise}
The court ladies, and gentlemen, play cards, and dice, and chess, and backgammon, for money, sometimes for large sums. They play tennis, which is a new and fashionable game, played indoors, in a special court, with walls, and a sloping roof, and a net. And bowls, on the lawns. And they write poems, and love letters, and sing, and gossip, and flirt, and plot, and try to win the king's favour.

[long-pause]

And every morning, the king goes to Mass, in his chapel, with the choir of the Chapel Royal, the finest choir in England, singing. And every day, he works, a little, with his secretaries, and his council, and reads the letters, and the reports, which Wolsey sends him. And then he goes hunting.

[long-pause]

{img:london-bridge-1520-houses-shops-arches-river-boats-chapel}
Let us walk through London, in the spring of 1520.

[pause]

The city lies along the north bank of the Thames, inside its old walls, about two kilometres long, and one kilometre deep, with seven gates: Ludgate, and Newgate, and Aldersgate, and Cripplegate, and Moorgate, and Bishopsgate, and Aldgate. Outside the walls, the suburbs are spreading, along the roads, and along the river, to the west, toward Westminster.

[long-pause]

The only bridge across the Thames is London Bridge: one of the wonders of Europe. It is built of stone, with nineteen arches, and on top of it, there are houses, and shops, three, four, five storeys high, on both sides of a narrow street, so that you could walk across the bridge without seeing the river at all. In the middle, there is a chapel, of Saint Thomas Becket. And at the southern end, there is a great gatehouse, with a drawbridge.

[pause]

The water rushes so fast between the narrow arches of the bridge, at high and low tide, that it is dangerous to row through. People say that London Bridge was made for wise men to go over, and fools to go under. So many people get out of their boats, before the bridge, and walk around, and get back in, on the other side.

[pause]

{img:watermen-boats-thames-passengers-crying-oars-westward-ho}
The river is the main road of London. There are thousands of boats, and the watermen, who row passengers up and down, and across, the river, calling out, eastward ho, and westward ho, to find customers. There are ships, from all over Europe, at the wharves, below the bridge, unloading wine from France, and spices from Venice, and cloth from Flanders, and timber, and furs, from the Baltic, and loading English wool, and cloth.

[long-pause]

The streets of London are narrow, and crowded, and noisy, and dirty. The houses are of timber, and plaster, with overhanging upper storeys, which jut out over the street, so that the houses on either side almost touch, at the top, and the street below is dark, even at midday.

[pause]

In the middle of the streets, there is a channel, the kennel, where the rain water, and the waste, flows away, toward the river. People throw their rubbish out of the windows. Pigs, and dogs, and chickens, wander in the streets. And the smell, in summer, is terrible.

[pause]

{img:narrow-london-street-overhanging-timber-houses-signs-crowds}
But London is also a rich, and beautiful, and exciting city. There are more than a hundred parish churches, with their towers, and spires, and bells, ringing all day. There are the great halls of the guilds, the companies of the merchants, and craftsmen: the goldsmiths, and the mercers, and the grocers, and the drapers, and the fishmongers. There are fine houses of rich merchants, with gardens. There are the great monasteries, and friaries, with their churches, and their cloisters, and their gardens.

[long-pause]

And above everything, on the hill, at the western end of the city, there is Saint Paul's Cathedral, the old Gothic cathedral, enormous, longer than any cathedral in England, with its great spire, the tallest in England, about a hundred and fifty metres high, which can be seen for many kilometres around.

[pause]

The middle of Saint Paul's, the long nave, is used as a meeting place, and a market, and a short cut, called Paul's Walk, where people come to walk up and down, and gossip, and do business, and hire servants, and lawyers, and hear the news, all day long. And in the churchyard, outside, there are the booksellers, with their shops, selling the new printed books.

[long-pause]

{img:london-apprentice-shop-opening-shutters-dawn-street-cheapside}
Let us spend an ordinary day in London, in 1520, with an apprentice, a boy of sixteen, learning to be a goldsmith, in Cheapside, the great market street of London, where the goldsmiths have their shops.

[pause]

He lives in his master's house, as all apprentices do, above the shop, and sleeps in the attic, or under the counter, in the shop, with the other apprentices. He will serve his master for seven years, without pay, only food, and clothes, and a bed, and in return, his master will teach him the craft.

[long-pause]

He gets up at dawn, at about five o'clock in summer, when the church bells ring. He takes down the wooden shutters, from the front of the shop, and the lower shutter becomes a counter, on which the goods are displayed: silver cups, and spoons, and rings, and chains, and brooches.

[pause]

Breakfast is bread, and ale, or a little cheese. And then the work begins. The master, and the journeymen, work at the back of the shop, at their benches, by the window, for the light, hammering, and engraving, and polishing silver, and gold. The apprentice helps, and learns, and sweeps the floor, and runs errands, and stands at the front of the shop, calling out to the people passing: what do you lack? What do you lack?

[pause]

{img:goldsmith-workshop-bench-silver-cup-engraving-window-light}
Cheapside is the widest and grandest street in London, lined with the shops of the goldsmiths, and the mercers, who sell silk, and velvet, and the haberdashers, and the grocers, who sell spices, and sugar, and dried fruit. In the middle of the street, there is the Eleanor Cross, a tall stone monument, and the Great Conduit, a fountain, where people fetch water, in great wooden tankards, carried by water carriers.

[long-pause]

At about eleven o'clock, the household eats dinner, the main meal of the day, at the master's table: the master, and his wife, and their children, and the journeymen, and the apprentices, and the servants, all together. Pottage, a thick soup, of vegetables, and grain, and bread, and meat, beef, or mutton, or on Fridays, and in Lent, fish. And ale.

[pause]

In the afternoon, the work goes on, until the evening. Then the shop is closed, at about seven, or eight, and the shutters put up.

[pause]

{img:apprentices-playing-football-fields-outside-city-walls-evening}
In the evening, after supper, the apprentices are free, for a little while. They go out, into the streets, and the fields, outside the city walls, at Moorfields, and Finsbury, and play football, a wild, rough game, with a pig's bladder, or practise archery, at the butts, as every Englishman must, by law. Or wrestle. Or watch the bear baiting, across the river, in Southwark. Or go to the taverns, although they are not supposed to.

[long-pause]

The apprentices of London are famous for being rowdy. When there is trouble in the city, they gather, in hundreds, shouting clubs, clubs, and fight. Three years before, in 1517, on the first of May, there was a great riot, called Evil May Day, when the apprentices attacked the houses of foreign merchants. And afterwards, the queen, Catherine, herself, knelt before the king, and begged for mercy for them, and many were pardoned.

[pause]

And at nine o'clock, the curfew bell rings, from the church of Saint Mary-le-Bow, in Cheapside, and the apprentices must be home, and the city gates are shut, and the watchmen walk the dark streets, with their lanterns, and their bells, calling out the hours.

[long-pause]

{img:tudor-doctor-physician-gown-urine-flask-herbs-apothecary-shop}
Life in Tudor England is often short, and illness is always close.

[pause]

There are no antibiotics, no vaccines, and doctors know very little about the causes of disease. They believe, as the ancient Greeks did, that the body is made of four humours, blood, and phlegm, and yellow bile, and black bile, and that illness comes when they are out of balance. They treat illness by bleeding, and purging, and with medicines made of herbs, and minerals, and strange ingredients.

[long-pause]

The most feared illness of Henry's England is the sweating sickness: a strange disease, which appeared in England in 1485, the year Henry's father became king, and came back, in great outbreaks, several times, in 1508, and 1517, and 1528, and 1551, and then vanished, and has never been seen again. Nobody knows what it was.

[pause]

It struck suddenly, often in summer, and killed very quickly: people said that you could be merry at dinner, and dead at supper. It seemed to strike the rich, and the young, and the strong, more than the poor. Henry was terrified of it. Whenever it appeared, he fled from London, and moved from house to house, in the country, to escape it, with only a few servants, and took his own medicines, and prayed.

[pause]

{img:herb-garden-tudor-apothecary-gathering-lavender-sage-rosemary}
Most ordinary people, when they are ill, do not see a doctor, because doctors are expensive. They use remedies from the herb garden, made by the women of the family, who know a great deal about plants: sage, for sore throats, and lavender, for headaches, and feverfew, for fevers, and rosemary, for memory, and comfrey, for broken bones, and honey, for wounds.

[long-pause]

Henry himself is very interested in medicine. He writes his own recipes for medicines, and ointments, for his friends, and his servants, and for his own sore leg. Some of them still survive, in a book, in the British Library: plasters, and ointments, made of herbs, and oils, and wax, and pearls, and other ingredients.

[pause]

And in 1518, two years before our story, Henry founded the Royal College of Physicians, in London, to make sure that doctors were properly trained, and qualified, which still exists today.

[long-pause]

{img:barber-surgeon-shop-striped-pole-basin-tools-london-street}
In the towns, there are also barber-surgeons, who cut hair, and shave beards, and pull teeth, and set broken bones, and let blood, and treat wounds, in their shops, with a striped pole outside, red and white, for the blood and the bandages. In 1540, Henry will join the barbers and the surgeons together, in a single company, the Company of Barber-Surgeons, in London, and a famous painting, by Holbein, shows him giving them their charter.

[long-pause]

{img:thomas-more-house-chelsea-family-gathered-garden-thames-1520}
One of the most remarkable men in England, in 1520, is Thomas More.

[pause]

He is forty-two years old: a lawyer, and a judge, and a scholar, and a writer, and a member of the king's council. He is a friend of the king, who likes to visit him, at his house, and walk with him, in his garden, with his arm around his neck, and talk about astronomy, and theology, and poetry.

[long-pause]

More is famous all over Europe for a little book, which he wrote, in Latin, four years before, in 1516, called Utopia. It describes an imaginary island, in the New World, called Utopia, which means no place, where everyone lives in peace, and equality, and nobody owns anything privately, and everyone works six hours a day, and spends the rest of the time learning, and the people choose their own rulers, and practise many different religions, and tolerate each other, and gold is so little valued that they use it to make chamber pots, and chains for prisoners.

[pause]

It is a clever, funny, serious book, which makes people think about the problems of their own country: the poverty, and the injustice, and the greed of the rich, and the enclosure of the common land, for sheep. In Utopia, More writes, sheep, which used to be so meek, and tame, have become so greedy that they eat up men.

[pause]

{img:scholar-writing-latin-book-study-books-candle-window-tudor}
The word utopia has been used ever since, for a perfect imaginary society.

[long-pause]

More is a warm, witty, kind, and very religious man. He is a close friend of the great Dutch scholar, Erasmus of Rotterdam, the most famous writer in Europe, who stays with him, in London, and describes him in a letter: his complexion is fair, his hair is dark brown, his eyes greyish blue, his expression cheerful, and friendly. He is always smiling, and joking.

[pause]

More loves his family, and his home. A few years later, he will build a new house, at Chelsea, a village by the Thames, west of London, with a large garden, running down to the river, with an orchard, and a gallery, and a chapel, and a library. He will live there with his second wife, Alice, and his children, and his son-in-law, and his grandchildren, and his servants, and his pets: a monkey, and dogs, and rabbits, and birds, and a fox, and a weasel.

[pause]

{img:holbein-style-family-portrait-room-tudor-household-reading-calm}
He believes that girls should be as well educated as boys, which is unusual in his time, and he teaches his daughters Latin, and Greek, and philosophy, and astronomy. His eldest daughter, Margaret, becomes one of the most learned women in England, and corresponds with Erasmus, in Latin.

[long-pause]

A few years later, the German painter Hans Holbein will come to England, and stay with More, and paint his portrait, and a great picture of the whole More family, together, at Chelsea: the first large family group portrait in English art. The painting is lost, but Holbein's drawing for it survives, and shows them all, reading, and talking, and the little dog, and the monkey, climbing up the dress of Lady Alice.

[pause]

More's story, later, will be a sad one, and we will come back to it. But in 1520, he is happy, and respected, and the king's friend.

[long-pause]

{img:erasmus-scholar-fur-coat-writing-desk-books-cambridge-room}
One of the most famous visitors to Tudor England is the Dutch scholar Erasmus of Rotterdam.

[pause]

Erasmus is the most famous writer in Europe, in 1520. He was born in Rotterdam, around 1466, the son of a priest, and became a monk, and then a scholar, and travelled all over Europe, writing books, in Latin, and making friends with the scholars, and the princes, of every country. He is witty, and wise, and gentle, and sharp, and believes that people should study the Bible, and the great writers of ancient Greece and Rome, and live simple, good lives, and that the Church should be reformed, peacefully, with learning, and kindness.

[long-pause]

He has visited England several times, and loves it. He is a close friend of Thomas More, and stayed in his house, in London, where he wrote his most famous book, In Praise of Folly, a funny, clever satire, about the follies of the world, the Church, and the scholars, and the princes. He dedicated it to More, with a joke: the Latin word for folly, moria, sounds like More's name.

[pause]

He taught Greek at Cambridge University, from 1511 to 1514, at Queens' College, where there is still a tower, called Erasmus's Tower. He complained, in his letters, about the cold, and the bad beer, and the bad wine, and the plague, and the expense. But he also wrote that the English girls were very pretty, and that they had a charming custom, of kissing visitors, when they arrived, and when they left.

[pause]

{img:queens-college-cambridge-old-court-tower-river-cam-spring}
In 1516, he published the first printed edition of the New Testament in Greek, with a new Latin translation, which scholars all over Europe used, and which helped William Tyndale, and Martin Luther, to translate the Bible into English, and German.

[long-pause]

Erasmus wanted peace: between the kings of Europe, and within the Church. He was delighted by the Field of the Cloth of Gold, and the hope of peace between England and France. But in the following years, Europe will be torn apart, by the quarrels of the Reformation, and wars. And Erasmus, who wanted everyone to be reasonable, will be attacked by both sides.

[pause]

He passed away, in 1536, in Basel, in Switzerland. And today, the great European student exchange programme, which lets millions of young people study in other countries, is named after him: Erasmus.

[long-pause]

{img:erasmus-letter-writing-candle-many-letters-friends-europe-desk}
Erasmus wrote thousands of letters, to his friends, all over Europe, in Latin, witty, and warm, and full of news, and gossip, and ideas, and many of them still survive. In one of them, he describes the house of Thomas More, in London, as a school, and a university, of Christian life, where everyone, the children, and the servants, studied, and read, and nobody was ever idle, or quarrelled, and everyone was cheerful.

[long-pause]

{img:tudor-village-thatched-cottages-green-church-maypole-spring}
Most English people, in 1520, live not in London, but in villages, in the countryside.

[pause]

Let us visit a village, in the south of England, in the spring of 1520. It lies in a valley, beside a little river, with a stone church, with a square tower, and a manor house, and about thirty houses, along a lane, and around a green, where the geese graze, and the children play, and the maypole stands, in May.

[long-pause]

The houses are built of timber, with walls of wattle and daub, woven sticks, covered with clay, and straw, and dung, and painted with whitewash, and with roofs of thatch, of straw, or reeds. Most have one or two rooms, on the ground floor, with a hearth, and perhaps a loft, above, for sleeping, and storage. Some, of the richer farmers, the yeomen, have more rooms, and an upper floor, and a chimney, and glass in the windows, which are new, and fashionable.

[pause]

Around the village, there are the great open fields, divided into long strips, and the meadows, by the river, for hay, and the common, where everyone can graze their animals, and the woods, for firewood, and pigs.

[pause]

{img:ploughing-oxen-open-field-strips-tudor-farmers-spring-sky}
The villagers work hard, all year round, with the seasons: ploughing, and sowing, in the spring, and the autumn. Haymaking, in June. The harvest, in August and September, when everyone works in the fields, from dawn to dusk, men, and women, and children, cutting the corn with sickles, and binding it into sheaves, and carting it to the barns. Threshing, in the winter, in the barns, with flails.

[long-pause]

There are the sheep, which must be watched, and moved, and washed, in the river, and sheared, in early summer. And the cows, which must be milked, twice a day. And the pigs, and the hens, and the geese, and the bees.

[pause]

And there are the craftsmen of the village: the blacksmith, at his forge, and the carpenter, and the miller, at the mill, by the river, and the thatcher, and the weaver, and the alewife, who brews ale, and sells it, from her house, with a bush, a bunch of leaves, on a pole, outside the door, as a sign.

[pause]

{img:village-alewife-house-bush-sign-door-villagers-ale-evening}
And there is the parson, the priest of the parish, who lives in the parsonage, beside the church, and says Mass, every Sunday, and on the holy days, and baptizes the babies, and marries the young people, and buries the dead, and visits the sick, and often teaches a few of the village children to read.

[long-pause]

For many villagers, in the 1520s, life is becoming harder. Because some landowners are enclosing the common fields, and the commons, with hedges, and fences, and turning them into great pastures, for sheep, which are more profitable than corn, and need fewer workers. And the farmers, and labourers, who lived on the land, are thrown off, and must find work elsewhere, or become beggars, on the roads.

[pause]

This is what Thomas More meant, when he wrote that the sheep were eating men.

[long-pause]

{img:tudor-children-playing-hoop-spinning-top-village-lane-summer}
What is life like for children in Tudor England, in 1520?

[pause]

Many babies, and small children, do not survive, because of illness. So parents love, and treasure, those who do, and worry about them, and pray for them. Babies are wrapped tightly, in swaddling bands, and sleep in wooden cradles, beside the fire, and are fed by their mothers, or, in rich families, by a wet nurse.

[long-pause]

Children play with toys: wooden dolls, and hobby horses, sticks with a horse's head, which they ride around the house, and spinning tops, and whips, and hoops, and balls, and marbles, of clay, and little pewter toys, figures of knights, and horses, and tiny plates, and jugs, for dolls' houses, some of which have been found, in the mud, by the Thames, in London. And they play games: blind man's buff, and hide and seek, and leapfrog, and hopscotch, and prisoners' base, and football.

[pause]

From a young age, children work. In the country, they help in the fields, and with the animals: scaring birds from the newly sown fields, and minding the geese, and the pigs, and gathering wood, and fetching water, and helping at harvest. Girls help their mothers, with the spinning, and the cooking, and the dairy, and the little children.

[pause]

{img:tudor-grammar-school-boys-hornbook-schoolmaster-bench-latin}
Some boys go to school: to the little school, kept by the parish priest, or a schoolmaster, where they learn to read, from a hornbook, a little wooden board, with a handle, with a sheet of paper, with the alphabet, and the Lord's Prayer, covered with a thin, transparent sheet of horn, to protect it. And then, perhaps, to a grammar school, in the town, where they learn Latin, from dawn to dusk, six days a week, with a stern schoolmaster, and a birch rod.

[long-pause]

At about fourteen, many boys leave home, to become apprentices, in the towns, for seven years, or servants, in the houses of richer families. Girls, too, often go into service, in other households, from about twelve or fourteen, and learn to run a house, and save money for their marriage.

[pause]

The children of the nobility are educated by tutors, at home, in Latin, and French, and music, and dancing, and riding, and good manners. And they are often sent to live in the household of another great lord, or at court, as pages, and maids of honour, to learn the ways of the world.

[pause]

{img:princess-mary-child-virginals-lesson-tutor-palace-room-light}
The little Princess Mary, in 1520, aged four, already plays the virginals, and is learning Latin, and French, and will be one of the best-educated princesses in Europe. Her mother, Catherine, asked the Spanish scholar Juan Luis Vives to write a book about how she should be educated, which says that girls should study, and read, as well as boys.

[long-pause]

{img:tudor-banquet-great-hall-roast-meats-pies-subtlety-sugar-sculpture}
What do the English eat, in 1520?

[pause]

It depends very much on who you are. The rich eat enormous quantities of meat. The poor eat mostly bread, and pottage, and cheese, and a little bacon.

[long-pause]

At the king's court, there are two great meals a day: dinner, at about ten or eleven o'clock in the morning, and supper, at about four or five o'clock in the afternoon. Hundreds of people eat at court, every day, in the great hall, and in the chambers, and the king pays for it all.

[pause]

A great feast has two or three courses, and each course has many dishes, all put on the table at once, so that people can choose what they like. There is beef, and mutton, and pork, and veal, and venison, from the king's parks, roasted on spits, or boiled, or baked in pies. There are birds of every kind: chickens, and capons, and geese, and ducks, and pheasants, and partridges, and quails, and larks, and herons, and swans, and peacocks, roasted, and served again in their feathers, for show. There are rabbits, and hares. There are pies, of every kind, with pastry crusts, decorated, and gilded.

[pause]

{img:tudor-kitchen-huge-fireplace-spits-cooks-boys-hampton-court}
On Fridays, and in Lent, and on the many fast days, there is no meat, only fish: salmon, and trout, and pike, and carp, from the ponds, and eels, and herrings, and cod, and oysters, and crabs, and lobsters, and porpoise, and seal, which the Church counted as fish.

[long-pause]

The rich love sugar, and spices: pepper, and ginger, and cinnamon, and cloves, and nutmeg, and saffron, brought from Asia, by the Portuguese, and Venetian, merchants, which are very expensive. They put sugar in almost everything, even meat, and fish.

[pause]

And at the end of a great feast, there is the banquet: not a meal, but a course of sweet things, eaten in a special room, or a little banqueting house, in the garden: sugar plate, and marzipan, and gingerbread, and candied fruits, and jellies, and sweet wafers, and spiced wine, called hippocras. And subtleties: wonderful sculptures, made of sugar, and marzipan, in the shape of castles, and ships, and animals, and coats of arms, painted, and gilded, to amaze the guests.

[pause]

{img:marzipan-sugar-subtlety-castle-ship-banquet-table-candles-gold}
The rich drink wine, from France, and Spain, and the Rhine. Everyone else drinks ale, made from barley malt, water, and yeast, without hops, which is thick, and sweet, and does not keep long. Beer, made with hops, is just beginning to come into England, from Flanders, and the old-fashioned English do not like it.

[long-pause]

The poor eat dark bread, of rye, or barley, or mixed grains, and pottage, a thick stew, of peas, or beans, or oats, with onions, and leeks, and cabbage, and herbs, from the garden, cooked in a pot, over the fire, all day. And cheese, and butter, and eggs, and a little bacon, from the family pig, and milk, and ale. Fresh meat is rare, a treat, for festivals.

[pause]

Potatoes, and tomatoes, and turkeys, and tea, and coffee, and chocolate, are not yet known in England. They will come, from the New World, and the East, in the following decades, and change everything.

[long-pause]

{img:tudor-christmas-great-hall-yule-log-holly-ivy-feast-candles}
The greatest feast of the Tudor year is Christmas: not one day, but twelve, from Christmas Day, to Twelfth Night, on the sixth of January.

[pause]

At court, Christmas is a time of feasting, and music, and masques, and plays, and dancing, and games, every day, and every night. The king, and the queen, and the whole court, spend it at Greenwich, or Richmond, or Eltham, and the halls are decorated with holly, and ivy, and bay, and rosemary, and a great Yule log burns in the hearth.

[long-pause]

A Lord of Misrule is chosen, a courtier, or a servant, who is in charge of the entertainments, and the games, and the jokes, for the twelve days, and everyone must obey his silly commands, even the king. There are plays, and pageants, and disguisings, and mummers. On Twelfth Night, there is a great cake, with a bean inside, and whoever finds the bean in his slice becomes the King of the Bean, for the night.

[pause]

The food is splendid: roast beef, and venison, and boar's head, carried in on a great dish, with an apple in its mouth, while everyone sings the Boar's Head Carol. And roast goose, and swan, and peacock. And great Christmas pies, filled with every kind of meat, and dried fruits, and spices, the ancestors of the mince pies that the English still eat at Christmas. And plum pottage, a thick, spicy porridge, of meat, and dried fruit, which, over the centuries, will turn into the Christmas pudding.

[pause]

{img:boars-head-carried-feast-trumpets-singers-tudor-hall-christmas}
On New Year's Day, the king, and the courtiers, give each other gifts. The king gives gold, and silver cups, and plates, to his courtiers, and they give him gifts, of jewels, and books, and clothes, and horses, and hawks, and sweets. Every gift is written down, on a great roll of parchment, the New Year's gift roll, some of which still survive.

[long-pause]

In the villages, too, Christmas is the great feast of the year. The farm work stops, for twelve days. The lord of the manor often invites his tenants, and the villagers, to a great Christmas dinner, in his hall. The young people go from house to house, singing carols, and wassailing: carrying a great bowl of hot, spiced ale, with roasted apples, floating in it, and wishing everyone good health, and good luck for the new year, and being given food, and drink, and coins, in return.

[pause]

{img:wassailers-singing-snowy-village-door-bowl-lantern-night}
And on Plough Monday, after Twelfth Night, the ploughmen drag a plough through the village, decorated with ribbons, and ask for money, and then, the next day, the work of the farming year begins again.

[long-pause]

{img:tudor-gentleman-doublet-slashed-sleeves-codpiece-flat-cap-gown}
The English of 1520 love fine clothes. And the court of Henry the Eighth is one of the most splendid, and colourful, in Europe.

[pause]

A gentleman wears a shirt, of fine white linen, with a little frill at the neck, and the wrists, often embroidered, in black silk. Over it, a doublet: a close-fitting jacket, padded, of silk, or velvet, or satin, with sleeves, often slashed, with little cuts, so that the shirt, or a lining of a different colour, can be pulled through, in little puffs. Then hose, tight stockings, of wool, or silk, covering the legs. And over everything, a gown: a long, loose coat, with wide sleeves, and a broad collar, of fur.

[long-pause]

On his head, a flat cap, of velvet, with a feather, and perhaps a jewel. On his feet, wide, square-toed shoes, of leather, or velvet, slashed, like the doublet. And jewels everywhere: rings on every finger, gold chains around the neck, jewelled buttons, and brooches.

[pause]

The king himself dresses more splendidly than anyone. He wears cloth of gold, and cloth of silver, purple velvet, and ermine, and rubies, and diamonds, and pearls, sewn all over his clothes. A Venetian ambassador described him, at a great feast, wearing a doublet of white satin, with a gown of purple velvet, lined with ermine, and a collar of gold, with rubies, and diamonds, and pearls, so that he shone like the sun.

[pause]

{img:tudor-lady-gable-hood-square-neckline-gown-sleeves-velvet-jewels}
A lady wears a long gown, with a square neckline, and long, wide sleeves, turned back, to show a rich lining, of fur, or silk, and a skirt, falling to the ground, over a kirtle, an underdress. On her head, she wears a gable hood, shaped like the roof of a house, with long lappets, hanging down at the sides, and a black veil, behind, which covers all her hair. Or, later, the French hood, a smaller, rounded hood, which shows a little of the hair, at the front.

[long-pause]

There are laws, called sumptuary laws, about who may wear what. Only the king, and his family, may wear purple silk, and cloth of gold. Only dukes, and marquesses, and earls, may wear cloth of gold, of a certain kind. Only knights, and above, may wear velvet, in their gowns. Ordinary people may not wear silk, or fur, except the cheaper furs, like rabbit, and lamb. The laws are meant to keep everyone in their place, so that you can tell, by looking at someone, what rank they are.

[pause]

Ordinary people wear clothes of wool, and linen, in plain colours: russet, a reddish brown, and grey, and blue, and green, and white, made at home, or by the village tailor. A countryman wears a shirt, and a loose tunic, belted, and hose, and a woollen cap, and leather shoes, or boots. A countrywoman wears a long gown, of wool, with an apron, and a linen coif, a close cap, on her head, and a shawl.

[pause]

{img:tudor-countrywoman-coif-apron-wool-gown-basket-eggs-lane}
And everyone, rich and poor, owns very few clothes, because cloth is expensive, and clothes are made by hand. They are mended, and remade, and handed down, and left in wills, to children, and servants. A fine gown might be worn for many years, and then cut down, and made into a smaller gown, for a daughter, and then into a cushion cover.

[long-pause]

{img:bristol-harbour-1497-small-ship-matthew-cabot-departure-dawn}
In 1520, the world is getting bigger.

[pause]

Twenty-eight years before, in 1492, Christopher Columbus, sailing for Spain, crossed the Atlantic, and found the islands of the Caribbean, which he thought were near Asia. And five years later, in 1497, an Italian sailor, called John Cabot, sailing from Bristol, in a little ship called the Matthew, with eighteen men, sailing for King Henry the Seventh, crossed the Atlantic, further north, and landed, probably, on the coast of Newfoundland, or Nova Scotia, and claimed it for England.

[long-pause]

Cabot reported that the sea there was so full of fish, cod, that they could be caught simply by lowering a basket into the water. Soon, fishermen from England, and France, and Portugal, and the Basque country, were sailing across the Atlantic, every summer, to fish on the Grand Banks, off Newfoundland, and bring home salted cod.

[pause]

In 1519, the year before our story, a Portuguese captain, Ferdinand Magellan, sailing for Spain, set out with five ships to sail around the world. In 1520, he is crossing the Pacific Ocean, for the first time. Only one of his ships will come home, three years later, the first to sail around the world.

[pause]

{img:world-map-1520-new-world-outline-sea-monsters-ships-parchment}
And in 1519 and 1520, a Spanish adventurer, Hernán Cortés, has reached the great Aztec city of Tenochtitlan, in Mexico, and the news of its wealth, and its gold, is reaching Europe. In 1520, the German artist Albrecht Dürer sees the Aztec treasures, sent by Cortés, in Brussels, and writes that he has never seen anything so beautiful.

[long-pause]

The news of these discoveries reaches England slowly, through merchants, and ambassadors, and printed books, and maps. Thomas More, in his Utopia, imagines an island, in the New World, discovered by a sailor, who sailed with the Italian explorer Amerigo Vespucci, after whom America was named.

[pause]

Strange new things begin to arrive in England, from the New World, and the East, in these years: tobacco, and turkeys, and later potatoes, and tomatoes, and chocolate. Parrots, and monkeys. And stories, of new lands, and peoples, across the ocean.

[pause]

{img:merchant-showing-parrot-curiosities-new-world-tudor-house-wonder}
In 1520, England is still a small country, on the edge of Europe, with a small navy. But in the following decades, under Henry's daughter, Elizabeth, English sailors, Drake, and Raleigh, and many others, will cross the oceans, and the first English colonies will be founded in America, and England will begin to look out, across the sea, to the whole world.

[long-pause]

{img:english-parish-church-1520-painted-walls-rood-screen-candles-mass}
In 1520, England is a Catholic country, as it has been for nearly a thousand years, and part of the great Catholic Church of western Europe, under the Pope in Rome.

[pause]

No one in England, in 1520, can imagine that, within twenty years, everything will change: that the monasteries will be closed, and the Pope's authority rejected, and the churches stripped of their images. But that is still in the future.

[long-pause]

In 1520, the parish churches of England are full of colour.

[pause]

The walls are painted, with great pictures: of the saints, and of Christ, and of the Last Judgment, over the chancel arch, showing heaven, and hell, so that the people, who mostly cannot read, can learn the stories of the faith, by looking at the pictures. The windows are filled with stained glass, glowing, red, and blue, and gold. There are statues of the saints, painted, and gilded, with candles burning in front of them, and offerings, and flowers.

[pause]

{img:rood-screen-carved-painted-saints-candles-parish-church-dim-light}
Across the church, between the nave, where the people stand, and the chancel, where the priest says Mass, there is a great carved wooden screen, the rood screen, painted, and gilded, with pictures of saints, and on top, a great carved crucifix, the rood, with figures of Mary, and Saint John, on either side.

[pause]

The Mass is said in Latin, which most people do not understand. But they know the moments: when the bell rings, and the priest raises the bread, the Host, above his head, for everyone to see, they kneel, and pray.

[long-pause]

The year is full of holy days, and festivals, and processions: Christmas, and Candlemas, and Easter, and Whitsun, and Corpus Christi, in early summer, when the whole town walks in procession, through the streets, behind the Host, carried under a canopy, with banners, and candles, and the guilds, in their best clothes. And the craftsmen's guilds perform plays, on wagons, in the streets: the mystery plays, telling the whole story of the Bible, from the Creation, to the Last Judgment, in a single day, from dawn to dusk, in York, and Chester, and Coventry, and other towns.

[pause]

{img:mystery-play-wagon-street-york-guild-actors-crowd-summer}
There are the monasteries, and nunneries, and friaries, about eight hundred of them, all over England, with their great churches, and their monks, and nuns, and friars, and their lands, and their schools, and their hospitals, and their almshouses, where the poor, and the sick, and travellers, are fed, and cared for.

[pause]

And there are the pilgrimages: to the shrine of Saint Thomas Becket, at Canterbury, the greatest shrine in England, covered with gold, and jewels. To Our Lady of Walsingham, in Norfolk. To the holy wells, and the shrines of local saints. King Henry himself has walked to Walsingham, barefoot, for the last mile, to pray for a son.

[long-pause]

But in Germany, three years before, in 1517, a monk called Martin Luther has begun to question the Church, and its teachings, and its power. His books are being printed, and copied, and some of them are already being read, secretly, in England, by scholars, at Cambridge, who meet, in an inn called the White Horse, to talk about them.

[pause]

In 1521, Henry will write his book against Luther, and burn Luther's books, in London, at Saint Paul's. He does not know that, a few years later, he himself will break with Rome.

[long-pause]

{img:printing-press-london-1520-workshop-compositors-type-paper-sheets}
In 1520, books are changing the world.

[pause]

About seventy years before, around 1450, a German goldsmith, Johannes Gutenberg, in the city of Mainz, invented a new way of making books: printing, with movable type, little metal letters, which could be arranged, to make words, and lines, and pages, and inked, and pressed onto paper, in a great wooden press, like a wine press, so that hundreds, or thousands, of copies of a book could be made, quickly, and cheaply, instead of being copied by hand, one by one.

[long-pause]

The first printer in England was William Caxton, a merchant, who learned printing in Flanders, and set up a press, at Westminster, in 1476, beside the abbey, under the sign of the red pale. He printed about a hundred books, many of them in English: The Canterbury Tales, by Geoffrey Chaucer, and the stories of King Arthur, by Thomas Malory, called Le Morte d'Arthur.

[pause]

After him, other printers set up presses in London: Wynkyn de Worde, Caxton's assistant, who moved the press to Fleet Street, which became the street of printers, and later of newspapers, for five hundred years. And Richard Pynson, the king's printer.

[pause]

{img:st-pauls-churchyard-bookstalls-books-readers-browsing-1520}
By 1520, there are booksellers' shops all around Saint Paul's churchyard, in London, selling printed books: books of prayers, and saints' lives, and sermons, and Latin grammar, for schoolboys, and almanacs, with the dates of the festivals, and the phases of the moon, and advice about farming, and medicine. And stories, and romances, and ballads, and jests.

[long-pause]

More and more people can read. Perhaps one man in ten, in the country, and many more in London, and some women. Schools are being founded, in the towns, by merchants, and bishops: grammar schools, where boys learn Latin, and English. Saint Paul's School, in London, was founded in 1509, by John Colet, the Dean of Saint Paul's, a friend of Erasmus, and More.

[pause]

And a few years later, in 1526, a young scholar, William Tyndale, will print the first New Testament in English, translated from the Greek, so that, as he said, a boy that drives the plough could know the Scriptures. It will be forbidden, and smuggled into England, in bales of cloth, and read in secret. But its words, and phrases, will live on, in the English language, for five hundred years: let there be light. The powers that be. The salt of the earth. Fight the good fight.

[long-pause]

{img:candlelit-room-family-reading-printed-book-aloud-tudor-evening}
In the evenings, in the houses of merchants, and yeomen, and gentlemen, all over England, families gather by the fire, and someone reads aloud, from a printed book: a book of prayers, or a story, or a ballad, or the news, while the others listen, and sew, and mend, and the children fall asleep.

[long-pause]

{img:dover-harbour-1520-royal-fleet-ships-flags-white-cliffs-castle}
At the end of May 1520, the king, and the queen, and the whole court, gather at Dover, on the south coast of England, to cross the Channel to France.

[pause]

Dover is a busy little port, under the great white chalk cliffs, with the old castle, on the hill above, guarding the narrowest crossing of the sea, between England and France: only about thirty-three kilometres. On a clear day, from the cliffs, you can see the coast of France, a thin grey line, on the horizon.

[long-pause]

The harbour is full of ships: dozens of them, of every size, to carry the king, and the court, and the thousands of people, and the horses, and the baggage, and the food, and the tents, and the furniture, and the tapestries, and the cloth of gold, across the sea. The king's own ship is decorated with flags, and banners, and streamers, in green and white, the Tudor colours, and with sails of cloth of gold, some people say.

[pause]

Before Henry leaves, the Emperor Charles the Fifth, Catherine's nephew, the young ruler of Spain, and the Netherlands, and Germany, comes, by sea, to visit him, unexpectedly, and they meet at Dover, and ride together to Canterbury, and spend a few days together, at the archbishop's palace, and Catherine is overjoyed to see her nephew, for the first time. Because the emperor, too, wants to be Henry's friend, and does not want England and France to become too friendly.

[pause]

{img:ships-crossing-channel-calm-sea-summer-morning-sails-france-coast}
Then, on the thirty-first of May, the king's fleet sails, across the calm, blue summer sea, to Calais, the last English town in France, which England has held for nearly two hundred years: a busy, walled town, by the sea, with English soldiers, and English merchants, who trade in wool, and English churches, and English streets.

[long-pause]

From Calais, the court rides, in a great procession, across the flat green countryside, to the little town of Guînes, near the border, where the golden palace, and the tents, are waiting. And on the seventh of June, the two kings meet, in the valley, between Guînes and Ardres, on the Field of the Cloth of Gold.

[long-pause]

{img:calais-walled-town-harbour-english-flags-1520-merchants-quay}
Calais, in 1520, is a little piece of England in France. Its people speak English, and pay taxes to the English king, and send members to the English Parliament. The great wool merchants of England, the Staplers, have their headquarters there, and all English wool sold to Europe must pass through it. England will lose it, at last, in 1558, under Queen Mary, who said, it is told, that when she died, they would find the word Calais written on her heart.

[long-pause]

{img:field-of-cloth-of-gold-1520-tents-golden-pavilions-plain-guines}
In the summer of 1520, the most splendid event of the age takes place: the meeting of King Henry the Eighth of England, and King Francis the First of France, at a place called the Field of the Cloth of Gold.

[pause]

It is the idea of Cardinal Wolsey. England and France have been enemies for hundreds of years. Wolsey wants peace, between the two kingdoms, and between all the kingdoms of Europe. And so, he arranges for the two young kings to meet, in person, in friendship, and to celebrate, together, with feasts, and tournaments, and music, for more than two weeks.

[long-pause]

The meeting takes place in the north of France, near Calais, which belongs to England, in a valley, between the English town of Guînes, and the French town of Ardres, on the border, in June 1520.

[pause]

Both kings are determined to outshine the other. They bring their whole courts with them: Henry brings about six thousand people, across the Channel, in a great fleet of ships, and Francis brings as many. Lords, and ladies, and knights, and bishops, and musicians, and cooks, and servants, and horses, thousands of them.

[pause]

For Henry, the English workmen, more than two thousand of them, build a temporary palace, at Guînes, in a few weeks: a great square building, with a courtyard, about a hundred metres on each side, with walls of brick, at the bottom, and above, of timber, and painted canvas, painted to look like stone, with real glass windows, so many that people said it was like a house of glass. Inside, there are great halls, and chambers, hung with tapestries, and cloth of gold, and silk, and a chapel, with an altar of gold, and jewels.

[long-pause]

In front of the palace, there are two fountains, which flow with wine: red wine, and white wine, and spiced wine, free for anyone to drink, from silver cups. Above one of them, there is a statue of Bacchus, the god of wine, with the words, in French: make good cheer, who will.

[pause]

Around the palace, and the French camp at Ardres, there are thousands of tents, and pavilions, of every colour, many of them covered with cloth of gold, which glitter in the summer sun, so that the whole valley seems to be made of gold. That is why it is called the Field of the Cloth of Gold.

[pause]

{img:wine-fountain-statue-bacchus-silver-cups-crowd-summer-day}
The French king's great tent is covered with cloth of gold, outside, and lined with blue velvet, embroidered with golden lilies, the symbol of France, and with a ceiling painted with the stars. But on the first night, a great wind blows, and many of the French tents are blown down.

[long-pause]

On the seventh of June, 1520, the two kings meet, for the first time, in the valley, between the two camps.

[pause]

They ride toward each other, slowly, from opposite sides of the valley, on their great horses, dressed in cloth of gold, and silver, and jewels, followed by their nobles, while the trumpets sound, and the cannons fire. In the middle of the valley, they stop, and look at each other. And then they spur their horses, and ride forward, and, still on horseback, they embrace, and take off their hats, and embrace again.

[pause]

{img:two-kings-on-horseback-embracing-valley-golden-light-nobles-watching}
Then they dismount, and walk, arm in arm, into a tent of cloth of gold, and talk, privately, with Wolsey. And then they come out, smiling, and the crowds cheer.

[pause]

For the next seventeen days, there are feasts, and tournaments, and dances, and music, and masques, and wrestling, and archery, and church services, and the exchange of gifts, between the two courts.

[long-pause]

{img:king-francis-first-tall-long-nose-dark-hair-velvet-portrait}
King Francis the First of France is twenty-five years old in 1520, three years younger than Henry. He is tall, even taller than Henry, and very charming, with dark hair, and a long nose, and a great love of art, and beauty, and women, and war.

[pause]

He has invited the great Italian artist, Leonardo da Vinci, to France, where Leonardo spent the last years of his life, in a little manor house, near the king's castle, at Amboise, and passed away, the year before, in 1519. Francis owns Leonardo's painting of the Mona Lisa, which is still in France, in the Louvre.

[long-pause]

Henry and Francis are rivals in everything: in looks, and clothes, and palaces, and horses, and tournaments, and power, and glory. Each wants to be the greatest king in Europe.

[pause]

There is a famous story about their meeting. One day, at the Field of the Cloth of Gold, after a feast, Henry, in high spirits, challenged Francis to a wrestling match. The two kings wrestled, in front of their courts. And Francis, it is said, threw Henry to the ground. Henry was furious, and wanted to try again. But the courtiers quickly separated them, and the two queens, Catherine, and Claude, the wife of Francis, persuaded them to go in to dinner. The English chroniclers, loyally, do not mention it. But the French ones do.

[pause]

{img:two-queens-catherine-claude-pavilion-ladies-gowns-conversation}
Another day, Francis, early in the morning, without any guards, rode over, alone, with only two or three companions, to the English palace, and walked into Henry's bedroom, and woke him up, and said: brother, I am your prisoner. Henry was delighted, and gave him a precious jewelled collar, and Francis gave Henry a bracelet worth even more. It was a gesture of trust, and friendship.

[long-pause]

The two queens met too, and the ladies of the two courts, and dined together, and danced together, and admired each other's clothes, and gossiped.

[pause]

And on the last day, the twenty-third of June, there was a great open-air Mass, celebrated by Cardinal Wolsey, on a great platform, in front of both courts, with the choirs of both kings singing together. And at the end, a great firework, in the shape of a dragon, or a salamander, the symbol of King Francis, flew across the sky, over the crowds.

[pause]

{img:open-air-mass-platform-choirs-two-courts-banners-summer-morning}
Then the two kings said goodbye, and swore eternal friendship, and went home.

[long-pause]

Two years later, England and France were at war again.

[pause]

The Field of the Cloth of Gold cost both kings an enormous fortune, and achieved almost nothing. But it was remembered, for centuries, as the most splendid moment of the Renaissance: two young kings, in the golden summer of 1520, in a valley full of golden tents, and fountains of wine, and music, embracing on horseback, and promising peace.

[pause]

A great painting of the event, made a few years later, still hangs in Hampton Court: full of tiny people, and tents, and horses, and the temporary palace, and the wine fountain, and the kings, and even a dragon, flying in the sky.

[long-pause]

{img:tudor-joust-tiltyard-wooden-barrier-knights-lances-armour-crowds}
The greatest sport of the Tudor court is the joust.

[pause]

Two knights, in full armour, on great horses, covered with rich cloths, in their colours, charge at each other, along a wooden barrier, called the tilt, which keeps the horses apart, and try to strike each other with long wooden lances, on the helmet, or the shield, or the breastplate, and break the lance, or knock the other knight out of the saddle.

[long-pause]

The lances are made of light wood, and designed to break easily, in a shower of splinters, which makes a great noise, and a great show, and the crowd cheers. Points are given for breaking a lance, and for striking the helmet, and for unhorsing an opponent.

[pause]

The armour of the jousters is made especially for jousting: very heavy, and thick, on the left side, which faces the opponent, with a great helmet, with only a narrow slit to see through. Henry's own armour, made by the best armourers, from Germany, and Italy, and in his own workshops, at Greenwich, can still be seen today, in the Tower of London, and it shows how tall, and strong, and slim, he was, as a young man.

[pause]

{img:henry-viii-armour-display-tower-of-london-tall-polished-later}
Henry loves jousting more than anything. He jousts at every great feast, and celebration, and wins, often, because he is strong, and skilled, and brave, and because, perhaps, some of his opponents are careful not to beat the king. At the Field of the Cloth of Gold, the two kings, Henry and Francis, jousted every day, and both did very well, although they did not joust against each other.

[long-pause]

A tournament is a great spectacle. There are galleries, for the king, and the queen, and the ladies, and the courtiers, hung with tapestries, and cloth of gold. There are heralds, in their coats of arms, and trumpeters. There are pavilions, for the knights, to arm themselves. And there are crowds of ordinary people, around the lists, watching.

[pause]

The knights come in, in procession, with their squires, and pages, and servants, all dressed in their colours, sometimes in costumes, as characters from the old stories of chivalry: knights of King Arthur, or wild men, or pilgrims. Each knight carries a token, a ribbon, or a sleeve, from a lady, tied to his helmet, or his lance.

[pause]

{img:ladies-gallery-tournament-ribbons-tokens-knights-saluting}
And at the end of the day, the prizes are given out, by the queen, or the ladies, and there is a great feast, and dancing, late into the night.

[long-pause]

Jousting is dangerous. Knights are often hurt. In 1524, Henry himself forgot to close the visor of his helmet, and was struck on the head by a lance, and was lucky not to be badly hurt. And in 1536, sixteen years after our story, he will have a terrible fall, in a joust, at Greenwich, and be unconscious for two hours, and his leg will be badly injured, and he will never joust again. Some historians think that this fall changed him, and made him the angry, suspicious, unpredictable king of his later years.

[pause]

But in 1520, he is young, and strong, and jousts in the summer sunshine, and wins, and the crowds cheer, and the queen smiles.

[long-pause]

{img:tower-of-london-white-tower-walls-moat-1520-thames-boats}
At the eastern end of the city of London, beside the river, there is the Tower of London.

[pause]

It is an old royal fortress, built by William the Conqueror, more than four hundred and fifty years before, with a great square keep, of white stone, the White Tower, in the middle, and walls, and towers, and a moat, all around. In 1520, it is a royal palace, and an armoury, and a treasury, and the royal mint, where the coins of England are made, and a prison, for important prisoners. And a zoo.

[long-pause]

For nearly three hundred years, the kings of England have kept wild animals at the Tower, in the royal menagerie, in a building near the main gate, called the Lion Tower: lions, and leopards, and other animals, given as gifts to the kings, by foreign rulers.

[pause]

There have been a polar bear, which was allowed to swim, and fish, in the Thames, on a long chain. And an elephant, given by the king of France, in the thirteenth century, which was kept in a special house, and fed with wine. And lions, many of them, named after the kings, and queens, and people believed that when a king died, his lion died too.

[pause]

{img:lion-tower-menagerie-lions-cages-visitors-looking-tudor}
In Tudor times, Londoners can visit the menagerie, and look at the lions, and the leopards, for a small fee, or by bringing a cat, or a dog, to feed to the lions. It is one of the sights of London, which every visitor wants to see.

[long-pause]

The menagerie remained at the Tower for more than six hundred years, until 1835, when the last animals were moved to the new London Zoo, in Regent's Park.

[pause]

Today, at the Tower, there are sculptures, of wire, of the animals that once lived there: lions, and a polar bear, and an elephant, and baboons. And there are the ravens: six of them, at least, always, because a legend says that if the ravens ever leave the Tower, the kingdom will fall. They are looked after by a Yeoman Warder, called the Ravenmaster, and have their wings clipped, a little, so that they cannot fly far.

[pause]

{img:ravens-tower-green-yeoman-warder-ravenmaster-feeding-morning}
And the Yeoman Warders, the Beefeaters, still live in the Tower, with their families, inside the walls, and every night, at ten o'clock, they lock the gates, in the Ceremony of the Keys, as they have done, every night, without fail, for more than seven hundred years.

[long-pause]

{img:tudor-musicians-lute-viol-recorders-sackbut-gallery-hall-candles}
Music is everywhere in the England of Henry the Eighth.

[pause]

At court, there are dozens of musicians: trumpeters, and drummers, for the great ceremonies, and the tournaments. Players of the shawm, and the sackbut, an early trombone, for the outdoor music, and the processions. Players of the lute, and the viol, and the recorder, and the flute, and the harp, and the virginals, for the quiet music of the chambers. And the singers of the Chapel Royal, men and boys, who sing at the services, every day, the beautiful, complicated church music of the time, in many parts.

[long-pause]

The king himself is a fine musician, and composer. More than thirty pieces of music, songs, and pieces for instruments, are said to be by him, in a book of music from his court, called the Henry the Eighth Manuscript, which is now in the British Library.

[pause]

His most famous song is Pastime with Good Company: Pastime with good company, I love, and shall, until I die. Grudge who will, but none deny, so God be pleased, thus live will I. For my pastance, hunt, sing, and dance, my heart is set. All goodly sport, for my comfort, who shall me let?

[pause]

{img:king-singing-with-courtiers-chamber-music-book-lute-evening}
There is a famous old story that he wrote the song Greensleeves, for Anne Boleyn. But this is almost certainly not true: Greensleeves was written later, in the time of his daughter, Elizabeth.

[long-pause]

And there is dancing. Everyone at court must dance well, and the dancing masters teach the courtiers the latest dances, from France, and Italy: the stately basse dance, slow, and graceful, and gliding. The pavane, slow and dignified, like a procession, in which the ladies show off their fine gowns. The galliard, fast and lively, with leaps, and kicks, and jumps, in which the young men show off their strength, and agility, and Henry, a great dancer, leaps higher than anyone.

[pause]

And there is music outside the court, everywhere: in the churches, with their organs, and choirs. In the streets, with the ballad singers, selling printed songs. In the taverns, with fiddlers, and pipers. In the villages, with the bagpipes, and the pipe and tabor, a little drum, and a pipe, played together by one man, for the country dances, around the maypole, on May Day.

[pause]

{img:may-day-village-green-maypole-dancers-ribbons-pipe-tabor-spring}
On May Day, early in the morning, the young people of the villages go out into the woods, to gather may, the white hawthorn blossom, and green branches, to decorate their houses, and the church. And they bring back a tall tree, the maypole, and set it up on the village green, decorated with ribbons, and garlands, and dance around it, all day, to the music of the pipes, and the drums, and choose a May Queen, and a May King, and eat, and drink, and play games, until night.

[long-pause]

Even the king goes maying. In 1515, Henry and Catherine, and the whole court, rode out, on May morning, into the woods at Shooters Hill, near Greenwich, and were met by two hundred of the king's archers, dressed in green, as Robin Hood, and his merry men, who invited them into the greenwood, to a breakfast of venison, in arbours, made of green branches, and flowers, with birds singing, in cages.

[pause]

So the music of Tudor England is the music of the court, and the church, and the street, and the village green, all together, and the young king loves them all.

[long-pause]

{img:village-archery-butts-sunday-men-longbows-targets-green-churchyard}
In Tudor England, every man must be an archer.

[pause]

The law says that every Englishman, between the ages of seventeen and sixty, must own a longbow, and arrows, and practise archery, regularly, on Sundays, and holy days, after church, at the butts: earth mounds, with targets, set up on the village green, or in a field, near every village, and town. Fathers must buy bows for their sons, from the age of seven, and teach them to shoot.

[long-pause]

The longbow is the great English weapon, which won the famous battles of the Hundred Years' War, a century before: Crécy, and Poitiers, and Agincourt. It is made of a single piece of yew wood, as tall as a man, or taller, and needs great strength to draw: the archers on the Mary Rose had bows which needed a pull of fifty or seventy kilograms, and their skeletons show that their left arms, and shoulders, were bigger, and stronger, than their right, from years of practice.

[pause]

A good archer can shoot ten or twelve arrows a minute, and hit a target at two hundred metres.

[pause]

{img:longbow-yew-arrows-goose-feathers-quiver-close-tudor-archer}
King Henry himself is a fine archer, and loves to shoot, with his guard, and his friends, and makes bets on the results. He encourages archery all over the country, and bans other games, like bowls, and tennis, and cards, and dice, for ordinary people, so that they will practise archery instead. But the people play them anyway.

[long-pause]

On Sunday afternoons, in summer, after church, the men and boys of the village gather at the butts, and shoot, in turns, and the women, and children, watch, and cheer, and bring food, and ale. There are competitions, and prizes: a silver arrow, or a new bow, or a pig.

[pause]

But by 1520, guns are beginning to replace bows, in war, and the young men are less interested in archery than their grandfathers were, which worries the king, and his council. In a few decades, the longbow will disappear from war. But archery will remain a sport, and a pastime, in England, until today.

[long-pause]

{img:mary-rose-warship-sails-portsmouth-harbour-1520-tudor-flags}
In 1520, one of the finest ships in Henry's navy is the Mary Rose.

[pause]

She was built at Portsmouth, in 1510 and 1511, one of the first ships built for the king's new navy. She is a great carrack, a large warship, about forty metres long, with four masts, and high castles, at the front, and the back, and rows of gun ports, along her sides, with heavy guns, of bronze, and iron, a new idea, which made her one of the most powerful warships in the world.

[long-pause]

She was probably named after the king's favourite sister, Mary, and the Tudor rose, the symbol of the family. Henry loved her. An admiral wrote to him that she was the noblest ship of sail, of any great ship, at this hour, that I trow be in Christendom.

[pause]

She served for thirty-four years, in Henry's wars, against France, and Scotland. And in the summer of 1520, she was one of the ships that guarded the Channel, while the king crossed to France, for the Field of the Cloth of Gold.

[pause]

Life on board the Mary Rose, for her crew of about four hundred, sailors, and gunners, and soldiers, and archers, was crowded, and hard, and cramped, below decks, with the guns, and the stores, and the barrels of food, and beer, and the animals.

[long-pause]

In July 1545, twenty-five years after our story, during a battle with the French fleet, in the Solent, near Portsmouth, the Mary Rose sank, in front of the king, who was watching from the shore, at Southsea Castle. We will not describe it. Few of her crew survived.

[pause]

She lay at the bottom of the Solent, in the mud, for four hundred and thirty-seven years. The mud preserved half of her hull, and thousands of things inside her: the belongings of the crew, and the ship's equipment, and stores.

[pause]

{img:mary-rose-museum-preserved-hull-galleries-soft-light-visitors}
In 1982, after years of work by divers, and archaeologists, she was lifted out of the sea, in a great cradle, watched, on television, by millions of people, all over the world. And today, she is in a museum, in Portsmouth, in a special building, where you can see the great wooden hull, and the guns, and more than nineteen thousand objects found inside her.

[long-pause]

And those objects tell us, wonderfully, about the everyday lives of ordinary Tudor people: the sailors, and the archers, and the gunners, and the carpenter, and the cook, and the barber-surgeon.

[pause]

There are their wooden bowls, and plates, and spoons, with their initials carved on them. Their combs, for removing lice. Their shoes, and their clothes, and their leather jerkins. Their dice, and their backgammon boards, and their little pocket sundials, and their rosaries, and their whistles, and their musical instruments: a fiddle, and pipes, and a tabor drum. The cook's great cauldrons, and the bones of the beef, and the pork, and the fish, they ate. The barber-surgeon's chest, with his medicines, and his instruments, and his razors. The carpenter's tools. The archers' longbows, and thousands of arrows.

[pause]

{img:mary-rose-objects-wooden-bowls-combs-shoes-whistle-dice-museum}
And the bones of the ship's dog, a little terrier, which lived on board, and caught rats, and which the crew called, the archaeologists believe, Hatch, because he was found near a hatch. His skeleton is in the museum too.

[long-pause]

{img:tudor-palace-autumn-evening-quiet-courtyard-fallen-leaves-later}
The golden summer of 1520 did not last.

[pause]

In the years that followed, King Henry grew desperate for a son. Catherine was growing older, and could have no more children. And in about 1526, Henry fell in love with one of the queen's ladies, a clever, dark-eyed, witty young woman, called Anne Boleyn, who had grown up at the French court, and would not be his mistress, but wanted to be his wife.

[long-pause]

Henry asked the Pope to annul his marriage to Catherine, saying that it had never been valid, because she had been his brother's wife. The Pope refused, partly because Catherine's nephew, the Emperor Charles the Fifth, the most powerful ruler in Europe, did not want it.

[pause]

So, in the end, Henry broke with the Pope, and the Church of Rome, and made himself the Supreme Head of the Church of England, and married Anne, in 1533. It was the beginning of the English Reformation, which changed England forever.

[pause]

{img:empty-monastery-cloister-ruins-roofless-church-1540s-quiet-later}
We will not follow the rest of the story in detail tonight. It is enough to say that the later years of Henry's reign were troubled, and often sad. Henry married six times, in all. Cardinal Wolsey fell from power, in 1529, and passed away, a year later, on his way to London. Thomas More, who would not accept the king as head of the Church, lost his life, in 1535, calmly, and bravely, saying that he died the king's good servant, but God's first. The monasteries were closed, and their lands sold, and their buildings left to fall into ruin, or turned into houses.

[long-pause]

Catherine of Aragon was sent away from court, and lived quietly, in the country, still calling herself the queen, and passed away, in 1536, at Kimbolton Castle, still loving Henry, and writing to him, at the end: mine eyes desire you above all things.

[pause]

Henry himself grew old, and heavy, and ill, with a wound in his leg that never healed, and angry, and suspicious. He passed away in January 1547, at the age of fifty-five.

[pause]

{img:three-tudor-children-portraits-edward-mary-elizabeth-gallery-later}
All three of his children became rulers of England, one after another: his son, Edward the Sixth, a clever, serious boy, who reigned for six years, and passed away at fifteen. Then Mary, Catherine's daughter, the little girl of 1520, who became Queen Mary the First. And then Elizabeth, the daughter of Anne Boleyn, who became Queen Elizabeth the First, and reigned for forty-five years, the golden age of England, the age of Shakespeare, and Drake, and the Armada.

[long-pause]

But in the summer of 1520, none of this has happened yet. The king is young, and his queen is beside him, and his little daughter is playing in the gardens of Greenwich, and the tents of cloth of gold are shining in the summer sun, in the valley in France, and the wine fountains are flowing, and everything seems possible.

[long-pause]

{img:hampton-court-today-red-brick-gatehouse-visitors-sunny}
Today, you can still visit many of the places of Tudor England, and walk where Henry, and Catherine, and Wolsey, and More, walked, five hundred years ago.

[pause]

Hampton Court Palace still stands, by the river Thames, with its red brick, and its great gatehouses, and its twisted chimneys, and its courtyards, and its Great Hall, with its magnificent carved wooden roof, built by Henry, and its tapestries, and its huge Tudor kitchens, where, on some days, cooks in Tudor costume still roast meat, on great spits, in front of the enormous fireplaces, and bake pies, and make pottage, the Tudor way.

[long-pause]

In the palace, there is the great painting of the Field of the Cloth of Gold, full of tiny figures, and golden tents, and the wine fountain, and the dragon in the sky. And there is the real tennis court, built on the site of the court where Henry played, and still used today, one of the oldest tennis courts in the world.

[pause]

There are the gardens, with their knot gardens, and their famous maze, of tall hedges, planted about a hundred and seventy years after Henry, the oldest hedge maze in Britain, where families still get lost, happily, on summer afternoons. And the great vine, planted in 1768, still producing grapes, the largest, and one of the oldest, grape vines in the world.

[pause]

In London, there is the Tower of London, with Henry's armour, so tall, and strong, in the White Tower, and the crown jewels, and the ravens, and the Yeomen Warders, the Beefeaters, in their Tudor uniforms, red and gold, and dark blue, as they have worn for five hundred years.

[long-pause]

At Greenwich, where Henry was born, and loved to live, his palace is gone, but on the site, there are the beautiful buildings of the Old Royal Naval College, by the river, and the park, where he hunted, rising up the hill, to the Royal Observatory, and the line of zero longitude, with a wonderful view over the river, and London.

[pause]

In Portsmouth, there is the Mary Rose, in her museum, with all the belongings of her crew. And in Westminster Abbey, there are the tombs of Henry's parents, Henry the Seventh, and Elizabeth of York, in the beautiful chapel that Henry the Seventh built, with its stone ceiling like lace. And the tombs of Mary, and Elizabeth. And in Peterborough Cathedral, there is the grave of Catherine of Aragon, where, every year, people leave pomegranates, her symbol, and flowers, and there is a banner, with her name, and the words: Katharine, Queen of England.

[pause]

{img:catherine-of-aragon-grave-peterborough-pomegranates-flowers-banner}
And all over England, there are Tudor houses, and inns, and villages, with their black timber, and white plaster, and their crooked roofs, and their tall chimneys, and their little diamond-paned windows, still lived in, today.

[long-pause]

So we leave Tudor England tonight, on a summer evening in 1520, with the golden tents shining in the valley in France, and the young king laughing among his friends, and the music of the lutes drifting across the water at Greenwich.

[pause]

The apprentice in Cheapside closing his shutters, the villagers dancing round the maypole, the cardinal in his red brick palace, and the little princess asleep, with all of the future still before her.

[pause]
