Salisbury Cathedral has stood in its meadow for almost eight hundred years. And it has seen a great deal of history. Kings and queens have come and gone. Wars have been fought. The world has changed beyond recognition. But the cathedral has stayed, quiet and beautiful, among the rivers and the meadows.

--

In 1386, a clock was made for the cathedral. It has no face and no hands. It simply strikes the hours, on a bell, so that the priests know when it is time to pray. It is made of iron, with big wheels and weights. And it is still there, in the cathedral today. It is thought to be the oldest working mechanical clock in the world. It has been ticking, on and off, for more than six hundred years.

In the fifteen hundreds, the religion of England changed. King Henry the Eighth broke away from the Pope in Rome, and the Church of England was formed. The Use of Sarum, the beautiful old way of worship of Salisbury, was replaced by new services, in English. Many statues and paintings in churches were destroyed. At Salisbury, some of the old statues and decorations were lost. But the building itself survived.

{img:christopher-wren-surveying-spire-lean-plumb-line-1668-later}
In 1668, the great architect Christopher Wren, who later built St Paul's Cathedral in London, was asked to look at the spire of Salisbury. People were worried about its lean. Wren studied it carefully. He measured how much it was leaning, and decided that it was safe, and that the lean had stopped. He recommended some iron ties, to make it stronger. And he was right. The spire has not moved since.

In the seventeen hundreds, another architect, called James Wyatt, made big changes inside the cathedral. He wanted to make it look tidy and plain. He removed many of the old tombs, screens and chapels. He took out some of the old stained glass, and threw it into a ditch. Many people today think that this was a great loss. But at the time, it was the fashion. And some of the old glass was later found again, and put back in the windows.

--

{img:john-constable-painting-salisbury-cathedral-from-meadows-easel-cows-later}
In the early eighteen hundreds, a great English painter, called John Constable, came to Salisbury many times, to visit his friend, the bishop's nephew. He loved the cathedral and the meadows around it. He painted it again and again, from different places, in different lights. From the bishop's garden. From the water meadows. With a rainbow over it. His paintings of Salisbury Cathedral are among the most famous paintings in England. They hang in great museums, in London and in New York.

In the twentieth century, the cathedral needed a great deal of repair. The stone of the spire and the walls had been damaged by centuries of wind, rain and frost. In 1985, a great appeal was launched, to raise money for the repairs. And since then, the masons of Salisbury have been working on the cathedral, almost without stopping, replacing worn stones, cleaning, carving and repairing. It is one of the longest building works in the world.

{img:modern-cathedral-masons-workshop-carving-new-stone-traditional-tools}
And here is a wonderful thing. The cathedral still has its own masons. A team of stonemasons, who work in a workshop in the Close, just like the medieval lodge. They use some of the same tools as the medieval masons. Mallets and chisels, squares and templates. They carve new stones, to replace the old ones that have worn away, copying the old shapes exactly. Some of them are apprentices, learning the craft, just like Tom, almost eight hundred years ago.

--

So the work that began in 1220, in the meadow by the five rivers, has never really stopped. Generation after generation, masons have cared for the great church, cutting, carving and repairing. And each one has added their mark, somewhere in the stones. Medieval marks, and modern ones, side by side. A long chain of hands, reaching back across the centuries, to Walter, and Tom, and old Master Nicholas, and the very first stone, laid by the bishop, on a spring day in 1220.
