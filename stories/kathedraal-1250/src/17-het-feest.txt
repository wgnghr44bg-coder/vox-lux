In the middle of summer, there is a great feast. It is Midsummer, the feast of Saint John the Baptist, on the twenty-fourth of June. The days are at their longest. The nights are short and warm. And on Midsummer Eve, the people of Salisbury celebrate, all night long. The workers on the cathedral have a holiday. And everyone in the town comes out to join the fun.

--

In the evening, the streets are decorated with green branches, birch and fennel and Saint John's wort, and with flowers, especially white lilies. Lamps and candles are hung at the doors. The houses are open, and people visit each other, and share food and ale. And in the meadows by the river, outside the town, great bonfires are lit. They are called Saint John's fires.

The people dance around the bonfires, holding hands, singing old songs. Young people jump over the fires, for luck. Musicians play pipes, drums and fiddles. There are wrestling matches, races and games. Food is cooked over the fires. Roast meat, sausages and bread. Ale flows freely. And the sky stays light until very late, glowing pink and gold in the west, while the bonfires blaze in the meadows.

{img:people-jumping-over-small-bonfire-laughing-summer-night-medieval-dance}
The masons have their own celebration too. They hold a feast in the lodge, with the master mason, the carpenters and their families. There is a long table, with food and ale, and the masons sing their own songs, about stone and building and the old masters. The master mason makes a little speech, thanking everyone for their work this year. And then everyone goes out to the meadows, to dance around the fires.

Tom dances with his friends, Hugh and Robin, and with the other apprentices. And then he dances with a girl from the town, called Alice, the daughter of a cloth weaver, who has red hair and laughs at everything. Tom has noticed her at the market for a long time. He is very shy. But on Midsummer Eve, by the light of the fire, everything is easier. They dance, and talk, and laugh. And Tom thinks that Midsummer is the best night of the year.

--

{img:mystery-play-wagon-stage-medieval-street-actors-crowd-watching-summer}
During the summer, there are also plays. Groups of craftsmen, from the different trades, perform little plays about stories from the Bible. Noah and the flood. Jonah and the whale. The birth of Jesus. They act on wagons, which are pulled from street to street, so that everyone in town can see them. The actors wear simple costumes, and the plays are full of jokes and songs, as well as the holy stories. The crowds love them.

The masons and carpenters often play the story of Noah's ark. Because they are the builders. The carpenters build a real little ark on the wagon, with a roof and a door. Noah is played by an old carpenter with a long white beard. His wife, played by a young apprentice in a dress, refuses to get into the ark, and argues with him, while the rain comes down. The crowd roars with laughter. And then, at last, she gets in, and the animals follow, two by two, played by children in masks.

{img:children-in-animal-masks-noahs-ark-play-wagon-laughing-crowd}
Joan plays one of the animals. A sheep, with a white woollen mask, and a fleece on her back. She is supposed to walk into the ark quietly. But Pip, her little dog, runs onto the wagon after her, barking, and the crowd thinks it is part of the play, and cheers. Joan is embarrassed at first. But then she laughs too. And the old carpenter playing Noah says that God must have sent an extra dog, just in case.

--

Late at night, the bonfires burn low. The music stops. The people walk home through the warm, starry night, tired and happy. Tom walks with his family, along the river. The sky in the north is still faintly light, even at midnight. The cathedral stands dark against the sky, with its scaffolding like a black web. An owl hoots from the trees by the river. And in the meadows, the last embers of the fires glow red, like little stars fallen to the earth.
