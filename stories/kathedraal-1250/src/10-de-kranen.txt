How do you lift a block of stone, weighing a tonne, to the top of a wall thirty metres high? Without engines, without electricity, without steel cables? The medieval builders had a clever answer. The great wheel. A huge wooden wheel, taller than a house, turned by people walking inside it, like a hamster in a wheel. It is one of the most remarkable machines of the Middle Ages.

--

The great wheel stands on a wooden platform, on top of the walls or the scaffolding. It is about four or five metres high, made of oak, with a wide floor of planks inside the rim. One or two men walk inside the wheel, on the planks, step by step, like walking up a never-ending staircase. As they walk, the wheel turns. And as the wheel turns, it winds a strong hemp rope around its axle. The rope goes up over a long wooden arm, and down to the ground, where the stone is tied to it.

The wheel gives the men great strength. Two men walking in a wheel can lift a stone that twenty men could not lift with their arms. Slowly, slowly, the stone rises into the air, swinging a little, higher and higher, until it reaches the top of the wall. There, the setters swing it onto the wall, and lower it into its place, on a bed of mortar. Then the rope is lowered again, for the next stone.

{img:wooden-scaffolding-poles-lashed-ropes-hurdles-masons-high-on-wall}
The walls are surrounded by scaffolding. It is made of long wooden poles, tied together with ropes. Some of the poles are set into holes in the walls, called putlog holes. The platforms are made of woven hurdles, like big flat baskets, made of willow or hazel branches. They are light and strong. The workers walk on them high above the ground, carrying tools and mortar. You can still see the putlog holes in the walls of many old cathedrals today.

Mortar holds the stones together. It is made of lime, sand and water. The lime comes from burning chalk or limestone in a kiln, at a very high heat, until it turns into a white powder. The mortar men mix it with sand and water in big wooden troughs, with hoes, until it is smooth and thick, like porridge. Then they carry it up the ladders in wooden buckets, or it is lifted up by the great wheel. It sets slowly, over weeks and months, and becomes as hard as stone.

--

{img:wooden-centering-arch-frame-masons-placing-voussoirs-vault-high}
To build an arch, the carpenters first make a wooden frame, in the exact shape of the arch. It is called centring. The masons lay the wedge-shaped stones of the arch on top of the wooden frame, one by one, from both sides, until they meet at the top. The last stone, at the very top, is called the keystone. When it is in place, the arch holds itself up. Then, carefully, the wooden frame is taken away. And the arch stands, by itself, for centuries.

The vaults, the stone ceilings high above, are built in the same way, on wooden frames. They are made of thin stone ribs, which cross each other, and thin stone panels between them. It is very delicate work. The vault must be strong enough to hold itself up, but light enough not to push the walls outward. The master mason calculates everything carefully. And outside the walls, he builds buttresses, strong stone supports, to hold the walls in place.

{img:boy-looking-up-at-new-stone-vault-ribs-high-above-light-awe}
Tom likes to watch the great wheel. Sometimes, he is allowed to walk inside it, with an older worker. It is hard work. Step after step, after step, and the wheel turns slowly, and somewhere far above, a stone is rising into the air. You cannot see it from inside the wheel. You just have to walk, and trust the men at the top. Tom thinks it is a little like building the cathedral itself. Everyone does their part, without seeing the whole.

--

Accidents sometimes happen. A rope breaks, a scaffold gives way, a worker slips. Building a cathedral is dangerous work. The masons are careful, and they say a prayer each morning, before they climb. But the great wheel at Salisbury has worked for thirty years, carrying thousands of stones, one by one, up into the sky. And in the cathedral of Salisbury, high up in the tower, there is still an old wooden wheel today, which was used in the Middle Ages. It has not turned for hundreds of years. But it is still there, waiting.
