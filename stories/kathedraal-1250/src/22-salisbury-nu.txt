Today, Salisbury Cathedral is one of the most visited cathedrals in Britain. Hundreds of thousands of people come every year, from all over the world. They walk across the wide green lawn of the Close, and look up at the spire, rising high into the sky. And they go inside, through the great west door, into the cool, pale, silvery light of the nave.

--

Inside, the cathedral looks very much as it did in 1258. The long nave, with its rows of columns, pale grey with dark marble shafts. The pointed arches. The high vaults. The silvery windows. The light is soft and calm. Visitors walk slowly, looking up, talking quietly. Some sit on the chairs, and just look. Some light a candle. And every day, the services still go on, as they have for almost eight hundred years.

In the middle of the nave, there is a modern font, for baptisms. It is a large, low, cross-shaped basin of dark stone, filled with water, so still and smooth that it reflects the columns, the arches and the windows above, like a mirror. Visitors bend over it, and see the whole cathedral, upside down, in the water. It is one of the most beautiful things in the church.

{img:salisbury-font-water-mirror-reflecting-vaults-windows-nave-today}
You can still see the old clock of 1386, standing in the nave, with its iron wheels slowly turning. You can see the tomb of William Longespée, the Earl of Salisbury, in his painted armour. And in the chapter house, you can see Magna Carta, in its glass case, in the dim light, and the stone frieze, with its little carved figures, telling the stories of the Bible. And if you look very closely, at the scene of Noah's ark, perhaps you can find a tiny dog.

You can also climb the tower. There are three hundred and thirty-two steps, up narrow stone stairs, through the roof spaces, past the great medieval oak beams, with the carpenters' marks still on them. Past the old wooden windlass, which lifted the stones. Up to the bottom of the spire, where a little door opens onto a balcony, high above the town. From there, you can see for kilometres in every direction. The town, the rivers, the meadows, Old Sarum on its hill, and the green chalk hills beyond.

--

{img:view-from-salisbury-tower-balcony-town-rivers-old-sarum-hill-distance}
At the top of the spire, peregrine falcons make their nest every spring. They are the fastest birds in the world. They have lived on the cathedral for many years. Every year, they lay their eggs in a little box, high up on the tower, and raise their chicks. There is a camera, so that people can watch them online, from all over the world. The falcons do not know that they are living on a famous cathedral. To them, it is just a very tall, very safe cliff.

Old Sarum, the windy hill where the first cathedral stood, is still there too. You can walk up to it, and through the old earth banks, and see the ruins of the Norman castle, and the outline of the old cathedral, marked in the grass. The wind still blows across the hill, as it did in 1220. And from the edge of the banks, you can look down into the valley, and see the spire of the new cathedral, rising out of the meadows, three kilometres away.

{img:old-sarum-ruins-today-grass-outline-old-cathedral-wind-view-to-spire}
And in the water meadows, below the cathedral, there is a path, where you can walk along the river, through the grass, among the cows. It is the same view that John Constable painted. The river. The trees. The meadows. And the cathedral, rising above them, with its spire. On a summer evening, when the sun is low, and the light is golden, it looks exactly like his paintings. And perhaps, exactly as it looked to Tom and Walter, walking home from work, long ago.

--

The town of Salisbury still has its chequer pattern of streets, laid out by the bishop in the twelve hundreds. Some of the water channels have been covered over. But the market still takes place in the market square, on Tuesdays and Saturdays, as it has for eight hundred years. And people still live in the old streets, and walk to the cathedral, and look up at the spire, and feel proud of the great building in their town.
