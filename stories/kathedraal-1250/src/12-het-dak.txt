On top of the stone vaults, there is a great roof. It is made of wood, and covered with lead. It protects the stone vaults below from the rain and the snow. And building it is the work of the carpenters, who are just as skilled as the masons, and just as important.

--

The roof of Salisbury Cathedral is made of oak. Huge oak beams, from great old trees, some of them hundreds of years old. The king himself gave many of the trees, from his royal forests. The carpenters go into the forests, choose the trees, and cut them down with axes. They shape the beams roughly in the forest, while the wood is still green and soft. Then the beams are carried to Salisbury, on carts pulled by teams of oxen.

The roof is made of many triangular frames, called trusses, standing in a long row, from one end of the building to the other, like the ribs of a great animal. The carpenters build each truss on the ground first, fitting the beams together with joints, cut precisely with chisels, and fixed with wooden pegs, not nails. Then they take it apart, carry the pieces up onto the walls, and put it together again, up there, high in the air.

{img:carpenters-yard-cutting-oak-joints-axes-adzes-timber-framing-ground}
The carpenters mark every piece of wood with special signs, cut into the wood with a chisel, so that they know which piece goes where. These are called carpenters' marks. Like the masons' marks, they can still be seen today, in the roof of Salisbury Cathedral. Up there, in the dark, dusty roof space, the great oak beams still stand, with their marks, just as the carpenters left them, almost eight hundred years ago.

Tom's friend Robin is the son of one of the carpenters. Sometimes, he takes Tom up into the roof, where his father is working. They climb up long ladders, inside the walls, and come out onto the top of the stone vaults, under the half-built roof. It is like being inside the skeleton of a giant. Great beams rise up all around them, into the darkness. The smell of fresh oak is everywhere. And through the gaps in the roof, they can see the sky.

--

{img:plumber-laying-lead-sheets-on-cathedral-roof-fire-melting-pot-sky}
When the timber roof is finished, it is covered with lead. Lead is a soft, heavy, grey metal, which does not rust, and keeps out the water perfectly. The men who work with lead are called plumbers, from the Latin word for lead, plumbum. They melt the lead in big pots over fires, on the ground. Then they pour it onto a bed of sand, to make flat sheets. And then they carry the sheets up onto the roof, and lay them over the timber, overlapping, so that the rain runs off.

The plumbers also make the gutters, and the pipes that carry the rainwater away from the roof. At the ends of the pipes, the masons carve stone figures, with open mouths, which spit the water out, away from the walls. These are called gargoyles. The word comes from an old French word for throat. Some gargoyles look like monsters, some like animals, and some like funny people. When it rains, they gurgle and spit.

{img:view-from-cathedral-roof-medieval-town-meadows-rivers-boys-sitting}
Tom and Robin love to sit on the roof, high above the town, when the workers are having their lunch. From up there, they can see everything. The whole new town, with its chequer streets and its water channels. The market place, full of tiny people. The meadows and the rivers, shining in the sun. The road to Old Sarum, and the old hill in the distance. And beyond, the green chalk hills, stretching away to the horizon, dotted with white sheep.

--

Carpenters are needed for everything on the building site. The scaffolding, the centring for the arches, the great wheels for lifting, the workshops, the carts, the ladders, the doors. Without them, nothing could be built. Robin's father says that a cathedral is built of stone, but stands on wood. And when Tom looks at all the scaffolding around the walls, and all the wooden frames and machines, he thinks that Robin's father is right.
