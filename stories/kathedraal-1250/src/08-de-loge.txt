The centre of the stonemasons' work is the lodge. It is a long wooden building, with a thatched roof, beside the cathedral, on the south side. It is open at the front, so that the light can come in. Inside, there are rows of benches, called bankers, made of strong timber, on which the stones are laid while they are being worked. And at each banker, there is a mason, chipping, carving and shaping stone.

--

The lodge is noisy and dusty. The sound of hammers and chisels on stone fills the air all day long. Tink, tink, tink. A fine white dust covers everything. The masons' clothes, their hair, their eyebrows, the floor and the benches. The masons wear leather aprons and caps to protect themselves. And they talk and joke as they work, in the accents of many different parts of England, and some from France.

The masons use simple tools. A mallet, made of wood, and an axe for rough shaping. Chisels of many different shapes and sizes, for different jobs. Flat chisels, pointed chisels, toothed chisels, called claws, and small gouges for carving. A square, to check that the corners are exactly at right angles. A straight edge. A compass. And the wooden templates, made from the master's drawings.

{img:mason-hands-chisel-mallet-carving-stone-moulding-close-detail}
There are different kinds of masons. The rough masons, who shape the plain blocks for the walls. The freemasons, who carve the fine work, the mouldings, the window tracery, the capitals and the decorations. They are called freemasons because they work in freestone, a fine stone that can be carved freely in any direction. Walter is a freemason. And there are the setters, who work on the walls and the scaffolding, laying the stones in place with mortar.

Each mason has his own mark. A simple sign, like a little arrow, a star, a cross or a letter. He carves it into the side of every stone he finishes. So the master knows who made each stone, and how many stones each mason has cut. The masons are paid for their work, and a mason who makes bad stones will be found out. Walter's mark is a little shape like a bird's foot. Today, you can still find masons' marks on the stones of Salisbury Cathedral, if you look closely.

--

{img:masons-marks-carved-symbols-on-cathedral-stone-wall-close-arrows-crosses}
The lodge is also like a club, or a family. The masons have their own rules, and their own customs. They eat their meals together in the lodge. They help each other when they are sick or hurt. They hold feasts on holy days. They have secret signs and words, which only masons know, so that they can recognise each other when they travel to other building sites. Masons often travel from one cathedral to another, all over England and even to France, wherever there is work.

The masons are proud of their skill. And they have a sense of humour. Sometimes, high up on the cathedral, where nobody will ever see them, they carve funny little faces. A man pulling a face. A woman with a headache. A monkey. A dragon eating its own tail. Some people think these are portraits of people they knew, perhaps their master, or a priest they did not like. The faces are still there today, looking down from the high walls.

{img:carved-stone-head-corbel-funny-face-high-on-cathedral-wall}
Walter is carving a capital, the decorated top of a column. It is shaped like a bell, with curling leaves around it, called stiff leaf, which is the fashion of the time. The leaves look almost alive, curling over at their tips, as if blown by the wind. He works slowly and carefully, a little at a time, for weeks. He checks the shape with his templates. He smooths the surfaces. And slowly, out of the rough grey block, the leaves appear.

--

At the end of the day, when the bell rings, the masons put down their tools, brush off the dust, and go home. The lodge is quiet. The half-finished stones lie on the bankers, waiting for the morning. The evening sun shines in through the open front, lighting up the floating dust, like gold. And the curling leaves of Walter's capital catch the light, and seem, for a moment, almost to move.
