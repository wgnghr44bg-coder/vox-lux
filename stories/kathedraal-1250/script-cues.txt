Tonight we travel back almost eight hundred years, to the year 1250. We go to the south of England, to a wide green valley where five rivers meet. In the middle of the valley, among the water meadows, a great building is rising. Its pale grey stone walls are surrounded by wooden scaffolding. Its tall, pointed windows are still empty. Its roof is only half finished. It is a cathedral. And it is being built by hand.

[long-pause]

It is early morning in spring. A soft mist lies over the meadows. The rivers flow slowly between the willows. Cows are grazing in the wet grass. Herons stand in the shallows, very still. And out of the mist rises the great church, pale and enormous, much bigger than anything else in the valley. Bigger than any castle. Bigger than any palace. It looks like a mountain of stone, growing out of the green fields.

[pause]

Around the church, the building site is waking up. Smoke rises from the fires of the workshops. Carts loaded with stone are rumbling along the road. Men are climbing the ladders and the scaffolding, carrying tools. Hammers are beginning to ring on stone. And from a little wooden bell tower beside the church, a bell rings out, calling the workers to the start of another long day.

[pause]

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-masons-carving-stone-blocks-lodge-morning-light-chisels-dust}
This is Salisbury Cathedral. It is one of the most beautiful cathedrals in the world. And it is special for another reason. Most great cathedrals took hundreds of years to build. But Salisbury was built in just thirty-eight years, from 1220 to 1258. So it was all built in one style, by one generation of builders. When it was finished, it looked exactly as its designers had planned. And it still does today.

[pause]

Tonight, we will spend a year on the building site of Salisbury Cathedral, in 1250, when the work is in full swing. We will follow the family of a stonemason, and his son, a boy of thirteen, who is beginning his apprenticeship. We will see how the stones are cut in the quarry, and carried to the site. How they are shaped and carved. How they are lifted high into the air by great wooden cranes, turned by men walking inside them.

[pause]

{img:treadwheel-crane-medieval-men-walking-inside-wheel-lifting-stone-high-wall}
We will meet the master mason, who designs the building with only a compass and a straight rule. The carpenters, who build the great wooden roof. The glaziers, who make the windows. And the bishop and the priests, who will one day pray in the finished church. We will see the new town that is growing up around the cathedral. And at the end, we will watch the great church being consecrated, before the king of England himself.

[long-pause]

It was hard and sometimes dangerous work, building a cathedral. But we will mostly see the patience, the skill, and the care. Thousands of people, working together for a whole lifetime, to build something beautiful that they knew would last for centuries after they were gone. It is one of the most remarkable things that people have ever done.

[pause]

{img:pale-stone-cathedral-walls-rising-green-meadows-cows-grazing-spring-sun}
So let us begin, on a misty spring morning in 1250, in the valley of the five rivers. The bell is ringing. The hammers are beginning to sound. And a boy of thirteen is walking toward the great half-built church, beside his father, with a new leather apron over his arm, and his heart beating fast.

[long-pause]

{img:old-sarum-hilltop-fortress-ramparts-old-cathedral-castle-wind-1200}
Before the cathedral was built in the valley, there was an older cathedral, on a hill, about three kilometres to the north. The place was called Sarum. Today, it is called Old Sarum. It is a big round hill, surrounded by enormous earth banks and ditches, made by the people of the Iron Age, long before the Romans. Later, the Romans, the Saxons and the Normans all used it. And on top of the hill, the Normans built a castle, and a cathedral, side by side.

[long-pause]

The old cathedral at Sarum was built about a hundred and fifty years before our story. But it was not a happy place. The hill was high and bare, and the wind blew constantly, so loudly that the priests in the church could hardly hear themselves sing. There was very little water. The priests had to buy it from the soldiers of the castle, at a high price. In winter, it was bitterly cold. And the cathedral was crowded inside the walls, with no room to grow.

[pause]

Worst of all, the priests and the soldiers of the castle did not get along. They quarrelled constantly. There is a famous story, that one day, the priests went out of the castle for a procession, and when they came back, the soldiers had locked the gates, and would not let them in. The priests had to spend the whole night outside, in the cold and the wind.

[pause]

{img:bishop-richard-poore-medieval-robes-looking-at-valley-plans-hopeful}
So the bishop, a man called Richard Poore, decided to do something bold. He would move the whole cathedral, and the whole town, down from the windy hill, into the green valley below. Where there was plenty of water, from the five rivers. Where there was room to build. And where there were no soldiers. He asked the Pope for permission, and the Pope agreed.

[pause]

There is a lovely old legend about how the new place was chosen. The story says that the bishop asked an archer to shoot an arrow from the walls of Old Sarum, and promised to build the new cathedral wherever the arrow landed. The arrow flew far, far down into the valley. It hit a white deer, which ran on, and fell down in a meadow by the river. And there, the bishop built his cathedral. Of course, the meadow is more than three kilometres from the hill, much too far for any arrow. But it is a good story.

[long-pause]

{img:foundation-stones-laid-ceremony-1220-bishop-crowds-meadow-banners}
On the twenty-eighth of April, in the year 1220, the new cathedral was founded. There was a great ceremony, in the meadow. The bishop laid the first three foundation stones. One for the Pope. One for the archbishop of Canterbury. And one for himself. Then the great nobles of the county came forward, and each laid a stone. And a crowd of people watched, and sang, and prayed. The work had begun.

[pause]

The first part to be built was the far eastern end, the Lady Chapel, dedicated to the Virgin Mary. It took five years. In 1225, it was finished, and the first services were held in it. The bishop brought the bones of three earlier bishops from Old Sarum, and buried them in the new church. Then the builders moved on to the next part, and the next, working slowly westward, year after year.

[pause]

{img:people-leaving-old-sarum-carts-belongings-walking-down-to-valley}
Slowly, the people of Old Sarum moved down into the valley too. The priests, the merchants, the craftsmen and their families. They left the windy hill, and built new houses in the new town, near the new cathedral. Later, the old cathedral on the hill was taken apart, and its stones were reused. Some of them were brought down into the valley, and built into the walls of the new close around the cathedral.

[long-pause]

By 1250, Old Sarum is almost empty. Only the castle is still there, with a few soldiers, and a few houses. The wind still blows across the hill, as it always has. And from the top of the old banks, you can look down into the valley, and see the great new cathedral rising among the green meadows, pale and beautiful, with the rivers shining around it. The bishop's bold idea has worked.

[long-pause]

{img:gothic-cathedral-interior-tall-pointed-arches-light-columns-heavenly}
Why did people in the Middle Ages build cathedrals? A cathedral is the main church of a bishop, the leader of the church in a large area, called a diocese. The word comes from the Latin word cathedra, which means a chair. Because in the cathedral, there is the bishop's special chair, or throne. But a cathedral was much more than just a big church.

[long-pause]

For the people of the Middle Ages, a cathedral was a picture of heaven on earth. When you walked inside, you were supposed to feel that you had left the ordinary world behind, and entered the house of God. Everything was designed to lift your eyes and your heart upward. The tall columns, rising like the trunks of great trees. The pointed arches, meeting high above, like hands in prayer. And the windows, full of coloured light.

[pause]

In the time of our story, a new style of building has spread across Europe. Today, we call it Gothic. It began in France, about a hundred years before, near Paris. And it changed everything. Before, churches were built in a heavy style, with thick walls, round arches and small windows. Inside, they were dark. The new Gothic style used pointed arches, which are much stronger. So the walls could be thinner and taller, and the windows could be much bigger.

[pause]

{img:pointed-arch-versus-round-arch-diagram-feeling-stone-masons-explaining}
The Gothic builders discovered something wonderful. With pointed arches, ribbed vaults and slender columns, they could build higher and lighter than anyone had ever built before. They could fill the walls with windows. And the light could pour in, through coloured glass, filling the church with glowing light. For them, light was a symbol of God. So the more light, the closer to heaven.

[pause]

In France, the great Gothic cathedrals are rising at this time. Notre-Dame in Paris. Chartres, with its famous blue windows. Reims, where the kings of France are crowned. Amiens, the tallest of them all. And in England, the builders are learning the new style, and making it their own. They build in a slightly simpler, more elegant way, which is called Early English Gothic. And Salisbury is its most perfect example.

[long-pause]

{img:villagers-donating-coins-chest-cathedral-building-fund-priest-blessing}
Building a cathedral costs an enormous amount of money. Where does it come from? The bishop gives money from his own income. The priests of the cathedral give a part of their income, every year. The king gives timber from his forests, and gifts of money. Rich nobles and their wives give money, hoping for prayers for their souls. And ordinary people give too. Pennies, eggs, chickens, a day's work. Preachers travel around the diocese, asking for gifts for the new cathedral.

[pause]

For many people, giving to the cathedral is a way of giving to God. They believe it will help them in this life, and in the next. And they are proud of their cathedral. It belongs to everyone in the diocese, rich and poor. It is the biggest and most beautiful thing that any of them will ever see. It will be there for their children, and their grandchildren, and for hundreds of years after that.

[pause]

{img:cathedral-silhouette-sunset-town-around-it-tallest-building-medieval}
A cathedral is also the heart of a town. It brings pilgrims, visitors and trade. It needs workers, and workers need houses, food and clothes. Around a new cathedral, a new town grows. Markets, inns, shops and workshops. And so, in the valley of the five rivers, a whole new city is being born, around the great church.

[long-pause]

For the builders, building a cathedral is the work of a lifetime. Many of the men who began the work in 1220 will never see it finished. Their sons will continue it. And their sons may continue it after them. It requires great patience, and great faith. Each man knows that he is only one small part of a great work, much bigger than himself. And he does his part as well as he can, stone by stone, day by day.

[long-pause]

{img:stonemason-family-medieval-house-new-sarum-father-mother-children-doorway}
In the new town around the cathedral, on a street called Blue Boar Row, there lives the family of a stonemason. The father is called Walter. He is about forty years old, with a short grey beard, broad shoulders and big, strong hands, covered in little white scars from the chisel. He has worked on the cathedral for twenty years, since he was a young man. He came from a village near Bristol, where his father was a mason too.

[long-pause]

Walter is a skilled mason. He does not just cut plain blocks of stone. He carves the delicate parts. The capitals on top of the columns, with their curling leaves. The mouldings around the arches. The little carved heads that look down from the walls. He is one of the best carvers on the site. The master mason trusts him with the most difficult work. And he is paid well, for a craftsman. Four pence a day, in summer.

[pause]

His wife is called Margery. She is a baker. She bakes bread in a big oven at the back of the house, and sells it to the workers on the building site, and to the people of the town. Every morning, before dawn, she is up, kneading dough and lighting the oven. By the time the workers arrive at the cathedral, she is there with her basket of warm loaves. She knows all the workers by name. And she knows all the gossip of the site.

[pause]

{img:baker-mother-basket-warm-bread-loaves-building-site-workers-buying-dawn}
They have two children. The son is called Tom. He is thirteen years old. He is tall for his age, quiet and thoughtful, with his father's big hands. Since he was a little boy, he has watched his father carve stone. He has played on the edges of the building site, among the piles of stone and the wooden scaffolding. He has made little carvings of his own, from the leftover pieces, with an old chisel. And this spring, he is starting his apprenticeship, to become a mason himself.

[pause]

The daughter is called Joan. She is nine. She helps her mother with the bread, and takes the leftover crusts to feed the ducks on the river. She is cheerful and chatty, and she has a little dog, called Pip, who follows her everywhere. Joan is not very interested in stone. She thinks it is boring and dusty. She prefers the river, and the water meadows, and the flowers that grow there.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-town-house-interior-family-supper-bread-pottage-candle-evening}
The family's house is new, like all the houses in the town. It is made of a timber frame, filled with plaster, with a thatched roof. It has two rooms downstairs, and one upstairs, under the roof. At the back, there is a yard, with the bake oven, a little garden, a few chickens and a pig. And at the front of the house, there is a channel of clear water, running along the street. Because in the new town, water runs through all the streets.

[pause]

In the evenings, the family eats supper together. Bread, of course, from Margery's oven. A thick pottage of beans and vegetables, with a little bacon. Cheese. And weak ale, which Margery brews herself. Walter talks about the work at the cathedral. Margery tells the gossip. Joan tells about the ducks. And Tom listens to his father, and asks him questions about stone, about carving, and about the master mason, until his mother tells him to eat his supper.

[pause]

{img:father-showing-son-small-carved-stone-leaf-candlelight-proud}
After supper, Walter sometimes takes out a little piece of stone, and a small chisel, and shows Tom how to carve. A leaf. A flower. A little face. His hands move slowly and carefully, and the stone seems to come alive. Tom watches every movement. And then he tries himself, on another piece. His leaves are still clumsy. But they are getting better.

[long-pause]

On the night before his apprenticeship begins, Tom cannot sleep. He lies on his straw mattress, under the roof, and listens to the sound of the water flowing in the channel outside. Tomorrow, he will go to the cathedral, not as a boy who plays among the stones, but as an apprentice. He will be given his own tools. He will work in the lodge with the masons. And one day, perhaps, he will carve stones that will be there for hundreds of years.

[long-pause]

{img:new-sarum-1250-grid-streets-water-channels-timber-houses-cathedral-behind}
The town around the cathedral is very new. Thirty years ago, there was nothing here but meadows and rivers. Now, it is a busy town, with hundreds of houses and more than a thousand people. And it is a very unusual town. Because it was planned from the very beginning, by the bishop and his officials. Most medieval towns grew slowly, over centuries, with crooked, winding streets. But this town was laid out on purpose, in a neat pattern of straight streets, crossing each other at right angles, like a chessboard.

[long-pause]

The blocks of land between the streets are called chequers, because they look like the squares of a chessboard. Each chequer is divided into plots, which the bishop rents out to people who want to build a house. The plots are long and narrow, with the house at the front, on the street, and a garden or a yard at the back. Merchants, craftsmen, innkeepers and workers have come from all over the region to live here.

[pause]

The most remarkable thing about the new town is the water. The engineers have dug channels from the rivers, and led them through the streets. So along almost every street, there is a little stream of clear water, flowing in a stone-lined channel. People use the water for washing, for their animals and for their gardens. Visitors call the town the Venice of England. The children play in the channels in summer, and sail little boats made of wood and leaves.

[pause]

{img:children-sailing-toy-boats-water-channel-medieval-street-summer}
In the middle of the town, there is a big open market place. Every Tuesday, there is a market, and the square is full of stalls. Farmers from the villages around bring their grain, vegetables, cheese, butter, eggs, chickens, sheep and pigs. There are stalls selling cloth, leather, pots and tools. There are butchers, fishmongers and bakers. And once a year, there is a great fair, with merchants from far away, and entertainers, and crowds of people.

[pause]

Around the cathedral itself, there is a large, quiet, green space, surrounded by a wall. It is called the Close. Inside the Close live the priests of the cathedral, called canons, in fine stone houses with gardens. The Close is peaceful and calm, with lawns and old trees. The gates are locked at night. It is like a little separate town, for the church, inside the bigger town.

[long-pause]

{img:cathedral-close-green-lawn-canons-houses-stone-wall-gate-quiet}
The town is full of people who work for the cathedral, or because of it. There are the masons and the carpenters, the glaziers and the plumbers, who work on the building. There are the carters, who bring the stone and the timber. There are the inns, where visitors and pilgrims stay. There are the smiths, who make and mend tools for the builders. And there are the bakers, the brewers, the butchers and the cooks, who feed them all.

[pause]

The town is also becoming famous for something else. Wool, and woollen cloth. The chalk hills around Salisbury are covered with huge flocks of sheep. Their wool is some of the best in England. The weavers and dyers of the town make it into cloth, which is sold far and wide. Slowly, the town is growing rich. And part of that wealth flows into the building of the cathedral.

[pause]

{img:sheep-flocks-chalk-downs-shepherd-wiltshire-hills-summer-sky}
For Tom and Joan, the town is home. They know every street, every channel, every shop. They know the baker on the next corner, who is their mother's rival, and the blacksmith, who lets Tom work the bellows, and the old woman who sells honey cakes at the market. They know which channels have the best water for sailing boats, and which ones have eels in them. And they know the way to the cathedral by heart, because they walk there every day.

[long-pause]

In the evening, from the bridge over the river, you can see the whole town spread out. The rows of thatched roofs, the smoke rising from the chimneys, the channels of water shining in the sunset. And in the middle, rising above everything, the great grey cathedral, with its scaffolding, glowing pink in the last light. It is a young town, full of hope, built around a young cathedral. And everyone in it is proud of both.

[long-pause]

{img:chilmark-stone-quarry-medieval-quarrymen-cutting-limestone-blocks-wedges}
Every stone in Salisbury Cathedral had to come from somewhere. Most of them came from a quarry about twenty kilometres away, near a village called Chilmark. There, under the green hills, there is a layer of fine limestone. It is pale grey, with a hint of green. It is hard and strong, but not too hard to carve. It is perfect for building. And for thirty years, the quarrymen of Chilmark have been cutting it out of the ground, for the cathedral.

[long-pause]

Tom's first task as an apprentice is to go to the quarry with his father, to choose stone for the carvings that Walter is working on. They travel there on an empty cart, pulled by oxen, early in the morning. The road winds through the green valleys and over the chalk hills, past villages and sheep farms. It takes almost the whole morning. And then they come to the quarry.

[pause]

The quarry is a big hole in the side of a hill, with steep walls of grey stone. Some of it is cut into the hillside, like a cave, with tunnels going deep underground, following the best layer of stone. Dozens of men are working there. Quarrymen, with picks, wedges and hammers. They cut long channels around a big block of stone. Then they drive iron wedges into the channels, and hammer them, until the block splits away from the rock.

[pause]

{img:quarry-tunnel-underground-lantern-light-men-working-stone-face}
Cutting stone is hard and skilful work. The quarrymen must know exactly how the stone lies, where the natural cracks are, and which way it will split. If they make a mistake, a block can crack in the wrong place, and a whole day's work is wasted. The best quarrymen can look at a rock face and tell you, just by looking, where the good stone is, and how to cut it.

[pause]

Some of the blocks are roughly shaped at the quarry, to make them lighter for the journey. This saves a lot of work. The master mason sends templates to the quarry. These are patterns, made of thin wood, showing the exact shape that a stone must have. The quarrymen cut the blocks to fit the templates, roughly, leaving a little extra stone, for the masons at the cathedral to carve precisely.

[long-pause]

{img:ox-cart-loaded-stone-block-chalk-road-sheep-downs-slow-journey}
Moving the stone is a huge job. A single block can weigh a tonne or more. It is lifted onto a strong wooden cart with a crane, and pulled by a team of oxen, slowly, along the road to Salisbury. A cart can only carry one or two blocks. And the journey takes a whole day. Every day, many carts are on the road between Chilmark and Salisbury, carrying stone. In total, the cathedral needs about sixty thousand tonnes of stone.

[pause]

Walter talks with the master of the quarry, and they look at many blocks together. Walter taps them with a little hammer, and listens to the sound. A good block rings clearly. A block with a hidden crack sounds dull. He chooses three blocks for his carvings. Tom watches and listens, and tries to learn. Walter lets him tap a block himself. It rings like a bell. Tom smiles.

[pause]

{img:father-son-tapping-stone-block-listening-quarry-afternoon-light}
They eat their lunch with the quarrymen, sitting on blocks of stone, in the sun. Bread and cheese, onions and ale. The quarrymen are rough, friendly men, covered in grey dust from head to foot. They tell Tom stories about the quarry. About the old tunnels, which go so deep into the hill that you can get lost in them. About the fossils they find in the stone, little shells and sea creatures, turned to stone. They give Tom one of these fossils, a little curled shell. He keeps it all his life.

[long-pause]

In the evening, Tom and Walter ride home on a cart loaded with stone, very slowly, behind the oxen. The sun is setting over the hills. The sheep are going home to their folds. And far away, across the valley, Tom sees the cathedral, rising out of the meadows, pale in the evening light. He thinks that every stone in it has made this same slow journey, from the quarry, behind the oxen, in the evening light. Thousands and thousands of them. One by one.

[long-pause]

{img:master-mason-medieval-compass-tracing-floor-plaster-drawing-arches}
At the head of all the builders, there is one man. The master mason. He is the architect, the engineer and the chief builder, all in one. He designs the cathedral. He decides the shape of every arch, every column and every window. He tells the masons what to cut, and the carpenters what to build. He checks every stone. And he is responsible for making sure that the great building stands up, and does not fall down.

[long-pause]

In 1250, the master mason of Salisbury is a man called Nicholas of Ely. He came from the east of England, from the great cathedral of Ely, where he learned his trade. He has been in charge of the work at Salisbury for many years. He is about sixty years old, with white hair and a lined face. He walks slowly around the building site, every day, with his master's staff, looking at everything, and talking quietly to the masons.

[pause]

For many years, he worked together with a priest of the cathedral, a canon called Elias of Dereham. Elias was a remarkable man. He was a scholar, a priest and an expert in building. He helped to plan the cathedral from the very beginning, together with the bishop. He passed away a few years ago, in 1245. But together, Elias and Nicholas created the design of the whole cathedral.

[pause]

{img:tracing-floor-loft-plaster-floor-full-size-window-drawing-compass-lines}
How does a master mason design a cathedral, without paper, without computers and without modern mathematics? He uses geometry. Simple shapes, like circles, squares and triangles, drawn with a compass and a straight rule. Every part of the cathedral is based on these shapes. The size of one part decides the size of the next. Everything fits together in perfect proportion, like music. The masons call this sacred geometry.

[pause]

High up in a room in the masons' workshop, there is a special floor, made of smooth plaster. It is called the tracing floor. Here, Nicholas draws the shapes of the parts of the building at their full size. A whole window. An arch. The shape of a column. He draws them with a big compass and a long straight edge, scratching the lines into the plaster. Then the carpenters make wooden templates from these drawings, which the masons use to cut the stones. When the drawings are no longer needed, the floor is covered with fresh plaster, and Nicholas draws again.

[long-pause]

{img:master-mason-checking-stone-with-square-and-plumb-line-mason-lodge}
Nicholas does not do the hard work of cutting stone himself any more. But he knows how to do every job on the site. He started as an apprentice, like Tom, at the age of thirteen. He spent years cutting and carving stone. He travelled to other cathedrals, to learn from other masters. He studied the great churches of France. And slowly, he rose to become a master. It is the highest position that a craftsman can reach.

[pause]

He is paid very well. Much more than the ordinary masons. He has a house in the town, and receives a fine robe every year, and a fur for winter. He eats at the table of the canons. He is a respected man. But he also carries a great responsibility. If a wall cracks, or an arch falls, it is his fault. He thinks about the building day and night. He even dreams about it.

[pause]

{img:old-master-mason-walking-site-tom-apprentice-listening-respect}
One day, Nicholas stops at the workshop where Tom is sweeping up the stone dust. He looks at the boy for a while. Then he asks him, gently, if he knows why the arches of the cathedral are pointed, and not round. Tom says that he does not know. So Nicholas picks up a piece of chalk, and draws on a flat stone. He draws two circles, overlapping. And where they cross, there is a pointed arch. He explains that the pointed arch pushes its weight downward, more than sideways, so it is stronger. Tom looks at the drawing, and suddenly understands.

[long-pause]

Nicholas smiles, and says that every great builder starts by asking why. Then he walks on, slowly, with his staff. Tom keeps the stone with the chalk drawing for a long time. He looks at it every day. And whenever he walks through the cathedral, and looks up at the high pointed arches, he thinks of two circles, overlapping, and the old master's quiet voice, explaining.

[long-pause]

{img:masons-lodge-long-timber-workshop-bankers-stone-blocks-masons-working-dust}
The centre of the stonemasons' work is the lodge. It is a long wooden building, with a thatched roof, beside the cathedral, on the south side. It is open at the front, so that the light can come in. Inside, there are rows of benches, called bankers, made of strong timber, on which the stones are laid while they are being worked. And at each banker, there is a mason, chipping, carving and shaping stone.

[long-pause]

The lodge is noisy and dusty. The sound of hammers and chisels on stone fills the air all day long. Tink, tink, tink. A fine white dust covers everything. The masons' clothes, their hair, their eyebrows, the floor and the benches. The masons wear leather aprons and caps to protect themselves. And they talk and joke as they work, in the accents of many different parts of England, and some from France.

[pause]

The masons use simple tools. A mallet, made of wood, and an axe for rough shaping. Chisels of many different shapes and sizes, for different jobs. Flat chisels, pointed chisels, toothed chisels, called claws, and small gouges for carving. A square, to check that the corners are exactly at right angles. A straight edge. A compass. And the wooden templates, made from the master's drawings.

[pause]

{img:mason-hands-chisel-mallet-carving-stone-moulding-close-detail}
There are different kinds of masons. The rough masons, who shape the plain blocks for the walls. The freemasons, who carve the fine work, the mouldings, the window tracery, the capitals and the decorations. They are called freemasons because they work in freestone, a fine stone that can be carved freely in any direction. Walter is a freemason. And there are the setters, who work on the walls and the scaffolding, laying the stones in place with mortar.

[pause]

Each mason has his own mark. A simple sign, like a little arrow, a star, a cross or a letter. He carves it into the side of every stone he finishes. So the master knows who made each stone, and how many stones each mason has cut. The masons are paid for their work, and a mason who makes bad stones will be found out. Walter's mark is a little shape like a bird's foot. Today, you can still find masons' marks on the stones of Salisbury Cathedral, if you look closely.

[long-pause]

{img:masons-marks-carved-symbols-on-cathedral-stone-wall-close-arrows-crosses}
The lodge is also like a club, or a family. The masons have their own rules, and their own customs. They eat their meals together in the lodge. They help each other when they are sick or hurt. They hold feasts on holy days. They have secret signs and words, which only masons know, so that they can recognise each other when they travel to other building sites. Masons often travel from one cathedral to another, all over England and even to France, wherever there is work.

[pause]

The masons are proud of their skill. And they have a sense of humour. Sometimes, high up on the cathedral, where nobody will ever see them, they carve funny little faces. A man pulling a face. A woman with a headache. A monkey. A dragon eating its own tail. Some people think these are portraits of people they knew, perhaps their master, or a priest they did not like. The faces are still there today, looking down from the high walls.

[pause]

{img:carved-stone-head-corbel-funny-face-high-on-cathedral-wall}
Walter is carving a capital, the decorated top of a column. It is shaped like a bell, with curling leaves around it, called stiff leaf, which is the fashion of the time. The leaves look almost alive, curling over at their tips, as if blown by the wind. He works slowly and carefully, a little at a time, for weeks. He checks the shape with his templates. He smooths the surfaces. And slowly, out of the rough grey block, the leaves appear.

[long-pause]

At the end of the day, when the bell rings, the masons put down their tools, brush off the dust, and go home. The lodge is quiet. The half-finished stones lie on the bankers, waiting for the morning. The evening sun shines in through the open front, lighting up the floating dust, like gold. And the curling leaves of Walter's capital catch the light, and seem, for a moment, almost to move.

[long-pause]

{img:apprentice-boy-leather-apron-sweeping-stone-dust-lodge-masons-working}
Tom's apprenticeship will last seven years. During that time, he will work for his father and the other masons, and learn everything about stone. He will not be paid much, only a few pennies, and his food. But at the end, if he has learned well, he will be a mason himself. He will be able to work anywhere in England, on any church, castle or bridge. It is a good trade, and an honourable one.

[long-pause]

At first, Tom does the simplest jobs. He sweeps the dust from the floor of the lodge. He carries water. He fetches tools. He sharpens the chisels on a grinding stone, which is turned by a handle, while he holds the chisel against it, with water dripping on the stone to keep it cool. Sharpening chisels is very important. A blunt chisel can spoil a stone. Tom learns to make them sharp enough to shave the hair on his arm.

[pause]

Then he learns to work stone himself. His father gives him a rough block, and shows him how to make one side of it flat. You hold the chisel at a certain angle, and strike it with the mallet, again and again, always in the same direction, removing a thin layer of stone each time. Then you check the surface with a straight edge, to see where it is still too high. And you work on those parts, until the whole surface is perfectly flat.

[pause]

{img:boy-carving-first-flat-stone-father-guiding-hands-chisel-angle}
It takes Tom three days to make one side of his first block flat. And when his father checks it with the straight edge, there is still a little gap in the middle. He must do it again. It is frustrating. But Walter is patient. He says that he took a whole week, with his first stone, when he was Tom's age. And that the old master he worked for made him do it four times, before he was satisfied.

[pause]

Slowly, Tom gets better. After a few months, he can make a flat surface in a day. Then he learns to make a square corner, and then a simple moulding, a long, curved shape along the edge of a stone. He learns to use the templates, and to check his work with the square and the compass. He learns which chisels to use for which jobs. And he learns to read the stone, to see the grain in it, and to know how it will split.

[long-pause]

{img:apprentice-practising-carving-leaf-small-stone-candle-evening-home}
He also learns other things. How to measure. How to use simple geometry, to draw shapes with a compass and a straight edge. How to lift and carry heavy stones safely, without hurting his back. How to mix mortar. How to work on the scaffolding, high above the ground, without being afraid. And how to behave in the lodge, with the other masons. To be polite, to work hard, and to keep the secrets of the craft.

[pause]

In the evenings, at home, Tom practises carving. His father gives him small pieces of leftover stone. And by the light of a candle, he tries to carve a leaf, like the ones on his father's capital. At first, his leaves are stiff and lumpy. But slowly, they begin to curl. Joan says that they look like cabbages. Tom throws a pillow at her. But secretly, he agrees.

[pause]

{img:apprentices-together-lunch-break-lodge-bread-ale-laughing-dust}
There are other apprentices in the lodge. Boys of thirteen, fourteen and fifteen, from different parts of England. Tom becomes friends with them. There is Hugh, a cheerful boy from Wells, where there is another great cathedral. And Robin, a quiet boy from a village nearby, whose father was a carpenter on the cathedral roof. The boys eat their lunch together, and talk about stone, and girls, and the masters, and what they will build when they are grown up.

[long-pause]

At the end of his first year, the master mason, Nicholas, comes to see Tom's work. He looks at the flat stones, the square corners and the simple mouldings. He runs his fingers over them, and checks them with his square. Then he nods, and says that the boy will make a good mason. Tom's father says nothing. But that evening, at supper, he gives Tom a present. A new set of chisels, of the best steel, in a leather roll. Tom holds them as if they were made of gold.

[long-pause]

{img:great-wheel-treadwheel-crane-medieval-men-walking-inside-rope-lifting-block}
How do you lift a block of stone, weighing a tonne, to the top of a wall thirty metres high? Without engines, without electricity, without steel cables? The medieval builders had a clever answer. The great wheel. A huge wooden wheel, taller than a house, turned by people walking inside it, like a hamster in a wheel. It is one of the most remarkable machines of the Middle Ages.

[long-pause]

The great wheel stands on a wooden platform, on top of the walls or the scaffolding. It is about four or five metres high, made of oak, with a wide floor of planks inside the rim. One or two men walk inside the wheel, on the planks, step by step, like walking up a never-ending staircase. As they walk, the wheel turns. And as the wheel turns, it winds a strong hemp rope around its axle. The rope goes up over a long wooden arm, and down to the ground, where the stone is tied to it.

[pause]

The wheel gives the men great strength. Two men walking in a wheel can lift a stone that twenty men could not lift with their arms. Slowly, slowly, the stone rises into the air, swinging a little, higher and higher, until it reaches the top of the wall. There, the setters swing it onto the wall, and lower it into its place, on a bed of mortar. Then the rope is lowered again, for the next stone.

[pause]

{img:wooden-scaffolding-poles-lashed-ropes-hurdles-masons-high-on-wall}
The walls are surrounded by scaffolding. It is made of long wooden poles, tied together with ropes. Some of the poles are set into holes in the walls, called putlog holes. The platforms are made of woven hurdles, like big flat baskets, made of willow or hazel branches. They are light and strong. The workers walk on them high above the ground, carrying tools and mortar. You can still see the putlog holes in the walls of many old cathedrals today.

[pause]

Mortar holds the stones together. It is made of lime, sand and water. The lime comes from burning chalk or limestone in a kiln, at a very high heat, until it turns into a white powder. The mortar men mix it with sand and water in big wooden troughs, with hoes, until it is smooth and thick, like porridge. Then they carry it up the ladders in wooden buckets, or it is lifted up by the great wheel. It sets slowly, over weeks and months, and becomes as hard as stone.

[long-pause]

{img:wooden-centering-arch-frame-masons-placing-voussoirs-vault-high}
To build an arch, the carpenters first make a wooden frame, in the exact shape of the arch. It is called centring. The masons lay the wedge-shaped stones of the arch on top of the wooden frame, one by one, from both sides, until they meet at the top. The last stone, at the very top, is called the keystone. When it is in place, the arch holds itself up. Then, carefully, the wooden frame is taken away. And the arch stands, by itself, for centuries.

[pause]

The vaults, the stone ceilings high above, are built in the same way, on wooden frames. They are made of thin stone ribs, which cross each other, and thin stone panels between them. It is very delicate work. The vault must be strong enough to hold itself up, but light enough not to push the walls outward. The master mason calculates everything carefully. And outside the walls, he builds buttresses, strong stone supports, to hold the walls in place.

[pause]

{img:boy-looking-up-at-new-stone-vault-ribs-high-above-light-awe}
Tom likes to watch the great wheel. Sometimes, he is allowed to walk inside it, with an older worker. It is hard work. Step after step, after step, and the wheel turns slowly, and somewhere far above, a stone is rising into the air. You cannot see it from inside the wheel. You just have to walk, and trust the men at the top. Tom thinks it is a little like building the cathedral itself. Everyone does their part, without seeing the whole.

[long-pause]

Accidents sometimes happen. A rope breaks, a scaffold gives way, a worker slips. Building a cathedral is dangerous work. The masons are careful, and they say a prayer each morning, before they climb. But the great wheel at Salisbury has worked for thirty years, carrying thousands of stones, one by one, up into the sky. And in the cathedral of Salisbury, high up in the tower, there is still an old wooden wheel today, which was used in the Middle Ages. It has not turned for hundreds of years. But it is still there, waiting.

[long-pause]

{img:purbeck-marble-dark-polished-shafts-columns-pale-stone-cathedral-interior}
When you walk inside Salisbury Cathedral, one of the first things you notice is the columns. They are made of two kinds of stone. The main parts are pale grey, from Chilmark. But around them, there are thin, dark, shining shafts, almost black, polished until they gleam like glass. These dark shafts are made of a very special stone. It is called Purbeck marble.

[long-pause]

Purbeck marble is not really marble. It is a kind of limestone, full of tiny fossil shells of little snails that lived in the water, millions of years ago. It comes from a small peninsula on the coast of Dorset, called the Isle of Purbeck, about sixty kilometres from Salisbury. When it is first cut, it is a dull grey-brown. But when it is polished, for many hours, it becomes dark, glossy and beautiful, with tiny white shapes of shells inside it.

[pause]

In the thirteenth century, Purbeck marble is very fashionable. It is used in all the great churches of England. For columns, for tombs, for fonts and for decorations. The dark shining stone makes a beautiful contrast with the pale limestone. And it gives the inside of the cathedral a special feeling of richness and elegance. The master masons love it.

[pause]

{img:purbeck-coast-quarry-dorset-sea-cliffs-workers-cutting-dark-stone}
On the Isle of Purbeck, there is a whole village of workers who quarry and polish the marble. They are called marblers. They cut the stone from the quarries, near the sea. Then they shape it into long, thin columns, round and straight. And then they polish it. They rub it with sand and water, and then with finer and finer powders, for hours and hours, until it shines. It is slow, patient work. Some of the marblers spend their whole lives polishing columns.

[pause]

The finished columns are carried to Salisbury. Sometimes by boat, along the coast, and then up the river. Sometimes by cart, over the hills. They must be very carefully packed in straw, because the polished surface scratches easily, and the long, thin columns can break. When they arrive at the cathedral, the masons fit them around the main columns, holding them in place with rings of bronze or stone.

[long-pause]

{img:marbler-polishing-dark-column-sand-water-cloth-workshop-shine}
One day in summer, Walter takes Tom with him on a journey to Purbeck, to choose some marble for a new part of the cathedral. They travel south, through the green valleys and over the chalk hills, for two days. And then, from the top of a hill, Tom sees the sea for the first time in his life. It is enormous, blue and shining, stretching away to the horizon. He stands and stares, with his mouth open, until his father laughs and pulls him on.

[pause]

They visit the workshops of the marblers, in a village near the coast. The air smells of the sea and wet stone. Men are sitting in the sun, polishing long columns, rubbing and rubbing. The columns gleam dark and wet. Walter talks with the master marbler, and they choose the columns together, looking at each one carefully, checking for cracks and flaws.

[pause]

{img:boy-seeing-sea-first-time-hilltop-purbeck-blue-water-father-behind}
In the evening, Walter and Tom walk down to the beach. Tom has never seen waves before. He takes off his shoes, and walks into the water, and it is cold, and salty, and moves around his feet like something alive. He picks up shells and smooth pebbles on the beach. He finds a stone with a fossil in it, like the ones in the marble, and puts it in his pocket. The sun sets over the sea. And Tom thinks it is the most beautiful evening of his life.

[long-pause]

Back in Salisbury, months later, Tom watches the dark marble columns being fitted into the cathedral. He runs his hand over the cool, smooth stone, and sees the tiny white shells inside it. Little snails that lived in the water, millions of years ago, long before there were people. And now they are part of a cathedral. He thinks about that for a long time. The world is very old, he thinks. And very strange. And very beautiful.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-carpenters-building-roof-trusses-oak-beams-high-on-cathedral-walls}
On top of the stone vaults, there is a great roof. It is made of wood, and covered with lead. It protects the stone vaults below from the rain and the snow. And building it is the work of the carpenters, who are just as skilled as the masons, and just as important.

[long-pause]

The roof of Salisbury Cathedral is made of oak. Huge oak beams, from great old trees, some of them hundreds of years old. The king himself gave many of the trees, from his royal forests. The carpenters go into the forests, choose the trees, and cut them down with axes. They shape the beams roughly in the forest, while the wood is still green and soft. Then the beams are carried to Salisbury, on carts pulled by teams of oxen.

[pause]

The roof is made of many triangular frames, called trusses, standing in a long row, from one end of the building to the other, like the ribs of a great animal. The carpenters build each truss on the ground first, fitting the beams together with joints, cut precisely with chisels, and fixed with wooden pegs, not nails. Then they take it apart, carry the pieces up onto the walls, and put it together again, up there, high in the air.

[pause]

{img:carpenters-yard-cutting-oak-joints-axes-adzes-timber-framing-ground}
The carpenters mark every piece of wood with special signs, cut into the wood with a chisel, so that they know which piece goes where. These are called carpenters' marks. Like the masons' marks, they can still be seen today, in the roof of Salisbury Cathedral. Up there, in the dark, dusty roof space, the great oak beams still stand, with their marks, just as the carpenters left them, almost eight hundred years ago.

[pause]

Tom's friend Robin is the son of one of the carpenters. Sometimes, he takes Tom up into the roof, where his father is working. They climb up long ladders, inside the walls, and come out onto the top of the stone vaults, under the half-built roof. It is like being inside the skeleton of a giant. Great beams rise up all around them, into the darkness. The smell of fresh oak is everywhere. And through the gaps in the roof, they can see the sky.

[long-pause]

{img:plumber-laying-lead-sheets-on-cathedral-roof-fire-melting-pot-sky}
When the timber roof is finished, it is covered with lead. Lead is a soft, heavy, grey metal, which does not rust, and keeps out the water perfectly. The men who work with lead are called plumbers, from the Latin word for lead, plumbum. They melt the lead in big pots over fires, on the ground. Then they pour it onto a bed of sand, to make flat sheets. And then they carry the sheets up onto the roof, and lay them over the timber, overlapping, so that the rain runs off.

[pause]

The plumbers also make the gutters, and the pipes that carry the rainwater away from the roof. At the ends of the pipes, the masons carve stone figures, with open mouths, which spit the water out, away from the walls. These are called gargoyles. The word comes from an old French word for throat. Some gargoyles look like monsters, some like animals, and some like funny people. When it rains, they gurgle and spit.

[pause]

{img:view-from-cathedral-roof-medieval-town-meadows-rivers-boys-sitting}
Tom and Robin love to sit on the roof, high above the town, when the workers are having their lunch. From up there, they can see everything. The whole new town, with its chequer streets and its water channels. The market place, full of tiny people. The meadows and the rivers, shining in the sun. The road to Old Sarum, and the old hill in the distance. And beyond, the green chalk hills, stretching away to the horizon, dotted with white sheep.

[long-pause]

Carpenters are needed for everything on the building site. The scaffolding, the centring for the arches, the great wheels for lifting, the workshops, the carts, the ladders, the doors. Without them, nothing could be built. Robin's father says that a cathedral is built of stone, but stands on wood. And when Tom looks at all the scaffolding around the walls, and all the wooden frames and machines, he thinks that Robin's father is right.

[long-pause]

{img:medieval-glazier-workshop-cutting-glass-pieces-hot-iron-table-drawing}
A cathedral needs windows. Big, tall windows, to let in the light. And in the Middle Ages, the windows of a cathedral are works of art. They are made of hundreds of pieces of coloured glass, held together with strips of lead, making pictures and patterns that glow when the sun shines through them. The craftsmen who make them are called glaziers.

[long-pause]

Glass is made by melting sand, with ash from burned plants or wood, in a very hot furnace. The glassmakers work in the forests, where there is plenty of wood for their furnaces. They blow the hot, liquid glass into a big bubble, with a long iron pipe. Then they cut the bubble open, and flatten it into a sheet. To make coloured glass, they add metals to the hot glass. Copper makes red or green. Cobalt makes a deep blue. Iron makes yellow and brown.

[pause]

The glaziers buy the sheets of glass, and make the windows in their workshop, near the cathedral. First, the master glazier draws the design of the window, full size, on a big wooden table, painted white. Then he lays the pieces of glass on the drawing, and cuts them to the right shapes. There are no diamond glass cutters yet. Instead, he uses a hot iron, drawing it along the glass, so that it cracks along the line. Then he trims the edges with a special iron tool, called a grozing iron, nibbling the glass into shape.

[pause]

{img:grisaille-window-pale-silvery-glass-leaf-patterns-light-pouring-in}
Then the glazier paints details onto the glass. Faces, hands, folds of clothes, leaves and patterns. He uses a brown paint, made of ground glass and iron. Then the glass is fired in a small oven, so that the paint melts into the glass and becomes permanent. Then the pieces are fitted together with strips of lead, shaped like the letter H in cross-section, so that the glass slides into the grooves on each side. The lead is soldered at the joints. And the window is ready.

[pause]

At Salisbury, most of the windows are made in a special style, called grisaille, which means greyish. Instead of being full of strong colours, they are made mostly of pale, silvery-white glass, with delicate patterns of leaves and stems painted on them in grey. Just a few small pieces of colour, red, blue or yellow, are added here and there. The grisaille windows let in much more light than the dark coloured windows of other cathedrals. They fill the church with a soft, silvery light, like morning.

[long-pause]

{img:colourful-stained-glass-window-saints-deep-blue-red-sunlight-through}
There are also some windows with rich colours, showing stories from the Bible, and the lives of the saints. The most important ones are at the east end of the cathedral, above the altar, where the sun rises. In the early morning, the sun shines through them, and throws pools of coloured light, red, blue and gold, onto the stone floor and the columns. It looks like jewels scattered across the church.

[pause]

Joan, Tom's sister, is not very interested in stone. But she is very interested in glass. She loves the colours. She spends hours watching the glaziers at work, in their workshop, from the doorway. One of them, an old man called Master Ralph, notices her. He lets her come in, and shows her how the glass is cut, and painted, and fitted together. He gives her little pieces of broken coloured glass, red and blue and green, to play with.

[pause]

{img:girl-holding-coloured-glass-pieces-to-sunlight-glazier-workshop-smiling}
Joan holds the pieces of glass up to the sun, and looks at the world through them. Through the red one, everything is rosy. Through the blue one, everything is cool and calm, like under the sea. Through the green one, everything looks like a forest. She makes a little window of her own, from the broken pieces, stuck together with clay, and puts it in the window of her bedroom, at home. When the morning sun shines through it, her room is full of coloured light.

[long-pause]

Many years later, when Joan is grown up, she will marry a glazier, the son of Master Ralph. And she will help him in the workshop, cutting and painting glass, for the cathedral and for other churches. Women sometimes worked in the family workshops, in the Middle Ages, alongside their husbands. And perhaps, somewhere in the windows of Salisbury, there is still a little piece of glass, cut and painted by a girl who once looked at the world through a broken red pane.

[long-pause]

{img:building-site-dawn-workers-arriving-bell-tower-wooden-cathedral-scaffold}
A day on the building site begins at dawn. In summer, that is very early, about four or five o'clock in the morning. A bell rings, in the little wooden bell tower beside the cathedral. And from all over the town, the workers come walking, with their tools and their bags of bread. Masons, carpenters, labourers, carters, mortar men and boys. Hundreds of them.

[long-pause]

The workers are paid by the day. The skilled masons and carpenters are paid more. The labourers, who carry, dig and mix, are paid less. In summer, when the days are long, they work about twelve hours. In winter, when the days are short, they work less, and are paid less. And in the coldest months, the building stops. Because mortar cannot be used in frost. It freezes, and does not set properly.

[pause]

So in winter, the walls are covered with straw and thatch, to protect them from the frost and the rain. And the masons work inside the lodge, carving and shaping stones, ready for the spring. The lodge is warm, with a fire, and the masons can work there all winter. When spring comes, they have a great pile of finished stones, ready to be laid on the walls.

[pause]

{img:masons-lunch-break-sitting-on-stones-bread-cheese-ale-sunshine-cathedral}
During the day, there are breaks. At about nine o'clock, a short break for breakfast. At noon, a longer break for dinner, when the workers sit in the shade, and eat bread, cheese, onions and cold meat, and drink ale. Margery comes with her basket of bread, and sells it to the workers. In the hot summer afternoons, there is sometimes a short rest, a nap in the shade, before the work starts again.

[pause]

There are also many holidays. In the Middle Ages, there are dozens of holy days in the year, the feast days of the saints, when nobody works. Easter, Whitsun, Christmas, and many more. On these days, the workers go to church, and then have the rest of the day free. There are games, fairs, feasts and dancing. So, in a year, a mason works for about two hundred and fifty days, not every day.

[long-pause]

{img:workers-leaving-site-evening-sunset-cathedral-glowing-tools-shoulders}
The building site is busy and noisy all day. The ring of the hammers on the stones. The creak of the great wheels. The shouts of the men on the scaffolding. The rumble of the carts arriving with stone. The bang of the carpenters' axes. The hiss of the mortar being mixed. Dogs barking. Children playing at the edges. And over everything, the bells of the cathedral, ringing for the services, which go on in the finished part of the church, even while the building continues.

[pause]

Yes, services go on. The eastern part of the cathedral has been finished for many years. And every day, the priests sing their prayers there, seven times a day, and say Mass. So while the masons hammer and the carpenters saw at the western end, the priests sing quietly at the eastern end. Sometimes, the sound of the singing drifts out over the building site, and the workers stop for a moment, and listen.

[pause]

{img:worker-pausing-listening-choir-singing-through-cathedral-doors-dust}
At the end of the day, the bell rings again. The workers put down their tools. They brush off the dust. They climb down from the scaffolding. And they walk home, tired, through the evening streets, to their families and their suppers. The building site becomes quiet. Only a watchman stays, to guard the tools and the materials through the night.

[long-pause]

Tom walks home with his father, in the evening light. His arms ache from the chisel. His hands are covered in white dust. But he is happy. He has worked a whole day, with the masons, on the cathedral. He looks back at the great building, glowing in the sunset, with its scaffolding and its cranes. And he thinks that today, it is a little bit taller than it was yesterday. Just a little. Because of him.

[long-pause]

{img:canons-choir-stalls-singing-psalms-candles-finished-east-end-cathedral}
The cathedral is not only a building. It is also a community of people, who pray in it every day. At its head is the bishop. And with him, there are the canons. The canons are priests, who live in the Close around the cathedral, in their fine stone houses. There are more than fifty of them. And together, they are called the chapter.

[long-pause]

The canons have a duty to pray in the cathedral, every day, at the seven hours of prayer, and to sing the Mass. But many of them are busy with other work too. Some are scholars and teachers. Some work for the king, or the bishop, as clerks and lawyers. Some live far away, and only come to Salisbury now and then. So they pay younger priests, called vicars, to sing the services in their place. The vicars live together in a house in the Close.

[pause]

There are also boys who sing in the choir. They are called choristers. They are about eight to fourteen years old, with high, clear voices. They live together in a house in the Close, with a master, who teaches them to sing, to read Latin and to behave. They sing in the cathedral every day, in white robes, with the priests. In the evenings, they sometimes play in the Close, chasing each other around the lawns, until the master calls them in.

[pause]

{img:choirboys-white-robes-walking-in-procession-cathedral-close-evening}
Salisbury Cathedral is famous for its services. The priests here have developed their own special way of celebrating the services, with its own prayers, its own music and its own processions. It is called the Use of Sarum. It is so beautiful and so well organised that many other churches in England, Wales, Scotland and Ireland copy it. For three hundred years, the Use of Sarum will be the most important way of worship in the whole of Britain.

[pause]

On Sundays and great feast days, there are processions. The priests and the choristers walk in a long line through the cathedral, and around the Close, in their rich robes, carrying crosses, candles and banners, singing. Incense smokes from silver censers. The bells ring. And the people of the town line the way, to watch. Joan loves the processions. She especially loves the banners, embroidered in gold and silk, with pictures of saints.

[long-pause]

{img:bishop-richard-poore-tomb-effigy-or-portrait-mitre-crozier-cathedral}
The bishop in 1250 is called William of York. He is the third bishop since the cathedral was founded. Bishop Richard Poore, who moved the cathedral from Old Sarum, became bishop of Durham, in the north, and passed away many years ago. But the people of Salisbury remember him, and pray for him. He is the father of their town and their cathedral.

[pause]

The bishop has a palace in the Close, a great stone house with a hall, a chapel and a garden. He is a powerful man. He owns a great deal of land, in the town and around it. He is the lord of the town. He collects rents and taxes, holds a court, and makes the rules for the market. And he looks after the churches and the priests of the whole diocese, which is very large, covering several counties.

[pause]

{img:bishop-palace-close-garden-stone-hall-lawn-trees-quiet-afternoon}
The canons and the builders work closely together. Some of the canons are in charge of the building fund, and pay the workers. They keep careful accounts. They write letters to ask for money. And they watch the building grow, with pride and impatience. Every year, they want it finished sooner. And every year, the master mason tells them that a cathedral cannot be hurried.

[long-pause]

One of the canons is an old man, who loves to watch the masons. He often comes to the lodge, and sits on a stone, watching them carve. He asks Tom about his work, and tells him stories about the old cathedral at Old Sarum, where he was a young priest, long ago, in the wind on the hill. He says that he never thought he would live to see the new cathedral. And now, he says, with a smile, he hopes he will live to see it finished.

[long-pause]

{img:magna-carta-parchment-latin-text-seal-medieval-chest-candlelight}
In the treasury of Salisbury Cathedral, in a strong wooden chest, there is a precious document. It is a sheet of parchment, written in Latin, in small, neat handwriting, by a royal clerk. It is one of the original copies of the most famous document in English history. Magna Carta. The Great Charter.

[long-pause]

Magna Carta was agreed in 1215, thirty-five years before our story, in a meadow by the river Thames, called Runnymede. King John, the brother of Richard the Lionheart, was very unpopular. He had lost wars in France. He had taken too much money from his barons, the great lords of England. He had treated people unjustly. So the barons rebelled against him. And they forced him to agree to a charter, a written promise, to respect their rights and the laws of England.

[pause]

The charter has sixty-three parts. Many of them are about the rights of the barons, the church and the towns. But some of them are about everyone. One famous part says that no free man shall be arrested or imprisoned, or have his lands taken away, except by the lawful judgement of his equals, or by the law of the land. Another says that the king will not sell, deny or delay justice to anyone. These were very important ideas. They meant that even the king had to obey the law.

[pause]

{img:runnymede-meadow-1215-king-john-barons-tents-river-thames-sealing}
After the charter was agreed, many copies, perhaps a dozen or more, were written out by royal clerks, and sent to the important places of England. Each copy was sealed with the king's great seal. Today, only four of these original copies survive. And one of them is at Salisbury. It was probably brought to Old Sarum by one of the men who had been at Runnymede, and then moved to the new cathedral.

[pause]

In fact, there is a close connection between Magna Carta and Salisbury. Elias of Dereham, the canon who helped to design the cathedral, was at Runnymede in 1215. He was one of the men who helped to make the charter, and he was given copies to take to different parts of England. And William Longespée, the Earl of Salisbury, was one of the king's advisers at Runnymede. He is buried in Salisbury Cathedral, in a beautiful tomb, with a painted stone figure of himself, in armour.

[long-pause]

{img:knight-effigy-tomb-william-longespee-painted-armour-cathedral-nave}
The charter did not work at first. King John soon broke his promises, and there was a war. But after John passed away, the next king, his son Henry the Third, issued the charter again, several times, with some changes. And slowly, it became part of the law of England. Every king had to promise to respect it. And over the centuries, its ideas about justice and freedom spread all over the world.

[pause]

Tom has heard about Magna Carta. Everyone has. One day, the old canon who likes to watch the masons takes Tom to the treasury, and shows him the charter. It is smaller than Tom expected. A single sheet of parchment, about the size of a large book, covered in tiny writing. Tom cannot read Latin. But the canon reads a few lines to him, and explains them. No free man shall be imprisoned, except by the law of the land.

[pause]

{img:old-canon-showing-boy-parchment-treasury-room-window-light-reverence}
Tom thinks about this. He is a free man's son. He will be a free mason, one day. And the law says that nobody, not even the king, can take away his freedom without a fair trial. He feels proud, and safe. He thanks the old canon. And as he walks back to the lodge, he looks up at the cathedral, rising into the sky, and thinks that it is not only the stones that are built to last.

[long-pause]

Today, the Salisbury copy of Magna Carta is still in the cathedral. It is kept in the chapter house, in a special glass case, in a dim light, to protect it. It is the best preserved of the four surviving copies. Visitors from all over the world come to see it. They bend over the glass case, and look at the tiny Latin writing, nearly eight hundred years old. And many of them are surprised by how small it is, for something so important.

[long-pause]

{img:midsummer-eve-bonfire-medieval-town-meadow-people-dancing-cathedral-silhouette}
In the middle of summer, there is a great feast. It is Midsummer, the feast of Saint John the Baptist, on the twenty-fourth of June. The days are at their longest. The nights are short and warm. And on Midsummer Eve, the people of Salisbury celebrate, all night long. The workers on the cathedral have a holiday. And everyone in the town comes out to join the fun.

[long-pause]

In the evening, the streets are decorated with green branches, birch and fennel and Saint John's wort, and with flowers, especially white lilies. Lamps and candles are hung at the doors. The houses are open, and people visit each other, and share food and ale. And in the meadows by the river, outside the town, great bonfires are lit. They are called Saint John's fires.

[pause]

The people dance around the bonfires, holding hands, singing old songs. Young people jump over the fires, for luck. Musicians play pipes, drums and fiddles. There are wrestling matches, races and games. Food is cooked over the fires. Roast meat, sausages and bread. Ale flows freely. And the sky stays light until very late, glowing pink and gold in the west, while the bonfires blaze in the meadows.

[pause]

{img:people-jumping-over-small-bonfire-laughing-summer-night-medieval-dance}
The masons have their own celebration too. They hold a feast in the lodge, with the master mason, the carpenters and their families. There is a long table, with food and ale, and the masons sing their own songs, about stone and building and the old masters. The master mason makes a little speech, thanking everyone for their work this year. And then everyone goes out to the meadows, to dance around the fires.

[pause]

Tom dances with his friends, Hugh and Robin, and with the other apprentices. And then he dances with a girl from the town, called Alice, the daughter of a cloth weaver, who has red hair and laughs at everything. Tom has noticed her at the market for a long time. He is very shy. But on Midsummer Eve, by the light of the fire, everything is easier. They dance, and talk, and laugh. And Tom thinks that Midsummer is the best night of the year.

[long-pause]

{img:mystery-play-wagon-stage-medieval-street-actors-crowd-watching-summer}
During the summer, there are also plays. Groups of craftsmen, from the different trades, perform little plays about stories from the Bible. Noah and the flood. Jonah and the whale. The birth of Jesus. They act on wagons, which are pulled from street to street, so that everyone in town can see them. The actors wear simple costumes, and the plays are full of jokes and songs, as well as the holy stories. The crowds love them.

[pause]

The masons and carpenters often play the story of Noah's ark. Because they are the builders. The carpenters build a real little ark on the wagon, with a roof and a door. Noah is played by an old carpenter with a long white beard. His wife, played by a young apprentice in a dress, refuses to get into the ark, and argues with him, while the rain comes down. The crowd roars with laughter. And then, at last, she gets in, and the animals follow, two by two, played by children in masks.

[pause]

{img:children-in-animal-masks-noahs-ark-play-wagon-laughing-crowd}
Joan plays one of the animals. A sheep, with a white woollen mask, and a fleece on her back. She is supposed to walk into the ark quietly. But Pip, her little dog, runs onto the wagon after her, barking, and the crowd thinks it is part of the play, and cheers. Joan is embarrassed at first. But then she laughs too. And the old carpenter playing Noah says that God must have sent an extra dog, just in case.

[long-pause]

Late at night, the bonfires burn low. The music stops. The people walk home through the warm, starry night, tired and happy. Tom walks with his family, along the river. The sky in the north is still faintly light, even at midnight. The cathedral stands dark against the sky, with its scaffolding like a black web. An owl hoots from the trees by the river. And in the meadows, the last embers of the fires glow red, like little stars fallen to the earth.

[long-pause]

{img:salisbury-cathedral-1258-completed-nave-west-front-scaffolding-removed-crowds}
The years go by. Tom grows up. He finishes his apprenticeship, and becomes a mason. He carves stones for the cathedral, just like his father. The great church grows longer and taller, year by year. The nave is finished. The roof is covered with lead. The windows are filled with glass. And at last, in the autumn of 1258, the main part of the cathedral is ready.

[long-pause]

On the twenty-ninth of September, 1258, the feast of Saint Michael, there is a great ceremony. The cathedral is to be consecrated. That means that it is blessed and dedicated to God, officially, and becomes a holy place. It is the greatest day in the history of the town. People come from all over the diocese, and from much further away. Lords and ladies, bishops and abbots, priests and monks, merchants and farmers. The streets are packed with people.

[pause]

And the king himself is coming. King Henry the Third, the son of King John, comes to Salisbury for the consecration, with his queen, Eleanor, and his court. Henry loves churches. He is building a great new abbey church at Westminster, in London. He loves beautiful buildings, and he has given many gifts to Salisbury Cathedral. Timber from his forests, money and fine things for the altars.

[pause]

{img:king-henry-iii-arriving-salisbury-procession-banners-crowds-1258}
The ceremony is led by the archbishop of Canterbury, the most important churchman in England. It begins early in the morning. The archbishop walks three times around the outside of the cathedral, in a procession, sprinkling the walls with holy water. Then he knocks on the great west door with his staff, and the door opens, and he goes inside, followed by the bishops, the priests, the king, the queen and the great lords.

[pause]

Inside, the archbishop blesses every part of the cathedral. The altars, the walls, the floor. He anoints twelve crosses on the walls with holy oil, one for each of the apostles. Candles are lit in front of each cross. The choir sings. Incense fills the air. And then there is a great Mass, the first Mass in the finished cathedral, with the king and queen kneeling in front of the altar.

[long-pause]

{img:cathedral-interior-consecration-mass-candles-choir-light-through-windows}
Tom is inside, with his father and the other masons. They are standing at the back, among the crowd, in their best clothes. Tom looks up. The great pale columns rise all around him, with their dark marble shafts. The pointed arches. The high vaults, with their ribs meeting far above. The silvery windows, full of light. The incense rising in clouds through the sunbeams. And the sound of the choir, filling the whole enormous space.

[pause]

Walter puts his hand on Tom's shoulder. He has worked on this building for almost thirty years. He has carved hundreds of stones. Some of them are up there, at the top of the columns, the capitals with their curling leaves. And now it is finished. He has tears in his eyes. He does not say anything. He does not need to. Tom understands. And next to them, old Master Nicholas stands very still, looking up at the vaults, with a quiet smile on his face.

[pause]

{img:old-master-mason-and-masons-standing-proud-finished-cathedral-light}
After the ceremony, there is a great feast. The king gives a feast for the lords and the clergy, in the bishop's palace. And in the meadows, there is a feast for the people, with food and ale, paid for by the bishop. The masons and carpenters are given an extra day's pay, and a feast of their own, in the lodge. There are bonfires and music and dancing, late into the night.

[long-pause]

That night, Tom walks alone around the outside of the cathedral, in the moonlight. The scaffolding is gone from most of the walls now. The pale stone shines silver under the moon. The great building is quiet and still, after the crowds and the music of the day. Tom touches the wall with his hand. Somewhere in these walls, there are stones he has cut. With his mark on them, a little shape like a bird's foot, the same as his father's, with a small line added. And they will be there, he thinks, long after he is gone.

[long-pause]

{img:salisbury-cloisters-medieval-arcades-green-garth-cedar-quiet-light}
Even after the consecration, the building work goes on. A cathedral is never really finished. There are always new parts to add, and old parts to repair. And at Salisbury, in the years after 1258, two beautiful new buildings are added, on the south side of the cathedral. The cloisters and the chapter house.

[long-pause]

The cloisters are a square of covered walkways, around a green lawn, next to the cathedral. Each walkway has a row of tall, open arches, on the side facing the lawn, filled with delicate stone tracery, like lace. The cloisters at Salisbury are the largest in England. They are a place for the canons to walk, to think, to pray and to read, sheltered from the rain and the wind. And they are one of the most peaceful places in the world.

[pause]

Tom works on the cloisters for many years. He carves the tracery of the arches. Delicate circles, filled with smaller circles and pointed shapes, like flowers. It is very fine work. Each piece must fit perfectly with the next. He uses the skills that his father taught him, and that the old master taught him, long ago, with two overlapping circles drawn in chalk. Now, he draws the circles himself, on the tracing floor, with a great compass.

[pause]

{img:chapter-house-octagonal-central-column-fan-vault-windows-light-salisbury}
The chapter house is even more beautiful. It is an eight-sided building, at the side of the cloisters. It is where the canons meet, every day, to discuss the business of the cathedral. In the middle of the room, there is a single slender column, of Purbeck marble, which rises up and spreads out into the vault, like the branches of a palm tree, or a fountain of stone. Around the walls, there are tall windows, filling the room with light. And around the bottom of the walls, there are stone seats, for the canons.

[pause]

Above the seats, all the way around the walls, there is a wonderful carved frieze. It is a long series of small scenes, carved in stone, telling stories from the Bible. The creation of the world. Adam and Eve in the garden. Noah and the ark. The tower of Babel. Abraham. Joseph and his brothers. Moses. More than sixty scenes, with hundreds of little figures, all carved with great care and charm, and once painted in bright colours.

[long-pause]

{img:carved-stone-frieze-bible-scenes-noah-ark-small-figures-chapter-house}
Tom is one of the carvers of the frieze. He carves the scene of Noah's ark. A little wooden ship, with Noah looking out of a window, and the animals peering out. And on the roof of the ark, he carves a tiny dog. Nobody has asked him to. It is not in the Bible. But he thinks of Joan's little dog Pip, running onto the stage in the play, many years ago. And he smiles, and carves it anyway. The master looks at it, and laughs, and lets it stay.

[pause]

The figures on the frieze are full of life. They have expressive faces and lively gestures. They wear the clothes of the thirteenth century. Adam digs with a spade, like an English farmer. Eve spins with a spindle, like an English housewife. Noah builds his ark with an axe, like a carpenter of Salisbury. The carvers put their own world into the old stories. And so, the frieze is not only a picture of the Bible. It is also a picture of their own lives.

[pause]

{img:mason-carving-small-figures-frieze-close-chisel-concentration-light}
By now, Tom is married. He married Alice, the weaver's daughter with red hair, whom he danced with on Midsummer Eve. They have a little house in the town, with a garden, and three children. Their eldest son, Will, is eight years old. And he already plays at the edge of the building site, with little pieces of leftover stone, and an old chisel, just as Tom did, when he was a boy.

[long-pause]

In the evenings, after work, Tom sometimes walks in the finished cloisters, alone, when the canons have gone. The light falls through the tracery, making patterns on the stone floor. The lawn in the middle is green and quiet. A blackbird sings. And Tom walks slowly around the four walkways, looking at the arches, and remembering the faces of the men who built them. His father. Old Master Nicholas. Hugh and Robin. All the masons of the lodge. They are all here, he thinks, in the stones.

[long-pause]

{img:salisbury-cathedral-spire-tallest-medieval-england-meadows-river-summer}
When the cathedral was consecrated in 1258, it did not yet have its most famous feature. The spire. In the beginning, it had only a low tower, at the crossing, in the middle of the church. But about fifty years later, around the year 1310, the people of Salisbury decided to build something extraordinary. A tall stone spire, rising high above the tower, pointing up to heaven.

[long-pause]

The spire was built between about 1310 and 1330. First, the builders made the old tower higher, adding two more storeys, with tall windows. And then, on top of the tower, they built the spire itself. A tall, slender, eight-sided cone of stone, rising up and up, getting narrower and narrower, until it ended in a point, with a cross on top. It is about one hundred and twenty-three metres tall, from the ground to the top of the cross.

[pause]

It became the tallest church spire in England. And it still is today. For many centuries, it was the tallest building in the whole country. You can see it from many kilometres away, across the green valleys and the chalk hills. It looks like a great finger, pointing at the sky. It is one of the most famous and most beautiful sights in England.

[pause]

{img:masons-building-spire-wooden-scaffolding-inside-cone-high-above-town}
Building the spire was very difficult and very daring. The stone walls of the spire are thin. At the bottom, they are about sixty centimetres thick, and at the top, only about twenty centimetres. Inside, there is a wooden scaffolding, a tall frame of timber, which the builders used to build the spire from the inside, and which is still there today. The builders worked high above the ground, in the wind, laying stone after stone, up and up.

[pause]

But the spire was very heavy. About six and a half thousand tonnes. And the cathedral had not been built to carry such a weight. The old foundations were only about one and a half metres deep, on gravel. The columns at the crossing, which carry the tower and the spire, began to bend, very slightly, under the weight. The builders added strong stone arches, and iron bands, to hold everything together. And the spire held. But it leans a little, still today, about seventy centimetres to the south-west.

[long-pause]

{img:old-mason-grandfather-looking-up-at-new-spire-grandchildren-beside-him}
Tom did not live to see the spire. He passed away peacefully, as an old man, in the 1290s, in his little house in the town, with his family around him. But his son Will became a mason too, like his father and his grandfather. And Will's son, Tom's grandson, was a young mason when the spire was built. Perhaps he climbed up inside it, on the wooden scaffolding, and laid some of its stones, high above the town, in the wind.

[pause]

So the cathedral was built by families, generation after generation. Grandfathers, fathers, sons and grandsons. Each one learning from the one before. Each one adding their part. Each one carving their mark in the stones. Without ever seeing the whole thing finished, as it is today. They trusted that others would continue their work, after them. And they did.

[pause]

{img:cathedral-spire-sunset-golden-light-river-avon-swans-meadows-peaceful}
In the evening, when the sun sets behind the cathedral, the spire glows golden, against the pink sky. The swans glide on the river below. The cows graze in the water meadows. The bells ring for evensong. And the great stone finger points up into the sky, as it has for almost seven hundred years. It is one of the most peaceful and beautiful sights in the world. And it was built by hand, stone by stone, by people like Tom.

[long-pause]

In the spire, high up inside, the old wooden scaffolding of the builders is still there. And the old wooden windlass, the lifting wheel that they used to raise the stones. Visitors who climb the tower today can see them, in the dim light, among the great timbers. They have not moved for almost seven hundred years. It is as if the builders had just put down their tools, and gone home for supper, and might come back tomorrow.

[long-pause]

{img:salisbury-cathedral-1386-clock-mechanism-iron-wheels-tower-room-later}
Salisbury Cathedral has stood in its meadow for almost eight hundred years. And it has seen a great deal of history. Kings and queens have come and gone. Wars have been fought. The world has changed beyond recognition. But the cathedral has stayed, quiet and beautiful, among the rivers and the meadows.

[long-pause]

In 1386, a clock was made for the cathedral. It has no face and no hands. It simply strikes the hours, on a bell, so that the priests know when it is time to pray. It is made of iron, with big wheels and weights. And it is still there, in the cathedral today. It is thought to be the oldest working mechanical clock in the world. It has been ticking, on and off, for more than six hundred years.

[pause]

In the fifteen hundreds, the religion of England changed. King Henry the Eighth broke away from the Pope in Rome, and the Church of England was formed. The Use of Sarum, the beautiful old way of worship of Salisbury, was replaced by new services, in English. Many statues and paintings in churches were destroyed. At Salisbury, some of the old statues and decorations were lost. But the building itself survived.

[pause]

{img:christopher-wren-surveying-spire-lean-plumb-line-1668-later}
In 1668, the great architect Christopher Wren, who later built St Paul's Cathedral in London, was asked to look at the spire of Salisbury. People were worried about its lean. Wren studied it carefully. He measured how much it was leaning, and decided that it was safe, and that the lean had stopped. He recommended some iron ties, to make it stronger. And he was right. The spire has not moved since.

[pause]

In the seventeen hundreds, another architect, called James Wyatt, made big changes inside the cathedral. He wanted to make it look tidy and plain. He removed many of the old tombs, screens and chapels. He took out some of the old stained glass, and threw it into a ditch. Many people today think that this was a great loss. But at the time, it was the fashion. And some of the old glass was later found again, and put back in the windows.

[long-pause]

{img:john-constable-painting-salisbury-cathedral-from-meadows-easel-cows-later}
In the early eighteen hundreds, a great English painter, called John Constable, came to Salisbury many times, to visit his friend, the bishop's nephew. He loved the cathedral and the meadows around it. He painted it again and again, from different places, in different lights. From the bishop's garden. From the water meadows. With a rainbow over it. His paintings of Salisbury Cathedral are among the most famous paintings in England. They hang in great museums, in London and in New York.

[pause]

In the twentieth century, the cathedral needed a great deal of repair. The stone of the spire and the walls had been damaged by centuries of wind, rain and frost. In 1985, a great appeal was launched, to raise money for the repairs. And since then, the masons of Salisbury have been working on the cathedral, almost without stopping, replacing worn stones, cleaning, carving and repairing. It is one of the longest building works in the world.

[pause]

{img:modern-cathedral-masons-workshop-carving-new-stone-traditional-tools}
And here is a wonderful thing. The cathedral still has its own masons. A team of stonemasons, who work in a workshop in the Close, just like the medieval lodge. They use some of the same tools as the medieval masons. Mallets and chisels, squares and templates. They carve new stones, to replace the old ones that have worn away, copying the old shapes exactly. Some of them are apprentices, learning the craft, just like Tom, almost eight hundred years ago.

[long-pause]

So the work that began in 1220, in the meadow by the five rivers, has never really stopped. Generation after generation, masons have cared for the great church, cutting, carving and repairing. And each one has added their mark, somewhere in the stones. Medieval marks, and modern ones, side by side. A long chain of hands, reaching back across the centuries, to Walter, and Tom, and old Master Nicholas, and the very first stone, laid by the bishop, on a spring day in 1220.

[long-pause]

{img:salisbury-cathedral-today-spire-close-lawn-visitors-sunny-summer}
Today, Salisbury Cathedral is one of the most visited cathedrals in Britain. Hundreds of thousands of people come every year, from all over the world. They walk across the wide green lawn of the Close, and look up at the spire, rising high into the sky. And they go inside, through the great west door, into the cool, pale, silvery light of the nave.

[long-pause]

Inside, the cathedral looks very much as it did in 1258. The long nave, with its rows of columns, pale grey with dark marble shafts. The pointed arches. The high vaults. The silvery windows. The light is soft and calm. Visitors walk slowly, looking up, talking quietly. Some sit on the chairs, and just look. Some light a candle. And every day, the services still go on, as they have for almost eight hundred years.

[pause]

In the middle of the nave, there is a modern font, for baptisms. It is a large, low, cross-shaped basin of dark stone, filled with water, so still and smooth that it reflects the columns, the arches and the windows above, like a mirror. Visitors bend over it, and see the whole cathedral, upside down, in the water. It is one of the most beautiful things in the church.

[pause]

{img:salisbury-font-water-mirror-reflecting-vaults-windows-nave-today}
You can still see the old clock of 1386, standing in the nave, with its iron wheels slowly turning. You can see the tomb of William Longespée, the Earl of Salisbury, in his painted armour. And in the chapter house, you can see Magna Carta, in its glass case, in the dim light, and the stone frieze, with its little carved figures, telling the stories of the Bible. And if you look very closely, at the scene of Noah's ark, perhaps you can find a tiny dog.

[pause]

You can also climb the tower. There are three hundred and thirty-two steps, up narrow stone stairs, through the roof spaces, past the great medieval oak beams, with the carpenters' marks still on them. Past the old wooden windlass, which lifted the stones. Up to the bottom of the spire, where a little door opens onto a balcony, high above the town. From there, you can see for kilometres in every direction. The town, the rivers, the meadows, Old Sarum on its hill, and the green chalk hills beyond.

[long-pause]

{img:view-from-salisbury-tower-balcony-town-rivers-old-sarum-hill-distance}
At the top of the spire, peregrine falcons make their nest every spring. They are the fastest birds in the world. They have lived on the cathedral for many years. Every year, they lay their eggs in a little box, high up on the tower, and raise their chicks. There is a camera, so that people can watch them online, from all over the world. The falcons do not know that they are living on a famous cathedral. To them, it is just a very tall, very safe cliff.

[pause]

Old Sarum, the windy hill where the first cathedral stood, is still there too. You can walk up to it, and through the old earth banks, and see the ruins of the Norman castle, and the outline of the old cathedral, marked in the grass. The wind still blows across the hill, as it did in 1220. And from the edge of the banks, you can look down into the valley, and see the spire of the new cathedral, rising out of the meadows, three kilometres away.

[pause]

{img:old-sarum-ruins-today-grass-outline-old-cathedral-wind-view-to-spire}
And in the water meadows, below the cathedral, there is a path, where you can walk along the river, through the grass, among the cows. It is the same view that John Constable painted. The river. The trees. The meadows. And the cathedral, rising above them, with its spire. On a summer evening, when the sun is low, and the light is golden, it looks exactly like his paintings. And perhaps, exactly as it looked to Tom and Walter, walking home from work, long ago.

[long-pause]

The town of Salisbury still has its chequer pattern of streets, laid out by the bishop in the twelve hundreds. Some of the water channels have been covered over. But the market still takes place in the market square, on Tuesdays and Saturdays, as it has for eight hundred years. And people still live in the old streets, and walk to the cathedral, and look up at the spire, and feel proud of the great building in their town.

So we leave Salisbury tonight, in the year 1250, as the evening falls. The sun is setting over the water meadows. The rivers are shining gold and pink. And the great half-built cathedral stands in the middle of the valley, with its scaffolding and its cranes, glowing in the last warm light, like a ship of stone, sailing on a green sea.

[long-pause]

On the building site, the work is over for the day. The great wheel has stopped turning. The hammers are silent. The scaffolding is empty. The half-finished stones lie on the bankers in the lodge, waiting for the morning. And a watchman is walking slowly around the site, with a lantern, checking that everything is safe for the night.

[pause]

{img:watchman-lantern-building-site-dusk-stone-blocks-quiet-cathedral}
In the cathedral, in the finished part at the eastern end, the candles are lit. The priests and the choristers are singing evensong. Their voices rise up into the high stone vaults, and drift out through the windows, over the quiet building site, and over the Close, and over the meadows. A blackbird is singing on the roof of the lodge, as if it were joining in.

[long-pause]

In the town, the smoke is rising from the chimneys. The water is murmuring in the channels along the streets. In a small house on Blue Boar Row, a family is sitting down to supper. Fresh bread from the oven. A pot of pottage. A jug of ale. The father is telling about a capital he is carving, with curling leaves. The mother is telling the gossip from the market. The little girl is telling about the ducks on the river. And the boy, with his new leather apron hanging on the wall, is listening to every word.

[pause]

Night falls over the valley of the five rivers. The moon rises, and shines on the pale walls of the cathedral, and on the scaffolding, and on the cranes, and on the piles of stone. A soft mist rises from the meadows. An owl glides silently over the river. The cows are lying down in the wet grass. And everything is still.

[long-pause]

In a small room under the roof, a boy of thirteen is lying on his straw mattress, looking out of the little window at the moonlit cathedral. He is thinking about two circles, overlapping, and a pointed arch between them. About the sound of a stone that rings like a bell. About the great wheel, turning slowly, and a stone rising into the sky. And slowly, his eyes close. And he dreams of building, stone by stone, something that will last forever.
