{img:templar-commandery-champagne-1250-dawn-stone-chapel-farm-buildings-mist}
Tonight we travel back almost eight hundred years, to the year 1250. We go to the countryside of Champagne, in the east of France. It is a land of gentle hills, wheat fields, vineyards and small rivers. And on the edge of a quiet village, there stands a group of stone buildings around a courtyard, with a little chapel in the middle. It is a house of the Knights Templar.

[long-pause]

It is very early in the morning, long before sunrise. The sky is still dark, with a few stars. The fields are silent. And then, from the little chapel, a bell begins to ring. Softly at first, and then more clearly, across the sleeping countryside. In the dormitory beside the chapel, a group of men are getting up from their narrow beds, in the darkness. They do not speak. They put on their mantles, and walk quietly to the chapel, to pray.

[pause]

Some of them wear white mantles, with a red cross on the shoulder. They are the knights. Others wear black or brown mantles, also with a red cross. They are the sergeants, the brothers who are not knights. They stand together in the dim chapel, lit by a few candles. And they begin to sing, softly, in Latin, the first prayers of the day. Outside, the first birds are waking up.

[pause]

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

{img:templar-knight-white-mantle-red-cross-standing-chapel-door-candlelight}
The Knights Templar were one of the most famous groups of the Middle Ages. They were monks, who had promised to live in poverty, obedience and chastity, like other monks. But they were also knights, who carried swords and rode horses. They were founded to protect pilgrims on their way to Jerusalem. And over time, they became one of the richest and most powerful organisations in Europe.

[pause]

Today, there are many legends about them. Stories about secret treasures, hidden knowledge and mysterious rituals. Most of these stories were invented much later. Tonight, we will try to see the real Templars. Not as legends, but as people. Men who prayed, worked, farmed, kept accounts, looked after horses, and travelled far across the sea.

[pause]

{img:young-man-1250-champagne-fields-looking-at-commandery-walking-road}
We will follow a young man from Champagne, called Guillaume. He is nineteen years old, the son of a minor knight. He joins the Templars, and we will see how he is received into the order. We will learn the rules of their life, what they ate, how they prayed and how they slept. We will visit the farms and the mills that the Templars owned, and see how they became the bankers of kings.

[long-pause]

Then we will travel with Guillaume across the sea, to the Holy Land. We will see the great city of Acre, with its crowded markets and its many peoples. We will visit the Templars' fortress by the sea, and their mighty castle on the coast. And we will meet the people who lived there, Christians, Muslims and Jews. There were wars in those days, and we will look at them only from a distance.

[pause]

{img:acre-1250-harbour-ships-city-walls-towers-mediterranean-sunlight}
So let us begin. Not yet in the Holy Land, but in the quiet countryside of France, on a spring morning in 1250. The bell of the little chapel is ringing. The brothers are singing their prayers. And at the gate of the house, a young man is standing, waiting, his heart beating fast. He has come to ask if he may become a Templar.

[long-pause]

{img:jerusalem-1119-temple-mount-al-aqsa-dome-pilgrims-walls-morning}
The story of the Templars began more than a hundred and thirty years before our story, in Jerusalem. In the year 1099, an army from western Europe had taken the city, after a long journey and a hard war. This was the First Crusade. The crusaders set up a new kingdom, called the Kingdom of Jerusalem. And after that, thousands of pilgrims from Europe began to travel to the Holy Land, to visit the places where Jesus had lived.

[long-pause]

But the journey was dangerous. The roads from the coast to Jerusalem passed through hills and deserts, where robbers attacked travellers. Many pilgrims were robbed, or worse. So, around the year 1120, a knight from Champagne, called Hugues de Payens, had an idea. Together with a few friends, he decided to protect the pilgrims on the roads. And they would live like monks, in poverty and prayer, while they did it.

[pause]

The king of Jerusalem liked the idea. He gave the knights a place to live, in a part of his palace. The palace was on the Temple Mount, the hill where the ancient Temple of Solomon had once stood. The crusaders thought that the great mosque on the hill, the al-Aqsa Mosque, was the Temple of Solomon itself. So the knights became known as the Poor Fellow-Soldiers of Christ and of the Temple of Solomon. Or simply, the Templars.

[pause]

{img:hugues-de-payens-few-knights-poor-simple-clothes-sharing-horse-legend}
At first, there were only nine of them, the story says. They were so poor that, according to a legend, two knights had to share one horse. This picture, two knights riding one horse, became the symbol on the seal of the order. It showed their poverty and their brotherhood. In reality, there may have been a few more than nine. But they were certainly few, and poor.

[pause]

For about ten years, the little group grew slowly. Then, in 1129, Hugues de Payens travelled back to Europe, to ask for support. At a great meeting of churchmen in the city of Troyes, in Champagne, the Templars were officially recognised by the Church. They were given a rule of life, a list of rules for how they should live, pray, eat and dress. From then on, they were a real religious order.

[long-pause]

{img:bernard-of-clairvaux-monk-writing-abbey-cell-window-quill}
They had a powerful friend. His name was Bernard of Clairvaux. He was a monk, the most famous churchman of his time, and the nephew of one of the first Templars. Bernard wrote a letter in praise of the new knighthood. He wrote that the Templars were a new kind of knight, gentle as lambs in their own house, and brave as lions on the road. Because of Bernard, the Templars became famous all over Europe.

[pause]

After that, gifts began to pour in. Kings, nobles and ordinary people gave the Templars land, money, horses, farms, houses and mills. They believed that helping the Templars was a way to help the Holy Land, and to earn a blessing for their souls. Young men from noble families came to join the order. Within twenty years, the Templars had houses all over Europe, from Portugal to England, and from Spain to Hungary.

[pause]

{img:templar-houses-across-europe-map-feeling-farms-chapels-countryside}
The Templars used the money from their lands in Europe to support their brothers in the Holy Land. They bought horses, weapons and food. They built castles. They paid for ships. Every year, a part of the income of every Templar house in Europe was sent to the east. So for every Templar knight in the Holy Land, there were many brothers in Europe, working on farms and in houses, to support him.

[long-pause]

By 1250, the Templars have existed for more than a hundred and twenty years. They are rich and famous. They own thousands of houses and farms. But the Holy Land has changed. Jerusalem has been lost again, six years ago. The crusaders now hold only a strip of land along the coast, with the great port of Acre as their capital. And the Templars have moved their headquarters there, to a fortress by the sea.

[long-pause]

{img:small-manor-house-champagne-1250-stone-tower-courtyard-family-horses}
Guillaume was born in a small manor house in Champagne, not far from the city of Troyes. His father is a knight, but not a rich one. He owns a little land, a few farms, a mill and a small stone house with a tower. The family is proud of its name. But there is not much money. And Guillaume is the third son.

[long-pause]

In those days, the oldest son usually inherited the family land. The younger sons had to find their own way in the world. Some became priests or monks. Some went to serve a great lord, as knights. Some went on crusade. And some joined the military orders, like the Templars or the Hospitallers. For a younger son of a poor knight, the Templars offered a respected life, a purpose, and a place in a famous brotherhood.

[pause]

Guillaume has grown up learning the skills of a knight. Since he was seven, he has learned to ride a horse, to use a sword and a lance, and to look after weapons and armour. He served as a page and then as a squire in the house of a richer lord, where he also learned good manners, how to serve at table and how to behave at court. He can ride better than almost anyone his age. And he loves horses more than anything.

[pause]

{img:squire-training-lance-quintain-tilting-yard-young-man-horse-1250}
But Guillaume is also a thoughtful young man. He goes to church every day, and he likes the quiet of the chapel. He has listened to the stories of the old knights, about the Holy Land, about Jerusalem and the Holy Sepulchre, the church built over the place where Jesus was buried. He has heard about the Templars, the knights who are also monks. And slowly, a wish has grown in him, to join them.

[pause]

His family has a connection with the Templars. His great-uncle was a Templar, many years ago, and gave the order a small piece of land. The Templar house nearby, called a commandery, stands on that land. Guillaume has visited it many times as a boy. He has seen the brothers praying in their chapel, and working in their fields, and riding out on their horses. He has always admired them.

[long-pause]

{img:father-and-son-talking-by-fireplace-stone-hall-evening-1250}
When Guillaume tells his father that he wants to become a Templar, his father is quiet for a long time. Then he says that it is a good choice, and an honourable one. But he also says that it is a choice for life. Once he has made his vows, Guillaume can never leave the order. He will own nothing. He will not marry. He will obey every order of his superiors. And he may be sent anywhere in the world, even to the Holy Land, and never come back.

[pause]

Guillaume's mother cries. She does not want her son to go far away. But she also knows that he has thought about it carefully. She sews him a new linen shirt, and gives him a little wooden cross that belonged to her own mother. His sister, who is twelve, makes him promise to write. His older brothers slap him on the back and wish him good luck. They are a little jealous, perhaps.

[pause]

{img:young-man-riding-to-commandery-gate-spring-morning-fields-chapel-bell}
One spring morning, Guillaume rides to the Templar commandery, with his father. He is wearing his best clothes. He carries nothing with him, because he knows that a Templar must give up all his possessions. At the gate, they are met by a sergeant, who takes their horses. And they are led into the courtyard, where the commander of the house is waiting.

[long-pause]

The commander is called Brother Hugues. He is about fifty years old, with a long grey beard, a calm face and kind eyes. He has served in the Holy Land for many years, and has come back to France to run this house. He greets Guillaume's father as an old friend. Then he looks at Guillaume for a long time. He asks him why he wants to join. Guillaume answers as honestly as he can. Brother Hugues nods slowly. Then he says that the brothers will meet in the chapel tonight, to decide.

[long-pause]

{img:templar-chapel-night-candles-brothers-gathered-chapter-meeting-quiet}
That evening, the brothers of the commandery gather in the chapel. There are about twelve of them. Some knights, some sergeants, and a chaplain, a priest who is also a member of the order. They stand in a circle, in the candlelight. Brother Hugues asks them if anyone knows a reason why Guillaume should not be received. Nobody does. So he sends two brothers to fetch the young man.

[long-pause]

Guillaume is waiting in a small room near the chapel. The two brothers come to him. They speak to him seriously. They tell him that he must think carefully about what he is asking. They say that he sees the Templars in their fine mantles, riding fine horses, eating good food. But he does not see the hard rules inside. When he wants to sleep, he will be woken to pray. When he wants to eat, he may be sent to work. When he wants to be in one place, he will be sent to another.

[pause]

They ask him a series of questions. Is he a knight, the son of a knight, and born of a lawful marriage? Is he married or engaged? Has he made vows to another religious order? Does he owe money that he cannot pay? Is he healthy? Is he anyone's servant? Guillaume answers each question truthfully. Then the brothers go back to the chapel and report his answers.

[pause]

{img:young-man-kneeling-before-commander-chapel-candlelight-reception-vows}
At last, Guillaume is brought into the chapel. He kneels in front of Brother Hugues, with his hands together. And he says the words he has prepared. Sire, I have come before God and before you and before the brothers, and I ask you, for God and Our Lady, to welcome me into your company. Brother Hugues asks him again if he is sure. He tells him once more about the hardships. Guillaume says yes. He is sure.

[pause]

Then Guillaume makes his vows. He promises, before God and the Virgin Mary, to obey the master of the Temple and his superiors, all the days of his life. He promises to live in chastity, without a wife. He promises to own nothing of his own. He promises to follow the customs of the house. And he promises to help to defend the Holy Land, and never to leave the order, unless he is given permission.

[long-pause]

{img:white-mantle-red-cross-placed-on-shoulders-kneeling-man-chapel-brothers}
Then Brother Hugues takes a white mantle, with a red cross on the shoulder, and places it around Guillaume's shoulders. He fastens the cord at his neck. The chaplain says a prayer and a psalm. The brothers sing. Brother Hugues raises Guillaume to his feet, and gives him the kiss of peace. And then each of the brothers comes forward, and welcomes him as a brother.

[pause]

The white mantle is a sign of purity. Only knights may wear it. It is the most precious thing that Guillaume will now have. And yet, it is not really his. Nothing belongs to him any more. Everything he uses, his clothes, his bed, his horse, his sword, belongs to the order. He is only allowed to use them. If he loses or damages them, he will be punished.

[pause]

{img:new-templar-brother-sitting-with-commander-learning-rule-book-candle}
After the ceremony, Brother Hugues sits down with Guillaume and explains the most important rules. How to behave in chapel and at table. How to ask permission for things. What punishments there are for breaking the rules. Which bells he must listen for, day and night. It is a lot to remember. Guillaume listens carefully. He has many questions. But a good Templar does not ask too many questions. So he just nods.

[long-pause]

That night, Guillaume sleeps for the first time in the dormitory of the commandery, with the other brothers. He lies on a simple bed, with a straw mattress, a sheet and a blanket. A lamp burns all night in the dormitory, as the rule demands. He does not sleep much. He lies awake, listening to the breathing of the other brothers, and thinking about his new life. Outside, an owl is calling. And far away, a dog is barking. He feels very small, and very happy, and a little afraid.

[long-pause]

{img:templar-commandery-courtyard-barn-chapel-well-dovecote-farm-life}
The Templar house where Guillaume now lives is called a commandery. There are hundreds of commanderies all over Europe. Most of them are not castles. They are more like big farms, with a chapel. They are run by a commander, with a small group of brothers, and many workers and servants. Their main job is to make money and supplies for the order, to send to the Holy Land.

[long-pause]

Guillaume's commandery stands around a big square courtyard. On one side, there is the chapel. It is a simple building of pale stone, with a round window above the door, and a little bell tower. Inside, it is plain and quiet, with painted walls showing the life of Christ, and Templar knights on horseback. The brothers spend many hours here every day.

[pause]

Next to the chapel, there is the main house. On the ground floor, there is a hall, where the brothers eat their meals, and a kitchen. Upstairs, there is the dormitory, where they sleep. There is also a small room for the commander, where he keeps the accounts and the important documents in a wooden chest. And there is a room for guests, because the Templars often give shelter to travellers.

[pause]

{img:huge-medieval-barn-timber-roof-grain-stored-carts-templar-farm}
On the other sides of the courtyard, there are the farm buildings. There is a huge barn, with a high wooden roof, for storing grain and hay. Some Templar barns were so well built that they still stand today, almost eight hundred years later. There are stables for the horses and the oxen, a cowshed, a sheepfold and a pigsty. There is a bakery, a brewhouse, a blacksmith's forge and a workshop for repairing tools.

[pause]

In one corner of the courtyard, there is a round stone tower, with hundreds of little holes in its walls. It is a dovecote, where pigeons nest. Only nobles and religious houses are allowed to keep a dovecote. The pigeons give meat and eggs. And their droppings are very good fertiliser for the fields. There is also a well, in the middle of the courtyard, and a fishpond behind the barn, full of carp.

[long-pause]

{img:templar-commandery-fields-workers-harvest-vineyards-mill-river-landscape}
Around the commandery, there are fields, meadows, woods and vineyards. The Templars own a lot of land here, given to them by local families over many years. Some of the land is farmed by the commandery itself, with hired workers. Other land is rented out to peasants, who pay the Templars rent, in money or in grain, chickens and wine. The Templars also own a watermill on the river, where the peasants must bring their grain to be ground.

[pause]

Only a few of the people at the commandery are actual Templar brothers. Most are workers. Ploughmen, shepherds, cowherds, millers, bakers, cooks and servants. Some are local villagers, who come to work each day. Some live at the commandery, as servants. And there are also people called confratres, friends of the order, who are not brothers, but who have given gifts and receive the prayers of the brothers in return.

[pause]

{img:templar-brothers-walking-across-courtyard-workers-greeting-morning-sun}
Brother Hugues, the commander, runs everything. He decides what to plant and when to harvest. He checks the accounts. He settles disputes between the workers and the peasants. He welcomes visitors. And once a year, he sends a sum of money to the provincial master of the Templars, for the Holy Land. It is a big responsibility. He is helped by the other brothers, each with his own job.

[long-pause]

At first, Guillaume is surprised by all this. He thought that being a Templar would mean riding into battle, and praying in great churches. Instead, he finds himself checking the sheep, counting sacks of grain and helping to repair the mill. But Brother Hugues tells him that this work is just as important. Without the farms of Europe, he says, there would be no knights in the Holy Land. Every sack of grain is a small piece of the defence of Jerusalem. Guillaume thinks about this, and decides that it is true.

[pause]

In the evening, the courtyard of the commandery is a peaceful place. The workers have gone home to the village. The animals are in their stables. The brothers walk slowly across the courtyard to Vespers. The pigeons coo in the dovecote. And the smell of fresh bread drifts from the bakery, where tomorrow's loaves are already being prepared.

[long-pause]

{img:templar-rule-book-manuscript-old-french-pages-candle-wooden-desk}
The life of a Templar is governed by the Rule. It is a long book of rules, which tells the brothers how to live, every hour of the day. It was first written in Latin in 1129, at the council of Troyes. Later, it was translated into French, so that the brothers, who mostly could not read Latin, could understand it. And many new rules were added over the years. By 1250, it has hundreds of articles.

[long-pause]

The Rule tells the brothers what to wear. Their clothes must be simple and plain, without decoration. The knights wear white mantles, as a sign of purity. The sergeants wear black or brown. All brothers wear the red cross on their mantle, on the left shoulder, over the heart. Their clothes must not be too long or too short. Their shoes must have no points or laces. And they may not wear fur, except lambskin or sheepskin in winter.

[pause]

The Rule tells them how to look. Their hair must be cut short. But they must not shave their beards. So all Templars have beards. The Rule says they must not be proud of their appearance, or look at themselves too much. They must not decorate their horses or their weapons with gold or silver. Everything must be simple. Their beauty, the Rule says, should be inside them, not outside.

[pause]

{img:templar-brothers-bearded-short-hair-plain-mantles-walking-cloister}
The Rule also tells them how to behave. They must not speak too much, or laugh loudly. They must not gossip or boast. They must not go anywhere without permission. They must not write or receive letters without the commander reading them. They must not gamble, or hunt with hawks, or go to parties. The only animal they may hunt is the lion. Since there are not many lions in Champagne, this is not a problem.

[pause]

And the Rule tells them how to treat each other. They must be gentle and patient with each other, like brothers. They must look after the sick and the old. They must share everything. If a brother is in trouble, the others must help him. If a brother does something wrong, he must confess it in front of all the brothers, at the weekly meeting called the chapter. And then he receives a punishment.

[long-pause]

{img:chapter-meeting-brothers-seated-benches-commander-speaking-chapel}
The punishments are sometimes strict. For small faults, a brother might have to eat his meals sitting on the floor, for a few days. Or eat only bread and water. For bigger faults, he might lose his mantle for a time, and have to work with the servants. For the most serious faults, like leaving the order or running away in battle, he might be expelled forever. But most punishments are mild. And most brothers try very hard to follow the Rule.

[pause]

Each week, on Sunday, the brothers hold their chapter meeting. They sit on benches around the walls of the chapel. The commander speaks to them about the Rule and about the business of the house. Then brothers who have broken a rule stand up, kneel, and confess. The others listen. The commander decides the punishment. It is a strange custom. But it keeps the brothers honest, and humble.

[pause]

{img:templar-brother-reading-aloud-to-others-refectory-meal-silence}
Guillaume finds the Rule hard at first. He likes to talk, and to laugh, and to ride where he likes. Now he must ask permission for everything. He must be silent at meals. He cannot even give a gift to his own sister without asking. But slowly, he gets used to it. And he begins to find something peaceful in it. He does not have to decide things for himself any more. He only has to follow the Rule, and do his best.

[long-pause]

One old knight in the commandery, called Brother Raoul, has been a Templar for forty years. He has a long white beard and bright blue eyes. He served in the Holy Land for twenty years, and has many scars. He tells Guillaume that the Rule is like a good saddle. At first, it is stiff and uncomfortable. But after a while, it fits you perfectly, and you cannot ride without it. Guillaume laughs. But later, he finds that the old man was right.

[long-pause]

{img:templar-brothers-night-office-chapel-candles-singing-dark-matins}
The day of a Templar is divided by prayer. Like other monks, the Templars pray at set times, day and night. These times are called the hours. They are announced by the bell of the chapel. When the bell rings, every brother must stop what he is doing, and go to pray. Even in the middle of the night.

[long-pause]

The first prayer of the day is called Matins. It is said in the middle of the night, around two or three o'clock. The bell rings, and the brothers get up from their beds in the dark, put on their shoes and mantles, and go to the chapel. They sing psalms and say prayers, for about an hour. Then they go to check on their horses, in the stables. And then they may go back to bed, until dawn.

[pause]

At dawn, there is the prayer called Prime. Then the brothers hear Mass. Then they go to work. At about nine o'clock, there is a short prayer called Terce. At midday, there is Sext. In the afternoon, there is None. In the late afternoon, there is Vespers. And at the end of the day, before going to bed, there is Compline. Seven times a day, and once at night, the brothers pray.

[pause]

{img:templar-saying-paternosters-stables-horse-quiet-prayer-morning-light}
But many of the Templars cannot read. They cannot follow the long Latin prayers in the books. So the Rule allows them to say a simpler prayer instead. They say the Lord's Prayer, the Paternoster, a certain number of times for each hour. Thirteen times for Matins. Seven times for each of the other hours. Nine times for Vespers. They can say them anywhere. In the chapel, in the stables, or on the road.

[pause]

Guillaume can read a little. But he prefers to say the Paternosters, like most of the brothers. He counts them on his fingers. Sometimes he loses count, especially at Matins, when he is still half asleep. Then he starts again, just to be sure. Brother Raoul says that God does not mind if you lose count, as long as you mean the words.

[long-pause]

{img:templar-brothers-working-fields-afternoon-wheat-horses-cart-sunlight}
Between the prayers, there is work. In the morning, the brothers look after their horses, their weapons and their equipment. They train with the sword and the lance, in a field behind the commandery. Then they work on the farm, or in the house, according to their job. Some check the fields and the workers. Some go to the market, to buy and sell. Some keep the accounts. Some travel to other commanderies with messages.

[pause]

In the afternoon, there is more work, and more training. The knights practise riding together in formation, because in battle, Templars always ride together, in close order, like a wall. They must never break the formation, without permission. Guillaume is very good at this. He has always been a fine rider. The other brothers soon notice it.

[pause]

{img:templar-chapel-vespers-evening-golden-light-through-window-brothers}
In the evening, after Vespers, the brothers eat their supper. Then there is Compline, the last prayer of the day. After Compline, there is silence. The brothers must not speak, except for something urgent. They go to check on their horses one last time. Then they go to the dormitory, and lie down on their beds, in their shirts and breeches. The lamp is left burning. And they sleep, until the bell rings for Matins.

[long-pause]

At first, Guillaume finds it hard to get up in the middle of the night. He is always tired. But after a few weeks, his body gets used to it. And he begins to love Matins most of all. The chapel is dark, lit only by a few candles. Outside, the world is silent. The brothers sing softly together, their voices rising up into the dark roof. It feels as if they are the only people awake in the whole world, keeping watch over everyone else, while they sleep.

[pause]

On feast days, there is more singing, and the chapel is decorated with flowers and candles. The chaplain wears his finest robes. Sometimes, people from the village come to Mass in the little chapel too, standing at the back in their Sunday clothes. And afterwards, the brothers have a better meal than usual, and a little extra wine.

[long-pause]

{img:templar-refectory-long-tables-brothers-eating-silence-reader-lectern}
The Templars eat two meals a day. Dinner at midday, and supper in the evening. They eat together in the hall of the commandery, which is called the refectory. And their meals, like everything else, follow the Rule.

[long-pause]

When the bell rings for dinner, the brothers come into the hall and stand at their places, along long wooden tables. The chaplain says a blessing. Then they sit down and eat, in silence. While they eat, one brother reads aloud from a holy book, from a little stand at the side of the hall. The others listen. If a brother needs something, like salt or bread, he must ask for it with signs, not words.

[pause]

The brothers eat in pairs. Two brothers share one bowl, and eat from it together. This was a custom from the early days, when the Templars were poor. It also teaches them to be fair and to share. Each brother must make sure that his companion gets enough, and not take the best pieces for himself. Guillaume shares his bowl with Brother Raoul. The old knight always pushes the best pieces of meat toward the young man.

[pause]

{img:two-templar-brothers-sharing-one-bowl-bread-wine-cups-table}
The food is simple, but good and filling. The Templars are fighting men, and they need strength. So unlike many monks, they are allowed to eat meat. But only three times a week, on Sundays, Tuesdays and Thursdays. On the other days, they eat vegetables, beans, cheese, eggs and fish. On Fridays, they eat no meat, and during Lent, they fast. There is always plenty of bread. And each brother receives a cup of wine with his meal, mixed with water.

[pause]

The food in Guillaume's commandery comes mostly from its own farm. Bread from its own wheat, ground at its own mill. Wine from its own vineyards. Meat from its own pigs, sheep and cattle. Eggs from its own hens, pigeons from the dovecote, and carp from the fishpond. Cheese from its own cows. Vegetables and herbs from its own garden. Almost nothing needs to be bought, except salt and spices.

[long-pause]

{img:monastery-kitchen-medieval-cook-pots-hearth-bread-vegetables-servants}
The cook of the commandery is a servant called Mathieu. He is a big, cheerful man from the village, who has worked for the Templars for twenty years. He makes thick soups of beans and vegetables, roasts of pork and mutton, and pies of fish and eggs. On feast days, he makes something special. Roast chicken, or pigeon pie, or a sweet dish of rice cooked with almond milk. The brothers are not supposed to care about food. But they all look forward to feast days.

[pause]

One part of the Rule is very kind. It says that a tenth of all the bread from the commandery must be given to the poor. And whatever food is left over from the brothers' meals must also be given to the poor, who come to the gate every day. Old men and women, widows, beggars, travellers and children. Mathieu hands out bread and soup to them at the gate, after every meal.

[pause]

{img:poor-people-receiving-bread-soup-at-commandery-gate-servant-ladle}
Guillaume is given the job of helping Mathieu at the gate, after the evening meal. At first, he is not happy about it. He is a knight, and this seems like a servant's work. But Brother Hugues tells him that the poor are the guests of Christ. And slowly, Guillaume comes to like it. He gets to know the poor people who come every day. An old blind man, who tells funny stories. A widow with three small children. A boy who has no family at all.

[long-pause]

After supper, the brothers stand again, and the chaplain says a prayer of thanks. Then they go to Compline. As they walk across the courtyard, in the evening light, Guillaume looks back at the gate. The widow and her children are sitting on the grass outside, eating their bread. The little boy waves at him. Guillaume waves back. And he thinks that perhaps this, too, is part of defending the Holy Land.

[long-pause]

{img:templar-stables-war-horses-squire-grooming-straw-morning-light}
For a Templar knight, horses are the most important thing of all. A knight without a horse is not a knight. So the Templars take great care of their horses. Every knight has three or four horses. And they are looked after with as much care as the brothers themselves.

[long-pause]

Each knight has a big, strong war horse, called a destrier. It is trained for battle, to stay calm in noise and crowds, and to carry a knight in armour. It is the most expensive horse of all. A good destrier can cost as much as a small farm. Each knight also has one or two riding horses, for travelling, which are lighter and more comfortable. And there is a pack horse or a mule, for carrying his equipment.

[pause]

Each knight also has a squire. The squire is a young man who helps the knight. He looks after the horses, cleans the armour and the weapons, carries the equipment, and helps the knight to dress. Some squires are young men who hope to become Templars themselves one day. Others are servants, paid for their work. Guillaume's squire is a cheerful boy of sixteen called Thibaut, from a village nearby.

[pause]

{img:knight-and-squire-checking-horse-hooves-stable-yard-friendly}
The Rule says a lot about horses. The brothers must check on their horses every day, and several times. After Matins in the night, they go to the stables. After Prime in the morning, they go again. They make sure the horses have enough food and water, that they are healthy, and that their saddles and bridles are in good order. If a horse is ill, they must tell the brother in charge of the horses at once.

[pause]

The horses are fed well. Barley, oats and hay, and sometimes beans. The Rule says exactly how much each horse should receive. The brothers may not give their own horse more than the others, without permission. They may not race their horses, or ride them too fast without reason. They may not lend them to anyone. And they may not let them be damaged, through carelessness.

[long-pause]

{img:destrier-war-horse-white-caparison-red-cross-standing-calm-meadow}
Guillaume's war horse is a big grey stallion, called Ferrant, which means iron grey. He is strong, calm and very intelligent. When Guillaume first meets him, the horse looks at him for a long time, and then pushes his nose into Guillaume's chest. From that moment, they are friends. Every morning, Guillaume brushes him, talks to him, and gives him a piece of bread from his own breakfast, when nobody is looking.

[pause]

The Templars also keep the armour and weapons of the knights. Each knight has a coat of mail, made of thousands of little iron rings. A helmet. A shield, painted with the colours of the order, black and white. A sword, a lance, a dagger and a mace. Thibaut keeps them all clean and oiled, in a wooden chest in the armoury. In battle, the knights also wear a white surcoat over the mail, with the red cross on the front.

[pause]

{img:templar-banner-black-white-beauseant-flag-wind-blue-sky}
The banner of the Templars is a simple flag, divided into two parts, black and white. It is called the Beauséant. In battle, it is carried by a special brother, and it must always stay raised. As long as the banner is flying, the brothers must stay and gather around it. It is the heart of the Templars in any battle. But we will not follow them into battle tonight.

[long-pause]

On quiet summer evenings, after Vespers, Guillaume sometimes takes Ferrant out to the meadow behind the commandery, to let him graze. He sits on a stone in the grass, and watches the big horse eat, slowly, with his tail swishing at the flies. The sun goes down over the fields. The swallows dive over the meadow. And Guillaume thinks that a horse is perhaps the best friend a man can have. Ferrant lifts his head, looks at him, and seems to agree.

[pause]

In winter, the horses are given extra hay and warm blankets. The stables are cosy, full of the smell of straw and the soft sounds of horses chewing. On the coldest nights, Guillaume goes to the stables after Matins, and stands for a while beside Ferrant, warming his hands on the horse's neck. The big horse breathes out slowly, in clouds of warm steam, and leans gently against him.

[long-pause]

{img:paris-temple-fortress-1250-tall-keep-towers-walls-medieval-city}
The Templars were not only knights and farmers. They also became something unexpected. They became the bankers of Europe. And the centre of their banking was a great fortress in Paris, called the Temple.

[long-pause]

How did this happen? It began with the pilgrims. People going on pilgrimage to the Holy Land needed money for the long journey. But carrying gold and silver was dangerous. Robbers could steal it on the road. So the Templars offered a clever service. A pilgrim could leave his money at a Templar house in Europe. The Templars gave him a letter, saying how much he had left with them. Then, when he arrived in the Holy Land, he could show the letter at a Templar house there, and receive his money.

[pause]

It was one of the first kinds of cheque in history. The Templars had houses everywhere, from England to Acre, and they trusted each other. So money could travel safely, on paper, across the whole known world. Soon, not only pilgrims, but also merchants, nobles and even kings began to use this service. And the Templars became very good at handling money.

[pause]

{img:templar-treasurer-counting-coins-ledger-chests-stone-vault-candlelight}
The Temple in Paris is a huge fortress, outside the old walls of the city. It has a tall square tower, called the keep, with four round towers at its corners. Inside, there are the houses of the brothers, a large church, gardens and storerooms. And deep inside, there is the treasury. Strong rooms, with heavy doors and great iron locks, full of chests of gold and silver coins.

[pause]

The king of France keeps his own treasure at the Temple. The treasurer of the Temple is also, in a way, the treasurer of the king. He collects taxes, pays the king's bills, and keeps careful accounts. Kings of England also keep money with the Templars, at their Temple in London. Nobles leave their jewels and important documents there, for safekeeping. The Templars are trusted because they are honest, careful and well guarded.

[long-pause]

{img:merchant-depositing-money-templar-clerk-writing-letter-of-credit}
The Templars also lend money. To kings who need to pay for wars or crusades. To nobles who need to pay ransoms. To merchants and towns. They are not supposed to charge interest, because the Church forbids it. But they find other ways to earn something from their loans. They are very careful and very good at it. In those days, there were few people in Europe who understood money as well as the Templars.

[pause]

Right now, in 1250, the Templars are busy with a very important matter. The king of France, Louis the Ninth, has gone on crusade to Egypt. Things have gone badly. The king and many of his knights have been captured. To free them, a huge ransom must be paid. The Templars help to gather the money. News of it travels slowly, all the way to the commanderies of Champagne.

[pause]

{img:brothers-receiving-news-messenger-courtyard-letter-serious-faces}
Guillaume's commandery also handles money, on a smaller scale. Local people sometimes leave valuables with the brothers. Brother Hugues keeps careful accounts of all the rents, sales and payments. He writes them in a big book, and once a year, he checks them with the provincial master. Guillaume helps him sometimes, because he can read and count. He is surprised by how much there is to write down.

[long-pause]

One day, Brother Hugues takes Guillaume to Paris, to deliver the money of the commandery to the Temple. It is the first time Guillaume has been to Paris. He stares at the crowds, the churches, the bridges full of houses, and the great river Seine. And when they arrive at the Temple, he stares at the great dark keep, rising over the walls. Inside, he sees brothers from all over France, coming and going. And he understands, for the first time, how big and powerful his order really is.

[pause]

On the way home from Paris, Brother Hugues tells Guillaume that money is a dangerous thing for a monk. It can make a man proud and greedy. That is why the brothers must never think of the money as their own. It belongs to God, and to the poor, and to the Holy Land. Guillaume listens, and remembers. Years later, he will tell the same thing to the young brothers of his own house.

[long-pause]

{img:commander-reading-letter-to-young-templar-courtyard-autumn-leaves}
In the autumn of 1250, a letter arrives at the commandery. It comes from the master of the Templars in France. It says that the Templars in the Holy Land need more brothers. Many have been lost, in the war in Egypt. Young knights are needed, to go east. And Guillaume's name is on the list.

[long-pause]

Brother Hugues calls Guillaume into his room, and reads him the letter. Guillaume listens in silence. He has been at the commandery for less than a year. He has grown to love it. The chapel, the fields, the horses, the brothers. But he knew, when he made his vows, that this could happen. He knew that he might be sent to the Holy Land. And part of him has always wanted to go.

[pause]

He asks Brother Hugues one question. May Ferrant come with him? Brother Hugues smiles. He says that horses are always needed in the Holy Land. And that Ferrant will go with him, if the ships have room. Guillaume is so relieved that he almost forgets to bow before he leaves the room.

[pause]

{img:young-knight-saying-goodbye-mother-sister-manor-gate-autumn}
Before he leaves, Guillaume is allowed to visit his family one last time, with another brother as his companion. His mother holds him for a long time. His father tells him to be brave, and to be wise, which is harder. His sister gives him a little piece of embroidered cloth, with a cross on it, which she made herself. He asks Brother Hugues if he may keep it. Brother Hugues says yes, this time.

[pause]

On his last evening at the commandery, the brothers hold a little chapter for him. They pray for him, and give him their blessing. Brother Raoul, the old knight, gives him advice about the Holy Land. Drink only clean water, he says. Keep your head covered in the sun. Be patient with the people there, and learn their ways. And never forget why you went. Then the old man embraces him, and turns away quickly, so that nobody can see his eyes.

[long-pause]

{img:small-group-templars-riding-south-road-france-autumn-pack-horses}
Early the next morning, Guillaume rides out of the gate, on Ferrant, with Thibaut riding behind him on the pack horse. Two other young brothers from nearby commanderies are going with them. They ride south, through the heart of France. Through Burgundy, with its vineyards turning red and gold. Along the great river Rhône, past old Roman cities. Every night, they stay at a Templar house along the way.

[pause]

It takes them almost three weeks to reach the sea. Guillaume has never travelled so far. He sees mountains for the first time, far away, with snow on their tops. He sees the country change, from the green fields of the north, to the dry, rocky hills of the south, with olive trees, cypresses and the smell of wild herbs. The light becomes brighter. The air becomes warmer. And one afternoon, from the top of a hill, he sees the sea.

[pause]

{img:first-view-mediterranean-sea-from-hill-blue-water-young-knight-awe}
He stops Ferrant and stares. He has never seen the sea before. It is enormous, blue and shining, stretching to the horizon. The sunlight sparkles on the water like a thousand stars. Little boats with white sails are moving on it. And somewhere, beyond that horizon, very far away, is the Holy Land. Thibaut comes up beside him, and stares too. Neither of them says a word.

[long-pause]

That evening, they ride into the port of Marseille. It is a busy, noisy city, full of sailors, merchants and pilgrims, speaking many languages. In the harbour, there are dozens of ships. And among them, there are ships with a red cross on their sails. They belong to the Templars. In a few days, Guillaume will board one of them. His long journey to the east is about to begin.

[long-pause]

{img:templar-ship-marseille-harbour-loading-horses-pilgrims-red-cross-sail}
The Templars have their own ships. They need them to carry brothers, pilgrims, horses, weapons, food and money across the sea to the Holy Land. Every spring and every autumn, a fleet of ships sails from the ports of France, Italy and Spain to the east. The journey is called a passage. And Guillaume will sail on the autumn passage.

[long-pause]

The ship is big and round, with two masts and large sails, and high wooden castles at the front and the back. Below the deck, there are special places for horses, with slings to hold them up when the sea is rough. Loading the horses is difficult. They are led down a wooden ramp into the hold, through a door in the side of the ship, which is then sealed shut. Ferrant does not like it. Guillaume talks to him softly all the way, until the big horse calms down.

[pause]

On board, there are many people. Templar brothers, knights and sergeants, going to the Holy Land. Pilgrims, rich and poor, going to visit the holy places. Merchants with their goods. Sailors. There are men and women, old and young. Some are excited. Some are afraid. Many of them have never been to sea. They sleep on deck, or in small, crowded spaces below, on straw mattresses.

[pause]

{img:ship-sailing-open-mediterranean-blue-sea-sails-full-sunny-day}
The ship sails out of Marseille on a calm morning. The wind fills the sails. The city gets smaller and smaller, and then disappears. And soon there is nothing around them but the blue sea and the blue sky. The voyage to Acre takes about five or six weeks, depending on the wind. They sail along the coast of Italy, past Sicily, past the Greek islands, to Cyprus, and then to the coast of the Holy Land.

[pause]

The first days are hard. Many people are seasick, including Guillaume and Thibaut. They lie on the deck, pale and miserable, while the ship rolls up and down. But after a few days, they get used to it. And then the voyage becomes quite pleasant. The weather is warm. The sea is blue. Dolphins swim beside the ship. And at night, the stars are brighter than Guillaume has ever seen them.

[long-pause]

{img:pilgrims-praying-on-ship-deck-evening-sunset-priest-sailors}
On board, the Templars keep their Rule, as best they can. They say their prayers at the right hours, standing on the deck. They eat their meals together. The pilgrims join them for Mass on Sundays, which a priest says on deck. In the evenings, the pilgrims sing hymns, and the sailors sing their own songs, and sometimes they all sing together, under the stars.

[pause]

Guillaume makes friends with an old pilgrim from Flanders, a merchant called Jan. He is going to the Holy Land for the third time, to pray for his wife, who passed away last year. He tells Guillaume about Acre, about the markets and the churches, and about the people of the east. He teaches him a few words of Arabic. Peace be with you. Thank you. How much does it cost. Guillaume repeats them, until he can say them properly.

[pause]

{img:storm-at-sea-sailors-lowering-sails-ship-rolling-grey-waves-distance}
Halfway across, near Crete, there is a storm. The wind howls and the waves are huge. The ship rolls so much that people are thrown from one side to the other. Guillaume goes down into the hold, to calm Ferrant, who is kicking and neighing. He stays with him all night, holding his head, talking to him, while the ship groans and creaks around them. In the morning, the storm passes. Everyone is safe. Ferrant is tired, but unhurt.

[long-pause]

They stop at Cyprus for a few days, to take on fresh water and food. Cyprus is a beautiful island, with mountains and orange groves, ruled by a Christian king. The Templars once owned the whole island, for a short time. Then they sail on, toward the east. And one morning, at dawn, a sailor at the top of the mast shouts out. Land! There, low on the horizon, is a pale line of coast. The Holy Land. Guillaume falls to his knees on the deck, and prays.

[pause]

In the evenings on the ship, the sea is calm and the sky is full of colour. The sailors sit together on the deck, mending ropes and telling stories. The pilgrims pray quietly. Guillaume sits at the rail, looking out over the darkening water, with the warm wind on his face. Sometimes, he goes down into the hold to visit Ferrant, and stands with him for a while in the creaking darkness.

[long-pause]

{img:acre-1250-city-from-sea-walls-towers-churches-harbour-ships-morning}
The ship sails into the harbour of Acre. It is the biggest and richest city of the crusaders, and the capital of what is left of the Kingdom of Jerusalem. It stands on a point of land, sticking out into the sea, surrounded by strong walls and towers. As the ship comes closer, Guillaume sees the towers of churches, the domes of old mosques, the roofs of houses and palaces, and the masts of hundreds of ships in the harbour.

[long-pause]

Acre is a very busy city. About forty thousand people live here. Maybe more. It is a great trading port. Ships from Venice, Genoa and Pisa come here, to buy silk, spices, sugar, glass and cotton, which have come from the east by camel caravan. The merchants of each Italian city have their own quarter, with their own streets, churches, warehouses and towers. They often quarrel with each other.

[pause]

When Guillaume steps off the ship, onto the stone quay, he is overwhelmed. The noise. The crowds. The colours. The smells. Sailors are shouting in Italian. Merchants are bargaining in Arabic. Porters are carrying huge loads on their backs. Camels are kneeling by the warehouses, with their strange, proud faces. The sun is bright and hot, even in late autumn. And the air smells of spices, salt, fish and dust.

[pause]

{img:acre-market-street-1250-spices-silk-merchants-many-peoples-awnings}
The people of Acre come from everywhere. There are Franks, as the people from western Europe are called here. French, Italians, Germans, English. There are local Christians, who speak Arabic, and have lived here for centuries. There are Greeks, Armenians and Syrians, with their own churches. There are Jews, with their synagogues and their scholars. And there are Muslims, merchants and workers, who come and go from the lands around.

[pause]

In the markets of Acre, you can buy things that Guillaume has never seen before. Oranges and lemons, dates and figs, pomegranates and melons. Sugar, which is grown in fields near the coast, and boiled in big sugar mills. Silk and cotton cloth in beautiful colours. Glass from Tyre, the best in the world. Spices from India, pepper, cinnamon, cloves and ginger. And many strange and wonderful foods.

[long-pause]

{img:knight-tasting-orange-market-stall-local-merchant-smiling-sunny}
At a market stall, Jan, the old pilgrim from Flanders, buys Guillaume an orange. Guillaume has never eaten one. He peels it, slowly, and the sweet smell fills the air. He tastes it. It is sweet and sour, and full of juice. He laughs out loud. The merchant behind the stall laughs too, and says something in Arabic. Guillaume answers with one of the words that Jan taught him. Thank you. The merchant smiles, and gives him another orange, for free.

[pause]

Many of the Franks who have lived in Acre for a long time have adopted the ways of the east. They wear light, loose clothes of cotton and silk, which are cooler in the heat. They wrap cloths around their heads against the sun. They eat local food, and bathe in the bathhouses. Some speak Arabic. An earlier writer said that those who were once Westerners had now become Easterners. Guillaume finds it all very strange at first. But he is curious, and he likes it.

[pause]

{img:acre-churches-towers-sunset-from-wall-city-roofs-sea-glow}
Acre is also a city of churches. There are dozens of them, built by the different peoples and orders. The great cathedral of the Holy Cross. Churches of the Italians, the French, the English. And the churches of the military orders. The Hospitallers have a huge building, with a hospital that can care for hundreds of sick people. The Teutonic Knights, from Germany, have their own quarter. And the Templars have theirs, at the end of the city, by the sea.

[long-pause]

That evening, Guillaume and the other new brothers walk through the streets of Acre, to the Templar quarter. The sun is setting over the sea, turning everything golden. From the churches, the bells are ringing for Vespers. From somewhere else, far away, a voice is calling Muslims to prayer. Guillaume stops for a moment and listens to both sounds, mixing together in the warm evening air. Then he walks on, toward the great dark towers by the sea.

[long-pause]

{img:templar-fortress-acre-by-sea-great-tower-golden-lions-waves-walls}
At the southwestern corner of Acre, right by the sea, there stands the great fortress of the Templars. It is the headquarters of the whole order, the home of the Grand Master. It is one of the strongest buildings in the city. And it is the place where Guillaume will now live.

[long-pause]

The fortress is called the Temple. Its walls are very thick, built of big blocks of pale stone. At the entrance, there is a huge tower. On top of each of its corners, there is a small turret, and on each turret, there is a lion, made of gilded copper, shining in the sun. The waves of the sea break against the walls on one side. And on the other side, the fortress looks out over the city.

[pause]

Inside, there is a big courtyard, with buildings all around it. There is a church, the most important Templar church in the world. There are halls and dormitories for the brothers, stables for hundreds of horses, storerooms, kitchens and workshops. There is a treasury, where the money of the order is kept. And there is a palace for the Grand Master, with fine rooms looking out over the sea.

[pause]

{img:templar-grand-master-elderly-white-mantle-council-hall-brothers}
The Grand Master is the head of all the Templars, everywhere in the world. He is elected by the brothers, for life. He has great power. But he must also obey the Rule, and listen to the advice of his council of senior brothers. He has his own household, with a chaplain, a clerk, servants, and several horses. Guillaume sees him only from a distance, a tall, grey man in a white mantle, walking slowly across the courtyard, with other brothers around him.

[pause]

Life in the Temple of Acre is much like life in the commandery in Champagne. The bells ring for the hours. The brothers pray, eat in silence, and look after their horses. But there are many more brothers here. Hundreds of them. Knights, sergeants, chaplains, squires and servants. And there are more duties. Guarding the walls. Escorting travellers and merchants. Patrolling the countryside around the city.

[long-pause]

{img:secret-tunnel-templar-acre-stone-vaulted-passage-lantern-light}
The Templars of Acre have a secret. Underneath the city, there is a tunnel. It runs from the Templar fortress, under the streets, all the way to the harbour on the other side of the city. It is about three hundred and fifty metres long, cut into the rock and roofed with stone. The brothers can use it to move people, money and goods from the fortress to the port, safely and secretly. Very few people know about it.

[pause]

Guillaume is given a place in the dormitory of the young knights. His bed is near a window, through which he can hear the sea. At night, he listens to the waves breaking against the walls, below. It is a soothing sound. It reminds him of the wind in the trees at home. Ferrant is given a stall in the great stables, among hundreds of other horses. Every morning, Guillaume visits him, and brings him an orange peel, which the horse loves.

[pause]

{img:young-templar-looking-out-window-at-sea-night-moon-reflection}
On his first night in Acre, Guillaume cannot sleep. He gets up quietly, and goes to the window. The moon is shining on the sea, making a silver path to the horizon. Somewhere out there, far to the west, is France, and Champagne, and the little commandery with its chapel and its dovecote. And somewhere to the east, beyond the hills, is Jerusalem, which he may never see. He thinks about both. Then he says a Paternoster, thirteen times, and goes back to bed.

[long-pause]

In the morning, the bell rings for Prime. The sun rises over the hills of Galilee, and turns the walls of the fortress golden. The gulls are crying over the harbour. The city is waking up. And Guillaume puts on his white mantle, and goes to the chapel, to begin his first day as a Templar in the Holy Land.

[pause]

Before long, Guillaume knows every corner of the Temple. The cool, dark storerooms, smelling of spices and grain. The busy stables. The kitchens, where the cooks make dishes he has never tasted before, with rice, lentils, dates and lemons. And the high wall by the sea, where the brothers stand guard at night, watching the lights of the ships out on the dark water.

[long-pause]

{img:chateau-pelerin-atlit-castle-on-peninsula-sea-massive-walls-towers}
In the spring, Guillaume is sent south along the coast, to the greatest castle of the Templars. It is called Château Pèlerin, which means the Pilgrims' Castle. It stands on a small rocky peninsula, sticking out into the sea, about thirty kilometres south of Acre. Today, the place is called Atlit.

[long-pause]

The Templars built the castle about thirty years before our story, in 1218. Pilgrims helped them to build it, which is how it got its name. While digging the foundations, the builders found an old wall, and a lot of ancient coins. So they had money to help pay for the castle. And they found a spring of fresh water, inside the walls. That was a great blessing. A castle with its own water can never be forced to surrender by thirst.

[pause]

The castle is enormous. On the land side, there are two huge walls, one behind the other, with great square towers. The inner wall is about thirty metres high, and twelve metres thick. Nothing could break through it. On the other three sides, there is the sea. The castle has its own little harbour, where ships can bring supplies. In all its history, Château Pèlerin was never taken by force.

[pause]

{img:inside-castle-atlit-courtyard-church-twelve-sided-great-hall-vaults}
Inside the walls, there is a whole little town. There is a church with twelve sides, like the Church of the Holy Sepulchre in Jerusalem. There are great vaulted halls, stables, storerooms and kitchens. There are baths. There are gardens, orchards and even fishponds. The castle can hold thousands of people, if needed. In times of danger, the people from the villages around come inside the walls for safety.

[pause]

Around the castle, the Templars own villages, fields, orchards and vineyards. Local peasants live in the villages and farm the land. Many of them are Arabic-speaking Christians, and some are Muslims. There are also salt pans by the sea, where salt is made by letting seawater dry in the sun. And there are fields of sugar cane. The castle is like a big commandery, but by the sea, in a much warmer land.

[long-pause]

{img:sugar-cane-fields-coast-holy-land-workers-mill-sea-in-distance}
Guillaume is amazed by the sugar cane. It grows tall, like giant grass, in the warm, wet fields near the coast. The workers cut it, and take it to a mill, where it is crushed, and the sweet juice is boiled in big copper pots, until it turns into brown sugar. In Europe, sugar is very rare and expensive, a luxury for the rich. Here, the brothers can taste it every day. Thibaut eats so much of it that he gets a stomach ache.

[pause]

The work at the castle is calm, most of the time. The brothers guard the walls, look after the horses, check the fields and the villages, and patrol the coast road. Sometimes there is news of danger, and everyone must be ready. But most days are peaceful. Guillaume rides along the beach in the morning, with Ferrant, along the edge of the waves. The horse loves the sea. He splashes in the shallow water, like a young colt.

[pause]

{img:knight-riding-horse-along-beach-waves-splashing-morning-castle-behind}
In the evenings, Guillaume likes to sit on the wall of the castle, looking out over the sea. The sun sets in the west, over the water, turning everything red and gold. Fishing boats come back to the little harbour. The brothers sing Vespers in the twelve-sided church. And Guillaume thinks that this castle, standing between the sea and the hills, is one of the most beautiful places he has ever seen.

[long-pause]

He stays at Château Pèlerin for two years. He learns to speak some Arabic, from the local workers. He learns to ride in the heat, and to live in a land that is so different from his own. He becomes friends with some of the local people. And he grows from a young man into a man. When he leaves, to return to Acre, he looks back at the castle from the coast road for a long time, until it disappears behind the dunes.

[long-pause]

{img:pilgrims-on-road-holy-land-templar-escort-riders-hills-olive-trees}
The Templars were founded to protect pilgrims. And even in 1250, after Jerusalem has been lost, pilgrims still come to the Holy Land. They visit the holy places that are still in Christian hands, or that they are allowed to visit under peace agreements. And the Templars still ride beside them, to keep them safe on the roads.

[long-pause]

Every spring, the ships from Europe bring hundreds of pilgrims to Acre. Men and women, rich and poor, young and old. Some come to pray for a loved one. Some come to ask forgiveness for their sins. Some come because they have made a promise to God. Some come simply because they have always dreamed of seeing the places where Jesus lived. For many of them, it is the greatest journey of their lives.

[pause]

From Acre, the pilgrims travel to the holy places in groups. One of the most popular journeys is to Nazareth, in the hills of Galilee, where Jesus grew up. It is about a day's ride from Acre. And the Templars often provide an escort. A group of knights and sergeants rides with the pilgrims, at the front and at the back, watching the road.

[pause]

{img:nazareth-hills-village-church-pilgrims-arriving-evening-light}
Guillaume rides with such a group, one spring. There are about fifty pilgrims, from France, Germany and England. Among them is Jan, the old merchant from Flanders, whom Guillaume met on the ship. They are happy to see each other again. They ride side by side, and talk about everything that has happened since the voyage. Jan says that Guillaume looks much older now, and browner from the sun.

[pause]

The road winds up into the hills, through olive groves and little villages. The spring flowers are blooming everywhere. Red anemones, yellow daisies, purple irises. The air smells of herbs and earth. The pilgrims sing hymns as they walk. Some walk barefoot, as a sign of humility. In the evening, they reach Nazareth, a small town on a hillside, with a big church built over the place where, they believe, the angel came to Mary.

[long-pause]

{img:pilgrims-praying-in-church-nazareth-candles-kneeling-tears}
In the church, the pilgrims kneel and pray. Many of them cry. They have travelled thousands of kilometres, for months, to be here. Jan kneels in a corner, and prays for his wife for a long time. Guillaume kneels beside him. He prays for his family, for the brothers at home, for old Brother Raoul. And he prays that he may be a good Templar, and a good man.

[pause]

The pilgrims also visit the Sea of Galilee, a beautiful lake surrounded by hills, where Jesus once walked by the shore and talked to the fishermen. The water is calm and blue. Fishermen are still fishing there, in little boats, just as in the time of the Gospels. The pilgrims fill little bottles with water from the lake, to take home. Some of them wade into the water, and wash their faces.

[pause]

{img:sea-of-galilee-calm-blue-lake-fishing-boats-hills-pilgrims-shore}
Many pilgrims collect souvenirs. Little bottles of holy water or oil. Small badges made of lead, with a picture of the holy place, which they sew onto their hats. Palm leaves from Jericho. Stones and pebbles from the holy places. When they go home, they will show these things to their families and friends, and tell the story of their journey for the rest of their lives.

[long-pause]

On the way back to Acre, Jan rides beside Guillaume in silence for a long time. Then he says that this will be his last pilgrimage. He is getting old. He will go home to Flanders, to his children and grandchildren, and stay there. He thanks Guillaume for keeping them safe. At the harbour of Acre, they say goodbye. Jan gives Guillaume a little lead badge, with a picture of the church of Nazareth. Guillaume asks the commander for permission to keep it. And this time too, the answer is yes.

[pause]

At night, on the road, the pilgrims sleep in a guesthouse in a little village, or in tents. The Templars take turns standing guard, while the others sleep. Guillaume stands under the stars, with his cloak around him, listening to the crickets and the soft breathing of the sleeping pilgrims. The hills are silver in the moonlight. And everything is very quiet and very peaceful.

[long-pause]

{img:frankish-and-muslim-merchants-trading-acre-market-friendly-handshake}
Today, people often imagine the crusades as a time of endless war between Christians and Muslims. And there were wars, terrible ones. But there were also long years of peace, and of daily life side by side. Christians and Muslims traded with each other, worked together, made treaties, and sometimes even became friends.

[long-pause]

In the markets of Acre, Muslim merchants from Damascus sell their goods every day. Spices, cloth, glass and fruit. Caravans of camels come and go from the cities of Syria. Frankish merchants buy and sell with them. Muslim workers come into the city to work. Muslim peasants farm the land around the castles of the Templars, and pay their rents. And Muslim travellers pass through the city, on their way to Mecca, or to visit family.

[pause]

The rulers on both sides often make truces. These are peace agreements, for a few years at a time. During a truce, people can travel freely. Merchants can trade. Pilgrims can visit holy places. Sometimes, the truces last for ten years or more. During these times, life in the Holy Land is quiet and peaceful. The Templars themselves sometimes negotiate these truces, because they know the local rulers and their languages.

[pause]

{img:muslim-scholar-praying-small-mosque-templar-knight-standing-respectfully-door}
There is an old, famous story, written by a Muslim nobleman called Usama, about a hundred years before our story. He was a poet, a soldier and a diplomat, from Syria. When he visited Jerusalem, in a time of peace, he went to pray in a small mosque near the al-Aqsa Mosque, which the Templars occupied. He wrote that the Templars, who were his friends, let him pray there undisturbed.

[pause]

But one day, a Frank who had just arrived from Europe grabbed him and turned him around, telling him to pray toward the east, in the Christian way. The Templars rushed over and pulled the man away. They apologised to Usama, and explained that the man was new, and did not understand the ways of the east. Usama wrote that those Franks who had lived in the east for a long time were much better than those who had just arrived.

[long-pause]

{img:arab-physician-teaching-frankish-knight-herbs-medicine-courtyard}
Guillaume, too, learns much from the people of the east. In Acre, he becomes friends with a local Christian, an old man called Elias, who speaks Arabic and Greek. Elias sells medicines and herbs in a small shop near the Templar quarter. He knows a great deal about medicine, from old Arabic books. He teaches Guillaume about herbs and remedies, and about the stars, which the Arab scholars have studied for centuries.

[pause]

The Franks learned many things from the east during the crusades. New foods, like sugar, lemons, apricots and rice. New spices. New kinds of cloth, like muslin and damask, named after the cities of Mosul and Damascus. New ideas in medicine, mathematics and astronomy. Numbers, like the ones we use today, came from India through the Arab world to Europe. And many words, like sugar, cotton, lemon and syrup, came into European languages from Arabic.

[pause]

{img:old-man-and-young-knight-sitting-rooftop-acre-evening-looking-at-stars}
On warm evenings, Guillaume sometimes sits with Elias on the flat roof of his house, looking at the stars. Elias tells him the Arabic names of the stars. Aldebaran, the follower. Betelgeuse, the hand of the giant. Vega, the falling eagle. Many stars still have Arabic names today. Guillaume repeats them, slowly. Below them, the city is settling down for the night. Lamps are being lit. And from the minarets of the few mosques that remain, and from the bell towers of the churches, the evening prayers are called.

[long-pause]

Guillaume thinks a lot about these things. He came to the Holy Land to defend it, as a soldier of Christ. He still believes in that. But he has also learned that the people on the other side are people too. Merchants, farmers, scholars, families, just like his own. He thinks that perhaps peace is a better way to serve God than war. He does not say this out loud. But he thinks it, quietly, on the roof under the stars.

[long-pause]

{img:older-templar-knight-grey-in-beard-training-young-brothers-acre-courtyard}
The years pass. Guillaume serves in the Holy Land for more than fifteen years. He lives in Acre, at Château Pèlerin, and at other Templar castles. He rides on patrols and escorts. He guards walls and gates. He sits on the council of the house. And slowly, he becomes an experienced, respected brother of the order.

[long-pause]

There are difficult years. Wars break out, and then truces are made, and then wars break out again. Some of Guillaume's brothers are lost. The Christians lose some of their castles and towns. Each time, the brothers pray for those who have passed away, and carry on. We will not follow the battles. It is enough to say that Guillaume was brave, and fair, and that he came through them.

[pause]

Thibaut, his squire, becomes a sergeant brother of the order, with a brown mantle and a red cross. He is very proud. He becomes the brother in charge of the horses at Château Pèlerin, because he knows more about horses than anyone. Guillaume and Thibaut remain close friends, all their lives. They are like brothers, which, of course, they are.

[pause]

{img:old-grey-war-horse-retired-grazing-meadow-castle-walls-evening-light}
Ferrant, the big grey horse, grows old. He carries Guillaume for many years, faithfully. But at last, he is too old to work. Guillaume asks permission for him to be retired, and to spend his last years in a meadow near Château Pèlerin, by the sea. Permission is given. The old horse grazes there in the sun, and walks in the shallow waves in the mornings. When Guillaume visits, Ferrant still lifts his head and trots over to him, and pushes his nose into his chest, as he did on the first day.

[pause]

Guillaume learns many things in these years. He learns Arabic well enough to talk with the local people and with merchants. He learns to read and write better, and helps the brothers with their accounts and letters. He learns to judge people, and to listen before he speaks. And he learns patience, which, Brother Raoul told him long ago, is the most important virtue of a Templar.

[long-pause]

{img:templar-letter-from-france-sealed-reading-by-window-sea-light}
Sometimes letters come from France. Letters from the commandery, from Brother Hugues, telling him the news. The harvest was good. The mill has been repaired. Old Brother Raoul passed away peacefully, in his sleep, at a great age, and was buried in the little chapel. Guillaume reads that letter many times. He prays for the old man, who taught him so much. And he remembers his words about the Rule being like a good saddle.

[pause]

Letters also come from his family. His father has passed away. His older brother now runs the family lands. His sister has married, and has three children. His mother is old, but still well, and still prays for him every day. Guillaume writes back whenever he can, with the permission of his commander. He tells them about the Holy Land, about oranges and sugar, the castle by the sea, and the stars.

[pause]

{img:templar-knight-praying-alone-chapel-acre-candle-quiet-evening}
In the evenings, in the chapel of the Temple of Acre, Guillaume often stays a little longer after Compline, when the other brothers have gone to bed. He kneels in the dim light of a single candle, and prays. For his family. For the brothers who have passed away. For the people of the Holy Land, of every faith. For peace. And for Ferrant, in his meadow by the sea.

[long-pause]

And then, one day, after fifteen years in the east, a letter comes from the Grand Master. Guillaume is to return to France. Brother Hugues, the commander of his old commandery in Champagne, has grown too old to run the house. And the order has chosen Guillaume to take his place. He is to be the new commander. Guillaume reads the letter twice. Then he goes out to the meadow by the sea, to say goodbye to an old grey horse.

[long-pause]

{img:templar-ship-sailing-west-sunset-mediterranean-knight-at-rail}
Guillaume sails back to France in the spring, on a Templar ship, from Acre to Marseille. He stands at the rail as the ship leaves the harbour, and watches the city get smaller. The towers of the Temple, with their golden lions, shining in the morning sun. The walls, the churches, the hills of Galilee behind. He has lived here for fifteen years. Half of his life as a grown man. He does not know if he will ever see it again.

[long-pause]

Thibaut stands beside him. He is staying in the Holy Land, at Château Pèlerin, with his horses. He has come to the harbour to say goodbye. The two men embrace on the quay, for a long time. Thibaut says that he will look after Ferrant. Guillaume says that he knows. Then Thibaut stands on the quay and waves, until the ship is far out at sea, and he is only a little dot among the crowd.

[pause]

The voyage home is calm. The sea is blue. The wind is kind. Guillaume says his prayers on deck, and talks with the other passengers. Pilgrims going home, merchants, and a few other brothers returning to Europe. He thinks about the past fifteen years. About everything he has seen and learned. And about the little commandery in Champagne, which he left as a young man, and to which he now returns as its commander.

[pause]

{img:knight-riding-north-through-france-spring-fields-poppies-vineyards}
From Marseille, he rides north, through the whole of France, along the same roads he took fifteen years before. Past the olive trees and the cypresses of the south. Along the river Rhône. Through the vineyards of Burgundy. Everything looks the same, and yet different. Or perhaps it is he who has changed. He sees things he never noticed before. He notices how green everything is, after the dry hills of the Holy Land.

[pause]

At last, one evening in late spring, he rides over a low hill, and sees the commandery below him, in the valley. The little chapel with its bell tower. The great barn. The dovecote. The fields of young wheat, green and waving. The mill on the river. It looks exactly the same as when he left. He stops his horse on the hill, and looks at it for a long time, with tears in his eyes.

[long-pause]

{img:old-commander-greeting-returning-knight-gate-embrace-evening-light}
At the gate, old Brother Hugues is waiting for him. He is very old now, white-haired and bent, leaning on a stick. But his eyes are still kind and clear. Guillaume gets off his horse and kneels before him. Brother Hugues raises him up, and embraces him. Welcome home, my brother, he says. Welcome home.

[pause]

The brothers of the commandery have gathered in the courtyard to greet him. Some of them he knows from before. Some are new, young men who have joined since he left. They look at him with curiosity and respect. A knight who has served fifteen years in the Holy Land. Who has seen Acre and Château Pèlerin, and Nazareth and the Sea of Galilee. To them, he is almost a legend.

[pause]

{img:returning-templar-in-chapel-kneeling-familiar-candles-evening-vespers}
That evening, Guillaume goes to the little chapel for Vespers. He kneels in the same place where he knelt as a young man, on the night he was received into the order. The same painted walls. The same candles. The same smell of incense and old stone. The brothers sing the same psalms. And Guillaume closes his eyes, and listens, and feels that he has come home.

[long-pause]

After Compline, he walks out into the courtyard. The stars are coming out. The air smells of cut grass and wood smoke. The pigeons are cooing softly in the dovecote. An owl is calling from the woods, just as it did on his first night here, so many years ago. And Guillaume stands in the dark courtyard, under the stars, and thinks of Elias on his rooftop in Acre, naming the stars in Arabic. They are the same stars, here and there. That thought comforts him.

[long-pause]

{img:templar-commander-middle-aged-desk-accounts-book-window-farm-view}
Guillaume becomes the commander of the house. Brother Hugues stays with him, as an adviser, for two more years, until he passes away peacefully, one winter night. He is buried in the chapel, next to old Brother Raoul. Guillaume holds a long vigil for him. And then he takes up the work that the old man taught him.

[long-pause]

Being a commander is a big responsibility. Guillaume must run the whole estate. The fields, the vineyards, the mill and the farm. He must manage the workers and the servants, and deal with the peasants who rent the land. He must keep the accounts, and send money to the Holy Land every year. He must welcome guests and travellers, and give food to the poor at the gate. And he must look after the brothers, and make sure they follow the Rule.

[pause]

Guillaume is a good commander. He is fair and patient. He listens before he decides. He uses what he learned in the east. He plants new crops in the garden, from seeds he brought back. He grows a few lemon trees in pots, which must be carried indoors in winter. They never give much fruit, in the cold of Champagne. But the brothers like to smell the flowers in spring.

[pause]

{img:commander-teaching-young-squires-horses-meadow-commandery-afternoon}
He takes a special interest in the young brothers and squires. He teaches them to ride, and to look after their horses, as Brother Raoul once taught him. He tells them stories about the Holy Land. About Acre and its markets, about the castle by the sea, about the pilgrims and the stars. He tells them that the people of the east are people like them. And he tells them about a big grey horse called Ferrant, who loved oranges and the sea.

[pause]

He also takes care of the poor. Every day, after the meals, he makes sure that the bread and the food are given out at the gate. He knows the poor people of the village by name. When the harvest is bad, he opens the granaries, and gives grain to the families who need it. The people of the village come to love the commander, with his grey beard and his brown face, who speaks a little Arabic and grows lemon trees in Champagne.

[long-pause]

{img:commander-giving-grain-to-villagers-barn-door-bad-harvest-winter}
News comes from the Holy Land, from time to time, carried by brothers returning to Europe. It is not always good news. The Christians are losing more castles and towns. The great castle of Safed has fallen. Jaffa and Antioch have fallen. Acre is still standing, and Château Pèlerin. But the Holy Land of the crusaders is getting smaller and smaller. Guillaume prays for his brothers there every day.

[pause]

A letter comes, one autumn, from Thibaut. It is written by a clerk, because Thibaut never learned to write well. It says that Ferrant has passed away, peacefully, in his meadow by the sea, at a great age. Thibaut buried him on the hillside, under an olive tree, where he can see the waves. Guillaume reads the letter alone in his room. Then he goes to the stables, and stands for a long time with his hand on the neck of a young grey horse, who reminds him a little of the old one.

[pause]

{img:old-commander-walking-commandery-garden-lemon-trees-pots-evening}
Guillaume grows old in the commandery. His beard turns white. His back grows stiff. He walks with a stick, like Brother Hugues once did. But he still gets up for Matins every night. He still checks the horses. He still welcomes the poor at the gate. And on summer evenings, he still sits in the meadow behind the commandery, watching the horses graze, and the swallows dive over the grass, as the sun goes down.

[long-pause]

He passes away peacefully, in his bed, at a great age, with his brothers around him, praying. He is buried in the little chapel, next to Brother Hugues and Brother Raoul. On his grave, there is a simple stone, with his name, and a red cross. And in his hand, the brothers place a small lead badge from Nazareth, and a little piece of embroidered cloth, made long ago by his sister.

[long-pause]

{img:acre-1291-last-days-ships-leaving-harbour-smoke-distance-later}
In 1291, about forty years after Guillaume first arrived there, the city of Acre fell. After a long siege, the Mamluk army of Egypt broke through the walls. The Templars defended their fortress by the sea to the very end. We will not follow those last days. Many people escaped by ship, to Cyprus. A few months later, the Templars left Château Pèlerin, the castle that had never been taken. They sailed away, and never came back. The crusader kingdom was over.

[long-pause]

The Templars moved their headquarters to Cyprus. They hoped to return to the Holy Land one day. But without the Holy Land, many people in Europe began to ask what the Templars were for. They were still rich, with thousands of houses and farms across Europe. They were still powerful bankers. And they answered only to the Pope. Some people were jealous of them. And some people owed them a lot of money.

[pause]

One of those people was the king of France, Philip the Fourth, who was called Philip the Fair. He was always short of money. He owed the Templars a great deal. And he wanted their wealth. So he made a plan. On Friday, the thirteenth of October, 1307, at dawn, the king's soldiers arrested all the Templars in France, at the same moment, in every commandery in the country. Among them were many old men, who had never been to war.

[pause]

{img:templar-arrest-1307-soldiers-at-commandery-gate-dawn-sorrow-distance}
The Templars were accused of terrible things. Of denying Christ, and of strange and secret rituals. Today, most historians believe that these accusations were false, invented by the king's men to destroy the order. Many of the brothers were treated very harshly, and some said whatever their questioners wanted to hear. We will not look at those sad years closely. It was one of the great injustices of the Middle Ages.

[pause]

In 1312, the Pope dissolved the order of the Templars. The Templar order ceased to exist. Its lands and houses were given, mostly, to the Hospitallers, the other great military order. In 1314, the last Grand Master, an old man called Jacques de Molay, passed away in Paris. Before the end, he declared that the order had been innocent. And many people, even then, believed him.

[long-pause]

{img:hospitallers-taking-over-commandery-new-banner-chapel-quiet-later}
In other countries, the Templars were treated more kindly. In Portugal, the king created a new order, called the Order of Christ. Many former Templars joined it, and it took over their lands and castles. Later, the ships of the great Portuguese explorers sailed with the red cross of the Order of Christ on their sails. In Spain, Germany and England, most Templars were allowed to live out their lives in peace, in other monasteries.

[pause]

In 2001, a historian found an old document in the archives of the Vatican, in Rome. It is called the Chinon Parchment. It shows that, in 1308, the Pope had secretly questioned the leaders of the Templars, and had forgiven them. He had found that they were not heretics. But under pressure from the king of France, he still dissolved the order a few years later.

[pause]

{img:old-chapel-commandery-ruin-ivy-countryside-later-centuries}
After the Templars were gone, the legends began. Stories of hidden treasure, which the Templars had supposedly smuggled away before their arrest. Stories of secret knowledge, holy objects and mysterious ceremonies. Over the centuries, these legends grew and grew. Writers and filmmakers still love them today. But most of them are only stories. The real Templars were monks and knights, farmers and bankers, who lived simple, disciplined lives, and who mostly never saw a treasure.

[long-pause]

And their commanderies? Many of them still stand, all over Europe. Some became farms. Some became churches. Some fell into ruin. In the countryside of Champagne, and all over France, England and Spain, you can still find little stone chapels and great barns, built by the Templars, almost eight hundred years ago. The people who live near them often do not even know who built them. But the stones remember.

[long-pause]

{img:temple-church-london-round-nave-effigies-knights-stone-floor-today}
Today, there are still many places where you can see the world of the Templars. One of the most beautiful is in London. It is the Temple Church, near the river Thames. It was built by the Templars in the twelfth century, as the church of their headquarters in England. Its main part is round, like the Church of the Holy Sepulchre in Jerusalem.

[long-pause]

Inside the Temple Church, on the floor, there are stone figures of knights, lying on their backs, with their swords and their shields, and their legs crossed. They are tombs of knights who were friends of the Templars, from the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. The church was badly damaged by bombs in the Second World War. But it has been carefully restored. And it is still a working church today, with beautiful music.

[pause]

In Portugal, in the town of Tomar, there is a great castle and convent of the Templars, called the Convent of Christ. In the middle of it, there is the Templar church, round and full of paintings and gold. It is one of the most beautiful buildings in Portugal. Visitors walk through its cloisters and its halls, and imagine the knights who once prayed there.

[pause]

{img:acre-today-old-city-walls-sea-harbour-fishing-boats-sunset}
In Acre, which is now in Israel, the old city still stands by the sea, with its walls and its harbour. Much of it was rebuilt after the time of the crusaders. But under the streets, there are still the great vaulted halls of the Hospitallers, which have been dug out and can be visited. And in 1994, a man trying to fix a blocked drain discovered something extraordinary. The Templar tunnel.

[pause]

The tunnel, which once ran from the Templar fortress to the harbour, had been forgotten for seven hundred years. Today, visitors can walk through it, with lights along the way. It is narrow and cool, with a curved stone roof, cut into the rock. Walking through it, you can imagine the brothers hurrying along it, carrying messages, money or supplies, in the time of Guillaume. The fortress itself is gone. It fell into the sea long ago. But the tunnel remains.

[long-pause]

{img:atlit-castle-ruins-today-peninsula-sea-waves-sunset-quiet}
Château Pèlerin, at Atlit, is still there too, standing in ruins on its little peninsula by the sea. Its great walls are broken now, and its towers have fallen. The place is used by the army today, and is not open to visitors. But from the beach nearby, you can see its tall, broken walls, rising against the sky, with the waves breaking at its feet. Seabirds nest in its stones. And the sea still shines around it, as it did when Guillaume rode along the beach with Ferrant.

[pause]

In France, many Templar commanderies still stand in the countryside. Some have been restored, and can be visited. Their chapels, their barns and their dovecotes. In some of them, there are little museums. In Champagne, near Troyes, in the village of Payns, where Hugues de Payens was born, there is a small museum about the Templars. And in Troyes itself, the old city where the order was recognised, you can still walk through the narrow medieval streets.

[pause]

{img:restored-templar-commandery-france-today-chapel-dovecote-visitors}
In Paris, the great Temple fortress is gone. It was pulled down in the early eighteen hundreds. But the area is still called the Temple. There is a little park, called the Square du Temple, where the fortress once stood. And there is a metro station called Temple. Most Parisians who use it do not think about the knights. But the name has survived, for more than eight hundred years.

[long-pause]

And all over the world, people still read and write about the Templars, and visit their places. Some for the legends. Some for the history. Some simply because they are moved by the story of these men, who promised to live simply, to pray, to share, and to protect travellers on dangerous roads. It is a good promise. And it is a promise that people still understand today.

[long-pause]

{img:templar-commandery-champagne-evening-sunset-chapel-bell-fields-golden}
So we leave the Templars tonight, in the year 1250, as the evening falls. In the countryside of Champagne, the sun is setting over the fields. The wheat is golden. The vineyards are green on the hills. And the little chapel of the commandery is glowing in the last warm light. The bell is ringing for Vespers, softly, across the quiet fields.

[long-pause]

In the courtyard, the brothers are walking slowly to the chapel, in their white and brown mantles. The workers are coming back from the fields, with their tools on their shoulders. The pigeons are flying home to the dovecote. In the stables, the horses are munching their hay. And at the gate, an old man and a little boy are waiting for their bread, sitting on the grass in the evening sun.

In the chapel, the candles are lit. The brothers stand in their places, and begin to sing. Their voices rise softly into the dark roof, old and young together. The same psalms that monks have sung for hundreds of years. Among them is a young man of nineteen, in a new white mantle, singing the words for the first time, a little unsure, a little out of tune, but with all his heart.

[long-pause]

Far away, across the sea, the same evening is falling over Acre. The sun is setting over the water, turning the walls of the Temple golden. The lions on the tower are shining. The ships in the harbour are rocking gently. In the markets, the merchants are packing up their spices and their silks. And in the chapel of the Temple, other brothers are singing the same Vespers, in the same words, at the same hour.

[pause]

{img:moon-rising-over-acre-sea-templar-tower-silver-reflections-calm}
The night comes softly over both places. Over the green fields of Champagne, and the dry hills of Galilee. Over the commandery with its dovecote, and the castle by the sea. The stars come out, one by one. The same stars, over France and over the Holy Land. Aldebaran, Betelgeuse and Vega. The follower, the hand of the giant, and the falling eagle.

[long-pause]

In the dormitory of the commandery, the lamp is burning softly, as the Rule demands. The brothers are asleep on their narrow beds. Outside, an owl is calling from the woods. A horse shifts in its stall. The bell is silent now, until Matins. And the young man in the new white mantle lies awake for a little while, listening to the night, before he too falls asleep, under the quiet stars.
