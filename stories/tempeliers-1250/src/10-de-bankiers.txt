The Templars were not only knights and farmers. They also became something unexpected. They became the bankers of Europe. And the centre of their banking was a great fortress in Paris, called the Temple.

--

How did this happen? It began with the pilgrims. People going on pilgrimage to the Holy Land needed money for the long journey. But carrying gold and silver was dangerous. Robbers could steal it on the road. So the Templars offered a clever service. A pilgrim could leave his money at a Templar house in Europe. The Templars gave him a letter, saying how much he had left with them. Then, when he arrived in the Holy Land, he could show the letter at a Templar house there, and receive his money.

It was one of the first kinds of cheque in history. The Templars had houses everywhere, from England to Acre, and they trusted each other. So money could travel safely, on paper, across the whole known world. Soon, not only pilgrims, but also merchants, nobles and even kings began to use this service. And the Templars became very good at handling money.

{img:templar-treasurer-counting-coins-ledger-chests-stone-vault-candlelight}
The Temple in Paris is a huge fortress, outside the old walls of the city. It has a tall square tower, called the keep, with four round towers at its corners. Inside, there are the houses of the brothers, a large church, gardens and storerooms. And deep inside, there is the treasury. Strong rooms, with heavy doors and great iron locks, full of chests of gold and silver coins.

The king of France keeps his own treasure at the Temple. The treasurer of the Temple is also, in a way, the treasurer of the king. He collects taxes, pays the king's bills, and keeps careful accounts. Kings of England also keep money with the Templars, at their Temple in London. Nobles leave their jewels and important documents there, for safekeeping. The Templars are trusted because they are honest, careful and well guarded.

--

{img:merchant-depositing-money-templar-clerk-writing-letter-of-credit}
The Templars also lend money. To kings who need to pay for wars or crusades. To nobles who need to pay ransoms. To merchants and towns. They are not supposed to charge interest, because the Church forbids it. But they find other ways to earn something from their loans. They are very careful and very good at it. In those days, there were few people in Europe who understood money as well as the Templars.

Right now, in 1250, the Templars are busy with a very important matter. The king of France, Louis the Ninth, has gone on crusade to Egypt. Things have gone badly. The king and many of his knights have been captured. To free them, a huge ransom must be paid. The Templars help to gather the money. News of it travels slowly, all the way to the commanderies of Champagne.

{img:brothers-receiving-news-messenger-courtyard-letter-serious-faces}
Guillaume's commandery also handles money, on a smaller scale. Local people sometimes leave valuables with the brothers. Brother Hugues keeps careful accounts of all the rents, sales and payments. He writes them in a big book, and once a year, he checks them with the provincial master. Guillaume helps him sometimes, because he can read and count. He is surprised by how much there is to write down.

--

One day, Brother Hugues takes Guillaume to Paris, to deliver the money of the commandery to the Temple. It is the first time Guillaume has been to Paris. He stares at the crowds, the churches, the bridges full of houses, and the great river Seine. And when they arrive at the Temple, he stares at the great dark keep, rising over the walls. Inside, he sees brothers from all over France, coming and going. And he understands, for the first time, how big and powerful his order really is.

On the way home from Paris, Brother Hugues tells Guillaume that money is a dangerous thing for a monk. It can make a man proud and greedy. That is why the brothers must never think of the money as their own. It belongs to God, and to the poor, and to the Holy Land. Guillaume listens, and remembers. Years later, he will tell the same thing to the young brothers of his own house.
