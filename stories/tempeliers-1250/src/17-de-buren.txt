Today, people often imagine the crusades as a time of endless war between Christians and Muslims. And there were wars, terrible ones. But there were also long years of peace, and of daily life side by side. Christians and Muslims traded with each other, worked together, made treaties, and sometimes even became friends.

--

In the markets of Acre, Muslim merchants from Damascus sell their goods every day. Spices, cloth, glass and fruit. Caravans of camels come and go from the cities of Syria. Frankish merchants buy and sell with them. Muslim workers come into the city to work. Muslim peasants farm the land around the castles of the Templars, and pay their rents. And Muslim travellers pass through the city, on their way to Mecca, or to visit family.

The rulers on both sides often make truces. These are peace agreements, for a few years at a time. During a truce, people can travel freely. Merchants can trade. Pilgrims can visit holy places. Sometimes, the truces last for ten years or more. During these times, life in the Holy Land is quiet and peaceful. The Templars themselves sometimes negotiate these truces, because they know the local rulers and their languages.

{img:muslim-scholar-praying-small-mosque-templar-knight-standing-respectfully-door}
There is an old, famous story, written by a Muslim nobleman called Usama, about a hundred years before our story. He was a poet, a soldier and a diplomat, from Syria. When he visited Jerusalem, in a time of peace, he went to pray in a small mosque near the al-Aqsa Mosque, which the Templars occupied. He wrote that the Templars, who were his friends, let him pray there undisturbed.

But one day, a Frank who had just arrived from Europe grabbed him and turned him around, telling him to pray toward the east, in the Christian way. The Templars rushed over and pulled the man away. They apologised to Usama, and explained that the man was new, and did not understand the ways of the east. Usama wrote that those Franks who had lived in the east for a long time were much better than those who had just arrived.

--

{img:arab-physician-teaching-frankish-knight-herbs-medicine-courtyard}
Guillaume, too, learns much from the people of the east. In Acre, he becomes friends with a local Christian, an old man called Elias, who speaks Arabic and Greek. Elias sells medicines and herbs in a small shop near the Templar quarter. He knows a great deal about medicine, from old Arabic books. He teaches Guillaume about herbs and remedies, and about the stars, which the Arab scholars have studied for centuries.

The Franks learned many things from the east during the crusades. New foods, like sugar, lemons, apricots and rice. New spices. New kinds of cloth, like muslin and damask, named after the cities of Mosul and Damascus. New ideas in medicine, mathematics and astronomy. Numbers, like the ones we use today, came from India through the Arab world to Europe. And many words, like sugar, cotton, lemon and syrup, came into European languages from Arabic.

{img:old-man-and-young-knight-sitting-rooftop-acre-evening-looking-at-stars}
On warm evenings, Guillaume sometimes sits with Elias on the flat roof of his house, looking at the stars. Elias tells him the Arabic names of the stars. Aldebaran, the follower. Betelgeuse, the hand of the giant. Vega, the falling eagle. Many stars still have Arabic names today. Guillaume repeats them, slowly. Below them, the city is settling down for the night. Lamps are being lit. And from the minarets of the few mosques that remain, and from the bell towers of the churches, the evening prayers are called.

--

Guillaume thinks a lot about these things. He came to the Holy Land to defend it, as a soldier of Christ. He still believes in that. But he has also learned that the people on the other side are people too. Merchants, farmers, scholars, families, just like his own. He thinks that perhaps peace is a better way to serve God than war. He does not say this out loud. But he thinks it, quietly, on the roof under the stars.
