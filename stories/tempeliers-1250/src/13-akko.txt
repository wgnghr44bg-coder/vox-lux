The ship sails into the harbour of Acre. It is the biggest and richest city of the crusaders, and the capital of what is left of the Kingdom of Jerusalem. It stands on a point of land, sticking out into the sea, surrounded by strong walls and towers. As the ship comes closer, Guillaume sees the towers of churches, the domes of old mosques, the roofs of houses and palaces, and the masts of hundreds of ships in the harbour.

--

Acre is a very busy city. About forty thousand people live here. Maybe more. It is a great trading port. Ships from Venice, Genoa and Pisa come here, to buy silk, spices, sugar, glass and cotton, which have come from the east by camel caravan. The merchants of each Italian city have their own quarter, with their own streets, churches, warehouses and towers. They often quarrel with each other.

When Guillaume steps off the ship, onto the stone quay, he is overwhelmed. The noise. The crowds. The colours. The smells. Sailors are shouting in Italian. Merchants are bargaining in Arabic. Porters are carrying huge loads on their backs. Camels are kneeling by the warehouses, with their strange, proud faces. The sun is bright and hot, even in late autumn. And the air smells of spices, salt, fish and dust.

{img:acre-market-street-1250-spices-silk-merchants-many-peoples-awnings}
The people of Acre come from everywhere. There are Franks, as the people from western Europe are called here. French, Italians, Germans, English. There are local Christians, who speak Arabic, and have lived here for centuries. There are Greeks, Armenians and Syrians, with their own churches. There are Jews, with their synagogues and their scholars. And there are Muslims, merchants and workers, who come and go from the lands around.

In the markets of Acre, you can buy things that Guillaume has never seen before. Oranges and lemons, dates and figs, pomegranates and melons. Sugar, which is grown in fields near the coast, and boiled in big sugar mills. Silk and cotton cloth in beautiful colours. Glass from Tyre, the best in the world. Spices from India, pepper, cinnamon, cloves and ginger. And many strange and wonderful foods.

--

{img:knight-tasting-orange-market-stall-local-merchant-smiling-sunny}
At a market stall, Jan, the old pilgrim from Flanders, buys Guillaume an orange. Guillaume has never eaten one. He peels it, slowly, and the sweet smell fills the air. He tastes it. It is sweet and sour, and full of juice. He laughs out loud. The merchant behind the stall laughs too, and says something in Arabic. Guillaume answers with one of the words that Jan taught him. Thank you. The merchant smiles, and gives him another orange, for free.

Many of the Franks who have lived in Acre for a long time have adopted the ways of the east. They wear light, loose clothes of cotton and silk, which are cooler in the heat. They wrap cloths around their heads against the sun. They eat local food, and bathe in the bathhouses. Some speak Arabic. An earlier writer said that those who were once Westerners had now become Easterners. Guillaume finds it all very strange at first. But he is curious, and he likes it.

{img:acre-churches-towers-sunset-from-wall-city-roofs-sea-glow}
Acre is also a city of churches. There are dozens of them, built by the different peoples and orders. The great cathedral of the Holy Cross. Churches of the Italians, the French, the English. And the churches of the military orders. The Hospitallers have a huge building, with a hospital that can care for hundreds of sick people. The Teutonic Knights, from Germany, have their own quarter. And the Templars have theirs, at the end of the city, by the sea.

--

That evening, Guillaume and the other new brothers walk through the streets of Acre, to the Templar quarter. The sun is setting over the sea, turning everything golden. From the churches, the bells are ringing for Vespers. From somewhere else, far away, a voice is calling Muslims to prayer. Guillaume stops for a moment and listens to both sounds, mixing together in the warm evening air. Then he walks on, toward the great dark towers by the sea.
