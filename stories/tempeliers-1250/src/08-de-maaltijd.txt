The Templars eat two meals a day. Dinner at midday, and supper in the evening. They eat together in the hall of the commandery, which is called the refectory. And their meals, like everything else, follow the Rule.

--

When the bell rings for dinner, the brothers come into the hall and stand at their places, along long wooden tables. The chaplain says a blessing. Then they sit down and eat, in silence. While they eat, one brother reads aloud from a holy book, from a little stand at the side of the hall. The others listen. If a brother needs something, like salt or bread, he must ask for it with signs, not words.

The brothers eat in pairs. Two brothers share one bowl, and eat from it together. This was a custom from the early days, when the Templars were poor. It also teaches them to be fair and to share. Each brother must make sure that his companion gets enough, and not take the best pieces for himself. Guillaume shares his bowl with Brother Raoul. The old knight always pushes the best pieces of meat toward the young man.

{img:two-templar-brothers-sharing-one-bowl-bread-wine-cups-table}
The food is simple, but good and filling. The Templars are fighting men, and they need strength. So unlike many monks, they are allowed to eat meat. But only three times a week, on Sundays, Tuesdays and Thursdays. On the other days, they eat vegetables, beans, cheese, eggs and fish. On Fridays, they eat no meat, and during Lent, they fast. There is always plenty of bread. And each brother receives a cup of wine with his meal, mixed with water.

The food in Guillaume's commandery comes mostly from its own farm. Bread from its own wheat, ground at its own mill. Wine from its own vineyards. Meat from its own pigs, sheep and cattle. Eggs from its own hens, pigeons from the dovecote, and carp from the fishpond. Cheese from its own cows. Vegetables and herbs from its own garden. Almost nothing needs to be bought, except salt and spices.

--

{img:monastery-kitchen-medieval-cook-pots-hearth-bread-vegetables-servants}
The cook of the commandery is a servant called Mathieu. He is a big, cheerful man from the village, who has worked for the Templars for twenty years. He makes thick soups of beans and vegetables, roasts of pork and mutton, and pies of fish and eggs. On feast days, he makes something special. Roast chicken, or pigeon pie, or a sweet dish of rice cooked with almond milk. The brothers are not supposed to care about food. But they all look forward to feast days.

One part of the Rule is very kind. It says that a tenth of all the bread from the commandery must be given to the poor. And whatever food is left over from the brothers' meals must also be given to the poor, who come to the gate every day. Old men and women, widows, beggars, travellers and children. Mathieu hands out bread and soup to them at the gate, after every meal.

{img:poor-people-receiving-bread-soup-at-commandery-gate-servant-ladle}
Guillaume is given the job of helping Mathieu at the gate, after the evening meal. At first, he is not happy about it. He is a knight, and this seems like a servant's work. But Brother Hugues tells him that the poor are the guests of Christ. And slowly, Guillaume comes to like it. He gets to know the poor people who come every day. An old blind man, who tells funny stories. A widow with three small children. A boy who has no family at all.

--

After supper, the brothers stand again, and the chaplain says a prayer of thanks. Then they go to Compline. As they walk across the courtyard, in the evening light, Guillaume looks back at the gate. The widow and her children are sitting on the grass outside, eating their bread. The little boy waves at him. Guillaume waves back. And he thinks that perhaps this, too, is part of defending the Holy Land.
