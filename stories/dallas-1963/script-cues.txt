Tonight we are going a little more than sixty years back, to a bright and growing city on the wide plains of North Texas.

[long-pause]

It is an autumn morning in 1963, and the sun is coming up over Dallas.

[pause]

The light falls first on the tops of the tall buildings downtown, turning their glass and stone to gold, while the streets below are still in shadow. On the roof of one of the older towers, a great red horse with wings, made of neon tubes, is still glowing softly against the paling sky.

[pause]

If gentle stories like this one help you sleep, you are welcome to subscribe to the channel. Now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

The winged horse is called Pegasus, and it stands on top of the Magnolia Building, the headquarters of an oil company.

[pause]

When the Magnolia Building was built, in the 1920s, it was the tallest building in Texas, and when the flying red horse was put on top of it, in 1934, it could be seen for miles across the flat land around the city, turning slowly on its high steel frame. People in Dallas have grown very fond of it. For travellers driving in at night along the highways, it is the first sign that they have reached the city.

[pause]

{img:pegasus-neon-sign-night-building}
Now, in 1963, there are taller buildings all around it, new towers of glass and steel and polished stone, with names like Republic National Bank and Southland Life. But the red horse is still there, and it is still the symbol of Dallas.

[long-pause]

Dallas is a young city, and it is growing fast.

[pause]

About seven hundred thousand people live here now, and every year there are more. New neighbourhoods spread out across the prairie in every direction, with long, straight streets of brick and wooden houses, green lawns, and young trees. New highways loop around the city, carrying cars in and out of downtown. New schools and churches and shopping centres open almost every month.

[pause]

{img:new-suburb-street-ranch-houses}
Just a few generations ago, this was cattle country, and cotton country. Dallas was a small market town on the banks of the Trinity River, where farmers came to sell their cotton and buy their supplies. Then came the railways, and then the oil, and then the banks and the insurance companies and the factories.

[long-pause]

The oil fields of East Texas were discovered in 1930, about a hundred miles away, and they made Dallas rich.

[pause]

The oil companies built their offices here, and so did the banks that lent them money. Then came the businesses that sold them things: machinery and trucks and pipes and office furniture. Then came the department stores and the restaurants and the hotels for all the people who worked in them.

[pause]

{img:oil-company-office-men-hats-1963}
In the 1960s, there are new kinds of businesses too. Not far from downtown, a company called Texas Instruments makes tiny electronic parts for radios and computers and rockets. A few years ago, one of its engineers, a quiet, very tall man named Jack Kilby, invented something called the integrated circuit, a little chip of material that holds a whole electronic circuit inside it. Nobody yet knows how important it will become.

[long-pause]

The people of Dallas are proud of their city.

[pause]

They like to call it Big D. They are proud of its tall buildings, its busy streets, its fine stores, its football team, and its State Fair, which is held every October in a great park east of downtown, with rides and prize cattle and fried food, and a giant statue of a smiling cowboy, called Big Tex, who stands at the gate and says hello to the visitors through a loudspeaker.

[pause]

{img:state-fair-big-tex-families}
They are proud of Love Field too, the city's airport, which is named after an army pilot from long ago. The new jet airliners land there now, one after another, and the people of Dallas can fly to New York or Los Angeles in just a few hours.

[long-pause]

On the edge of downtown, the Trinity River winds slowly past, between wide grassy levees built to hold back its floods.

[pause]

It is not a grand river. For most of the year, it is a slow, brown stream, lined with cottonwoods and willows, with herons standing in the shallows. But it was the reason the city was founded, more than a hundred years ago, when a young lawyer and trader named John Neely Bryan built a little log cabin on its bank, at a place where travellers could cross, and began to trade with the people passing by.

[pause]

A copy of that little cabin stands in the middle of downtown today, dwarfed by the towers around it, to remind the people of Dallas how it all began.

[long-pause]

The weather in Dallas is big, like everything else.

[pause]

In summer, it is very hot, often well over a hundred degrees, and the air shimmers over the pavement. People spend the afternoons indoors, in rooms cooled by the new air-conditioners humming in their windows. In spring, great thunderstorms roll across the plains, with lightning and hail and sometimes tornadoes.

[pause]

But in the autumn, the weather is lovely. The days are warm and sunny, the skies are a deep, clear blue, and the evenings are cool and fresh. It is the best time of the year in Dallas.

[long-pause]

{img:dallas-downtown-autumn-afternoon-traffic}
And so, in the late autumn of 1963, the city goes about its business.

[pause]

The office workers go downtown every morning, in their suits and their dresses, by car or by bus. The children go to school. The housewives shop and cook and clean. The oilmen make their deals over lunch. The shopkeepers start to think about Christmas, which is only a month away.

[pause]

There is a feeling in the air of a city on its way up, confident and busy and modern.

[pause]

And in a few weeks' time, something will happen that will make the name of Dallas known all over the world, in a way that nobody in the city has ever imagined.

[pause]

But not yet. For now, it is simply an ordinary autumn in Texas.

[long-pause]

At home, the mother begins her day's work.

[pause]

She does the washing in her new electric washing machine, and hangs the clothes out to dry on a line in the back yard, in the warm Texas sunshine. She vacuums the carpets and dusts the furniture. She makes the beds. She plans the dinner.

[pause]

In the middle of the morning, she has a cup of coffee with her neighbour, at the kitchen table, and they talk about their children, and the school, and the church bake sale, and the new dresses at the department store downtown.

[long-pause]

In the afternoon, she drives to the supermarket.

[pause]

The supermarket is a new kind of shop. It is enormous and very bright, with long aisles of shelves full of cans and boxes and packets, all with colourful labels. There are frozen dinners and cake mixes and instant coffee, things that were unknown only twenty years ago. There are fresh oranges from California and bananas from Central America, and a long glass counter full of meat.

[pause]

{img:supermarket-aisle-1963-shopping-cart}
She pushes her shopping cart slowly up and down the aisles, choosing carefully, and then waits in line at the checkout, while the girl at the till rings up the prices on her big metal cash register, and a boy in a white apron packs everything into brown paper bags.

In the late afternoon, the children come home from school, and the street fills with noise.

[pause]

The boys ride their bicycles up and down the pavement, or play catch in the front yards, or gather in someone's garage to look at a new baseball glove. The girls play hopscotch on the driveway, or skip rope, or sit on the front steps with their dolls. Somebody's mother calls out from a doorway that it is nearly dinnertime, and somebody's dog runs barking after a bicycle.

[pause]

On some afternoons, the ice cream truck comes slowly down the street, playing its little tinkling tune, and every child within hearing distance comes running, holding out a nickel or a dime.

[long-pause]

In the evening, the father comes home, and the family sits down to dinner together.

[pause]

There is meatloaf, or fried chicken, or a pot roast with potatoes and carrots, and green beans, and rolls with butter, and iced tea. In Texas, everyone drinks iced tea, with lots of ice, all year round. For dessert, there might be pecan pie, or a slice of chocolate cake.

[pause]

They talk about their day. The children tell what happened at school. The father tells about his work. The mother tells the news from the neighbourhood.

[long-pause]

After dinner, the family gathers in the living room, around the television.

[pause]

{img:family-watching-black-white-tv}
The television is a big wooden cabinet with a small grey screen, and the pictures are in black and white. There are only three or four channels. In the evenings, there are western serials and comedy shows, quiz shows and variety shows with singers and dancers, and at half past five or six o'clock, the evening news, read by a calm, serious man in a dark suit.

[pause]

The children lie on the carpet in front of the screen, propped up on their elbows. The parents sit on the sofa. The dog sleeps by the door.

[long-pause]

On Friday nights in autumn, much of Dallas goes to the high school football games.

[pause]

The games are played under bright lights, in stadiums full of cheering families. There are marching bands in shining uniforms, and cheerleaders, and boys selling popcorn and hot dogs in the stands. The whole town turns out, and people talk about the games all weekend.

[pause]

{img:friday-night-football-stadium-lights}
On Saturdays, the families go shopping, or work in the garden, or drive out of the city for a picnic beside one of the lakes. On Sundays, they go to church, in their best clothes, and afterwards have a big Sunday dinner, and maybe a nap in the afternoon.

[long-pause]

At bedtime, the mother tucks the children in, and turns out the light.

[pause]

The house grows quiet. Outside, the street is dark and still, except for the soft glow of the street lamps and the light of a television flickering behind the curtains of the house across the way. A dog barks somewhere. A car drives slowly by.

[pause]

In the cool autumn air, under the wide Texas sky, the city of Dallas goes to sleep.

[long-pause]

{img:main-street-dallas-shoppers-1963}
Downtown Dallas is the heart of the city.

[pause]

On a weekday morning, the streets are crowded with people. Men in dark suits and narrow ties and hats hurry along the pavements with their briefcases. Women in smart dresses and gloves and little round hats walk from shop to shop. Office girls in pairs, secretaries and typists, chat and laugh on their way to work. City buses pull in and out at the corners, and the traffic lights click from red to green.

[long-pause]

The main shopping street is Main Street, running east and west through the middle of downtown, with Elm Street on one side of it and Commerce Street on the other.

[pause]

These three long streets are lined with stores, banks, hotels, cinemas, restaurants and offices, in buildings of every age, from old red brick to new shining glass. At their western end, they all come together and pass under a railway bridge, into a little green park with lawns and trees and white concrete colonnades, beside the edge of downtown.

[pause]

{img:dealey-plaza-park-colonnade-1963}
That little park is called Dealey Plaza, after a newspaper publisher who did a great deal for the city. It is a quiet, pleasant spot, and office workers sometimes sit there on the benches in the sunshine to eat their lunch.

[long-pause]

The most famous store in Dallas is Neiman Marcus.

[pause]

It stands on the corner of Main and Ervay Streets, an elegant building of stone and glass, and it sells the finest clothes and jewellery and perfume in the whole Southwest. Ladies come from all over Texas, and from much further away, to shop there.

[pause]

Inside, the floors are carpeted, the lighting is soft, and the saleswomen are very polite. There are dresses from Paris and Rome and New York, furs and hats and gloves, silk scarves, crystal and silver. Every Christmas, the store publishes a famous catalogue, full of extraordinary gifts, matching his-and-hers aeroplanes, or a pair of little submarines, that people read for fun, even if they could never afford them.

[long-pause]

Not far away, there are the bigger department stores, where ordinary families do their shopping.

[pause]

There are floors and floors of clothes and shoes and household goods, toys and books and records, with escalators going up and down in the middle. There are lunch counters, where shoppers can sit on round stools at a long counter and order a sandwich, a bowl of chili, or a slice of pie and a cup of coffee.

[pause]

{img:lunch-counter-diner-stools-1963}
In the weeks before Christmas, the windows of the department stores are decorated with scenes of snow and reindeer and toys, and children press their noses against the glass to see them. In the toy departments, Santa Claus sits on a big chair, and the children wait in line to tell him what they want.

[long-pause]

At lunchtime, the downtown restaurants and cafés are full.

[pause]

There are elegant restaurants in the big hotels, the Adolphus and the Baker and the Statler Hilton, with white tablecloths and waiters in jackets. There are steak houses and Mexican restaurants, cafeterias with long counters of food behind glass, and little diners where a hot lunch costs less than a dollar.

[pause]

Businessmen talk over their steaks about oil wells and land deals and the price of cotton. Secretaries share a sandwich and a cola on a bench in the park.

[long-pause]

{img:movie-theatre-marquee-evening}
In the evenings, downtown changes.

[pause]

The office workers go home, and the streets grow quieter. But the lights of the cinemas come on, along Elm Street, where there are several grand old movie palaces, with tall signs that light up in red and gold, and long lines of people waiting outside to buy their tickets.

[pause]

Inside, there are thick carpets and chandeliers, and seats of red velvet, and a great curtain that opens slowly before the film begins. A ticket costs about a dollar. A box of popcorn costs a dime.

[long-pause]

Near Dealey Plaza, at the corner of Elm and Houston Streets, there is an old brick warehouse, seven storeys tall.

[pause]

It was built at the beginning of the century, for a company that made farm machinery. Now, it is used by a company that stores and sends out school textbooks to the schools of Texas. It is called the Texas School Book Depository.

[pause]

It is a plain, ordinary building, of red brick, with rows of windows, and a big sign on the roof advertising a car rental company. Every day, the workers there unpack boxes of books, and sort them, and pack them up again, and send them out to schools all over the state.

[pause]

Nobody in Dallas pays much attention to it.

[long-pause]

Across Elm Street from the Book Depository, there is another old building, called the Dal-Tex Building, full of small offices and showrooms for clothing companies.

[pause]

Dallas has a big clothing business, especially for women's dresses. There are dozens of small companies here, which design and make dresses and sell them to stores all over the country. Their showrooms and workshops fill several of the old buildings near Dealey Plaza.

[pause]

{img:dress-factory-showroom-seamstresses}
On the upper floors, in long rooms full of sewing machines, women in neat smocks work at their machines all day, while designers sketch at their drawing tables, and salesmen show the new collections to visiting buyers.

[long-pause]

From the windows of the Dal-Tex Building, and from the Book Depository across the street, there is a fine view over Dealey Plaza.

[pause]

The three streets, Main and Elm and Commerce, curve gently down through the little park, past the lawns and the trees and the colonnades, and pass together under the railway bridge called the Triple Underpass. On the north side of Elm Street, there is a gentle grassy slope, with a wooden fence at the top, and a little row of trees.

[pause]

{img:dealey-plaza-view-from-above-autumn}
It is a pleasant view, especially in the afternoon, when the sun shines across the grass, and the shadows of the trees stretch out over the curving road.

[pause]

In November 1963, almost nobody in the world outside Dallas has ever heard of it.

[long-pause]

{img:dressmaker-office-1963-man-glasses}
On the fourth floor of the Dal-Tex Building, overlooking Elm Street and Dealey Plaza, there is the office of a small dress company called Jennifer Juniors.

[pause]

It belongs to a man named Abraham Zapruder.

[long-pause]

Abraham Zapruder is fifty-eight years old in 1963. He is a short, round, kindly man, with a bald head, thick glasses, and a warm, gentle smile. His employees call him Mr. Z, and they like him very much.

[pause]

He was born in 1905 in a small town in what was then the Russian Empire, in a Jewish family. When he was fifteen, the family left their home and came to America, crossing the Atlantic on a crowded ship, and settled in Brooklyn, in New York.

[pause]

{img:immigrant-family-new-york-1920}
Young Abraham went to work in the clothing factories of New York, as a pattern maker, cutting out the paper shapes from which dresses are sewn. He went to school in the evenings to learn English. He worked hard, and he learned the business from top to bottom.

[long-pause]

In 1941, he moved to Dallas, with his wife Lillian and their two young children, to work for a dress company there.

[pause]

He liked Dallas. He liked the warm weather and the friendly people and the feeling of a city that was growing. And after some years, he started his own company, which he called Jennifer Juniors.

[pause]

The company designs and makes dresses for young women and girls: pretty, inexpensive dresses with full skirts and little collars, which are sold in department stores all over the country. It is a small business, but a successful one. About twenty people work there, designers and seamstresses and clerks and salesmen.

[long-pause]

Mr. Zapruder is a devoted family man.

[pause]

He lives with Lillian in a comfortable house in a quiet neighbourhood of Dallas, with a garden full of flowers. Their son, Henry, is a lawyer, working in Washington. Their daughter, Myrna, is married, and has children of her own, and Mr. Zapruder adores his grandchildren.

[pause]

{img:grandfather-grandchildren-garden-sunday}
On Sundays, the family gathers at the house for dinner, and Mr. Zapruder plays with the little ones in the garden, and tells them stories, and takes pictures of them.

[long-pause]

Because Abraham Zapruder has a hobby. He loves to make home movies.

[pause]

A few months ago, he bought a new film camera, a Bell and Howell, the best model in the shop. It is small and light, with a gold-coloured metal body, and it takes little reels of colour film, eight millimetres wide.

[pause]

{img:vintage-8mm-home-movie-camera-hand}
He takes it with him everywhere. He films his grandchildren playing on the lawn. He films the family at birthday parties and holidays, around the table, opening presents. He films the flowers in his garden, and the dogs, and his wife waving at him from the porch.

[pause]

In the evenings, he sets up a small projector in the living room, pulls down a white screen, and shows the films to the family, while the projector whirs softly in the dark.

[long-pause]

At work, Mr. Zapruder is busy and cheerful.

[pause]

He arrives at the office every morning at about nine, says good morning to everyone, and goes into his small office to look at the new designs. He talks to the buyers on the telephone. He walks through the workroom, where the seamstresses are sewing the dresses, and stops to chat with them, and asks after their families.

[pause]

His receptionist is a young woman named Marilyn Sitzman. She is in her twenties, friendly and lively, and she has worked for the company for some time. She sits at the front desk, answering the telephone, typing letters, and greeting the visitors.

[long-pause]

From the windows of the office, the staff of Jennifer Juniors can look down on Dealey Plaza.

[pause]

They can see the traffic going by on Elm Street below, and the green lawns and the trees, and the white concrete colonnades, and the railway bridge at the far end. In the mornings, the sunlight falls across the park. In the evenings, the street lamps come on, and the headlights of the cars stream past in the dusk.

[pause]

{img:window-view-street-park-dusk-office}
Sometimes, at lunchtime, the girls from the workroom take their sandwiches down to the park, and sit on the low walls in the sunshine, and watch the people go by.

[long-pause]

At lunchtime, Mr. Zapruder often walks over to a little café nearby, or eats a sandwich at his desk while he looks through the newspaper.

[pause]

In the afternoons, the buyers come in from the department stores, and he shows them the new dresses for the spring, holding them up one by one, talking about the colours and the fabrics and the little details, a bow here, a row of buttons there. He loves his work. He has been making dresses for more than forty years, and he still enjoys it.

[pause]

When the day is over, he locks up the office, says goodnight to the cleaning lady, and drives home through the evening traffic, with the radio playing, to Lillian, and his supper, and his garden.

In November 1963, there is exciting news in the office.

[pause]

The President of the United States, John F. Kennedy, is coming to Dallas. He will drive through the city in an open car, in a long parade of cars, which is called a motorcade, so that the people of Dallas can see him. And the route of the motorcade will come right through Dealey Plaza, along Elm Street, just below the windows of the Dal-Tex Building.

Mr. Zapruder is an admirer of President Kennedy. He voted for him. And he would very much like to see him.

At first, he decides not to take his camera to work that day.

[pause]

The weather forecast for the morning is for rain, and he thinks it may not be good for filming. And besides, he is a little shy about it.

Like many young people in America in 1963, Bill and Gayle admire President Kennedy.

He is young and handsome and full of energy. They talk it over at the kitchen table. Bill will take the morning off work. They will bring the boys, so that one day they can say that they saw the President of the United States. They will go downtown, and find a good place to stand along the route of the motorcade.

The boys do not really understand what a President is. But they understand that there will be a parade, and that is enough.

They study the route of the motorcade in the newspaper.

It will start at the airport, at Love Field, a little before noon, and come along the broad roads into the city, past the neighbourhoods north of downtown. Then it will turn onto Main Street and drive slowly right through the middle of downtown, where the crowds will be biggest. At the far end of Main Street, it will turn right onto Houston Street, and then left onto Elm Street, and go down through Dealey Plaza, and under the railway bridge, and out onto the freeway, toward a big hall called the Trade Mart, where the President will give a speech at lunch.

Bill and Gayle decide that downtown will be too crowded, with the little boys. They will go to Dealey Plaza instead, at the end of the route, where there will be fewer people, and more room for the children.

All week, the whole city has been talking about the visit.

[pause]

The newspapers print the route of the motorcade on their front pages, with maps, and the times when the President is expected to pass each corner. Offices give their workers permission to take a longer lunch hour, so that they can go out and watch. Schools tell parents that children may be excused from classes to see the President, if their parents take them.

[pause]

In the department stores downtown, the shop assistants are told which windows they may watch from. Some of the office buildings along Main Street are selling places at their windows. Everywhere, people are planning where they will stand.

[long-pause]

On the evening before the visit, Gayle lays out the boys' clothes for the morning.

[pause]

Little striped shirts and long trousers, warm jackets in case it is cold, and their best shoes. She makes sandwiches to take with them. Bill checks the weather forecast on the television. It says that it will rain in the morning, but that it should clear up by midday.

[pause]

The boys are put to bed early. They are so excited about the parade that it takes them a long time to fall asleep.

[pause]

At last, the little house grows quiet. Bill and Gayle sit together on the sofa for a while, watching the late news, and then they turn out the lights and go to bed.

[pause]

Outside, the night is cool and cloudy, and a light rain begins to fall softly on the roof.

In Washington, in the White House, the President of the United States is getting ready for a journey to Texas.

[pause]

John Fitzgerald Kennedy is forty-six years old. He has been President for nearly three years, since January 1961, when he became the youngest man ever elected to the office, and the first who was born in the twentieth century.

[long-pause]

He is tall and slim, with thick reddish-brown hair, a sun-tanned face, and a wide, easy smile. He speaks with the broad accent of Boston, where he grew up, in a large, wealthy, ambitious Irish-American family, the second of nine children.

[pause]

During the Second World War, he served in the navy in the Pacific, in command of a small, fast patrol boat. One night, his boat was rammed and cut in half by a Japanese warship in the dark. He swam for hours, towing an injured crewman by the strap of his life jacket, held between his teeth, until they reached a small island, and in the end, all the survivors were rescued.

[pause]

{img:young-naval-officer-pacific-1943}
He came home a hero. But he also came home with a bad back, which has troubled him ever since. Most days, he is in pain, though he rarely lets anyone see it.

[long-pause]

As President, he has had a difficult time.

[pause]

There is the long, cold rivalry with the Soviet Union, and the danger of nuclear war. Just a year ago, in October 1962, the world held its breath for thirteen days, when the Soviets placed missiles on the island of Cuba, not far from the coast of Florida, and the two great powers stood on the edge of war, before both sides at last stepped back.

[pause]

There is the great struggle at home for the civil rights of Black Americans, who in much of the country are still not allowed to vote, or to go to the same schools and restaurants as white people. In June, Kennedy went on television and spoke to the nation, saying that this was a moral question, as old as the Scriptures and as clear as the American Constitution.

[long-pause]

And there is the race to the moon.

[pause]

In 1961, Kennedy promised that America would land a man on the moon, and bring him safely back, before the end of the decade. Now, in 1963, the rockets are being built, and the astronauts are being trained, and people all over the country are excited about it.

[pause]

The President has many supporters, and also many opponents. And next year, in November 1964, there will be another election. He wants very much to win a second term.

[long-pause]

His wife is Jacqueline Kennedy, whom everyone calls Jackie.

[pause]

She is thirty-four years old, slim and dark-haired, with wide-set eyes and a soft, quiet voice. She grew up in a wealthy family in New York and Virginia, studied in Paris, speaks French and Spanish and Italian, and loves art and history and horses.

[pause]

{img:first-lady-white-house-restoration-room}
As First Lady, she has restored the rooms of the White House, filling them with fine old American furniture and paintings, and in 1962 she showed them to the whole country in a television programme. Millions of people watched. Women everywhere copy her clothes and her hairstyle.

[long-pause]

The Kennedys have two young children.

[pause]

Caroline is almost six, a bright little girl with fair hair, who has her own pony, called Macaroni, and goes to a small school in the White House. John, whom everyone calls John-John, will be three years old on the twenty-fifth of November. He likes to play under his father's desk in the Oval Office, hiding behind its little door, while his father works.

[pause]

{img:little-boy-under-presidents-desk}
In August of this year, the Kennedys had a third child, a little boy named Patrick. But he was born too early, and he lived for only two days. The President and his wife were heartbroken, and they grew closer than ever in their grief.

[long-pause]

At the White House, the family has made a home for themselves, as much as anyone can in such a place.

[pause]

On the third floor, there is a little school for Caroline and a few of her friends, with small desks and a blackboard, and a teacher. On the lawn outside, there is a treehouse, a swing set and a trampoline. There are dogs, and a cat, and hamsters, and Caroline's pony. In the evenings, when he can, the President comes up from his office to have supper with the children and read them a story before bed.

[pause]

On weekends, the family often goes away, to a rented house in the Virginia countryside, where Jackie can ride her horses, or to the Kennedy family homes by the sea, in Massachusetts and Florida, where they sail and swim and play games on the lawn.

[long-pause]

Jackie has not been on a political trip with her husband for a long time.

[pause]

But this time she has agreed to come. It will be her first public trip since the loss of the baby. The President is very pleased. He knows that the crowds will love her.

[pause]

She thinks carefully about what to wear. For the day in Dallas, she chooses a pink wool suit with a navy blue collar, and a little pink pillbox hat to match, which her husband particularly likes.

[long-pause]

The trip to Texas is important for political reasons.

[pause]

Texas is a big state, with many votes, and Kennedy won it only narrowly in 1960, with the help of his Vice President, Lyndon Johnson, who is a Texan. But now the Democratic Party in Texas is divided. The Governor, John Connally, and one of the state's senators, Ralph Yarborough, are bitter rivals, and they will hardly speak to each other.

[pause]

{img:governor-texas-portrait-office-1963}
The President hopes that, by travelling with them both, he can bring them together, and make friends for himself, and raise money for the campaign next year.

[long-pause]

Some of his advisers are worried about Dallas.

[pause]

It is a city where many people strongly dislike the President's politics. A month earlier, his ambassador to the United Nations, Adlai Stevenson, was jostled and shouted at by an angry crowd there. Some people have suggested that the President should not go.

[pause]

But Kennedy is not worried. He likes crowds, and he likes to go out among the people, shake their hands, and talk with them. He does not want to be shut away behind bulletproof glass.

[long-pause]

{img:air-force-one-andrews-takeoff-morning}
On the morning of Thursday, the twenty-first of November, the President and the First Lady say goodbye to their children at the White House.

[pause]

Caroline and John are sad to see them go. The President picks up John and hugs him. Then the helicopter lifts off from the lawn, and flies them to Andrews Air Force Base, outside Washington, where the big blue and white presidential jet, Air Force One, is waiting.

[pause]

At about eleven o'clock, the plane takes off, and turns south and west, toward Texas.

[long-pause]

{img:san-antonio-crowds-sunny-1963}
Air Force One lands in San Antonio, in the south of Texas, in the early afternoon of the twenty-first of November.

[pause]

It is a warm, sunny day. A huge, friendly crowd is waiting at the airport, waving flags and banners, and as the President and Mrs. Kennedy step out of the plane, a great cheer goes up.

[pause]

Vice President Lyndon Johnson and his wife, Lady Bird, are there to greet them, and so is Governor Connally, with his wife, Nellie.

[long-pause]

In San Antonio, the President drives through the city in an open car, past cheering crowds, to dedicate a new building at an air force medical centre, where scientists study the effects of space travel on the human body.

[pause]

He gives a short speech. He tells a story about an Irish writer, who, as a boy, would throw his cap over a high orchard wall when he and his friends were afraid to climb it, so that they had no choice but to go after it.

[pause]

{img:president-speaking-outdoor-podium-texas}
This nation, he says, has tossed its cap over the wall of space, and we have no choice but to follow it.

[long-pause]

In the afternoon, the presidential party flies on to Houston, on the coast of the Gulf of Mexico.

[pause]

Houston is a huge, sprawling city, hot and humid, full of oil refineries and the new buildings of the space programme. Here too, the crowds are friendly and large, lining the streets to see the President pass.

[pause]

In the evening, the President and Mrs. Kennedy go to a ballroom in a hotel, to a gathering of a Mexican-American organization. Jackie steps up to the microphone and speaks to the crowd in Spanish, and they cheer and applaud her, delighted.

[pause]

Then there is a big dinner for a Texas congressman, and more speeches, and more handshakes.

[long-pause]

Late that night, they fly on to Fort Worth, a city about thirty miles west of Dallas.

[pause]

They land after eleven o'clock, in the dark, and drive through the quiet streets to the Hotel Texas, where a suite has been prepared for them on the eighth floor.

[pause]

{img:hotel-suite-paintings-lamps-night}
The suite has a surprise in it. Some of the leading people of Fort Worth have borrowed works of art from the city's museums and collectors, and hung them on the walls, to welcome the art-loving First Lady. There are paintings by Van Gogh and Monet, a sculpture by Picasso, and works by famous American artists, all arranged around the rooms.

[pause]

But the Kennedys are so tired that they barely notice. They go to bed a little after midnight.

[long-pause]

The next morning, Friday, the twenty-second of November, the President wakes early.

[pause]

Outside the hotel window, it is grey and drizzling. In the parking lot across the street, a crowd of several thousand people has been gathering since before dawn, standing patiently in the light rain, hoping to see him.

[pause]

{img:rainy-parking-lot-crowd-umbrellas-morning}
At a quarter to nine, the President comes out of the hotel, without a hat or a raincoat, and crosses the street to speak to them from the back of a truck. The crowd cheers. Someone calls out, asking where Mrs. Kennedy is.

[pause]

Mrs. Kennedy is organizing herself, the President answers, with a grin. It takes her a little longer, but of course, she looks better than we do when she does it.

[pause]

The crowd laughs.

[long-pause]

Then he goes back into the hotel, to a big breakfast in the ballroom, given by the Fort Worth Chamber of Commerce.

[pause]

There are about two thousand people there, the businessmen and community leaders of the city, sitting at long tables. Halfway through the breakfast, Mrs. Kennedy arrives at last, in her pink suit and her pink hat, and the whole room rises to its feet and applauds her.

[pause]

At the end, the people of Fort Worth give the President a gift: a fine Texas cowboy hat. Everyone calls out for him to put it on. He smiles, and holds it up, and says that he will put it on in Washington, on Monday, and then they can all come and see it.

[long-pause]

Back in the suite, before leaving, the President and Mrs. Kennedy find a little catalogue that lists all the paintings in their rooms, with the names of the people who arranged them.

[pause]

Jackie is touched. They telephone one of the organizers, a lady named Ruth Carter Johnson, to thank her. She is at home, and very surprised and pleased to hear from them.

[pause]

{img:window-view-sun-breaking-through-clouds}
Outside, the weather is changing. The clouds are beginning to break up, and patches of blue sky are appearing. By late morning, the sun is shining brightly.

[long-pause]

At about half past eleven, Air Force One takes off from the airport in Fort Worth, for the short flight to Dallas.

[pause]

It is the shortest flight of the whole trip, only about thirteen minutes. The plane barely has time to climb before it begins to come down again.

[pause]

{img:presidential-jet-flying-over-texas-plains}
From the windows, the President and Mrs. Kennedy can see the flat, brown plains of Texas spread out below them, criss-crossed with roads, dotted with farms and little towns, and then, ahead, the towers of downtown Dallas, shining in the midday sun.

[long-pause]

{img:love-field-crowd-fence-sunshine}
At Love Field, the crowd has been waiting since early morning.

[pause]

There are several thousand people pressed against the chain-link fence along the edge of the runway: office workers on their lunch break, mothers with children on their shoulders, students who have skipped classes, old people who have brought folding chairs. They carry little flags and handmade signs, and they shade their eyes against the bright sun, watching the sky to the west.

[long-pause]

At about twenty to twelve, someone points, and calls out.

[pause]

A shining blue and white jet is coming down out of the sky, very low and very slow, its engines roaring. It touches down on the runway, rolls past the crowd, turns, and comes to a stop in front of the terminal. On its side, in tall letters, are the words United States of America.

[pause]

{img:air-force-one-landed-steps-crowd}
The steps are wheeled up to the door. The door opens. And there, in the doorway, in the sunshine, stand the President and Mrs. Kennedy.

[pause]

The crowd lets out a tremendous cheer.

[long-pause]

Mrs. Kennedy comes down the steps first, in her pink suit and pink hat, smiling and squinting a little in the bright light. At the bottom, the wife of the mayor of Dallas steps forward and gives her a large bouquet of red roses.

[pause]

In San Antonio and Houston, she was given yellow roses, the yellow rose of Texas. Here in Dallas, they are red.

[pause]

The President follows her down, tall and tanned and smiling, in a dark grey suit and a blue tie. He shakes hands with the mayor, and with the Vice President and Mrs. Johnson, who have flown in on their own plane a few minutes earlier, and with the Governor and Mrs. Connally.

[long-pause]

Then, instead of going straight to the cars, the President does what he loves to do best.

[pause]

He walks over to the fence, where the crowd is waiting, and begins to shake hands.

[pause]

Mrs. Kennedy goes with him. They walk slowly along the fence together, reaching over to shake the hands that are stretched out toward them, smiling, saying a few words here and there. People call out to them, and laugh, and cry. A little girl holds up a sign. A woman holds up her baby.

[pause]

{img:president-shaking-hands-fence-crowd}
The Secret Service agents walk close behind them, watching the crowd, a little nervous. But the crowd is happy and friendly. Nobody in it seems to wish them anything but well.

[long-pause]

At last, the President and Mrs. Kennedy walk to the cars.

[pause]

The motorcade is lined up on the tarmac, a long row of shining automobiles and motorcycles. In the middle of it is the President's car.

[pause]

It is a big, dark blue Lincoln Continental convertible, specially built for the President, very long and low, with little flags on the front fenders and three rows of seats. There is a bubble-shaped plastic roof that can be put on top in bad weather. But this morning, because the sun has come out, the roof has been taken off and left behind. The President wants the people to see him.

[long-pause]

{img:dark-blue-convertible-limousine-flags}
In the front seat of the car are two Secret Service agents, the driver and his partner. In the middle row, on little folding seats, sit Governor John Connally and his wife Nellie. And in the back seat, the President sits on the right, and Mrs. Kennedy on the left, with her roses on the seat between them.

[pause]

Behind them comes a car full of Secret Service agents, standing on the running boards and sitting with the windows down. Then the car with the Vice President and Mrs. Johnson. Then more cars, with congressmen and officials, and buses full of reporters and photographers.

[long-pause]

At about five minutes to twelve, the motorcade begins to move.

[pause]

The motorcycle policemen start their engines and ride out in front, two by two, their lights flashing. The cars follow, slowly, one after another, out of the airport and onto the road toward the city.

[pause]

The day is beautiful now. The sky is clear and blue, with only a few white clouds. The sun is warm. It is about seventy degrees, a perfect Texas autumn day.

[long-pause]

All along the route, people are waiting.

[pause]

At first, along the roads near the airport, they stand in little groups, outside their houses and their shops and their offices, waving as the cars go by. Children sit on their fathers' shoulders. Workers come out of a factory to watch, still in their overalls. A group of schoolchildren holds up a big hand-painted sign asking the President to stop and shake their hands.

[pause]

{img:schoolchildren-sign-roadside-waving}
And he does. The motorcade stops for a moment, and the President leans out of the car and shakes the children's hands, and they jump up and down with excitement.

[long-pause]

Then the cars drive on, toward the city, along a broad street lined with trees and houses and small apartment buildings, and the crowds grow larger and larger.

[pause]

In the back seat, the President waves, and smiles, and Mrs. Kennedy waves too, her white-gloved hand raised, holding her pink hat in place now and then against the breeze. The sun is very bright, and she would like to put on her sunglasses. But the President asks her not to, so that the people can see her face.

[pause]

{img:convertible-tree-lined-avenue-crowds-waving}
In the seat in front of them, Nellie Connally turns around now and then to speak to them, smiling, delighted at the size of the crowds.

[pause]

And ahead of them, the tall buildings of downtown Dallas are drawing nearer.

[long-pause]

{img:downtown-main-street-crowds-confetti}
At about twenty past twelve, the motorcade turns onto Main Street, and enters downtown Dallas.

[pause]

The crowds here are enormous. People are packed ten and twenty deep along the pavements, on both sides of the street, all the way along. They lean out of every window of the tall office buildings above. They stand on the roofs, on the ledges, on the steps of the banks and the hotels, on top of parked cars and mailboxes.

[long-pause]

As the President's car comes into view, a great roar goes up, and rolls along the street like a wave.

[pause]

From the windows high above, the office workers throw down little pieces of paper, torn-up sheets of typing paper and ticker tape, which flutter down through the sunshine like snow, and settle on the street and the cars and the people below.

[pause]

The President waves and smiles. Mrs. Kennedy waves and smiles. The motorcycles roar. The Secret Service agents walk and jog alongside the car, close to the bumpers, watching the crowd. The noise is tremendous.

[long-pause]

All along Main Street, people are seeing the President, many of them for the only time in their lives.

[pause]

A young secretary, leaning out of an office window, sees him pass right below her and waves so hard that she nearly drops her handbag. A businessman, standing on the steps of his bank, takes off his hat. A small boy on his father's shoulders waves a little flag. An old woman, who remembers seeing President Roosevelt pass along this same street many years ago, wipes her eyes with her handkerchief.

[pause]

{img:father-child-on-shoulders-waving-flag}
The motorcade moves slowly, about eleven miles an hour, so that everyone can have a good look. It takes about ten minutes to go the whole length of Main Street, through the middle of downtown.

[long-pause]

In the President's car, the mood is joyful.

[pause]

Governor Connally is smiling. He has been a little worried about how Dallas would receive the President. But the crowds are bigger and friendlier than anyone expected.

[pause]

Nellie Connally turns around in her seat to look at the President, and says, with a happy smile: Mr. President, you can't say Dallas doesn't love you.

[pause]

And the President smiles back at her, and answers: No, you certainly can't.

[long-pause]

Behind the President's car, in an open convertible, ride Vice President Johnson and Lady Bird, smiling and waving to the people on the pavements, with Senator Ralph Yarborough beside them. Lady Bird has a bouquet of yellow roses on her lap.

[pause]

Behind them come the cars of the congressmen and the officials, and then the buses with the reporters and the photographers, who lean out of the windows with their cameras to catch the scene.

[pause]

Further back still, in a little procession of their own, come the local politicians and the guests of honour, and at the very end, an ambulance and a few more police cars, which always follow a President wherever he goes.

[long-pause]

At the western end of Main Street, the tall buildings come to an end.

[pause]

Ahead is the green open space of Dealey Plaza, with its lawns and its trees and its white colonnades. The motorcade turns right onto Houston Street, and goes slowly up one short block, beside the old county buildings, toward the red brick Book Depository on the corner.

[pause]

{img:houston-street-corner-red-brick-building}
The crowds here are smaller, and more scattered. People stand along the kerb in little groups, and on the grass of the plaza, and on the steps of the buildings. Some of the people who work in the Book Depository have come out onto the front steps to watch.

[long-pause]

In Dealey Plaza, the Newman family is waiting.

[pause]

Bill and Gayle Newman have found a good place to stand, on the grass on the north side of Elm Street, a little way down the slope from the Book Depository, near the bottom of the grassy hill. The boys are restless after waiting so long, but excited. Billy stands holding his father's hand. Clayton is in his mother's arms.

[pause]

{img:young-family-standing-on-grass-park}
They can hear the cheering from the downtown streets, coming closer and closer. Then they see the motorcycles turning onto Houston Street, and the long, dark blue car behind them.

[long-pause]

Up on the slope, a little higher, near the white concrete colonnade, Abraham Zapruder is standing on a low concrete wall, with his camera in his hands.

[pause]

He has come down from his office with his receptionist, Marilyn Sitzman, and a few of his employees. He has picked this spot because it gives a good view of the whole curve of Elm Street, and he has climbed up on the wall to see over the heads of the people below.

[pause]

He suffers from dizziness, and he is afraid of losing his balance on the narrow wall. So Marilyn stands behind him, holding onto his coat, to keep him steady.

[pause]

He lifts the little gold-coloured camera to his eye, and waits.

[long-pause]

At the corner of Houston and Elm, the President's car makes a slow, sharp turn to the left, in front of the Book Depository, and begins to go down the gentle slope of Elm Street, toward the railway bridge.

[pause]

It is just after half past twelve. The sun is shining. The people on the grass are waving and cheering. The President is smiling, and lifting his hand to wave.

[pause]

{img:elm-street-curve-sunlight-trees-quiet}
In five more minutes, the motorcade would have reached the Trade Mart, where two thousand people are waiting for him at lunch, and the President was going to give a speech.

[pause]

And then, for a moment, everything changes.

[long-pause]

{img:dealey-plaza-wide-calm-noon}
It is twelve thirty in the afternoon, on Friday, the twenty-second of November, 1963.

[pause]

The long blue car is moving slowly down the gentle curve of Elm Street, through the sunshine, past the green lawns of Dealey Plaza. The people on the grass are waving. The President is waving back.

[long-pause]

Then there is a sharp, flat sound, from somewhere behind and above.

[pause]

Some people think it is a firecracker. Some think a motorcycle has backfired. A few of the pigeons on the roof of the Book Depository rise into the air in a flutter of wings.

[pause]

There is a pause, of a few seconds. Then there is another sound, and then, very soon after it, another.

[long-pause]

And in those few seconds, in the back seat of the car, the President of the United States is struck, and falls against his wife.

[pause]

Governor Connally, in the seat in front, has been wounded too.

[pause]

Mrs. Kennedy turns toward her husband. Nellie Connally pulls the Governor down into her lap, to shelter him, and bends over him.

[long-pause]

On the grass beside Elm Street, Bill Newman understands, suddenly, that something is terribly wrong.

[pause]

He turns to his wife and says: Get down. And the two of them throw themselves down on the grass, and cover their little boys with their own bodies, holding them close, pressing them down to the ground, sheltering them with their arms and their backs.

[pause]

{img:green-grass-slope-afternoon-quiet}
The boys do not understand what is happening. Clayton begins to cry. Billy lies very still underneath his father.

[pause]

Bill and Gayle lie there on the grass, holding their children, and do not move.

[long-pause]

In the car, a Secret Service agent named Clint Hill, who has been riding on the running board of the car behind, jumps down and runs forward, and climbs onto the back of the President's car.

[pause]

The car begins to speed up. Clint Hill holds on, and shields the President and Mrs. Kennedy with his body, as the big blue car accelerates away, down Elm Street, under the railway bridge, and out of sight.

[pause]

The whole thing has taken only about eight or nine seconds.

[long-pause]

Up on the concrete wall, Abraham Zapruder has kept his camera running the whole time.

[pause]

He did not stop. He did not even know, at first, that he was still filming. He simply stood there, frozen, with the camera at his eye, watching through the little window of the viewfinder.

[pause]

When the car disappeared under the bridge, he lowered the camera. Marilyn Sitzman helped him down from the wall. He was crying, and he could hardly speak.

[pause]

{img:colonnade-concrete-wall-empty-afternoon}
Then he walked slowly back across the plaza toward his office, holding the camera in his hand.

[pause]

He did not know it yet, but the little reel of film inside it would become the most closely studied piece of film in history.

[long-pause]

In Dealey Plaza, there is confusion.

[pause]

People are running in every direction. Some run up the grassy slope toward the wooden fence at the top. Some run toward the Book Depository. Some simply stand still, staring after the car, unable to believe what they have seen. Others are lying on the grass, like the Newmans, holding their children.

[pause]

{img:police-officers-walking-plaza-distance}
Policemen come running, and motorcycles, and soon there are police cars too, their sirens wailing. The officers go up the slope, and into the railway yard behind the fence, and into the Book Depository, searching.

[long-pause]

After a while, the Newmans get up from the grass.

[pause]

They are shaking. They hold their boys tightly, and look around them, at the people running and the police cars arriving and the empty street where the car has gone.

[pause]

A television cameraman from a Dallas station finds them, and asks them what they saw. Within half an hour, Bill and Gayle are standing in a television studio, still holding their little boys, telling the people of Dallas what they have seen, in quiet, shaken voices.

[pause]

They are among the very first people in the world to describe what happened.

[long-pause]

Inside the Book Depository, on the sixth floor, the police find a rifle, hidden between stacks of boxes of books, and near one of the windows that look down over Elm Street, a little nest of boxes, piled up like a wall.

[pause]

One of the men who works in the building, a young man of twenty-four named Lee Harvey Oswald, is missing. He was seen in the building a few minutes after the shots, drinking a soda in the lunchroom on the second floor. Then he walked out of the building, and disappeared into the city.

[long-pause]

{img:empty-elm-street-afternoon-light-flowers}
Within a few minutes, the news is going out across the country.

[pause]

At first, it is only a few words, on the radio and the television. Shots have been fired at the President's motorcade in Dallas. The President may have been hurt.

[pause]

All over America, people stop what they are doing, and listen. In offices, in shops, in schools, in factories, in homes, people gather around radios and televisions, and wait for more news.

[pause]

And in Dealey Plaza, the sun goes on shining on the green grass, and the curve of Elm Street, and the empty road beneath the railway bridge.

[long-pause]

{img:parkland-hospital-entrance-1963}
The President's car races along the freeway, toward a large hospital a few miles away, called Parkland Memorial Hospital.

[pause]

It arrives at the emergency entrance at about twelve thirty-eight. Doctors and nurses come running out, with stretchers. The President and the Governor are carried inside, into two separate emergency rooms, and the doors are closed behind them.

[long-pause]

Mrs. Kennedy stays with her husband.

[pause]

She will not leave him. When a nurse gently asks her to wait outside, she says, quietly but firmly, that she wants to be with him. And so she stands outside the door of the emergency room, and then inside it, in her pink suit, holding herself very straight and very still.

[pause]

{img:hospital-corridor-quiet-1963-light}
In the next room, the doctors work on Governor Connally. His wounds are serious, but he will survive. Nellie Connally waits in the corridor, praying.

[long-pause]

The doctors do everything they can for the President.

[pause]

There are many of them, the best surgeons in the hospital. They work for nearly twenty minutes, quickly and calmly, without stopping. But in their hearts, most of them know from the first moment that there is very little they can do.

[pause]

A priest is sent for, from a church nearby. Father Oscar Huber comes as quickly as he can, and goes into the room, and gives the President the last rites of the Catholic Church, while Mrs. Kennedy kneels beside him and prays.

[long-pause]

At one o'clock in the afternoon, the doctors stop.

[pause]

President John Fitzgerald Kennedy has died. He was forty-six years old.

[pause]

Mrs. Kennedy takes off her wedding ring, and slips it onto her husband's finger.

[long-pause]

At about half past one, in a nurses' classroom in the hospital, the President's assistant press secretary, Malcolm Kilduff, stands before a crowd of reporters.

[pause]

He is pale, and his voice is shaking, and he has to stop for a moment before he can speak. Then he reads a short statement. President John F. Kennedy died at approximately one o'clock Central Standard Time today, here in Dallas.

[pause]

The reporters rush to the telephones.

[long-pause]

{img:people-gathered-store-window-television}
Within minutes, the news is everywhere.

[pause]

On the television in New York, the famous news anchor Walter Cronkite, who has been reading the bulletins as they come in, takes a sheet of paper from a colleague, reads it, and takes off his glasses. He looks up at the clock on the wall. He tells the country that the President has died, at one o'clock Central Standard Time, two o'clock Eastern Standard Time, some thirty-eight minutes ago. Then he puts his glasses back on, and has to pause for a moment, to steady his voice.

[long-pause]

All over America, people hear the news.

[pause]

In schools, teachers come into the classrooms and tell the children, and some of the teachers cry, and the children do not know what to do, because they have never seen a teacher cry before. The schools send everyone home early.

[pause]

{img:classroom-1963-teacher-children-quiet}
In offices and shops, people stand in silent groups around radios. In the streets of New York and Chicago and Los Angeles, strangers stop each other to ask if it is true. Crowds gather in front of the windows of television shops, watching the screens in silence. Church bells begin to ring.

[long-pause]

In Dallas, the city is stunned.

[pause]

In the downtown streets, where an hour ago the crowds were cheering and the paper was fluttering down from the windows, people stand still on the pavements, unable to speak. Some of them weep openly. Office workers go back up to their desks, and sit, and stare at their typewriters.

[pause]

In the Dal-Tex Building, Abraham Zapruder sits in his office, with his head in his hands. On the desk beside him is his little camera.

[pause]

At the Trade Mart, where two thousand people have been waiting for lunch with the President, the news is announced from the stage. Some of the guests bow their heads, and someone begins to pray aloud, and the others join in.

[long-pause]

At the hospital, Vice President Lyndon Johnson is waiting in a small, quiet room, with his wife, Lady Bird, and a few Secret Service agents.

[pause]

He is now the President of the United States.

[pause]

{img:small-hospital-room-tall-man-waiting}
He is a tall, heavy, powerful man of fifty-five, with a long face and big ears and a deep Texas drawl. He has spent his life in politics, and he is one of the most skilful politicians in the country. But on this afternoon, he is quiet and grave, and he knows that he must act carefully and calmly, for the sake of the whole country.

[pause]

The Secret Service agents tell him that he must leave the hospital at once, and go back to the airport, to Air Force One, where he will be safe.

[long-pause]

Mrs. Kennedy will not leave without her husband.

[pause]

So, at a little after two o'clock, a bronze coffin is brought to the hospital, and the President is placed inside it, and it is carried out to a waiting car. Mrs. Kennedy walks beside it, with her hand resting on the lid.

[pause]

{img:hearse-leaving-hospital-afternoon-quiet}
She climbs into the back of the car, beside the coffin, and the car drives slowly away from Parkland Hospital, toward Love Field, where, only a little more than two hours earlier, she had stepped down from the plane into the sunshine, and been given a bouquet of red roses.

[long-pause]

{img:air-force-one-love-field-afternoon-quiet}
At Love Field, Air Force One stands on the tarmac in the afternoon sun, with its engines quiet and its door open.

[pause]

The crowd that cheered here at lunchtime has gone. The fence where the President shook hands is empty. Only a few police cars and airport workers remain, and the Secret Service agents, standing by the steps, watching.

[long-pause]

Lyndon Johnson and Lady Bird have arrived first. They wait inside the plane, in the President's cabin, with the blinds pulled down over the windows, because of the bright sun and the heat.

[pause]

Then the car with the coffin arrives. The Secret Service agents and the President's friends lift the heavy coffin and carry it slowly up the steps, and into the back of the plane, where some seats have been removed to make room for it. Mrs. Kennedy follows behind it, and sits down beside it.

[pause]

The plane is very hot inside. The air conditioning has been turned off while it waited on the ground.

[long-pause]

Before the plane takes off, there is one more thing that must be done.

[pause]

Lyndon Johnson has decided to take the oath of office here in Dallas, before they leave, so that the whole country, and the whole world, will know that the government of the United States goes on, without a pause.

[pause]

He asks for a judge to be found. And so a federal judge from Dallas, an old friend of his, a woman named Sarah T. Hughes, is driven to the airport as fast as possible.

[long-pause]

{img:small-cabin-oath-gathering-people}
Judge Hughes is sixty-seven years old, a small, energetic, grey-haired woman, one of the first women ever to become a federal judge in the United States. She climbs up the steps of the plane and squeezes into the President's cabin, where about twenty-seven people are crowded together, standing shoulder to shoulder.

[pause]

Someone finds a small Bible, a Catholic missal, that belonged to President Kennedy, on a table in his bedroom on the plane.

[pause]

They wait for Mrs. Kennedy.

[long-pause]

After a few minutes, she comes in, very quietly.

[pause]

She is still wearing her pink suit. She has not changed her clothes. Someone offered her a fresh dress, and she said no. She wants people to see what has happened.

[pause]

{img:widow-pink-suit-standing-quietly}
She stands beside Lyndon Johnson, on his left. Lady Bird stands on his right. The judge stands in front of him, holding the little book.

[long-pause]

At two thirty-eight in the afternoon, Lyndon Johnson raises his right hand, places his left hand on the book, and repeats the oath after Judge Hughes.

[pause]

I do solemnly swear that I will faithfully execute the office of President of the United States, and will to the best of my ability, preserve, protect and defend the Constitution of the United States.

[pause]

So help me God.

[long-pause]

The White House photographer, Cecil Stoughton, stands on a seat in the corner of the cabin with his camera, and takes a picture.

[pause]

It will become one of the most famous photographs in American history: the new President, tall and grave, with his hand raised; his wife beside him, looking up at him; and on his other side, Mrs. Kennedy, in her pink suit, with a stunned, faraway look on her face.

[pause]

It is the only photograph ever taken of a President being sworn in on an aeroplane.

[long-pause]

When it is over, the new President turns to his wife and kisses her on the forehead. Lady Bird takes Mrs. Kennedy's hand. The judge leaves the plane.

[pause]

A few minutes later, the door is closed, and the engines start, and Air Force One taxis out onto the runway, and takes off into the late afternoon sky, heading east, toward Washington.

[pause]

{img:jet-climbing-evening-sky-clouds}
Behind it, Dallas grows small and disappears.

[long-pause]

Far below the plane, the land of Texas slides past in the late afternoon light: the long brown plains, the little towns, the roads running straight to the horizon, the stock ponds glinting like coins in the fields.

[pause]

Then come the forests and the rivers of Louisiana and Mississippi and Tennessee, and the long blue line of the Appalachian Mountains, and the evening falls as the plane flies on toward the east, into the dusk.

[long-pause]

The flight to Washington takes about two and a half hours.

[pause]

In the front of the plane, the new President makes telephone calls, and talks with his advisers, and begins to plan what he will say and do when he arrives. He telephones President Kennedy's mother, Rose, in Massachusetts, to tell her how sorry he is. Lady Bird telephones Nellie Connally, at the hospital in Dallas.

[pause]

In the back of the plane, Mrs. Kennedy sits beside her husband's coffin, with a few of his oldest friends and aides around her. They talk quietly about him. Sometimes they are silent.

[long-pause]

{img:night-airport-arrival-floodlights-crowd}
At about six o'clock in the evening, Air Force One lands at Andrews Air Force Base, outside Washington, in the darkness.

[pause]

A large crowd is waiting, behind ropes, in the floodlights, and a great many television cameras. The new President steps down onto the tarmac, and speaks for a moment to the nation, in a few short, simple sentences.

[pause]

This is a sad time for all people, he says. We have suffered a loss that cannot be weighed. I will do my best. That is all I can do. I ask for your help, and God's.

[long-pause]

Mrs. Kennedy stays with the coffin as it is lowered from the plane, and placed in a grey navy ambulance, and driven away into the night, to a navy hospital near Washington.

[pause]

She rides with it, in the back, all the way. She does not go home to rest until the early hours of the next morning.

[pause]

When people ask her, gently, if she would like to change out of her pink suit, she still says no.

[long-pause]

Back in Dallas, night has fallen.

[pause]

In Dealey Plaza, people have begun to come quietly, one by one and in little groups, to stand on the grass beside Elm Street, near the place where it happened. Some of them have brought flowers, which they lay down on the lawn. Some of them simply stand, with their heads bowed.

[pause]

{img:dealey-plaza-night-flowers-people-quiet}
The street lamps shine on the curving road, and on the white colonnades, and on the growing heap of flowers on the grass. Above them, the dark windows of the Book Depository look down over the plaza. And beyond the railway bridge, the traffic goes slowly by on the freeway, with its headlights shining in the dark.

[long-pause]

{img:police-headquarters-dallas-night-1963}
While the plane was flying east, the police in Dallas had been searching for the young man who had left the Book Depository.

[pause]

His name, as we have heard, was Lee Harvey Oswald. He was twenty-four years old, a slim, quiet young man with thinning brown hair, who had once been a Marine, and had spent some years living in the Soviet Union, before coming back to Texas with his Russian wife, Marina, and their little daughter. In October, he had found a job at the Book Depository, filling orders for schoolbooks.

[long-pause]

About forty-five minutes after the shots in Dealey Plaza, in a quiet neighbourhood across the river called Oak Cliff, a Dallas police officer named J. D. Tippit stopped a man walking along the pavement, to ask him some questions.

[pause]

The officer lost his life there, on the quiet street. He was thirty-nine years old, a kind, steady man with a wife and three children.

[pause]

A little while later, a shoe shop manager saw a man hurry into a cinema nearby, without buying a ticket, and he told the girl at the ticket booth, and she called the police.

[long-pause]

The cinema was called the Texas Theatre, and that afternoon, it was showing a war film to a small audience of about a dozen people.

[pause]

Many police officers arrived. They turned up the house lights, and walked slowly down the aisles. In one of the rows near the back, they found Lee Harvey Oswald, sitting in the dark. There was a short struggle in the seats, and then he was taken away, out through the crowd that had gathered in front of the cinema, and driven to police headquarters downtown.

[pause]

{img:old-cinema-facade-marquee-afternoon}
That night, he was charged with the murder of Officer Tippit, and then with the murder of the President. He said that he had not done it.

[long-pause]

On Saturday, the twenty-third of November, it rained, in Dallas and in Washington.

[pause]

The whole country seemed to stop. The shops were mostly closed. The cinemas were dark. The television stations cancelled all their usual programmes, and showed nothing but the news, hour after hour, and quiet music, and church services.

[pause]

{img:rainy-saturday-family-watching-tv-quiet}
In Washington, President Kennedy lay in the East Room of the White House, in a closed coffin draped with the American flag, while his family and friends and the members of the government came to pay their respects.

[pause]

In Dallas, people sat at home and watched the television, and talked in low voices, and many of them felt a heavy sorrow that something like this had happened in their own city.

[long-pause]

In the house of the Newman family, the boys were playing in the living room, as usual, and the television was on.

[pause]

Bill and Gayle had hardly slept. They had told their story many times now, to the police and to reporters. They sat together on the sofa, holding hands, watching the news, and every now and then one of them would get up and go and look at the boys, just to see that they were all right.

[long-pause]

Abraham Zapruder had also had a sleepless night.

[pause]

On Friday afternoon, he had taken his film to a laboratory to be developed, with the help of the Secret Service, and copies had been made for the government. That night, he had a terrible dream, in which he saw a sign in Times Square, in New York, saying that people could come and see the President's death.

[pause]

On Saturday, he sold the film to Life magazine, on the condition that they would treat it with respect, and not show its most painful moment to the public. And he gave a large part of the money he received to the widow of Officer Tippit, and her three children.

[long-pause]

On Sunday morning, the twenty-fourth of November, the police were moving Oswald from the police headquarters to the county jail, a few blocks away.

[pause]

The move was to take place in the basement garage of the police building, and the television cameras were there, broadcasting live to the whole country. Millions of people were watching at home.

[pause]

{img:basement-garage-reporters-cameras-1963}
As Oswald was led out through the crowd of reporters, between two detectives, a man stepped forward from the crowd. He was a Dallas nightclub owner named Jack Ruby. He fired a single shot, and Oswald fell.

[pause]

He died later that day, at Parkland Hospital, the same hospital where the President had been taken two days earlier.

[long-pause]

Now there would never be a trial.

[pause]

Jack Ruby was arrested at once. He said that he had acted because he was upset, and because he wanted to spare Mrs. Kennedy the pain of coming back to Dallas for a trial. He was convicted, and he died in prison a few years later, of an illness.

[pause]

{img:sunday-church-bells-dallas-steeple}
Many questions were left unanswered, and some of them have been argued about ever since.

[long-pause]

That same Sunday, in Washington, the President's coffin was carried from the White House to the Capitol, on a horse-drawn gun carriage, called a caisson, along Pennsylvania Avenue, through enormous, silent crowds.

[pause]

There, in the great round hall under the dome of the Capitol, called the Rotunda, the coffin was placed on a black platform, and the doors were opened to the public.

[pause]

All through that Sunday afternoon and evening, and through the night, and into Monday morning, the people came. They stood in a line that stretched for miles through the cold streets of Washington, waiting for hours in the dark, wrapped in coats and scarves. About a quarter of a million people walked slowly past the coffin, in silence, to say goodbye.

[long-pause]

In Dallas, on Sunday evening, the churches were full.

[pause]

People came to pray, and to sing hymns, and to sit quietly together. Some of the ministers spoke about what had happened, and about the city, and about how people must be kind to one another.

[pause]

{img:church-interior-candles-congregation-evening}
And in Dealey Plaza, the flowers on the grass beside Elm Street had grown into a great bank of colour, wreaths and bouquets and single roses, with little cards and notes tucked among them, stretching along the lawn under the street lamps.

[long-pause]

{img:washington-cold-bright-morning-crowds}
Monday, the twenty-fifth of November, 1963, is a cold, bright, clear day in Washington.

[pause]

It is the day of President Kennedy's funeral. The schools and offices and shops are closed, all over the country. Almost everyone in America is watching on television. In other countries too, all over the world, millions of people are watching, or listening on the radio.

[long-pause]

In the morning, the coffin is carried down the steps of the Capitol, and placed once more on the caisson, drawn by six grey horses.

[pause]

Behind it, a soldier leads a riderless horse, a black horse named Black Jack, with an empty saddle and a pair of riding boots placed backwards in the stirrups. It is an old military tradition, for a fallen leader. Black Jack is lively and restless, and tosses his head and dances sideways along the street, as if he does not understand what is happening.

[pause]

The procession moves slowly back along Pennsylvania Avenue, to the White House, with the muffled drums beating, slowly and steadily, all the way.

[long-pause]

At the White House, Mrs. Kennedy steps out to walk behind the coffin.

[pause]

She is dressed all in black, with a black veil over her face. Beside her walk the President's brothers, Robert and Edward. Behind them walk the new President and Mrs. Johnson, and then the leaders of the world: the President of France, General de Gaulle, very tall in his uniform; the Emperor of Ethiopia, Haile Selassie, small and dignified; the Queen of Greece, the King of Belgium, Prince Philip from Britain, and many others, kings and presidents and prime ministers, walking together through the streets of Washington.

[pause]

{img:funeral-procession-dignitaries-walking-street}
They walk slowly, eight blocks, to St. Matthew's Cathedral, while the crowds along the pavements stand in complete silence. The only sounds are the drums, and the bagpipes of a Scottish regiment, and the clop of the horses' hooves.

[long-pause]

In the cathedral, there is a funeral mass.

[pause]

The Cardinal Archbishop of Boston, Richard Cushing, an old friend of the Kennedy family, who married the President and Mrs. Kennedy ten years before, leads the service. A famous tenor sings Ave Maria. The bishop reads from the President's own words.

[pause]

Mrs. Kennedy sits in the front row, with her two children, Caroline and John, beside her.

[long-pause]

When the service is over, the family comes out onto the steps of the cathedral, in the bright, cold sunlight, as the coffin is carried out and placed again on the caisson.

[pause]

It is little John's third birthday.

[pause]

He is standing beside his mother on the steps, in a little light blue coat and short trousers, with his sister beside him. His mother bends down and whispers something to him. And as the coffin goes by, the little boy steps forward, and raises his right hand to his forehead, and gives his father a salute.

[pause]

{img:cathedral-steps-family-cold-sunlight}
The photograph of that moment will be seen all over the world, and remembered forever.

[long-pause]

Then the long procession sets off again, across the Potomac River, to Arlington National Cemetery, on the green hills of Virginia, where the soldiers of the United States are buried.

[pause]

On a gentle slope below the white house that once belonged to General Robert E. Lee, a grave has been prepared, looking out over the river toward the city of Washington, and the Lincoln Memorial, and the dome of the Capitol beyond.

[pause]

{img:arlington-hillside-view-washington-autumn}
President Kennedy had visited this place only a few months earlier, and had said, looking at the view, that he could stay there forever.

[long-pause]

At the graveside, there is a short service.

[pause]

Fifty jet planes fly overhead, one for each state, and then Air Force One itself, flying low over the cemetery, and dipping its wings in a last salute. A bugler plays the slow, sad notes of Taps. The soldiers fire three volleys into the air. The flag is lifted from the coffin and folded into a neat triangle, and given to Mrs. Kennedy.

[pause]

Then Mrs. Kennedy steps forward and, with a lighted torch, she lights a small flame at the head of the grave. It is called the eternal flame, and it is meant to burn forever, in memory of the President.

[pause]

It is burning still today.

[long-pause]

That afternoon, all over America, the country stood still.

[pause]

At the moment the funeral began, trains stopped on their tracks for a minute of silence. Buses pulled over to the side of the road. In New York, the subway trains stopped underground. In the cities, the traffic lights were left on red, and nobody moved. The flags hung at half mast on every public building in the land.

[pause]

In Dallas too, the city stopped. The shops were closed, and the offices, and the schools. The people of Dallas sat at home in front of their televisions, as people did everywhere, and watched the long, slow procession through the streets of Washington, and the little boy on the cathedral steps.

[long-pause]

That evening, in the White House, Mrs. Kennedy gives a small birthday party for John, as she had promised, with a cake and candles, and a few of his little friends, so that he would not remember his third birthday only as a sad day.

[pause]

{img:white-house-window-evening-light-november}
And across the country, as the sun goes down, the long weekend comes to an end. People turn off their televisions, and sit for a while in the quiet. The next morning, they will go back to work, and to school, and to their ordinary lives.

[pause]

But many of them will remember, for the rest of their lives, exactly where they were, and what they were doing, when they heard the news from Dallas.

[long-pause]

{img:commission-hearing-room-1964}
A week after the funeral, President Johnson asked the Chief Justice of the United States, Earl Warren, to lead a commission to find out everything that could be found out about what had happened in Dallas.

[pause]

For ten months, the Warren Commission worked. Its members and lawyers questioned more than five hundred people. They studied the photographs and the films, among them the film of Abraham Zapruder, frame by frame. They visited Dealey Plaza, and looked out of the window on the sixth floor of the Book Depository.

[long-pause]

In September 1964, the Commission published its report, a book of more than eight hundred pages, with twenty-six more volumes of evidence.

[pause]

It concluded that Lee Harvey Oswald had fired the shots from the sixth floor window, and that he had acted alone. It concluded that Jack Ruby, too, had acted alone.

[pause]

{img:library-shelf-thick-report-volumes}
Many people accepted its findings. Many others did not. Over the years that followed, there have been many books, and investigations, and arguments, about what happened in those few seconds in Dealey Plaza. Some of those questions are still discussed today.

[long-pause]

The people who were there that day went on with their lives, as people do.

[pause]

Governor John Connally recovered from his wounds, and went back to his work as Governor of Texas. He served for many more years in public life. Nellie Connally lived until 2006, and late in her life she wrote a book about that day, and about the last words she ever spoke to President Kennedy, in the car, in the sunshine.

[pause]

She always said that the people of Dallas had loved him that day, and that she would never forget their faces in the crowd.

[long-pause]

Abraham Zapruder went back to his dress business, and to his family, and to his grandchildren.

[pause]

He was a gentle man, and the events of that day stayed with him always. He gave his testimony to the Warren Commission, and he wept as he described what he had seen. He said that he never wanted to use his camera again, and that he had stopped taking films of his family.

[pause]

He lived until 1970. Today, the original film is kept in the National Archives, in Washington, in a cool, dark vault.

[long-pause]

{img:family-boys-grown-older-1970s-garden}
The Newman family went home, and raised their boys.

[pause]

Bill and Gayle Newman never forgot the moment they lay down on the grass to shelter their children. For the rest of their lives, people would ask them about it, and they would tell the story, quietly and simply, as it had happened. Billy and Clayton grew up, and had children of their own, and later, grandchildren.

[long-pause]

Mrs. Kennedy moved out of the White House a few weeks after the funeral, with Caroline and John.

[pause]

{img:new-york-apartment-window-park-autumn}
A few days after the funeral, she gave an interview to a writer named Theodore White, in which she spoke about her husband, and about a musical that he had loved, called Camelot, about the legend of King Arthur and his round table. She said that he liked to listen to the record at night, before he went to sleep, and especially the last lines of the song, about one brief shining moment.

[pause]

After that, people began to speak of the Kennedy years as Camelot.

[pause]

She later moved to New York, where she raised her children, worked as a book editor, and lived quietly, away from politics, until she passed away in 1994. She is buried beside President Kennedy at Arlington, under the eternal flame.

[long-pause]

In Washington, the Kennedy children grew up.

[pause]

Caroline studied law, and wrote books, and later served her country as an ambassador, first to Japan, and then to Australia. John grew up to be a lawyer and a magazine publisher in New York, handsome and well liked, much loved by the American people, who had watched him grow since the day of his salute on the cathedral steps. He lost his life in 1999, in an aeroplane accident off the coast of Massachusetts, together with his wife and her sister.

[long-pause]

President Lyndon Johnson served out the rest of President Kennedy's term, and was elected in his own right in 1964, by a very large majority.

[pause]

In 1964 and 1965, he worked with Congress to pass the great Civil Rights Act and the Voting Rights Act, which President Kennedy had begun, and which changed the lives of millions of Americans.

[pause]

And in July 1969, as President Kennedy had promised, two American astronauts walked on the surface of the moon, and came safely home.

[long-pause]

For the city of Dallas, the years after 1963 were difficult.

[pause]

For a long time, people in other parts of the country, and the world, thought of Dallas as the city where the President was killed. Many people in Dallas felt a deep sadness about it, and some felt ashamed, though they had done nothing wrong, and though most of them had stood on the pavements that day, waving and cheering, glad to see him.

[pause]

{img:dallas-skyline-1970s-evening-lights}
Slowly, the city came to terms with what had happened. It grew, and changed, and became one of the largest cities in America. It built new towers and new museums, new parks and new neighbourhoods. A football team called the Dallas Cowboys became famous all over the world.

[long-pause]

And in 1970, the city built a memorial to President Kennedy, one block east of Dealey Plaza.

[pause]

It was designed by a famous architect, Philip Johnson, and it is very simple. It is a square of tall white concrete walls, open to the sky, with no roof, and narrow openings at the north and the south. Inside, in the middle, there is a low, dark slab of granite, with only the name of the President on it, in gold letters.

[pause]

{img:white-concrete-memorial-open-sky}
It is meant to be a quiet place, a place to stop for a moment, away from the noise of the city, and look up at the sky.

[long-pause]

{img:dealey-plaza-today-morning-trees}
Dealey Plaza is still there today, at the western edge of downtown Dallas, and it looks very much as it did in 1963.

[pause]

The three streets still curve down through the little park, past the green lawns and the trees, and under the old railway bridge. The white concrete colonnades still stand on either side. The grassy slope on the north side of Elm Street is still there, with its wooden fence at the top.

[long-pause]

Every day, visitors come to the plaza, from all over the world.

[pause]

They walk slowly along the pavement of Elm Street, and stand on the grass, and look up at the old red brick building on the corner. They sit on the low concrete walls, and read the plaques. Some of them stand where the Newman family stood, and some of them stand on the little wall where Abraham Zapruder stood with his camera.

[pause]

In 1993, the whole plaza, with the buildings around it, was made a National Historic Landmark, so that it would stay the way it was, and be remembered.

[long-pause]

The Texas School Book Depository still stands on the corner of Elm and Houston Streets.

[pause]

The book company moved out many years ago. Today, most of the building is used as offices by the county. But the sixth floor has been turned into a museum.

[pause]

{img:brick-building-corner-blue-sky-today}
It opened in 1989, and it tells the story of President Kennedy's life, of his visit to Dallas, of the events of that day, and of everything that came after. Visitors walk slowly through the quiet rooms, past photographs and films and recordings, and the voices of people remembering.

[pause]

In the corner of the floor, behind a glass wall, the window is still there, with the boxes of books arranged just as the police found them.

[long-pause]

Love Field is still the city's airport, much bigger now, though most of the long-distance flights now go from a newer airport further out.

[pause]

Parkland Hospital is still there too, though it has moved into a new building, much larger, just across the road from the old one. The Texas Theatre in Oak Cliff still shows films, and has become a much-loved neighbourhood cinema again.

[pause]

And on the roof of the old Magnolia Building, the great red winged horse, Pegasus, still turns slowly in the night, glowing above the streets, just as it did in 1963.

[long-pause]

Not far from Dealey Plaza, in the middle of downtown, there is a little park with the copy of John Neely Bryan's log cabin in it, and a plain old county courthouse of red stone, built in the 1890s, with turrets and arched windows.

[pause]

People call the old courthouse Old Red. Today it is a museum of the history of Dallas, and it tells the whole story of the city, from the little log cabin on the riverbank to the oil boom and the tall towers, and the long autumn of 1963, and the years that came after.

[pause]

On a sunny afternoon, families walk through its rooms, and children press buttons and listen to the voices of old Texans telling stories, and look at the photographs of the city as it used to be.

[long-pause]

The Kennedy Memorial, the square of white walls one block from the plaza, is a calm and quiet place.

[pause]

Visitors step in through the narrow opening, out of the noise of the traffic, and stand for a while beside the dark granite slab with the President's name on it. Around them, the white walls rise up, cutting off the city. Above them, there is only the sky.

[pause]

{img:memorial-interior-granite-slab-sky}
Some people leave flowers. Some sit on the steps. Some simply stand, and look up at the clouds drifting over the open square, and then step out again into the sunshine.

[long-pause]

Every year, on the twenty-second of November, people gather in Dealey Plaza, at half past twelve, to remember.

[pause]

They stand together on the grass, and there is a moment of silence, and sometimes a short prayer, or a song, or a few words. Then the people slowly go back to their work, and the traffic moves on along Elm Street.

[pause]

{img:november-afternoon-plaza-people-flowers}
In 2013, on the fiftieth anniversary, thousands of people came, in a cold, grey drizzle, and the bells of the churches of Dallas rang out across the city at the moment it happened.

[long-pause]

Dallas is a very big city now, more than ten times larger than the small market town on the Trinity River that it once was.

[pause]

Its skyline is full of new towers, shining blue and green and gold in the sunset. At night, the buildings are outlined in coloured lights, and one of them, a tall tower with a great glowing ball on top, can be seen from miles away.

[pause]

In the neighbourhoods, families still have breakfast in their kitchens, and children still walk to school, and on Friday nights in autumn, the high school football stadiums are still full, under the bright lights.

[long-pause]
