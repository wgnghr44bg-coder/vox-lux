In the autumn of 1963, Dallas was one of the fastest growing cities in America. New suburbs spread across the prairie, jet airliners landed at Love Field, and a red neon horse glowed above downtown. Most people now know the city for a single hour of a single day. Yet for its residents, it was a place of school buses, supermarkets and Friday night football. In this story, we will look at everyday life in Dallas that year.

The most famous sign in Dallas was a red horse with wings, and it sat on top of an oil company. The winged horse was called Pegasus. It stood on the roof of the Magnolia Building, the headquarters of an oil company. The building had opened in 1922, and for a time it was the tallest in Texas. The red neon horse was added in 1934. It was enormous and turned slowly on a steel frame, glowing against the night sky. On clear nights, it could be seen from far out on the prairie. Drivers coming into the city used it as a landmark, much as sailors use a lighthouse. By 1963, taller towers of glass, steel and polished stone surrounded it. They carried the names of banks and insurance companies. Yet the old horse kept its place in local affection. Families pointed it out to visiting relatives. People who had moved away described it to their friends. Nobody had planned for a company sign to become a city symbol. It earned that role simply by remaining there, night after night, for decades.

[long-pause]

{img:02-love-field-jet-airliner-terminal-1963}

At the city airport, the new jet airliners made New York feel like a short afternoon away. The airport was called Love Field. It had been named after an army pilot of the early days of flying, and by 1963 it was one of the busiest in the South. The new jet airliners landed there, one after another, with a long whistle that made people on the ground look up. A few years earlier, a trip to New York or Los Angeles had taken a whole day by propeller plane, with stops along the way. Now it could take just a few hours. Passengers dressed up to fly. Men wore suits and ties, and women wore gloves and hats, and stewardesses served meals on real china. Families came to the airport on a Sunday afternoon just to watch the planes. They stood at the fence with ice cream cones and counted the landings. Flying still felt like a small miracle. Dallas was proud to have the runways, and proud to be a place that people flew to.

[long-pause]

{img:03-new-suburb-street-ranch-houses}

Dallas was so new that many of its streets had been open fields only a few years before. In 1963, nearly seven hundred thousand people lived in Dallas, and the population was growing every year. The city spread across the prairie in every direction. New neighbourhoods appeared with long, straight streets and houses of brick or pale wood. Each house had a lawn, a driveway and a young tree held up by a stake. Whole districts consisted of freshly built homes. Builders worked from sunrise to sunset. New schools, churches and shopping centres opened almost every month. Families arrived from smaller towns across Texas and Oklahoma, hoping for steady work and a better life. Many had never lived in a city before. Federal home loans and new highways made suburban houses affordable for millions of American families in these years. Dallas was a striking example of this national trend. For many newcomers, a house with a yard represented security and success. The city felt like a place where people could start again.

[long-pause]

{img:04-oil-wells-east-texas-1930s}

Dallas has no oil wells of its own, yet oil made it rich. The great oil field was discovered in East Texas in 1930, about a hundred miles away. A wildcatter drilled a well that most people thought was foolish, and it came in. Soon thousands of wells were pumping. The oil came out of the ground in the countryside. But the money, the paperwork and the planning all came to Dallas. Oil companies opened offices downtown. The banks that lent them money moved in next door. Then came the businesses that sold pipes, trucks and machinery, and the lawyers who wrote their contracts. Hotels and restaurants followed, for all the people who worked in them. By 1963, a lunch table in a Dallas restaurant could hold a deal worth more than a whole farm. Men leaned over their steaks and talked about land, leases and the price of a barrel. Yet most families in the city never saw an oil well. They simply lived in a town that oil had built.

[long-pause]

{img:05-electronics-lab-engineer-1958}

In a quiet laboratory in Dallas, one tall engineer invented the tiny chip that now sits inside every phone. Texas Instruments was based in Dallas and made electronic components for radios, computers and military equipment. In the summer of 1958, a newly hired engineer named Jack Kilby worked almost alone in its laboratory. Most staff were on holiday, and new employees had no vacation time yet. Kilby was tall, quiet and thoughtful. He was working on a problem that troubled the whole industry. Electronic circuits needed many separate parts joined by tiny wires, which made them large, costly and unreliable. Kilby wondered whether all the parts could be made from a single piece of material. In September 1958, he demonstrated a small sliver of germanium containing a simple working circuit. It was the first integrated circuit. In 1963, few people outside the industry understood its importance. Yet it became the foundation of modern electronics. In 2000, Kilby received the Nobel Prize in Physics. Today, descendants of his chip are found in almost every electronic device.

[long-pause]

{img:06-state-fair-big-tex-families}

Every October, a giant cowboy at the State Fair said howdy to everyone who walked in. The State Fair of Texas took place each autumn in a large park east of downtown. It had a Ferris wheel, a midway, prize cattle, pigs and quilts. And it had Big Tex, a huge cowboy figure that stood at the gate. He wore an enormous hat and boots, and he was taller than a house. A hidden voice boomed out of him, greeting every family with a friendly howdy. Children stared up at him with their mouths open. The fair also gave the world a favourite snack. Sausages dipped in cornmeal batter and fried on a stick had been sold there since the early 1940s. People called them corny dogs. In the evening, the lights came on across the fairground, and the smell of hot oil and sugar drifted through the crowd. For many Dallas families, the fair was the highlight of the year. It felt like proof that this big city still had a small-town heart.

[long-pause]

{img:07-cotton-bowl-stadium-crowd-october}

Once a year, the whole fairground turned into a football battlefield between Texas and Oklahoma. Next to the State Fair grounds stood a large stadium called the Cotton Bowl. Every October, during fair season, the University of Texas played the University of Oklahoma there. The rivalry was intense. On game day, the stadium divided down the middle. One half wore burnt orange, the other crimson. Families brought blankets and flasks of coffee. The roar of the crowd could be heard far beyond the stadium walls. After the game, fans poured into the fair, still arguing over every play. The annual game had been played in Dallas since the 1920s. In 1960, the city also gained a new professional team, the Dallas Cowboys. They played their early home games in the Cotton Bowl and struggled at first. Within a decade, they became one of the most famous teams in America. Football in Texas was more than a sport. It was a source of community pride and identity.

[long-pause]

{img:08-trinity-river-levees-cottonwoods}

The whole city began beside a slow brown river that most visitors never noticed. Dallas grew up on the banks of the Trinity River. It is not a grand river. For most of the year, it moves slowly between muddy banks, lined with cottonwoods and willows, with herons standing in the shallows. In 1841, a young lawyer and trader named John Neely Bryan built a log cabin there. It stood at a place where travellers could cross, and soon others settled near him. Farmers brought cotton to sell. Then came the railways, which turned a small market town into a real city. The river could also be dangerous. After a bad flood in 1908, the people of Dallas built tall grassy levees to hold the water back. By 1963, walkers strolled along them at the weekend. A copy of Bryan's cabin stood in the middle of downtown, small and dark among the towers. It was there to remind busy people where it all began.

[long-pause]

{img:09-texas-storm-clouds-prairie-evening}

In Dallas, the weather could be so big that people planned their whole year around it. Dallas summers were very hot. Temperatures often rose above a hundred degrees Fahrenheit, and heat shimmered above the pavements. People spent afternoons indoors, cooled by new window air conditioners. Air conditioning spread rapidly in Texas during the 1950s and changed daily life profoundly. Spring brought dramatic thunderstorms across the plains. They carried lightning, hail and sometimes tornadoes. Dallas lies in a region later nicknamed Tornado Alley, and families watched the sky carefully. Some kept storm cellars. Autumn was widely considered the best season. The days were warm and bright, the sky deep blue and the evenings pleasantly cool. School started, football season began and the State Fair filled the park. Yet the weather could still change quickly. A sunny morning might turn into a damp afternoon. On the twenty-second of November, 1963, morning rain cleared shortly before noon. That change in the weather would have lasting consequences.

[long-pause]

{img:10-dallas-downtown-autumn-afternoon-traffic}

By late 1963, Dallas felt like a city that believed it was only going up. In 1963, Dallas had a strong sense of confidence. Office workers filled downtown each morning in suits and smart dresses. Oilmen made deals over lunch. Shopkeepers prepared for Christmas, now only a month away. Residents proudly called their city Big D. They took pride in its tall buildings, fine stores, football teams and State Fair. Jet airliners at Love Field could reach New York or Los Angeles in a few hours. The local economy was growing quickly. Yet prosperity was not shared equally. Black residents still lived under segregation. Many schools, neighbourhoods and public places remained separated by race. Civil rights activists were pressing for change, and some progress had begun. Dallas also had a reputation for strong conservative politics. Some groups were openly hostile to President Kennedy. Even so, most residents felt optimistic about the future. For many families, it was simply an ordinary autumn in a growing Texas city.

[long-pause]

{img:11-suburban-kitchen-1963-breakfast}

A typical Dallas family began its day to the sound of a coffee percolator bubbling. The kitchen was bright, and probably painted a cheerful pale yellow. A big white refrigerator hummed in the corner. A gas stove clicked and glowed. On the counter stood a toaster and a glass coffee percolator, which popped and gurgled as it brewed. On the windowsill, a small radio played the weather, the news and a song or two. The mother stood at the stove in a cotton dress and an apron, frying eggs and bacon. Her hair had been neatly set the night before. The father sat at the table in a white shirt and narrow tie, reading the morning paper. In a few minutes, he would drive to work along the new freeway. The children came in, dressed for school, and sat down to cereal and orange juice. A family like this did not think of itself as special. Yet this is exactly the kind of morning that history tends to forget, and which is worth remembering.

[long-pause]

{img:12-kids-walking-to-school-sidewalk}

Most children walked to school together, with their books held by a leather strap. In 1963, most children carried their books fastened together with a leather strap. Backpacks were not yet common. Children swung lunch boxes, often decorated with cartoon characters or cowboys. Groups of children walked to school together through the neighbourhood. Schools were usually long, low buildings of pale brick with large windows. Inside, wooden desks stood in neat rows. Each morning, pupils stood, placed their hands over their hearts and recited the Pledge of Allegiance. Lessons included reading, arithmetic, spelling and Texas history. Rules were strict. Children were expected to sit quietly and raise their hands before speaking. At lunchtime, they traded sandwiches and baseball cards. Dallas schools were still largely segregated in 1963, despite a Supreme Court ruling nine years earlier. Integration was proceeding very slowly. Many adults who grew up in this period later described school days as orderly and predictable. Their memories reflect both the security and the limits of that time.

[long-pause]

{img:13-housewife-clothesline-backyard-texas-sun}

Washing machines were common, but most clothes still dried on a line in the sun. Once the house was empty, the mother began her work. She did the washing in an electric machine in the kitchen or a small room off the garage. Then she carried the wet clothes out into the back yard and pegged them to a long line. In the Texas sun, sheets dried in less than an hour, and they smelled of warm air. She vacuumed the carpets, dusted the furniture and made every bed. She wrote out a list for the grocery shop and planned the evening meal. In the middle of the morning, she often had coffee with a neighbour. They sat at the kitchen table and talked about the children, the school, the church bake sale and the new dresses in the downtown stores. Housework was long, and much of it was lonely. Yet it was also the centre of a whole neighbourhood, a web of small favours and borrowed cups of sugar that held a street together.

[long-pause]

{img:14-supermarket-aisle-1963-shopping-cart}

The supermarket was so new that some shoppers still felt a little amazed by it. Supermarkets spread rapidly across America in the 1950s. They were large and bright, with long aisles of colourful packaged goods. Shoppers found cake mixes, instant coffee and frozen dinners heated in aluminium trays. Only a few years earlier, many of these products had barely existed. Oranges came from California and bananas from Central America. Meat lay in long glass counters. Shoppers pushed wire carts along the aisles. At the checkout, cashiers entered prices on heavy metal registers. Young employees packed groceries into brown paper bags and often carried them to the car. A week's shopping for a family might cost around twenty dollars. Prices were low by modern standards, but wages were lower too. Most households watched their spending carefully. Advertising increasingly shaped what people bought. Television commercials introduced new brands into living rooms every evening. The supermarket became a symbol of postwar prosperity, convenience and choice for millions of American families.

[long-pause]

{img:15-ice-cream-truck-children-street-1963}

In the 1960s, the tune of the ice cream truck brought children running from every yard. By late afternoon, suburban streets filled with children home from school. Boys rode bicycles along the pavements. Others played catch in front yards or examined new baseball gloves in garages. Girls drew hopscotch squares on driveways, skipped rope or sat on front steps. Dogs chased the bicycles. On some afternoons, an ice cream truck turned the corner, playing a tinkling tune. Children came running with nickels and dimes. They queued along the hot kerb, choosing between frozen bars and cones. Parents generally allowed children to play outdoors without constant supervision. Neighbours kept an eye on each other's children. Many people who grew up in this period later described it with great fondness. Sociologists note that such freedom was common in American suburbs of the time. It depended on quiet streets, large families and stay-at-home parents. For many, these afternoons came to represent a simple and carefree childhood.

[long-pause]

{img:16-family-dinner-table-fried-chicken-iced-tea}

In Texas, everybody drank iced tea, all year round, with plenty of ice. In the evening, the father came home, and the family sat down to dinner together. The table was set with plain plates and napkins. The meal might be meatloaf, fried chicken or a pot roast with potatoes and carrots. There were green beans, soft rolls with butter, and always a tall glass of iced tea. Dessert could be pecan pie or a slice of chocolate cake. Dinner was the one time when everyone was in the same room. The children told what happened at school. The father described his day at the office. The mother passed on the news from the neighbourhood. If someone was late, the food waited under a cloth. Before eating, many families bowed their heads and said a short prayer. Nobody picked up a telephone, because the telephone was fixed to the kitchen wall and the cord only reached so far. Perhaps the evening was slower for that reason.

[long-pause]

{img:17-family-watching-black-white-tv}

In 1963, most families watched the same three channels, and the evening news was only half an hour long. After dinner, the family moved into the living room. The television was a big wooden cabinet with a small grey screen, and the picture was black and white. There were only three main networks. Evenings brought westerns, comedy shows, quiz shows and variety hours with singers and dancers. The children lay on the carpet, propped up on their elbows. The parents sat on the sofa. The dog slept near the door. At suppertime or just after, a calm man in a dark suit read the evening news. Only a few weeks earlier, in September 1963, the CBS news had grown from fifteen minutes to a full half hour, the first on American television to do so. The anchor was a steady, friendly reporter named Walter Cronkite. Millions of people let him into their living rooms each night. Soon they would trust him with the hardest news of their lives. But on this evening, the weather was the main story.

[long-pause]

{img:18-friday-night-football-stadium-lights}

On Friday nights in autumn, the whole town seemed to move to the football stadium. In Texas, high school football held a special place in community life. On Friday nights in autumn, families drove to brightly lit stadiums. Marching bands played in shining uniforms. Cheerleaders led the crowd from the sidelines. Popcorn and hot dogs were sold in the stands. Grandparents, neighbours and young children all came to watch. The players, only seventeen or eighteen years old, were local celebrities. Weekends followed familiar routines. Saturdays were for shopping, gardening or trips to nearby lakes. Sundays meant church services, followed by large family dinners and afternoon naps. For many families, this predictable rhythm gave a strong sense of security. Historians of American life often describe the early 1960s as a period of stability for the white middle class. Yet change was approaching. Civil rights protests, the Vietnam War and new cultural movements would soon reshape American society. In November 1963, however, most weekends still followed the same comfortable pattern.

[long-pause]

{img:19-corner-drugstore-soda-fountain-boys}

On Saturday afternoons, a nickel and a bicycle could take a boy straight to the soda fountain. On Saturdays, many boys and girls rode their bicycles to the corner drugstore. At the back, there was a long counter with round stools and a shining soda fountain. A young man in a white paper hat stood behind it and made milkshakes, root beer floats and cherry colas. Everything was made by hand, one glass at a time. A milkshake cost around a quarter, and a soda a little less. Children counted their coins carefully on the counter. The shelves around them held comic books, bubble gum and model aeroplanes. Near the door, a rack of magazines showed film stars and astronauts. The pharmacist worked behind a high counter, filling prescriptions in small brown bottles. He knew every family in the neighbourhood by name. A drugstore like this was more than a shop. It was a meeting place, where news was traded and friendships were made over a tall glass and two straws.

[long-pause]

{img:20-little-house-night-porch-light}

At bedtime, the whole neighbourhood grew quiet, apart from a dog barking far away. At the end of the day, the mother tucked the children into bed and turned out the light. Perhaps she read a short story first, or hummed a song. The house grew still. Outside, the street was dark, apart from the soft glow of the street lamps and the flicker of a television behind a curtain across the road. A dog barked once and stopped. A car drove slowly by, its headlights sweeping across the ceiling. The father checked the front door, turned off the radio, and wound the alarm clock. In the cool autumn air, under the huge Texas sky, the whole city drifted off to sleep. This was the life of most people in Dallas in 1963. It was a life without great drama, full of small routines. Yet small routines are exactly what make a person feel at home. Tomorrow would begin in the same way, with a coffee percolator bubbling in the dark.

[long-pause]

{img:21-main-street-dallas-shoppers-1963}

On a weekday morning, downtown Dallas looked like a parade of hats and gloves. Downtown Dallas was the heart of the city. By nine o'clock, its pavements were crowded. Men in dark suits and narrow ties carried briefcases, and most wore hats. Women wore smart dresses, short gloves and small round hats. Young secretaries walked in pairs to their offices. Buses pulled in and out at the corners, and traffic lights clicked from red to green. Main Street was the principal shopping street. Parallel to it ran Elm Street and Commerce Street. All three were lined with stores, banks, hotels, cinemas and offices. Some buildings were old red brick, while others were new and gleaming. The sounds of heels, horns and newspaper sellers filled the streets. Dressing well for a trip downtown was considered a sign of respect. Fashion historians note that hats and gloves were still common in 1963, though they were beginning to disappear. Within a few years, more casual styles would become normal.

[long-pause]

{img:22-neiman-marcus-store-front-main-street}

The most famous shop in Dallas sold gifts so strange that people read its catalogue just for fun. Neiman Marcus stood at the corner of Main and Ervay Streets. It opened in 1907 and became one of the most famous luxury stores in America. It sold fashionable clothing, jewellery, perfume, furs and fine household goods. Customers travelled from across Texas and beyond to shop there. Inside, floors were carpeted, lighting was soft and staff spoke quietly. Dresses came from Paris and Rome. The store became especially famous for its Christmas catalogue. Each year it included extraordinary gifts. In 1960, it offered matching his-and-hers private aeroplanes. Later catalogues featured even stranger items. Newspapers reported on the catalogue every year. Many families read it purely for entertainment. Few could afford anything inside. The store's founders believed that excellent service and memorable publicity could attract customers from far away. Their approach succeeded. Neiman Marcus helped make Dallas known as a centre of fashion and retail far beyond Texas.

[long-pause]

{img:23-department-store-escalator-shoppers-1963}

The big department stores had escalators, lunch counters and a Santa Claus on the toy floor. A few streets away stood the larger department stores, where ordinary families did their shopping. They had floors and floors of clothes, shoes and household goods. There were toys, books and records, and an escalator going up and down in the middle. For many children, riding the escalator was an adventure in itself. Each store had a lunch counter, where shoppers sat on round stools and ordered a sandwich, a bowl of chili, or a slice of pie and a cup of coffee. Prices were low, and the waitresses called everyone honey. Customers paid with cash or put things on an account, and a clerk wrote the amount in a small book. In the weeks before Christmas, the windows were filled with scenes of snow, reindeer and tin toys. Children pressed their noses to the glass. On the toy floor, Santa Claus sat on a big chair, and the line of waiting children curled around the corner.

[long-pause]

{img:24-lunch-counter-diner-stools-1963}

In 1963, a hot lunch in downtown Dallas could cost less than a dollar. At noon, downtown restaurants filled quickly. Large hotels such as the Adolphus, the Baker and the Statler Hilton offered dining rooms with white tablecloths and uniformed waiters. Steak houses, Mexican restaurants and cafeterias served office workers. Small diners offered daily specials, often for less than a dollar. Businessmen discussed oil wells and property deals over lunch. Secretaries shared sandwiches on park benches. Salesmen ate pie at diner counters. Many restaurants had regular customers whose orders the staff knew by heart. That familiarity made the growing city feel more personal. Yet many lunch counters in Dallas were still segregated in the early 1960s. Black customers were often refused service or seated separately. Sit-in protests across the South challenged these practices. Change in Dallas came gradually, often through negotiation rather than confrontation. By the mid-1960s, federal law would ban segregation in restaurants nationwide. At one o'clock, the lunch crowds returned to work.

[long-pause]

{img:25-movie-theatre-marquee-evening}

In the evenings, the old movie palaces on Elm Street lit up in red and gold. When the offices closed, downtown grew quieter. But along Elm Street, the great old cinemas switched on their lights. The signs were tall, and they glowed in red and gold. People waited in long lines outside, chatting and checking their watches. Inside, the carpets were thick, there were chandeliers in the lobby, and the seats were made of red velvet. A great curtain opened slowly before each film began. A ticket cost about a dollar, and a box of popcorn cost a dime. Couples went on dates there. Families made an outing of it. Teenagers sat in the back rows. There was usually a newsreel before the main film, and a cartoon, and a trailer for what was coming next. For two hours, the ordinary world disappeared. Then everyone walked out into the cool night, still half in the story, and the lights of the signs shone in the car windows.

[long-pause]

{img:26-dress-factory-showroom-seamstresses}

Dallas was one of the great dress-making cities in America, with seamstresses on the upper floors. In 1963, Dallas was one of the leading centres of the women's clothing industry in the United States. Dozens of companies designed and manufactured dresses there. Their products were sold to department stores across the country. Many firms occupied older brick buildings near Dealey Plaza, at the western end of downtown. On the upper floors, rows of women worked at sewing machines throughout the day. The machines clattered, and the air smelled of fabric and hot irons. Designers sketched at drawing tables. Salesmen presented new collections to visiting buyers. The work was fast and demanding, and wages were modest. Yet many workers took pride in their skill. A dress made in Dallas might be worn at a wedding in Ohio or a dance in Georgia. The local garment industry grew partly because Dallas had good rail connections and a large workforce. In later decades, much clothing manufacturing moved overseas, and most of these workshops closed.

[long-pause]

{img:27-dealey-plaza-park-colonnade-1963}

At the western end of downtown lay a small, peaceful park where office workers ate their lunch. At the far end of Main, Elm and Commerce Streets, the three roads came together and passed under a railway bridge. Beside them lay a little green park, with lawns, trees and white concrete colonnades. It was called Dealey Plaza. The name honoured George Dealey, a newspaper publisher who had done a great deal for the city. The plaza was built in the 1930s and opened in 1940. It was a pleasant, orderly place, a bit like a small outdoor room. The three streets curved gently down through it. On the north side of Elm Street rose a grassy slope with a wooden fence on top and a few trees. In the afternoon sun, the shadows stretched long across the grass. Office workers sat on the low walls to eat their sandwiches and read the paper. Pigeons walked between their feet. In 1963, almost nobody outside Dallas had heard of it.

[long-pause]

{img:28-school-book-depository-red-brick-building}

One of the plainest buildings in Dallas was a seven-storey warehouse full of school books. At the corner of Elm and Houston Streets stood an old brick building, seven floors tall. It had been built at the start of the century, for a company that sold farm equipment. By 1963, it was used by a firm that stored and sent out school textbooks to the schools of Texas. It was called the Texas School Book Depository. It was a plain, ordinary place, with rows of windows and a big sign on the roof for a car rental company. Every day, men and women unpacked boxes, sorted books, packed them again and sent them out by truck. The floors were wooden and the elevators were slow. Workers ate lunch on the steps in good weather. Across Elm Street stood another old building, called the Dal-Tex, full of small offices. Nobody gave either building a second thought. They were part of the scenery, like the pigeons and the colonnades.

[long-pause]

{img:29-dressmaker-office-1963-man-glasses}

The man at the desk was called Mr. Z, and his employees were very fond of him. His real name was Abraham Zapruder. In 1963, he was fifty-eight years old, short and round, with a bald head, thick glasses and a warm, gentle smile. He ran a small dress company called Jennifer Juniors, on the fourth floor of the Dal-Tex Building, overlooking Elm Street and Dealey Plaza. About twenty people worked for him. They were designers, seamstresses, clerks and salesmen, and they called him Mr. Z. He arrived at the office at about nine every morning and said good morning to everyone. Then he went to his little office to look at the new designs. He talked to buyers on the telephone. He walked through the workroom and stopped to chat with the seamstresses, and he asked after their families. He was not a man who raised his voice. People who worked for him remembered that he treated them as if they were part of one big family. That was how he preferred to run a business.

[long-pause]

{img:30-immigrant-family-new-york-1920}

Abraham Zapruder had arrived in America as a teenager, on a crowded ship, with almost nothing. Abraham Zapruder was born in 1905 in a small town in the Russian Empire, now part of Ukraine. His family was Jewish. In 1920, when he was fifteen, they emigrated to the United States and settled in Brooklyn, New York. He began working in the clothing industry as a pattern maker. Pattern makers cut the paper shapes from which garments are sewn. He attended evening classes to learn English. Over the years, he mastered every stage of the business, from design to finished garment. Later, he moved to Dallas and helped found a dress company called Jennifer Juniors. His story was typical of millions of immigrants in the early twentieth century. Many arrived with little money and built steady lives through hard work in industries such as clothing manufacturing. Most remained unknown outside their own families. Zapruder's name became famous only because of a few seconds of home movie film he shot in November 1963.

[long-pause]

{img:31-dallas-dress-company-sign-1941}

In 1941, Abraham Zapruder moved his family to Dallas for the weather and the opportunities. With his wife Lillian and their two young children, he left the cold winters of the north and moved to Texas. He had been offered a job with a dress company there. He liked Dallas immediately. He liked the warm weather, the friendly people and the feeling of a city that was growing. After some years, he started his own company and named it Jennifer Juniors. It designed and made dresses for young women and girls. They were pretty, inexpensive dresses with full skirts and small collars, sold in department stores across the country. It was a small business, but a successful one. For years, Mr. Zapruder had spent his days among bolts of cloth and paper patterns. He had a good eye for what young girls wanted to wear. And he had a good heart, which was perhaps more important to the people who knew him.

[long-pause]

{img:32-grandfather-grandchildren-garden-sunday}

Abraham Zapruder was a devoted family man who loved filming his grandchildren. Abraham Zapruder and his wife Lillian lived in a comfortable home in a quiet Dallas neighbourhood. Their son Henry was a lawyer in Washington. Their daughter Myrna was married with children of her own. Zapruder was devoted to his grandchildren. Family gatherings were frequent, especially on Sundays. He enjoyed spending time with the children in the garden, showing them the flowers he grew. He was also an enthusiastic amateur filmmaker. He owned a high-quality Bell and Howell 8-millimetre home movie camera and liked recording family events. Home movie cameras had become popular in middle-class American households during the 1950s. Families filmed birthdays, holidays and children's first steps. Most of these films were watched only by relatives. Zapruder had no interest in fame. His family later described him as modest and private. That personal background helps explain why the events of November 1963 affected him so deeply for the rest of his life.

[long-pause]

{img:33-vintage-8mm-home-movie-camera-hand}

The motorcade halted for a moment. The President leaned out of the car and shook the children's hands while they jumped up and down with excitement. It was a very small thing. But for those children, it was a memory that lasted all their lives. The sign they had painted was crooked, and the letters were uneven, and it was perfect. It showed how simple the afternoon felt to the people who had come out to see it.

[long-pause]

{img:67-convertible-tree-lined-avenue-crowds-waving}

Mrs. Kennedy wanted to wear her sunglasses, but the President asked her to leave them off. Then the cars drove on toward the city, along a broad street lined with trees, houses and small apartment buildings. The crowds grew larger and larger. In the back seat, the President waved and smiled. Mrs. Kennedy waved too, her white-gloved hand raised, holding her pink hat in place now and then against the breeze. The sun was very bright, and she would have liked to put on her sunglasses. But the President asked her not to, so that the people could see her face. In the seat in front of them, Nellie Connally turned around now and then to speak to them, smiling, delighted at the size of the crowds. It was a happy, easy ride. Ahead, the tall buildings of downtown Dallas were drawing nearer, shining in the midday sun. Everyone in the car felt relieved. Some of the worries that had followed them for weeks had quietly disappeared.

[long-pause]

{img:68-downtown-main-street-crowds-confetti}

Paper fell from the office windows like snow, and the crowds were twenty deep. At about twenty past twelve, the motorcade turned onto Main Street and entered downtown Dallas. The crowds here were enormous. People stood ten and twenty deep along the pavements, on both sides of the street. They leaned out of every window of the tall office buildings. They stood on the roofs, on the ledges, on the steps of banks and hotels, on top of parked cars and mailboxes. As the President's car came into view, a great roar went up and rolled down the street like a wave. From the windows high above, office workers threw down torn-up sheets of paper. It fluttered down through the sunshine like snow and settled on the cars and the people below. The President waved and smiled. Mrs. Kennedy waved and smiled. The noise was tremendous. For a moment, downtown felt like one great festival, with the whole city standing on its feet.

[long-pause]

{img:69-father-child-on-shoulders-waving-flag}

Many people on Main Street saw a President for the only time in their lives. A young secretary, leaning out of an office window, saw him pass right below her. She waved so hard that she nearly dropped her handbag. A businessman standing on the steps of his bank took off his hat. A small boy on his father's shoulders waved a little flag. An old woman who remembered seeing President Roosevelt pass along this same street many years earlier wiped her eyes with her handkerchief. The motorcade moved slowly, at about eleven miles an hour, so that everyone could get a good look. It took roughly ten minutes to drive the whole length of Main Street. Photographers ran alongside. Shop assistants waved handkerchiefs. A man in a bus shelter held a transistor radio to his ear. It was one of the few times when a large city felt entirely united. Whatever people had argued about before, they were all looking the same way.

[long-pause]

{img:70-car-interior-governor-wife-smiling-back-seat}

Nellie Connally turned around and said, "Mr. President, you can't say Dallas doesn't love you." As the motorcade passed through downtown, the crowds were larger and friendlier than many had expected. Governor John Connally had worried about the reception, because Dallas had a reputation for political hostility toward Kennedy. Yet the streets were filled with cheering people. In the presidential car, Nellie Connally turned around to speak to the President. She told him that he could not say Dallas did not love him. According to her later accounts, Kennedy replied that no, you certainly could not. It was one of the last conversations in the car before the shooting. Nellie Connally repeated this story many times in later years. She wanted it known that Dallas had welcomed the President warmly that day. Historians have confirmed that large and enthusiastic crowds lined the route through downtown. Their presence contrasts sharply with the image of Dallas that developed after the assassination. The city struggled with that image for decades.

[long-pause]

{img:71-johnson-car-roses-lady-bird-crowd}

In the car behind, Lady Bird Johnson held a bouquet of yellow roses on her lap. Behind the President's car, in an open convertible, rode the Vice President, Lyndon Johnson, and Lady Bird, smiling and waving to the people on the pavements. Senator Ralph Yarborough rode with them, the same senator who had been quarrelling with the Governor. Lady Bird held a bouquet of yellow roses on her lap. Behind them came the cars of the congressmen and the officials, and then the buses of reporters, who leaned out with their cameras to capture the scene. Further back still came the local politicians and guests of honour, and at the very end, an ambulance. Lyndon Johnson was a tall man, well over six feet, with big hands and a deep Texas drawl, and he was waving to his fellow Texans. He loved crowds as much as Kennedy did. Today he was a Vice President, enjoying a fine day in his home state, and he had no reason to think of anything beyond it.

[long-pause]

{img:72-houston-street-corner-red-brick-building}

At the western end of Main Street, the tall buildings ended, and a green park began. At the western end of Main Street, the tall buildings gave way to Dealey Plaza. The motorcade turned right onto Houston Street and moved slowly along one short block. Ahead stood the red brick Texas School Book Depository. Crowds here were thinner and more scattered than downtown. People stood in small groups along the kerb, on the grassy areas of the plaza and on building steps. Some employees of the Book Depository had come outside to watch. The motorcade then made a sharp left turn onto Elm Street, slowing almost to a stop. This turn later became one of the most discussed details of the day. At twelve thirty, shots were fired at the President's car from an upper window of the Book Depository. President Kennedy was fatally wounded. Governor Connally was seriously injured but survived. The calm autumn scene in Dealey Plaza changed in a matter of seconds. The moment would be remembered by millions for the rest of their lives.

[long-pause]

{img:73-young-family-standing-on-grass-park}

On the grass beside the road, a young family and a dressmaker with a camera were waiting. In Dealey Plaza, the Newman family was waiting. Bill and Gayle had found a good place to stand, on the grass on the north side of Elm Street, a little way down the slope from the Book Depository. The boys were restless after waiting so long, but excited. Billy stood holding his father's hand. Clayton was in his mother's arms. Up on the slope, a little higher, near a white concrete colonnade, Abraham Zapruder stood on a low concrete wall with his camera. He had come down from his office with his receptionist, Marilyn Sitzman. He chose this spot because it gave him a good view of the whole curve of Elm Street. Because he suffered from dizziness, Marilyn stood behind him, holding onto his coat to keep him steady. He lifted the little gold camera to his eye. And he waited, as everybody else was waiting, for the sound of cheering to come closer.

[long-pause]

{img:74-trade-mart-lunch-tables-waiting-guests}

Two thousand people sat at set tables, waiting for lunch with the President. At the end of the route, a big hall called the Trade Mart was waiting. It was a large modern building, where merchants showed their goods to buyers. For the lunch that day, long rows of round tables had been set with white cloths, small flower arrangements and the best china. About two thousand guests had arrived in their finest clothes. They were the business leaders, the civic leaders and the wives of many of them. Waiters stood ready with trays. A chef had planned the menu for days. A band played softly in a corner. On the stage stood a speaker's table with a microphone, and the President's speech was already waiting. Guests glanced at their watches and chatted about how well the parade must be going. Some had heard the cheering on the radio. Everything was ready, down to the last napkin. It was a lunch that everybody had looked forward to, and that nobody would ever forget.

[long-pause]

{img:75-elm-street-curve-sunlight-trees-quiet}

Within a few seconds, a happy parade turned into the saddest hour in the history of the city. It was a little after half past twelve. The long blue car turned the corner and moved slowly down the gentle slope of Elm Street. Then something happened that no one on those lawns had ever imagined. Some people thought they heard a firecracker. Some thought a motorcycle had backfired. In a few seconds, the President was gravely hurt, and Governor Connally was wounded too. A Secret Service agent ran forward and climbed onto the back of the car. The car sped away, down Elm Street, under the railway bridge, and out of sight. The whole thing took only a few seconds. On the grass, Bill Newman called to his wife to get down, and the two of them covered their little boys with their own bodies and held them close. On the wall above, Abraham Zapruder kept his camera running. There is no need to dwell on those seconds tonight. What matters is how people behaved afterwards, and how gentle many of them were.

[long-pause]

{img:76-hospital-entrance-doctors-nurses-afternoon}

The doctors at Parkland Hospital did everything they could, but the President died at one o'clock. The car raced to Parkland Memorial Hospital, a few miles away, arriving at about twelve thirty-eight. Doctors and nurses ran out with stretchers. The President and the Governor were carried inside, into two separate rooms. Mrs. Kennedy stayed beside her husband. She would not leave. Governor Connally was seriously wounded, but he would recover. The doctors worked on the President for nearly twenty minutes, quickly and calmly, but most of them knew from the first moment that there was very little they could do. A priest came and gave him the last rites, while Mrs. Kennedy knelt and prayed. At one o'clock in the afternoon, the doctors stopped. President John Fitzgerald Kennedy had died. He was forty-six years old. Quietly, Mrs. Kennedy took off her wedding ring and slipped it onto her husband's finger. Then she stood very straight and still, in the pink suit, in the corridor.

[long-pause]

{img:77-newsroom-anchor-glasses-clock-1963}

The most trusted voice on television took off his glasses and had to pause before he could speak. In a classroom at the hospital, an assistant press secretary stood before a crowd of reporters. He was pale and his voice shook. He read a short statement saying the President had died at about one o'clock. The reporters rushed to the telephones. In New York, Walter Cronkite, who had been reading bulletins as they came in, took a sheet of paper from a colleague, read it, and took off his glasses. He looked at the clock on the wall. Then he told the country that the President had died. He put his glasses back on, and had to pause for a moment to steady his voice. All across America, people heard the news. Teachers told their students, and some of them wept, and the children had never seen a teacher cry before. Schools sent everyone home. In offices and shops, people stood in silent groups around radios. Church bells began to ring in many towns.

[long-pause]

{img:78-air-force-one-cabin-oath-ceremony}

The new President took his oath of office inside a hot, crowded aeroplane cabin. At Love Field, Air Force One stood on the tarmac in the afternoon sun, its door open. The crowd that had cheered there at lunchtime had gone. Lyndon Johnson decided to take the oath of office in Dallas before they flew, so that the whole world would know that the government of the United States went on without a pause. A federal judge from Dallas, Sarah Hughes, an old friend, was driven to the airport as fast as possible. She was sixty-seven years old, small and energetic, one of the first women ever to become a federal judge in America. About twenty-seven people crowded into the cabin. Mrs. Kennedy came in quietly, still in her pink suit. Someone had offered her a fresh dress, but she had said no. At two thirty-eight, Lyndon Johnson raised his right hand and repeated the oath. A photographer standing on a seat took a picture. It remains the only photograph of a President sworn in on an aeroplane.

[long-pause]

{img:79-air-force-one-sunset-flight-east}

Under the evening sky, the plane flew east, carrying a grieving widow and a new President. A few minutes later, the door closed, the engines started, and Air Force One taxied out and took off into the late afternoon sky, heading east toward Washington. Behind it, Dallas grew small and disappeared. Far below, the land of Texas slid past in the late light: the long brown plains, the small towns, the roads running straight to the horizon, the stock ponds glinting like coins in the fields. Then came the forests and rivers of Louisiana and Mississippi and Tennessee, and the long blue line of the Appalachian Mountains. In the front of the plane, the new President made telephone calls. He called President Kennedy's mother, Rose, in Massachusetts, to tell her how sorry he was. In the back, Mrs. Kennedy sat beside the coffin with a few of her husband's oldest friends. They talked quietly about him. Sometimes they were silent. At about six, the plane landed at Andrews in the darkness.

[long-pause]

{img:80-dealey-plaza-flowers-night-street-lamps}

That night, people began to leave flowers on the grass, one bouquet at a time. Back in Dallas, night had fallen. In Dealey Plaza, people began to come quietly, one by one and in small groups, to stand on the grass beside Elm Street. Some brought flowers and laid them down on the lawn. Some simply stood, with their heads bowed. The street lamps shone on the curving road, on the white colonnades and on the growing heap of flowers on the grass. Above them, the dark windows of the Book Depository looked down over the plaza. And beyond the railway bridge, the traffic went slowly by on the freeway, with its headlights shining in the dark. During the weekend, the flowers grew into a great bank of colour, with wreaths and single roses and small cards tucked among them. Across the city, the churches were full on Sunday. People came to pray, to sing hymns, and to sit quietly together. Ministers spoke about kindness.

[long-pause]

{img:81-television-shop-window-crowd-watching}

For three days, the whole country stopped and watched the same pictures in silence. The next day, Saturday, it rained, in Dallas and in Washington. The whole country seemed to stop. Shops were mostly closed. The cinemas were dark. The television stations cancelled all their usual programmes and showed nothing but the news, hour after hour, along with quiet music and church services. In the Newman house, the boys played in the living room, as usual, and the television was on. Bill and Gayle had hardly slept. They sat together on the sofa holding hands, and every now and then one of them got up to look at the boys, just to see that they were all right. Abraham Zapruder also had a sleepless night. On Saturday, he sold his film to a magazine, on the condition that it would be treated with respect. And he gave a large part of the money to the family of a police officer who had lost his life that weekend. It was the kind of quiet generosity that history seldom notices.

[long-pause]

{img:82-funeral-washington-horses-caisson-crowds}

A little boy turned three on the day of his father's funeral, and he raised his hand in a salute. On Monday, the twenty-fifth of November, the sky over Washington was cold, bright and clear. Schools, offices and shops were closed all over the country. Almost everyone in America was watching on television. In the morning, the coffin was carried on a gun carriage drawn by six grey horses. Behind it, a soldier led a riderless black horse named Black Jack, with an empty saddle and a pair of boots turned backwards in the stirrups. Kings, presidents and prime ministers walked through the streets. Outside the cathedral, John Kennedy Junior, who had turned three that very day, stood beside his mother. As the coffin passed, the little boy stepped forward and raised his right hand to his forehead. The photograph of that moment was seen all over the world. Then the long procession went to Arlington, on the green hills of Virginia. Mrs. Kennedy lit a small flame at the head of the grave. It is burning still.

[long-pause]

{img:83-government-hearing-room-1964-men-in-suits}

A week after the funeral, the new President asked the Chief Justice to find out everything he could. President Johnson asked the Chief Justice of the United States, Earl Warren, to lead a commission to find out all that could be found out about what had happened in Dallas. For ten months, the Warren Commission worked. Its members and lawyers questioned more than five hundred people. They studied photographs and films, among them the film of Abraham Zapruder, frame by frame. They visited Dealey Plaza. In September 1964, the Commission published its report, a book of more than eight hundred pages, with twenty-six more volumes of evidence. It concluded that Lee Harvey Oswald, a young man who worked at the Book Depository, had acted alone, as had the man who later harmed him. Many people accepted these findings. Many others did not. Over the years that followed, there have been books, investigations and arguments. Some of those questions are discussed still.

[long-pause]

{img:84-abraham-zapruder-older-man-garden-quiet}

The dressmaker never wanted to use his camera again, and his film now rests in a cool, dark vault. Abraham Zapruder went back to his dress business, his family and his grandchildren. He was a gentle man, and the events of that day stayed with him always. He gave testimony to the Warren Commission, and he wept as he described what he had seen. He said that he never wanted to use his camera again, and that he stopped filming his family. He lived until 1970. Today, the original film is kept in the National Archives, in a cool dark vault, where it is protected. The Newman family went home and raised their boys. Bill and Gayle never forgot the moment they lay on the grass to shelter their children. For the rest of their lives, people asked them about it, and they told the story quietly and simply, as it had happened. Billy and Clayton grew up and had children of their own, and later grandchildren. Ordinary people had become witnesses without ever asking.

[long-pause]

{img:85-jackie-kennedy-new-york-library-quiet}

Jackie called the Kennedy years Camelot, after a musical her husband loved to play at night. Mrs. Kennedy moved out of the White House a few weeks after the funeral, with Caroline and John. A few days after the funeral, she gave an interview to a writer named Theodore White. She spoke about her husband and about a musical that he had loved, called Camelot, about the legend of King Arthur and his round table. He liked to listen to the record at night, before he went to sleep, she said, especially the last lines of the title song, about one brief shining moment. After that, people began to speak of the Kennedy years as Camelot. She later moved to New York, where she raised her children, worked as a book editor, and lived quietly, away from politics, until she passed away in 1994. She is buried beside President Kennedy at Arlington, under the eternal flame. The children grew up, and Caroline later served as an ambassador to Japan and to Australia.

[long-pause]

{img:86-apollo-moon-landing-astronaut-flag-1969}

Six years later, as the dead President had promised, two Americans walked on the moon. President Lyndon Johnson served out the rest of Kennedy's term, and in 1964 he was elected in his own right by a very large majority. In 1964 and 1965, he worked with Congress to pass the Civil Rights Act and the Voting Rights Act, which Kennedy had begun, and which changed the lives of millions of Americans. Governor Connally recovered from his wounds and went back to his work as Governor of Texas. He served for many more years in public life. And in July 1969, as the President had promised in 1961, two American astronauts walked on the surface of the moon and came safely home. The promise of a few words in a speech had turned into a rocket, a landing and a footprint. It is a quiet reminder that ideas can outlive the people who speak them. Even in a story with a great sorrow in it, there can be a small light.

[long-pause]

{img:87-kennedy-memorial-dallas-white-walls-sky}

One block from the plaza, Dallas built a quiet, roofless room with only the sky above it. For the city of Dallas, the years after 1963 were difficult. For a long time, people elsewhere thought of it only as the city where the President was killed. Many people in Dallas felt a deep sadness about it, and some felt ashamed, though they had done nothing wrong, and though most had stood on the pavements that day, waving and cheering. Slowly, the city came to terms with what had happened. It grew, and changed, and became one of the largest cities in America. And in 1970, it built a memorial one block east of Dealey Plaza. It was designed by the architect Philip Johnson, and it is very simple. It is a square of tall white concrete walls, open to the sky, with narrow openings. Inside, in the middle, lies a low dark slab of granite with only the President's name on it, in gold letters. It is meant to be a calm place, away from the noise of the city.

[long-pause]

{img:88-sixth-floor-museum-quiet-window-boxes}

The old school book warehouse is now a museum, and Dealey Plaza is a protected landmark. Dealey Plaza is still there today, at the western edge of downtown Dallas, and it looks very much as it did in 1963. The three streets still curve down through the little park, past the green lawns and the trees and under the old railway bridge. The white concrete colonnades still stand on either side. In 1993, the whole plaza, with the buildings around it, was made a National Historic Landmark, so that it would stay the way it was and be remembered. The old Book Depository still stands on the corner. The book company moved out many years ago, and most of the building is now used as county offices. But the sixth floor has been turned into a museum, which opened in 1989. It tells the story of the President's life, his visit to Dallas, and everything that came after. Visitors walk slowly through quiet rooms, past photographs and films and the voices of people remembering.

[long-pause]

{img:89-old-red-courthouse-log-cabin-park}

Near the plaza stands an old red courthouse, a museum that tells the whole story of the city. Not far from Dealey Plaza, in the middle of downtown, lies a little park. In it stands the copy of John Neely Bryan's log cabin, small and dark and dwarfed by the towers around it. Beside it rises an old county courthouse of red stone, built in the 1890s, with turrets and arched windows. People call it Old Red. Today it is a museum of the history of Dallas. It tells the whole story of the city, from the little cabin on the riverbank to the oil boom, the tall towers, the long autumn of 1963 and the years that followed. On a sunny afternoon, families walk through its rooms. Children press buttons and listen to the voices of old Texans telling stories. They look at photographs of the city as it used to be. The courthouse has stood through heat, hail and change, and it still seems to be keeping watch over the city's memory.

[long-pause]

{img:90-dallas-skyline-dusk-lights-neon-horse}

The red winged horse still glows above the streets of Dallas today. Each year on the twenty-second of November, people gather in Dealey Plaza at twelve thirty for a moment of remembrance. Some pray quietly, while others simply stand in silence. Then traffic resumes along Elm Street. On the fiftieth anniversary in 2013, thousands attended despite cold drizzle, and church bells rang across the city. The former Book Depository now houses The Sixth Floor Museum, which explains the events of that day. Dallas has grown into one of the largest metropolitan areas in the United States. Its skyline is filled with modern towers glowing blue, green and gold at sunset. Yet many older landmarks remain. The Magnolia Building still stands downtown. The original Pegasus sign was replaced with a careful replica in 1999, and the old horse has since been restored as well. Every night, the red winged horse shines above the city once more. It links modern Dallas with the city of 1963.
