{img:01-pegasus-neon-horse-tower-dawn}

High above the streets of Dallas, on the roof of an old office tower, a red horse with wings turned slowly in the dark. It was made of glass tubes filled with glowing gas. Its wings flickered as it turned, and drivers saw it from miles away across the flat Texas land. For almost thirty years, it had told them the same thing. You are nearly home.

[pause]

In the autumn of 1963, that horse looked down on one of the fastest growing cities in America. Wide new streets. Brand new houses. Shopping carts, television sets and football on Friday nights. Yet most of us only know this city from a single hour of a single day. The people who lived there knew it as breakfast tables, school buses and busy shop windows. So what was life really like in Dallas in 1963? Come along, and we'll walk through those days one by one.

[long-pause]

The most famous sign in Dallas was a red horse with wings, and it sat on top of an oil company.

[pause]

The horse is called Pegasus. It stood on the roof of the Magnolia Building, which was the headquarters of an oil company. The building went up in the early 1920s, and for a while it was the tallest in Texas. The neon horse arrived in 1934. It was huge, and it turned slowly on a steel frame, glowing red against the sky. On a clear night, you could see it from far out on the prairie. Drivers coming in along the highways looked for it the way sailors look for a lighthouse. By 1963, taller towers stood all around it. They were made of glass, steel and polished stone, and they carried the names of banks and insurance firms. Yet the old horse still held its place. Children counted on it. Grown men who had moved away in the war years told their friends about it. Nobody had planned for a flying horse to become a symbol. It simply stayed there, night after night, until it felt like part of the family.

[long-pause]

{img:02-love-field-jet-airliner-terminal-1963}

At the city airport, the new jet airliners made New York feel like a short afternoon away.

[pause]

The airport was called Love Field. It had been named after an army pilot of the early days of flying, and by 1963 it was one of the busiest in the South. The new jet airliners landed there, one after another, with a long whistle that made people on the ground look up. A few years earlier, a trip to New York or Los Angeles had taken a whole day by propeller plane, with stops along the way. Now it could take just a few hours. Passengers dressed up to fly. Men wore suits and ties, and women wore gloves and hats, and stewardesses served meals on real china. Families came to the airport on a Sunday afternoon just to watch the planes. They stood at the fence with ice cream cones and counted the landings. Flying still felt like a small miracle. Dallas was proud to have the runways, and proud to be a place that people flew to.

[long-pause]

{img:03-new-suburb-street-ranch-houses}

Dallas was so new that many of its streets had been open fields only a few years before.

[pause]

In 1963, nearly seven hundred thousand people lived in Dallas, and more arrived every year. The city spread out across the prairie in every direction. New neighbourhoods appeared with long, straight streets and houses of brick or pale wood. Each house had a lawn, a driveway and a thin young tree held up by a stake. You could drive for ten minutes and see nothing but fresh paint. Builders worked from sunrise to sunset. New schools, churches and shopping centres opened almost every month. Families came from smaller towns across Texas and Oklahoma, hoping for steady work and a bigger life. Many of them had never lived in a city before. They learned its streets together, and they learned who lived next door. A young father might say, with some pride, that his house had been built the same spring as his son. The city felt like a place where you could start again.

[long-pause]

{img:04-oil-wells-east-texas-1930s}

Dallas has no oil wells of its own, yet oil made it rich.

[pause]

The great oil field was discovered in East Texas in 1930, about a hundred miles away. A wildcatter drilled a well that most people thought was foolish, and it came in. Soon thousands of wells were pumping. The oil came out of the ground in the countryside. But the money, the paperwork and the planning all came to Dallas. Oil companies opened offices downtown. The banks that lent them money moved in next door. Then came the businesses that sold pipes, trucks and machinery, and the lawyers who wrote their contracts. Hotels and restaurants followed, for all the people who worked in them. By 1963, a lunch table in a Dallas restaurant could hold a deal worth more than a whole farm. Men leaned over their steaks and talked about land, leases and the price of a barrel. Yet most families in the city never saw an oil well. They simply lived in a town that oil had built.

[long-pause]

{img:05-electronics-lab-engineer-1958}

In a quiet laboratory in Dallas, one tall engineer invented the tiny chip that now sits inside every phone.

[pause]

Not far from downtown stood the offices of a company called Texas Instruments. It made small electronic parts for radios, for computers and for rockets. In the summer of 1958, a new engineer named Jack Kilby sat there almost alone. Most of his colleagues had gone on holiday. He was tall, quiet and thoughtful, and he had a problem to solve. Electronic circuits needed many separate parts, joined by many tiny wires. What if all the parts could be made from the same piece of material? That September, he showed his bosses a little sliver of germanium with a simple circuit built inside. It looked very plain. But it worked. It was called the integrated circuit. In 1963, almost nobody outside the industry knew what it meant. People talked about oil, cattle and football. Yet this small chip would go on to change nearly everything in daily life. Would you have guessed it, looking at that sliver?

[long-pause]

{img:06-state-fair-big-tex-families}

Every October, a giant cowboy at the State Fair said howdy to everyone who walked in.

[pause]

The State Fair of Texas took place each autumn in a large park east of downtown. It had a Ferris wheel, a midway, prize cattle, pigs and quilts. And it had Big Tex, a huge cowboy figure that stood at the gate. He wore an enormous hat and boots, and he was taller than a house. A hidden voice boomed out of him, greeting every family with a friendly howdy. Children stared up at him with their mouths open. The fair also gave the world a favourite snack. Sausages dipped in cornmeal batter and fried on a stick had been sold there since the early 1940s. People called them corny dogs. In the evening, the lights came on across the fairground, and the smell of hot oil and sugar drifted through the crowd. For many Dallas families, the fair was the highlight of the year. It felt like proof that this big city still had a small-town heart.

[long-pause]

{img:07-cotton-bowl-stadium-crowd-october}

Once a year, the whole fairground turned into a football battlefield between Texas and Oklahoma.

[pause]

Right beside the State Fair stood a great stadium called the Cotton Bowl. Every October, during fair week, two famous college teams met there. They came from Texas and from Oklahoma, and the fans were fiercely loyal. For one Saturday, the stadium split down the middle. One half wore burnt orange and the other wore crimson. Fathers and sons wore hats. Mothers carried blankets and flasks of coffee. The crowd roared so loudly that you could hear it from the midway outside. Afterwards, people spilled out into the fair, still arguing over every play. The game had been played in Dallas since the 1920s, and in 1963, it was already an old tradition. Not long before, in 1960, the city had also welcomed a brand new professional team called the Dallas Cowboys. Football in Texas was never only a game. It was a way of belonging, and a reason to put on your best jacket.

[long-pause]

{img:08-trinity-river-levees-cottonwoods}

The whole city began beside a slow brown river that most visitors never noticed.

[pause]

Dallas grew up on the banks of the Trinity River. It is not a grand river. For most of the year, it moves slowly between muddy banks, lined with cottonwoods and willows, with herons standing in the shallows. In 1841, a young lawyer and trader named John Neely Bryan built a log cabin there. It stood at a place where travellers could cross, and soon others settled near him. Farmers brought cotton to sell. Then came the railways, which turned a small market town into a real city. The river could also be dangerous. After a bad flood in 1908, the people of Dallas built tall grassy levees to hold the water back. By 1963, walkers strolled along them at the weekend. A copy of Bryan's cabin stood in the middle of downtown, small and dark among the towers. It was there to remind busy people where it all began.

[long-pause]

{img:09-texas-storm-clouds-prairie-evening}

In Dallas, the weather could be so big that people planned their whole year around it.

[pause]

Summers were very hot. Often the thermometer rose well above a hundred degrees, and the air shimmered above the pavement. People spent the afternoons indoors, in rooms cooled by new window air conditioners humming in the wall. Spring was different. Great thunderstorms rolled across the plains. They brought lightning, hail as big as marbles and sometimes tornadoes. Families kept an eye on the sky, and a few kept a storm cellar. But then came the autumn. The days were warm and bright. The sky turned a deep, clear blue. The evenings were cool enough for a light jacket. Many people said it was the best season in Texas. School started, football began, and the fair filled the park. Yet even in autumn, the weather could change within an hour. It might be sunny in the morning and drizzling by noon. Have you ever lived somewhere where you wore a different coat in the afternoon?

[long-pause]

{img:10-dallas-downtown-autumn-afternoon-traffic}

By late 1963, Dallas felt like a city that believed it was only going up.

[pause]

There was a quiet confidence in the air. Office workers poured into downtown each morning in suits and smart dresses, by car or by bus. Children went to school. Housewives shopped, cooked and cleaned. Oilmen made deals over lunch, and shopkeepers began to think about Christmas, which was only a month away. People called their city Big D, and they said it with pride. They were proud of the tall buildings, the fine stores, the football and the fair. They were proud of the new airport, where jet airliners could carry you to New York or Los Angeles in a few hours. Of course, not everyone shared in that comfort equally. Black families in Dallas still lived by the rules of segregation, and their share of the city's good fortune was much smaller. Yet most people felt that the next year would be better than the last. It was an ordinary autumn in Texas, and for now, that was enough.

[long-pause]

{img:11-suburban-kitchen-1963-breakfast}

The neon horse fades in the light of the rising sun. Down in one of the new neighbourhoods, a kitchen light is already on.

[pause]

A typical Dallas family began its day to the sound of a coffee percolator bubbling.

[pause]

The kitchen was bright, and probably painted a cheerful pale yellow. A big white refrigerator hummed in the corner. A gas stove clicked and glowed. On the counter stood a toaster and a glass coffee percolator, which popped and gurgled as it brewed. On the windowsill, a small radio played the weather, the news and a song or two. The mother stood at the stove in a cotton dress and an apron, frying eggs and bacon. Her hair had been neatly set the night before. The father sat at the table in a white shirt and narrow tie, reading the morning paper. In a few minutes, he would drive to work along the new freeway. The children came in, dressed for school, and sat down to cereal and orange juice. A family like this did not think of itself as special. Yet this is exactly the kind of morning that history tends to forget, and which is worth remembering.

[long-pause]

{img:12-kids-walking-to-school-sidewalk}

Most children walked to school together, with their books held by a leather strap.

[pause]

There were no backpacks yet. Children carried their books fastened together with a strap, and swung their lunch boxes at their sides. Other children joined them from houses along the way, until the group became a small, chattering crowd. They passed lawns, driveways and parked cars with big round fenders. The school was usually a long, low building of pale brick with large windows. Inside, the wooden desks stood in neat rows. Each morning, the children stood beside their desks, put their hands on their hearts and said the Pledge of Allegiance to the flag. Then came reading, arithmetic, spelling and a bit of Texas history. Teachers had strict rules, and children were expected to sit quietly and raise a hand before speaking. At lunchtime, they traded sandwiches and swapped baseball cards. In the afternoon, a bell rang, and the whole building emptied in minutes. Do you remember a school bell from your own childhood?

[long-pause]

{img:13-housewife-clothesline-backyard-texas-sun}

Washing machines were common, but most clothes still dried on a line in the sun.

[pause]

Once the house was empty, the mother began her work. She did the washing in an electric machine in the kitchen or a small room off the garage. Then she carried the wet clothes out into the back yard and pegged them to a long line. In the Texas sun, sheets dried in less than an hour, and they smelled of warm air. She vacuumed the carpets, dusted the furniture and made every bed. She wrote out a list for the grocery shop and planned the evening meal. In the middle of the morning, she often had coffee with a neighbour. They sat at the kitchen table and talked about the children, the school, the church bake sale and the new dresses in the downtown stores. Housework was long, and much of it was lonely. Yet it was also the centre of a whole neighbourhood, a web of small favours and borrowed cups of sugar that held a street together.

[long-pause]

{img:14-supermarket-aisle-1963-shopping-cart}

The supermarket was so new that some shoppers still felt a little amazed by it.

[pause]

In the afternoon, the mother drove to the supermarket. It was huge and very bright, with long aisles of shelves and colourful boxes. There were cake mixes, instant coffee and frozen dinners that you could heat in the oven in an aluminium tray. A few years earlier, almost none of this had existed. There were oranges from California, bananas from Central America, and a long glass counter filled with fresh meat. She pushed her wire cart slowly up and down the aisles, choosing with care. At the checkout, a young woman typed in the prices on a big metal cash register, while a boy in a white apron packed everything into brown paper bags. Then he carried them out to the car. A week's shopping for a family might cost around twenty dollars. Prices were small, but so were wages. Most families watched every cent, and they knew exactly what a loaf of bread cost.

[long-pause]

{img:15-ice-cream-truck-children-street-1963}

When the ice cream truck played its tune, every child on the street dropped everything.

[pause]

By four o'clock, the children were home from school, and the street filled with noise. Boys rode bicycles up and down the pavement. Others played catch in the front yards, or gathered in a garage to inspect a new baseball glove. Girls drew hopscotch squares on the driveway, or skipped rope, or sat on the front steps with their dolls. Dogs ran barking after the bicycles. On some afternoons, a little truck turned the corner, playing a tinkling tune. Every child within hearing came running, holding out a nickel or a dime. They stood in a line on the hot kerb, deciding between a frozen bar and a cone. There was no hurry. Parents did not watch every minute, because the whole neighbourhood watched. Someone's mother called from a doorway when dinner was nearly ready. It was a simple kind of freedom, and many people remember it with a particular, sweet longing.

[long-pause]

{img:16-family-dinner-table-fried-chicken-iced-tea}

In Texas, everybody drank iced tea, all year round, with plenty of ice.

[pause]

In the evening, the father came home, and the family sat down to dinner together. The table was set with plain plates and napkins. The meal might be meatloaf, fried chicken or a pot roast with potatoes and carrots. There were green beans, soft rolls with butter, and always a tall glass of iced tea. Dessert could be pecan pie or a slice of chocolate cake. Dinner was the one time when everyone was in the same room. The children told what happened at school. The father described his day at the office. The mother passed on the news from the neighbourhood. If someone was late, the food waited under a cloth. Before eating, many families bowed their heads and said a short prayer. Nobody picked up a telephone, because the telephone was fixed to the kitchen wall and the cord only reached so far. Perhaps the evening was slower for that reason.

[long-pause]

{img:17-family-watching-black-white-tv}

In 1963, most families watched the same three channels, and the evening news was only half an hour long.

[pause]

After dinner, the family moved into the living room. The television was a big wooden cabinet with a small grey screen, and the picture was black and white. There were only three main networks. Evenings brought westerns, comedy shows, quiz shows and variety hours with singers and dancers. The children lay on the carpet, propped up on their elbows. The parents sat on the sofa. The dog slept near the door. At suppertime or just after, a calm man in a dark suit read the evening news. Only a few weeks earlier, in September 1963, the CBS news had grown from fifteen minutes to a full half hour, the first on American television to do so. The anchor was a steady, friendly reporter named Walter Cronkite. Millions of people let him into their living rooms each night. Soon they would trust him with the hardest news of their lives. But on this evening, the weather was the main story.

[long-pause]

{img:18-friday-night-football-stadium-lights}

On Friday nights in autumn, the whole town seemed to move to the football stadium.

[pause]

In Texas, high school football was nearly a religion. On Friday nights, families put on jackets and drove to the stadium under the bright lights. The marching band played in shining uniforms. Cheerleaders shouted from the sideline. Boys sold popcorn and hot dogs in the stands, and the smell of fried onions drifted along the benches. The whole town came: grandparents, neighbours, little brothers and sisters who didn't care about the score. The players, who were only seventeen or eighteen, felt like heroes. On Saturdays, families shopped, worked in the garden or drove out to a lake for a picnic. On Sundays, they went to church in their best clothes, and ate a big dinner afterwards. Then came a nap in the afternoon. The week had a shape, and everybody knew it. Perhaps that is why a normal week felt safe. You always knew what was coming next, and you rarely worried about it.

[long-pause]

{img:19-corner-drugstore-soda-fountain-boys}

On Saturday afternoons, a nickel and a bicycle could take a boy straight to the soda fountain.

[pause]

On Saturdays, many boys and girls rode their bicycles to the corner drugstore. At the back, there was a long counter with round stools and a shining soda fountain. A young man in a white paper hat stood behind it and made milkshakes, root beer floats and cherry colas. Everything was made by hand, one glass at a time. A milkshake cost around a quarter, and a soda a little less. Children counted their coins carefully on the counter. The shelves around them held comic books, bubble gum and model aeroplanes. Near the door, a rack of magazines showed film stars and astronauts. The pharmacist worked behind a high counter, filling prescriptions in small brown bottles. He knew every family in the neighbourhood by name. A drugstore like this was more than a shop. It was a meeting place, where news was traded and friendships were made over a tall glass and two straws.

[long-pause]

{img:20-little-house-night-porch-light}

At bedtime, the whole neighbourhood grew quiet, apart from a dog barking far away.

[pause]

At the end of the day, the mother tucked the children into bed and turned out the light. Perhaps she read a short story first, or hummed a song. The house grew still. Outside, the street was dark, apart from the soft glow of the street lamps and the flicker of a television behind a curtain across the road. A dog barked once and stopped. A car drove slowly by, its headlights sweeping across the ceiling. The father checked the front door, turned off the radio, and wound the alarm clock. In the cool autumn air, under the huge Texas sky, the whole city drifted off to sleep. This was the life of most people in Dallas in 1963. It was a life without great drama, full of small routines. Yet small routines are exactly what make a person feel at home. Tomorrow would begin in the same way, with a coffee percolator bubbling in the dark.

[long-pause]

{img:21-main-street-dallas-shoppers-1963}

The street lamps are dark now, but the city will wake again. Let us follow a father's car downtown, where the day is just beginning.

[pause]

On a weekday morning, downtown Dallas looked like a parade of hats and gloves.

[pause]

Downtown was the heart of the city. By nine o'clock, the pavements were crowded. Men in dark suits and narrow ties hurried along with their briefcases, and most of them wore hats. Women in smart dresses, short gloves and little round hats walked from shop to shop. Pairs of young secretaries chatted on their way to work. City buses pulled in and out at the corners, and the traffic lights clicked from red to green. The main shopping street was Main Street. On either side of it ran Elm Street and Commerce Street, and all three were lined with stores, banks, hotels, cinemas and offices. Some buildings were old red brick, and some were new and shining. In the noise, you could hear heels, horns and the bell of a newspaper seller. Dressing well was not vanity. It was a sign of respect, for yourself and for the city. And in 1963, almost everyone dressed to go downtown.

[long-pause]

{img:22-neiman-marcus-store-front-main-street}

The most famous shop in Dallas sold gifts so strange that people read its catalogue just for fun.

[pause]

Neiman Marcus stood at the corner of Main and Ervay Streets. It had opened in 1907, and it sold the finest clothes, jewellery and perfume in the Southwest. Ladies came from all over Texas, and from much further away, to shop there. Inside, the floors were carpeted, the lighting was soft, and the saleswomen spoke gently. There were dresses from Paris and Rome, furs and hats, silk scarves, crystal and silver. Every Christmas, the store published a special catalogue. Most people could never afford what was in it. Some of the gifts were wonderfully extravagant. In 1960, for example, it offered a matching pair of small aeroplanes, one for him and one for her. Newspapers wrote about the catalogue every year, and families passed it around the dinner table, just to laugh and dream. You did not have to buy anything to feel the glow. Even walking through the doors was a small treat.

[long-pause]

{img:23-department-store-escalator-shoppers-1963}

The big department stores had escalators, lunch counters and a Santa Claus on the toy floor.

[pause]

A few streets away stood the larger department stores, where ordinary families did their shopping. They had floors and floors of clothes, shoes and household goods. There were toys, books and records, and an escalator going up and down in the middle. For many children, riding the escalator was an adventure in itself. Each store had a lunch counter, where shoppers sat on round stools and ordered a sandwich, a bowl of chili, or a slice of pie and a cup of coffee. Prices were low, and the waitresses called everyone honey. Customers paid with cash or put things on an account, and a clerk wrote the amount in a small book. In the weeks before Christmas, the windows were filled with scenes of snow, reindeer and tin toys. Children pressed their noses to the glass. On the toy floor, Santa Claus sat on a big chair, and the line of waiting children curled around the corner.

[long-pause]

{img:24-lunch-counter-diner-stools-1963}

A hot lunch downtown could cost less than a dollar, and the waitress knew your order.

[pause]

At noon, the cafés and restaurants of downtown filled up. In the big hotels, such as the Adolphus, the Baker and the Statler Hilton, the tables had white cloths and the waiters wore jackets. There were steak houses, Mexican restaurants and cafeterias with long counters of food behind glass. And there were little diners where a plate of the day cost less than a dollar. Businessmen talked over their steaks about oil wells and land deals. Secretaries shared a sandwich and a cola on a bench in the park. Salesmen ate pie at the counter and told the same jokes they told every day. Many places had a regular crowd. The waitress would simply set down the usual without being asked, and pour the coffee before you sat. It was a small, warm kind of service, and it made a big city feel like a village. Then at one o'clock, everyone hurried back to work.

[long-pause]

{img:25-movie-theatre-marquee-evening}

In the evenings, the old movie palaces on Elm Street lit up in red and gold.

[pause]

When the offices closed, downtown grew quieter. But along Elm Street, the great old cinemas switched on their lights. The signs were tall, and they glowed in red and gold. People waited in long lines outside, chatting and checking their watches. Inside, the carpets were thick, there were chandeliers in the lobby, and the seats were made of red velvet. A great curtain opened slowly before each film began. A ticket cost about a dollar, and a box of popcorn cost a dime. Couples went on dates there. Families made an outing of it. Teenagers sat in the back rows. There was usually a newsreel before the main film, and a cartoon, and a trailer for what was coming next. For two hours, the ordinary world disappeared. Then everyone walked out into the cool night, still half in the story, and the lights of the signs shone in the car windows.

[long-pause]

{img:26-dress-factory-showroom-seamstresses}

Dallas was one of the great dress-making cities in America, with seamstresses on the upper floors.

[pause]

Look up from the pavement near Dealey Plaza, and you would have seen old brick buildings full of small clothing companies. Dallas had a big business in women's dresses. Dozens of firms designed and made them there, then sold them to stores across the country. Their showrooms and workshops filled several old buildings. On the upper floors, women in neat smocks sat at long rows of sewing machines all day. The machines clattered, and the room smelled of cloth and hot irons. Designers sketched at tables. Salesmen showed new collections to visiting buyers, holding up one dress at a time and talking about the colours, the buttons and the bows. The work was fast and exacting, and it paid modestly. Yet there was pride in it. A dress that left that room might be worn at a wedding in Ohio or a dance in Georgia, and the seamstress would never know.

[long-pause]

{img:27-dealey-plaza-park-colonnade-1963}

At the western end of downtown lay a small, peaceful park where office workers ate their lunch.

[pause]

At the far end of Main, Elm and Commerce Streets, the three roads came together and passed under a railway bridge. Beside them lay a little green park, with lawns, trees and white concrete colonnades. It was called Dealey Plaza. The name honoured George Dealey, a newspaper publisher who had done a great deal for the city. The plaza was built in the 1930s and opened in 1940. It was a pleasant, orderly place, a bit like a small outdoor room. The three streets curved gently down through it. On the north side of Elm Street rose a grassy slope with a wooden fence on top and a few trees. In the afternoon sun, the shadows stretched long across the grass. Office workers sat on the low walls to eat their sandwiches and read the paper. Pigeons walked between their feet. In 1963, almost nobody outside Dallas had heard of it.

[long-pause]

{img:28-school-book-depository-red-brick-building}

One of the plainest buildings in Dallas was a seven-storey warehouse full of school books.

[pause]

At the corner of Elm and Houston Streets stood an old brick building, seven floors tall. It had been built at the start of the century, for a company that sold farm equipment. By 1963, it was used by a firm that stored and sent out school textbooks to the schools of Texas. It was called the Texas School Book Depository. It was a plain, ordinary place, with rows of windows and a big sign on the roof for a car rental company. Every day, men and women unpacked boxes, sorted books, packed them again and sent them out by truck. The floors were wooden and the elevators were slow. Workers ate lunch on the steps in good weather. Across Elm Street stood another old building, called the Dal-Tex, full of small offices. Nobody gave either building a second thought. They were part of the scenery, like the pigeons and the colonnades.

[long-pause]

{img:29-dressmaker-office-1963-man-glasses}

On the fourth floor of that old building across the street, a light is on in a small office. A kindly man is looking at dress designs.

[pause]

The man at the desk was called Mr. Z, and his employees were very fond of him.

[pause]

His real name was Abraham Zapruder. In 1963, he was fifty-eight years old, short and round, with a bald head, thick glasses and a warm, gentle smile. He ran a small dress company called Jennifer Juniors, on the fourth floor of the Dal-Tex Building, overlooking Elm Street and Dealey Plaza. About twenty people worked for him. They were designers, seamstresses, clerks and salesmen, and they called him Mr. Z. He arrived at the office at about nine every morning and said good morning to everyone. Then he went to his little office to look at the new designs. He talked to buyers on the telephone. He walked through the workroom and stopped to chat with the seamstresses, and he asked after their families. He was not a man who raised his voice. People who worked for him remembered that he treated them as if they were part of one big family. That was how he preferred to run a business.

[long-pause]

{img:30-immigrant-family-new-york-1920}

Abraham Zapruder had arrived in America as a teenager, on a crowded ship, with almost nothing.

[pause]

He was born in 1905 in a small town in what was then the Russian Empire, into a Jewish family. When he was fifteen, the family left their home and sailed across the Atlantic to America. They settled in Brooklyn, in New York. Young Abraham went to work in the clothing factories as a pattern maker, cutting out the paper shapes from which dresses are sewn. He went to evening school to learn English. He worked hard, and little by little he learned the whole business, from the first sketch to the last button. Millions of immigrants had made the same journey, and many of them built quiet, hard-working lives in the same way. Few were ever remembered. Yet if you had asked him, he would probably have said that America had been kind to him. He was proud to be a citizen, and he never forgot the long voyage across the ocean.

[long-pause]

{img:31-dallas-dress-company-sign-1941}

In 1941, Abraham Zapruder moved his family to Dallas for the weather and the opportunities.

[pause]

With his wife Lillian and their two young children, he left the cold winters of the north and moved to Texas. He had been offered a job with a dress company there. He liked Dallas immediately. He liked the warm weather, the friendly people and the feeling of a city that was growing. After some years, he started his own company and named it Jennifer Juniors. It designed and made dresses for young women and girls. They were pretty, inexpensive dresses with full skirts and small collars, sold in department stores across the country. It was a small business, but a successful one. For years, Mr. Zapruder had spent his days among bolts of cloth and paper patterns. He had a good eye for what young girls wanted to wear. And he had a good heart, which was perhaps more important to the people who knew him.

[long-pause]

{img:32-grandfather-grandchildren-garden-sunday}

On Sundays, the dressmaker became a grandfather who told stories in his garden.

[pause]

Abraham Zapruder was a devoted family man. He and Lillian lived in a comfortable house in a quiet neighbourhood, with a garden full of flowers. Their son, Henry, was a lawyer in Washington. Their daughter, Myrna, was married and had children of her own. Mr. Zapruder adored his grandchildren. On Sundays, the whole family gathered at the house for dinner. Afterwards, he went out into the garden with the little ones, and played with them on the lawn, and told them stories. He liked to show them the flowers he had grown. He was not a rich man, but he was a content one. He had no interest in fame or in important people. What he liked was an easy Sunday, a full table and a camera in his hands. Is there someone in your own life who makes ordinary afternoons feel special?

[long-pause]

{img:33-vintage-8mm-home-movie-camera-hand}

A few months earlier, he had bought the best home movie camera in the shop.

[pause]

His hobby was making home movies. That autumn, he had bought a new camera, a Bell and Howell. It was the best model in the store, small and light, with a gold-coloured metal body. It took little reels of colour film, only eight millimetres wide. A reel gave him just a few minutes of pictures, and each film had to be developed in a laboratory. He took the camera everywhere. He filmed his grandchildren on the lawn. He filmed birthday parties and holidays, with the family around the table opening presents. He filmed the flowers in his garden, and the dogs, and Lillian waving from the porch. In the evening, he set up a small projector in the living room and pulled down a white screen. The projector whirred softly in the dark while the family watched themselves. Home movies were a precious thing in those days. They were the only way to bring a moment back.

[long-pause]

{img:34-window-view-street-park-dusk-office}

From the office windows, you could see right down into a small green park.

[pause]

The staff of Jennifer Juniors had a good view. From the fourth floor, they looked down on Dealey Plaza. They saw the traffic moving along Elm Street, the green lawns and trees, the white colonnades and the railway bridge at the far end. In the morning, the sunlight fell across the park. In the evening, the street lamps came on, and car headlights streamed past in the dusk. At lunchtime, the girls from the workroom often took their sandwiches down to the park. They sat on the low walls in the sunshine and watched people go by. Mr. Zapruder usually walked to a small café nearby, or ate at his desk with the newspaper. In the afternoon, buyers came in from the department stores. He showed them the spring dresses one by one, talking about colours, fabrics and small details. A bow here. A row of buttons there. He loved it, even after forty years.

[long-pause]

{img:35-man-at-home-evening-camera-table}

On the morning of the big visit, Mr. Zapruder almost left his camera at home.

[pause]

In November 1963, there was exciting news in the office. The President of the United States, John F. Kennedy, was coming to Dallas. He would ride through the city in an open car, in a long parade of cars called a motorcade. The route would pass right through Dealey Plaza, along Elm Street, just below the windows of the Dal-Tex Building. Mr. Zapruder admired the President. He had voted for him. And he very much wanted to see him. But on the morning of the visit, the sky was grey, and a forecast had promised rain. He thought his camera might not be much use, and he was also a little shy about filming in public. He decided to leave it at home. Then the sun came out. His secretary, Lillian Rogers, told him he simply must fetch it. You will never forgive yourself, she said, if you miss this chance.

[long-pause]

{img:36-car-driving-home-sunny-morning-1963}

A short drive home to fetch a camera was all it took to change what the world could see.

[pause]

Reluctantly, Abraham Zapruder drove back to his house. The morning had turned warm and bright. Along the way, he saw people hurrying toward downtown, carrying small flags and holding children by the hand. He got his camera from its place at home, checked the film, and put it in his pocket. Then he drove back through the traffic, which was already thick. Nobody could have guessed what he was carrying. He parked, walked to the office, and joined the others who were getting ready to go out and watch. His receptionist, Marilyn Sitzman, a friendly woman in her twenties, said she would come along. Mr. Zapruder suffered from dizziness, and he did not like heights. But he was excited too, in a quiet way. It was the kind of excitement a grandfather feels before a parade. He wanted something to show his grandchildren, so that they could say that they had seen the President.

[long-pause]

{img:37-young-couple-two-small-boys-home-1963}

Across the city, in a small house in a quiet neighbourhood, another family is getting ready for the same morning.

[pause]

Bill and Gayle Newman were barely out of their teens, and they had two little boys.

[pause]

Bill Newman was twenty-two years old, a tall, friendly young man with short dark hair, who worked as an electrician. His wife, Gayle, was twenty-one. She had an open face and a warm laugh. They had two small sons: Billy, who was four, and Clayton, who was two. They were an ordinary young family, like thousands of others in Dallas. They did not have much money. The house was small, with two bedrooms, a little kitchen, and a living room with a sofa and a television. Bill left for work early, in his work clothes, with his tools in the back of his car. Gayle stayed at home with the boys. She cooked, cleaned and did the washing, and she took the boys to the park. In the evening, she read them stories. They were not rich. But in many ways, they were exactly where they wished to be.

[long-pause]

{img:38-backyard-swing-set-two-boys-playing}

The boys loved cowboys, cartoons and a small tree in the yard that they tried to climb.

[pause]

Billy and Clayton were lively. They ran through the house all day, playing cowboys and soldiers. They built towers of wooden blocks and knocked them down again. They rode their tricycles up and down the pavement outside. At the back of the house, there was a swing set, a sandbox, and a small tree. The boys liked to climb it, or at least to try. In the afternoons, they loved the television, especially the cartoons and the cowboy shows. At bedtime, they took their baths, put on their pyjamas, and Gayle tucked them into the beds they shared in one small room. She read them a story, sang a song, and kissed them goodnight. Sometimes Bill came in too and told them about his day. They were the kind of children who fall asleep halfway through a sentence. It was a gentle, noisy and very ordinary home.

[long-pause]

{img:39-family-picnic-lake-zoo-weekend}

On weekends, the Newmans packed a picnic and drove out to a lake.

[pause]

On Saturdays, the family often went out together. They drove to the lake for a picnic, with sandwiches in a basket and a thermos of lemonade. Or they visited Gayle's parents. Sometimes they went to the zoo, where the boys pointed at the elephants, the lions and the monkeys, and Bill lifted them onto his shoulders so they could see over the fence. On Sundays, they went to church. The boys wore little white shirts and bow ties, and sat very still, for a while. Afterwards, there was a big family dinner at the grandparents' house. These small weekends were the heart of their life. They had no great plans, no savings to speak of and no fancy car. Yet they were surrounded by people who loved them. Perhaps that is how most happy lives are built: not on large things, but on many small, warm Sundays.

[long-pause]

{img:40-newspaper-headline-kitchen-table-1963}

Like many young Americans, Bill and Gayle felt that the new President spoke for their generation.

[pause]

Bill and Gayle admired President Kennedy. He was young and handsome and full of energy. He spoke well, and he seemed to promise that the future would be bright and exciting. He talked about sending a man to the moon, and he told young people they could do great things. They liked his wife, Jacqueline, too, who was so elegant and graceful, and their two young children, Caroline and John. They had seen the President many times on television and in magazines. But they had never seen him in person. So when they read in the newspaper that he was coming to Dallas on Friday, the twenty-second of November, they talked it over at the kitchen table. Bill would take the morning off work. They would bring the boys, so that one day the boys could say that they had seen the President of the United States. The boys did not know what a President was. But they understood there would be a parade.

[long-pause]

{img:41-map-route-on-newspaper-hands}

The newspapers printed the exact route of the parade, so that everyone could choose a spot.

[pause]

All week, the whole city talked about the visit. The newspapers printed the route on their front pages, with maps and the times when the President was expected to pass each corner. The motorcade would start at Love Field, the airport, a little before noon. It would drive along broad roads into the city, then turn onto Main Street and move slowly through the middle of downtown, where the crowds were expected to be biggest. At the end of Main Street, it would turn onto Houston Street, then onto Elm Street, and go down through Dealey Plaza. Then it would pass under the railway bridge and out onto the freeway toward a big hall called the Trade Mart, where the President would speak at lunch. Bill and Gayle studied the map. Downtown would be too crowded for two little boys, they decided. They would go to Dealey Plaza instead, near the end, where there was more room.

[long-pause]

{img:42-office-workers-lunch-hour-watching-parade-prep}

Offices let their workers take a longer lunch hour so that they could see the President.

[pause]

Excitement spread through the city like a warm wind. Offices gave their workers permission to take a longer lunch hour. Schools told parents that children could be excused from lessons to see the President, if their parents went with them. In the department stores, shop assistants were told which windows they were allowed to watch from. Some office buildings along Main Street were even selling places at their windows. Flags appeared. Children made signs from cardboard and paint. Dressmakers ironed their best blouse. Everywhere, people were planning where they would stand. A President in an open car was a rare thing, and in 1963, there was no better way to feel part of history than to stand on a pavement and wave. Dallas was proud to be the host. Whatever people thought about politics, they wanted to show Texas hospitality. For a few days, the whole city felt like a place preparing for a party.

[long-pause]

{img:43-little-house-night-porch-light-rain}

The night before the parade, the boys were so excited that they could hardly fall asleep.

[pause]

On the evening before the visit, Gayle laid out the boys' clothes for the morning. There were little striped shirts and long trousers, warm jackets in case it was cold, and their best shoes. She made sandwiches to take along. Bill checked the weather forecast on the television. It said rain in the morning, but clearing by midday. The boys went to bed early. They were so excited about the parade that it took them a long time to fall asleep. At last, the little house grew quiet. Bill and Gayle sat on the sofa for a while, watching the late news, and then they turned off the lights. Outside, the night was cool and cloudy. A light rain began to fall softly on the roof. Somewhere across the city, a dressmaker was setting out his camera, a mother was ironing a pink suit, and a whole city was dreaming about tomorrow.

[long-pause]

{img:44-white-house-autumn-evening-1963}

The same soft rain is falling, a thousand miles to the east. In Washington, a light still burns in the White House.

[pause]

The President who was about to travel to Texas was the youngest ever elected.

[pause]

John Fitzgerald Kennedy was forty-six years old. He had been President since January 1961, when he became the youngest man ever elected to the office, and the first who was born in the twentieth century. He was tall and slim, with thick reddish-brown hair, a sun-tanned face and a wide, easy smile. He spoke with the broad accent of Boston, where he had grown up in a large and ambitious Irish-American family. He was the second of nine children. Many Americans liked him at once. He seemed full of life, quick with a joke, and he was happy to be photographed with his children, his dogs and his friends. At the same time, he carried a heavy load. A President is always on duty, and in 1963 the world was a nervous place. Yet he kept his humour. Would you have found it easy to joke with the world watching?

[long-pause]

{img:45-young-naval-officer-pacific-1943}

Years before, he had saved his crew after a collision in the dark, by swimming for hours.

[pause]

During the Second World War, John Kennedy served in the navy in the Pacific. He commanded a small, fast patrol boat. One night in 1943, a Japanese warship ran into his boat in the dark and cut it in half. The crew swam to a small island. Kennedy towed an injured crewman by holding the strap of his life jacket in his teeth, and he swam for hours. In the end, the survivors were found and rescued. He came home a hero. But he also came home with a bad back, which troubled him for the rest of his life. Most days, he was in pain, though he rarely let anyone see it. He often used a rocking chair in the Oval Office, because it eased his back. Many people did not know how much he hid. Courage, in his case, was not only about battles. It was also about carrying on quietly through pain.

[long-pause]

{img:46-oval-office-president-desk-evening}

Just a year earlier, the world had held its breath for thirteen days.

[pause]

As President, Kennedy had a difficult time. There was a long cold rivalry with the Soviet Union, and there was the danger of nuclear war. In October 1962, the Soviets placed missiles on the island of Cuba, not far from the coast of Florida. For thirteen days, the two great powers stood on the edge of a terrible war. Then both sides stepped back. There was also a great struggle at home for the civil rights of Black Americans. In much of the country, they were still not allowed to vote freely, or to go to the same schools and restaurants as white people. In June 1963, Kennedy went on television and spoke to the nation. He said that this was a moral question, as old as the Scriptures and as clear as the American Constitution. Not everyone cheered. But many Americans listened, and some of them began to think differently.

[long-pause]

{img:47-apollo-rocket-gantry-florida-evening}

In 1961, he had promised that an American would walk on the moon before the end of the decade.

[pause]

The race to the moon was a bright spot in the news. In 1961, Kennedy had promised that America would land a man on the moon and bring him safely back before the end of the decade. At the time, it sounded almost impossible. America had barely put a person into space. But now, in 1963, the rockets were being built, the astronauts were in training, and people all over the country were excited. Engineers worked day and night in Florida, Texas and California. In Houston, a new space centre was growing out of the prairie. The President had many supporters and many opponents. The next election would take place in November 1964, and he very much wanted to win a second term. Yet he also wanted to see America reach for something grand. The moon, for him, was a symbol of a country that still believed it could do hard things.

[long-pause]

{img:48-first-lady-white-house-restoration-room}

The First Lady had turned the White House into a museum of American history.

[pause]

Jacqueline Kennedy, whom everyone called Jackie, was thirty-four years old. She was slim and dark-haired, with wide-set eyes and a soft, quiet voice. She had grown up in a wealthy family in New York and Virginia, studied in Paris, and spoke French, Spanish and Italian. She loved art, history and horses. As First Lady, she restored the rooms of the White House, filling them with fine old American furniture and paintings. In 1962, she showed her work to the whole country in a television programme. Tens of millions watched. Women everywhere copied her clothes and her hairstyle. She spoke about the house the way a mother speaks about a child, and she was determined that it should be worthy of the nation. It was the sort of care that few visitors noticed, and yet everyone felt. A room can look like a home, or like a hotel. She wanted a home.

[long-pause]

{img:49-little-boy-under-presidents-desk}

The President's small son liked to play hide-and-seek under his father's desk.

[pause]

The Kennedys had two young children. Caroline was almost six, a bright little girl with fair hair. She had her own pony, called Macaroni, and she went to a small school on the third floor of the White House. John, whom everyone called John-John, would turn three on the twenty-fifth of November. He loved to play under his father's desk in the Oval Office, hiding behind its small hidden door while his father worked. There were also dogs, a cat, hamsters and ponies. On the lawn stood a treehouse, a swing set and a trampoline. In the evening, when he could, the President came up from his office to have supper with the children and to read them a story before bed. For a few moments each day, the most powerful man in the world was simply a father. That was a thing he valued enormously.

[long-pause]

{img:50-kennedy-family-sea-sailing-summer}

On weekends, the Kennedy family escaped to the sea or the countryside.

[pause]

The family often left Washington at the end of the week. Sometimes they went to a rented house in the Virginia countryside, where Jackie could ride her horses. At other times, they travelled to the Kennedy family homes by the sea, in Massachusetts and Florida. There they sailed, swam, and played games on the lawn. The President loved boats. His family loved the sea air. Brothers, sisters, cousins and friends arrived, and the house was full of noise and laughter. But that summer, there had been a deep sorrow. In August 1963, the Kennedys had a third child, a little boy named Patrick. He was born too early, and he lived for only two days. The President and his wife were heartbroken. The loss drew them closer than ever. For a while, Jackie stayed out of public life, and the President was seen to be gentler.

[long-pause]

{img:51-governor-texas-portrait-office-1963}

The trip to Texas was partly about two politicians who could barely stand each other.

[pause]

The Kennedys decided to go to Texas for a political reason. It was a big state with many votes, and Kennedy had won it only narrowly in 1960, with the help of his Vice President, Lyndon Johnson, who was a Texan. But now the Democratic Party in Texas was divided. The Governor, John Connally, and one of the state's senators, Ralph Yarborough, were bitter rivals and hardly spoke to each other. The President hoped that, by travelling with both of them, he could bring them together. He also wanted to make friends and to raise money for the campaign the following year. It was the kind of work that politicians do all the time, cheerful, tiring and full of handshakes. Some advisers had a worry about Dallas, where a few people strongly disliked the President's politics. But Kennedy said he liked crowds. He did not want to be shut away behind glass.

[long-pause]

{img:52-pink-suit-pillbox-hat-laid-out-hotel}

For the trip, Jackie chose a pink wool suit and a small matching hat.

[pause]

It was Jackie's first public trip with her husband since the loss of the baby. The President was very pleased that she had agreed to come. He knew the crowds would love her. She thought carefully about what to wear, because she knew how many eyes would be on her. For the day in Dallas, she chose a pink wool suit with a navy blue collar and a little pink pillbox hat to match. It was a design inspired by French fashion, and her husband particularly liked it. She packed gloves, a handbag, and a few changes of clothing. The suit would be seen all over the world in photographs. Later, it would become a symbol of the day itself. But on the evening she packed it, it was just a favourite suit, folded carefully into a suitcase, beside a pair of white gloves.

[long-pause]

{img:53-air-force-one-andrews-takeoff-morning}

On Thursday, the twenty-first of November, the great blue and white plane lifts off from Washington. It heads south and west into a warm afternoon.

[pause]

The children waved goodbye at the White House, and a helicopter carried their parents to the plane.

[pause]

On the morning of the twenty-first, the President and the First Lady said goodbye to their children. Caroline and John were sad to see them go. The President picked up John and hugged him. Then a helicopter lifted off from the White House lawn and flew the couple to Andrews Air Force Base, outside Washington. There, the big blue and white presidential jet, Air Force One, was waiting. At about eleven o'clock, the plane took off and turned south and west toward Texas. Inside, the cabin was comfortable, with sofas and a desk and a small bedroom. The President read speeches and newspaper clippings. Jackie looked out of the window. It was a trip full of speeches, cheers and long days, but it was also a time when they were together. They both expected a tiring, happy week. Nothing in the air or on the ground hinted at anything else.

[long-pause]

{img:54-san-antonio-crowds-sunny-1963}

In San Antonio, he told a story about an Irish boy who threw his cap over an orchard wall.

[pause]

Air Force One landed in San Antonio in the early afternoon. It was a warm, sunny day. A huge, friendly crowd was waiting at the airport, waving flags and banners, and a great cheer went up as the President and Mrs. Kennedy stepped out. Vice President Johnson and his wife, Lady Bird, were there to greet them. So were Governor Connally and his wife, Nellie. In San Antonio, the President rode through the city in an open car to dedicate a new building at an air force medical centre, where scientists studied the effects of space travel on the human body. He gave a short speech. He told a story about an Irish writer who, as a boy, would throw his cap over a high orchard wall when his friends were afraid to climb it, so that they had no choice but to go after it. This nation, he said, has tossed its cap over the wall of space. And we have no choice but to follow it.

[long-pause]

{img:55-houston-ballroom-hotel-evening-spanish}

In Houston, Jackie surprised the crowd by speaking to them in Spanish.

[pause]

In the afternoon, the presidential party flew on to Houston, on the coast of the Gulf of Mexico. Houston was a huge, sprawling city, hot and humid, full of oil refineries and the new buildings of the space programme. The crowds were friendly and large. In the evening, the President and Mrs. Kennedy went to a ballroom in a hotel, to a gathering of a Mexican-American organisation. Jackie stepped up to the microphone and spoke to the crowd in Spanish. They cheered and applauded, delighted. It was one of those quiet moments of kindness that political tours rarely planned, yet people remembered afterwards. She had studied the language years earlier, and now it proved useful in a ballroom full of strangers. The President grinned. Then came a big dinner for a Texas congressman, more speeches, and more handshakes. It was a long day, and it was not over yet.

[long-pause]

{img:56-hotel-suite-paintings-lamps-night}

In Fort Worth, local people hung real paintings by Van Gogh and Monet in the hotel suite.

[pause]

Late that night, they flew on to Fort Worth, a city about thirty miles west of Dallas. They landed after eleven o'clock, in the dark, and drove through the quiet streets to the Hotel Texas, where a suite had been prepared for them on the eighth floor. The suite held a surprise. Some of the leading people of Fort Worth had borrowed works of art from the city's museums and collectors, and hung them on the walls to welcome the art-loving First Lady. There were paintings by Van Gogh and Monet, a sculpture by Picasso, and works by well-known American artists. They were arranged carefully around the rooms. But the Kennedys were so tired that they barely noticed. They went to bed a little after midnight. It had been a long, hot, noisy day. Outside the window, the streets of Fort Worth were damp and quiet.

[long-pause]

{img:57-rainy-parking-lot-crowd-umbrellas-morning}

On Friday morning, a crowd stood in the drizzle outside the hotel, hoping to see him.

[pause]

The next morning, Friday, the twenty-second of November, the President woke early. Outside the hotel window, it was grey and drizzling. In the parking lot across the street, a crowd of several thousand people had been gathering since before dawn. They stood patiently in the light rain. At a quarter to nine, the President came out of the hotel without a hat or a raincoat and crossed the street to speak to them from the back of a truck. The crowd cheered. Someone called out, asking where Mrs. Kennedy was. Mrs. Kennedy is organising herself, the President answered, with a grin. It takes her a little longer, but of course, she looks better than we do when she does it. The crowd laughed. It was a warm moment in a cool, damp morning. People later remembered the cheerfulness more than the rain.

[long-pause]

{img:58-ballroom-breakfast-chamber-commerce-hat}

At breakfast, the whole room stood up and applauded when Mrs. Kennedy finally arrived.

[pause]

Then he went back into the hotel, to a big breakfast in the ballroom, given by the Fort Worth Chamber of Commerce. About two thousand people were there, the businessmen and community leaders of the city, sitting at long tables. Halfway through the meal, Mrs. Kennedy arrived at last, in her pink suit and pink hat, and the whole room rose to its feet and applauded. At the end, the people of Fort Worth gave the President a gift, a fine Texas cowboy hat. Everyone called for him to put it on. He smiled and held it up. He said he would wear it later, and then they could all come and see. The crowd laughed again. It was a happy morning, full of jokes and warm handshakes. The President looked rested and cheerful, and Jackie looked radiant. Few people in the ballroom could have guessed how much they would treasure these small, bright moments.

[long-pause]

{img:59-window-view-sun-breaking-through-clouds}

The sky cleared at last, and by the time the plane took off, the sun was shining.

[pause]

Back in the suite, before leaving, the President and Mrs. Kennedy found a small catalogue that listed all the paintings in their rooms, with the names of the people who had arranged them. Jackie was touched. They telephoned one of the organisers, a lady named Ruth Carter Johnson, to thank her. She was at home and very surprised and pleased to hear from them. Outside, the weather was changing. The clouds were beginning to break up and patches of blue sky were appearing. By late morning, the sun was shining brightly. The air was warm and fresh, and the pavements steamed a little as the rain dried. At about half past eleven, Air Force One took off from Fort Worth for the shortest flight of the trip. It would take only about thirteen minutes. From the window, the President and the First Lady could see the flat brown plains of Texas, crossed with roads, and dotted with farms and small towns. And then, ahead, the shining towers of Dallas.

[long-pause]

{img:60-love-field-crowd-fence-sunshine}

The plane banks gently over the rooftops. Below, a crowd has been waiting at the airport fence since early morning.

[pause]

Thousands of people waited at the airport fence, some with folding chairs and handmade signs.

[pause]

At Love Field, the crowd had been waiting since early morning. Several thousand people pressed against the chain-link fence at the edge of the runway. There were office workers on their lunch break, mothers with children on their shoulders, students who had skipped class, and old people who had brought folding chairs. They carried little flags and handmade signs. They shaded their eyes against the bright sun and watched the sky to the west. A radio played from someone's car. Children ran back and forth along the fence. Somebody sold lemonade from a cooler. The airport itself was named after an army pilot of an earlier time, and by 1963 it handled the new jet airliners that were shrinking the country. But today, nobody was thinking about airliners. They were thinking about one blue and white plane. It was a rare thing to see the President in person. And almost everyone had put on their best clothes for the occasion.

[long-pause]

{img:61-air-force-one-landed-steps-crowd}

At about twenty to twelve, a shining blue and white jet came down out of the sky.

[pause]

Someone pointed, and called out. A shining blue and white jet was coming down out of the clear sky, very low and slow, its engines roaring. It touched down on the runway, rolled past the crowd, turned, and stopped in front of the terminal. On its side, in tall letters, were the words United States of America. The steps were wheeled up to the door. The door opened. And there, in the sunshine, stood the President and Mrs. Kennedy. The crowd let out a tremendous cheer. It was a bright, warm day, and the sky had cleared completely. Cameras clicked. Children jumped up and down. A band began to play. For a moment, the whole airport seemed to hold one happy breath. It is hard now to imagine a scene so open and so relaxed. People had come simply to say hello, and they were smiling at one another as they cheered.

[long-pause]

{img:62-red-roses-bouquet-first-lady-steps}

In San Antonio and Houston, Jackie received yellow roses, but in Dallas they were red.

[pause]

Mrs. Kennedy came down the steps first, in her pink suit and pink hat, smiling and squinting a little in the bright light. At the bottom, the wife of the mayor of Dallas stepped forward and gave her a large bouquet of red roses. In San Antonio and Houston, she had been given yellow roses, the yellow rose of Texas. Here in Dallas, they were red. The President followed, tall and tanned, in a dark grey suit and a blue tie. He shook hands with the mayor, with the Vice President and Mrs. Johnson, who had flown in on their own plane a few minutes earlier, and with the Governor and Mrs. Connally. Everyone looked pleased and at ease. It was a lovely, formal little ceremony, in the middle of a busy day. The roses would travel with Jackie that day, resting on the seat of the car beside her, deep red against her pink suit.

[long-pause]

{img:63-president-shaking-hands-fence-crowd}

Instead of going straight to the cars, the President walked over to the fence and shook hands.

[pause]

Then, instead of heading straight for the cars, the President did what he loved best. He walked over to the fence where the crowd was waiting and began to shake hands. Mrs. Kennedy went with him. They walked slowly along the fence together, reaching over to take the hands that were stretched out toward them, smiling and saying a few words here and there. People called out and laughed. Some of them cried. A little girl held up a sign. A woman held up her baby. The Secret Service agents walked close behind, watching the crowd, a little nervous. But the crowd was happy and friendly. Nobody in it seemed to wish them anything but well. For a few minutes, the Kennedys were not distant figures from a television set. They were warm hands, familiar faces and kind words. Many of those people kept the memory of that handshake for the rest of their lives.

[long-pause]

{img:64-dark-blue-convertible-limousine-flags}

The President's car was a long dark blue convertible, and its plastic roof had been left behind.

[pause]

At last, the President and Mrs. Kennedy walked to the cars. The motorcade was lined up on the tarmac, a long row of shining automobiles and motorcycles. In the middle of it stood the President's car. It was a big, dark blue Lincoln Continental convertible, specially built for the President. It was very long and low, with little flags on the front fenders and three rows of seats. A bubble-shaped plastic roof could be put on top in bad weather. But this morning, because the sun had come out, the roof had been taken off and left behind. The President wanted the people to see him. In the front seat sat two Secret Service agents. In the middle row, on small folding seats, sat Governor Connally and his wife, Nellie. In the back sat the President on the right and Mrs. Kennedy on the left, with her roses on the seat between them.

[long-pause]

{img:65-motorcade-cars-motorcycle-police-row}

Behind the President came a long line of cars, buses and motorcycles, all moving slowly.

[pause]

Behind the President's car came another car full of Secret Service agents. Then came the open convertible with the Vice President and Lady Bird Johnson. After that followed more cars, carrying congressmen and officials, and several buses filled with reporters and photographers, who leaned out of the windows with their cameras. At the very end came an ambulance and a few more police cars, as always follow a President wherever he goes. At about five minutes to twelve, the motorcade began to move. The motorcycle policemen started their engines and rode out in front, two by two, their lights flashing. The cars followed, slowly, one after another. It took a long time for such a procession to leave an airport. The day was beautiful now. The sky was clear and blue, with a few white clouds. It was about seventy degrees, a perfect Texas autumn day.

[long-pause]

{img:66-schoolchildren-sign-roadside-waving}

When schoolchildren held up a hand-painted sign, the President asked his driver to stop.

[pause]

All along the route, people were waiting. At first, along the roads near the airport, they stood in small groups outside their houses, shops and offices, waving as the cars went by. Children sat on their fathers' shoulders. Workers came out of a factory in their overalls. And a group of schoolchildren held up a big hand-painted sign, asking the President to stop and shake their hands. He did. The motorcade halted for a moment. The President leaned out of the car and shook the children's hands while they jumped up and down with excitement. It was a very small thing. But for those children, it was a memory that lasted all their lives. The sign they had painted was crooked, and the letters were uneven, and it was perfect. It showed how simple the afternoon felt to the people who had come out to see it.

[long-pause]

{img:67-convertible-tree-lined-avenue-crowds-waving}

Mrs. Kennedy wanted to wear her sunglasses, but the President asked her to leave them off.

[pause]

Then the cars drove on toward the city, along a broad street lined with trees, houses and small apartment buildings. The crowds grew larger and larger. In the back seat, the President waved and smiled. Mrs. Kennedy waved too, her white-gloved hand raised, holding her pink hat in place now and then against the breeze. The sun was very bright, and she would have liked to put on her sunglasses. But the President asked her not to, so that the people could see her face. In the seat in front of them, Nellie Connally turned around now and then to speak to them, smiling, delighted at the size of the crowds. It was a happy, easy ride. Ahead, the tall buildings of downtown Dallas were drawing nearer, shining in the midday sun. Everyone in the car felt relieved. Some of the worries that had followed them for weeks had quietly disappeared.

[long-pause]

{img:68-downtown-main-street-crowds-confetti}

And now the cars slow down, and turn onto the broad main street of the city. The sound ahead is like the sea.

[pause]

Paper fell from the office windows like snow, and the crowds were twenty deep.

[pause]

At about twenty past twelve, the motorcade turned onto Main Street and entered downtown Dallas. The crowds here were enormous. People stood ten and twenty deep along the pavements, on both sides of the street. They leaned out of every window of the tall office buildings. They stood on the roofs, on the ledges, on the steps of banks and hotels, on top of parked cars and mailboxes. As the President's car came into view, a great roar went up and rolled down the street like a wave. From the windows high above, office workers threw down torn-up sheets of paper. It fluttered down through the sunshine like snow and settled on the cars and the people below. The President waved and smiled. Mrs. Kennedy waved and smiled. The noise was tremendous. For a moment, downtown felt like one great festival, with the whole city standing on its feet.

[long-pause]

{img:69-father-child-on-shoulders-waving-flag}

Many people on Main Street saw a President for the only time in their lives.

[pause]

A young secretary, leaning out of an office window, saw him pass right below her. She waved so hard that she nearly dropped her handbag. A businessman standing on the steps of his bank took off his hat. A small boy on his father's shoulders waved a little flag. An old woman who remembered seeing President Roosevelt pass along this same street many years earlier wiped her eyes with her handkerchief. The motorcade moved slowly, at about eleven miles an hour, so that everyone could get a good look. It took roughly ten minutes to drive the whole length of Main Street. Photographers ran alongside. Shop assistants waved handkerchiefs. A man in a bus shelter held a transistor radio to his ear. It was one of the few times when a large city felt entirely united. Whatever people had argued about before, they were all looking the same way.

[long-pause]

{img:70-car-interior-governor-wife-smiling-back-seat}

Nellie Connally turned around and said, "Mr. President, you can't say Dallas doesn't love you."

[pause]

In the President's car, the mood was joyful. Governor Connally was smiling. He had been a little worried about how Dallas would receive the President. But the crowds were bigger and friendlier than anyone had expected. Nellie Connally turned around in her seat to look at the President, and said, with a happy smile, Mr. President, you can't say Dallas doesn't love you. And the President smiled back at her and answered, No, you certainly can't. It was a light, friendly little exchange, and it was one of the last conversations that anyone had in that car. Afterwards, Nellie Connally would remember it again and again. She always said that the people of Dallas had loved him that day, and that she would never forget their faces in the crowd. She wanted that to be remembered, as much as anything else.

[long-pause]

{img:71-johnson-car-roses-lady-bird-crowd}

In the car behind, Lady Bird Johnson held a bouquet of yellow roses on her lap.

[pause]

Behind the President's car, in an open convertible, rode the Vice President, Lyndon Johnson, and Lady Bird, smiling and waving to the people on the pavements. Senator Ralph Yarborough rode with them, the same senator who had been quarrelling with the Governor. Lady Bird held a bouquet of yellow roses on her lap. Behind them came the cars of the congressmen and the officials, and then the buses of reporters, who leaned out with their cameras to capture the scene. Further back still came the local politicians and guests of honour, and at the very end, an ambulance. Lyndon Johnson was a tall man, well over six feet, with big hands and a deep Texas drawl, and he was waving to his fellow Texans. He loved crowds as much as Kennedy did. Today he was a Vice President, enjoying a fine day in his home state, and he had no reason to think of anything beyond it.

[long-pause]

{img:72-houston-street-corner-red-brick-building}

At the western end of Main Street, the tall buildings ended, and a green park began.

[pause]

At the end of Main Street, the tall buildings came to an end. Ahead lay the green open space of Dealey Plaza, with its lawns and trees and white colonnades. The motorcade turned right onto Houston Street and moved slowly up one short block, beside the old county buildings, toward the red brick Book Depository on the corner. The crowds here were smaller and more scattered. People stood along the kerb in little groups, on the grass of the plaza, and on the steps of the buildings. Some of the people who worked in the Book Depository had come out onto the front steps to watch. The sun was shining. The grass was green. The sky was blue. A pigeon walked across the pavement. If you had stood there at that moment with a cup of coffee, you would have thought it was one of the loveliest days of the year.

[long-pause]

{img:73-young-family-standing-on-grass-park}

On the grass beside the road, a young family and a dressmaker with a camera were waiting.

[pause]

In Dealey Plaza, the Newman family was waiting. Bill and Gayle had found a good place to stand, on the grass on the north side of Elm Street, a little way down the slope from the Book Depository. The boys were restless after waiting so long, but excited. Billy stood holding his father's hand. Clayton was in his mother's arms. Up on the slope, a little higher, near a white concrete colonnade, Abraham Zapruder stood on a low concrete wall with his camera. He had come down from his office with his receptionist, Marilyn Sitzman. He chose this spot because it gave him a good view of the whole curve of Elm Street. Because he suffered from dizziness, Marilyn stood behind him, holding onto his coat to keep him steady. He lifted the little gold camera to his eye. And he waited, as everybody else was waiting, for the sound of cheering to come closer.

[long-pause]

{img:74-trade-mart-lunch-tables-waiting-guests}

Two thousand people sat at set tables, waiting for lunch with the President.

[pause]

At the end of the route, a big hall called the Trade Mart was waiting. It was a large modern building, where merchants showed their goods to buyers. For the lunch that day, long rows of round tables had been set with white cloths, small flower arrangements and the best china. About two thousand guests had arrived in their finest clothes. They were the business leaders, the civic leaders and the wives of many of them. Waiters stood ready with trays. A chef had planned the menu for days. A band played softly in a corner. On the stage stood a speaker's table with a microphone, and the President's speech was already waiting. Guests glanced at their watches and chatted about how well the parade must be going. Some had heard the cheering on the radio. Everything was ready, down to the last napkin. It was a lunch that everybody had looked forward to, and that nobody would ever forget.

[long-pause]

{img:75-elm-street-curve-sunlight-trees-quiet}

The sun is still shining on the green grass. But from here, we will follow the day gently and from a distance, as the whole country did.

[pause]

Within a few seconds, a happy parade turned into the saddest hour in the history of the city.

[pause]

It was a little after half past twelve. The long blue car turned the corner and moved slowly down the gentle slope of Elm Street. Then something happened that no one on those lawns had ever imagined. Some people thought they heard a firecracker. Some thought a motorcycle had backfired. In a few seconds, the President was gravely hurt, and Governor Connally was wounded too. A Secret Service agent ran forward and climbed onto the back of the car. The car sped away, down Elm Street, under the railway bridge, and out of sight. The whole thing took only a few seconds. On the grass, Bill Newman called to his wife to get down, and the two of them covered their little boys with their own bodies and held them close. On the wall above, Abraham Zapruder kept his camera running. There is no need to dwell on those seconds tonight. What matters is how people behaved afterwards, and how gentle many of them were.

[long-pause]

{img:76-hospital-entrance-doctors-nurses-afternoon}

The doctors at Parkland Hospital did everything they could, but the President died at one o'clock.

[pause]

The car raced to Parkland Memorial Hospital, a few miles away, arriving at about twelve thirty-eight. Doctors and nurses ran out with stretchers. The President and the Governor were carried inside, into two separate rooms. Mrs. Kennedy stayed beside her husband. She would not leave. Governor Connally was seriously wounded, but he would recover. The doctors worked on the President for nearly twenty minutes, quickly and calmly, but most of them knew from the first moment that there was very little they could do. A priest came and gave him the last rites, while Mrs. Kennedy knelt and prayed. At one o'clock in the afternoon, the doctors stopped. President John Fitzgerald Kennedy had died. He was forty-six years old. Quietly, Mrs. Kennedy took off her wedding ring and slipped it onto her husband's finger. Then she stood very straight and still, in the pink suit, in the corridor.

[long-pause]

{img:77-newsroom-anchor-glasses-clock-1963}

The most trusted voice on television took off his glasses and had to pause before he could speak.

[pause]

In a classroom at the hospital, an assistant press secretary stood before a crowd of reporters. He was pale and his voice shook. He read a short statement saying the President had died at about one o'clock. The reporters rushed to the telephones. In New York, Walter Cronkite, who had been reading bulletins as they came in, took a sheet of paper from a colleague, read it, and took off his glasses. He looked at the clock on the wall. Then he told the country that the President had died. He put his glasses back on, and had to pause for a moment to steady his voice. All across America, people heard the news. Teachers told their students, and some of them wept, and the children had never seen a teacher cry before. Schools sent everyone home. In offices and shops, people stood in silent groups around radios. Church bells began to ring in many towns.

[long-pause]

{img:78-air-force-one-cabin-oath-ceremony}

The new President took his oath of office inside a hot, crowded aeroplane cabin.

[pause]

At Love Field, Air Force One stood on the tarmac in the afternoon sun, its door open. The crowd that had cheered there at lunchtime had gone. Lyndon Johnson decided to take the oath of office in Dallas before they flew, so that the whole world would know that the government of the United States went on without a pause. A federal judge from Dallas, Sarah Hughes, an old friend, was driven to the airport as fast as possible. She was sixty-seven years old, small and energetic, one of the first women ever to become a federal judge in America. About twenty-seven people crowded into the cabin. Mrs. Kennedy came in quietly, still in her pink suit. Someone had offered her a fresh dress, but she had said no. At two thirty-eight, Lyndon Johnson raised his right hand and repeated the oath. A photographer standing on a seat took a picture. It remains the only photograph of a President sworn in on an aeroplane.

[long-pause]

{img:79-air-force-one-sunset-flight-east}

Under the evening sky, the plane flew east, carrying a grieving widow and a new President.

[pause]

A few minutes later, the door closed, the engines started, and Air Force One taxied out and took off into the late afternoon sky, heading east toward Washington. Behind it, Dallas grew small and disappeared. Far below, the land of Texas slid past in the late light: the long brown plains, the small towns, the roads running straight to the horizon, the stock ponds glinting like coins in the fields. Then came the forests and rivers of Louisiana and Mississippi and Tennessee, and the long blue line of the Appalachian Mountains. In the front of the plane, the new President made telephone calls. He called President Kennedy's mother, Rose, in Massachusetts, to tell her how sorry he was. In the back, Mrs. Kennedy sat beside the coffin with a few of her husband's oldest friends. They talked quietly about him. Sometimes they were silent. At about six, the plane landed at Andrews in the darkness.

[long-pause]

{img:80-dealey-plaza-flowers-night-street-lamps}

That night, people began to leave flowers on the grass, one bouquet at a time.

[pause]

Back in Dallas, night had fallen. In Dealey Plaza, people began to come quietly, one by one and in small groups, to stand on the grass beside Elm Street. Some brought flowers and laid them down on the lawn. Some simply stood, with their heads bowed. The street lamps shone on the curving road, on the white colonnades and on the growing heap of flowers on the grass. Above them, the dark windows of the Book Depository looked down over the plaza. And beyond the railway bridge, the traffic went slowly by on the freeway, with its headlights shining in the dark. During the weekend, the flowers grew into a great bank of colour, with wreaths and single roses and small cards tucked among them. Across the city, the churches were full on Sunday. People came to pray, to sing hymns, and to sit quietly together. Ministers spoke about kindness.

[long-pause]

{img:81-television-shop-window-crowd-watching}

For three days, the whole country stopped and watched the same pictures in silence.

[pause]

The next day, Saturday, it rained, in Dallas and in Washington. The whole country seemed to stop. Shops were mostly closed. The cinemas were dark. The television stations cancelled all their usual programmes and showed nothing but the news, hour after hour, along with quiet music and church services. In the Newman house, the boys played in the living room, as usual, and the television was on. Bill and Gayle had hardly slept. They sat together on the sofa holding hands, and every now and then one of them got up to look at the boys, just to see that they were all right. Abraham Zapruder also had a sleepless night. On Saturday, he sold his film to a magazine, on the condition that it would be treated with respect. And he gave a large part of the money to the family of a police officer who had lost his life that weekend. It was the kind of quiet generosity that history seldom notices.

[long-pause]

{img:82-funeral-washington-horses-caisson-crowds}

A little boy turned three on the day of his father's funeral, and he raised his hand in a salute.

[pause]

On Monday, the twenty-fifth of November, the sky over Washington was cold, bright and clear. Schools, offices and shops were closed all over the country. Almost everyone in America was watching on television. In the morning, the coffin was carried on a gun carriage drawn by six grey horses. Behind it, a soldier led a riderless black horse named Black Jack, with an empty saddle and a pair of boots turned backwards in the stirrups. Kings, presidents and prime ministers walked through the streets. Outside the cathedral, John Kennedy Junior, who had turned three that very day, stood beside his mother. As the coffin passed, the little boy stepped forward and raised his right hand to his forehead. The photograph of that moment was seen all over the world. Then the long procession went to Arlington, on the green hills of Virginia. Mrs. Kennedy lit a small flame at the head of the grave. It is burning still.

[long-pause]

{img:83-government-hearing-room-1964-men-in-suits}

In the weeks and years that follow, the shock settles slowly into memory. The people we have met go back to their lives, quietly, as people do.

[pause]

A week after the funeral, the new President asked the Chief Justice to find out everything he could.

[pause]

President Johnson asked the Chief Justice of the United States, Earl Warren, to lead a commission to find out all that could be found out about what had happened in Dallas. For ten months, the Warren Commission worked. Its members and lawyers questioned more than five hundred people. They studied photographs and films, among them the film of Abraham Zapruder, frame by frame. They visited Dealey Plaza. In September 1964, the Commission published its report, a book of more than eight hundred pages, with twenty-six more volumes of evidence. It concluded that Lee Harvey Oswald, a young man who worked at the Book Depository, had acted alone, as had the man who later harmed him. Many people accepted these findings. Many others did not. Over the years that followed, there have been books, investigations and arguments. Some of those questions are discussed still.

[long-pause]

{img:84-abraham-zapruder-older-man-garden-quiet}

The dressmaker never wanted to use his camera again, and his film now rests in a cool, dark vault.

[pause]

Abraham Zapruder went back to his dress business, his family and his grandchildren. He was a gentle man, and the events of that day stayed with him always. He gave testimony to the Warren Commission, and he wept as he described what he had seen. He said that he never wanted to use his camera again, and that he stopped filming his family. He lived until 1970. Today, the original film is kept in the National Archives, in a cool dark vault, where it is protected. The Newman family went home and raised their boys. Bill and Gayle never forgot the moment they lay on the grass to shelter their children. For the rest of their lives, people asked them about it, and they told the story quietly and simply, as it had happened. Billy and Clayton grew up and had children of their own, and later grandchildren. Ordinary people had become witnesses without ever asking.

[long-pause]

{img:85-jackie-kennedy-new-york-library-quiet}

Jackie called the Kennedy years Camelot, after a musical her husband loved to play at night.

[pause]

Mrs. Kennedy moved out of the White House a few weeks after the funeral, with Caroline and John. A few days after the funeral, she gave an interview to a writer named Theodore White. She spoke about her husband and about a musical that he had loved, called Camelot, about the legend of King Arthur and his round table. He liked to listen to the record at night, before he went to sleep, she said, especially the last lines of the title song, about one brief shining moment. After that, people began to speak of the Kennedy years as Camelot. She later moved to New York, where she raised her children, worked as a book editor, and lived quietly, away from politics, until she passed away in 1994. She is buried beside President Kennedy at Arlington, under the eternal flame. The children grew up, and Caroline later served as an ambassador to Japan and to Australia.

[long-pause]

{img:86-apollo-moon-landing-astronaut-flag-1969}

Six years later, as the dead President had promised, two Americans walked on the moon.

[pause]

President Lyndon Johnson served out the rest of Kennedy's term, and in 1964 he was elected in his own right by a very large majority. In 1964 and 1965, he worked with Congress to pass the Civil Rights Act and the Voting Rights Act, which Kennedy had begun, and which changed the lives of millions of Americans. Governor Connally recovered from his wounds and went back to his work as Governor of Texas. He served for many more years in public life. And in July 1969, as the President had promised in 1961, two American astronauts walked on the surface of the moon and came safely home. The promise of a few words in a speech had turned into a rocket, a landing and a footprint. It is a quiet reminder that ideas can outlive the people who speak them. Even in a story with a great sorrow in it, there can be a small light.

[long-pause]

{img:87-kennedy-memorial-dallas-white-walls-sky}

One block from the plaza, Dallas built a quiet, roofless room with only the sky above it.

[pause]

For the city of Dallas, the years after 1963 were difficult. For a long time, people elsewhere thought of it only as the city where the President was killed. Many people in Dallas felt a deep sadness about it, and some felt ashamed, though they had done nothing wrong, and though most had stood on the pavements that day, waving and cheering. Slowly, the city came to terms with what had happened. It grew, and changed, and became one of the largest cities in America. And in 1970, it built a memorial one block east of Dealey Plaza. It was designed by the architect Philip Johnson, and it is very simple. It is a square of tall white concrete walls, open to the sky, with narrow openings. Inside, in the middle, lies a low dark slab of granite with only the President's name on it, in gold letters. It is meant to be a calm place, away from the noise of the city.

[long-pause]

{img:88-sixth-floor-museum-quiet-window-boxes}

The old school book warehouse is now a museum, and Dealey Plaza is a protected landmark.

[pause]

Dealey Plaza is still there today, at the western edge of downtown Dallas, and it looks very much as it did in 1963. The three streets still curve down through the little park, past the green lawns and the trees and under the old railway bridge. The white concrete colonnades still stand on either side. In 1993, the whole plaza, with the buildings around it, was made a National Historic Landmark, so that it would stay the way it was and be remembered. The old Book Depository still stands on the corner. The book company moved out many years ago, and most of the building is now used as county offices. But the sixth floor has been turned into a museum, which opened in 1989. It tells the story of the President's life, his visit to Dallas, and everything that came after. Visitors walk slowly through quiet rooms, past photographs and films and the voices of people remembering.

[long-pause]

{img:89-old-red-courthouse-log-cabin-park}

Near the plaza stands an old red courthouse, a museum that tells the whole story of the city.

[pause]

Not far from Dealey Plaza, in the middle of downtown, lies a little park. In it stands the copy of John Neely Bryan's log cabin, small and dark and dwarfed by the towers around it. Beside it rises an old county courthouse of red stone, built in the 1890s, with turrets and arched windows. People call it Old Red. Today it is a museum of the history of Dallas. It tells the whole story of the city, from the little cabin on the riverbank to the oil boom, the tall towers, the long autumn of 1963 and the years that followed. On a sunny afternoon, families walk through its rooms. Children press buttons and listen to the voices of old Texans telling stories. They look at photographs of the city as it used to be. The courthouse has stood through heat, hail and change, and it still seems to be keeping watch over the city's memory.

[long-pause]

{img:90-dallas-skyline-dusk-lights-neon-horse}

The red horse still turns above the streets of Dallas, just as it did in 1963.

[pause]

Every year, on the twenty-second of November, people gather in Dealey Plaza at half past twelve to remember. They stand together on the grass. There is a moment of silence, and sometimes a short prayer or a song. Then they slowly go back to their work, and the traffic moves on along Elm Street. In 2013, on the fiftieth anniversary, thousands of people came, in a cold grey drizzle, and the bells of the churches of Dallas rang across the city. Dallas is a very big city now, more than ten times larger than the small market town on the Trinity River it once was. Its skyline is full of new towers, shining blue and green and gold in the sunset. In the neighbourhoods, families still have breakfast in their kitchens, children still walk to school, and on Friday nights in autumn, the football stadiums are still full. And on the roof of the old Magnolia Building, the great red winged horse still turns slowly in the night, glowing above the streets.

[long-pause]

The city is quiet now, and so are we.

[pause]

The shop windows are dark, and the school buses are parked in their long rows. The porch lights glow along the cool streets, one house after another. In a kitchen, a radio clicks off. A screen door settles into its frame. Far away, a train sounds its horn, low and slow.

[pause]

Above it all, on top of the old tower, the red horse turns once more against the stars, its wings glowing softly, on and on, over the sleeping prairie.

[pause]

If you are still awake, another gentle story is lined up on the screen, ready whenever you are. And if this one has helped, a subscription lets more tired listeners find these nights.

[pause]

Let the neon fade to a faint red glow. Let the plains grow wide and dark. Let your breath become as slow as the turning horse.

[pause]

Sleep well, and good night.
