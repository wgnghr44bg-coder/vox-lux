Tonight we are going a little more than sixty years back, to a bright and growing city on the wide plains of North Texas.

--

It is an autumn morning in 1963, and the sun is coming up over Dallas.

The light falls first on the tops of the tall buildings downtown, turning their glass and stone to gold, while the streets below are still in shadow. On the roof of one of the older towers, a great red horse with wings, made of neon tubes, is still glowing softly against the paling sky.

If gentle stories like this one help you sleep, you are welcome to subscribe to the channel. Now, let us begin.

--

The winged horse is called Pegasus, and it stands on top of the Magnolia Building, the headquarters of an oil company.

When the Magnolia Building was built, in the 1920s, it was the tallest building in Texas, and when the flying red horse was put on top of it, in 1934, it could be seen for miles across the flat land around the city, turning slowly on its high steel frame. People in Dallas have grown very fond of it. For travellers driving in at night along the highways, it is the first sign that they have reached the city.

{img:pegasus-neon-sign-night-building}
Now, in 1963, there are taller buildings all around it, new towers of glass and steel and polished stone, with names like Republic National Bank and Southland Life. But the red horse is still there, and it is still the symbol of Dallas.

--

Dallas is a young city, and it is growing fast.

About seven hundred thousand people live here now, and every year there are more. New neighbourhoods spread out across the prairie in every direction, with long, straight streets of brick and wooden houses, green lawns, and young trees. New highways loop around the city, carrying cars in and out of downtown. New schools and churches and shopping centres open almost every month.

{img:new-suburb-street-ranch-houses}
Just a few generations ago, this was cattle country, and cotton country. Dallas was a small market town on the banks of the Trinity River, where farmers came to sell their cotton and buy their supplies. Then came the railways, and then the oil, and then the banks and the insurance companies and the factories.

--

The oil fields of East Texas were discovered in 1930, about a hundred miles away, and they made Dallas rich.

The oil companies built their offices here, and so did the banks that lent them money. Then came the businesses that sold them things: machinery and trucks and pipes and office furniture. Then came the department stores and the restaurants and the hotels for all the people who worked in them.

{img:oil-company-office-men-hats-1963}
In the 1960s, there are new kinds of businesses too. Not far from downtown, a company called Texas Instruments makes tiny electronic parts for radios and computers and rockets. A few years ago, one of its engineers, a quiet, very tall man named Jack Kilby, invented something called the integrated circuit, a little chip of material that holds a whole electronic circuit inside it. Nobody yet knows how important it will become.

--

The people of Dallas are proud of their city.

They like to call it Big D. They are proud of its tall buildings, its busy streets, its fine stores, its football team, and its State Fair, which is held every October in a great park east of downtown, with rides and prize cattle and fried food, and a giant statue of a smiling cowboy, called Big Tex, who stands at the gate and says hello to the visitors through a loudspeaker.

{img:state-fair-big-tex-families}
They are proud of Love Field too, the city's airport, which is named after an army pilot from long ago. The new jet airliners land there now, one after another, and the people of Dallas can fly to New York or Los Angeles in just a few hours.

--

On the edge of downtown, the Trinity River winds slowly past, between wide grassy levees built to hold back its floods.

It is not a grand river. For most of the year, it is a slow, brown stream, lined with cottonwoods and willows, with herons standing in the shallows. But it was the reason the city was founded, more than a hundred years ago, when a young lawyer and trader named John Neely Bryan built a little log cabin on its bank, at a place where travellers could cross, and began to trade with the people passing by.

A copy of that little cabin stands in the middle of downtown today, dwarfed by the towers around it, to remind the people of Dallas how it all began.

--

The weather in Dallas is big, like everything else.

In summer, it is very hot, often well over a hundred degrees, and the air shimmers over the pavement. People spend the afternoons indoors, in rooms cooled by the new air-conditioners humming in their windows. In spring, great thunderstorms roll across the plains, with lightning and hail and sometimes tornadoes.

But in the autumn, the weather is lovely. The days are warm and sunny, the skies are a deep, clear blue, and the evenings are cool and fresh. It is the best time of the year in Dallas.

--

{img:dallas-downtown-autumn-afternoon-traffic}
And so, in the late autumn of 1963, the city goes about its business.

The office workers go downtown every morning, in their suits and their dresses, by car or by bus. The children go to school. The housewives shop and cook and clean. The oilmen make their deals over lunch. The shopkeepers start to think about Christmas, which is only a month away.

There is a feeling in the air of a city on its way up, confident and busy and modern.

And in a few weeks' time, something will happen that will make the name of Dallas known all over the world, in a way that nobody in the city has ever imagined.

But not yet. For now, it is simply an ordinary autumn in Texas.
