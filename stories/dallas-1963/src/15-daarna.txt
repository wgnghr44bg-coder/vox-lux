A week after the funeral, President Johnson asked the Chief Justice of the United States, Earl Warren, to lead a commission to find out everything that could be found out about what had happened in Dallas.

For ten months, the Warren Commission worked. Its members and lawyers questioned more than five hundred people. They studied the photographs and the films, among them the film of Abraham Zapruder, frame by frame. They visited Dealey Plaza, and looked out of the window on the sixth floor of the Book Depository.

--

In September 1964, the Commission published its report, a book of more than eight hundred pages, with twenty-six more volumes of evidence.

It concluded that Lee Harvey Oswald had fired the shots from the sixth floor window, and that he had acted alone. It concluded that Jack Ruby, too, had acted alone.

{img:library-shelf-thick-report-volumes}
Many people accepted its findings. Many others did not. Over the years that followed, there have been many books, and investigations, and arguments, about what happened in those few seconds in Dealey Plaza. Some of those questions are still discussed today.

--

The people who were there that day went on with their lives, as people do.

Governor John Connally recovered from his wounds, and went back to his work as Governor of Texas. He served for many more years in public life. Nellie Connally lived until 2006, and late in her life she wrote a book about that day, and about the last words she ever spoke to President Kennedy, in the car, in the sunshine.

She always said that the people of Dallas had loved him that day, and that she would never forget their faces in the crowd.

--

Abraham Zapruder went back to his dress business, and to his family, and to his grandchildren.

He was a gentle man, and the events of that day stayed with him always. He gave his testimony to the Warren Commission, and he wept as he described what he had seen. He said that he never wanted to use his camera again, and that he had stopped taking films of his family.

He lived until 1970. Today, the original film is kept in the National Archives, in Washington, in a cool, dark vault.

--

{img:family-boys-grown-older-1970s-garden}
The Newman family went home, and raised their boys.

Bill and Gayle Newman never forgot the moment they lay down on the grass to shelter their children. For the rest of their lives, people would ask them about it, and they would tell the story, quietly and simply, as it had happened. Billy and Clayton grew up, and had children of their own, and later, grandchildren.

--

Mrs. Kennedy moved out of the White House a few weeks after the funeral, with Caroline and John.

{img:new-york-apartment-window-park-autumn}
A few days after the funeral, she gave an interview to a writer named Theodore White, in which she spoke about her husband, and about a musical that he had loved, called Camelot, about the legend of King Arthur and his round table. She said that he liked to listen to the record at night, before he went to sleep, and especially the last lines of the song, about one brief shining moment.

After that, people began to speak of the Kennedy years as Camelot.

She later moved to New York, where she raised her children, worked as a book editor, and lived quietly, away from politics, until she passed away in 1994. She is buried beside President Kennedy at Arlington, under the eternal flame.

--

In Washington, the Kennedy children grew up.

Caroline studied law, and wrote books, and later served her country as an ambassador, first to Japan, and then to Australia. John grew up to be a lawyer and a magazine publisher in New York, handsome and well liked, much loved by the American people, who had watched him grow since the day of his salute on the cathedral steps. He lost his life in 1999, in an aeroplane accident off the coast of Massachusetts, together with his wife and her sister.

--

President Lyndon Johnson served out the rest of President Kennedy's term, and was elected in his own right in 1964, by a very large majority.

In 1964 and 1965, he worked with Congress to pass the great Civil Rights Act and the Voting Rights Act, which President Kennedy had begun, and which changed the lives of millions of Americans.

And in July 1969, as President Kennedy had promised, two American astronauts walked on the surface of the moon, and came safely home.

--

For the city of Dallas, the years after 1963 were difficult.

For a long time, people in other parts of the country, and the world, thought of Dallas as the city where the President was killed. Many people in Dallas felt a deep sadness about it, and some felt ashamed, though they had done nothing wrong, and though most of them had stood on the pavements that day, waving and cheering, glad to see him.

{img:dallas-skyline-1970s-evening-lights}
Slowly, the city came to terms with what had happened. It grew, and changed, and became one of the largest cities in America. It built new towers and new museums, new parks and new neighbourhoods. A football team called the Dallas Cowboys became famous all over the world.

--

And in 1970, the city built a memorial to President Kennedy, one block east of Dealey Plaza.

It was designed by a famous architect, Philip Johnson, and it is very simple. It is a square of tall white concrete walls, open to the sky, with no roof, and narrow openings at the north and the south. Inside, in the middle, there is a low, dark slab of granite, with only the name of the President on it, in gold letters.

{img:white-concrete-memorial-open-sky}
It is meant to be a quiet place, a place to stop for a moment, away from the noise of the city, and look up at the sky.
