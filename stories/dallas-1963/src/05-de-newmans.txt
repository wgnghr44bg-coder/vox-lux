In a small house in a quiet part of Dallas, there lives a young family called Newman.

Bill Newman is twenty-two years old, a tall, friendly young man with short dark hair, who works as an electrician. His wife, Gayle, is twenty-one, with a pretty, open face and a warm laugh. They have two little boys: Billy, who is four, and Clayton, who is two.

--

They are an ordinary young family, like thousands of others in Dallas.

They do not have very much money. Their house is small, with two bedrooms, a little kitchen, and a living room with a sofa and a television. There is a yard at the back with a swing set and a sandbox, and a tree that the boys like to climb, or try to.

{img:backyard-swing-set-two-boys-playing}
Bill goes off to work early every morning, in his work clothes, with his tools in the back of his car. Gayle stays home with the boys. She cooks and cleans and does the washing, and takes the boys to the park, and reads them stories at bedtime.

--

Billy and Clayton are lively little boys.

They run about the house all day, playing cowboys and soldiers, building towers of wooden blocks and knocking them down again, riding their tricycles up and down the pavement outside. They love the television, especially the cartoons and the cowboy shows in the afternoon.

{img:mother-reading-bedtime-story-two-boys}
At bedtime, they have their baths, and put on their pyjamas, and Gayle tucks them into their beds in the little room they share. She reads them a story, and sings them a song, and kisses them goodnight. Sometimes Bill comes in too, and tells them about his day.

--

On weekends, the family goes out together.

They drive out to the lake for a picnic, or visit Gayle's parents, or go to the zoo, where the boys can see the elephants and the lions and the monkeys. On Sundays, they go to church, the boys in their little white shirts and bow ties, and afterwards there is a big family dinner at the grandparents' house.

They are not rich, and they work hard. But they are happy.

--

Like many young people in America in 1963, Bill and Gayle admire President Kennedy.

He is young and handsome and full of energy. He speaks well, and he seems to promise that the future will be bright and exciting, that America will send a man to the moon, and that young people can do great things. They like his wife, Jacqueline, too, so elegant and graceful, and their two young children, Caroline and John.

They have seen the President many times on television, and in the newspapers and magazines. But they have never seen him in person.

--

{img:newspaper-headline-kitchen-table-1963}
So when they read in the newspaper that the President is coming to Dallas, on Friday, the twenty-second of November, they decide that they will go and see him.

They talk it over at the kitchen table. Bill will take the morning off work. They will bring the boys, so that one day they can say that they saw the President of the United States. They will go downtown, and find a good place to stand along the route of the motorcade.

The boys do not really understand what a President is. But they understand that there will be a parade, and that is enough.

--

They study the route of the motorcade in the newspaper.

It will start at the airport, at Love Field, a little before noon, and come along the broad roads into the city, past the neighbourhoods north of downtown. Then it will turn onto Main Street and drive slowly right through the middle of downtown, where the crowds will be biggest. At the far end of Main Street, it will turn right onto Houston Street, and then left onto Elm Street, and go down through Dealey Plaza, and under the railway bridge, and out onto the freeway, toward a big hall called the Trade Mart, where the President will give a speech at lunch.

{img:map-route-on-newspaper-hands}
Bill and Gayle decide that downtown will be too crowded, with the little boys. They will go to Dealey Plaza instead, at the end of the route, where there will be fewer people, and more room for the children.

--

All week, the whole city has been talking about the visit.

The newspapers print the route of the motorcade on their front pages, with maps, and the times when the President is expected to pass each corner. Offices give their workers permission to take a longer lunch hour, so that they can go out and watch. Schools tell parents that children may be excused from classes to see the President, if their parents take them.

In the department stores downtown, the shop assistants are told which windows they may watch from. Some of the office buildings along Main Street are selling places at their windows. Everywhere, people are planning where they will stand.

--

On the evening before the visit, Gayle lays out the boys' clothes for the morning.

Little striped shirts and long trousers, warm jackets in case it is cold, and their best shoes. She makes sandwiches to take with them. Bill checks the weather forecast on the television. It says that it will rain in the morning, but that it should clear up by midday.

{img:little-house-night-porch-light}
The boys are put to bed early. They are so excited about the parade that it takes them a long time to fall asleep.

At last, the little house grows quiet. Bill and Gayle sit together on the sofa for a while, watching the late news, and then they turn out the lights and go to bed.

Outside, the night is cool and cloudy, and a light rain begins to fall softly on the roof.
