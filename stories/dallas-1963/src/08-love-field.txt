At Love Field, the crowd has been waiting since early morning.

There are several thousand people pressed against the chain-link fence along the edge of the runway: office workers on their lunch break, mothers with children on their shoulders, students who have skipped classes, old people who have brought folding chairs. They carry little flags and handmade signs, and they shade their eyes against the bright sun, watching the sky to the west.

--

At about twenty to twelve, someone points, and calls out.

A shining blue and white jet is coming down out of the sky, very low and very slow, its engines roaring. It touches down on the runway, rolls past the crowd, turns, and comes to a stop in front of the terminal. On its side, in tall letters, are the words United States of America.

{img:air-force-one-landed-steps-crowd}
The steps are wheeled up to the door. The door opens. And there, in the doorway, in the sunshine, stand the President and Mrs. Kennedy.

The crowd lets out a tremendous cheer.

--

Mrs. Kennedy comes down the steps first, in her pink suit and pink hat, smiling and squinting a little in the bright light. At the bottom, the wife of the mayor of Dallas steps forward and gives her a large bouquet of red roses.

In San Antonio and Houston, she was given yellow roses, the yellow rose of Texas. Here in Dallas, they are red.

The President follows her down, tall and tanned and smiling, in a dark grey suit and a blue tie. He shakes hands with the mayor, and with the Vice President and Mrs. Johnson, who have flown in on their own plane a few minutes earlier, and with the Governor and Mrs. Connally.

--

Then, instead of going straight to the cars, the President does what he loves to do best.

He walks over to the fence, where the crowd is waiting, and begins to shake hands.

Mrs. Kennedy goes with him. They walk slowly along the fence together, reaching over to shake the hands that are stretched out toward them, smiling, saying a few words here and there. People call out to them, and laugh, and cry. A little girl holds up a sign. A woman holds up her baby.

{img:president-shaking-hands-fence-crowd}
The Secret Service agents walk close behind them, watching the crowd, a little nervous. But the crowd is happy and friendly. Nobody in it seems to wish them anything but well.

--

At last, the President and Mrs. Kennedy walk to the cars.

The motorcade is lined up on the tarmac, a long row of shining automobiles and motorcycles. In the middle of it is the President's car.

It is a big, dark blue Lincoln Continental convertible, specially built for the President, very long and low, with little flags on the front fenders and three rows of seats. There is a bubble-shaped plastic roof that can be put on top in bad weather. But this morning, because the sun has come out, the roof has been taken off and left behind. The President wants the people to see him.

--

{img:dark-blue-convertible-limousine-flags}
In the front seat of the car are two Secret Service agents, the driver and his partner. In the middle row, on little folding seats, sit Governor John Connally and his wife Nellie. And in the back seat, the President sits on the right, and Mrs. Kennedy on the left, with her roses on the seat between them.

Behind them comes a car full of Secret Service agents, standing on the running boards and sitting with the windows down. Then the car with the Vice President and Mrs. Johnson. Then more cars, with congressmen and officials, and buses full of reporters and photographers.

--

At about five minutes to twelve, the motorcade begins to move.

The motorcycle policemen start their engines and ride out in front, two by two, their lights flashing. The cars follow, slowly, one after another, out of the airport and onto the road toward the city.

The day is beautiful now. The sky is clear and blue, with only a few white clouds. The sun is warm. It is about seventy degrees, a perfect Texas autumn day.

--

All along the route, people are waiting.

At first, along the roads near the airport, they stand in little groups, outside their houses and their shops and their offices, waving as the cars go by. Children sit on their fathers' shoulders. Workers come out of a factory to watch, still in their overalls. A group of schoolchildren holds up a big hand-painted sign asking the President to stop and shake their hands.

{img:schoolchildren-sign-roadside-waving}
And he does. The motorcade stops for a moment, and the President leans out of the car and shakes the children's hands, and they jump up and down with excitement.

--

Then the cars drive on, toward the city, along a broad street lined with trees and houses and small apartment buildings, and the crowds grow larger and larger.

In the back seat, the President waves, and smiles, and Mrs. Kennedy waves too, her white-gloved hand raised, holding her pink hat in place now and then against the breeze. The sun is very bright, and she would like to put on her sunglasses. But the President asks her not to, so that the people can see her face.

{img:convertible-tree-lined-avenue-crowds-waving}
In the seat in front of them, Nellie Connally turns around now and then to speak to them, smiling, delighted at the size of the crowds.

And ahead of them, the tall buildings of downtown Dallas are drawing nearer.
