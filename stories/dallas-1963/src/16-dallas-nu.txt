Dealey Plaza is still there today, at the western edge of downtown Dallas, and it looks very much as it did in 1963.

The three streets still curve down through the little park, past the green lawns and the trees, and under the old railway bridge. The white concrete colonnades still stand on either side. The grassy slope on the north side of Elm Street is still there, with its wooden fence at the top.

--

Every day, visitors come to the plaza, from all over the world.

They walk slowly along the pavement of Elm Street, and stand on the grass, and look up at the old red brick building on the corner. They sit on the low concrete walls, and read the plaques. Some of them stand where the Newman family stood, and some of them stand on the little wall where Abraham Zapruder stood with his camera.

In 1993, the whole plaza, with the buildings around it, was made a National Historic Landmark, so that it would stay the way it was, and be remembered.

--

The Texas School Book Depository still stands on the corner of Elm and Houston Streets.

The book company moved out many years ago. Today, most of the building is used as offices by the county. But the sixth floor has been turned into a museum.

{img:brick-building-corner-blue-sky-today}
It opened in 1989, and it tells the story of President Kennedy's life, of his visit to Dallas, of the events of that day, and of everything that came after. Visitors walk slowly through the quiet rooms, past photographs and films and recordings, and the voices of people remembering.

In the corner of the floor, behind a glass wall, the window is still there, with the boxes of books arranged just as the police found them.

--

Love Field is still the city's airport, much bigger now, though most of the long-distance flights now go from a newer airport further out.

Parkland Hospital is still there too, though it has moved into a new building, much larger, just across the road from the old one. The Texas Theatre in Oak Cliff still shows films, and has become a much-loved neighbourhood cinema again.

And on the roof of the old Magnolia Building, the great red winged horse, Pegasus, still turns slowly in the night, glowing above the streets, just as it did in 1963.

--

Not far from Dealey Plaza, in the middle of downtown, there is a little park with the copy of John Neely Bryan's log cabin in it, and a plain old county courthouse of red stone, built in the 1890s, with turrets and arched windows.

People call the old courthouse Old Red. Today it is a museum of the history of Dallas, and it tells the whole story of the city, from the little log cabin on the riverbank to the oil boom and the tall towers, and the long autumn of 1963, and the years that came after.

On a sunny afternoon, families walk through its rooms, and children press buttons and listen to the voices of old Texans telling stories, and look at the photographs of the city as it used to be.

--

The Kennedy Memorial, the square of white walls one block from the plaza, is a calm and quiet place.

Visitors step in through the narrow opening, out of the noise of the traffic, and stand for a while beside the dark granite slab with the President's name on it. Around them, the white walls rise up, cutting off the city. Above them, there is only the sky.

{img:memorial-interior-granite-slab-sky}
Some people leave flowers. Some sit on the steps. Some simply stand, and look up at the clouds drifting over the open square, and then step out again into the sunshine.

--

Every year, on the twenty-second of November, people gather in Dealey Plaza, at half past twelve, to remember.

They stand together on the grass, and there is a moment of silence, and sometimes a short prayer, or a song, or a few words. Then the people slowly go back to their work, and the traffic moves on along Elm Street.

{img:november-afternoon-plaza-people-flowers}
In 2013, on the fiftieth anniversary, thousands of people came, in a cold, grey drizzle, and the bells of the churches of Dallas rang out across the city at the moment it happened.

--

Dallas is a very big city now, more than ten times larger than the small market town on the Trinity River that it once was.

Its skyline is full of new towers, shining blue and green and gold in the sunset. At night, the buildings are outlined in coloured lights, and one of them, a tall tower with a great glowing ball on top, can be seen from miles away.

In the neighbourhoods, families still have breakfast in their kitchens, and children still walk to school, and on Friday nights in autumn, the high school football stadiums are still full, under the bright lights.

--

And in Dealey Plaza, when the evening comes, the light turns golden across the grass, and then soft and grey.

The street lamps come on along Elm Street. The last visitors walk slowly away. The trains rumble over the old railway bridge, and the cars go by beneath it, with their headlights shining. On the lawn, sometimes, there are still a few fresh flowers, left by someone during the day.

{img:dealey-plaza-dusk-lamps-quiet-today}
Above the city, the stars come out, faint behind the glow of the lights, over the wide Texas plains.

--

That is all for tonight's story, of an autumn in Dallas long ago, of a bright November morning and the long weekend that followed, and of a city that slowly learned to remember.
