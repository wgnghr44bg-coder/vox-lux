BODY: Not far from Dealey Plaza, in the middle of downtown, lies a little park. In it stands the copy of John Neely Bryan's log cabin, small and dark and dwarfed by the towers around it. Beside it rises an old county courthouse of red stone, built in the 1890s, with turrets and arched windows. People call it Old Red. Today it is a museum of the history of Dallas. It tells the whole story of the city, from the little cabin on the riverbank to the oil boom, the tall towers, the long autumn of 1963 and the years that followed. On a sunny afternoon, families walk through its rooms. Children press buttons and listen to the voices of old Texans telling stories. They look at photographs of the city as it used to be. The courthouse has stood through heat, hail and change, and it still seems to be keeping watch over the city's memory.

HEAD: The red horse still turns above the streets of Dallas, just as it did in 1963.
BODY: Every year, on the twenty-second of November, people gather in Dealey Plaza at half past twelve to remember. They stand together on the grass. There is a moment of silence, and sometimes a short prayer or a song. Then they slowly go back to their work, and the traffic moves on along Elm Street. In 2013, on the fiftieth anniversary, thousands of people came, in a cold grey drizzle, and the bells of the churches of Dallas rang across the city. Dallas is a very big city now, more than ten times larger than the small market town on the Trinity River it once was. Its skyline is full of new towers, shining blue and green and gold in the sunset. In the neighbourhoods, families still have breakfast in their kitchens, children still walk to school, and on Friday nights in autumn, the football stadiums are still full. And on the roof of the old Magnolia Building, the great red winged horse still turns slowly in the night, glowing above the streets.
