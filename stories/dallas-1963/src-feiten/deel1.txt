IMG: pegasus-neon-horse-tower-dawn | the red neon flying horse on top of a tall old office tower in Dallas at dawn, glowing softly
HEAD: The most famous sign in Dallas was a red horse with wings, and it sat on top of an oil company.
BODY: The winged horse was called Pegasus. It stood on the roof of the Magnolia Building, the headquarters of an oil company. The building had opened in 1922, and for a time it was the tallest in Texas. The red neon horse was added in 1934. It was enormous and turned slowly on a steel frame, glowing against the night sky. On clear nights, it could be seen from far out on the prairie. Drivers coming into the city used it as a landmark, much as sailors use a lighthouse. By 1963, taller towers of glass, steel and polished stone surrounded it. They carried the names of banks and insurance companies. Yet the old horse kept its place in local affection. Families pointed it out to visiting relatives. People who had moved away described it to their friends. Nobody had planned for a company sign to become a city symbol. It earned that role simply by remaining there, night after night, for decades.

IMG: love-field-jet-airliner-terminal-1963 | a modern 1960s airport terminal with a silver jet airliner at the gate and travellers in hats and suits in the afternoon
HEAD: At the city airport, the new jet airliners made New York feel like a short afternoon away.
BODY: The airport was called Love Field. It had been named after an army pilot of the early days of flying, and by 1963 it was one of the busiest in the South. The new jet airliners landed there, one after another, with a long whistle that made people on the ground look up. A few years earlier, a trip to New York or Los Angeles had taken a whole day by propeller plane, with stops along the way. Now it could take just a few hours. Passengers dressed up to fly. Men wore suits and ties, and women wore gloves and hats, and stewardesses served meals on real china. Families came to the airport on a Sunday afternoon just to watch the planes. They stood at the fence with ice cream cones and counted the landings. Flying still felt like a small miracle. Dallas was proud to have the runways, and proud to be a place that people flew to.

IMG: new-suburb-street-ranch-houses | a quiet new street of brick ranch houses with young trees and green lawns in the late afternoon
HEAD: Dallas was so new that many of its streets had been open fields only a few years before.
BODY: In 1963, nearly seven hundred thousand people lived in Dallas, and the population was growing every year. The city spread across the prairie in every direction. New neighbourhoods appeared with long, straight streets and houses of brick or pale wood. Each house had a lawn, a driveway and a young tree held up by a stake. Whole districts consisted of freshly built homes. Builders worked from sunrise to sunset. New schools, churches and shopping centres opened almost every month. Families arrived from smaller towns across Texas and Oklahoma, hoping for steady work and a better life. Many had never lived in a city before. Federal home loans and new highways made suburban houses affordable for millions of American families in these years. Dallas was a striking example of this national trend. For many newcomers, a house with a yard represented security and success. The city felt like a place where people could start again.

IMG: oil-wells-east-texas-1930s | an East Texas oil field with wooden derricks at sunset, a few men in work clothes
HEAD: Dallas has no oil wells of its own, yet oil made it rich.
BODY: The great oil field was discovered in East Texas in 1930, about a hundred miles away. A wildcatter drilled a well that most people thought was foolish, and it came in. Soon thousands of wells were pumping. The oil came out of the ground in the countryside. But the money, the paperwork and the planning all came to Dallas. Oil companies opened offices downtown. The banks that lent them money moved in next door. Then came the businesses that sold pipes, trucks and machinery, and the lawyers who wrote their contracts. Hotels and restaurants followed, for all the people who worked in them. By 1963, a lunch table in a Dallas restaurant could hold a deal worth more than a whole farm. Men leaned over their steaks and talked about land, leases and the price of a barrel. Yet most families in the city never saw an oil well. They simply lived in a town that oil had built.

IMG: electronics-lab-engineer-1958 | an engineer in a white shirt and narrow tie at a lab bench with small circuit parts in warm light
HEAD: In a quiet laboratory in Dallas, one tall engineer invented the tiny chip that now sits inside every phone.
BODY: Texas Instruments was based in Dallas and made electronic components for radios, computers and military equipment. In the summer of 1958, a newly hired engineer named Jack Kilby worked almost alone in its laboratory. Most staff were on holiday, and new employees had no vacation time yet. Kilby was tall, quiet and thoughtful. He was working on a problem that troubled the whole industry. Electronic circuits needed many separate parts joined by tiny wires, which made them large, costly and unreliable. Kilby wondered whether all the parts could be made from a single piece of material. In September 1958, he demonstrated a small sliver of germanium containing a simple working circuit. It was the first integrated circuit. In 1963, few people outside the industry understood its importance. Yet it became the foundation of modern electronics. In 2000, Kilby received the Nobel Prize in Physics. Today, descendants of his chip are found in almost every electronic device.

IMG: state-fair-big-tex-families | a giant friendly cowboy statue at the State Fair gate in Dallas, families walking past in autumn
HEAD: Every October, a giant cowboy at the State Fair said howdy to everyone who walked in.
BODY: The State Fair of Texas took place each autumn in a large park east of downtown. It had a Ferris wheel, a midway, prize cattle, pigs and quilts. And it had Big Tex, a huge cowboy figure that stood at the gate. He wore an enormous hat and boots, and he was taller than a house. A hidden voice boomed out of him, greeting every family with a friendly howdy. Children stared up at him with their mouths open. The fair also gave the world a favourite snack. Sausages dipped in cornmeal batter and fried on a stick had been sold there since the early 1940s. People called them corny dogs. In the evening, the lights came on across the fairground, and the smell of hot oil and sugar drifted through the crowd. For many Dallas families, the fair was the highlight of the year. It felt like proof that this big city still had a small-town heart.

HEAD: Once a year, the whole fairground turned into a football battlefield between Texas and Oklahoma.
BODY: Next to the State Fair grounds stood a large stadium called the Cotton Bowl. Every October, during fair season, the University of Texas played the University of Oklahoma there. The rivalry was intense. On game day, the stadium divided down the middle. One half wore burnt orange, the other crimson. Families brought blankets and flasks of coffee. The roar of the crowd could be heard far beyond the stadium walls. After the game, fans poured into the fair, still arguing over every play. The annual game had been played in Dallas since the 1920s. In 1960, the city also gained a new professional team, the Dallas Cowboys. They played their early home games in the Cotton Bowl and struggled at first. Within a decade, they became one of the most famous teams in America. Football in Texas was more than a sport. It was a source of community pride and identity.

HEAD: The whole city began beside a slow brown river that most visitors never noticed.
BODY: Dallas grew up on the banks of the Trinity River. It is not a grand river. For most of the year, it moves slowly between muddy banks, lined with cottonwoods and willows, with herons standing in the shallows. In 1841, a young lawyer and trader named John Neely Bryan built a log cabin there. It stood at a place where travellers could cross, and soon others settled near him. Farmers brought cotton to sell. Then came the railways, which turned a small market town into a real city. The river could also be dangerous. After a bad flood in 1908, the people of Dallas built tall grassy levees to hold the water back. By 1963, walkers strolled along them at the weekend. A copy of Bryan's cabin stood in the middle of downtown, small and dark among the towers. It was there to remind busy people where it all began.

HEAD: In Dallas, the weather could be so big that people planned their whole year around it.
BODY: Dallas summers were very hot. Temperatures often rose above a hundred degrees Fahrenheit, and heat shimmered above the pavements. People spent afternoons indoors, cooled by new window air conditioners. Air conditioning spread rapidly in Texas during the 1950s and changed daily life profoundly. Spring brought dramatic thunderstorms across the plains. They carried lightning, hail and sometimes tornadoes. Dallas lies in a region later nicknamed Tornado Alley, and families watched the sky carefully. Some kept storm cellars. Autumn was widely considered the best season. The days were warm and bright, the sky deep blue and the evenings pleasantly cool. School started, football season began and the State Fair filled the park. Yet the weather could still change quickly. A sunny morning might turn into a damp afternoon. On the twenty-second of November, 1963, morning rain cleared shortly before noon. That change in the weather would have lasting consequences.

HEAD: By late 1963, Dallas felt like a city that believed it was only going up.
BODY: In 1963, Dallas had a strong sense of confidence. Office workers filled downtown each morning in suits and smart dresses. Oilmen made deals over lunch. Shopkeepers prepared for Christmas, now only a month away. Residents proudly called their city Big D. They took pride in its tall buildings, fine stores, football teams and State Fair. Jet airliners at Love Field could reach New York or Los Angeles in a few hours. The local economy was growing quickly. Yet prosperity was not shared equally. Black residents still lived under segregation. Many schools, neighbourhoods and public places remained separated by race. Civil rights activists were pressing for change, and some progress had begun. Dallas also had a reputation for strong conservative politics. Some groups were openly hostile to President Kennedy. Even so, most residents felt optimistic about the future. For many families, it was simply an ordinary autumn in a growing Texas city.

On the counter stood a toaster and a glass coffee percolator, which popped and gurgled as it brewed. On the windowsill, a small radio played the weather, the news and a song or two. The mother stood at the stove in a cotton dress and an apron, frying eggs and bacon. Her hair had been neatly set the night before. The father sat at the table in a white shirt and narrow tie, reading the morning paper. In a few minutes, he would drive to work along the new freeway. HEAD: Most children walked to school together, with their books held by a leather strap.
BODY: In 1963, most children carried their books fastened together with a leather strap. Backpacks were not yet common. Children swung lunch boxes, often decorated with cartoon characters or cowboys. Groups of children walked to school together through the neighbourhood. Schools were usually long, low buildings of pale brick with large windows. Inside, wooden desks stood in neat rows. Each morning, pupils stood, placed their hands over their hearts and recited the Pledge of Allegiance. Lessons included reading, arithmetic, spelling and Texas history. Rules were strict. Children were expected to sit quietly and raise their hands before speaking. At lunchtime, they traded sandwiches and baseball cards. Dallas schools were still largely segregated in 1963, despite a Supreme Court ruling nine years earlier. Integration was proceeding very slowly. Many adults who grew up in this period later described school days as orderly and predictable. Their memories reflect both the security and the limits of that time.

BODY: Once the house was empty, the mother began her work. She did the washing in an electric machine in the kitchen or a small room off the garage. Then she carried the wet clothes out into the back yard and pegged them to a long line. In the Texas sun, sheets dried in less than an hour, and they smelled of warm air. She vacuumed the carpets, dusted the furniture and made every bed. She wrote out a list for the grocery shop and planned the evening meal. In the middle of the morning, she often had coffee with a neighbour. They sat at the kitchen table and talked about the children, the school, the church bake sale and the new dresses in the downtown stores. Housework was long, and much of it was lonely. Yet it was also the centre of a whole neighbourhood, a web of small favours and borrowed cups of sugar that held a street together.

HEAD: The supermarket was so new that some shoppers still felt a little amazed by it.
BODY: Supermarkets spread rapidly across America in the 1950s. They were large and bright, with long aisles of colourful packaged goods. HEAD: The most famous shop in Dallas sold gifts so strange that people read its catalogue just for fun.
BODY: Neiman Marcus stood at the corner of Main and Ervay Streets. It opened in 1907 and became one of the most famous luxury stores in America. It sold fashionable clothing, jewellery, perfume, furs and fine household goods. Customers travelled from across Texas and beyond to shop there. Inside, floors were carpeted, lighting was soft and staff spoke quietly. Dresses came from Paris and Rome. The store became especially famous for its Christmas catalogue. Each year it included extraordinary gifts. In 1960, it offered matching his-and-hers private aeroplanes. Later catalogues featured even stranger items. Newspapers reported on the catalogue every year. Many families read it purely for entertainment. Few could afford anything inside. The store's founders believed that excellent service and memorable publicity could attract customers from far away. Their approach succeeded. Neiman Marcus helped make Dallas known as a centre of fashion and retail far beyond Texas.
