BODY: The horse is called Pegasus. It stood on the roof of the Magnolia Building, which was the headquarters of an oil company. The building went up in the early 1920s, and for a while it was the tallest in Texas. The neon horse arrived in 1934. It was huge, and it turned slowly on a steel frame, glowing red against the sky. On a clear night, you could see it from far out on the prairie. Drivers coming in along the highways looked for it the way sailors look for a lighthouse. By 1963, taller towers stood all around it. They were made of glass, steel and polished stone, and they carried the names of banks and insurance firms. Yet the old horse still held its place. Children counted on it. Grown men who had moved away in the war years told their friends about it. Nobody had planned for a flying horse to become a symbol. It simply stayed there, night after night, until it felt like part of the family.

HEAD: At the city airport, the new jet airliners made New York feel like a short afternoon away.
BODY: The airport was called Love Field. It had been named after an army pilot of the early days of flying, and by 1963 it was one of the busiest in the South. The new jet airliners landed there, one after another, with a long whistle that made people on the ground look up. A few years earlier, a trip to New York or Los Angeles had taken a whole day by propeller plane, with stops along the way. Now it could take just a few hours. Passengers dressed up to fly. Men wore suits and ties, and women wore gloves and hats, and stewardesses served meals on real china. Families came to the airport on a Sunday afternoon just to watch the planes. They stood at the fence with ice cream cones and counted the landings. Flying still felt like a small miracle. Dallas was proud to have the runways, and proud to be a place that people flew to.

HEAD: Dallas was so new that many of its streets had been open fields only a few years before.
BODY: In 1963, nearly seven hundred thousand people lived in Dallas, and more arrived every year. The city spread out across the prairie in every direction. New neighbourhoods appeared with long, straight streets and houses of brick or pale wood. Each house had a lawn, a driveway and a thin young tree held up by a stake. You could drive for ten minutes and see nothing but fresh paint. Builders worked from sunrise to sunset. New schools, churches and shopping centres opened almost every month. Families came from smaller towns across Texas and Oklahoma, hoping for steady work and a bigger life. Many of them had never lived in a city before. They learned its streets together, and they learned who lived next door. A young father might say, with some pride, that his house had been built the same spring as his son. The city felt like a place where you could start again.

HEAD: Dallas has no oil wells of its own, yet oil made it rich.
BODY: The great oil field was discovered in East Texas in 1930, about a hundred miles away. A wildcatter drilled a well that most people thought was foolish, and it came in. Soon thousands of wells were pumping. The oil came out of the ground in the countryside. But the money, the paperwork and the planning all came to Dallas. Oil companies opened offices downtown. The banks that lent them money moved in next door. Then came the businesses that sold pipes, trucks and machinery, and the lawyers who wrote their contracts. Hotels and restaurants followed, for all the people who worked in them. By 1963, a lunch table in a Dallas restaurant could hold a deal worth more than a whole farm. Men leaned over their steaks and talked about land, leases and the price of a barrel. Yet most families in the city never saw an oil well. They simply lived in a town that oil had built.

HEAD: In a quiet laboratory in Dallas, one tall engineer invented the tiny chip that now sits inside every phone.
BODY: Not far from downtown stood the offices of a company called Texas Instruments. It made small electronic parts for radios, for computers and for rockets. In the summer of 1958, a new engineer named Jack Kilby sat there almost alone. Most of his colleagues had gone on holiday. He was tall, quiet and thoughtful, and he had a problem to solve. Electronic circuits needed many separate parts, joined by many tiny wires. What if all the parts could be made from the same piece of material? That September, he showed his bosses a little sliver of germanium with a simple circuit built inside. It looked very plain. But it worked. It was called the integrated circuit. In 1963, almost nobody outside the industry knew what it meant. People talked about oil, cattle and football. Yet this small chip would go on to change nearly everything in daily life. Would you have guessed it, looking at that sliver?

HEAD: Every October, a giant cowboy at the State Fair said howdy to everyone who walked in.
BODY: The State Fair of Texas took place each autumn in a large park east of downtown. It had a Ferris wheel, a midway, prize cattle, pigs and quilts. And it had Big Tex, a huge cowboy figure that stood at the gate. He wore an enormous hat and boots, and he was taller than a house. A hidden voice boomed out of him, greeting every family with a friendly howdy. Children stared up at him with their mouths open. The fair also gave the world a favourite snack. Sausages dipped in cornmeal batter and fried on a stick had been sold there since the early 1940s. People called them corny dogs. In the evening, the lights came on across the fairground, and the smell of hot oil and sugar drifted through the crowd. For many Dallas families, the fair was the highlight of the year. It felt like proof that this big city still had a small-town heart.

HEAD: Once a year, the whole fairground turned into a football battlefield between Texas and Oklahoma.
BODY: Right beside the State Fair stood a great stadium called the Cotton Bowl. Every October, during fair week, two famous college teams met there. Fathers and sons wore hats. Mothers carried blankets and flasks of coffee. The crowd roared so loudly that you could hear it from the midway outside. Afterwards, people spilled out into the fair, still arguing over every play. The game had been played in Dallas since the 1920s, and in 1963, it was already an old tradition. Not long before, in 1960, the city had also welcomed a brand new professional team called the Dallas Cowboys. Football in Texas was never only a game. It was a way of belonging, and a reason to put on your best jacket.

HEAD: The whole city began beside a slow brown river that most visitors never noticed.
BODY: Dallas grew up on the banks of the Trinity River. It is not a grand river. For most of the year, it moves slowly between muddy banks, lined with cottonwoods and willows, with herons standing in the shallows. In 1841, a young lawyer and trader named John Neely Bryan built a log cabin there. It stood at a place where travellers could cross, and soon others settled near him. Farmers brought cotton to sell. Then came the railways, which turned a small market town into a real city. The river could also be dangerous. After a bad flood in 1908, the people of Dallas built tall grassy levees to hold the water back. By 1963, walkers strolled along them at the weekend. A copy of Bryan's cabin stood in the middle of downtown, small and dark among the towers. It was there to remind busy people where it all began.

HEAD: In Dallas, the weather could be so big that people planned their whole year around it.
BODY: Summers were very hot. Often the thermometer rose well above a hundred degrees, and the air shimmered above the pavement. People spent the afternoons indoors, in rooms cooled by new window air conditioners humming in the wall. Spring was different. Great thunderstorms rolled across the plains. They brought lightning, hail as big as marbles and sometimes tornadoes. Families kept an eye on the sky, and a few kept a storm cellar. But then came the autumn. The days were warm and bright. The sky turned a deep, clear blue. The evenings were cool enough for a light jacket. Many people said it was the best season in Texas. School started, football began, and the fair filled the park. Yet even in autumn, the weather could change within an hour. It might be sunny in the morning and drizzling by noon. Have you ever lived somewhere where you wore a different coat in the afternoon?

HEAD: By late 1963, Dallas felt like a city that believed it was only going up.
BODY: There was a quiet confidence in the air. Office workers poured into downtown each morning in suits and smart dresses, by car or by bus. Children went to school. Black families in Dallas still lived by the rules of segregation, and their share of the city's good fortune was much smaller. Yet most people felt that the next year would be better than the last. It was an ordinary autumn in Texas, and for now, that was enough.
