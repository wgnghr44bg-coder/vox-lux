Tonight we are going more than two thousand years back, to the city of Alexandria in Egypt, in the year 31 before Christ.

[long-pause]

On the shore of the Mediterranean Sea, at the edge of the great green Nile delta, stands a city of white marble and golden light. It has a lighthouse taller than any building in the world. It has a library holding the knowledge of the ancient world. And it has a palace by the sea, where a queen lives.

[pause]

Her name is Cleopatra. She is the last pharaoh of Egypt. Tonight we will spend a little time in her world. In her city, along her river and among her people, in the last years when Egypt was still free.

[pause]

If you enjoy quiet journeys into the distant past, you are welcome to subscribe to the channel, so that the next one is easy to find.

[long-pause]

{img:nile-delta-green-fields-palms-canals-river-branches-haze}
Let us picture Egypt in the time of Cleopatra.

[pause]

It is one of the oldest countries in the world. For three thousand years, kings called pharaohs have ruled here. They built temples, pyramids and tombs along the banks of the Nile. When Cleopatra is born, the great pyramids are already two and a half thousand years old. They are as old to her as she is to us.

[long-pause]

Egypt is a strange and wonderful land. It is a long, narrow ribbon of green along a river, right through the middle of a huge desert.

[pause]

The Nile flows north from the mountains of Africa, for more than a thousand kilometres, to the sea. On both sides of it, for a few kilometres, there are fields, palm trees and villages. And then, suddenly, the desert begins. Endless sand and rock under the burning sun.

[pause]

In the north, near the sea, the river splits into many branches. It spreads out into a great triangle of green land, full of fields, marshes and canals. The Greeks call it the Delta, because it looks like their letter delta, a triangle.

[pause]

And at the western edge of the Delta, on a strip of land between the sea and a great lake, lies the city of Alexandria.

[long-pause]

Alexandria is not an old Egyptian city. It is a Greek city in Egypt.

[pause]

It was founded three hundred years earlier by Alexander the Great, the young king who conquered half the world. The story goes that he marked out the plan of the city on the ground with barley flour, because there was no chalk. Then birds came and ate the flour. The fortune tellers said it was a good sign. The city would feed people from many lands.

[pause]

{img:young-alexander-marking-city-plan-barley-flour-birds-coast}
They were right. In Cleopatra's time, Alexandria is the greatest city in the world after Rome. It is perhaps the most beautiful and the most learned. Half a million people may live there. Greeks and Egyptians, Jews and Syrians, Arabs and Romans, and people from every land around the sea.

[long-pause]

It is a city of trade, learning, beauty and pleasure.

[pause]

Its harbours are full of ships carrying Egyptian grain to Rome, and goods from Africa, Arabia and India. Its streets are wide and straight, lined with columns, temples, palaces and gardens. Its scholars are the most famous in the world. Its people are lively and witty and love music, horse races and festivals.

[pause]

{img:canopic-way-colonnades-crowds-chariots-white-marble-sunlight}
And at its heart, by the sea, stands the palace of the Greek kings and queens of Egypt. Cleopatra lives there with her children and her court. It is the year 31 before Christ. And everything is about to change.

[long-pause]

{img:ptolemy-first-general-portrait-diadem-macedonian-calm}
When Alexander the Great died in Babylon, at only thirty-two, his generals divided his great empire between them.

[pause]

One of them, a nobleman called Ptolemy, took Egypt. He had been Alexander's friend since childhood. He was a wise and careful man. He made himself king of Egypt, and started a family of kings and queens who would rule Egypt for nearly three hundred years.

[long-pause]

Ptolemy even took Alexander's body while it was being carried home, and brought it to Alexandria. He buried it in a great tomb in the middle of the city, so the city would share the great conqueror's glory. In Cleopatra's time, people still come from all over the world to see it.

[pause]

All the kings of the family were called Ptolemy. Most of the queens were called Cleopatra, Berenice or Arsinoe. Our Cleopatra is the seventh queen with that name.

[pause]

The Ptolemies were Greeks, not Egyptians. They spoke Greek and lived like Greek kings, with Greek friends, Greek soldiers and Greek customs.

[pause]

But to the Egyptian people they were pharaohs. They were crowned in the old Egyptian way, with the ancient crowns. They built Egyptian temples along the Nile to the old gods. On the temple walls, they are shown as pharaohs, with Egyptian clothes and crowns, and their names written in hieroglyphs, the old picture writing.

[long-pause]

So they ruled two peoples in two ways. Greek kings to the Greeks, and pharaohs to the Egyptians.

[pause]

The first Ptolemies were great rulers. They made Egypt rich and powerful, and Alexandria the most splendid city in the world. They built the great lighthouse and the great library, and invited the best scholars from all over Greece.

[pause]

{img:ptolemaic-pharaoh-relief-temple-wall-offering-to-gods-hieroglyphs}
But the later Ptolemies were weaker. The family quarrelled and fought among themselves, brothers against sisters, mothers against sons, all for the throne. Following an old royal custom, they often married their own brothers and sisters. There were riots and plots in the palace.

[long-pause]

And meanwhile, in the west, a new power was rising. Rome.

[pause]

By the time Cleopatra was born, Rome had conquered Greece and most lands around the Mediterranean. Egypt was the last great kingdom still free. And the Romans looked at Egypt, with its endless fields of grain and its gold, with greedy eyes.

[pause]

{img:roman-envoys-arriving-alexandria-harbour-galley-togas}
Cleopatra's father loved to play the flute, so people called him the Flute Player. He kept his throne only by paying huge bribes to powerful Romans. Once, the people of Alexandria drove him out. He fled to Rome, and came back only with the help of a Roman army.

[pause]

That was the world Cleopatra was born into. A rich, ancient and beautiful country, ruled by a quarrelling Greek family, living in the shadow of Rome. How long could Egypt stay free?

[long-pause]

{img:ptolemaia-grand-procession-alexandria-floats-elephants-crowds}
The people of Alexandria love festivals. The most famous one in the city's history was a great procession held two hundred years before Cleopatra. A writer who saw it described it, and it was remembered for centuries.

[pause]

It went on all day long, from dawn until dark, through the stadium and the streets.

[long-pause]

There were huge wagons pulled by hundreds of men, carrying giant statues. One showed the god of wine, five metres tall, pouring wine from a golden cup. There was a wine press with sixty men treading grapes and singing, while new wine ran out into the street. There was a giant wineskin made of leopard skins, holding thousands of litres. It leaked wine all along the way, so the people could drink.

[pause]

There were animals from all over Africa and Asia. Elephants pulling chariots, camels and ostriches, antelopes and zebras, leopards and cheetahs. There was a giraffe, a white bear, a rhinoceros, and parrots and peacocks in cages.

[pause]

{img:exotic-animals-procession-giraffe-elephants-ostriches-street}
Thousands of people walked in it, dressed as gods and spirits, or as soldiers in shining armour. Boys and girls sang. Hundreds of musicians played. And a golden statue of Alexander the Great rode in a chariot pulled by elephants.

[long-pause]

In Cleopatra's time there are still great festivals in Alexandria. For the gods, for the birthdays of the kings and queens, and for the rising of the Nile. There are processions and feasts, chariot races and plays, and music and dancing in the streets all night.

[pause]

At the festival of the goddess Isis in spring, a beautiful ship decorated with flowers is launched into the sea. It is meant to bless the sailing season. The people gather on the shore, singing and throwing flowers on the water.

[pause]

{img:festival-ship-isis-flowers-launched-harbour-crowds-singing}
And in summer, when the Nile begins to rise, the whole of Egypt celebrates, from Alexandria to the far south. There is music and dancing and feasting, and thanks to the gods for the gift of the river. Everyone hopes the flood will be good. A low flood means hunger. A good flood means a happy year.

[long-pause]

{img:cleopatra-portrait-diadem-greek-hairstyle-pearls-calm-intelligent}
Cleopatra the Seventh was born in Alexandria in the winter of 69 before Christ.

[pause]

In the year 31 she is thirty-eight. She has been queen of Egypt for twenty-one years, since she was eighteen. She is one of the most famous women in all of history. And yet we know surprisingly little about her for certain. Almost everything written about her was written by her enemies, the Romans, after her death.

[long-pause]

What did she look like?

[pause]

Her coins show a woman with a strong face, a large curved nose and a firm chin. Her hair is pulled back in a bun, in the Greek style, with braids like the slices of a melon. Around her head she wears a royal band of white cloth. On Egyptian temple walls, she is shown in the old Egyptian way, as a goddess with a tall crown.

[pause]

A Greek writer who lived about a hundred and fifty years later talked to people whose grandparents had known her court. He says her beauty was not so remarkable in itself. But her presence was captivating and her conversation impossible to resist. Her voice, he says, was sweet, like an instrument with many strings.

[long-pause]

She was very clever and very well educated. She spoke many languages. Greek, of course, her own language. And Egyptian, which she was the first of her whole family to bother to learn. She spoke the languages of the Arabs, the Hebrews, the Syrians and others, so she could talk to their ambassadors without an interpreter.

[pause]

She studied with the scholars of Alexandria. Mathematics, the stars, medicine and history. Later writers said she even wrote books about medicine and beauty. Perhaps some of that is true.

[pause]

{img:young-princess-studying-scrolls-library-alexandria-scholars}
She was also a skilful ruler. She managed the harvests, the grain and the money of Egypt carefully. She made Egypt rich again after her father's troubled reign. The Egyptian people loved her. They saw her as a living goddess, the goddess Isis on earth.

[long-pause]

Her early life was hard and dangerous.

[pause]

When she was about eleven, her father was driven out of Alexandria. Her older sister seized the throne. When her father came back with a Roman army, that sister was put to death. Then, when her father died, Cleopatra became queen at eighteen. She had to rule together with her younger brother, who was only ten. By the custom of the family, he also became her husband.

[pause]

{img:young-queen-and-boy-king-throne-room-alexandria-courtiers}
But her brother's advisers turned against her and drove her out of Alexandria. She fled to Syria, gathered an army, and came back to fight for her throne.

[pause]

And at that very moment, in the summer of 48 before Christ, the most powerful man in Rome sailed into Alexandria. His name was Julius Caesar. Would he help her? Or would he help her brother?

[long-pause]

{img:alexandria-grid-streets-canopic-way-gate-of-sun-panorama}
Let us walk through Alexandria as it is in the time of Cleopatra.

[pause]

The city is long and narrow. It stretches about five kilometres along the shore, between the sea in the north and a great lake in the south. It is laid out in a perfect grid of straight streets, designed by a Greek architect three hundred years before.

[long-pause]

The main street runs right through the city from east to west, from the Gate of the Sun to the Gate of the Moon. It is enormously wide, about thirty metres, wide enough for many chariots side by side. On both sides there are rows of marble columns, so people can walk in the shade.

[pause]

One Greek writer described his first sight of this street. He said the columns seemed to go on forever, and he could not decide where to look first, because there was so much beauty everywhere.

[pause]

The city is divided into five quarters, named after the first five letters of the Greek alphabet. Most Greeks live in the centre. A large and important Jewish community lives in the east, with its own synagogues and its own council. Most Egyptians live in the west, in an old village that was there before Alexander came.

[long-pause]

In the northeast, by the sea, lies the palace quarter. It takes up perhaps a quarter of the whole city.

[pause]

It is not one palace but many. Palaces, halls, gardens and courtyards, built by one king after another, along the shore and on a little island in the harbour. On that island, Cleopatra has her own private palace by the sea.

[pause]

In the palace quarter there are also the great library, the tomb of Alexander, a theatre and a royal harbour for the royal ships.

[long-pause]

Everywhere in the city there are temples, to Greek gods and Egyptian gods, and to gods who are both at once.

[pause]

The greatest is the temple of Serapis, on a hill in the Egyptian quarter. Serapis was a god invented by the first Ptolemy, to please both Greeks and Egyptians. He is a bearded god with a basket of grain on his head, and he protects Alexandria. His temple stands on a high platform, with a hundred steps leading up to it. Inside stands a huge statue of the god, covered in gold and precious stones.

[pause]

{img:serapeum-temple-hill-steps-colonnades-statue-sunlit}
There are sports grounds where young Greek men exercise and study. There is a great track for chariot races, and there are theatres, parks and public baths.

[long-pause]

And there are the markets, full of the goods of the world. Grain, papyrus and linen from Egypt. Spices and perfumes from Arabia. Ivory and gold from Africa. Pepper and precious stones from India. Wine and olive oil from Greece. Even silk from faraway China, which costs a fortune.

[pause]

{img:alexandria-market-spices-papyrus-rolls-linen-ivory-merchants}
The people of Alexandria are proud and clever and quick with words. They love jokes and nicknames and gossip, and making fun of their rulers. They love their beautiful city. It has everything in the world, they say, except snow. What it does not have, though, is a strong army. And that worries the queen more than anything.

[long-pause]

{img:great-synagogue-alexandria-columns-congregation-scrolls-light}
One of the biggest communities in Alexandria in Cleopatra's time is the Jewish community.

[pause]

Jews have lived here since the city was founded, three hundred years before. Some came as soldiers for the kings. Others came as merchants, craftsmen and farmers. By Cleopatra's time there may be a hundred thousand of them or more, perhaps more than in Jerusalem itself. Most live in the east of the city, near the palace and the sea.

[long-pause]

The Jews of Alexandria speak Greek in daily life. Many have Greek names, wear Greek clothes and go to the theatre. But they keep their own faith, their own laws and their own customs, like the Sabbath, the festivals and the food laws.

[pause]

They have their own council of elders, their own courts and many synagogues. The Great Synagogue was so huge, it is said, that people at the back could not hear the reader. So an official stood in the middle and waved a flag, to show everyone when to say Amen.

[pause]

{img:jewish-merchants-delta-quarter-street-greek-dress-market}
Because the Jews of Alexandria spoke Greek, many no longer understood Hebrew well. So about two hundred years before Cleopatra, the Hebrew Bible was translated into Greek, right here in Alexandria. The story goes that seventy-two scholars worked on a little island near the lighthouse, and finished in seventy-two days. This translation is still used today by the Greek Orthodox Church.

[long-pause]

Every year, the Jews of Alexandria held a festival on that island to celebrate the translation, with picnics and prayers on the beach by the sea.

[pause]

A little later, in the time of Jesus, a great Jewish thinker called Philo will live in Alexandria. He will write books in Greek that try to bring together the wisdom of the Bible and the ideas of the Greek thinkers.

[pause]

{img:beach-picnic-families-pharos-island-festival-lighthouse-behind}
Cleopatra seems to have been friendly to the Jews of Alexandria, and protected their rights. There were sometimes tensions between them and the Greeks of the city. But Alexandria was a place where many peoples and many faiths lived side by side. They learned from each other, and sometimes quarrelled, as people in great cities always do. Everyone knew, though, that if Rome ever took over, all of them would have new masters.

[long-pause]

{img:pharos-lighthouse-three-tiers-fire-top-statue-ships-dusk}
The most famous building in Alexandria, and one of the Seven Wonders of the World, is the great lighthouse. It is called the Pharos.

[pause]

It stands on a little island at the entrance to the great harbour. A long stone road, almost a kilometre and a half long, joins the island to the city.

[long-pause]

The lighthouse was built about two hundred and fifty years before Cleopatra, by the first two kings of her family.

[pause]

It is enormous, perhaps a hundred metres high or more. After the great pyramids, it is the tallest building in the world. It is made of great blocks of white stone, in three parts. At the bottom is a tall square tower with many windows and rooms inside. Above that is an eight-sided tower. And on top is a round tower with columns, where the fire burns.

[pause]

At the very top stands a great statue of a god. And at the corners of the first part, there are statues of sea gods blowing on big shells.

[long-pause]

At night, a great fire burns at the top, fed with wood and oil. The fuel is carried up inside the tower along a winding ramp, perhaps on donkeys. During the day, it is said, a big polished bronze mirror reflects the sunlight out to sea.

[pause]

The old writers say the light can be seen from fifty kilometres out at sea. The coast near Alexandria is flat and low and dangerous, full of rocks and sandbanks. Without the lighthouse, many ships would be lost. With it, they find their way safely into the harbour, by day and by night.

[pause]

{img:ship-at-night-sea-sailors-seeing-distant-lighthouse-glow}
Sailors coming from Rome, Greece or Syria watch the dark horizon for the light. When they see it, they breathe a sigh of relief. They know they are almost there.

[long-pause]

The lighthouse was so famous that its name, Pharos, became the word for lighthouse in many languages. In French it is phare, and in Italian and Spanish it is faro.

[pause]

There is a story about the builder. He wanted his own name to be remembered, but the king wanted his name on the lighthouse. So the builder carved his own name into the stone. Then he covered it with plaster, and painted the king's name on the plaster. After many years, the plaster fell away. And the builder's name was there for everyone to see.

[pause]

{img:carved-greek-inscription-stone-plaster-falling-away-sunlight}
The lighthouse stood for more than a thousand years. Then great earthquakes damaged it, and it fell. Later, a sultan of Egypt built a fort on the same spot, using some of its stones. That fort still stands there today.

[long-pause]

{img:great-harbour-alexandria-merchant-ships-grain-warehouses-quay}
Alexandria has two great harbours, one on each side of the long stone road that joins the city to the lighthouse island.

[pause]

The Great Harbour, in the east, next to the palace quarter, is the busiest. It is full of ships from all over the Mediterranean. There are big round merchant ships with one tall mast and a big square sail. There are long, slim warships. There are little fishing boats, and river boats that come down the Nile through a canal.

[long-pause]

Along the quays stand big warehouses for grain, papyrus, linen, glass, spices and wine. Cranes, worked by men walking inside big wheels, lift the cargo in and out of the ships. Porters carry sacks and jars on their backs. Merchants argue about prices. Officials check every ship and collect the taxes.

[pause]

The most important cargo of all is grain. Every summer, after the harvest, great fleets of grain ships sail from Alexandria, carrying the wheat of Egypt across the sea. Much of it goes to Rome. And that is exactly why Rome cares so much about Egypt. Whoever controls Egypt controls the bread of Rome.

[pause]

{img:huge-grain-ship-loading-sacks-cranes-sailors-alexandria-summer}
The other harbour, in the west, is quieter. Its name means the harbour of the happy return. A canal connects it to the great lake behind the city, which is full of boats bringing goods from the Nile.

[long-pause]

The sailors of Alexandria are among the best in the world. They know the winds, the currents and the stars. Every summer, a steady north wind blows across the sea toward Egypt. It makes the trip to Alexandria fast and easy, and the trip away from it slow and hard.

[pause]

A little before Cleopatra was born, Greek sailors from Egypt discovered a great secret. In the Indian Ocean, the winds blow one way in summer and the other way in winter. So ships could now sail across the open ocean from the Red Sea to India, and back again within a year. Soon, more than a hundred ships a year sail to India, bringing back pepper, spices, precious stones and cotton.

[pause]

{img:red-sea-port-ships-departing-india-monsoon-desert-coast}
In the evening, as the sun goes down over the Great Harbour, the sailors come ashore. They go to the taverns along the water to eat and drink and tell stories of faraway lands. The ships rock gently at anchor. And the great lighthouse begins to shine.

[long-pause]

{img:library-of-alexandria-scroll-shelves-scholars-reading-colonnade}
In the palace quarter of Alexandria there is a place like no other in the ancient world. It is called the Museum, and it has a great Library.

[pause]

The name Museum means the house of the Muses, the goddesses of the arts and learning. But it is not a museum as we know it. It is more like a university. Scholars, scientists, poets and thinkers from all over the Greek world live there, paid by the king. They get free food, a room and a salary, and they have nothing to do but study, think, write and teach.

[long-pause]

There are lecture halls and a big dining hall where the scholars eat together. There are covered walks and gardens. There is a place to watch the stars, and perhaps a garden of plants from all over the world.

[pause]

And there is the Library, the greatest collection of books in the ancient world.

[pause]

The books are scrolls, long rolls of papyrus, the paper of Egypt. Nobody knows exactly how many there were. The old writers say four hundred thousand, or even seven hundred thousand. Perhaps that is too many. But it was certainly the biggest library that had ever existed.

[long-pause]

The kings wanted to collect every book in the world.

[pause]

They sent buyers all over the Mediterranean. And they made a law that every ship coming into the harbour must be searched for books. Any books found were taken to the Library and copied. The owners got the copies. The Library kept the originals.

[pause]

One king borrowed the official copies of the great Greek plays from Athens. He paid a huge sum of silver as a promise to send them back. Then he kept the originals, sent back copies, and simply let Athens keep the silver.

[long-pause]

The scholars here did wonderful things.

[pause]

A man called Euclid wrote a book about geometry that was used in schools for more than two thousand years. A librarian called Eratosthenes measured the size of the whole earth. He compared the shadows at noon on the same day in two cities, far apart. And he came out almost exactly right. Another scholar suggested that the earth goes around the sun, eighteen hundred years before anyone in Europe believed it.

[pause]

{img:eratosthenes-measuring-shadow-stick-well-noon-sun-sand}
Doctors studied the human body, the brain and the pulse. Engineers invented a water clock and pumps. And scholars compared many old copies of Homer's poems, to find the true words.

[long-pause]

By Cleopatra's time, the greatest days of the Museum are past. But it is still a famous place of learning.

[pause]

Some people say part of the Library burned in 48 before Christ, when Julius Caesar was in Alexandria and fires spread from the harbour. Others say only some storehouses of books near the docks burned. Historians still argue about it. Over the following centuries the Library slowly faded away, and nobody knows exactly when it disappeared.

[pause]

{img:empty-library-hall-afternoon-sunbeam-dust-scrolls-quiet}
But in the year 31, scholars still walk in its halls and read its scrolls. They copy and argue and think, in the quiet rooms by the sea. And they hope that whatever happens in the wider world, their books will be left in peace.

[long-pause]

{img:papyrus-reeds-marsh-delta-tall-stems-umbrella-heads-boat}
In the marshes of the Delta grows a tall, graceful plant. Its green stem is as tall as two men, and on top it has a head of fine green threads, like an umbrella. It is papyrus.

[pause]

For three thousand years, the Egyptians have used it for almost everything. For boats and baskets, mats and sandals and rope. They even chew the sweet inside of the stem. And above all, they use it to make paper.

[long-pause]

To make a sheet of papyrus, workers cut the stems and peel off the green skin. They slice the white inside into long, thin strips. They lay the strips side by side, and then lay another layer across them. Then they beat the layers with a wooden hammer and press them under a weight. The plant's own sap glues them together. When they are dry, they rub them smooth with a stone.

[pause]

The sheets are glued end to end to make a long roll, several metres long. Our word paper comes from papyrus.

[pause]

Egypt is the only country that makes it. It sells papyrus all over the Mediterranean, and the kings grow rich from it.

[long-pause]

The Egyptians write on papyrus with reed pens and black ink made of soot. They use red ink for headings.

[pause]

There are three kinds of Egyptian writing. Hieroglyphs are the beautiful picture writing carved on temples. Then there is a quicker form for religious texts. And there is a very quick, joined-up writing for everyday things, like letters and accounts.

[pause]

{img:scribe-sitting-cross-legged-reed-pen-papyrus-roll-palette}
In the dry sands of Egypt, papyrus does not rot. So in the old rubbish heaps of ancient towns, archaeologists have found hundreds of thousands of pieces of papyrus. They tell us about the lives of ordinary people. Their letters and shopping lists, their tax receipts and their complaints.

[long-pause]

There is a letter from a boy to his father, complaining that his father did not take him to Alexandria. If you will not take me, he writes, I will not eat and I will not drink. So there.

[pause]

There is a wedding invitation. There is a letter from a wife asking her husband to come home. There is a receipt for buying a donkey.

[pause]

{img:papyrus-fragments-greek-letters-glass-museum-close-light}
And there may even be one word written by Cleopatra herself. On a royal order from two years before our story, at the bottom, in a different hand, someone has written one Greek word. It means: let it be done. Some scholars believe this is Cleopatra's own handwriting. If they are right, it is the only thing we have that she wrote with her own hand.

[long-pause]

{img:alexandria-dawn-flat-roofs-palms-sea-mist-temple-pylons}
Let us spend an ordinary day in Alexandria in the time of Cleopatra, with an ordinary family.

[pause]

The father is a Greek glassmaker with a little workshop in the city. Alexandria is famous for its glass. Beautiful bottles, bowls and beads in blue, green, purple and amber are sold all over the Mediterranean.

[long-pause]

The family lives in a house of mud brick, plastered and painted, two or three floors high, on a narrow street near a canal. On the ground floor are the workshop and the kitchen. Above are the rooms where the family lives and sleeps. On the flat roof there is a terrace, where they sit in the cool of the evening. On the hottest nights they sleep up there under the stars.

[pause]

The day begins at dawn. The sun rises red and gold over the Delta, and the city wakes up. Birds sing in the palm trees. Donkeys bray. The water carriers come down the street with their jars of water from the canal.

[pause]

{img:water-carrier-donkey-jars-narrow-street-morning-alexandria}
The mother, the daughters and the slave woman make breakfast. There is fresh bread from the clay oven in the courtyard, a little cheese, dates and figs, and some beer, or water mixed with wine.

[long-pause]

The father goes down to the workshop, where the furnace is already hot.

[pause]

Glass is made from sand, soda and lime, melted together in the great heat of the furnace until it glows. In these very years, a wonderful new trick is spreading. A glassmaker takes a little blob of hot glass on the end of a long hollow pipe, and blows into it. The glass swells like a bubble. He turns it and shapes it quickly, before it cools, into a bottle or a cup. It is much faster than the old ways. Soon glass will be cheap enough for ordinary people.

[pause]

In the afternoon, the father goes to the baths and then to the sports ground. There he talks with his friends about business and politics, about the queen and about the Romans. Lately, the talk is mostly about the Romans.

[long-pause]

The mother runs the household. She goes to the market with a slave to buy fish from the sea and the lake, and onions, lettuce, lentils and fruit. She spins and weaves fine white linen for the family's clothes.

[pause]

The children go to school, or play in the street and on the roof. They have clay dolls, little wooden horses on wheels, balls, spinning tops, and pet birds and cats.

[pause]

{img:children-playing-flat-roof-cats-toys-palm-trees-afternoon}
Cats are everywhere in Egypt, and they are loved and respected. Egyptians believe cats are sacred to a goddess. Harming a cat is a terrible crime. A Greek visitor in these years once saw a crowd attack a Roman who had accidentally killed a cat in the street. Nothing could save him, not even the king's officials.

[long-pause]

In the evening, the family gathers on the roof for supper. The sun sets over the lake in a blaze of orange and red, and the lighthouse begins to shine out at sea.

[pause]

A cool breeze comes in from the sea. The stars come out, bright and close. From the street below come the sounds of evening. People talking and laughing, music from a tavern, a dog barking.

[pause]

{img:family-supper-rooftop-sunset-lake-lighthouse-lamps-calm}
Then the family lies down to sleep on the roof, on mats and cushions, under a light linen sheet. Above them shine the stars. Far away they hear the sea, and see the faint glow of the great lighthouse on the horizon.

[long-pause]

{img:egyptian-bakery-women-grinding-grain-flat-loaves-clay-oven}
What do the people of Egypt eat and drink in the time of Cleopatra?

[pause]

The most important food is bread. The Egyptians bake many kinds. Flat round loaves, tall pointed loaves, loaves shaped like animals, and sweet breads with honey and dates. The women grind the grain on stones. A little sand from the stones often gets into the bread, and over the years it wears down people's teeth.

[long-pause]

The second most important food is beer. Egyptians drink it every day, men, women and children. It is thick, cloudy and filling, made from barley bread soaked in water and left to bubble. It is not very strong. Workers are often paid partly in bread and beer.

[pause]

The Greeks of Alexandria prefer wine. Some comes from vineyards around the lake behind the city, and its white wine is famous. Some comes from Greece.

[pause]

{img:market-stall-onions-leeks-garlic-cucumbers-melons-figs-baskets}
The Egyptians eat lots of vegetables. Onions, leeks and garlic, which the Greeks say Egyptians love more than anything. Lettuce, cucumbers, beans and lentils. And fruit, like dates, figs, grapes, pomegranates and melons.

[long-pause]

They eat fish from the Nile, the lakes and the sea, fresh or dried in the sun. They catch ducks, geese and quails in the marshes with nets. Meat is more expensive. It is eaten mostly by the rich, and at festivals.

[pause]

They cook with oil, olive oil for the rich and sesame oil for ordinary people. They sweeten food with honey and dates, and season it with salt and herbs like cumin and mint.

[pause]

{img:greek-banquet-alexandria-couches-fish-dishes-wine-garlands}
The rich Greeks of Alexandria hold great feasts in the Greek style, lying on couches. There are many dishes. Fish and oysters, prawns and roast meat, pastries and cakes, fruit and wine. There are garlands of flowers, perfumes, music and dancers. And at the palace, the feasts are the grandest of all.

[long-pause]

And everyone, rich and poor, loves the sweets of Egypt. Honey cakes, dates stuffed with nuts, and fig cakes.

[pause]

In the evenings, little food stalls line the streets of Alexandria. They sell hot bread, roasted chickpeas, fried fish, lentil soup and beer to people walking home. The smell of baking bread and roasting fish drifts through the warm evening air, along with voices, music and laughter.

[long-pause]

{img:nile-river-wide-sails-feluccas-palms-temple-golden-afternoon}
Everything in Egypt depends on the Nile.

[pause]

A Greek historian once said that Egypt is the gift of the Nile. Without the river, Egypt would be desert. With it, Egypt is one of the richest farming lands in the world.

[long-pause]

Every summer, the Nile floods.

[pause]

Far to the south, in the mountains of Ethiopia, heavy summer rains fall. The rivers swell, and the water flows north into the Nile and down into Egypt. In July the river begins to rise. In August and September it spills over its banks and spreads across the fields. The whole land becomes a great lake, with the villages and temples and palm groves standing up out of the water like islands.

[pause]

The flood brings water, and it brings rich black mud from the mountains. The mud settles on the fields and makes them fertile, year after year. The Egyptians call their country the black land, because of this black soil. The desert they call the red land.

[long-pause]

The Egyptians measure the height of the flood carefully every year. They use stone staircases beside the river, with marks on the walls.

[pause]

If the flood is too low, there will not be enough water for the fields, and people will go hungry. If it is too high, it will wash away villages. If it is just right, there will be a good harvest, and everyone will be happy.

[pause]

Cleopatra watches the flood anxiously every year. In some of the years before 31 the floods were bad, and there was hunger. Then she opened the royal storehouses and gave out grain to the people. Every summer the question is the same, and nobody can answer it until the water rises. Will this be a good year?

[long-pause]

In October and November the water goes down, and the fields appear again, covered in black mud. The farmers sow wheat and barley, flax for linen, beans and onions. The crops grow through the mild winter. In spring they are harvested.

[pause]

Then, in summer, the land is dry, and everyone waits for the river to rise again. It is a cycle that has repeated every year for thousands of years.

[pause]

{img:farmers-sowing-seed-black-mud-fields-oxen-november-light}
The Nile is also the great road of Egypt. Going north, boats drift with the current. Going south, they put up their sails, because in Egypt the wind almost always blows from the north. So it is easy to travel both ways. In the old picture writing, going north is a boat with its sail down, and going south is a boat with its sail up.

[long-pause]

On the river there are boats of every kind. Little reed boats and fishing boats, ferries and big barges loaded with grain and stone. And the golden barges of the kings and the gods.

[pause]

Along the banks grow palm trees, reeds and papyrus. There are ibises and herons and kingfishers. Crocodiles sun themselves on the sandbanks. In the evening, hippos wallow in the shallows. And farmers lift water from the river to their fields with a long pole, with a bucket on one end and a weight on the other.

[pause]

{img:shaduf-farmer-raising-water-riverbank-ibis-reeds-morning}
It is a scene that has hardly changed for thousands of years. In some places, it still has not changed today.

[long-pause]

{img:travellers-boat-nile-pyramids-giza-distance-palms-afternoon}
Even in the time of Cleopatra, Egypt was full of tourists.

[pause]

Greeks, Romans and other visitors came to see its ancient wonders, which were already thousands of years old. They sailed up the Nile from Alexandria. Local guides showed them the old cities, temples and pyramids, and told them stories. Some of the stories were true, and some were not.

[long-pause]

The first great sight, a few days up the river, was Memphis, the ancient capital of Egypt. There was a famous sacred bull there, which visitors could watch and feed in its stall.

[pause]

And then, at the edge of the desert, the pyramids of Giza.

[pause]

{img:great-pyramid-smooth-white-casing-stones-giza-sphinx-ancient}
In Cleopatra's time, the great pyramids still have their outer skin of smooth, white, polished stone. It shines in the sun so brightly that it hurts the eyes. Their tops are still sharp. One Greek visitor said the guides read him the writing on a pyramid. It listed, they told him, how many onions and radishes the builders had eaten. The guides were probably making it up.

[long-pause]

The Great Sphinx lies in front of them, half buried in sand that blows in from the desert, and must be cleared away now and then.

[pause]

The visitors were amazed. The pyramids were counted among the Seven Wonders of the World. They are the only one of the seven that still stands today. Some visitors climbed them. Some carved their names on them, as tourists always have.

[pause]

{img:colossi-of-memnon-two-great-statues-thebes-plain-dawn-visitors}
Further south, at Thebes, were the great temples with forests of huge columns, and the painted tombs of the kings, which visitors could already enter.

[pause]

And there were two enormous seated statues of a pharaoh on the plain. An earthquake had cracked one of them. And every morning at sunrise, as the stone warmed in the sun, it made a strange, soft singing sound, like a harp string breaking. The Greeks believed it was a hero greeting his mother, the goddess of the dawn. Visitors came from everywhere to hear it. They carved poems on its legs, which can still be read today.

[long-pause]

Those who travelled even further south could see the great stone quarries where obelisks were cut. And they could see the beautiful island of Philae, with its temple of Isis, among palm trees, rocks and rushing water.

[pause]

So for the people of Cleopatra's time, as for us, Egypt was already an ancient land of wonders. A land of gods and kings and mysteries, which they came to see, and remembered all their lives.

[long-pause]

{img:nile-wildlife-ibis-herons-hippos-crocodile-sandbank-dawn}
In the time of Cleopatra, the Nile is full of life.

[pause]

In the shallow water among the reeds live hippos. They are huge, heavy, grey animals. They spend the day in the water, with only their eyes, ears and nostrils showing. At night they come out to graze on the riverbanks, and sometimes in the farmers' fields, to the farmers' great annoyance.

[long-pause]

On the sandbanks, crocodiles lie in the sun with their mouths open to cool down. Little birds walk in and out of their mouths, picking food from their teeth. The Egyptians fear crocodiles, but also respect them. At one temple, sacred crocodiles are kept in pools. The priests feed them bread, meat and honey cakes. The crocodiles even wear golden earrings and bracelets on their front legs. Greek tourists come to watch them being fed, from a safe distance.

[pause]

{img:sacred-crocodile-temple-pool-priests-feeding-golden-earrings}
There are birds everywhere. The white ibis walks slowly through the fields with its long curved beak. There are herons, storks and pelicans, and kingfishers flashing blue over the water. In winter, thousands of ducks and geese come from the north to the marshes of the Delta.

[long-pause]

In the papyrus marshes there are wild cats, otters and mongooses. At the edge of the desert there are jackals, foxes, hares and gazelles.

[pause]

And in every house there are cats, the beloved, sacred cats of Egypt. They sleep in the sun on the roofs and doorsteps, and catch mice in the grain stores. There are dogs, and donkeys carrying loads along the dusty paths. Some people even keep baboons as pets, and train them to pick figs from the trees.

[pause]

{img:cat-sleeping-sunny-doorstep-egyptian-village-donkey-palm}
The Egyptians love their animals. They paint them, carve them and worship them. They even preserve and bury them in great animal cemeteries, by the million. Visitors could buy a little preserved animal at a temple, as a gift to the god.

[pause]

For the Egyptians, the animals of the Nile were part of the great order of the world. The gods had made that order. And it had to be kept in balance, like the rising and falling of the river.

[long-pause]

{img:egyptian-village-mud-brick-houses-palms-dovecotes-fields-river}
Most people in Egypt in Cleopatra's time do not live in Alexandria. They live in villages along the Nile, as their ancestors did for thousands of years. They are farmers. They speak Egyptian, worship the old gods, and live much as people did in the time of the pyramids.

[long-pause]

A village is a cluster of little mud-brick houses on a mound, above the level of the flood, among palm trees. The houses have thick walls, small windows and flat roofs. On the roofs the family stores fuel and grain, dries fish, and sleeps in summer.

[pause]

Inside it is cool and dim. There are a few rooms with earth floors and mats, a few stools, and pottery jars for water, grain and beer. In a niche in the wall stands a little figure of a household god. Often it is Bes, a funny little god who protects mothers and children.

[pause]

Outside, in the courtyard, there is an oven for baking bread. There is a place for a donkey, a few goats, some ducks and geese, and pigeons in tall mud towers.

[long-pause]

Around the village lie the fields and the little canals that bring water to them. Before each flood, the villagers must work together to repair the earth banks. If the banks break, the water could wash away the village.

[pause]

The farmers work hard all year. They plough with oxen and sow by hand. In spring they cut the wheat and barley with sickles. They spread it on a hard floor, where cattle walk over it to loosen the grain. Then they throw it into the wind, so the light husks blow away and the grain falls back. Officials come to measure the harvest and take the queen's share.

[pause]

{img:harvest-wheat-sickles-scribe-measuring-grain-heap-field-sun}
Most land belongs to the crown, the temples or rich landowners. The farmers rent it and pay with part of their harvest. Life is hard and the taxes are heavy. But in a good year, there is enough to eat.

[long-pause]

The village women work too. They help in the fields at harvest. At home they grind grain, bake bread, brew beer and weave linen. They carry water from the river in jars on their heads.

[pause]

Egyptian women have more rights than women in most ancient lands. They can own and inherit property, buy and sell, go to court, and even divorce their husbands. Many marriage contracts on papyrus survive from this time. They carefully list what the wife brought into the marriage, and what she will get back if it ends.

[pause]

{img:women-carrying-water-jars-heads-riverbank-evening-children}
The children help from a young age. They herd the goats and geese and scare birds away from the crops. They play in the river and the canals, with clay toys and balls of rags, and swim like fish.

[long-pause]

In the evening, the farmers come home as the sun sets behind the western desert. The Egyptians believe the west is the land of the dead. The sky turns red and gold and purple.

[pause]

The families eat supper in the courtyard or on the roof. Bread and onions, beans and fish, and beer. Donkeys bray, dogs bark and frogs croak in the canals. Then the village goes to sleep under the bright stars of Egypt. Far away in Alexandria, great decisions are being made. But here, life goes on as it always has.

[long-pause]

{img:western-desert-dunes-oasis-palms-spring-camels-evening}
Beyond the green valley of the Nile, on both sides, lies the desert.

[pause]

To the west stretches the great Western Desert, part of the Sahara. It goes on for more than a thousand kilometres. There is sand and stone, dunes and flat plains of gravel, and strange rocks carved by the wind into mushrooms and towers. To the east, between the Nile and the Red Sea, there are rocky mountains and dry valleys, where gold and fine stone are mined.

[long-pause]

The desert is not completely empty. Here and there, water rises from deep underground in springs and wells. Around them grow palm trees, and there are villages and fields and orchards, right in the middle of the sand. These places are called oases.

[pause]

The most famous is the oasis of Siwa, far out in the desert. It has lakes, palm groves and a temple of the god Ammon, whose oracle was famous all over the Greek world. Three hundred years before Cleopatra, Alexander the Great made the long, dangerous journey across the desert to ask the oracle a question. The priest greeted him as the son of the god. Nobody knows what else the oracle told him. Alexander never told anyone.

[pause]

{img:siwa-oasis-temple-ruins-palm-groves-salt-lake-sunset}
There are animals in the desert too. Gazelles and wild goats, little foxes with huge ears, jackals, ostriches, lizards and scorpions. And there are desert people with their camels and goats and tents. They travel from oasis to oasis, and know the desert's secret wells.

[long-pause]

{img:desert-night-stars-dunes-cold-clear-silence-milky-way}
The Egyptians fear the desert a little. To them it is the red land, the land of the god of storms and chaos. But they also love its silence and beauty. The golden dunes at sunset. The cold, clear nights with more stars than anyone could count. The strange rocks and the pure, dry air.

[pause]

A few hundred years after Cleopatra, the first Christian monks will go out into the Egyptian desert. They will live alone in caves and huts, praying in the silence. And there they will found the first monasteries in the world.

[long-pause]

{img:dendera-temple-hathor-columns-colourful-ceiling-light-shafts}
Egypt in Cleopatra's time is full of temples, and full of gods.

[pause]

The Egyptians have hundreds of gods. Ra, the sun god, sails across the sky every day in his boat. Osiris is the god of the dead and of the crops that grow again each year. Isis, his wife, is the great mother goddess, of magic and healing and love. Horus, their son, is a falcon god who protects the king. Hathor is the cow goddess of joy and music. Anubis, the jackal god, guides the dead. And Thoth, the ibis god, is the god of wisdom and writing.

[long-pause]

Many gods are shown as animals, or with animal heads. The Egyptians believe gods can live in animals. The cat belongs to one goddess, the ibis to Thoth, the falcon to Horus, the crocodile to another god.

[pause]

At Memphis there is a living sacred bull. It is black, with special white markings. It lives in a temple in great comfort and is worshipped as the living image of a god. When it dies, it is preserved and buried in a great underground tomb. Then the priests search all of Egypt for a new bull with exactly the right markings.

[pause]

{img:apis-bull-temple-courtyard-priests-garlands-memphis-calm}
At the very start of her reign, Cleopatra herself sailed up the Nile to welcome a new sacred bull in another city. An old inscription says she rowed the sacred boat herself, and all the people saw her.

[long-pause]

Her family built many great temples in the old Egyptian style along the Nile. Some of them are among the best preserved temples in Egypt.

[pause]

At Dendera there is the great temple of Hathor. Its tall columns are topped with the face of the goddess. Its ceiling is painted with the stars in blue and gold. And on the back wall, outside, there is a huge carving of Cleopatra and her son, making offerings to the gods.

[pause]

At Edfu stands the great temple of Horus, almost complete, with its huge gateway and its dark, mysterious inner room. And on an island in the far south stands the lovely temple of Isis, surrounded by the river and palm trees.

[long-pause]

Ordinary people do not go inside to worship. The temples are the houses of the gods, and only priests may enter the inner rooms.

[pause]

Every morning, in every temple, the priests wake the god. They open the doors of the shrine where the statue lives. They wash it, dress it in fine linen and rub it with scented oil. They offer it food, drink, incense and flowers, and sing hymns. In the evening, they close the shrine again, and the god sleeps.

[pause]

{img:priests-white-linen-shaved-heads-incense-sanctuary-candles}
The priests wear white linen and shave their heads and bodies, to be pure. They wash several times a day, and do not eat certain foods, like fish.

[long-pause]

But on festival days, the god comes out. The statue rides in a golden boat, carried on the priests' shoulders, in a great procession with music and dancing and incense. The people line the streets and the riverbanks to see the god pass, and to pray.

[pause]

Once a year, the statue of Hathor sails up the Nile in her golden boat to visit her husband Horus, in his temple many days away. Crowds of pilgrims follow her, feasting and celebrating all along the way.

[pause]

{img:golden-sacred-boat-procession-nile-crowds-music-festival}
Of all the gods, the most loved in Cleopatra's time is Isis. She is the faithful wife who brought her husband back to life, and the loving mother who protected her baby son. Her worship is spreading all over the Mediterranean, even to Rome. And Cleopatra presents herself to her people as the new Isis, the living goddess on earth. Now she will need all the help a goddess can give.

[long-pause]

{img:painted-coffin-portrait-tomb-chapel-flowers-quiet-light}
In Cleopatra's time, the Egyptians still believe, as their ancestors did for three thousand years, that life goes on after death.

[pause]

They believe every person has more than one soul. One soul is the life force, which needs food and drink. Another is shown as a bird with a human head. It can fly out of the tomb in the daytime, visit the living, and come back at night. If the body is kept safe, and the right prayers are said, and the person lived a good life, they will live forever in the Field of Reeds. That is a beautiful land like Egypt, but even better, where the crops grow tall and there is no sorrow.

[long-pause]

In the old story, the jackal god leads the dead person into the hall of judgement. There the heart is weighed on a great scale against a feather, the feather of truth. If the heart is light and pure, the person may pass into the next life. And before the gods, the dead person must say: I have not done wrong. I have not stolen. I have not lied. I have not made anyone weep. I have not let anyone go hungry.

[pause]

So the Egyptians try to live kind and honest lives, so that at the end their hearts will be light.

[pause]

{img:weighing-of-heart-scale-feather-anubis-papyrus-painting-calm}
To keep the body safe, the Egyptians make mummies, as they have for thousands of years. The body is dried with a special salt from desert lakes. It is rubbed with oils and spices and wrapped in many layers of fine linen, with little lucky charms tucked between them. Then it is laid in a painted coffin and buried in a tomb, with food and drink and the things the person will need in the next life.

[long-pause]

Even the Greeks and Romans living in Egypt took up this custom. In the centuries after Cleopatra, many of them were made into mummies too, in the Egyptian way. Over the face, they placed a portrait of the person, painted on a wooden board. These portraits are so lifelike that they seem to look at us across two thousand years. Hundreds of them can be seen in museums today. Men and women, young and old, even children, with dark eyes, jewellery and their own hairstyles, gazing out calmly as if they were alive.

[pause]

{img:fayum-style-portrait-young-woman-dark-eyes-gold-earrings-wood}
Families visited the tombs of their loved ones on festival days. They brought food and flowers and had picnics by the tombs. They talked to their dead and remembered them. For the Egyptians, the dead were not gone. They were only living somewhere else.

[long-pause]

{img:caesar-arriving-alexandria-harbour-48bc-galleys-crowds-palace}
In the autumn of 48 before Christ, Julius Caesar, the greatest general in Rome, sailed into Alexandria with a small army. He was chasing his rival Pompey, whom he had beaten in a Roman civil war.

[pause]

But Pompey was already dead. When he landed on the coast of Egypt a few days before, asking for help, the advisers of the young king had taken his life on the beach. They thought it would please Caesar. When Caesar heard what they had done, it is said, he turned away and wept.

[long-pause]

Caesar decided to stay in the palace and settle the quarrel between Cleopatra and her brother, which was tearing Egypt apart. He sent for both of them.

[pause]

But Cleopatra was outside the city with her army. Her brother's soldiers guarded the roads and the palace gates. How could she reach Caesar without being caught?

[pause]

An old writer tells the story. At dusk, she sailed into the harbour in a small boat with just one friend. Since there was no other way to get in unseen, she lay down inside a big sack for bedding. Her friend tied it up and carried it on his back, right through the gates and into Caesar's room. There he set it down and untied it. And out stepped the queen.

[pause]

Later stories said it was a carpet. But the old writer says it was a bedding sack.

[long-pause]

Caesar was fifty-two. Cleopatra was twenty-one. He was charmed by her courage, her cleverness and her wit. And they fell in love.

[pause]

Caesar decided that Cleopatra and her brother should rule together, as their father had wished. But the brother's advisers refused. The people of Alexandria rose up. For months, through the winter, the Egyptian army surrounded Caesar and Cleopatra in the palace.

[pause]

{img:palace-under-siege-quiet-night-walls-torches-harbour-distant}
In the end, Roman help arrived. The young king drowned in the Nile while fleeing after a battle. Cleopatra became queen again. This time she ruled with another, even younger brother, who was about twelve.

[long-pause]

And then, in the spring of 47, Caesar and Cleopatra are said to have sailed up the Nile together on the royal barge. It was a floating palace, with dining halls, bedrooms and gardens, rowed by hundreds of oarsmen, with many ships following behind.

[pause]

They sailed south past the pyramids and the temples, the green fields and the palm groves. Some historians doubt the journey happened, or think it was much shorter. But it is one of the great love stories of history.

[pause]

{img:royal-nile-barge-floating-palace-sunset-temples-banks-fleet}
That summer, after Caesar had left to fight more wars, Cleopatra gave birth to a son. The people of Alexandria called him Caesarion, which means little Caesar.

[long-pause]

Two years later, Cleopatra travelled to Rome with her little son, to visit Caesar. She stayed in his villa across the river. The Romans were fascinated by her, but also suspicious, and they gossiped about her endlessly.

[pause]

She was still in Rome on the Ides of March, in 44 before Christ, when Caesar was killed in the Senate. Suddenly her great protector was gone. Soon afterwards she went home to Alexandria with her son, who was three. A few months later her young brother died. She made little Caesarion her fellow ruler. Now she had to protect Egypt on her own.

[long-pause]

{img:golden-barge-purple-sails-silver-oars-cydnus-river-tarsus-41bc}
After Caesar's death, there was civil war in Rome again. When it ended, the Roman world was divided between two men. Octavian, Caesar's young adopted son, ruled the west. Mark Antony, Caesar's old friend and general, ruled the east.

[pause]

In the summer of 41 before Christ, Antony was in the city of Tarsus, in what is now Turkey. He ordered Cleopatra to come to him, and explain why she had not helped him in the war.

[long-pause]

She came, but in her own way, and in her own time.

[pause]

An old writer describes her arrival in one of the most famous passages of ancient times. She sailed up the river to Tarsus in a barge with a golden stern and purple sails. Its silver oars kept time to the music of flutes and harps. She lay under a canopy of golden cloth, dressed as Venus, the goddess of love. Young boys dressed as cupids stood beside her, fanning her. Her maids, dressed as sea nymphs, steered the boat.

[pause]

{img:queen-under-gold-canopy-barge-cupids-fanning-maids-ropes}
Sweet smells of incense drifted from the ship to the riverbanks, where great crowds gathered. And word went round that the goddess of love had come to feast with the god of wine.

[pause]

Sixteen hundred years later, Shakespeare put this scene almost word for word into his play about them. The barge she sat in, he wrote, like a polished throne, burned on the water.

[long-pause]

Antony invited her to dinner. She invited him to dine on her ship instead. He came, and he was dazzled. By thousands of lamps hung in patterns, by golden plates and jewelled cups, and by the queen.

[pause]

Mark Antony was about forty-two. He was big, strong, handsome and cheerful, with curly hair and a beard. He liked to think he looked like the hero Hercules. He was a brave soldier, loved by his men, generous, and fond of feasts and laughter.

[pause]

He fell in love with Cleopatra. That winter he went with her to Alexandria, and stayed there with her, instead of going back to Rome. In Rome, people noticed. And Octavian noticed most of all.

[long-pause]

In Alexandria, Antony and Cleopatra lived a life of pleasure and fun.

[pause]

They started a club of friends who feasted together every day. A doctor who was studying in Alexandria at the time later told a story about it. He once visited the royal kitchens and saw eight wild boars roasting at once. He thought there must be a huge party. But the cook laughed. There were only about twelve guests, he said. But nobody knew exactly when Antony would want to eat. So they roasted the boars one after another, so that one would always be perfect, whenever he asked.

[pause]

{img:royal-kitchen-alexandria-many-spits-cooks-busy-firelight}
They wandered the streets at night dressed as servants, knocking on doors and playing tricks. They went fishing together. Once, the story goes, Antony caught nothing. So he secretly had a diver fasten fish onto his hook. Cleopatra saw through the trick. The next day, she had a diver fasten an old salted fish onto his hook. When he pulled it up, everyone laughed. And Cleopatra said: leave fishing to us. Your catch is cities and kingdoms.

[long-pause]

In the spring of 40, Antony went back to Rome. To make peace with Octavian, he married Octavian's sister, a good and gentle woman. For three years, he did not see Cleopatra.

[pause]

Meanwhile, in Alexandria, Cleopatra gave birth to twins, a boy and a girl. She named them after the sun and the moon.

[pause]

{img:queen-with-twin-babies-nursery-palace-alexandria-sea-light}
But in 37, Antony came back to the East and sent for Cleopatra again. From then on, they stayed together. In 36 they had another son. And in Rome, Octavian waited, and watched.

[long-pause]

{img:palace-throne-room-alexandria-greek-egyptian-decor-courtiers-light}
What was life like in the palace in Alexandria in Cleopatra's time?

[pause]

The palace was a world of its own. It was a great group of buildings by the sea, full of people. There was the royal family with their children, nurses and teachers. There were the courtiers with their purple robes and gold. There were ministers, generals and writers, guards and doctors, star-watchers and scholars, cooks, servants, musicians and dancers.

[long-pause]

The rooms were splendid, in a mix of Greek and Egyptian styles. There were marble floors and mosaics, walls of coloured stone, and columns of granite and ebony. There were gardens with fountains and shady walks, pools, rare plants, and birds and animals from Africa and India.

[pause]

A Roman poet described a feast Cleopatra gave for Caesar. The roof was covered with gold, he wrote, and the walls with marble and ebony. The doors were decorated with tortoiseshell and emeralds. The couches were covered in purple and gold.

[pause]

{img:queen-reading-reports-scribes-ministers-morning-palace-office}
But Cleopatra was not lazy. Her days were full of work. Every morning she met her ministers and read reports from all over Egypt, about the harvest, the flood, the taxes and the grain stores. She received ambassadors and spoke to them in their own languages. She settled disputes and signed orders. She ran one of the richest countries in the world.

[long-pause]

And she was a mother of four children, who lived with her in the palace. Caesarion had a famous Greek tutor. The twins and their little brother were taught by a scholar who later wrote a history of the world.

[pause]

In the evenings there were feasts, music, plays and poetry, and conversation with scholars and visitors.

[pause]

{img:palace-terrace-evening-sea-view-children-mother-lamps-calm}
And sometimes, perhaps, Cleopatra walked alone in the evening on the terrace of her palace by the sea. She looked out over the harbour at the lighthouse shining on the dark water, and at the ships coming and going. And she thought about her children, and her country, and the young man in Rome who was growing more powerful every year.

[long-pause]

{img:banquet-hall-queen-pearl-earring-cup-vinegar-guests-watching}
There is a famous story, told by a Roman writer, about a bet between Antony and Cleopatra.

[pause]

Cleopatra bet Antony that she could spend an enormous fortune on a single dinner. Antony did not believe her. So she gave a feast. It was splendid, but not much more splendid than usual. Antony laughed and asked how much it had cost.

[long-pause]

Then Cleopatra asked for the second course. The servants set down in front of her one single cup, filled with vinegar.

[pause]

In her ears she wore two pearls, the largest in the world. She took one from her ear and dropped it into the vinegar. When it had dissolved, she drank it. She was about to do the same with the other one, when the judge of the bet stopped her, and said she had won.

[pause]

Would vinegar really dissolve a pearl? Scientists have tried it. Strong vinegar can slowly dissolve a pearl, especially if it is crushed first. Perhaps Cleopatra knew that. Or perhaps it is only a story. The writer says the second pearl was later cut in two and made into earrings for a statue of Venus in Rome.

[long-pause]

In 34 before Christ, Antony held a great ceremony in Alexandria.

[pause]

In the great sports hall of the city, in front of a huge crowd, there was a silver platform with two golden thrones for Antony and Cleopatra, and smaller thrones for the children. Cleopatra was dressed as the goddess Isis.

[pause]

{img:silver-platform-golden-thrones-royal-family-gymnasium-crowd}
Antony declared Cleopatra the Queen of Kings. He called Caesarion, now thirteen, the King of Kings and the true son of Julius Caesar. And he gave kingdoms to the children. To little Alexander, lands in the east, some of which had not even been conquered yet. To his twin sister, lands in North Africa. And to little Ptolemy, only two, Syria and lands along the coast.

[pause]

The little boys were dressed in the costumes of the lands they would rule. One wore a tall eastern hat. The other wore boots and a cloak like the old Macedonian kings.

[long-pause]

In Rome, this caused a scandal. Octavian used it to turn the Roman people against Antony. Antony has been bewitched by the Egyptian queen, he said. He is giving away Roman lands to her and her children. He wants to make Alexandria the capital of the world, instead of Rome.

[pause]

Octavian found Antony's will, which was kept by priestesses in Rome. He read it aloud to the Senate. In it, Antony asked to be buried in Alexandria beside Cleopatra, even if he died in Rome. The Romans were furious.

[pause]

{img:roman-senate-reading-document-senators-murmuring-candles}
In 32 before Christ, Rome declared war. Not on Antony, but on Cleopatra. And everyone knew that a great war was about to begin. One that would decide who ruled the whole Roman world.

[long-pause]

In Alexandria, life went on much as before. People went to the markets, the baths, the theatre and the races. Ships came and went. Scholars read in the Library. Along the Nile, the farmers watched the river rise and fall, paid their taxes, and hoped for a good harvest. They paid little attention to the quarrels of the great.

[pause]

But in the palace by the sea, Cleopatra and Antony began to prepare their fleet, their army and their treasure for the war that was coming.

[long-pause]

{img:actium-bay-ambracian-gulf-fleets-anchored-greek-coast-summer}
In the summer of 31 before Christ, two great fleets face each other off the west coast of Greece. The place is called Actium, at the mouth of a wide bay.

[pause]

Antony and Cleopatra have gathered a great army and more than two hundred warships. Many are huge, with towers on their decks. Cleopatra has brought sixty ships of her own from Egypt, and the treasure of Egypt to pay for the war. She is there herself, on her own flagship.

[long-pause]

Octavian's fleet is led by his friend Agrippa, a brilliant admiral. His ships are smaller, lighter and faster, and his sailors have more experience.

[pause]

All summer long, Antony's army and fleet are trapped in the bay, blocked in by Agrippa. Their camp lies in a marshy, unhealthy place. Many soldiers and rowers fall ill. Food runs short. Some of their friends slip away and join Octavian. Every week, their position gets worse.

[pause]

At last, Antony and Cleopatra decide they must break out of the bay and sail back to Egypt.

[long-pause]

On the second of September, 31 before Christ, the fleets meet at the mouth of the bay.

[pause]

We will watch from far away, as always. From a hill on the shore, we look down at the blue sea on a calm, bright September morning. Hundreds of ships are moving slowly in long lines, their oars rising and falling. From up here they look small and far away, like toys on a pond.

[pause]

For hours the two fleets face each other, waiting. Then, around midday, the wind rises, as it does every day in that place. And the battle begins far out on the water.

[long-pause]

Then, in the middle of the afternoon, something happens that people will argue about for two thousand years.

[pause]

Cleopatra's sixty ships, waiting behind the battle lines, suddenly raise their sails. They sail straight through a gap in the middle of the fighting, out to the open sea, south toward Egypt. And Antony, seeing her go, leaves his flagship, climbs into a smaller, faster ship, and follows her.

[pause]

{img:purple-sails-ships-sailing-away-open-sea-afternoon-wind}
Was it a betrayal, a panic, or a plan? Some historians think it was their plan all along. Break out with the treasure and as many ships as possible, and fight again in Egypt. Others think Cleopatra lost her nerve, or Antony did. We will never know.

[long-pause]

Most of Antony's fleet, left behind, surrendered to Octavian. A few days later, his great army on the shore, still waiting for him to come back, surrendered too.

[pause]

Antony caught up with Cleopatra's ship and was taken aboard. The old writer says he went and sat alone at the front of the ship, with his head in his hands, for three days, without speaking to her. Then her maids persuaded them to talk, and eat together. And they sailed on together to Egypt.

[pause]

{img:man-sitting-alone-ship-bow-head-in-hands-sea-evening}
When she reached Alexandria, Cleopatra sailed into the harbour with her ships decorated with flowers, as if she had won. She did not want the people to rise against her before she was ready.

[pause]

But they both knew that Octavian would come.

[long-pause]

{img:alexandria-palace-sea-evening-quiet-lamps-autumn-30bc}
The last year of Cleopatra's life, from the autumn of 31 to the summer of 30 before Christ, is a strange, sad and beautiful time.

[pause]

She does not give up. She makes plans. She has some of her ships dragged over land to the Red Sea. Perhaps she hopes to escape with her children and her treasure to India or Arabia. But desert people burn the ships.

[long-pause]

She sends messages to Octavian, asking him to let her children rule Egypt. He does not answer, or answers coldly.

[pause]

Meanwhile, in Alexandria, Antony and Cleopatra close their old club of friends and start a new one. They call it the Partners in Death, the friends who will die together. All winter long they hold feasts and parties, with music and dancing every night, as if there were no tomorrow.

[pause]

{img:evening-feast-palace-hall-friends-lamps-music-quiet-sadness}
Caesarion, now sixteen, is welcomed as a grown young man in a great ceremony. The people of Alexandria feast for days. Cleopatra wants them to see that Egypt has a young king who can rule after her.

[long-pause]

She also builds a tomb for herself. It is a great stone building next to a temple of Isis, by the sea, near the palace. She has all her greatest treasures carried into it. Gold and silver, pearls and emeralds, ebony and ivory. And piles of wood and kindling. If Octavian tries to take her treasure, she can burn it all.

[pause]

In the summer of 30 before Christ, Octavian arrives at Alexandria with his army.

[pause]

On the last night before he enters the city, an old writer tells a strange story. In the middle of the night, when Alexandria was quiet and full of fear, people suddenly heard music. Instruments of every kind, voices singing, and a joyful crowd dancing and shouting. The sound passed right through the city, to the gate facing the enemy. Then it went out through the gate, and faded away. The people believed it was the god of wine, whom Antony loved, leaving him.

[long-pause]

The next day, the first of August, Antony's last soldiers and his fleet went over to Octavian. Antony believed all was lost. He heard a false report that Cleopatra was dead, and ended his own life. We will not describe it. At the end he was brought to Cleopatra in her tomb, and died in her arms.

[pause]

Some days later, Cleopatra chose to end her own life too. She would not be taken to Rome and led in chains through the streets in Octavian's victory parade.

[pause]

{img:empty-royal-chamber-golden-couch-diadem-figs-basket-soft-light}
The story says a farmer brought her a basket of figs. Hidden among the figs was a small snake, an Egyptian cobra. The cobra was the royal snake of Egypt, the sign of the pharaohs. Octavian's messengers found her lying on a golden couch in her royal robes, with her two faithful maids beside her.

[pause]

One of the Romans asked a maid: was this well done? And she answered: it was very well done, and fitting for a queen descended from so many kings.

[long-pause]

Cleopatra was thirty-nine. She had ruled Egypt for twenty-two years.

[pause]

Octavian, despite his anger, gave orders that she should be buried beside Antony, as she had wished, with royal honours, in her tomb by the sea. That tomb has never been found.

[pause]

{img:sea-at-dusk-alexandria-lighthouse-glow-quiet-calm-ending}
With her death, three hundred years of her family's rule came to an end. And so did three thousand years of the pharaohs. Egypt became a province of Rome.

[long-pause]

{img:three-royal-children-palace-garden-alexandria-nurse-calm}
What became of Cleopatra's children?

[pause]

Caesarion, the son of Julius Caesar, was seventeen. Cleopatra had sent him away south with a teacher and some treasure, hoping he could escape to India. But his teacher persuaded him to come back, saying Octavian would let him rule Egypt. Octavian's advisers said two Caesars were one too many. And the young man was lost. We know little more.

[long-pause]

But the three younger children were taken to Rome. The twins were ten, and little Ptolemy was six. In Rome they had to walk in Octavian's great victory parade through the streets, behind a statue of their mother.

[pause]

And then something kind happened. They were given to Octavian's sister, Antony's former wife, the woman Antony had left for Cleopatra. She was a good and generous woman. She took them into her own household. She raised them with her own children, in her house in Rome, as part of her family.

[pause]

We do not know what happened to the two boys. They disappear from history. Perhaps they died young. Perhaps they lived quietly, under other names.

[long-pause]

But we do know what happened to the girl, Cleopatra Selene.

[pause]

When she was about fifteen, Octavian, now the emperor Augustus, arranged for her to marry a young prince called Juba. He was the son of a North African king, and had also been raised in Rome as a captive. Juba was a scholar who loved books and art. Augustus made Juba and Selene king and queen of a great kingdom in North Africa, in what is now Algeria and Morocco.

[pause]

{img:caesarea-mauretania-city-harbour-palace-north-africa-coast}
They ruled there together for many years. They made their capital on the coast into a beautiful city, like a little Alexandria, with a library, a theatre, temples and statues.

[long-pause]

Cleopatra Selene put her own name on the coins of the kingdom, in Greek: Cleopatra the Queen. On some of them she put a crocodile, the sign of Egypt, her mother's land.

[pause]

She and Juba had a son, and named him Ptolemy, after her family. He became king after them.

[pause]

{img:silver-coin-crocodile-greek-letters-queen-name-hand-light}
And so, in a little kingdom in North Africa, Cleopatra's daughter kept her mother's memory alive. She kept the family name, and something of the spirit of Alexandria, for one more generation.

[long-pause]

{img:roman-prefect-alexandria-grain-ships-loading-harbour}
After Cleopatra's death, Egypt belonged to Octavian personally. Three years later he took the name Augustus, and became the first Roman emperor.

[pause]

He did not make Egypt an ordinary Roman province. He kept it for himself, ruled by a governor he chose. No Roman senator was even allowed to visit Egypt without his permission. Egypt was too rich and too important to let anyone else control it.

[long-pause]

Above all, Egypt was the breadbasket of Rome. Every year, great fleets of grain ships sailed from Alexandria to Rome, carrying the wheat to feed the Roman people. Perhaps a third of all the grain eaten in Rome came from Egypt.

[pause]

Augustus visited Alexandria after Cleopatra's death. He went to see the tomb of Alexander the Great. He looked at the body of the great conqueror in its coffin, laid a golden crown on it, and scattered flowers. When they asked if he also wanted to see the tombs of Cleopatra's family, he said: I came to see a king, not corpses.

[pause]

{img:roman-visitor-at-tomb-of-alexander-flowers-golden-crown}
Alexandria stayed a great city for hundreds of years under the Romans. It was the second city of the empire, rich, busy and full of learning. Great scholars still worked there. One of them, a hundred and fifty years later, wrote books about the stars and drew maps of the world that were used for fourteen hundred years.

[long-pause]

And in the villages along the Nile, life went on as it always had. The river rose and fell. The farmers ploughed, sowed and harvested. The priests served the old gods. On the temple walls, the Roman emperors were now shown as pharaohs, with their names in hieroglyphs, just as Cleopatra had been before them.

[pause]

Over the following centuries, the old Egyptian religion slowly faded as Christianity spread. The last hieroglyphs were carved more than four hundred years after Cleopatra. After that, nobody could read them for fourteen hundred years. Then, in 1822, a young Frenchman called Champollion finally cracked the code. He used a stone found near Alexandria, with the same text written in Greek and in Egyptian. Priests had carved it in the time of Cleopatra's family.

[pause]

{img:rosetta-stone-three-scripts-museum-close-soft-light}
And Cleopatra herself became a legend, the most famous queen in history. The Roman poets wrote about her as a dangerous enemy. Shakespeare wrote about her as a great tragic heroine. Countless artists painted her, and great actresses played her in films.

[pause]

But perhaps the truest picture of her is still the one on her little coins. A clever, determined woman with a strong nose and her hair tied back, who tried for twenty-two years to keep her ancient country free.

[long-pause]

{img:cleopatras-needle-obelisk-thames-embankment-london-evening}
Cleopatra has been remembered and imagined for two thousand years.

[pause]

The Roman poets wrote about her as a dangerous enemy. One of them wrote a poem celebrating her death. Yet even he admitted that she died nobly, without fear, like a queen. A Greek writer told her story with fascination. He gave us most of the stories we know today.

[long-pause]

In 1606, Shakespeare wrote his great play about Antony and Cleopatra. In it she is passionate, proud, witty and unforgettable. Age cannot wither her, he wrote, nor custom stale her endless variety.

[pause]

Painters painted her again and again. At her feast with the pearl. Meeting Antony on her golden barge. In the twentieth century she was played in films by great actresses. The most famous film, from 1963, was one of the most expensive ever made.

[pause]

{img:painting-style-queen-on-golden-barge-river-old-master-colours}
And there are two famous stone pillars called Cleopatra's Needles. They are tall, slim pillars of red granite, carved with hieroglyphs. Once they stood in front of a temple in Alexandria that Cleopatra began to build. But they are much older than her. They were made for a pharaoh fourteen hundred years before her time, and brought to Alexandria later.

[pause]

In the nineteenth century, one of them was given to Britain. It was shipped to London in a special iron tube, which nearly sank in a storm. It was set up on the bank of the river Thames, and it still stands there. The other was given to the United States, and set up in Central Park in New York.

[long-pause]

{img:obelisk-central-park-new-york-trees-autumn-walkers}
So if you walk along the Thames in London in the evening, or through Central Park in New York, you can still see a piece of Cleopatra's Alexandria. It stands tall among the trees and the traffic. Its ancient picture writing, worn by two thousand years of sun and rain, still tells the names of pharaohs that almost nobody passing by can read.

[long-pause]

{img:alexandria-today-corniche-seafront-curving-bay-qaitbay-fort}
Today, Alexandria is a big modern city, the second city of Egypt, with more than five million people. It stretches for many kilometres along the shore.

[pause]

Much of the ancient city lies buried under the modern streets. Or under the sea. Over the centuries, earthquakes and the slow sinking of the land have pulled part of the old coast, and the palace quarter, beneath the water of the harbour.

[long-pause]

Along the seafront runs a long, curving promenade. In the evening, people walk there, sit in cafés, fish, and watch the sun go down over the sea.

[pause]

At one end of the bay, on the spot where the lighthouse once stood, there is a sturdy pale fort, built five hundred years ago with some of the lighthouse's stones. You can walk around it and look out to sea, where the great light once shone.

[pause]

And under the water of the harbour, divers have found the remains of the ancient city. Great stone blocks from the lighthouse. Columns and statues of Cleopatra's family dressed as pharaohs. Sphinxes. And the foundations of the palaces, including the little island where Cleopatra had her private palace. Some statues have been lifted out of the sea, and can be seen in museums.

[long-pause]

Near the place where the old Library may have stood, there is a great new library, opened in 2002. It is a huge round building, tilted toward the sea like the rising sun. Its granite walls are carved with letters from every alphabet in the world. Inside is a vast reading room with light pouring in from above.

[pause]

On a hill in the old Egyptian quarter stands one tall column of red granite, all that is left of the great temple of Serapis. Nearby there are underground tombs carved in the rock, decorated in a strange mix of Greek, Roman and Egyptian styles.

[pause]

{img:bibliotheca-alexandrina-tilted-disc-building-sea-sunlight}
And along the Nile, the great temples built by Cleopatra's family still stand. Dendera, with Cleopatra and her son on its back wall. Edfu and Philae. In the 1970s, the temple of Philae was moved stone by stone to a new island, to save it from the water of a great dam.

[long-pause]

The Nile still flows from the south, through the desert, to the sea. Its flood no longer comes every summer, because a great dam now holds back its water in a huge lake. But the green fields are still there along its banks, and the palm trees and the villages. And boats with white sails still glide on the water in the evening light.

[pause]

So we leave Alexandria for tonight, with the evening wind coming in from the sea, and the light of the old lighthouse long gone, but the waves still breaking on the stones where it stood.

[pause]

{img:felucca-white-sail-nile-sunset-palms-calm-water}
The great library by the sea, the farmers watching the river rise, the cats asleep on the warm roofs, and a queen who loved her ancient country, and tried to keep it free.

[pause]

If you enjoyed this evening in ancient Egypt, you are very welcome to subscribe to the channel, and to come back for another quiet story.
