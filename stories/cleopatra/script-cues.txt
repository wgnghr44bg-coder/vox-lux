Tonight we are going more than two thousand years back, to the city of Alexandria, in Egypt, in the year 31 before Christ.

[long-pause]

On the shore of the Mediterranean Sea, at the edge of the great green delta of the Nile, there stands a city of white marble and golden light, with a lighthouse taller than any building in the world, a library holding the knowledge of the ancient world, and a palace by the sea, where a queen lives.

[pause]

Her name is Cleopatra. She is the last pharaoh of Egypt. And tonight, we will spend a little time in her world: in her city, along her river, and among her people, in the last years of ancient Egypt's independence.

[pause]

If you enjoy quiet journeys into the distant past, you are welcome to subscribe to the channel, so that the next one is easy to find.

[long-pause]

{img:nile-delta-green-fields-palms-canals-river-branches-haze}
Let us imagine Egypt, as it is in the time of Cleopatra.

[pause]

It is one of the oldest countries in the world. For three thousand years, kings have ruled here, the pharaohs, and built their temples, and their pyramids, and their tombs, along the banks of the great river Nile. When Cleopatra is born, the great pyramids of Giza are already two and a half thousand years old. They are as old to her, as she is to us.

[long-pause]

Egypt is a strange and wonderful land: a long, narrow ribbon of green, along the river, through the middle of a vast desert.

[pause]

The Nile flows from the south, from the mountains of Africa, northward, for more than a thousand kilometres, through Egypt, to the sea. On both sides of the river, for a few kilometres, there are fields, and palm trees, and villages, green and fertile. And beyond them, suddenly, there is the desert: endless sand, and rock, and cliffs, golden and red and grey, under the burning sun.

[pause]

In the north, near the sea, the river divides into many branches, and spreads out, in a great triangle of green land, of fields, and marshes, and lakes, and canals: the Delta, so called by the Greeks, because it is shaped like their letter delta, a triangle.

[pause]

And at the western edge of the Delta, on a narrow strip of land between the sea and a great lake, there is the city of Alexandria.

[long-pause]

Alexandria is not an old Egyptian city. It is a Greek city, in Egypt.

[pause]

It was founded three hundred years before, in 331 before Christ, by Alexander the Great, the young king of Macedonia, who conquered the Persian Empire, and Egypt, and went on, as far as India. The story goes that he chose the place himself, and marked out the plan of the city on the ground, with barley flour, because there was no chalk. And the birds came, and ate the flour. And the soothsayers said that it was a good sign: the city would feed many people, from many lands.

[pause]

{img:young-alexander-marking-city-plan-barley-flour-birds-coast}
They were right. In the time of Cleopatra, Alexandria is the greatest city in the world, after Rome, and perhaps the most beautiful, and the most learned. Perhaps half a million people live there, or more: Greeks, and Egyptians, and Jews, and Syrians, and Persians, and Arabs, and Nubians, and Romans, and Indians, and people from every land around the Mediterranean.

[long-pause]

It is a city of trade, and learning, and beauty, and pleasure.

[pause]

Its harbours are full of ships, carrying the grain of Egypt to Rome, and the goods of Africa, and Arabia, and India, to the Mediterranean. Its streets are wide and straight, and lined with colonnades, and temples, and palaces, and gardens, and theatres. Its scholars are the most famous in the world. Its people are lively, and witty, and quarrelsome, and love music, and horse races, and festivals.

[pause]

{img:canopic-way-colonnades-crowds-chariots-white-marble-sunlight}
And at its heart, by the sea, there is the palace of the Ptolemies, the Greek kings and queens of Egypt, where Cleopatra lives, with her children, and her court, in the year 31 before Christ, the year when everything is about to change.

[long-pause]

{img:ptolemy-first-general-portrait-diadem-macedonian-calm}
When Alexander the Great passed away, in Babylon, in 323 before Christ, at the age of thirty-two, his generals divided his great empire between them.

[pause]

One of them, a Macedonian nobleman named Ptolemy, took Egypt. He was a friend of Alexander since childhood, a wise and careful man. He made himself king of Egypt, as Ptolemy the First, and founded a dynasty, a family of kings and queens, who ruled Egypt for nearly three hundred years.

[long-pause]

Ptolemy even took Alexander's body, as it was being carried home to Macedonia, and brought it to Egypt, and later to Alexandria, and buried it there, in a great tomb, in the middle of the city, so that Alexandria would have the protection, and the glory, of the great conqueror. Alexander's tomb is still there, in Cleopatra's time, and people come from all over the world to see it.

[pause]

All the kings of the family were called Ptolemy. And the queens were mostly called Cleopatra, or Berenice, or Arsinoe. Our Cleopatra is the seventh queen of that name.

[pause]

The Ptolemies were Greeks, not Egyptians. They spoke Greek, and lived like Greek kings, in their palace in Alexandria, with Greek courtiers, and Greek soldiers, and Greek scholars, and Greek customs.

[pause]

But to the Egyptian people, they were pharaohs. They were crowned in the old capital, Memphis, in the Egyptian way, with the ancient crowns of Upper and Lower Egypt. They built Egyptian temples, all along the Nile, to the old Egyptian gods, and were shown on the walls of the temples, in the old style, as pharaohs, making offerings to the gods, with Egyptian clothes, and crowns, and names, written in hieroglyphs.

[long-pause]

So they ruled two peoples, in two ways: as Greek kings to the Greeks, and as pharaohs to the Egyptians.

[pause]

The first Ptolemies were great rulers. They made Egypt rich, and powerful, and Alexandria the most splendid city in the world. They built the great lighthouse, and the library, and the museum, and invited the greatest scholars, and poets, and scientists, from all over the Greek world.

[pause]

{img:ptolemaic-pharaoh-relief-temple-wall-offering-to-gods-hieroglyphs}
But later Ptolemies were weaker. The family quarrelled, and fought, among themselves: brothers against sisters, mothers against sons, for the throne. Following an old Egyptian royal custom, they often married their own brothers and sisters, to keep the power in the family. There were rebellions, and riots, and plots, and murders, in the palace.

[long-pause]

And meanwhile, in the west, a new great power was rising: Rome.

[pause]

By the time Cleopatra was born, in 69 before Christ, Rome had conquered Greece, and most of the lands around the Mediterranean. Egypt was the last great independent kingdom left. And the Romans looked at Egypt, with its endless fields of grain, and its gold, and its riches, with greedy eyes.

[pause]

{img:roman-envoys-arriving-alexandria-harbour-galley-togas}
Cleopatra's father, Ptolemy the Twelfth, called the Flute Player, because he loved to play the flute, kept his throne only by paying enormous bribes to powerful Romans, and borrowing money to do it. Once, the people of Alexandria drove him out, and he fled to Rome, and came back only with the help of a Roman army.

[pause]

That was the world into which Cleopatra was born: a rich, ancient, beautiful country, ruled by a quarrelling Greek family, under the shadow of Rome.

[long-pause]

{img:ptolemaia-grand-procession-alexandria-floats-elephants-crowds}
The people of Alexandria love festivals. And the most famous festival in the city's history was the great procession of Ptolemy the Second, two hundred years before Cleopatra, which was described by a writer who saw it, and was remembered for centuries.

[pause]

It went on all day, through the stadium of Alexandria, and the streets, from dawn until dark.

[long-pause]

There were floats, great wagons, pulled by hundreds of men, with huge statues on them: a statue of the god Dionysus, five metres tall, pouring wine from a golden cup. A huge wine press, with sixty satyrs treading the grapes, singing, and the new wine running out into the street. A giant wineskin, made of leopard skins, holding thousands of litres of wine, which trickled out along the way, so that the people could drink.

[pause]

There were animals, from all over Africa and Asia: elephants, pulling chariots. Camels, and ostriches, and antelopes, and zebras, and leopards, and cheetahs, and lynxes, and a giraffe, and a white bear, and a rhinoceros from Ethiopia, and parrots, and peacocks, and pheasants, in cages.

[pause]

{img:exotic-animals-procession-giraffe-elephants-ostriches-street}
There were thousands of people, dressed as satyrs, and nymphs, and gods, and soldiers, in shining armour, and boys and girls, singing, and musicians, hundreds of them, playing lyres, and pipes, and drums. There was a golden statue of Alexander the Great, in a chariot drawn by elephants.

[long-pause]

In Cleopatra's time, there were still great festivals in Alexandria: for Dionysus, and for Serapis, and for Isis, and for the birthdays of the kings and queens, and for the rising of the Nile, with processions, and feasts, and games, and races, in the hippodrome, and plays, in the theatre, and music, and dancing, in the streets, all night.

[pause]

At the festival of Isis, in the spring, called the launching of the ship of Isis, a beautiful ship, decorated with flowers, was launched into the sea, from the harbour, to bless the sailing season, and the people gathered on the shore, singing, and throwing flowers on the water.

[pause]

{img:festival-ship-isis-flowers-launched-harbour-crowds-singing}
And at the great festival of the rising of the Nile, in the summer, when the flood began, the whole of Egypt celebrated, from Alexandria to the far south, with music, and dancing, and feasting, and thanks to the gods, for the gift of the river.

[long-pause]

{img:cleopatra-portrait-diadem-greek-hairstyle-pearls-calm-intelligent}
Cleopatra the Seventh was born in Alexandria, in the winter of 69 before Christ.

[pause]

In the year 31, she is thirty-eight years old. She has been queen of Egypt for twenty-one years, since she was eighteen. She is one of the most famous women in all of history. And yet, we know surprisingly little about her for certain, because almost everything that was written about her was written by her enemies, the Romans, after her death.

[long-pause]

What did she look like?

[pause]

Her coins show a woman with a strong face, a large, curved nose, a firm chin, and her hair pulled back, in a bun, in the Greek style, called the melon hairstyle, with braids, like the segments of a melon, and a royal diadem, a white band, tied around her head. In the Egyptian temples, she is shown in the old Egyptian style, as a goddess, with a tall crown, of horns, and a sun disc, and feathers.

[pause]

The Greek writer Plutarch, who lived about a hundred and fifty years later, but who talked to people whose grandfathers had known her court, says that her beauty was not, in itself, so remarkable that no one could compare with her. But that her presence was captivating, and her conversation irresistible, and her voice sweet, like an instrument of many strings, which she could turn easily to any language she liked.

[long-pause]

She was very clever, and very well educated. She spoke many languages: Greek, of course, her own language, and Egyptian, the first of all her family to bother to learn it, and also the languages of the Ethiopians, and the Troglodytes, and the Hebrews, and the Arabs, and the Syrians, and the Medes, and the Parthians, Plutarch says, so that she could speak to their ambassadors without an interpreter.

[pause]

She studied in the great Museum of Alexandria, with its scholars: mathematics, and astronomy, and philosophy, and medicine, and rhetoric, and history. Later writers said that she wrote books on medicine, and cosmetics, and weights and measures. Perhaps some of this is true.

[pause]

{img:young-princess-studying-scrolls-library-alexandria-scholars}
She was also a skilful ruler. She managed the economy of Egypt carefully, and the harvests, and the grain, and the money. She made Egypt rich again, after the troubles of her father's reign. She was popular with the Egyptian people, who saw her as a living goddess, Isis on earth.

[long-pause]

Her early life was difficult and dangerous.

[pause]

When she was about eleven, her father was driven out of Alexandria, and her older sister, Berenice, seized the throne. When her father came back, with a Roman army, Berenice was put to death. Then, when her father passed away, in 51 before Christ, Cleopatra, at eighteen, became queen, together with her younger brother, Ptolemy the Thirteenth, who was only ten, and who, according to the custom of the family, became her husband.

[pause]

{img:young-queen-and-boy-king-throne-room-alexandria-courtiers}
But her brother's advisers, a eunuch named Pothinus, and a general named Achillas, and a teacher named Theodotus, turned against her, and drove her out of Alexandria. She fled to Syria, and gathered an army, and came back, to fight for her throne.

[pause]

And at that very moment, in the summer of 48 before Christ, the greatest man in Rome arrived in Alexandria: Julius Caesar.

[long-pause]

{img:alexandria-grid-streets-canopic-way-gate-of-sun-panorama}
Let us walk through Alexandria, as it is in the time of Cleopatra.

[pause]

The city is long, and narrow, stretching for about five kilometres along the shore of the sea, between the Mediterranean in the north, and the great lake Mareotis in the south. It is laid out in a perfect grid, with straight streets, crossing each other at right angles, designed by a Greek architect, Dinocrates, three hundred years before.

[long-pause]

The main street, the Canopic Way, runs right through the city, from east to west, from the Gate of the Sun, to the Gate of the Moon. It is enormously wide, about thirty metres, wide enough for many chariots side by side, and lined on both sides with colonnades of marble, so that people can walk in the shade.

[pause]

The Greek writer Achilles Tatius, describing his first sight of the city, says that the street of columns seemed to go on forever, and that he could not decide where to look first, because there was so much beauty everywhere.

[pause]

The city is divided into five quarters, named after the first five letters of the Greek alphabet: Alpha, Beta, Gamma, Delta, Epsilon. The Greeks live mostly in the centre. The Jews, a large and important community, live in the Delta quarter, in the east, with their own synagogues, and their own council, and their own laws. The Egyptians live mostly in the west, in the old village of Rhakotis, which was there before Alexander came.

[long-pause]

In the north east of the city, by the sea, there is the palace quarter, the Brucheion, which takes up perhaps a quarter of the whole city.

[pause]

It is not one palace, but many: a great complex of palaces, and halls, and gardens, and courtyards, and colonnades, and temples, built by one Ptolemy after another, each adding something new, along the shore, and on a little peninsula, called Cape Lochias, and on a little island, in the harbour, called Antirhodos, where Cleopatra has her own private palace, by the sea.

[pause]

In the palace quarter, there are also the Museum, and the Library, and the tomb of Alexander, and the tombs of the Ptolemies, and a theatre, and a royal harbour, for the royal ships.

[long-pause]

Everywhere in the city, there are temples: to the Greek gods, and to the Egyptian gods, and to gods who are both Greek and Egyptian at once.

[pause]

The greatest is the Serapeum, on a hill in the Egyptian quarter: the great temple of Serapis, a god invented by the first Ptolemy, to please both Greeks and Egyptians, a bearded god, like Zeus, with a basket of grain on his head, who is the protector of Alexandria. His temple stands on a great platform, with a hundred steps leading up to it, and colonnades, and a daughter library, and inside, a huge statue of the god, of wood, covered with gold and precious stones.

[pause]

{img:serapeum-temple-hill-steps-colonnades-statue-sunlit}
There are gymnasia, where the young Greek men exercise, and study, and a great racecourse, the hippodrome, where there are chariot races, and a stadium, and theatres, and parks, and gardens, and public baths.

[long-pause]

And there are the markets, full of the goods of the world: grain, and papyrus, and linen, from Egypt. Spices, and incense, and perfumes, from Arabia. Ivory, and ebony, and gold, from Nubia, and Ethiopia. Pepper, and cotton, and precious stones, from India. Wine, and olive oil, from Greece. Silk, even, from far away, from China, which the Alexandrians buy at enormous prices.

[pause]

{img:alexandria-market-spices-papyrus-rolls-linen-ivory-merchants}
The Alexandrians are proud, and clever, and quick-tongued, and love jokes, and nicknames, and gossip, and making fun of their rulers. They love music, and dancing, and festivals, and the theatre, and the races. And they love their beautiful city, which, they say, has everything in the world, except snow.

[long-pause]

{img:great-synagogue-alexandria-columns-congregation-scrolls-light}
One of the largest communities in Alexandria, in the time of Cleopatra, is the Jewish community.

[pause]

Jews have lived in Alexandria since the city was founded, three hundred years before. Some came as soldiers, in the armies of the Ptolemies. Some came as merchants, and craftsmen, and farmers. By Cleopatra's time, there are perhaps a hundred thousand or more, perhaps more than in Jerusalem itself. They live mostly in the Delta quarter, in the east of the city, near the palace, and the sea.

[long-pause]

The Jews of Alexandria speak Greek, as their everyday language, and many of them have Greek names, and wear Greek clothes, and go to the Greek gymnasium, and the theatre. But they keep their own religion, and their own laws, and their own customs: the Sabbath, and the festivals, and the food laws.

[pause]

They have their own council of elders, and their own courts, and many synagogues, all over the city. The greatest of them, the Great Synagogue, was so huge, it is said, that the people at the back could not hear the reader, and an official stood in the middle, and waved a flag, to show the congregation when to say Amen.

[pause]

{img:jewish-merchants-delta-quarter-street-greek-dress-market}
Because the Jews of Alexandria spoke Greek, and many of them no longer understood Hebrew well, the Hebrew Bible was translated into Greek, here, in Alexandria, about two hundred years before Cleopatra. The story goes that seventy-two scholars, six from each of the twelve tribes of Israel, were invited by Ptolemy the Second, and worked on the island of Pharos, near the lighthouse, and finished the translation in seventy-two days. It is called the Septuagint, from the Latin word for seventy. It is still used today, by the Greek Orthodox Church.

[long-pause]

Every year, the Jews of Alexandria held a festival, on the island of Pharos, to celebrate the translation, with picnics, and prayers, on the beach, by the sea.

[pause]

A few years after Cleopatra, in the time of Jesus, a great Jewish philosopher, Philo of Alexandria, will live in the city, and write books, in Greek, which try to bring together the wisdom of the Bible and the philosophy of Plato. His books will be read by the early Christians, for centuries.

[pause]

{img:beach-picnic-families-pharos-island-festival-lighthouse-behind}
Cleopatra, it seems, was friendly to the Jews of Alexandria, and protected their rights, although there were sometimes tensions between them and the Greeks of the city. Alexandria, in her time, was a city where many peoples, and many faiths, lived side by side, and learned from each other, and sometimes quarrelled, as people in great cities always do.

[long-pause]

{img:pharos-lighthouse-three-tiers-fire-top-statue-ships-dusk}
The most famous building in Alexandria, and one of the Seven Wonders of the World, is the great lighthouse, the Pharos.

[pause]

It stands on a little island, also called Pharos, at the entrance to the great harbour, joined to the city by a long causeway, a mole, of stone, almost a kilometre and a half long, called the Heptastadion, the seven stades, because of its length.

[long-pause]

The lighthouse was built about two hundred and fifty years before Cleopatra's time, by the first two Ptolemies, and designed by a Greek architect, Sostratus of Cnidus.

[pause]

It is enormous: perhaps a hundred metres high, or more, the tallest building in the world, after the great pyramids. It is built of great blocks of white limestone, and granite, in three stages: a tall square tower at the bottom, with many windows, and rooms inside, for the keepers, and for storing fuel. Then an eight-sided tower above it. And on top, a round tower, with columns, where the fire burns.

[pause]

At the very top, there is a great statue, perhaps of Zeus the Saviour, or of Poseidon, the god of the sea. And at each corner of the first stage, there are statues of Tritons, sea gods, blowing on conch shells.

[long-pause]

At night, a great fire burns at the top of the lighthouse, fed with wood, and oil, which is carried up, inside the tower, by a spiral ramp, perhaps on donkeys. And during the day, it is said, a great polished mirror, of bronze, reflects the light of the sun, out to sea.

[pause]

The light can be seen, the ancient writers say, from fifty kilometres away, out at sea. The coast of Egypt, near Alexandria, is very flat, and low, and dangerous, with rocks, and sandbanks, and few landmarks. So the lighthouse guides the ships safely into the harbour, by day and by night.

[pause]

{img:ship-at-night-sea-sailors-seeing-distant-lighthouse-glow}
The sailors coming to Alexandria, from Rome, and Greece, and Syria, watch for the light, on the horizon, at night, and when they see it, they know they are nearly home, or nearly at the greatest city of the world.

[long-pause]

The lighthouse was so famous that its name, Pharos, became the word for lighthouse, in many languages: in Greek, and Latin, and in French, phare, and in Italian, and Spanish, faro.

[pause]

There is a story that the architect, Sostratus, wanted his own name to be remembered, but the king wanted his name on the lighthouse. So Sostratus carved his own name, and a dedication to the saviour gods, for the safety of sailors, into the stone, and then covered it with plaster, and painted the king's name on the plaster. And after many years, the plaster fell away, and the architect's name was revealed, forever.

[pause]

{img:carved-greek-inscription-stone-plaster-falling-away-sunlight}
The lighthouse stood for more than a thousand years, through the time of the Romans, and the Byzantines, and the Arabs, until it was damaged by great earthquakes, in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, and fell. In the fifteenth century, the Sultan of Egypt, Qaitbay, built a fort, on the same spot, using some of its stones. And the fort still stands there today.

[long-pause]

{img:great-harbour-alexandria-merchant-ships-grain-warehouses-quay}
Alexandria has two great harbours, on either side of the long causeway, the Heptastadion, that joins the city to the island of Pharos.

[pause]

The Great Harbour, in the east, beside the palace quarter, is the busiest. It is full of ships, from all over the Mediterranean: great round merchant ships, with one tall mast, and a big square sail, and a curved stern, shaped like the neck of a swan. Long, slender warships. Little fishing boats. And the river boats, which come down the Nile, and through a canal, from the lake, into the harbour.

[long-pause]

Along the quays, there are great warehouses, for grain, and papyrus, and linen, and glass, and spices, and wine. Cranes, worked by men in treadwheels, lift the cargoes in and out of the ships. Porters carry sacks and jars on their backs. Merchants check their goods, and argue about prices. Customs officials check every ship, and every cargo, and collect the taxes.

[pause]

The most important cargo of all is grain. Every summer, after the harvest, great fleets of grain ships sail from Alexandria, carrying the wheat of Egypt, across the sea. In Cleopatra's time, much of it goes to the cities of Greece, and Syria, and Rome. Later, under the Romans, enormous grain ships, the largest ships in the world, will carry it to Rome every year.

[pause]

{img:huge-grain-ship-loading-sacks-cranes-sailors-alexandria-summer}
The other harbour, in the west, is called the Eunostos, the harbour of the happy return. It is quieter, and connected, by a canal, to the great lake Mareotis, behind the city, which is itself a great harbour, full of boats, bringing the produce of Egypt, from the Nile, to the city.

[long-pause]

The sailors of Alexandria are among the best in the world. They know the winds, and the currents, and the stars. Every summer, the north wind, the etesian wind, blows steadily across the Mediterranean, toward Egypt, which makes the voyage to Alexandria fast and easy, and the voyage away from it slow and hard.

[pause]

And a few years before Cleopatra's birth, Greek sailors from Egypt discovered the secret of the monsoon winds, in the Indian Ocean, which blow from the south west in summer, and from the north east in winter. So that ships could now sail across the open ocean, from the Red Sea, to India, and back, within a year. And soon, every year, more than a hundred ships sail from the Egyptian ports of the Red Sea to India, bringing back pepper, and spices, and precious stones, and cotton, and silk.

[pause]

{img:red-sea-port-ships-departing-india-monsoon-desert-coast}
In the evening, in the Great Harbour, as the sun goes down, the sailors come ashore, and go to the taverns, along the waterfront, to eat, and drink, and tell stories of the sea, and of the far-off lands they have seen, while the ships rock gently at anchor, and the great lighthouse begins to shine.

[long-pause]

{img:library-of-alexandria-scroll-shelves-scholars-reading-colonnade}
In the palace quarter of Alexandria, there is a place unlike any other in the ancient world: the Museum, and its great Library.

[pause]

The Museum, in Greek the Mouseion, the house of the Muses, the goddesses of the arts and learning, is not a museum as we know it. It is more like a university, or an academy: a community of scholars, scientists, poets, and philosophers, from all over the Greek world, who live there, at the expense of the king, with free food and lodging, and a salary, and nothing to do but study, and think, and write, and teach.

[long-pause]

There are lecture halls, and a great dining hall, where the scholars eat together, and covered walks, and gardens, and an observatory, and perhaps a zoo, and a botanical garden, with plants from all over the world.

[pause]

And there is the Library: the greatest collection of books in the ancient world.

[pause]

The books are scrolls, rolls of papyrus, the paper made in Egypt, from the stems of the papyrus reed. Nobody knows exactly how many there were. The ancient writers say four hundred thousand, or even seven hundred thousand scrolls. Perhaps that is too many. But it was certainly the largest library that had ever existed.

[long-pause]

The Ptolemies wanted to collect every book in the world.

[pause]

They sent agents all over the Mediterranean, to buy books. And they made a law, that every ship that came into the harbour of Alexandria must be searched for books. Any books found were taken to the Library, and copied, and the copies were given back to the owners, while the Library kept the originals.

[pause]

One Ptolemy borrowed the official copies of the plays of the great Athenian tragic poets, Aeschylus, Sophocles, and Euripides, from Athens, and paid a huge deposit, fifteen talents of silver, as a guarantee that he would return them. And then he kept the originals, and sent back copies, and let Athens keep the deposit.

[long-pause]

The scholars of the Museum did wonderful things.

[pause]

Euclid, a mathematician, wrote a book on geometry, the Elements, which was used in schools for more than two thousand years. Eratosthenes, a librarian, measured the size of the earth, by comparing the length of shadows, at noon, on the same day, in Alexandria, and in Syene, far to the south, and was almost exactly right. Aristarchus of Samos suggested that the earth goes around the sun, and not the sun around the earth, eighteen hundred years before Copernicus.

[pause]

{img:eratosthenes-measuring-shadow-stick-well-noon-sun-sand}
Doctors, like Herophilus, studied the human body, and the brain, and the nerves, and the pulse. Engineers, like Ctesibius, invented a water clock, and a water organ, and pumps. Scholars made the first careful editions of Homer's poems, comparing many copies, to find the true text. And seventy-two Jewish scholars, the story goes, translated the Hebrew Bible into Greek, here in Alexandria: the translation called the Septuagint.

[long-pause]

In Cleopatra's time, the great age of the Museum is past, but it is still a famous place of learning, and scholars still come there from all over the world.

[pause]

Some people say that part of the Library burned in 48 before Christ, when Julius Caesar was in Alexandria, and fires spread from the harbour. Others say that only some warehouses of books, near the docks, were burned, and that the Library itself survived. Historians still argue about it. The Library slowly declined, over the following centuries, and nobody knows exactly when it disappeared.

[pause]

{img:empty-library-hall-afternoon-sunbeam-dust-scrolls-quiet}
But in the year 31, scholars still walk in its colonnades, and read its scrolls, and argue, and copy, and think, in the quiet halls, by the sea.

[long-pause]

{img:papyrus-reeds-marsh-delta-tall-stems-umbrella-heads-boat}
In the marshes of the Delta, there grows a tall, graceful plant, with a long, green, triangular stem, as tall as two men, and a head of fine green threads, like an umbrella, or a firework. It is papyrus.

[pause]

For three thousand years, the Egyptians have used it for everything: for boats, and baskets, and mats, and sandals, and rope, and boxes, and fuel, and even food, chewing the sweet pith of the stem. And above all, for making paper.

[long-pause]

To make a sheet of papyrus, the workers cut the stems, and peel off the green outer skin, and slice the white pith inside into long, thin strips. They lay the strips side by side, on a flat board, and then lay another layer across them, at right angles. Then they beat the layers with a wooden mallet, and press them, under a weight, and the natural sap of the plant glues them together. When they are dry, they rub them smooth with a stone, or a shell.

[pause]

The sheets are glued together, end to end, to make a long roll, of twenty sheets or more, several metres long. Our word paper comes from papyrus.

[pause]

Egypt is the only country that makes papyrus, and it sells it all over the Mediterranean, to Greece, and Rome, and everywhere. It is one of the great sources of the wealth of Egypt, and the Ptolemies control the trade, and grow rich on it.

[long-pause]

The Egyptians write on papyrus with reed pens, and black ink, made of soot and gum, and red ink, made of ochre, for headings. The Greeks write from left to right. The Egyptians, in the old script, often from right to left.

[pause]

There are three kinds of Egyptian writing: hieroglyphs, the beautiful picture writing, carved on temples and monuments. Hieratic, a quicker, simpler form, written with a brush, for religious texts. And demotic, a very quick, joined-up script, for everyday use: letters, and contracts, and accounts.

[pause]

{img:scribe-sitting-cross-legged-reed-pen-papyrus-roll-palette}
In the dry sands of Egypt, papyrus does not rot. And so, in the rubbish heaps of ancient towns, archaeologists have found hundreds of thousands of papyri, from the time of the Ptolemies and the Romans, which tell us about the everyday lives of ordinary people: their letters, and their shopping lists, and their tax receipts, and their contracts, and their school exercises, and their complaints.

[long-pause]

There is a letter from a boy, to his father, complaining that his father did not take him to Alexandria. If you will not take me, he writes, I will not eat, and I will not drink. There.

[pause]

There is a wedding invitation. There is a letter from a wife to her husband, asking him to come home. There is a list of what to pack for a journey. There is a receipt for the price of a donkey.

[pause]

{img:papyrus-fragments-greek-letters-glass-museum-close-light}
And there is even, perhaps, a single word, written by Cleopatra herself. On a papyrus, a royal order, from the year 33 before Christ, giving tax privileges to a Roman friend of Antony, at the bottom, in a different hand, someone has written one word, in Greek: ginesthoi. Let it be done. Some scholars believe that this is Cleopatra's own handwriting. If so, it is the only thing we have that she wrote with her own hand.

[long-pause]

{img:alexandria-dawn-flat-roofs-palms-sea-mist-temple-pylons}
Let us spend an ordinary day in Alexandria, in the time of Cleopatra, with an ordinary family.

[pause]

The father is a Greek, a maker of glass, with a little workshop, in the city. Alexandria is famous for its glass: beautiful bottles, and bowls, and cups, and beads, in many colours, blue and green and purple and amber, which are sold all over the Mediterranean.

[long-pause]

The family lives in a house of mud brick, plastered and painted, two or three storeys high, on a narrow street, near one of the canals. On the ground floor, there is the workshop, and the kitchen. Above, there are the rooms where the family lives and sleeps. And on the flat roof, there is a terrace, where they sit in the cool of the evening, and sleep, in the hottest nights of summer, under the stars.

[pause]

The day begins at dawn. The sun rises over the Delta, red and gold, and the city wakes up. The birds sing in the palm trees. The donkeys bray. The water carriers come along the street, with their jars, and their skins of water, from the canal.

[pause]

{img:water-carrier-donkey-jars-narrow-street-morning-alexandria}
The mother, and the daughters, and the slave woman, make breakfast: bread, baked fresh in the clay oven, in the courtyard, and a little cheese, and dates, and figs, and beer, or water mixed with wine.

[long-pause]

The father goes down to the workshop, where the furnace is already hot. His apprentices, and his slaves, are already at work.

[pause]

Glass is made from sand, and soda, from the salt lakes of Egypt, and lime, melted together in the furnace, at a great heat, until it becomes a glowing, liquid mass. In these very years, in Syria, and in Alexandria, a wonderful new invention is spreading: glassblowing. The craftsman takes a little blob of hot glass on the end of a long hollow pipe, and blows into it, and the glass swells, like a bubble, and he turns it, and shapes it, with tools, into a bottle, or a cup, or a bowl, quickly, before it cools. It is much faster than the old ways. And soon, glass will be cheap enough for ordinary people to use, all over the Roman world.

[pause]

In the afternoon, the father goes to the baths, and then to the gymnasium, to exercise, and to talk with his friends, about business, and politics, and the queen, and the Romans.

[long-pause]

The mother runs the household. She goes to the market, with a slave, to buy food: fish, from the sea, and the lake, and vegetables, onions, and leeks, and lettuce, and cucumbers, and lentils, and fruit, grapes, and pomegranates, and dates, and melons. She spins, and weaves linen, the fine white cloth of Egypt, for the family's clothes.

[pause]

The children go to school, or play in the streets, and on the roof, with their toys: clay dolls, and little wooden horses on wheels, and balls, and spinning tops, and pet birds, and cats.

[pause]

{img:children-playing-flat-roof-cats-toys-palm-trees-afternoon}
The cats of Egypt are everywhere, and greatly loved, and respected. The Egyptians believe that cats are sacred to the goddess Bastet. To harm a cat is a terrible crime. A Greek historian, Diodorus, who visited Alexandria in these years, saw a crowd of Egyptians attack a Roman, who had accidentally killed a cat, in the street, and nothing could save him, not even the king's officials.

[long-pause]

In the evening, the family gathers on the roof, for supper, as the sun sets over the lake, in the west, in a great blaze of orange, and red, and purple, and the lighthouse begins to shine, out at sea.

[pause]

The cool breeze comes in from the sea. The stars come out, bright and close. From the street below come the sounds of the evening: people talking, and laughing, and music, from a tavern, and a dog barking, and the call of the night watchman.

[pause]

{img:family-supper-rooftop-sunset-lake-lighthouse-lamps-calm}
And then, the family lies down to sleep, on the roof, on mats, and cushions, under a light linen sheet, with the stars above, and the sound of the sea, far away, and the faint glow of the great lighthouse, on the horizon.

[long-pause]

{img:egyptian-bakery-women-grinding-grain-flat-loaves-clay-oven}
What do the people of Egypt eat and drink, in the time of Cleopatra?

[pause]

The most important food is bread. The Egyptians bake many kinds: flat round loaves, and tall conical loaves, and loaves shaped like animals, and sweet breads, with honey, and dates, and figs. The bread is made from wheat, or barley, ground on stone grinders, by the women, and often contains a little sand, from the grinding stones, which wears down people's teeth.

[long-pause]

The second most important food is beer. The Egyptians drink beer every day, men and women and children: a thick, cloudy, nourishing beer, made from barley bread, soaked in water, and left to ferment, and strained, sometimes flavoured with dates. It is not very strong. Workers are often paid partly in bread and beer.

[pause]

The Greeks of Alexandria prefer wine, from the vineyards of the Delta, and around the lake Mareotis, whose white wine is famous, and from Greece, and the islands.

[pause]

{img:market-stall-onions-leeks-garlic-cucumbers-melons-figs-baskets}
The Egyptians eat a great many vegetables: onions, and leeks, and garlic, which the Greeks say the Egyptians love more than anything, and lettuce, and cucumbers, and radishes, and beans, and lentils, and chickpeas. And fruit: dates, and figs, and grapes, and pomegranates, and melons, and the fruit of the sycamore fig, and the doum palm.

[long-pause]

They eat fish, from the Nile, and the lakes, and the sea: grey mullet, and perch, and catfish, and tilapia, fresh, or salted, or dried in the sun. And birds: ducks, and geese, and pigeons, and quails, caught in the marshes with nets. Meat, beef, and mutton, and goat, is more expensive, and eaten mostly by the rich, and at festivals.

[pause]

They cook with oil: olive oil, from Greece, for the rich, and sesame oil, and linseed oil, and castor oil, for ordinary people. They sweeten their food with honey, and dates. And they season it with salt, and herbs, cumin, and coriander, and dill, and mint.

[pause]

{img:greek-banquet-alexandria-couches-fish-dishes-wine-garlands}
The rich Greeks of Alexandria give great banquets, in the Greek style, reclining on couches, with many dishes: fish, and seafood, oysters, and prawns, and eels, and roast meats, and game, and pastries, and cakes, and fruits, and wine, and garlands of flowers, and perfumes, and music, and dancers.

[long-pause]

And everyone, rich and poor, Greek and Egyptian, loves the sweet things of Egypt: honey cakes, and dates stuffed with nuts, and fig cakes, and the sweet sticky juice of the carob pod.

[pause]

In the evenings, in the streets of Alexandria, there are little food stalls, selling hot bread, and roasted chickpeas, and fried fish, and lentil soup, and beer, to the people walking home. The smell of baking bread, and roasting fish, drifts through the warm evening air, with the sound of voices, and music, and laughter.

[long-pause]

{img:nile-river-wide-sails-feluccas-palms-temple-golden-afternoon}
Everything in Egypt depends on the Nile.

[pause]

The ancient Greek historian Herodotus said that Egypt is the gift of the Nile. Without the river, Egypt would be desert. With it, Egypt is one of the richest farming countries in the world.

[long-pause]

Every year, in the summer, the Nile floods.

[pause]

Far to the south, in the mountains of Ethiopia, the summer rains fall, heavy and constant, and the rivers swell, and the water flows north, into the Nile, and down into Egypt. In July, the river begins to rise. In August and September, it overflows its banks, and spreads out over the fields, all along the valley, and the Delta, so that the whole land becomes a great lake, with the villages, and the temples, and the palm groves, standing up out of the water, like islands.

[pause]

The flood brings water, and it brings silt: fine, black, rich mud, carried down from the mountains, which settles on the fields, and makes them fertile, year after year, without any need for manure. The Egyptians call their country Kemet, the black land, because of the black soil of the fields. And the desert, Deshret, the red land.

[long-pause]

The Egyptians measure the height of the flood carefully, every year, with nilometers: stone staircases, or wells, beside the river, with marks on the walls, to show how high the water has risen.

[pause]

If the flood is too low, there will not be enough water for the fields, and there will be famine. If it is too high, it will wash away the villages, and the dykes. If it is just right, about sixteen cubits, at Memphis, there will be a good harvest, and everyone will be happy.

[pause]

The kings, and later the Roman governors, use the height of the flood to decide how much tax the farmers must pay. Cleopatra, too, watches the flood anxiously, every year. In the years before 31, there were some bad floods, and some famine, and she opened the royal granaries, and gave out grain to the people.

[long-pause]

In October and November, the water goes down, and the fields appear again, covered with black mud. And the farmers sow their crops: wheat, and barley, and flax, for linen, and beans, and lentils, and onions, and vegetables. The crops grow through the mild winter. And in the spring, in March and April, they are harvested.

[pause]

Then, in the summer, the land is dry, and the farmers wait, for the river to rise again. It is a cycle, which has repeated every year, for thousands of years.

[pause]

{img:farmers-sowing-seed-black-mud-fields-oxen-november-light}
The Nile is also the great road of Egypt. Boats travel up and down it, all year round: going north, downstream, with the current, and going south, upstream, with their sails up, because the wind in Egypt almost always blows from the north. So it is easy to travel in both directions. The hieroglyph for going north is a boat with its sail down. The hieroglyph for going south is a boat with its sail up.

[long-pause]

On the river, there are boats of every kind: little reed boats, and fishing boats, and ferries, and great barges, carrying grain, and stone, and cattle, and the great golden barges of the kings and the gods.

[pause]

And along the banks, there are the palm trees, and the reeds, and the papyrus, and the water birds, ibises, and herons, and egrets, and ducks, and kingfishers, and the crocodiles, sunning themselves on the sandbanks, and the hippopotamuses, wallowing in the shallows, in the evenings, and the farmers, raising water from the river, to their fields, with a shaduf, a long pole, with a bucket at one end, and a weight at the other.

[pause]

{img:shaduf-farmer-raising-water-riverbank-ibis-reeds-morning}
It is a scene that has hardly changed for thousands of years, and in some places, still has not changed today.

[long-pause]

{img:travellers-boat-nile-pyramids-giza-distance-palms-afternoon}
Even in the time of Cleopatra, Egypt was full of tourists.

[pause]

Greeks, and Romans, and other visitors, came to Egypt, to see its ancient wonders, which were already thousands of years old. They sailed up the Nile, in boats, from Alexandria, and visited the old cities, and the temples, and the pyramids, with local guides, who told them stories, some true, and some not.

[long-pause]

The first great sight, a few days' sail up the river, was Memphis, the ancient capital of Egypt, a great city, with its temples, and its palaces, and the temple of Ptah, and the sacred Apis bull, which visitors could see, in its stall, and feed, and watch, and ask questions of, as an oracle.

[pause]

And then, on the edge of the desert, to the west of Memphis, the pyramids of Giza.

[pause]

{img:great-pyramid-smooth-white-casing-stones-giza-sphinx-ancient}
In Cleopatra's time, the great pyramids still have their outer casing of smooth, white, polished limestone, which shines in the sun, so bright that it dazzles the eyes. Their tops are still sharp. They are covered, the Greek historian Herodotus said, with hieroglyphs, which the guides translated for him, saying that they recorded how many onions, and radishes, and garlic, the workers who built the pyramid had eaten. Probably the guides were making it up.

[long-pause]

The Great Sphinx lies in front of them, half buried in the sand, which blows over it, from the desert, and must be cleared away, from time to time.

[pause]

The Greek and Roman visitors were amazed. The pyramids were counted among the Seven Wonders of the World, the only one which still survives today. Some visitors climbed them. Some carved their names on them, as tourists always have.

[pause]

{img:colossi-of-memnon-two-great-statues-thebes-plain-dawn-visitors}
Further south, at Thebes, the ancient religious capital, there were the great temples of Karnak and Luxor, with their forests of huge columns, and the tombs of the kings, in the Valley of the Kings, which visitors could already enter, and see the painted walls.

[pause]

And there were the Colossi of Memnon: two enormous seated statues of a pharaoh, Amenhotep the Third, on the plain, in front of the ruins of his temple. One of them had been cracked by an earthquake. And every morning, at sunrise, as the stone warmed in the sun, it made a strange, soft, singing sound, like a lyre string breaking. The Greeks believed that it was Memnon, a hero of the Trojan War, son of the goddess of the dawn, greeting his mother every morning. Visitors came from all over the world to hear it, and carved poems on its legs, in Greek and Latin, which can still be read today.

[long-pause]

Those who travelled further south, all the way to the first cataract of the Nile, at Syene, Aswan, could see the great granite quarries, where the obelisks were cut, and the beautiful island of Philae, with its temple of Isis, among the palm trees, and the rocks, and the rushing water.

[pause]

And so, for the people of Cleopatra's time, as for us, Egypt was already an ancient land of wonders, of gods and kings and mysteries, which they came to see, and wonder at, and remember, all their lives.

[long-pause]

{img:nile-wildlife-ibis-herons-hippos-crocodile-sandbank-dawn}
The Nile, in the time of Cleopatra, is full of life.

[pause]

In the shallow water, among the reeds and the papyrus, there are hippopotamuses: huge, heavy, grey animals, which spend the day in the water, with only their eyes, and ears, and nostrils showing, and come out at night, to graze on the river banks, and sometimes in the farmers' fields, to the farmers' great annoyance.

[long-pause]

On the sandbanks, there are crocodiles, lying in the sun, with their mouths open, to cool themselves, and little birds, plovers, walking in and out of their mouths, picking food from their teeth, as Herodotus describes, and as can still be seen in Africa today. The Egyptians fear the crocodiles, and respect them. In some places, like the city of Crocodilopolis, in the Fayum, sacred crocodiles are kept in pools, at the temple of Sobek, the crocodile god, and fed by the priests, with bread, and meat, and honey cakes, and wine, and wear golden earrings, and bracelets on their front legs. Greek tourists come to watch them being fed.

[pause]

{img:sacred-crocodile-temple-pool-priests-feeding-golden-earrings}
There are birds everywhere: the white ibis, sacred to Thoth, walking slowly in the fields, with its long curved beak. Herons, and egrets, and storks, and pelicans, and cormorants, and kingfishers, flashing blue over the water. Ducks, and geese, by the thousand, in the marshes of the Delta, in the winter, when they come from the north. Hoopoes, with their crests, and swallows, and doves, in the villages.

[long-pause]

In the papyrus marshes, there are wild cats, and mongooses, and otters. And on the edges of the desert, there are jackals, and foxes, and hares, and gazelles.

[pause]

And in every house, there are cats, the beloved, sacred cats of Egypt, sleeping in the sun, on the roofs, and the doorsteps, and catching mice in the granaries. And dogs, and monkeys, baboons, kept as pets, and trained to pick figs from the trees. And donkeys, the hard-working animals of every village, carrying loads, and people, along the dusty paths.

[pause]

{img:cat-sleeping-sunny-doorstep-egyptian-village-donkey-palm}
The Egyptians love their animals, and paint them, and carve them, and keep them, and worship them, and mummify them, and bury them, in great animal cemeteries, by the million: cats at Bubastis, ibises at Saqqara, crocodiles in the Fayum. Visitors could buy a little mummified animal, at the temple, as an offering to the god.

[pause]

For the Egyptians, the animals of the Nile were part of the great order of the world, which the gods had made, and which must be kept in balance, like the rising and falling of the river.

[long-pause]

{img:egyptian-village-mud-brick-houses-palms-dovecotes-fields-river}
Most of the people of Egypt, in the time of Cleopatra, do not live in Alexandria. They live in villages, along the Nile, as their ancestors have done for thousands of years. They are farmers, and speak Egyptian, and worship the old gods, and live very much as their ancestors did in the time of the pyramids.

[long-pause]

An Egyptian village is a cluster of little houses of mud brick, built on a mound, above the level of the flood, among palm trees. The houses have thick walls, and small windows, and flat roofs, of palm trunks and reeds and mud, where the family stores fuel, and grain, and dries fish, and sleeps in summer.

[pause]

Inside, it is cool, and dim. There are a few rooms, with floors of beaten earth, and mats, and a few stools, and chests, and pottery jars, for water, and grain, and oil, and beer. There is a little shrine, in a niche in the wall, with a figure of a household god, Bes, the funny little dwarf god, who protects mothers and children, or Taweret, the hippopotamus goddess of childbirth.

[pause]

Outside, in the courtyard, there is an oven, for baking bread, and a place for the animals: a donkey, a few goats, some ducks, and geese, and pigeons, in tall dovecotes, made of mud, like little towers.

[long-pause]

Around the village, there are the fields, and the irrigation canals, and the dykes, which the villagers must keep in good repair, working together, every year, before the flood.

[pause]

The farmers work hard, all year round. They plough with oxen, and wooden ploughs. They sow by hand. In the spring, they harvest the wheat and barley, with sickles, and thresh it, on threshing floors, with cattle, and winnow it, throwing it into the wind, so that the chaff blows away, and the grain falls. Scribes, sent by the queen's officials, come to measure the harvest, and take the tax.

[pause]

{img:harvest-wheat-sickles-scribe-measuring-grain-heap-field-sun}
Most of the land belongs to the crown, or to the temples, or to rich landowners. The farmers rent it, and pay with part of their harvest. Life is hard, and the taxes are heavy. But in a good year, there is enough to eat, and the family can live well enough.

[long-pause]

The women of the villages work too: in the fields, at harvest, and at home, grinding grain, and baking bread, and brewing beer, and spinning flax, and weaving linen, and carrying water from the river, in jars, on their heads, and looking after the children, and the animals.

[pause]

Egyptian women have more rights than women in most ancient countries. They can own property, and inherit it, and buy and sell, and make contracts, and go to court, and divorce their husbands, and keep their own property after divorce. Many marriage contracts, on papyrus, survive from this time, carefully recording what the wife brought to the marriage, and what she will receive if the marriage ends.

[pause]

{img:women-carrying-water-jars-heads-riverbank-evening-children}
The children help their parents, from a young age: herding the goats, and the geese, and scaring the birds away from the crops, and fetching water, and carrying food to the fields. They play in the river, and the canals, and the dust, with clay toys, and balls made of linen rags, and they swim like fish.

[long-pause]

In the evening, the farmers come home from the fields, as the sun goes down behind the western desert, which the Egyptians believe is the land of the dead, and the sky turns red, and gold, and purple.

[pause]

The families eat their supper, of bread, and onions, and beans, and lentils, and fish, and beer, in the courtyard, or on the roof. The donkeys bray, and the dogs bark, and the frogs croak in the canals. And then the village goes to sleep, under the bright stars of Egypt, which the priests have watched, and named, and recorded, for thousands of years.

[long-pause]

{img:western-desert-dunes-oasis-palms-spring-camels-evening}
Beyond the green valley of the Nile, on both sides, there is the desert.

[pause]

To the west, the great Western Desert, part of the Sahara, stretches away for more than a thousand kilometres: a vast sea of sand, and stone, and dunes, and flat plains of gravel, and strange rocks, carved by the wind into mushrooms, and towers. To the east, between the Nile and the Red Sea, the Eastern Desert, of rocky mountains, and dry valleys, where gold, and precious stones, and fine granite, and porphyry, are mined.

[long-pause]

The desert is not completely empty. Here and there, in the Western Desert, there are oases: places where water comes up from deep under the ground, in springs, and wells, and palm trees grow, and villages, and fields, and orchards, in the middle of the sand.

[pause]

The most famous is the oasis of Siwa, far out in the desert, near the border of Libya, with its lakes, and palm groves, and its temple of the god Ammon, whose oracle was famous all over the Greek world. Three hundred years before Cleopatra, Alexander the Great himself made the long, dangerous journey across the desert to Siwa, to ask the oracle a question. The priest greeted him as the son of the god. And nobody knows what else the oracle told him. He never told anyone.

[pause]

{img:siwa-oasis-temple-ruins-palm-groves-salt-lake-sunset}
In the desert, there are animals too: gazelles, and ibexes, and foxes, with huge ears, called fennecs, and jackals, and hyenas, and ostriches, and lizards, and scorpions. And the people of the desert, the nomads, with their camels, and their goats, and their tents, who travel from oasis to oasis, and know the desert's secret wells, and paths.

[long-pause]

{img:desert-night-stars-dunes-cold-clear-silence-milky-way}
The Egyptians fear the desert, a little. It is the red land, the land of Set, the god of chaos and storms, and of the dead, whose tombs are built at its edge. But they also love its silence, and its beauty: the golden dunes at sunset, the cold, clear nights, with more stars than you can count, the strange shapes of the rocks, the pure, dry air.

[pause]

And many of the first Christian monks, a few hundred years after Cleopatra, will go out into the Egyptian desert, to live alone, as hermits, in caves, and huts, in the silence, praying, and found the first monasteries in the world.

[long-pause]

{img:dendera-temple-hathor-columns-colourful-ceiling-light-shafts}
Egypt, in Cleopatra's time, is full of temples, and full of gods.

[pause]

The Egyptians have hundreds of gods: Ra, the sun god, who sails across the sky every day in his boat. Osiris, the god of the dead, and of the rebirth of the crops. Isis, his wife, the great mother goddess, the goddess of magic, and healing, and love. Horus, their son, the falcon god, the protector of the king. Hathor, the cow goddess, of joy, and music, and love. Anubis, the jackal god, who guides the dead. Thoth, the ibis god, of wisdom, and writing. And many more.

[long-pause]

Many of the gods are shown as animals, or with animal heads, because the Egyptians believe that the gods can live in animals, and that certain animals are sacred to them: the cat to Bastet, the ibis to Thoth, the falcon to Horus, the crocodile to Sobek, the bull to Apis.

[pause]

At Memphis, the old capital, there is a living sacred bull, the Apis bull, a black bull, with special white markings, who lives in a temple, in great comfort, and is worshipped as the living image of the god Ptah. When he passes away, he is mummified, and buried in a great underground tomb, with enormous stone coffins. And the priests search the whole of Egypt for a new bull, with the right markings.

[pause]

{img:apis-bull-temple-courtyard-priests-garlands-memphis-calm}
In the year 51, at the very beginning of her reign, Cleopatra herself sailed up the Nile, to the city of Hermonthis, to take part in the installation of a new sacred bull, the Buchis bull. An inscription records that she rowed the sacred boat herself, and that all the people saw her.

[long-pause]

The Ptolemies built many great temples, in the old Egyptian style, all along the Nile. Some of them are among the best preserved temples of ancient Egypt, and can still be seen today.

[pause]

At Dendera, there is the great temple of Hathor, the goddess of love, and joy, and music, with tall columns, topped with the face of the goddess, and a ceiling painted with the stars, and the signs of the zodiac, in blue and gold. On the back wall of the temple, outside, there is a huge carving of Cleopatra, and her son, Caesarion, making offerings to the gods, in the old Egyptian style.

[pause]

At Edfu, there is the great temple of Horus, the falcon god, almost complete, with its huge gateway, its pylons, and its courtyards, and its dark, mysterious sanctuary. At Philae, on an island in the Nile, in the far south, there is the beautiful temple of Isis, surrounded by the river, with palm trees.

[long-pause]

The temples are not places where ordinary people go to worship. They are the houses of the gods, and only the priests can enter the inner parts.

[pause]

Every day, in every temple, the priests wake the god, in the morning, in the dark sanctuary, by opening the doors of the shrine, where the statue of the god lives. They wash the statue, and dress it, in fine linen, and anoint it with perfumed oil, and offer it food, and drink, and incense, and flowers, and sing hymns. In the evening, they close the shrine again, and the god sleeps.

[pause]

{img:priests-white-linen-shaved-heads-incense-sanctuary-candles}
The priests wear white linen, and shave their heads, and their whole bodies, to be pure, and wash several times a day, and do not eat certain foods, like fish, and beans.

[long-pause]

But on festival days, the god comes out of the temple, in a golden boat, carried on the shoulders of the priests, in a great procession, with music, and dancing, and singing, and incense, and the people line the streets, and the river banks, to see the god pass, and ask him questions, and pray.

[pause]

Once a year, at the great festival of the Beautiful Meeting, the statue of Hathor, from Dendera, sails up the Nile, in her golden boat, to visit her husband, Horus, at Edfu, a journey of many days, with crowds of pilgrims following, and feasting, and celebrating, along the way.

[pause]

{img:golden-sacred-boat-procession-nile-crowds-music-festival}
Of all the gods, the most loved, in Cleopatra's time, is Isis: the faithful wife, and loving mother, who brought her husband back to life, and protected her baby son. Her worship is spreading, all over the Mediterranean world, even to Rome. And Cleopatra presents herself to the people of Egypt as Isis, the new Isis, the living goddess on earth.

[long-pause]

{img:painted-coffin-portrait-tomb-chapel-flowers-quiet-light}
The Egyptians, in the time of Cleopatra, still believe, as their ancestors have believed for three thousand years, that life goes on after death.

[pause]

They believe that every person has several souls: the ka, the life force, which needs food and drink, and the ba, the personality, shown as a bird with a human head, which can fly out of the tomb, in the daytime, and visit the world of the living, and come back at night. And that, if the body is preserved, and the right prayers are said, and the person has lived a good life, they will live forever, in the Field of Reeds, a beautiful land, like Egypt, but even better, where the crops grow tall, and there is no sorrow.

[long-pause]

In the old story, the dead person is led by Anubis, the jackal god, into the hall of judgment, where their heart is weighed, on a great scale, against a feather, the feather of Maat, the goddess of truth, and justice. If the heart is light, and pure, the person passes into the afterlife. And the dead person must say, before the gods: I have not done wrong. I have not stolen. I have not lied. I have not caused anyone to weep. I have not let anyone go hungry.

[pause]

So the Egyptians try to live good lives, kind and honest, so that their hearts will be light, at the end.

[pause]

{img:weighing-of-heart-scale-feather-anubis-papyrus-painting-calm}
To preserve the body, the Egyptians make mummies, as they have done for thousands of years. In Cleopatra's time, it is still done, for those who can afford it: the body is carefully dried, with natron, a kind of salt, from the desert lakes, and anointed with oils, and resins, and spices, and wrapped in many layers of fine linen, with amulets, little charms, placed among the wrappings, for protection. Then it is placed in a painted coffin, and buried in a tomb, with food, and drink, and things the person will need in the next life.

[long-pause]

Even the Greeks and Romans living in Egypt took up the custom. In the following centuries, many of them were mummified, in the Egyptian way, with a beautiful portrait of the dead person, painted on a wooden board, in wax, in the Greek style, placed over the face of the mummy, so lifelike that they seem to look at us, across two thousand years. Hundreds of these portraits, from the Fayum, an oasis south of Memphis, can be seen in museums today: men and women, young and old, children, with their dark eyes, and their jewellery, and their hairstyles, gazing out, calmly, as if they were alive.

[pause]

{img:fayum-style-portrait-young-woman-dark-eyes-gold-earrings-wood}
And the families visited the tombs of their dead, on festival days, and brought them food, and flowers, and had picnics, by the tombs, and remembered them, and talked to them. Because, for the Egyptians, the dead were not gone. They were only living somewhere else.

[long-pause]

{img:caesar-arriving-alexandria-harbour-48bc-galleys-crowds-palace}
In the autumn of 48 before Christ, Julius Caesar, the greatest general in Rome, arrived in Alexandria, with a small army, in pursuit of his rival, Pompey the Great, whom he had defeated in a civil war.

[pause]

But Pompey was already dead. When he landed on the coast of Egypt, a few days before, asking for help, the advisers of the young king Ptolemy, thinking to please Caesar, had taken his life, on the beach. And when Caesar arrived, and heard what they had done, he turned away, it is said, and wept.

[long-pause]

Caesar decided to stay in Alexandria, in the palace, and settle the quarrel between Cleopatra and her brother, which was tearing Egypt apart. And he sent for both of them.

[pause]

But Cleopatra was outside the city, with her army, and her brother's soldiers were guarding the roads, and the palace. How could she reach Caesar?

[pause]

Plutarch tells the story. She took a small boat, with only one companion, a Sicilian friend, called Apollodorus, and sailed into the harbour, at dusk, and landed near the palace. And since there was no other way to get in unseen, she lay down inside a sack for bedding, and Apollodorus tied it up with a strap, and carried it on his back, through the gates, and into Caesar's room. And there, he set it down, and untied it, and out stepped the queen.

[pause]

Later stories said it was a carpet. But Plutarch says a bedding sack.

[long-pause]

Caesar was fifty-two. Cleopatra was twenty-one. He was charmed by her courage, and her cleverness, and her wit. And they became lovers.

[pause]

Caesar decided that Cleopatra and her brother should rule together, as their father had wished. But the brother's advisers would not accept it, and the people of Alexandria rose up, and the Egyptian army besieged Caesar, and Cleopatra, in the palace, for months, through the winter.

[pause]

{img:palace-under-siege-quiet-night-walls-torches-harbour-distant}
In the end, Roman reinforcements came, and the young king was drowned in the Nile, fleeing after a battle, and Cleopatra became queen again, this time with another younger brother, Ptolemy the Fourteenth, who was only about twelve, as her husband, in name.

[long-pause]

And then, in the spring of 47, Caesar and Cleopatra, it is said, went on a journey up the Nile together, on the royal barge, a great floating palace, with banqueting halls, and bedrooms, and gardens, and shrines, and colonnades, rowed by hundreds of oarsmen, with a fleet of four hundred ships following.

[pause]

They sailed south, past the pyramids, and the temples, and the green fields, and the villages, and the palm groves, toward Ethiopia. Some historians doubt that this journey happened, or think that it was much shorter. But it is one of the great love stories of history.

[pause]

{img:royal-nile-barge-floating-palace-sunset-temples-banks-fleet}
In the summer of 47, after Caesar had left Egypt, to fight more wars, Cleopatra gave birth to a son. She called him Ptolemy Caesar. But the people of Alexandria called him Caesarion, little Caesar.

[long-pause]

Two years later, Cleopatra went to Rome, with her little son, and her court, to visit Caesar, and lived in his villa, in his gardens, across the river Tiber. The Romans were fascinated by her, and suspicious of her, and gossiped about her.

[pause]

She was still in Rome, on the Ides of March, in 44 before Christ, when Caesar was killed, in the Senate. And soon afterwards, she went home, to Alexandria, with her son, then three years old. And a few months later, her young brother passed away, and she made little Caesarion her co-ruler, as Ptolemy the Fifteenth.

[long-pause]

{img:golden-barge-purple-sails-silver-oars-cydnus-river-tarsus-41bc}
After the death of Caesar, there was civil war in Rome again. And when it was over, the Roman world was divided between two men: Octavian, Caesar's young adopted son, in the west, and Mark Antony, Caesar's old friend and general, in the east.

[pause]

In the summer of 41 before Christ, Antony was at Tarsus, a city in the south of what is now Turkey, and he summoned Cleopatra to come to him, to explain why she had not helped him in the war.

[long-pause]

She came, but in her own way, and in her own time.

[pause]

Plutarch describes her arrival, in one of the most famous passages in ancient literature. She sailed up the river Cydnus, to Tarsus, in a barge with a gilded stern, and purple sails, and oars of silver, which kept time to the music of flutes, and pipes, and lyres. She lay under a canopy of cloth of gold, dressed as Venus, the goddess of love, in a painting. Beautiful young boys, dressed as Cupids, stood on either side of her, fanning her. Her maids, dressed as sea nymphs, and Graces, steered the boat, and handled the ropes.

[pause]

{img:queen-under-gold-canopy-barge-cupids-fanning-maids-ropes}
And wonderful perfumes, from the burning incense, drifted from the ship to the banks of the river, where great crowds gathered, and the word went round that Venus had come to feast with Bacchus, for the good of Asia.

[pause]

Shakespeare, sixteen hundred years later, put this passage almost word for word into his play, Antony and Cleopatra: the barge she sat in, like a burnished throne, burned on the water.

[long-pause]

Antony invited her to dinner. She invited him instead to dine with her, on her ship. And he came, and was dazzled: by the thousands of lamps, hung in patterns, and the gold plates, and the jewelled cups, and the food, and the queen.

[pause]

Mark Antony was about forty-two. He was a big, strong, handsome, cheerful man, with a thick neck, and curly hair, and a beard, who liked to think that he looked like Hercules, from whom his family claimed descent. He was a brave soldier, much loved by his men, and generous, and fond of feasts, and wine, and laughter, and jokes.

[pause]

He fell in love with Cleopatra. And that winter, he went with her to Alexandria, and stayed there, with her, instead of going back to Rome.

[long-pause]

In Alexandria, Antony and Cleopatra lived a life of pleasure, and splendour, and fun.

[pause]

They formed a club, which they called the Inimitable Livers, whose members feasted together every day, with enormous, extravagant banquets. Plutarch's grandfather heard about it from a doctor, who had been a student in Alexandria at the time, who told him that he once visited the royal kitchens, and saw eight wild boars roasting at once. He thought there must be a huge party. But the cook laughed, and said there were only about twelve guests. But since nobody knew exactly when Antony would want to eat, they roasted eight boars, one after another, at different times, so that one would always be perfect, whenever he asked.

[pause]

{img:royal-kitchen-alexandria-many-spits-cooks-busy-firelight}
They wandered the streets of Alexandria at night, disguised as servants, and knocked on the doors of houses, and played jokes on the people. They went fishing together. Once, the story goes, Antony was not catching anything, and secretly told some fishermen to dive under the water, and fasten fish, already caught, onto his hook. Cleopatra saw through the trick. And the next day, she invited friends to watch, and when Antony let down his line, she had a diver fasten a salted fish, from the Black Sea, onto his hook. And when he pulled it up, everyone laughed. And Cleopatra said: leave the fishing rod to us, the rulers of Pharos and Canopus. Your game is cities, and kingdoms, and continents.

[long-pause]

In the spring of 40, Antony went back to Rome, and, to make peace with Octavian, he married Octavian's sister, Octavia, a good and gentle woman. And for three years, he did not see Cleopatra.

[pause]

Meanwhile, in Alexandria, Cleopatra gave birth to twins: a boy and a girl, Alexander Helios, the sun, and Cleopatra Selene, the moon.

[pause]

{img:queen-with-twin-babies-nursery-palace-alexandria-sea-light}
But in 37, Antony came back to the East, and sent for Cleopatra again. And from then on, they stayed together. And in 36, they had another son, Ptolemy Philadelphus.

[long-pause]

{img:palace-throne-room-alexandria-greek-egyptian-decor-courtiers-light}
What was life like in the palace of the Ptolemies, in Alexandria, in Cleopatra's time?

[pause]

The palace was a world of its own: a great complex of buildings, by the sea, full of people. There were the royal family, and their children, and their nurses, and tutors. The courtiers, called the friends of the king, and the kinsmen of the king, with their titles, and their purple robes, and their gold. The ministers, and the generals, and the officials, and the scribes. The guards, Macedonian and Greek and Gallic. The doctors, and the astronomers, and the scholars. The cooks, and the servants, and the musicians, and the dancers.

[long-pause]

The rooms were splendid, in a mixture of Greek and Egyptian styles: floors of marble, and mosaic, walls of coloured stone, and painted plaster, columns of granite, and ebony, and ivory, and gold. There were gardens, with fountains, and shady colonnades, and pools, and rare plants, and birds, and animals, from Africa, and India.

[pause]

The Roman poet Lucan, describing a banquet that Cleopatra gave for Julius Caesar, says that the roof was coated with gold, and the walls with marble, and onyx, and ebony, and the doors with tortoiseshell, and emeralds, and that the couches were covered with purple, and gold, and that the guests were served by slaves of every colour, from every land.

[pause]

{img:queen-reading-reports-scribes-ministers-morning-palace-office}
But Cleopatra was not idle. Her days were full of work. Every morning, she met her ministers, and read the reports from all over Egypt: about the harvests, and the flood, and the taxes, and the grain stores, and the temples, and the trade. She received ambassadors, from Rome, and Judaea, and Arabia, and Parthia, and spoke to them in their own languages. She judged disputes, and signed orders, and wrote letters. She managed one of the richest countries in the world.

[long-pause]

And she was a mother, of four children, who lived with her in the palace, with their tutors, and their nurses. Caesarion had a famous tutor, a Greek scholar, named Rhodon. And the twins, and little Ptolemy, were taught by a scholar named Nicolaus of Damascus, who later wrote a history of the world.

[pause]

In the evenings, there were banquets, and music, and plays, and poetry, and conversation, with scholars, and philosophers, and visitors, and friends.

[pause]

{img:palace-terrace-evening-sea-view-children-mother-lamps-calm}
And sometimes, perhaps, Cleopatra walked alone, in the evening, on the terrace of her palace, on the little island of Antirhodos, by the sea, and looked out over the harbour, at the lighthouse, shining on the dark water, and at the ships, coming and going, and thought about the future of her children, and her country.

[long-pause]

{img:banquet-hall-queen-pearl-earring-cup-vinegar-guests-watching}
There is a famous story, told by the Roman writer Pliny, about a bet between Antony and Cleopatra.

[pause]

Cleopatra bet Antony that she could spend ten million sesterces, an enormous fortune, on a single dinner. Antony did not believe her. And so, she gave a banquet, which was splendid, but not much more splendid than usual. Antony laughed, and asked how much it had cost.

[long-pause]

Then Cleopatra ordered the second course to be brought. The servants placed in front of her a single cup, filled with vinegar.

[pause]

She was wearing, in her ears, two pearls, the largest in the world, which had come down to her from the kings of the East. She took one of them from her ear, and dropped it into the vinegar, and when it had dissolved, she drank it. And she was about to do the same with the other, when the judge of the bet stopped her, and declared that she had won.

[pause]

Would vinegar really dissolve a pearl? Scientists have tried it. Strong vinegar can dissolve a pearl, slowly, especially if it is crushed. Perhaps Cleopatra knew this. Or perhaps it is only a story. Pliny says that the second pearl was later cut in two, and made into earrings for a statue of Venus, in the Pantheon, in Rome.

[long-pause]

In 34 before Christ, after a war in Armenia, Antony held a great ceremony, in Alexandria, which the Romans called the Donations of Alexandria.

[pause]

In the great gymnasium of the city, before a huge crowd, on a silver platform, there were two golden thrones, for Antony and Cleopatra, and smaller thrones, for the children. Cleopatra was dressed as the goddess Isis.

[pause]

{img:silver-platform-golden-thrones-royal-family-gymnasium-crowd}
Antony declared Cleopatra the Queen of Kings, and Caesarion, then thirteen, the King of Kings, and the true son of Julius Caesar. And he gave kingdoms to the children: to little Alexander Helios, Armenia, and Media, and Parthia, which had not even been conquered yet. To Cleopatra Selene, Cyrenaica, and Libya. And to the little Ptolemy, only two, Syria, and Phoenicia, and Cilicia.

[pause]

The little boys were dressed in the costumes of the lands they were to rule: Alexander in Median dress, with a tall tiara, and Ptolemy in Macedonian boots, and cloak, and cap.

[long-pause]

In Rome, this caused a scandal. Octavian used it to turn the Roman people against Antony. He said that Antony had been bewitched by the Egyptian queen, and was giving away the lands of Rome to her and her children, and wanted to make Alexandria the capital of the world, instead of Rome.

[pause]

Octavian found Antony's will, which had been left with the Vestal Virgins, in Rome, and read it aloud to the Senate. In it, Antony asked to be buried in Alexandria, beside Cleopatra, even if he died in Rome. The Romans were outraged.

[pause]

{img:roman-senate-reading-document-senators-murmuring-candles}
In 32 before Christ, Rome declared war. Not on Antony, but on Cleopatra. And everyone knew that the last great war of the Roman Republic was about to begin.

[long-pause]

In Alexandria, meanwhile, life went on, much as before. The people of the city went to the markets, and the baths, and the theatre, and the races. The ships came and went in the harbour. The scholars read in the Library. And the farmers along the Nile watched the river rise and fall, as they always had, and paid their taxes, and hoped for a good harvest, and paid little attention to the quarrels of the great.

[pause]

But in the palace, by the sea, Cleopatra and Antony began to prepare their fleet, and their army, and their treasure, for the war that was coming.

[long-pause]

{img:actium-bay-ambracian-gulf-fleets-anchored-greek-coast-summer}
In the summer of 31 before Christ, the two great fleets face each other, off the western coast of Greece, at a place called Actium, at the entrance to a wide bay, the Ambracian Gulf.

[pause]

Antony and Cleopatra have gathered a great army, and a great fleet, of more than two hundred warships, many of them huge, with ten banks of oars, and towers on their decks. Cleopatra has brought sixty ships of her own, from Egypt, and the treasure of Egypt, to pay for the war. She is there herself, with her fleet, in her flagship, the Antonia.

[long-pause]

Octavian's fleet is commanded by his friend, Marcus Agrippa, a brilliant admiral. His ships are smaller, and lighter, and faster, and his sailors are more experienced.

[pause]

All through the summer, Antony's army and fleet are trapped in the bay, blockaded by Agrippa. Their camp is in a marshy, unhealthy place, and many of their soldiers and rowers fall ill. Food runs short. Some of their allies, and Roman friends, desert them, and go over to Octavian.

[pause]

At last, Antony and Cleopatra decide that they must break out of the bay, with the fleet, and sail back to Egypt.

[long-pause]

On the second of September, 31 before Christ, the fleets meet, at the mouth of the bay.

[pause]

We will watch, as always, from far away: from a hill, on the shore, looking down at the blue sea, on a calm, bright September morning, where hundreds of ships are moving, slowly, in long lines, their oars rising and falling, their sails furled, small and distant, like toys on a pond.

[pause]

For hours, the two fleets face each other, waiting, and then, around midday, when the wind rises, as it does every day in that place, they begin to move, and the battle begins, far out on the water.

[long-pause]

And then, in the middle of the afternoon, something happens that people will argue about for two thousand years.

[pause]

Cleopatra's sixty ships, which have been waiting behind the battle lines, suddenly raise their sails, and sail straight through the gap in the middle of the fighting, out to the open sea, southward, toward Egypt. And Antony, seeing her go, leaves his flagship, and gets into a smaller, faster ship, and follows her.

[pause]

{img:purple-sails-ships-sailing-away-open-sea-afternoon-wind}
Was it a betrayal, or a panic, or a plan? Some historians think that it was their plan all along: to break out, with the treasure, and as many ships as possible, and escape to Egypt, and fight again there. Others think that Cleopatra lost her nerve, or that Antony did. We will never know.

[long-pause]

Most of Antony's fleet, left behind, surrendered to Octavian. And a few days later, his great army, on the shore, waiting for him to come back, surrendered too.

[pause]

Antony caught up with Cleopatra's ship, and was taken on board. Plutarch says that he went and sat alone in the bow, with his head in his hands, and did not speak to her, or see her, for three days. Then her maids persuaded them to speak to each other, and to eat together, and sleep. And they sailed on, together, to Egypt.

[pause]

{img:man-sitting-alone-ship-bow-head-in-hands-sea-evening}
In Alexandria, Cleopatra sailed into the harbour with her ships decorated with garlands, as if she had won a victory, so that the people would not rise against her before she could prepare.

[pause]

But they both knew that Octavian would come.

[long-pause]

{img:alexandria-palace-sea-evening-quiet-lamps-autumn-30bc}
The last year of Cleopatra's life, from the autumn of 31 to the summer of 30 before Christ, is a strange, sad, and beautiful time.

[pause]

She does not give up. She makes plans. She has her ships dragged across the narrow neck of land, from the Mediterranean, to the Red Sea, perhaps hoping to escape, with her children, and her treasure, to India, or Arabia. But the Arabs of the desert burn the ships.

[long-pause]

She sends messages to Octavian, asking him to let her children rule Egypt. He does not answer, or answers coldly.

[pause]

And meanwhile, in Alexandria, Antony and Cleopatra disband their old club, the Inimitable Livers, and form a new one, with their friends, which they call the Partners in Death, the friends who will die together. And they spend the winter feasting, and drinking, and giving parties, with music, and dancing, every night, as if there were no tomorrow.

[pause]

{img:evening-feast-palace-hall-friends-lamps-music-quiet-sadness}
Caesarion, now sixteen, is enrolled as a young man, among the ephebes, the young men of the city, in a great ceremony, and so is Antyllus, Antony's son by his first marriage. The people of Alexandria feast and celebrate for many days. Cleopatra wants them to see that Egypt has a king, a grown young man, who can rule after her.

[long-pause]

She also builds a tomb for herself: a great building, of stone, next to the temple of Isis, by the sea, near the palace. And into it, she has carried all her greatest treasures: gold, and silver, and pearls, and emeralds, and ebony, and ivory, and cinnamon, and great quantities of wood, and kindling. So that, if Octavian tries to take her treasure, she can burn it all.

[pause]

In the summer of 30 before Christ, Octavian arrives at Alexandria, with his army.

[pause]

On the last night before Octavian enters the city, Plutarch tells, in the middle of the night, when all was quiet in Alexandria, and everyone was full of fear, the people suddenly heard the sound of music, of instruments of every kind, and voices singing, and the noise of a joyful crowd, dancing, and shouting, like the followers of Dionysus, the god of wine, passing through the city, from the middle of the city, to the gate that faced the enemy, and then going out, and fading away. And the people believed that it was the god Dionysus, whom Antony loved, and imitated, leaving him.

[long-pause]

The next day, the first of August, Antony's last soldiers, and his fleet, went over to Octavian. And Antony, believing that all was lost, and hearing a false report that Cleopatra had died, ended his own life. We will not describe it. He was brought, at the end, to Cleopatra, in her tomb, and passed away in her arms.

[pause]

Some days later, Cleopatra herself chose to end her life, rather than be taken to Rome, and led in chains through the streets, in Octavian's triumph.

[pause]

{img:empty-royal-chamber-golden-couch-diadem-figs-basket-soft-light}
The story says that a basket of figs was brought to her, by a peasant, and that inside the figs, there was a little snake, an asp, an Egyptian cobra, the royal snake of Egypt, the symbol of the pharaohs, which the Egyptians believed would make its victim immortal. She was found by Octavian's messengers, lying on a golden couch, dressed in her royal robes, with her two faithful maids, Iras and Charmion, beside her.

[pause]

One of the Romans asked Charmion: was this well done? And Charmion answered: it was very well done, and fitting for a queen, descended from so many kings.

[long-pause]

Cleopatra was thirty-nine. She had ruled Egypt for twenty-two years.

[pause]

Octavian, in spite of his anger, gave orders that she should be buried beside Antony, as she had wished, with royal honours, in her tomb, by the sea, in Alexandria. The tomb has never been found.

[pause]

{img:sea-at-dusk-alexandria-lighthouse-glow-quiet-calm-ending}
And with her death, three hundred years of the Ptolemies came to an end, and three thousand years of the pharaohs. Egypt became a province of Rome.

[long-pause]

{img:three-royal-children-palace-garden-alexandria-nurse-calm}
What became of Cleopatra's children?

[pause]

Caesarion, the son of Julius Caesar, was seventeen. Cleopatra had sent him away, south, with a tutor, and treasure, toward the Red Sea, hoping that he could escape to India. But his tutor persuaded him to come back, saying that Octavian would let him rule Egypt. Octavian's advisers said that two Caesars were too many. And the young man was lost. We know little more.

[long-pause]

But the three younger children, the twins, Alexander Helios and Cleopatra Selene, who were ten, and little Ptolemy Philadelphus, who was six, were taken to Rome. And there, they walked in Octavian's great triumph, in the year 29, through the streets of Rome, behind a statue of their mother.

[pause]

And then, something kind happened. They were given to Octavian's sister, Octavia, Antony's former wife, whom he had left for Cleopatra. And Octavia, a good and generous woman, took them into her own household, and brought them up, with her own children, and Antony's children by his first wife, in her house on the Palatine Hill, in Rome, as part of her family.

[pause]

We do not know what happened to the two boys. They disappear from history. Perhaps they passed away young. Perhaps they lived quietly, under other names.

[long-pause]

But we know what happened to Cleopatra Selene.

[pause]

When she was about fifteen, Octavian, now the emperor Augustus, arranged for her to marry a young prince, Juba, the son of the king of Numidia, in North Africa, who had also been brought up in Rome, as a captive, after his father's defeat. Juba was a scholar, and a writer, who loved books, and art, and learning. And Augustus made Juba and Selene the king and queen of Mauretania, a great kingdom in North Africa, in what is now Algeria and Morocco.

[pause]

{img:caesarea-mauretania-city-harbour-palace-north-africa-coast}
They ruled there, together, for many years, from their capital, which they called Caesarea, on the coast of Algeria, and made it into a beautiful city, like a little Alexandria, with a library, and a theatre, and temples, and Greek and Roman and Egyptian statues, and scholars.

[long-pause]

Cleopatra Selene put her own name on the coins of the kingdom, in Greek: Cleopatra the Queen. And on some of them, she put a crocodile, the symbol of Egypt, and her mother's land.

[pause]

She and Juba had a son, whom they named Ptolemy, after her family. He became king of Mauretania after them.

[pause]

{img:silver-coin-crocodile-greek-letters-queen-name-hand-light}
And so, in a little kingdom in North Africa, the daughter of Cleopatra kept her mother's memory, and her family's name, and something of the spirit of Alexandria, alive, for another generation.

[long-pause]

{img:roman-prefect-alexandria-grain-ships-loading-harbour}
After the death of Cleopatra, Egypt became the personal property of Octavian, who, three years later, took the name Augustus, and became the first Roman emperor.

[pause]

He did not make Egypt an ordinary Roman province, ruled by the Senate. He kept it for himself, ruled by a governor, a prefect, whom he chose. And no Roman senator was allowed to visit Egypt without his permission. Because Egypt was too rich, and too important, to risk anyone else controlling it.

[long-pause]

Above all, Egypt was the breadbasket of Rome. Every year, great fleets of grain ships sailed from Alexandria to Rome, carrying the wheat of Egypt, to feed the people of Rome. Perhaps a third of all the grain eaten in Rome came from Egypt.

[pause]

Augustus visited Alexandria, after Cleopatra's death. He went to see the tomb of Alexander the Great, and looked at the body of the great conqueror, in its coffin, and laid a golden crown on it, and scattered flowers. And when they asked him if he also wanted to see the tombs of the Ptolemies, he said, it is reported: I came to see a king, not corpses.

[pause]

{img:roman-visitor-at-tomb-of-alexander-flowers-golden-crown}
Alexandria remained a great city, for hundreds of years, under the Romans: the second city of the Roman Empire, rich, and busy, and learned. The Museum and the Library went on, for a long time. Great scholars still worked there: the astronomer Ptolemy, the geographer, a hundred and fifty years later, who wrote books about the stars, and maps of the world, which were used for fourteen hundred years.

[long-pause]

And in the villages along the Nile, life went on, as it always had. The river rose, and fell. The farmers ploughed, and sowed, and harvested. The priests served the gods, in the old temples. And on the walls of the temples, the Roman emperors were shown as pharaohs, in the old Egyptian style, with their names in hieroglyphs, making offerings to the gods, as Cleopatra had been shown before them.

[pause]

The old Egyptian religion slowly faded, over the following centuries, as Christianity spread. The last hieroglyphic inscription was carved at Philae, in 394 after Christ. And then, nobody could read hieroglyphs any more, for fourteen hundred years, until a young Frenchman, Jean-François Champollion, deciphered them, in 1822, with the help of a stone found at Rosetta, near Alexandria, with the same text in Greek, and in Egyptian, written by priests in the time of the Ptolemies.

[pause]

{img:rosetta-stone-three-scripts-museum-close-soft-light}
And Cleopatra herself became a legend: the most famous queen in history. Written about by the Roman poets, as a dangerous enemy. By Plutarch, with fascination. By Shakespeare, as a great tragic heroine. Painted by countless artists. Played in films by great actresses.

[pause]

But perhaps the truest picture of her is still the one on her little coins: a clever, determined woman, with a strong nose, and her hair tied back, who tried, for twenty-two years, to keep her ancient country free.

[long-pause]

{img:cleopatras-needle-obelisk-thames-embankment-london-evening}
Cleopatra has been remembered, and imagined, and reimagined, for two thousand years.

[pause]

The Roman poets wrote about her as a dangerous enemy: Horace, who wrote a poem celebrating her death, called her a fatal monster, but also admitted that she died nobly, without fear, like a queen. Plutarch wrote about her with fascination, and gave us most of the stories we know.

[long-pause]

In 1606, William Shakespeare wrote his great play, Antony and Cleopatra, in which she is a passionate, proud, changeable, witty, unforgettable queen. Age cannot wither her, he wrote, nor custom stale her infinite variety.

[pause]

Painters painted her, again and again: at her banquet, with the pearl. Meeting Antony, on her golden barge. Dying, with the asp. In the twentieth century, she was played in films by great actresses, most famously Elizabeth Taylor, in 1963, in one of the most expensive films ever made.

[pause]

{img:painting-style-queen-on-golden-barge-river-old-master-colours}
And there are the two famous obelisks, called Cleopatra's Needles: tall, slender pillars of red granite, carved with hieroglyphs, which once stood in front of a temple in Alexandria, which Cleopatra began to build, for Julius Caesar, or for Antony. They are much older than Cleopatra: they were made for the pharaoh Thutmose the Third, fourteen hundred years before her, at Heliopolis, and brought to Alexandria by the Romans.

[pause]

In the nineteenth century, one of them was given to Britain, and taken to London, by sea, in a special iron cylinder, which nearly sank in a storm, and set up on the bank of the river Thames, in 1878, where it still stands. The other was given to the United States, and set up in Central Park, in New York, in 1881.

[long-pause]

{img:obelisk-central-park-new-york-trees-autumn-walkers}
So, if you walk along the Thames in London, in the evening, or through Central Park in New York, you can still see a piece of Cleopatra's Alexandria, standing tall, among the trees and the traffic, with its ancient hieroglyphs, worn by two thousand years of sun and rain, telling the names of pharaohs, that almost nobody passing by can read.

[long-pause]

{img:alexandria-today-corniche-seafront-curving-bay-qaitbay-fort}
Today, Alexandria is a great modern city, the second city of Egypt, with more than five million people, stretching for many kilometres along the shore of the Mediterranean.

[pause]

Much of the ancient city lies buried, under the modern streets and buildings. Or under the sea. Because over the centuries, earthquakes, and tsunamis, and the slow sinking of the land, have pulled part of the ancient coast, and the palace quarter, beneath the water of the harbour.

[long-pause]

Along the seafront, there is a long, curving promenade, the Corniche, where people walk, in the evening, and sit in cafés, and fish, and watch the sun go down over the sea.

[pause]

At one end of the bay, on the site of the ancient lighthouse, there stands the fort of Qaitbay, built in the fifteenth century, with some of the stones of the Pharos, a sturdy, pale fortress, with towers, and walls, by the waves. You can walk around it, and look out to sea, where the light once shone.

[pause]

And under the water, in the harbour, divers have found the remains of the ancient city: great blocks of stone, from the lighthouse, and columns, and statues of the Ptolemies, as pharaohs, and sphinxes, and the foundations of the palaces, and the island of Antirhodos, where Cleopatra had her private palace. Since the 1990s, French and Egyptian archaeologists have explored and mapped them. Some of the statues have been lifted out of the sea, and can be seen in museums.

[long-pause]

On the shore, near the place where the ancient Library may have stood, there is a great new library, the Bibliotheca Alexandrina, opened in 2002: a huge round building, like a great disc, tilted toward the sea, like the sun rising, its walls of granite carved with the letters of every alphabet in the world. Inside, there is a vast reading room, on many levels, under a great roof, with light pouring in, and room for millions of books.

[pause]

In the Egyptian quarter of the city, on its hill, there is a single tall column of red granite, still standing, called Pompey's Pillar, from the Serapeum, the great temple of Serapis, set up in honour of a Roman emperor, three hundred years after Cleopatra. And near it, there are the catacombs, underground tombs, carved in the rock, decorated in a strange mixture of Greek, and Roman, and Egyptian styles.

[pause]

{img:bibliotheca-alexandrina-tilted-disc-building-sea-sunlight}
And along the Nile, the great temples of the Ptolemies still stand: Dendera, with Cleopatra and Caesarion on its back wall, and Edfu, and Kom Ombo, and Philae, which was moved, stone by stone, in the 1970s, to a new island, to save it from the waters of a great dam.

[long-pause]

The Nile still flows, from the south, through the desert, to the sea. Its flood no longer comes every summer, because of the great Aswan Dam, built in the 1960s, which holds back its waters, in a huge lake. But the green fields are still there, along its banks, and the palm trees, and the villages, and the feluccas, with their white sails, gliding on the water, in the evening light.

[pause]

So we leave Alexandria for tonight, with the evening wind coming in from the sea, and the light of the old lighthouse long gone, but the waves still breaking on the stones where it stood.

[pause]

{img:felucca-white-sail-nile-sunset-palms-calm-water}
The great library by the sea, the farmers watching the river rise, the cats asleep on the warm roofs, and a queen who loved her ancient country, and tried to keep it free.
