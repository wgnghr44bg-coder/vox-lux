Let us spend an ordinary day in Alexandria, in the time of Cleopatra, with an ordinary family.

The father is a Greek, a maker of glass, with a little workshop, in the city. Alexandria is famous for its glass: beautiful bottles, and bowls, and cups, and beads, in many colours, blue and green and purple and amber, which are sold all over the Mediterranean.

--

The family lives in a house of mud brick, plastered and painted, two or three storeys high, on a narrow street, near one of the canals. On the ground floor, there is the workshop, and the kitchen. Above, there are the rooms where the family lives and sleeps. And on the flat roof, there is a terrace, where they sit in the cool of the evening, and sleep, in the hottest nights of summer, under the stars.

The day begins at dawn. The sun rises over the Delta, red and gold, and the city wakes up. The birds sing in the palm trees. The donkeys bray. The water carriers come along the street, with their jars, and their skins of water, from the canal.

{img:water-carrier-donkey-jars-narrow-street-morning-alexandria}
The mother, and the daughters, and the slave woman, make breakfast: bread, baked fresh in the clay oven, in the courtyard, and a little cheese, and dates, and figs, and beer, or water mixed with wine.

--

The father goes down to the workshop, where the furnace is already hot. His apprentices, and his slaves, are already at work.

Glass is made from sand, and soda, from the salt lakes of Egypt, and lime, melted together in the furnace, at a great heat, until it becomes a glowing, liquid mass. In these very years, in Syria, and in Alexandria, a wonderful new invention is spreading: glassblowing. The craftsman takes a little blob of hot glass on the end of a long hollow pipe, and blows into it, and the glass swells, like a bubble, and he turns it, and shapes it, with tools, into a bottle, or a cup, or a bowl, quickly, before it cools. It is much faster than the old ways. And soon, glass will be cheap enough for ordinary people to use, all over the Roman world.

In the afternoon, the father goes to the baths, and then to the gymnasium, to exercise, and to talk with his friends, about business, and politics, and the queen, and the Romans.

--

The mother runs the household. She goes to the market, with a slave, to buy food: fish, from the sea, and the lake, and vegetables, onions, and leeks, and lettuce, and cucumbers, and lentils, and fruit, grapes, and pomegranates, and dates, and melons. She spins, and weaves linen, the fine white cloth of Egypt, for the family's clothes.

The children go to school, or play in the streets, and on the roof, with their toys: clay dolls, and little wooden horses on wheels, and balls, and spinning tops, and pet birds, and cats.

{img:children-playing-flat-roof-cats-toys-palm-trees-afternoon}
The cats of Egypt are everywhere, and greatly loved, and respected. The Egyptians believe that cats are sacred to the goddess Bastet. To harm a cat is a terrible crime. A Greek historian, Diodorus, who visited Alexandria in these years, saw a crowd of Egyptians attack a Roman, who had accidentally killed a cat, in the street, and nothing could save him, not even the king's officials.

--

In the evening, the family gathers on the roof, for supper, as the sun sets over the lake, in the west, in a great blaze of orange, and red, and purple, and the lighthouse begins to shine, out at sea.

The cool breeze comes in from the sea. The stars come out, bright and close. From the street below come the sounds of the evening: people talking, and laughing, and music, from a tavern, and a dog barking, and the call of the night watchman.

{img:family-supper-rooftop-sunset-lake-lighthouse-lamps-calm}
And then, the family lies down to sleep, on the roof, on mats, and cushions, under a light linen sheet, with the stars above, and the sound of the sea, far away, and the faint glow of the great lighthouse, on the horizon.
