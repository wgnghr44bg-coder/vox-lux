In the palace quarter of Alexandria, there is a place unlike any other in the ancient world: the Museum, and its great Library.

The Museum, in Greek the Mouseion, the house of the Muses, the goddesses of the arts and learning, is not a museum as we know it. It is more like a university, or an academy: a community of scholars, scientists, poets, and philosophers, from all over the Greek world, who live there, at the expense of the king, with free food and lodging, and a salary, and nothing to do but study, and think, and write, and teach.

--

There are lecture halls, and a great dining hall, where the scholars eat together, and covered walks, and gardens, and an observatory, and perhaps a zoo, and a botanical garden, with plants from all over the world.

And there is the Library: the greatest collection of books in the ancient world.

The books are scrolls, rolls of papyrus, the paper made in Egypt, from the stems of the papyrus reed. Nobody knows exactly how many there were. The ancient writers say four hundred thousand, or even seven hundred thousand scrolls. Perhaps that is too many. But it was certainly the largest library that had ever existed.

--

The Ptolemies wanted to collect every book in the world.

They sent agents all over the Mediterranean, to buy books. And they made a law, that every ship that came into the harbour of Alexandria must be searched for books. Any books found were taken to the Library, and copied, and the copies were given back to the owners, while the Library kept the originals.

One Ptolemy borrowed the official copies of the plays of the great Athenian tragic poets, Aeschylus, Sophocles, and Euripides, from Athens, and paid a huge deposit, fifteen talents of silver, as a guarantee that he would return them. And then he kept the originals, and sent back copies, and let Athens keep the deposit.

--

The scholars of the Museum did wonderful things.

Euclid, a mathematician, wrote a book on geometry, the Elements, which was used in schools for more than two thousand years. Eratosthenes, a librarian, measured the size of the earth, by comparing the length of shadows, at noon, on the same day, in Alexandria, and in Syene, far to the south, and was almost exactly right. Aristarchus of Samos suggested that the earth goes around the sun, and not the sun around the earth, eighteen hundred years before Copernicus.

{img:eratosthenes-measuring-shadow-stick-well-noon-sun-sand}
Doctors, like Herophilus, studied the human body, and the brain, and the nerves, and the pulse. Engineers, like Ctesibius, invented a water clock, and a water organ, and pumps. Scholars made the first careful editions of Homer's poems, comparing many copies, to find the true text. And seventy-two Jewish scholars, the story goes, translated the Hebrew Bible into Greek, here in Alexandria: the translation called the Septuagint.

--

In Cleopatra's time, the great age of the Museum is past, but it is still a famous place of learning, and scholars still come there from all over the world.

Some people say that part of the Library burned in 48 before Christ, when Julius Caesar was in Alexandria, and fires spread from the harbour. Others say that only some warehouses of books, near the docks, were burned, and that the Library itself survived. Historians still argue about it. The Library slowly declined, over the following centuries, and nobody knows exactly when it disappeared.

{img:empty-library-hall-afternoon-sunbeam-dust-scrolls-quiet}
But in the year 31, scholars still walk in its colonnades, and read its scrolls, and argue, and copy, and think, in the quiet halls, by the sea.
