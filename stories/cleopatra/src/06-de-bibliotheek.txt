In the palace quarter of Alexandria there is a place like no other in the ancient world. It is called the Museum, and it has a great Library.

The name Museum means the house of the Muses, the goddesses of the arts and learning. But it is not a museum as we know it. It is more like a university. Scholars, scientists, poets and thinkers from all over the Greek world live there, paid by the king. They get free food, a room and a salary, and they have nothing to do but study, think, write and teach.

--

There are lecture halls and a big dining hall where the scholars eat together. There are covered walks and gardens. There is a place to watch the stars, and perhaps a garden of plants from all over the world.

And there is the Library, the greatest collection of books in the ancient world.

The books are scrolls, long rolls of papyrus, the paper of Egypt. Nobody knows exactly how many there were. The old writers say four hundred thousand, or even seven hundred thousand. Perhaps that is too many. But it was certainly the biggest library that had ever existed.

--

The kings wanted to collect every book in the world.

They sent buyers all over the Mediterranean. And they made a law that every ship coming into the harbour must be searched for books. Any books found were taken to the Library and copied. The owners got the copies. The Library kept the originals.

One king borrowed the official copies of the great Greek plays from Athens. He paid a huge sum of silver as a promise to send them back. Then he kept the originals, sent back copies, and simply let Athens keep the silver.

--

The scholars here did wonderful things.

A man called Euclid wrote a book about geometry that was used in schools for more than two thousand years. A librarian called Eratosthenes measured the size of the whole earth. He compared the shadows at noon on the same day in two cities, far apart. And he came out almost exactly right. Another scholar suggested that the earth goes around the sun, eighteen hundred years before anyone in Europe believed it.

{img:eratosthenes-measuring-shadow-stick-well-noon-sun-sand}
Doctors studied the human body, the brain and the pulse. Engineers invented a water clock and pumps. And scholars compared many old copies of Homer's poems, to find the true words.

--

By Cleopatra's time, the greatest days of the Museum are past. But it is still a famous place of learning.

Some people say part of the Library burned in 48 before Christ, when Julius Caesar was in Alexandria and fires spread from the harbour. Others say only some storehouses of books near the docks burned. Historians still argue about it. Over the following centuries the Library slowly faded away, and nobody knows exactly when it disappeared.

{img:empty-library-hall-afternoon-sunbeam-dust-scrolls-quiet}
But in the year 31, scholars still walk in its halls and read its scrolls. They copy and argue and think, in the quiet rooms by the sea. And they hope that whatever happens in the wider world, their books will be left in peace.
