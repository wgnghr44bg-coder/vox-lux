In the summer of 31 before Christ, the two great fleets face each other, off the western coast of Greece, at a place called Actium, at the entrance to a wide bay, the Ambracian Gulf.

Antony and Cleopatra have gathered a great army, and a great fleet, of more than two hundred warships, many of them huge, with ten banks of oars, and towers on their decks. Cleopatra has brought sixty ships of her own, from Egypt, and the treasure of Egypt, to pay for the war. She is there herself, with her fleet, in her flagship, the Antonia.

--

Octavian's fleet is commanded by his friend, Marcus Agrippa, a brilliant admiral. His ships are smaller, and lighter, and faster, and his sailors are more experienced.

All through the summer, Antony's army and fleet are trapped in the bay, blockaded by Agrippa. Their camp is in a marshy, unhealthy place, and many of their soldiers and rowers fall ill. Food runs short. Some of their allies, and Roman friends, desert them, and go over to Octavian.

At last, Antony and Cleopatra decide that they must break out of the bay, with the fleet, and sail back to Egypt.

--

On the second of September, 31 before Christ, the fleets meet, at the mouth of the bay.

We will watch, as always, from far away: from a hill, on the shore, looking down at the blue sea, on a calm, bright September morning, where hundreds of ships are moving, slowly, in long lines, their oars rising and falling, their sails furled, small and distant, like toys on a pond.

For hours, the two fleets face each other, waiting, and then, around midday, when the wind rises, as it does every day in that place, they begin to move, and the battle begins, far out on the water.

--

And then, in the middle of the afternoon, something happens that people will argue about for two thousand years.

Cleopatra's sixty ships, which have been waiting behind the battle lines, suddenly raise their sails, and sail straight through the gap in the middle of the fighting, out to the open sea, southward, toward Egypt. And Antony, seeing her go, leaves his flagship, and gets into a smaller, faster ship, and follows her.

{img:purple-sails-ships-sailing-away-open-sea-afternoon-wind}
Was it a betrayal, or a panic, or a plan? Some historians think that it was their plan all along: to break out, with the treasure, and as many ships as possible, and escape to Egypt, and fight again there. Others think that Cleopatra lost her nerve, or that Antony did. We will never know.

--

Most of Antony's fleet, left behind, surrendered to Octavian. And a few days later, his great army, on the shore, waiting for him to come back, surrendered too.

Antony caught up with Cleopatra's ship, and was taken on board. Plutarch says that he went and sat alone in the bow, with his head in his hands, and did not speak to her, or see her, for three days. Then her maids persuaded them to speak to each other, and to eat together, and sleep. And they sailed on, together, to Egypt.

{img:man-sitting-alone-ship-bow-head-in-hands-sea-evening}
In Alexandria, Cleopatra sailed into the harbour with her ships decorated with garlands, as if she had won a victory, so that the people would not rise against her before she could prepare.

But they both knew that Octavian would come.
