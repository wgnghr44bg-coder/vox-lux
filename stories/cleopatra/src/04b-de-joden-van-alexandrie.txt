One of the biggest communities in Alexandria in Cleopatra's time is the Jewish community.

Jews have lived here since the city was founded, three hundred years before. Some came as soldiers for the kings. Others came as merchants, craftsmen and farmers. By Cleopatra's time there may be a hundred thousand of them or more, perhaps more than in Jerusalem itself. Most live in the east of the city, near the palace and the sea.

--

The Jews of Alexandria speak Greek in daily life. Many have Greek names, wear Greek clothes and go to the theatre. But they keep their own faith, their own laws and their own customs, like the Sabbath, the festivals and the food laws.

They have their own council of elders, their own courts and many synagogues. The Great Synagogue was so huge, it is said, that people at the back could not hear the reader. So an official stood in the middle and waved a flag, to show everyone when to say Amen.

{img:jewish-merchants-delta-quarter-street-greek-dress-market}
Because the Jews of Alexandria spoke Greek, many no longer understood Hebrew well. So about two hundred years before Cleopatra, the Hebrew Bible was translated into Greek, right here in Alexandria. The story goes that seventy-two scholars worked on a little island near the lighthouse, and finished in seventy-two days. This translation is still used today by the Greek Orthodox Church.

--

Every year, the Jews of Alexandria held a festival on that island to celebrate the translation, with picnics and prayers on the beach by the sea.

A little later, in the time of Jesus, a great Jewish thinker called Philo will live in Alexandria. He will write books in Greek that try to bring together the wisdom of the Bible and the ideas of the Greek thinkers.

{img:beach-picnic-families-pharos-island-festival-lighthouse-behind}
Cleopatra seems to have been friendly to the Jews of Alexandria, and protected their rights. There were sometimes tensions between them and the Greeks of the city. But Alexandria was a place where many peoples and many faiths lived side by side. They learned from each other, and sometimes quarrelled, as people in great cities always do. Everyone knew, though, that if Rome ever took over, all of them would have new masters.
