One of the largest communities in Alexandria, in the time of Cleopatra, is the Jewish community.

Jews have lived in Alexandria since the city was founded, three hundred years before. Some came as soldiers, in the armies of the Ptolemies. Some came as merchants, and craftsmen, and farmers. By Cleopatra's time, there are perhaps a hundred thousand or more, perhaps more than in Jerusalem itself. They live mostly in the Delta quarter, in the east of the city, near the palace, and the sea.

--

The Jews of Alexandria speak Greek, as their everyday language, and many of them have Greek names, and wear Greek clothes, and go to the Greek gymnasium, and the theatre. But they keep their own religion, and their own laws, and their own customs: the Sabbath, and the festivals, and the food laws.

They have their own council of elders, and their own courts, and many synagogues, all over the city. The greatest of them, the Great Synagogue, was so huge, it is said, that the people at the back could not hear the reader, and an official stood in the middle, and waved a flag, to show the congregation when to say Amen.

{img:jewish-merchants-delta-quarter-street-greek-dress-market}
Because the Jews of Alexandria spoke Greek, and many of them no longer understood Hebrew well, the Hebrew Bible was translated into Greek, here, in Alexandria, about two hundred years before Cleopatra. The story goes that seventy-two scholars, six from each of the twelve tribes of Israel, were invited by Ptolemy the Second, and worked on the island of Pharos, near the lighthouse, and finished the translation in seventy-two days. It is called the Septuagint, from the Latin word for seventy. It is still used today, by the Greek Orthodox Church.

--

Every year, the Jews of Alexandria held a festival, on the island of Pharos, to celebrate the translation, with picnics, and prayers, on the beach, by the sea.

A few years after Cleopatra, in the time of Jesus, a great Jewish philosopher, Philo of Alexandria, will live in the city, and write books, in Greek, which try to bring together the wisdom of the Bible and the philosophy of Plato. His books will be read by the early Christians, for centuries.

{img:beach-picnic-families-pharos-island-festival-lighthouse-behind}
Cleopatra, it seems, was friendly to the Jews of Alexandria, and protected their rights, although there were sometimes tensions between them and the Greeks of the city. Alexandria, in her time, was a city where many peoples, and many faiths, lived side by side, and learned from each other, and sometimes quarrelled, as people in great cities always do.
