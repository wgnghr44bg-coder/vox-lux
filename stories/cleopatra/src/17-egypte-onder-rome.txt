After the death of Cleopatra, Egypt became the personal property of Octavian, who, three years later, took the name Augustus, and became the first Roman emperor.

He did not make Egypt an ordinary Roman province, ruled by the Senate. He kept it for himself, ruled by a governor, a prefect, whom he chose. And no Roman senator was allowed to visit Egypt without his permission. Because Egypt was too rich, and too important, to risk anyone else controlling it.

--

Above all, Egypt was the breadbasket of Rome. Every year, great fleets of grain ships sailed from Alexandria to Rome, carrying the wheat of Egypt, to feed the people of Rome. Perhaps a third of all the grain eaten in Rome came from Egypt.

Augustus visited Alexandria, after Cleopatra's death. He went to see the tomb of Alexander the Great, and looked at the body of the great conqueror, in its coffin, and laid a golden crown on it, and scattered flowers. And when they asked him if he also wanted to see the tombs of the Ptolemies, he said, it is reported: I came to see a king, not corpses.

{img:roman-visitor-at-tomb-of-alexander-flowers-golden-crown}
Alexandria remained a great city, for hundreds of years, under the Romans: the second city of the Roman Empire, rich, and busy, and learned. The Museum and the Library went on, for a long time. Great scholars still worked there: the astronomer Ptolemy, the geographer, a hundred and fifty years later, who wrote books about the stars, and maps of the world, which were used for fourteen hundred years.

--

And in the villages along the Nile, life went on, as it always had. The river rose, and fell. The farmers ploughed, and sowed, and harvested. The priests served the gods, in the old temples. And on the walls of the temples, the Roman emperors were shown as pharaohs, in the old Egyptian style, with their names in hieroglyphs, making offerings to the gods, as Cleopatra had been shown before them.

The old Egyptian religion slowly faded, over the following centuries, as Christianity spread. The last hieroglyphic inscription was carved at Philae, in 394 after Christ. And then, nobody could read hieroglyphs any more, for fourteen hundred years, until a young Frenchman, Jean-François Champollion, deciphered them, in 1822, with the help of a stone found at Rosetta, near Alexandria, with the same text in Greek, and in Egyptian, written by priests in the time of the Ptolemies.

{img:rosetta-stone-three-scripts-museum-close-soft-light}
And Cleopatra herself became a legend: the most famous queen in history. Written about by the Roman poets, as a dangerous enemy. By Plutarch, with fascination. By Shakespeare, as a great tragic heroine. Painted by countless artists. Played in films by great actresses.

But perhaps the truest picture of her is still the one on her little coins: a clever, determined woman, with a strong nose, and her hair tied back, who tried, for twenty-two years, to keep her ancient country free.
