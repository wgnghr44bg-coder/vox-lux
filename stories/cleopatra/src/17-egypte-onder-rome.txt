After Cleopatra's death, Egypt belonged to Octavian personally. Three years later he took the name Augustus, and became the first Roman emperor.

He did not make Egypt an ordinary Roman province. He kept it for himself, ruled by a governor he chose. No Roman senator was even allowed to visit Egypt without his permission. Egypt was too rich and too important to let anyone else control it.

--

Above all, Egypt was the breadbasket of Rome. Every year, great fleets of grain ships sailed from Alexandria to Rome, carrying the wheat to feed the Roman people. Perhaps a third of all the grain eaten in Rome came from Egypt.

Augustus visited Alexandria after Cleopatra's death. He went to see the tomb of Alexander the Great. He looked at the body of the great conqueror in its coffin, laid a golden crown on it, and scattered flowers. When they asked if he also wanted to see the tombs of Cleopatra's family, he said: I came to see a king, not corpses.

{img:roman-visitor-at-tomb-of-alexander-flowers-golden-crown}
Alexandria stayed a great city for hundreds of years under the Romans. It was the second city of the empire, rich, busy and full of learning. Great scholars still worked there. One of them, a hundred and fifty years later, wrote books about the stars and drew maps of the world that were used for fourteen hundred years.

--

And in the villages along the Nile, life went on as it always had. The river rose and fell. The farmers ploughed, sowed and harvested. The priests served the old gods. On the temple walls, the Roman emperors were now shown as pharaohs, with their names in hieroglyphs, just as Cleopatra had been before them.

Over the following centuries, the old Egyptian religion slowly faded as Christianity spread. The last hieroglyphs were carved more than four hundred years after Cleopatra. After that, nobody could read them for fourteen hundred years. Then, in 1822, a young Frenchman called Champollion finally cracked the code. He used a stone found near Alexandria, with the same text written in Greek and in Egyptian. Priests had carved it in the time of Cleopatra's family.

{img:rosetta-stone-three-scripts-museum-close-soft-light}
And Cleopatra herself became a legend, the most famous queen in history. The Roman poets wrote about her as a dangerous enemy. Shakespeare wrote about her as a great tragic heroine. Countless artists painted her, and great actresses played her in films.

But perhaps the truest picture of her is still the one on her little coins. A clever, determined woman with a strong nose and her hair tied back, who tried for twenty-two years to keep her ancient country free.
