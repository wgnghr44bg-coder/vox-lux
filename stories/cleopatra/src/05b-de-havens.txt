Alexandria has two great harbours, one on each side of the long stone road that joins the city to the lighthouse island.

The Great Harbour, in the east, next to the palace quarter, is the busiest. It is full of ships from all over the Mediterranean. There are big round merchant ships with one tall mast and a big square sail. There are long, slim warships. There are little fishing boats, and river boats that come down the Nile through a canal.

--

Along the quays stand big warehouses for grain, papyrus, linen, glass, spices and wine. Cranes, worked by men walking inside big wheels, lift the cargo in and out of the ships. Porters carry sacks and jars on their backs. Merchants argue about prices. Officials check every ship and collect the taxes.

The most important cargo of all is grain. Every summer, after the harvest, great fleets of grain ships sail from Alexandria, carrying the wheat of Egypt across the sea. Much of it goes to Rome. And that is exactly why Rome cares so much about Egypt. Whoever controls Egypt controls the bread of Rome.

{img:huge-grain-ship-loading-sacks-cranes-sailors-alexandria-summer}
The other harbour, in the west, is quieter. Its name means the harbour of the happy return. A canal connects it to the great lake behind the city, which is full of boats bringing goods from the Nile.

--

The sailors of Alexandria are among the best in the world. They know the winds, the currents and the stars. Every summer, a steady north wind blows across the sea toward Egypt. It makes the trip to Alexandria fast and easy, and the trip away from it slow and hard.

A little before Cleopatra was born, Greek sailors from Egypt discovered a great secret. In the Indian Ocean, the winds blow one way in summer and the other way in winter. So ships could now sail across the open ocean from the Red Sea to India, and back again within a year. Soon, more than a hundred ships a year sail to India, bringing back pepper, spices, precious stones and cotton.

{img:red-sea-port-ships-departing-india-monsoon-desert-coast}
In the evening, as the sun goes down over the Great Harbour, the sailors come ashore. They go to the taverns along the water to eat and drink and tell stories of faraway lands. The ships rock gently at anchor. And the great lighthouse begins to shine.
