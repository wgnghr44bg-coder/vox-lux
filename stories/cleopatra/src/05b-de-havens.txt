Alexandria has two great harbours, on either side of the long causeway, the Heptastadion, that joins the city to the island of Pharos.

The Great Harbour, in the east, beside the palace quarter, is the busiest. It is full of ships, from all over the Mediterranean: great round merchant ships, with one tall mast, and a big square sail, and a curved stern, shaped like the neck of a swan. Long, slender warships. Little fishing boats. And the river boats, which come down the Nile, and through a canal, from the lake, into the harbour.

--

Along the quays, there are great warehouses, for grain, and papyrus, and linen, and glass, and spices, and wine. Cranes, worked by men in treadwheels, lift the cargoes in and out of the ships. Porters carry sacks and jars on their backs. Merchants check their goods, and argue about prices. Customs officials check every ship, and every cargo, and collect the taxes.

The most important cargo of all is grain. Every summer, after the harvest, great fleets of grain ships sail from Alexandria, carrying the wheat of Egypt, across the sea. In Cleopatra's time, much of it goes to the cities of Greece, and Syria, and Rome. Later, under the Romans, enormous grain ships, the largest ships in the world, will carry it to Rome every year.

{img:huge-grain-ship-loading-sacks-cranes-sailors-alexandria-summer}
The other harbour, in the west, is called the Eunostos, the harbour of the happy return. It is quieter, and connected, by a canal, to the great lake Mareotis, behind the city, which is itself a great harbour, full of boats, bringing the produce of Egypt, from the Nile, to the city.

--

The sailors of Alexandria are among the best in the world. They know the winds, and the currents, and the stars. Every summer, the north wind, the etesian wind, blows steadily across the Mediterranean, toward Egypt, which makes the voyage to Alexandria fast and easy, and the voyage away from it slow and hard.

And a few years before Cleopatra's birth, Greek sailors from Egypt discovered the secret of the monsoon winds, in the Indian Ocean, which blow from the south west in summer, and from the north east in winter. So that ships could now sail across the open ocean, from the Red Sea, to India, and back, within a year. And soon, every year, more than a hundred ships sail from the Egyptian ports of the Red Sea to India, bringing back pepper, and spices, and precious stones, and cotton, and silk.

{img:red-sea-port-ships-departing-india-monsoon-desert-coast}
In the evening, in the Great Harbour, as the sun goes down, the sailors come ashore, and go to the taverns, along the waterfront, to eat, and drink, and tell stories of the sea, and of the far-off lands they have seen, while the ships rock gently at anchor, and the great lighthouse begins to shine.
