Most people in Egypt in Cleopatra's time do not live in Alexandria. They live in villages along the Nile, as their ancestors did for thousands of years. They are farmers. They speak Egyptian, worship the old gods, and live much as people did in the time of the pyramids.

--

A village is a cluster of little mud-brick houses on a mound, above the level of the flood, among palm trees. The houses have thick walls, small windows and flat roofs. On the roofs the family stores fuel and grain, dries fish, and sleeps in summer.

Inside it is cool and dim. There are a few rooms with earth floors and mats, a few stools, and pottery jars for water, grain and beer. In a niche in the wall stands a little figure of a household god. Often it is Bes, a funny little god who protects mothers and children.

Outside, in the courtyard, there is an oven for baking bread. There is a place for a donkey, a few goats, some ducks and geese, and pigeons in tall mud towers.

--

Around the village lie the fields and the little canals that bring water to them. Before each flood, the villagers must work together to repair the earth banks. If the banks break, the water could wash away the village.

The farmers work hard all year. They plough with oxen and sow by hand. In spring they cut the wheat and barley with sickles. They spread it on a hard floor, where cattle walk over it to loosen the grain. Then they throw it into the wind, so the light husks blow away and the grain falls back. Officials come to measure the harvest and take the queen's share.

{img:harvest-wheat-sickles-scribe-measuring-grain-heap-field-sun}
Most land belongs to the crown, the temples or rich landowners. The farmers rent it and pay with part of their harvest. Life is hard and the taxes are heavy. But in a good year, there is enough to eat.

--

The village women work too. They help in the fields at harvest. At home they grind grain, bake bread, brew beer and weave linen. They carry water from the river in jars on their heads.

Egyptian women have more rights than women in most ancient lands. They can own and inherit property, buy and sell, go to court, and even divorce their husbands. Many marriage contracts on papyrus survive from this time. They carefully list what the wife brought into the marriage, and what she will get back if it ends.

{img:women-carrying-water-jars-heads-riverbank-evening-children}
The children help from a young age. They herd the goats and geese and scare birds away from the crops. They play in the river and the canals, with clay toys and balls of rags, and swim like fish.

--

In the evening, the farmers come home as the sun sets behind the western desert. The Egyptians believe the west is the land of the dead. The sky turns red and gold and purple.

The families eat supper in the courtyard or on the roof. Bread and onions, beans and fish, and beer. Donkeys bray, dogs bark and frogs croak in the canals. Then the village goes to sleep under the bright stars of Egypt. Far away in Alexandria, great decisions are being made. But here, life goes on as it always has.
