The most famous building in Alexandria, and one of the Seven Wonders of the World, is the great lighthouse, the Pharos.

It stands on a little island, also called Pharos, at the entrance to the great harbour, joined to the city by a long causeway, a mole, of stone, almost a kilometre and a half long, called the Heptastadion, the seven stades, because of its length.

--

The lighthouse was built about two hundred and fifty years before Cleopatra's time, by the first two Ptolemies, and designed by a Greek architect, Sostratus of Cnidus.

It is enormous: perhaps a hundred metres high, or more, the tallest building in the world, after the great pyramids. It is built of great blocks of white limestone, and granite, in three stages: a tall square tower at the bottom, with many windows, and rooms inside, for the keepers, and for storing fuel. Then an eight-sided tower above it. And on top, a round tower, with columns, where the fire burns.

At the very top, there is a great statue, perhaps of Zeus the Saviour, or of Poseidon, the god of the sea. And at each corner of the first stage, there are statues of Tritons, sea gods, blowing on conch shells.

--

At night, a great fire burns at the top of the lighthouse, fed with wood, and oil, which is carried up, inside the tower, by a spiral ramp, perhaps on donkeys. And during the day, it is said, a great polished mirror, of bronze, reflects the light of the sun, out to sea.

The light can be seen, the ancient writers say, from fifty kilometres away, out at sea. The coast of Egypt, near Alexandria, is very flat, and low, and dangerous, with rocks, and sandbanks, and few landmarks. So the lighthouse guides the ships safely into the harbour, by day and by night.

{img:ship-at-night-sea-sailors-seeing-distant-lighthouse-glow}
The sailors coming to Alexandria, from Rome, and Greece, and Syria, watch for the light, on the horizon, at night, and when they see it, they know they are nearly home, or nearly at the greatest city of the world.

--

The lighthouse was so famous that its name, Pharos, became the word for lighthouse, in many languages: in Greek, and Latin, and in French, phare, and in Italian, and Spanish, faro.

There is a story that the architect, Sostratus, wanted his own name to be remembered, but the king wanted his name on the lighthouse. So Sostratus carved his own name, and a dedication to the saviour gods, for the safety of sailors, into the stone, and then covered it with plaster, and painted the king's name on the plaster. And after many years, the plaster fell away, and the architect's name was revealed, forever.

{img:carved-greek-inscription-stone-plaster-falling-away-sunlight}
The lighthouse stood for more than a thousand years, through the time of the Romans, and the Byzantines, and the Arabs, until it was damaged by great earthquakes, in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, and fell. In the fifteenth century, the Sultan of Egypt, Qaitbay, built a fort, on the same spot, using some of its stones. And the fort still stands there today.
