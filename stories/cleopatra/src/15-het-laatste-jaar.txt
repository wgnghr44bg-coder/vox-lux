The last year of Cleopatra's life, from the autumn of 31 to the summer of 30 before Christ, is a strange, sad, and beautiful time.

She does not give up. She makes plans. She has her ships dragged across the narrow neck of land, from the Mediterranean, to the Red Sea, perhaps hoping to escape, with her children, and her treasure, to India, or Arabia. But the Arabs of the desert burn the ships.

--

She sends messages to Octavian, asking him to let her children rule Egypt. He does not answer, or answers coldly.

And meanwhile, in Alexandria, Antony and Cleopatra disband their old club, the Inimitable Livers, and form a new one, with their friends, which they call the Partners in Death, the friends who will die together. And they spend the winter feasting, and drinking, and giving parties, with music, and dancing, every night, as if there were no tomorrow.

{img:evening-feast-palace-hall-friends-lamps-music-quiet-sadness}
Caesarion, now sixteen, is enrolled as a young man, among the ephebes, the young men of the city, in a great ceremony, and so is Antyllus, Antony's son by his first marriage. The people of Alexandria feast and celebrate for many days. Cleopatra wants them to see that Egypt has a king, a grown young man, who can rule after her.

--

She also builds a tomb for herself: a great building, of stone, next to the temple of Isis, by the sea, near the palace. And into it, she has carried all her greatest treasures: gold, and silver, and pearls, and emeralds, and ebony, and ivory, and cinnamon, and great quantities of wood, and kindling. So that, if Octavian tries to take her treasure, she can burn it all.

In the summer of 30 before Christ, Octavian arrives at Alexandria, with his army.

On the last night before Octavian enters the city, Plutarch tells, in the middle of the night, when all was quiet in Alexandria, and everyone was full of fear, the people suddenly heard the sound of music, of instruments of every kind, and voices singing, and the noise of a joyful crowd, dancing, and shouting, like the followers of Dionysus, the god of wine, passing through the city, from the middle of the city, to the gate that faced the enemy, and then going out, and fading away. And the people believed that it was the god Dionysus, whom Antony loved, and imitated, leaving him.

--

The next day, the first of August, Antony's last soldiers, and his fleet, went over to Octavian. And Antony, believing that all was lost, and hearing a false report that Cleopatra had died, ended his own life. We will not describe it. He was brought, at the end, to Cleopatra, in her tomb, and passed away in her arms.

Some days later, Cleopatra herself chose to end her life, rather than be taken to Rome, and led in chains through the streets, in Octavian's triumph.

{img:empty-royal-chamber-golden-couch-diadem-figs-basket-soft-light}
The story says that a basket of figs was brought to her, by a peasant, and that inside the figs, there was a little snake, an asp, an Egyptian cobra, the royal snake of Egypt, the symbol of the pharaohs, which the Egyptians believed would make its victim immortal. She was found by Octavian's messengers, lying on a golden couch, dressed in her royal robes, with her two faithful maids, Iras and Charmion, beside her.

One of the Romans asked Charmion: was this well done? And Charmion answered: it was very well done, and fitting for a queen, descended from so many kings.

--

Cleopatra was thirty-nine. She had ruled Egypt for twenty-two years.

Octavian, in spite of his anger, gave orders that she should be buried beside Antony, as she had wished, with royal honours, in her tomb, by the sea, in Alexandria. The tomb has never been found.

{img:sea-at-dusk-alexandria-lighthouse-glow-quiet-calm-ending}
And with her death, three hundred years of the Ptolemies came to an end, and three thousand years of the pharaohs. Egypt became a province of Rome.
