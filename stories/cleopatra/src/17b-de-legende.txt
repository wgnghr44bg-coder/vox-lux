Cleopatra has been remembered, and imagined, and reimagined, for two thousand years.

The Roman poets wrote about her as a dangerous enemy: Horace, who wrote a poem celebrating her death, called her a fatal monster, but also admitted that she died nobly, without fear, like a queen. Plutarch wrote about her with fascination, and gave us most of the stories we know.

--

In 1606, William Shakespeare wrote his great play, Antony and Cleopatra, in which she is a passionate, proud, changeable, witty, unforgettable queen. Age cannot wither her, he wrote, nor custom stale her infinite variety.

Painters painted her, again and again: at her banquet, with the pearl. Meeting Antony, on her golden barge. Dying, with the asp. In the twentieth century, she was played in films by great actresses, most famously Elizabeth Taylor, in 1963, in one of the most expensive films ever made.

{img:painting-style-queen-on-golden-barge-river-old-master-colours}
And there are the two famous obelisks, called Cleopatra's Needles: tall, slender pillars of red granite, carved with hieroglyphs, which once stood in front of a temple in Alexandria, which Cleopatra began to build, for Julius Caesar, or for Antony. They are much older than Cleopatra: they were made for the pharaoh Thutmose the Third, fourteen hundred years before her, at Heliopolis, and brought to Alexandria by the Romans.

In the nineteenth century, one of them was given to Britain, and taken to London, by sea, in a special iron cylinder, which nearly sank in a storm, and set up on the bank of the river Thames, in 1878, where it still stands. The other was given to the United States, and set up in Central Park, in New York, in 1881.

--

{img:obelisk-central-park-new-york-trees-autumn-walkers}
So, if you walk along the Thames in London, in the evening, or through Central Park in New York, you can still see a piece of Cleopatra's Alexandria, standing tall, among the trees and the traffic, with its ancient hieroglyphs, worn by two thousand years of sun and rain, telling the names of pharaohs, that almost nobody passing by can read.
