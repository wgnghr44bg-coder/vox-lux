The Egyptians, in the time of Cleopatra, still believe, as their ancestors have believed for three thousand years, that life goes on after death.

They believe that every person has several souls: the ka, the life force, which needs food and drink, and the ba, the personality, shown as a bird with a human head, which can fly out of the tomb, in the daytime, and visit the world of the living, and come back at night. And that, if the body is preserved, and the right prayers are said, and the person has lived a good life, they will live forever, in the Field of Reeds, a beautiful land, like Egypt, but even better, where the crops grow tall, and there is no sorrow.

--

In the old story, the dead person is led by Anubis, the jackal god, into the hall of judgment, where their heart is weighed, on a great scale, against a feather, the feather of Maat, the goddess of truth, and justice. If the heart is light, and pure, the person passes into the afterlife. And the dead person must say, before the gods: I have not done wrong. I have not stolen. I have not lied. I have not caused anyone to weep. I have not let anyone go hungry.

So the Egyptians try to live good lives, kind and honest, so that their hearts will be light, at the end.

{img:weighing-of-heart-scale-feather-anubis-papyrus-painting-calm}
To preserve the body, the Egyptians make mummies, as they have done for thousands of years. In Cleopatra's time, it is still done, for those who can afford it: the body is carefully dried, with natron, a kind of salt, from the desert lakes, and anointed with oils, and resins, and spices, and wrapped in many layers of fine linen, with amulets, little charms, placed among the wrappings, for protection. Then it is placed in a painted coffin, and buried in a tomb, with food, and drink, and things the person will need in the next life.

--

Even the Greeks and Romans living in Egypt took up the custom. In the following centuries, many of them were mummified, in the Egyptian way, with a beautiful portrait of the dead person, painted on a wooden board, in wax, in the Greek style, placed over the face of the mummy, so lifelike that they seem to look at us, across two thousand years. Hundreds of these portraits, from the Fayum, an oasis south of Memphis, can be seen in museums today: men and women, young and old, children, with their dark eyes, and their jewellery, and their hairstyles, gazing out, calmly, as if they were alive.

{img:fayum-style-portrait-young-woman-dark-eyes-gold-earrings-wood}
And the families visited the tombs of their dead, on festival days, and brought them food, and flowers, and had picnics, by the tombs, and remembered them, and talked to them. Because, for the Egyptians, the dead were not gone. They were only living somewhere else.
