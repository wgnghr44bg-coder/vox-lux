In Cleopatra's time, the Egyptians still believe, as their ancestors did for three thousand years, that life goes on after death.

They believe every person has more than one soul. One soul is the life force, which needs food and drink. Another is shown as a bird with a human head. It can fly out of the tomb in the daytime, visit the living, and come back at night. If the body is kept safe, and the right prayers are said, and the person lived a good life, they will live forever in the Field of Reeds. That is a beautiful land like Egypt, but even better, where the crops grow tall and there is no sorrow.

--

In the old story, the jackal god leads the dead person into the hall of judgement. There the heart is weighed on a great scale against a feather, the feather of truth. If the heart is light and pure, the person may pass into the next life. And before the gods, the dead person must say: I have not done wrong. I have not stolen. I have not lied. I have not made anyone weep. I have not let anyone go hungry.

So the Egyptians try to live kind and honest lives, so that at the end their hearts will be light.

{img:weighing-of-heart-scale-feather-anubis-papyrus-painting-calm}
To keep the body safe, the Egyptians make mummies, as they have for thousands of years. The body is dried with a special salt from desert lakes. It is rubbed with oils and spices and wrapped in many layers of fine linen, with little lucky charms tucked between them. Then it is laid in a painted coffin and buried in a tomb, with food and drink and the things the person will need in the next life.

--

Even the Greeks and Romans living in Egypt took up this custom. In the centuries after Cleopatra, many of them were made into mummies too, in the Egyptian way. Over the face, they placed a portrait of the person, painted on a wooden board. These portraits are so lifelike that they seem to look at us across two thousand years. Hundreds of them can be seen in museums today. Men and women, young and old, even children, with dark eyes, jewellery and their own hairstyles, gazing out calmly as if they were alive.

{img:fayum-style-portrait-young-woman-dark-eyes-gold-earrings-wood}
Families visited the tombs of their loved ones on festival days. They brought food and flowers and had picnics by the tombs. They talked to their dead and remembered them. For the Egyptians, the dead were not gone. They were only living somewhere else.
