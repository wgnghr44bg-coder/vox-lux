Let us walk through Alexandria as it is in the time of Cleopatra.

The city is long and narrow. It stretches about five kilometres along the shore, between the sea in the north and a great lake in the south. It is laid out in a perfect grid of straight streets, designed by a Greek architect three hundred years before.

--

The main street runs right through the city from east to west, from the Gate of the Sun to the Gate of the Moon. It is enormously wide, about thirty metres, wide enough for many chariots side by side. On both sides there are rows of marble columns, so people can walk in the shade.

One Greek writer described his first sight of this street. He said the columns seemed to go on forever, and he could not decide where to look first, because there was so much beauty everywhere.

The city is divided into five quarters, named after the first five letters of the Greek alphabet. Most Greeks live in the centre. A large and important Jewish community lives in the east, with its own synagogues and its own council. Most Egyptians live in the west, in an old village that was there before Alexander came.

--

In the northeast, by the sea, lies the palace quarter. It takes up perhaps a quarter of the whole city.

It is not one palace but many. Palaces, halls, gardens and courtyards, built by one king after another, along the shore and on a little island in the harbour. On that island, Cleopatra has her own private palace by the sea.

In the palace quarter there are also the great library, the tomb of Alexander, a theatre and a royal harbour for the royal ships.

--

Everywhere in the city there are temples, to Greek gods and Egyptian gods, and to gods who are both at once.

The greatest is the temple of Serapis, on a hill in the Egyptian quarter. Serapis was a god invented by the first Ptolemy, to please both Greeks and Egyptians. He is a bearded god with a basket of grain on his head, and he protects Alexandria. His temple stands on a high platform, with a hundred steps leading up to it. Inside stands a huge statue of the god, covered in gold and precious stones.

{img:serapeum-temple-hill-steps-colonnades-statue-sunlit}
There are sports grounds where young Greek men exercise and study. There is a great track for chariot races, and there are theatres, parks and public baths.

--

And there are the markets, full of the goods of the world. Grain, papyrus and linen from Egypt. Spices and perfumes from Arabia. Ivory and gold from Africa. Pepper and precious stones from India. Wine and olive oil from Greece. Even silk from faraway China, which costs a fortune.

{img:alexandria-market-spices-papyrus-rolls-linen-ivory-merchants}
The people of Alexandria are proud and clever and quick with words. They love jokes and nicknames and gossip, and making fun of their rulers. They love their beautiful city. It has everything in the world, they say, except snow. What it does not have, though, is a strong army. And that worries the queen more than anything.
