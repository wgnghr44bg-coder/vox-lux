Let us walk through Alexandria, as it is in the time of Cleopatra.

The city is long, and narrow, stretching for about five kilometres along the shore of the sea, between the Mediterranean in the north, and the great lake Mareotis in the south. It is laid out in a perfect grid, with straight streets, crossing each other at right angles, designed by a Greek architect, Dinocrates, three hundred years before.

--

The main street, the Canopic Way, runs right through the city, from east to west, from the Gate of the Sun, to the Gate of the Moon. It is enormously wide, about thirty metres, wide enough for many chariots side by side, and lined on both sides with colonnades of marble, so that people can walk in the shade.

The Greek writer Achilles Tatius, describing his first sight of the city, says that the street of columns seemed to go on forever, and that he could not decide where to look first, because there was so much beauty everywhere.

The city is divided into five quarters, named after the first five letters of the Greek alphabet: Alpha, Beta, Gamma, Delta, Epsilon. The Greeks live mostly in the centre. The Jews, a large and important community, live in the Delta quarter, in the east, with their own synagogues, and their own council, and their own laws. The Egyptians live mostly in the west, in the old village of Rhakotis, which was there before Alexander came.

--

In the north east of the city, by the sea, there is the palace quarter, the Brucheion, which takes up perhaps a quarter of the whole city.

It is not one palace, but many: a great complex of palaces, and halls, and gardens, and courtyards, and colonnades, and temples, built by one Ptolemy after another, each adding something new, along the shore, and on a little peninsula, called Cape Lochias, and on a little island, in the harbour, called Antirhodos, where Cleopatra has her own private palace, by the sea.

In the palace quarter, there are also the Museum, and the Library, and the tomb of Alexander, and the tombs of the Ptolemies, and a theatre, and a royal harbour, for the royal ships.

--

Everywhere in the city, there are temples: to the Greek gods, and to the Egyptian gods, and to gods who are both Greek and Egyptian at once.

The greatest is the Serapeum, on a hill in the Egyptian quarter: the great temple of Serapis, a god invented by the first Ptolemy, to please both Greeks and Egyptians, a bearded god, like Zeus, with a basket of grain on his head, who is the protector of Alexandria. His temple stands on a great platform, with a hundred steps leading up to it, and colonnades, and a daughter library, and inside, a huge statue of the god, of wood, covered with gold and precious stones.

{img:serapeum-temple-hill-steps-colonnades-statue-sunlit}
There are gymnasia, where the young Greek men exercise, and study, and a great racecourse, the hippodrome, where there are chariot races, and a stadium, and theatres, and parks, and gardens, and public baths.

--

And there are the markets, full of the goods of the world: grain, and papyrus, and linen, from Egypt. Spices, and incense, and perfumes, from Arabia. Ivory, and ebony, and gold, from Nubia, and Ethiopia. Pepper, and cotton, and precious stones, from India. Wine, and olive oil, from Greece. Silk, even, from far away, from China, which the Alexandrians buy at enormous prices.

{img:alexandria-market-spices-papyrus-rolls-linen-ivory-merchants}
The Alexandrians are proud, and clever, and quick-tongued, and love jokes, and nicknames, and gossip, and making fun of their rulers. They love music, and dancing, and festivals, and the theatre, and the races. And they love their beautiful city, which, they say, has everything in the world, except snow.
