Everything in Egypt depends on the Nile.

A Greek historian once said that Egypt is the gift of the Nile. Without the river, Egypt would be desert. With it, Egypt is one of the richest farming lands in the world.

--

Every summer, the Nile floods.

Far to the south, in the mountains of Ethiopia, heavy summer rains fall. The rivers swell, and the water flows north into the Nile and down into Egypt. In July the river begins to rise. In August and September it spills over its banks and spreads across the fields. The whole land becomes a great lake, with the villages and temples and palm groves standing up out of the water like islands.

The flood brings water, and it brings rich black mud from the mountains. The mud settles on the fields and makes them fertile, year after year. The Egyptians call their country the black land, because of this black soil. The desert they call the red land.

--

The Egyptians measure the height of the flood carefully every year. They use stone staircases beside the river, with marks on the walls.

If the flood is too low, there will not be enough water for the fields, and people will go hungry. If it is too high, it will wash away villages. If it is just right, there will be a good harvest, and everyone will be happy.

Cleopatra watches the flood anxiously every year. In some of the years before 31 the floods were bad, and there was hunger. Then she opened the royal storehouses and gave out grain to the people. Every summer the question is the same, and nobody can answer it until the water rises. Will this be a good year?

--

In October and November the water goes down, and the fields appear again, covered in black mud. The farmers sow wheat and barley, flax for linen, beans and onions. The crops grow through the mild winter. In spring they are harvested.

Then, in summer, the land is dry, and everyone waits for the river to rise again. It is a cycle that has repeated every year for thousands of years.

{img:farmers-sowing-seed-black-mud-fields-oxen-november-light}
The Nile is also the great road of Egypt. Going north, boats drift with the current. Going south, they put up their sails, because in Egypt the wind almost always blows from the north. So it is easy to travel both ways. In the old picture writing, going north is a boat with its sail down, and going south is a boat with its sail up.

--

On the river there are boats of every kind. Little reed boats and fishing boats, ferries and big barges loaded with grain and stone. And the golden barges of the kings and the gods.

Along the banks grow palm trees, reeds and papyrus. There are ibises and herons and kingfishers. Crocodiles sun themselves on the sandbanks. In the evening, hippos wallow in the shallows. And farmers lift water from the river to their fields with a long pole, with a bucket on one end and a weight on the other.

{img:shaduf-farmer-raising-water-riverbank-ibis-reeds-morning}
It is a scene that has hardly changed for thousands of years. In some places, it still has not changed today.
