Everything in Egypt depends on the Nile.

The ancient Greek historian Herodotus said that Egypt is the gift of the Nile. Without the river, Egypt would be desert. With it, Egypt is one of the richest farming countries in the world.

--

Every year, in the summer, the Nile floods.

Far to the south, in the mountains of Ethiopia, the summer rains fall, heavy and constant, and the rivers swell, and the water flows north, into the Nile, and down into Egypt. In July, the river begins to rise. In August and September, it overflows its banks, and spreads out over the fields, all along the valley, and the Delta, so that the whole land becomes a great lake, with the villages, and the temples, and the palm groves, standing up out of the water, like islands.

The flood brings water, and it brings silt: fine, black, rich mud, carried down from the mountains, which settles on the fields, and makes them fertile, year after year, without any need for manure. The Egyptians call their country Kemet, the black land, because of the black soil of the fields. And the desert, Deshret, the red land.

--

The Egyptians measure the height of the flood carefully, every year, with nilometers: stone staircases, or wells, beside the river, with marks on the walls, to show how high the water has risen.

If the flood is too low, there will not be enough water for the fields, and there will be famine. If it is too high, it will wash away the villages, and the dykes. If it is just right, about sixteen cubits, at Memphis, there will be a good harvest, and everyone will be happy.

The kings, and later the Roman governors, use the height of the flood to decide how much tax the farmers must pay. Cleopatra, too, watches the flood anxiously, every year. In the years before 31, there were some bad floods, and some famine, and she opened the royal granaries, and gave out grain to the people.

--

In October and November, the water goes down, and the fields appear again, covered with black mud. And the farmers sow their crops: wheat, and barley, and flax, for linen, and beans, and lentils, and onions, and vegetables. The crops grow through the mild winter. And in the spring, in March and April, they are harvested.

Then, in the summer, the land is dry, and the farmers wait, for the river to rise again. It is a cycle, which has repeated every year, for thousands of years.

{img:farmers-sowing-seed-black-mud-fields-oxen-november-light}
The Nile is also the great road of Egypt. Boats travel up and down it, all year round: going north, downstream, with the current, and going south, upstream, with their sails up, because the wind in Egypt almost always blows from the north. So it is easy to travel in both directions. The hieroglyph for going north is a boat with its sail down. The hieroglyph for going south is a boat with its sail up.

--

On the river, there are boats of every kind: little reed boats, and fishing boats, and ferries, and great barges, carrying grain, and stone, and cattle, and the great golden barges of the kings and the gods.

And along the banks, there are the palm trees, and the reeds, and the papyrus, and the water birds, ibises, and herons, and egrets, and ducks, and kingfishers, and the crocodiles, sunning themselves on the sandbanks, and the hippopotamuses, wallowing in the shallows, in the evenings, and the farmers, raising water from the river, to their fields, with a shaduf, a long pole, with a bucket at one end, and a weight at the other.

{img:shaduf-farmer-raising-water-riverbank-ibis-reeds-morning}
It is a scene that has hardly changed for thousands of years, and in some places, still has not changed today.
