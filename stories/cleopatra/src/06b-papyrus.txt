In the marshes of the Delta grows a tall, graceful plant. Its green stem is as tall as two men, and on top it has a head of fine green threads, like an umbrella. It is papyrus.

For three thousand years, the Egyptians have used it for almost everything. For boats and baskets, mats and sandals and rope. They even chew the sweet inside of the stem. And above all, they use it to make paper.

--

To make a sheet of papyrus, workers cut the stems and peel off the green skin. They slice the white inside into long, thin strips. They lay the strips side by side, and then lay another layer across them. Then they beat the layers with a wooden hammer and press them under a weight. The plant's own sap glues them together. When they are dry, they rub them smooth with a stone.

The sheets are glued end to end to make a long roll, several metres long. Our word paper comes from papyrus.

Egypt is the only country that makes it. It sells papyrus all over the Mediterranean, and the kings grow rich from it.

--

The Egyptians write on papyrus with reed pens and black ink made of soot. They use red ink for headings.

There are three kinds of Egyptian writing. Hieroglyphs are the beautiful picture writing carved on temples. Then there is a quicker form for religious texts. And there is a very quick, joined-up writing for everyday things, like letters and accounts.

{img:scribe-sitting-cross-legged-reed-pen-papyrus-roll-palette}
In the dry sands of Egypt, papyrus does not rot. So in the old rubbish heaps of ancient towns, archaeologists have found hundreds of thousands of pieces of papyrus. They tell us about the lives of ordinary people. Their letters and shopping lists, their tax receipts and their complaints.

--

There is a letter from a boy to his father, complaining that his father did not take him to Alexandria. If you will not take me, he writes, I will not eat and I will not drink. So there.

There is a wedding invitation. There is a letter from a wife asking her husband to come home. There is a receipt for buying a donkey.

{img:papyrus-fragments-greek-letters-glass-museum-close-light}
And there may even be one word written by Cleopatra herself. On a royal order from two years before our story, at the bottom, in a different hand, someone has written one Greek word. It means: let it be done. Some scholars believe this is Cleopatra's own handwriting. If they are right, it is the only thing we have that she wrote with her own hand.
