TOPIC: New York harbour, November 1872
===
IMG: new-york-east-river-ships-masts-1872 | the busy East River waterfront of New York in 1872 with a forest of wooden ship masts, piers, warehouses, horse-drawn wagons and dockworkers, church spires behind, soft grey morning light
HEAD: In 1872, New York was one of the busiest ports in the world, with ships arriving from every ocean.
BODY: In the 1870s, New York was the largest city in the United States, with almost a million inhabitants. Its harbour was one of the busiest in the world. Historians note that thousands of ships arrived and departed every year, carrying goods and passengers to and from Europe, South America, the Caribbean and Asia. Along the East River and the Hudson River, piers stretched out into the water, crowded with sailing ships and steamships. The masts of the ships stood so close together that they looked like a forest. Even so, the age of sail was slowly coming to an end. Steamships were becoming faster and more reliable. Yet in 1872, many cargoes were still carried by sailing ships, which were cheaper to run. The waterfront was full of life, with sailors, merchants, dockworkers and shopkeepers. Taverns, boarding houses and shipping offices lined the streets near the water.
===
IMG: brooklyn-bridge-towers-construction-1872 | the tall stone towers of a great suspension bridge under construction over a river in New York in 1872, wooden scaffolding, sailing ships passing below, soft afternoon light
HEAD: While the Mary Celeste was being loaded, the towers of the Brooklyn Bridge were rising over the East River.
BODY: In 1872, a great engineering project was underway on the East River. Workers were building the Brooklyn Bridge, which would connect Manhattan to Brooklyn. Historians note that construction had begun in 1869, and the great stone towers were rising slowly above the river. When it opened in 1883, it would be the longest suspension bridge in the world. Even so, in 1872 it was still far from finished. The towers were surrounded by scaffolding, and workers dug deep foundations beneath the riverbed. The work was dangerous, and several workers died. The engineer John Roebling had died early in the project, and his son Washington Roebling took over. Washington became ill from working in the deep foundations, and his wife Emily helped him manage the project. For the sailors on the Mary Celeste, the rising towers would have been a familiar sight. They were a sign of a changing city.
===
IMG: sailors-boarding-house-waterfront-street | a cobbled waterfront street in New York in 1872 with sailors in pea coats and caps outside a brick boarding house and a tavern, a horse-drawn cart passing, gas lamps, soft evening light
HEAD: Sailors in New York lived in boarding houses near the docks while they waited for a ship.
BODY: When sailors arrived in New York, they usually stayed in boarding houses near the waterfront. Historians note that these houses provided beds, meals and sometimes help in finding a new ship. Some boarding house keepers were honest, while others cheated the sailors, taking their wages or forcing them onto ships. Sailors were often signed on through shipping agents, who matched crews with ships. Even so, the crew of the Mary Celeste seems to have been chosen carefully. Captain Briggs wrote that he had a good crew. The German sailors had come to New York on other ships and were looking for work. The voyage to Genoa would pay them a monthly wage, part of which they could receive in advance. Sailors' lives were hard and often lonely. They spent months at sea, far from their families, and their wages were low. Yet many loved the sea and could not imagine any other life.
===
IMG: ship-leaving-new-york-harbour-tug-1872 | a two-masted sailing ship leaving New York harbour behind a small steam tugboat in November 1872, the city waterfront and church spires behind, grey cold sky
HEAD: On the seventh of November 1872, the Mary Celeste left New York for Genoa.
BODY: The Mary Celeste left her pier in New York on the fifth of November 1872. She was towed out into the harbour and then anchored off Staten Island, because the weather was bad. Historians note that captains often waited for better conditions before heading out into the open ocean. On the seventh of November, the weather improved, and the Mary Celeste set sail. She passed out of New York harbour and into the Atlantic Ocean. On board were ten people: Captain Briggs, his wife Sarah, their daughter Sophia, the two mates, the cook and the four German sailors. Even so, no one watching her leave could have imagined what would happen. She was a well-equipped ship with an experienced captain and a good crew. The sails were raised, and the ship headed east towards Europe. It was the last time anyone on land would see the people on board.
===
IMG: dei-gratia-loading-petroleum-barrels | a two-masted sailing ship being loaded with barrels at a New York pier in November 1872, dockworkers with hand carts, a captain watching from the deck, warehouses behind, grey daylight
HEAD: A few piers away, another ship, the Dei Gratia, was preparing to sail to Europe as well.
BODY: While the Mary Celeste was being loaded, another brigantine was also in New York. She was the Dei Gratia, a Canadian ship from Nova Scotia. Her name is Latin and means by the grace of God. Historians note that she was loading a cargo of petroleum, bound for Gibraltar and then Genoa. Her captain was David Reed Morehouse, an experienced sailor from Nova Scotia. Even so, the two ships' fates would soon be linked. According to some stories, Captain Briggs and Captain Morehouse knew each other and had dinner together in New York before their voyages. Historians are not sure this is true. There is no firm evidence that they had met. The Dei Gratia left New York on the fifteenth of November 1872, eight days after the Mary Celeste. Both ships headed for the same part of the ocean, following similar routes across the Atlantic towards the Strait of Gibraltar.
===
IMG: staten-island-anchorage-ships-waiting-fog | several wooden sailing ships lying at anchor in calm grey water off a low green shore in light fog, small rowboats moving between them, November 1872, soft misty light
HEAD: Before heading out to sea, the Mary Celeste waited for two days at anchor near Staten Island.
BODY: Staten Island lies at the entrance to New York harbour. In the nineteenth century, ships often anchored near its shores while they waited for good weather, favourable winds or final supplies. Historians note that the Mary Celeste spent two days there in early November 1872. During this time, Captain Briggs may have written his last letters, which were sent ashore with the pilot or a passing boat. The crew checked the sails, ropes and cargo. Even so, waiting was part of a sailor's life. Ships depended on the wind and weather, and captains had to be patient. The waters around Staten Island were busy with ships arriving from Europe and other parts of the world. Ferries carried passengers between Staten Island and Manhattan. From the anchorage, the crew could see the city in the distance. On the seventh of November, the weather cleared, and the Mary Celeste raised her anchor and sailed.

TOPIC: Life aboard a sailing ship
===
IMG: ship-bell-watch-change-deck-night | sailors in oilskin coats changing the watch on the deck of a wooden sailing ship at night, one ringing the ship's bell, a lantern glowing, dark sea and starry sky
HEAD: On a sailing ship, the day was divided into watches, and the ship's bell rang every half hour.
BODY: Life on board a sailing ship followed a strict routine, organised around watches. The crew was divided into two groups, each led by one of the mates. Each group worked for four hours and then rested for four hours, day and night. Historians note that the ship's bell was rung every half hour to mark the passing of time. One bell meant the first half hour of a watch, two bells the first hour, and so on, up to eight bells at the end of the watch. Even so, the system had a clever twist. Two shorter watches in the early evening, called dog watches, made sure that the men did not work the same hours every day. The captain did not stand a watch, but he was always on call. On the Mary Celeste, the bell would have rung steadily through the days and nights of the voyage, marking the sailors' working hours across the ocean.
===
IMG: sailors-climbing-rigging-setting-sails | sailors climbing high in the rigging of a wooden sailing ship to set the square sails, ropes and canvas around them, wide sea below, bright windy daylight
HEAD: Sailors climbed high into the rigging to set and take in the square sails.
BODY: Handling the sails was the most important and dangerous work on a sailing ship. On a brigantine like the Mary Celeste, the square sails on the foremast were set and taken in by sailors who climbed the rigging. Historians note that they stood on ropes called footropes, high above the deck, and leaned over the yards, the long wooden spars that held the sails. They had to work quickly, especially when the wind grew stronger. In storms, the ship rolled and pitched, and the men had to hold on tightly. Even so, experienced sailors moved through the rigging with great skill. They knew every rope and sail by name. Smaller sails could be handled from the deck. The fore-and-aft mainsail was raised and lowered with ropes and pulleys. The work never stopped. The sails had to be adjusted every time the wind changed direction or strength, by day and by night.
===
IMG: sailors-meal-forecastle-hammocks-lamp | sailors eating a simple meal of biscuits and stew from tin plates in the cramped forecastle of a sailing ship, bunks and sea chests around them, a swinging oil lamp, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a low wooden ceiling, dim warm light
HEAD: The ordinary sailors lived in a cramped space at the front of the ship called the forecastle.
BODY: On a sailing ship, the ordinary sailors lived at the front of the ship, in a space called the forecastle, usually pronounced folk-sul. Historians note that it was small, crowded and often damp. The sailors slept in narrow bunks and kept their belongings in wooden sea chests. On the Mary Celeste, the forecastle was a small deckhouse with bunks for the crew. The men ate their meals there, sitting on their chests or bunks. Their food was brought from the galley by one of the crew. Even so, the forecastle was also a place of friendship. Sailors told stories, sang songs, mended their clothes and smoked their pipes there during their rest periods. Some carved small objects from wood or bone, or made models of ships. When the boarding party from the Dei Gratia searched the forecastle, they found the sailors' chests and clothes still there. Even their pipes had been left behind.
===
IMG: captain-sextant-noon-sun-deck | a sea captain in a dark coat holding a brass sextant to his eye to measure the height of the sun at noon on the deck of a wooden sailing ship, a mate with a notebook beside him, bright midday light
HEAD: Every day at noon, the captain measured the sun's height to find the ship's position.
BODY: In 1872, there was no radio or satellite navigation. Ships found their position using the sun, the stars and careful calculations. Every day at noon, the captain or one of the mates measured the height of the sun above the horizon with a sextant, a brass instrument with mirrors and a telescope. Historians note that this measurement gave the ship's latitude, its position north or south. To find the longitude, its position east or west, the navigator also needed to know the exact time in Greenwich, England. This was given by a chronometer, a very accurate clock kept in a wooden box. Even so, navigation also relied on dead reckoning. This meant calculating the ship's position from its speed, course and the time that had passed. On the Mary Celeste, the sextant and chronometer were missing when she was found. This suggests that Captain Briggs took them with him when he left the ship.
===
IMG: ship-cook-serving-coffee-morning-deck | a ship's cook handing mugs of steaming coffee to sailors on the deck of a sailing ship in the early morning, the galley door open behind him, calm sea and rising sun
HEAD: Sailors' food was simple, but a cup of hot coffee in the morning was a great comfort.
BODY: The food on a merchant ship in the 1870s was plain and filling. Historians note that sailors usually ate salted beef or pork, which was kept in barrels of brine, along with hard ship's biscuits, potatoes, beans, peas, rice and dried fruit. On Sundays, there might be a special dish, such as a pudding made with flour, fat and raisins, called duff. Fresh food, such as vegetables and eggs, lasted only a short time after leaving port. Even so, a good cook could make the food tasty. Coffee and tea were important, especially on cold nights and early mornings. The cook brought hot drinks to the men on watch. On the Mary Celeste, the food stores were found well stocked, with enough supplies for six months. The water casks were full. The supplies of food showed that the crew had not left the ship because they were hungry or thirsty.
===
IMG: sailors-mending-sails-deck-afternoon | sailors sitting cross-legged on the deck of a sailing ship mending a large piece of canvas sail with needles and palms, coils of rope around them, calm sea, warm afternoon light
HEAD: In calm weather, the crew mended sails, spliced ropes and painted the ship.
BODY: When the weather was calm, there was still plenty of work on a sailing ship. Historians note that captains kept their crews busy with maintenance. Sails were mended with heavy needles and a leather palm, a kind of thimble worn on the hand. Ropes were spliced, which meant joining two ropes together by weaving their strands. The deck was scrubbed, the brass was polished and the woodwork was painted. Even so, the work was not just about keeping busy. A well-maintained ship was safer and faster. Damaged sails or worn ropes could break in a storm, putting the ship and crew in danger. Sailors took pride in their skills and in the condition of their ship. On quiet afternoons, the steady work, the sound of the waves and the creak of the rigging made a peaceful scene. Many sailors later remembered these calm days at sea as the happiest part of their lives.
===
IMG: night-sky-stars-ship-wheel-helmsman | a lone helmsman in a wool coat steering a wooden sailing ship at the wheel under a vast starry night sky, a small lantern lighting the compass, calm dark sea
HEAD: At night, one sailor stood at the wheel, steering by the compass and the stars.
BODY: A ship at sea had to be steered at all times. During each watch, one sailor stood at the wheel at the back of the ship and kept her on course. Historians note that he steered by the compass, which was kept in a wooden stand called a binnacle, lit by a small lamp at night. He also watched the sails and the stars. Experienced helmsmen could feel the movement of the ship and the pressure of the wind, and they made small adjustments to keep her steady. Even so, steering at night could be lonely and tiring. The helmsman had to stay alert, watching for other ships, changes in the weather and the set of the sails. On clear nights, the sky was filled with stars, far brighter than on land. The only sounds were the waves, the wind in the rigging and the creaking of the ship's timbers as she sailed on through the darkness.

TOPIC: Across the winter Atlantic
===
IMG: stormy-atlantic-ship-heeling-waves | a wooden two-masted sailing ship heeling over in a stormy grey Atlantic, large waves breaking over her bow, reduced sails, dark clouds and spray
HEAD: In November 1872, the Mary Celeste sailed through stormy winter weather in the Atlantic.
BODY: The voyage of the Mary Celeste across the Atlantic took her through some rough weather. Historians know from the ship's log, which was found on board, that she sailed through strong winds and heavy seas during the first weeks of the voyage. Winter storms were common in the North Atlantic, with high waves and cold rain. The crew had to reduce sail and work hard to keep the ship on course. Even so, the log does not describe any serious problems. The ship made good progress, sailing at an average speed of several knots. A knot is a measure of speed at sea, equal to about one point eight kilometres an hour. The entries are short and routine, recording the ship's position, the wind and the weather. By late November, the Mary Celeste was approaching the Azores, having crossed most of the ocean. The voyage seemed to be going well.
===
IMG: azores-islands-green-cliffs-sea-1872 | green volcanic islands with steep cliffs and small white villages rising from a deep blue Atlantic Ocean in 1872, a sailing ship passing in the distance, soft cloudy light
HEAD: The Azores are a group of volcanic islands in the middle of the Atlantic Ocean, belonging to Portugal.
BODY: The Azores are nine volcanic islands in the middle of the Atlantic Ocean, about fifteen hundred kilometres west of Portugal. Historians note that they were discovered by Portuguese sailors in the fifteenth century and have belonged to Portugal ever since. The islands are green and mountainous, with steep cliffs, crater lakes and small towns with white houses. In the nineteenth century, they were an important stopping point for ships crossing the Atlantic. Ships called there for water, food and repairs, and whaling ships recruited sailors from the islands. Even so, the waters around the Azores could be dangerous. Sudden storms, strong currents and rocky coasts made sailing difficult. The island of Santa Maria is the most southeasterly of the group. It was there, on the morning of the twenty-fifth of November 1872, that the Mary Celeste's crew made their last recorded observation. They could see the island from the deck.
===
IMG: island-santa-maria-cliffs-morning-mist | the steep green cliffs of a volcanic island in the Atlantic seen from the deck of a sailing ship in the early morning, mist over the water and a few seabirds, soft grey light
HEAD: The last entry on the log slate noted that the island of Santa Maria was in sight.
BODY: The last entry on the log slate of the Mary Celeste was written at eight o'clock in the morning on the twenty-fifth of November 1872. It recorded that the island of Santa Maria lay about six miles to the southwest. Historians have studied this entry carefully. It shows that the ship was very close to land. If something went wrong soon after the entry, the crew might have decided to leave the ship and row to the island, believing they could reach safety there. Even so, no lifeboat ever arrived at Santa Maria. The people of the island had no record of any visitors from the ship. The wind and the waves may have carried the small boat away from land, or it may have been lost in rough seas. The entry on the slate was written calmly and gave no sign of danger. It is the last trace of the people of the Mary Celeste.
===
IMG: calm-sea-ship-drifting-sails-loose | a two-masted sailing ship drifting slowly on a calm grey sea with some sails loose and flapping, no one on deck, a long rope trailing from her stern, soft overcast light
HEAD: After her crew left, the Mary Celeste sailed on by herself for about nine days.
BODY: After the last log entry, the Mary Celeste continued to move across the ocean. When she was found on the fourth of December 1872, she was about four hundred miles east of the Azores. Historians note that she had travelled this distance without anyone on board. Some of her sails were still set, and the wind had pushed her steadily eastward. Even so, she had not sailed in a straight line. With no one at the wheel, she changed direction as the wind changed. At times she may have turned in circles or stopped altogether. Yet she stayed afloat and kept moving roughly in the direction she had been sailing. Sailors who studied the case were impressed. They said that a well-built ship could sail on her own for a long time if her sails were set in a certain way. The Mary Celeste had proved to be a strong ship, carrying on alone across the empty sea.
===
IMG: small-open-boat-rowing-rough-sea | a small wooden open boat with several people in it being rowed across a rough grey sea, waves rising around it, a distant sailing ship behind, dark clouds
HEAD: Everyone on board seems to have left the ship in her small lifeboat, called a yawl.
BODY: The Mary Celeste carried one lifeboat, a small open boat called a yawl. It was usually kept on the deck, on top of the main hatch. When the Dei Gratia's crew searched the ship, the yawl was missing. Historians note that there was no sign that it had been torn away by a storm. The rail where it would have been launched had been cut or removed. This suggests that the crew launched it deliberately. Even so, the yawl was very small for ten people, including a woman and a two-year-old child. It would have been crowded and uncomfortable, and dangerous in rough seas. Historians believe that Captain Briggs must have had a strong reason to leave his ship for such a small boat. Experienced captains knew that a small boat was much less safe than a sound ship. Whatever happened, the people of the Mary Celeste climbed down into the yawl and rowed away.

IMG: long-rope-trailing-stern-water | a thick rope hanging from the stern of a wooden sailing ship and trailing into the grey sea behind it, the frayed end in the water, soft overcast light
HEAD: A long rope was found hanging from the ship, its end frayed, as if it had once been tied to something.
BODY: When the boarding party examined the Mary Celeste, they found a thick rope, called the main peak halyard, hanging over the side of the ship and trailing in the water. Its end was frayed and broken. Historians note that the halyard was normally used to raise the mainsail. Some have suggested that the crew used it to tie the lifeboat to the ship, so that they could stay close by while they waited to see whether the ship was in danger. If the rope broke in rough weather, the small boat would have been left behind as the ship sailed on. Even so, this idea cannot be proven. The court in Gibraltar did not give the rope much attention at the time. Later investigators believed it might be an important clue. It suggests that the crew may have planned to return to the ship, and that they were separated from her by accident.

TOPIC: The Dei Gratia
===
IMG: dei-gratia-crew-deck-captain-telescope | a sea captain in a dark coat looking through a brass telescope from the deck of a wooden sailing ship, his crew in wool jackets gathered behind him, grey Atlantic sea, December 1872, soft cloudy light
HEAD: Captain David Morehouse of the Dei Gratia was an experienced sailor from Nova Scotia.
BODY: David Reed Morehouse came from Nova Scotia. Like many men from his region, he went to sea young and became a captain. Historians note that he was known as a good seaman and an honest man. He commanded the Dei Gratia, which was owned by a group of businessmen in Nova Scotia. The Dei Gratia was a brigantine, much like the Mary Celeste, and had a crew of eight men. Even so, Morehouse's reputation was later questioned. At the court in Gibraltar, some officials suggested that he might have plotted with Captain Briggs to fake the abandonment of the ship and share the salvage money. Historians have found no evidence for this. Morehouse continued to sail for many years after 1872 and later lived in Nova Scotia. He always said that he had found the Mary Celeste by chance, and that he had acted honestly in bringing her to Gibraltar.
===
IMG: first-mate-deveau-strong-sailor-deck | a strong, broad-shouldered first mate with a dark beard in a heavy wool jacket and cap standing at the rail of a sailing ship, ropes in his hands, grey sea behind, 1872, soft daylight
HEAD: The first mate of the Dei Gratia, Oliver Deveau, led the boarding party and later sailed the Mary Celeste to port.
BODY: Oliver Deveau was the first mate of the Dei Gratia. He was a strong and experienced sailor from Nova Scotia. Historians note that he was the main witness at the court in Gibraltar, and his detailed descriptions of the Mary Celeste are the most important source for what was found on board. He was the first to climb aboard and search the ship. Even so, his testimony was questioned by some officials, who suspected that the Dei Gratia's crew might have done something wrong. Deveau answered their questions calmly and in detail. He described the condition of the ship, the cabins, the hold and the missing items. He also explained how he and two other men had sailed the Mary Celeste to Gibraltar. The journey was difficult, with only three men to handle a ship that normally needed eight. Deveau's skill and determination were praised by many sailors.
===
IMG: two-ships-meet-ocean-small-boat-between | two wooden sailing ships close together on a grey Atlantic swell, a small rowing boat moving between them with three sailors, December 1872, soft overcast light
HEAD: When the Dei Gratia found the Mary Celeste, the two ships were about four hundred miles east of the Azores.
BODY: On the fourth of December 1872, the Dei Gratia was sailing towards Gibraltar, about four hundred miles east of the Azores. According to the crew, the man at the wheel first noticed a ship in the distance at around one o'clock in the afternoon. Captain Morehouse watched her through his telescope for some time. Historians know from the court records that he saw that she was sailing strangely, with some of her sails set and others torn, and no one at the wheel. He was worried that her crew might be in trouble. Even so, he approached carefully. After about two hours, the Dei Gratia came close enough to signal. There was no reply. Morehouse then sent Deveau and two others across in a small boat. Their discovery would make both ships famous. The meeting of the two ships, far out on the ocean, was the beginning of one of the great mysteries of the sea.
===
IMG: sailors-pumping-water-hold-ship | sailors working a ship's hand pump on the deck of a wooden sailing ship, water gushing out over the planks, open hatches and ropes around them, grey daylight
HEAD: The boarding party found some water in the hold, but not enough to sink the ship.
BODY: When Deveau searched the Mary Celeste, he found water in the hold. He measured it with a sounding rod, a long stick lowered into the pump well. Historians know from his testimony that there was about three and a half feet of water. This sounds like a lot, but for a ship of that size, it was not dangerous. Deveau said that the ship was in no danger of sinking. One of the two pumps had been taken apart, perhaps because someone had been trying to clear it. Even so, the water may be an important clue. If the crew had found water in the hold and could not measure it properly, they might have believed the ship was sinking faster than she really was. A broken pump or a faulty sounding could have caused alarm. Some historians believe that this mistaken fear may have led Captain Briggs to order everyone into the lifeboat.
===
IMG: open-hatches-deck-ship-wet-planks | the open wooden cargo hatches on the wet deck of a sailing ship, ropes and coils beside them, barrels visible below, grey sea and sky, soft overcast light
HEAD: Two of the hatches were open, and some of the ship's covers had been removed.
BODY: Deveau noted that some of the hatches of the Mary Celeste were open. Hatches were openings in the deck that led to the hold, where the cargo was stored. Historians note that the fore hatch and the lazarette hatch, a small storeroom at the back of the ship, were found with their covers off. This was unusual, because hatches were normally kept closed at sea to keep out water. Even so, the open hatches may have a simple explanation. The crew may have opened them to let air into the hold, perhaps because of the smell of alcohol fumes from leaking barrels. Or they may have been checking the cargo or looking for leaks. Some investigators believe that the open hatches support the idea that the crew feared an explosion. If they had smelled strong fumes, they may have opened the hatches to let them escape and then moved away from the ship in the lifeboat, to wait and see.
===
IMG: sea-chests-forecastle-belongings-left | wooden sea chests with clothes, boots and pipes neatly left in the empty forecastle of a sailing ship, bunks along the walls, soft light from a small hatch, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a low wooden ceiling
HEAD: The sailors had left behind their sea chests, clothes, boots and even their pipes.
BODY: When Deveau searched the forecastle, he found the sailors' belongings still there. Historians know from his testimony that their sea chests, clothes and boots were in place. Their pipes were left behind too. Sailors were very attached to their pipes and would normally take them anywhere. Even so, the forecastle showed no sign of a fight or a panic. Everything was in order, although some clothes were wet. Deveau noted that the men must have left in a hurry, since they did not take their belongings. Yet they had time to launch the lifeboat and take the navigation instruments. This combination of calm and haste is part of the puzzle. It suggests that the crew left quickly but without violence, probably on the captain's orders. They may have believed that they would soon return to the ship, and that they did not need to take their things with them. That return never came.

TOPIC: What the boarding party found
===
IMG: cabin-skylight-open-water-inside | a small ship's cabin with an open skylight above, water on the floor, a wet table and books and papers scattered by the damp, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a low wooden ceiling, grey light falling through the skylight
HEAD: The cabin was wet, because the skylight had been left open and seawater had come in.
BODY: Deveau found that the skylight of the captain's cabin had been left open. A skylight was a window in the roof of the cabin that let in light and air. Historians note that seawater had come in through it, wetting the cabin and its contents. The captain's bed was damp, and some clothes and books were wet. Even so, the cabin was otherwise in order. The furniture was in place, and the family's belongings had not been taken. The clock had stopped, because it had not been wound. Deveau also found the ship's logbook on the mate's desk. A sword in a scabbard was found under the captain's bed. Later, some officials thought that stains on it might be blood. Tests showed that they were not. Most historians believe the sword was simply a souvenir. The wet cabin, with its open skylight, gives a feeling of sudden departure. The family left without closing it behind them.
===
IMG: navigation-instruments-missing-empty-box | an empty wooden chronometer box lying open on a ship's cabin desk beside sea charts, dividers and a pencil, an oil lamp above, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a low wooden ceiling, soft light
HEAD: The ship's chronometer, sextant and navigation book were missing, which suggested an orderly departure.
BODY: Among the missing items on the Mary Celeste were the ship's chronometer and sextant, the two main instruments needed to navigate. The ship's register, a document showing her ownership, was also missing. Historians note that the navigation book, which contained tables used to calculate position, had been taken too. Even so, the ship's logbook had been left behind. These missing items tell an important story. A captain leaving his ship in an emergency would take the instruments he needed to find land and the papers that proved the ship's identity. Captain Briggs seems to have done exactly that. This suggests that he left the ship in a planned way, not in sudden panic. He had time to collect what he needed. Yet he left behind the logbook, food and water for the voyage. This points to a hurried departure, perhaps expecting to stay close to the ship and come back aboard later.
===
IMG: food-stores-barrels-pantry-ship | a ship's pantry and storeroom with barrels of flour, sacks of potatoes, tins and jars on wooden shelves, water casks in a corner, lit by a small lantern, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a low wooden ceiling, dim warm light
HEAD: There was enough food and water on board to last for six months.
BODY: When the Dei Gratia's crew searched the Mary Celeste, they found her stores full. Historians know from the court records that there were barrels of flour, salted meat, potatoes, butter and other foods, as well as plenty of fresh water. The supplies would have lasted for about six months. Even so, there was one curious detail. The galley stove had been moved out of its place, and pots and pans were scattered. Some investigators thought that rough seas had caused this. Others believed it was a sign of a sudden event. The plentiful food and water show clearly that the crew did not leave the ship because of hunger or thirst. There was also no sign of disease. If illness had struck, the people would probably have died on board, and their bodies would have been found. The good condition of the stores makes the decision to abandon the ship even more puzzling.

IMG: deveau-measuring-water-sounding-rod | a sailor lowering a long wooden sounding rod into the pump well on the deck of a sailing ship to measure water in the hold, two other sailors watching, grey daylight
HEAD: Deveau carefully measured the water in the hold and found that the ship was not in danger.
BODY: One of Deveau's first tasks was to find out whether the Mary Celeste was sinking. He used a sounding rod, a long marked stick, which he lowered into the pump well to measure the depth of water in the hold. Historians note that he found about three and a half feet of water. He then pumped it out, which took a few hours. After that, the ship stayed dry. Even so, the sounding rod may be a clue in itself. A sounding rod was found lying on the deck, not in its usual place. Some historians suggest that the crew had been taking soundings shortly before leaving. If the pump was not working and the sounding gave a false reading, the captain may have believed that the ship was filling with water quickly. In fact, she was not. This theory is one of the most widely accepted today, though it can never be proven.

IMG: bow-damage-cuts-wooden-hull | close view of the wooden bow of an old sailing ship showing long narrow cuts and splinters in the planks just above the waterline, grey sea below, soft overcast light
HEAD: There were some long cuts in the wood at the bow, which later caused much argument.
BODY: When the Mary Celeste was examined in Gibraltar, investigators found long, narrow cuts in the wood on both sides of the bow, near the waterline. Historians note that officials at the court suggested that someone had deliberately damaged the ship, perhaps to make it seem as if she had struck rocks. They thought this might be part of a plan to claim insurance money. Even so, experts who examined the cuts disagreed. Some said that they had been made by the ship's own movement, with planks splitting as she worked through the waves. Others thought that they were caused by the sea and were quite normal for a ship that had been at sea for some time. No firm conclusion was reached. The cuts became one of the many details that people used to support their favourite theories. Today, most historians believe that they were simply natural damage and not a sign of foul play.

IMG: torn-sails-rigging-damaged-ship | the torn and loose sails of a two-masted sailing ship flapping in the wind, some ropes hanging down, the empty deck below, grey sea and sky
HEAD: Some of the ship's sails were torn or loose, while others were still set correctly.
BODY: Deveau described the state of the Mary Celeste's sails in detail. Historians know that some sails were set, some had been furled, or rolled up, and some were torn or blown away. The foresail and the upper foretopsail had been blown away. The lower foretopsail was hanging by its corners. Even so, the jib, a triangular sail at the front, and the fore-topmast staysail were set correctly. The mainsail had been lowered and furled. Experts said that this arrangement suggested that the crew had shortened sail, perhaps because of bad weather or because they were planning to stop. With fewer sails set, the ship would move more slowly and be easier to control. Some of the damage may have happened after the crew left, as the ship drifted through stormy weather. The state of the sails gave clues about the ship's last hours with her crew, but no clear answer.

HEAD: Nothing that the boarding party found explained why the people of the Mary Celeste had left.
BODY: After searching the Mary Celeste from end to end, Deveau and his companions could find no explanation for her abandonment. Historians note that the ship was seaworthy, with food, water and cargo, and with no sign of violence, fire or disease. Some things were missing, others were left behind. Some details suggested hurry, others suggested order. Even so, the boarding party had to make a practical decision. They could not leave a valuable ship drifting on the ocean, where she might sink or be wrecked. They decided to sail her to Gibraltar, the nearest major port. The mystery of what had happened would have to be solved by others. At the time, the sailors of the Dei Gratia probably thought that the answer would soon be found. They expected that the missing people would turn up somewhere, picked up by another ship. They never did. The empty ship kept her secret.
