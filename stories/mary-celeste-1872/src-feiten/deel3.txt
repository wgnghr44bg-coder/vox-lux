TOPIC: Sailing her to Gibraltar
===
IMG: three-men-sailing-ship-storm-night | three sailors in oilskins struggling with ropes and sails on the deck of a two-masted sailing ship in a stormy sea at night, waves breaking over the rail, a lantern swinging
HEAD: Three men sailed the Mary Celeste through stormy weather towards Gibraltar.
BODY: Deveau, together with two sailors named Augustus Anderson and Charles Lund, sailed the Mary Celeste towards Gibraltar. Historians note that this was a difficult and dangerous task. The ship normally needed a crew of eight, but only three men were available. They had to handle the sails, steer the ship and pump out water, taking turns to rest. Even so, they worked hard and kept the ship on course. They first repaired some of the damaged sails and rigging. The Dei Gratia sailed nearby, keeping an eye on them. For several days, the weather was stormy, and the two ships were separated. The three men on the Mary Celeste had little sleep. They had to rely on their skill and strength. Their journey lasted about a week. When they finally reached Gibraltar, they had shown great courage and seamanship. Even sailors who doubted their story admired what they had done.
===
IMG: rock-of-gibraltar-harbour-ships-1872 | the great limestone Rock of Gibraltar rising above a busy harbour full of sailing ships and steamships in 1872, white buildings along the shore, calm blue water, soft morning light
HEAD: Gibraltar was a British fortress and port at the entrance to the Mediterranean Sea.
BODY: Gibraltar is a small peninsula on the southern tip of Spain, dominated by a great limestone rock more than four hundred metres high. Since 1713, it has belonged to Britain. Historians note that in the nineteenth century, it was an important naval base and port, guarding the entrance to the Mediterranean Sea. Ships from all over the world called there for supplies, repairs and news. It had British soldiers, officials and courts, including a court that dealt with matters of the sea, called a vice admiralty court. Even so, Gibraltar was also a lively town, with a mix of British, Spanish, Italian, Jewish and Moroccan inhabitants. Its streets were full of shops, markets and taverns. The Dei Gratia arrived on the twelfth of December 1872, and the Mary Celeste arrived early the next morning. The arrival of an abandoned ship caused great interest, and news of the strange discovery spread quickly through the town.
===
IMG: admiralty-officials-boarding-ship-harbour | British officials in dark frock coats and top hats boarding a two-masted sailing ship moored in a harbour, a marshal in uniform beside them, the Rock of Gibraltar behind, 1872, soft daylight
HEAD: Soon after she arrived, the Mary Celeste was taken into the custody of the court in Gibraltar.
BODY: As soon as the Mary Celeste arrived in Gibraltar, officials of the vice admiralty court took charge of her. Historians note that this was the normal procedure for a ship brought into port by salvors. The court would decide who owned the ship and cargo and how much the salvors should be paid. Even so, the case of the Mary Celeste was not normal. The officials wanted to know what had happened to her crew. The court's marshal placed the ship under arrest, which meant that she could not leave until the case was decided. The crew of the Dei Gratia were questioned. The ship was examined by a surveyor, an expert who inspected ships for damage. News of the case was sent by telegraph to New York, where the ship's owners and the families of the crew were waiting for news. The hearing that followed would last for several months.
===
IMG: telegraph-office-clerk-message-1872 | a telegraph clerk in a waistcoat tapping out a message on a brass telegraph key in a small office with wooden counters and wires, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft window light
HEAD: News of the abandoned ship travelled by telegraph across the ocean to New York.
BODY: By 1872, a telegraph cable lay across the floor of the Atlantic Ocean, connecting Europe and North America. Messages could be sent in minutes instead of weeks. Historians note that the first lasting transatlantic cable had been completed in 1866. When the Mary Celeste arrived in Gibraltar, the news was sent by telegraph to New York and to Lloyd's of London, the famous insurance market. Even so, the first messages contained only basic information. They said that the Mary Celeste had been found abandoned and brought into Gibraltar. They did not explain what had happened. In New York, James Winchester, the ship's owner, was shocked. He travelled to Gibraltar to see the ship and give evidence at the court. In Marion, the families of Captain Briggs and his wife received the news. Newspapers in America and Britain began to report the story, and readers were fascinated by the mystery of the empty ship.
===
IMG: harbour-gibraltar-evening-ships-lights | a calm harbour at Gibraltar in the evening with sailing ships at anchor, lights glowing in the windows of white buildings, the great rock dark against a pink and gold sky
HEAD: While the court met, the Mary Celeste lay quietly at anchor in the harbour of Gibraltar.
BODY: For several months, while the court in Gibraltar heard evidence, the Mary Celeste lay at anchor in the harbour. Historians note that she was guarded by court officials and examined several times by experts. Curious visitors came to look at her from the shore and from small boats. Sailors in the port's taverns talked about her and exchanged theories. Even so, the ship herself gave no new clues. She lay silent, rocking gently on the water, while lawyers and officials argued about her fate. The crew of the Dei Gratia waited too. Captain Morehouse needed to continue his voyage to Genoa with his cargo of petroleum. He was allowed to leave in early 1873, while Deveau stayed to give further evidence. The Dei Gratia later returned for the court's decision. The long wait was frustrating for everyone involved. Yet the harbour of Gibraltar was a beautiful place to wait.
===
IMG: genoa-harbour-unloading-barrels-1873 | the harbour of Genoa in 1873 with tall colourful houses climbing the hills, sailing ships at the quay and dockworkers unloading wooden barrels, warm Mediterranean light
HEAD: In 1873, the Mary Celeste finally sailed to Genoa, where her cargo was unloaded.
BODY: In March 1873, after the court in Gibraltar had finished examining her, the Mary Celeste was released. A new captain and crew were hired to sail her to Genoa, her original destination, with her cargo of alcohol. Historians note that she arrived in Genoa later that month. The barrels were unloaded and delivered to the buyers. Even so, an important discovery was made during the unloading. Nine of the 1,701 barrels were found to be empty. They were made of red oak, which is more porous than white oak and can allow liquid to seep through. This meant that alcohol had leaked out during the voyage. Historians and investigators later saw this as a possible clue. If alcohol had leaked into the hold, its fumes might have built up and alarmed the crew. After the cargo had been delivered, the Mary Celeste returned to New York, where her owners tried to find new work for her.

TOPIC: The court in Gibraltar
===
IMG: courtroom-gibraltar-judge-lawyers-1873 | a nineteenth century British courtroom with wooden panelling, a judge in a wig at a high bench, lawyers in black gowns and sailors giving evidence, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft window light
HEAD: The vice admiralty court in Gibraltar held a long hearing about the Mary Celeste.
BODY: The hearing about the Mary Celeste began in December 1872, in the vice admiralty court in Gibraltar. The judge was Sir James Cochrane, the chief justice of Gibraltar, who was in his seventies. Historians note that the main purpose of the hearing was to decide the salvage award, the reward to be paid to the crew of the Dei Gratia. Even so, it also became an inquiry into what had happened to the ship and her people. Witnesses were called, including Deveau, Wright and other members of the Dei Gratia's crew. They described in detail how they had found the ship and what they had seen on board. The court also heard from experts who had examined the ship. The hearing lasted several months, with long breaks. The records of the court survive and are one of the most important sources for the story. They contain the sailors' own words, describing the empty ship.
===
IMG: attorney-general-portrait-office-1872 | a stern Victorian official with grey whiskers in a dark frock coat sitting at a desk covered with legal papers in a high-ceilinged office in Gibraltar, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft window light
HEAD: Frederick Solly-Flood, the attorney general of Gibraltar, believed that a crime had been committed.
BODY: Frederick Solly-Flood was the attorney general of Gibraltar, the chief legal officer of the government. He took a close interest in the Mary Celeste case. Historians note that he was suspicious from the start. He believed that the crew had been murdered, either by the sailors themselves, who might have drunk the alcohol and become violent, or by the crew of the Dei Gratia. He also suspected that the two captains might have plotted together to claim the salvage money. Even so, Solly-Flood had little evidence to support his theories. The alcohol was not fit to drink. There were no signs of a struggle, and the crew's belongings were untouched. Historians describe him as a proud and stubborn man, who was determined to find wrongdoing. His suspicions made the hearing longer and more difficult. He ordered several investigations of the ship, hoping to find proof of a crime, but nothing was ever found.
===
IMG: surveyor-examining-ship-hull-notebook | a ship surveyor in a dark coat and bowler hat examining the wooden hull and rail of a sailing ship, writing notes in a small book, a harbour behind, soft daylight
HEAD: Experts examined the ship from top to bottom, looking for signs of violence or damage.
BODY: At the request of Solly-Flood, the Mary Celeste was examined several times by experts. Historians note that the main surveyor was John Austin, the surveyor of shipping in Gibraltar. He inspected the hull, the deck, the cabins and the hold. He also took a diver to examine the hull below the waterline. Even so, the experts found no evidence of collision, fire or violence. The hull was sound, and there was no sign that the ship had struck rocks or been in a battle. They noted the cuts at the bow but could not agree on their cause. They also found some marks on the rail and on the sword in the cabin, which Solly-Flood thought might be bloodstains. A doctor tested them. The marks were found not to be blood. The surveyors concluded that the Mary Celeste had been abandoned while in good condition, for a reason that could not be found.
===
IMG: captain-morehouse-witness-stand | a weathered sea captain in his best dark coat standing in a witness box in a wood-panelled Victorian courtroom, answering questions with his hat in his hands, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft light
HEAD: The crew of the Dei Gratia were questioned closely, and some officials suspected them of wrongdoing.
BODY: Because of Solly-Flood's suspicions, the crew of the Dei Gratia were questioned closely in court. Historians note that they were asked about every detail of their voyage and their discovery. They were asked why they had sailed so close, whether they had known her captain and whether they had seen anything strange. Even so, their answers were consistent. They described what they had found and how they had sailed the ship to Gibraltar. There was no evidence that they had done anything wrong. Some officials suggested that the Dei Gratia's crew might have killed the people on the Mary Celeste to claim the salvage money. Historians have rejected this idea. The Dei Gratia's crew were honest sailors with good reputations. They had been sailing a separate course, and they could not have caught up with the Mary Celeste until long after the last log entry. The suspicion against them was unfair, and it affected the size of their reward.
===
IMG: salvage-award-papers-coins-table | legal papers with red wax seals, a quill and a small stack of gold sovereigns on a heavy wooden table in a Victorian office, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft lamplight
HEAD: In March 1873, the court awarded the crew of the Dei Gratia a reward of 1,700 pounds.
BODY: On the fourteenth of March 1873, the vice admiralty court in Gibraltar made its decision. It awarded the crew of the Dei Gratia a salvage reward of 1,700 pounds. Historians note that this was about one-fifth of the value of the ship and cargo. It was a considerable sum, but much less than many expected. In similar cases, salvors often received a third or even a half of the value. Even so, the court did not explain its decision in detail. Many historians believe that the low award reflected the suspicions raised by Solly-Flood. The court did not accuse the Dei Gratia's crew of any crime, but it seemed to doubt their story. The money was divided among the captain, the officers and the crew, according to the custom of the sea. Deveau and his two companions received extra shares for sailing the Mary Celeste to port. The crew felt that they had been treated unfairly.
===
IMG: unanswered-questions-judge-reading-file | an elderly judge in a wig and black robe reading a thick file of papers by a tall window in a quiet Victorian chamber, books lining the walls, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft evening light
HEAD: The court ended its work without discovering what had happened to the ten people on board.
BODY: When the hearing in Gibraltar ended, the court had decided who should be paid and how much. Historians note that it had not answered the biggest question, the fate of the people of the Mary Celeste. The court found no evidence of a crime, but it also found no clear explanation for the abandonment. Even so, it was not the court's job to solve the mystery. Its main task had been to deal with the salvage claim. Solly-Flood sent reports to the British government and to the American consul, setting out his suspicions. The American government took an interest in the case, and some investigations were made in America. No new information was found. The missing lifeboat never reappeared. No bodies were washed ashore. No survivors came forward. The case was officially closed, but people did not stop wondering. Over the years, the mystery only grew larger.
===
IMG: american-consul-letter-desk-1873 | an American consul in a dark frock coat writing a letter at a desk under a framed flag in a consulate office in Gibraltar in 1873, papers and an inkstand beside him, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft window light
HEAD: The American consul in Gibraltar sent reports home, and the families of the missing waited for news.
BODY: The American consul in Gibraltar was Horatio Sprague, who represented the interests of American citizens and ships in the port. Historians note that he followed the case closely and sent detailed reports to the government in Washington. He believed that the crew of the Dei Gratia had acted honestly, and he defended them against Solly-Flood's suspicions. Sprague also sent news to the families of the missing people. He tried to answer their questions and returned the belongings found on board. Even so, he had no real answers to give. The families in Marion, New York and Germany waited for months, hoping that their loved ones might have been rescued. Sprague later corresponded with writers and investigators who were interested in the case. His reports are an important source for historians. They show the care and kindness with which he treated a difficult and sad situation, far from home.

TOPIC: What might have happened
===
IMG: alcohol-fumes-hold-barrels-lantern-haze | rows of wooden barrels in the dark hold of a sailing ship with a faint haze in the air, a lantern held up by a sailor peering in from the hatch above, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a low wooden ceiling, dim warm light
HEAD: One popular theory suggests that alcohol fumes in the hold made the captain fear an explosion.
BODY: One of the most widely accepted theories about the Mary Celeste involves her cargo of alcohol. Historians note that nine barrels were found empty in Genoa, which means that alcohol had leaked into the hold. Alcohol gives off strong fumes, which can catch fire. According to this theory, the crew may have opened the hold and smelled strong fumes, or heard a small explosion or rumbling sound. Captain Briggs, who had never carried alcohol before, may have feared that the ship was about to explode. He ordered everyone into the lifeboat, intending to stay close by until the danger had passed. Even so, the theory has a problem. There were no signs of fire or explosion on the ship. In 2006, scientists at University College London carried out an experiment. They showed that alcohol vapour could burn with a cool flame that leaves no scorch marks, which made the theory more believable.
===
IMG: waterspout-ocean-dark-clouds | a tall dark waterspout twisting from heavy storm clouds down to a churning grey sea, a small sailing ship far in the distance, dramatic light
HEAD: Some people have suggested that a waterspout, a tornado over the sea, may have frightened the crew.
BODY: A waterspout is a spinning column of air and water that forms over the sea, like a tornado over land. Waterspouts can be very powerful, and they can damage ships, tear sails and suck up water. Historians note that some writers have suggested that the Mary Celeste may have been struck by a waterspout. It could have damaged the sails, forced water into the hold and caused the instruments to give false readings. The crew might have believed that the ship was sinking and taken to the lifeboat. Even so, there is no clear evidence for this theory. A waterspout would probably have caused more damage than was found on the ship. Waterspouts are also relatively rare in that part of the Atlantic in winter. Yet the theory is not impossible. It shows how many different natural events might have frightened an experienced crew into leaving a ship that later turned out to be sound.
===
IMG: earthquake-sea-floor-waves-ship-trembling | a sailing ship on a strangely rough patch of grey sea with short choppy waves and churning water, under a calm sky, soft eerie light
HEAD: Another theory suggests that an undersea earthquake shook the ship near the Azores.
BODY: The Azores lie on a boundary between great plates of the Earth's crust, and earthquakes are common there. Some earthquakes happen under the sea, and their shock can be felt by ships passing above. Historians note that sailors have reported feeling their ships shake violently during such seaquakes, as if they had struck rocks. Some writers have suggested that a seaquake near Santa Maria may have shaken the Mary Celeste, perhaps loosening barrels in the hold and releasing alcohol fumes. The crew may have thought the ship had been damaged and abandoned her. Even so, there are no records of a seaquake at that time and place. Seismographs, instruments that record earthquakes, were not yet in use. So the theory cannot be checked. It remains one of many possible explanations. What it shows is that the area where the Mary Celeste was last recorded was a place where unexpected natural events could happen.
===
IMG: pump-taken-apart-deck-tools | a ship's hand pump taken apart on the wet deck of a sailing ship, its parts and tools laid out beside it, ropes and a sounding rod nearby, grey daylight
HEAD: A recent study suggested that a broken pump may have made the crew believe the ship was sinking.
BODY: In 2007, a documentary investigation by the Smithsonian Channel examined the Mary Celeste mystery again. Historians and researchers looked at the evidence carefully. They noted that one of the ship's pumps had been taken apart. They also noted that the Mary Celeste had recently been carrying coal, and coal dust and debris could have blocked the pumps. According to this theory, the crew discovered water in the hold and tried to pump it out, but the pumps did not work properly. Captain Briggs may have believed that the ship was taking on water quickly. With the island of Santa Maria in sight, he might have decided to abandon ship and head for land. Even so, the theory has its limits. It cannot explain everything, and it cannot be proven. Yet many historians find it the most convincing explanation. It fits the evidence of the pump, the sounding rod and the last log entry near the island.
===
IMG: pirates-sailing-ship-horizon-myth | a dark sailing ship on the far horizon of a calm sea under a pale sky, seen from the rail of another ship, an air of mystery, soft hazy light
HEAD: Stories about pirates, mutiny and sea monsters have all been told, but there is no evidence for them.
BODY: Over the years, many dramatic explanations have been offered for the Mary Celeste mystery. Some suggested that pirates attacked the ship and carried off the crew. Others said that the crew mutinied, killed the captain and his family and then escaped. Some writers even claimed that a giant squid or sea monster had snatched the people from the deck. Historians note that none of these theories fits the evidence. Pirates or mutineers would have taken the valuable cargo and personal belongings, which were left on board. There was no sign of a struggle or violence. Even so, these stories were popular, because they were exciting. Newspapers and magazines printed them, and readers enjoyed the thrill of a terrible secret. Historians prefer explanations based on the evidence, such as fear of an explosion or a sinking. Yet the dramatic theories show how the mystery captured people's imaginations, far beyond the facts.

IMG: lifeboat-drifting-alone-empty-sea | a small empty wooden rowing boat drifting alone on a vast calm grey sea under a pale sky, no land in sight, soft light
HEAD: Whatever the reason, the small boat and its passengers were never seen again.
BODY: Most historians agree on what happened to the people of the Mary Celeste, even if they disagree about why. They believe that the ten people left the ship in the small yawl, perhaps near the island of Santa Maria. They may have tied the yawl to the ship with the long halyard rope, hoping to return if the danger passed. Then something went wrong. Perhaps the wind rose and the rope broke, and the ship sailed away from them. Even so, no one knows for certain. The yawl may have been swamped by waves, or carried out into the ocean. With a woman, a small child and eight men in a small boat, they would have had little chance in rough seas. The ship that they had left behind sailed on safely for another nine days. It is a sad and simple explanation. Many historians believe that this is most likely what happened.

IMG: harbour-sunset-calm-sea-memory | a calm harbour at sunset with a single sailing ship at anchor silhouetted against a golden sky, gentle ripples on the water, peaceful mood
HEAD: In the end, the mystery may never be solved, but the people of the Mary Celeste are still remembered.
BODY: More than one hundred and fifty years after the Mary Celeste was found, no one knows for certain what happened to the people on board. Historians have studied the court records, the letters and the ship's details. They have weighed all the theories carefully. Even so, the final answer remains out of reach. Without survivors or new evidence, it may never be found. Yet the people of the Mary Celeste are still remembered. Captain Briggs, Sarah and little Sophia, the mates Albert Richardson and Andrew Gilling, the cook Edward Head and the four German sailors, Volkert and Boz Lorenzen, Arian Martens and Gottlieb Goodschaad, were real people with families who loved them. Their loss was a real tragedy. Behind the famous mystery is a simple human story. Ten people set out across the ocean in November 1872, hoping for a safe voyage, and never came home.

TOPIC: The legend and the last voyage
===
IMG: victorian-writer-desk-lamp-manuscript-1884 | a young Victorian doctor and writer with a moustache writing a story by lamplight at a desk in a small study in 1884, books and papers around him, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm lamplight
HEAD: In 1884, a young doctor named Arthur Conan Doyle wrote a story that made the ship famous.
BODY: In 1884, a young doctor named Arthur Conan Doyle wrote a short story about the Mary Celeste. He was not yet famous. His detective Sherlock Holmes would first appear three years later. The story was called J. Habakuk Jephson's Statement, and it was published in the Cornhill Magazine, a respected literary journal. Historians note that the story was written as if it were a true account by a survivor. In it, the ship was taken over by a passenger who wanted revenge. Even so, Conan Doyle changed many facts. He called the ship the Marie Celeste instead of the Mary Celeste. He also added invented details, such as warm cups of tea on the table and a lifeboat still on board. Many readers believed that the story was true. It made the Mary Celeste famous around the world. Even today, the wrong name, Marie Celeste, is often used.
===
IMG: victorian-readers-magazine-parlour-fire | a Victorian family reading a magazine together by the fire in a cosy parlour with patterned wallpaper, a gas lamp and a clock on the mantelpiece, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm firelight
HEAD: Readers in Britain and America were fascinated by the story, and many believed it was true.
BODY: When Conan Doyle's story appeared in 1884, it caused a sensation. Readers in Britain and America discussed it eagerly. Historians note that some people wrote to newspapers, asking whether the story was true. Frederick Solly-Flood, the attorney general of Gibraltar, was angry and called it a fiction. The American consul, Horatio Sprague, also pointed out that it was not accurate. Even so, the story continued to be read and believed. It set a pattern for later tellings of the mystery. Over the following decades, many writers produced their own versions, each adding new details and theories. Some claimed to have found survivors or secret documents. Most of these stories were invented. Historians have had to work hard to separate the facts from the fiction. Yet the stories kept the Mary Celeste in people's memories. Without them, the ship might have been forgotten, like many other abandoned vessels.
===
IMG: mary-celeste-trading-voyages-caribbean | a two-masted sailing ship anchored in a warm Caribbean bay with palm trees and wooden houses on the shore, small boats carrying cargo, bright tropical light
HEAD: After 1872, the Mary Celeste continued to sail for another twelve years, with many different owners.
BODY: After her famous voyage, the Mary Celeste returned to ordinary work. Her owners sold her in 1873, and she changed hands many times in the following years. Historians note that she carried cargoes along the coasts of America, to the Caribbean and to Africa and the Indian Ocean. Even so, she had a bad reputation. Sailors were superstitious and believed that she was an unlucky ship. Some refused to sail on her. It became difficult to find crews, and her owners lost money. Several of her captains had problems. One fell ill and died, and others had accidents. Her owners struggled to make her profitable. Historians note that many old sailing ships had similar problems, as steamships took over more and more of the trade. Yet the Mary Celeste's reputation made things worse. She sailed on for twelve more years, through the warm waters of the Caribbean and beyond, but her best days were behind her.

IMG: ship-wrecked-coral-reef-haiti-1885 | a two-masted sailing ship stuck on a coral reef in clear turquoise water off a green tropical coast in 1885, her sails hanging, a small boat rowing away, bright light
HEAD: In January 1885, her last captain deliberately wrecked the Mary Celeste on a reef off the coast of Haiti.
BODY: In 1884, the Mary Celeste was bought by a group of businessmen in Boston. They loaded her with a cargo that they claimed was valuable, and they insured it for a large sum. Historians note that the cargo was actually worth very little. The ship's captain, Gilman Parker, sailed her to the Caribbean. On the third of January 1885, he ran her onto the Rochelais Reef, a coral reef off the coast of Haiti, on purpose. The ship was wrecked, and the crew went ashore. Even so, the plan failed. The insurance companies became suspicious and investigated. They found that the cargo was nearly worthless. Parker and the businessmen were charged with fraud. The case was complicated, and Parker was not convicted, but he died a few months later. Several of the businessmen were ruined. The Mary Celeste ended her life as part of a crime, wrecked on a reef in tropical waters.
===
IMG: old-wooden-timbers-coral-reef-clear-water | the broken remains of old wooden ship timbers lying among corals on a shallow reef in clear blue tropical water, small fish around them, sunlight shining through the surface
HEAD: In 2001, an expedition claimed to have found the wreck of the Mary Celeste off Haiti.
BODY: In 2001, the American author and explorer Clive Cussler led an expedition to search for the wreck of the Mary Celeste. Historians note that Cussler was famous for his adventure novels and for founding an organisation that searched for historic shipwrecks. His team dived on the Rochelais Reef, off the coast of Haiti, and found the remains of an old wooden ship. They announced that they had found the Mary Celeste. Even so, later tests raised doubts. In 2005, scientists studied the wood from the wreck using tree-ring dating. They found that the wood came from trees that were still growing in the 1890s, many years after the Mary Celeste was wrecked. This meant that the wreck could not be hers. The search for the real wreck continues. Some believe that her remains still lie somewhere on the reef, broken up and covered by coral, waiting to be identified.

IMG: spencers-island-monument-shore-bay | a small stone monument on a quiet grassy shore beside a calm bay in Nova Scotia, a lighthouse and wooded hills in the distance, soft golden evening light
HEAD: Today, a monument at Spencer's Island in Nova Scotia marks the place where the ship was built.
BODY: At Spencer's Island, in Nova Scotia, a monument now stands near the place where the Amazon, later the Mary Celeste, was built in 1861. Historians note that the small community is proud of its connection to the famous ship. Visitors come to see the monument, walk along the shore and look out across the Bay of Fundy, which has some of the highest tides in the world. Even so, the shipyard itself has long disappeared. Spencer's Island is a quiet place today, with a small lighthouse and a few houses. In the nineteenth century, it was busy with shipbuilding, and hundreds of ships were launched from the shores of the Bay of Fundy. The Mary Celeste is now the most famous of them all. The people of the area remember her not as a ghost ship, but as a fine vessel built by local hands, whose story became one of the great puzzles of the sea.

HEAD: The name Mary Celeste is still used today for places that have been mysteriously abandoned.
BODY: More than a century and a half after she was found drifting in the Atlantic, the Mary Celeste is still famous. Her name has become a byword in English for any place that has been suddenly and mysteriously abandoned. People speak of a deserted office or an empty town as being like the Mary Celeste. Historians note that her story has inspired books, films, plays and documentaries. It is still studied by historians, scientists and sailors. Even so, the true story is quieter than the legends. It is the story of a well-built ship, an experienced captain and a good crew, and of a decision made in a moment of fear or uncertainty. It is a reminder of how dangerous the sea could be in the age of sail, and how small human beings were in the middle of the vast ocean. The ship sailed on, while her people were lost.

IMG: old-ship-model-museum-case-soft-light | an old wooden model of a two-masted sailing ship inside a glass display case in a quiet maritime museum gallery, soft warm light, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling
HEAD: Models, letters and records of the Mary Celeste are kept in museums and archives on both sides of the Atlantic.
BODY: Today, the story of the Mary Celeste can be explored in museums and archives on both sides of the Atlantic. Historians note that the records of the court in Gibraltar are kept in the archives there. Letters from the Briggs family and the American consul are kept in collections in the United States. Maritime museums in Nova Scotia, Massachusetts and elsewhere display models of the ship and tell her story. Even so, the most important objects are lost. The lifeboat, the instruments and the people who carried them were never found. What remains are papers, models and memories. Visitors to these museums can read the sailors' testimony and see the faces of the people involved in old photographs. They can imagine the small brigantine sailing out of New York on a grey November day in 1872, carrying ten people, a family's music and a cargo of alcohol across the winter Atlantic, towards a mystery that has never been solved.
