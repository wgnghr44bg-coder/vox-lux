TOPIC: The Mary Celeste found adrift
===
IMG: empty-brigantine-drifting-atlantic-1872 | an empty two-masted wooden sailing ship drifting on a calm grey Atlantic Ocean in 1872, some sails loose and torn, no one on deck, soft overcast morning light
HEAD: On the fourth of December 1872, sailors on the ship Dei Gratia saw a strange vessel drifting in the Atlantic.
BODY: On the afternoon of the fourth of December 1872, the crew of the Canadian ship Dei Gratia noticed a sailing ship in the distance. It was moving oddly, changing direction with the wind, as if no one were steering. The ships were in the Atlantic Ocean, between the Azores islands and the coast of Portugal. Captain David Morehouse of the Dei Gratia looked through his telescope. He recognised the ship as the Mary Celeste, an American brigantine that had left New York a week before his own ship. Historians know about this moment from the sailors' testimony at a later court hearing. The Dei Gratia sailed closer and signalled, but there was no reply. No one could be seen on deck or at the wheel. Even so, the ship did not look wrecked. Her hull seemed sound, and some of her sails were still set. Morehouse decided to send a small boat to investigate.
===
IMG: three-sailors-rowing-small-boat-swell | three sailors in thick woollen jackets and caps rowing a small wooden boat across a long grey Atlantic swell towards a drifting two-masted sailing ship, 1872, soft cloudy light
HEAD: Three sailors from the Dei Gratia rowed across to the drifting ship to find out what was wrong.
BODY: Captain Morehouse sent his first mate, Oliver Deveau, with the second mate, John Wright, and a seaman named John Johnson, to row across to the Mary Celeste. The sea was still rough after recent storms, and the small boat rose and fell on the long waves. Historians know from Deveau's testimony that the men climbed aboard and searched the ship from end to end. They called out, but no one answered. The deck was wet, and some ropes were hanging loose. Even so, there was no sign of violence or of a fire. The cabin was empty, the crew's quarters were empty, and the lifeboat was gone. Deveau and Wright looked through every room and the hold. The Mary Celeste had been completely abandoned. Of the ten people who had sailed from New York, not one remained on board. The men rowed back to their own ship to report what they had found.
===
IMG: empty-ship-deck-coiled-ropes-wheel | the empty wooden deck of a nineteenth century sailing ship with coiled ropes, an unattended ship's wheel, an open hatch and wet planks, grey sea around, soft overcast light
HEAD: The ship was in good condition, with food and water for six months, yet her people were gone.
BODY: One of the strangest things about the Mary Celeste was that she seemed perfectly able to sail. Deveau reported that the hull was sound and the masts were in good order. There was some water in the hold, and one of the pumps had been taken apart, but the ship was not sinking. Historians note that she still had about six months' supply of food and fresh water on board. The cargo was mostly untouched. The crew's sea chests, clothing and personal belongings were still in their quarters. Even so, some things were missing. The ship's lifeboat was gone, along with the captain's navigation instruments, a sextant and a chronometer, and most of the ship's papers. These clues suggested that the people on board had left in the lifeboat, in an orderly way, taking the instruments they would need to find land. Why they left a sound ship remains a mystery.
===
IMG: ship-log-slate-cabin-table-1872 | a ship's log slate with chalk writing and an open leather logbook lying on a wooden cabin table beside an oil lamp in a small ship's cabin, 1872, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a low wooden ceiling, soft light
HEAD: The last entry on the ship's log slate was written on the twenty-fifth of November 1872.
BODY: On a sailing ship, the crew recorded the ship's position, course and weather on a slate, a small board of stone that could be written on with chalk. Later, these notes were copied into the ship's logbook. On the Mary Celeste, the boarding party found both. Historians know from the court records that the last entry on the slate was made at eight o'clock in the morning on the twenty-fifth of November 1872. It noted that the ship was about six miles from the island of Santa Maria, in the Azores. That was nine days before she was found. Even so, the ship had continued to drift for hundreds of miles after that last entry. The logbook contained nothing unusual. There was no mention of trouble, illness or danger. Whatever happened on board must have happened suddenly, after the last entry, and the crew never had time to write it down.
===
IMG: captains-cabin-belongings-sewing-machine | a small captain's cabin on a nineteenth century sailing ship with a bunk, a sewing machine, a small organ, books and a child's toys left neatly in place, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a low wooden ceiling, soft daylight
HEAD: In the captain's cabin, the boarding party found the belongings of a family.
BODY: The captain's cabin of the Mary Celeste was where Captain Benjamin Briggs lived with his wife and young daughter. Deveau found the cabin wet, because the skylight had been left open and water had come in. Historians know from his testimony that the family's belongings were still there. There were clothes, books and a sewing machine. There was also a small organ called a melodeon, which Mrs Briggs liked to play. A child's toys and clothes were found as well. Even so, nothing seemed to have been disturbed by force. The beds were unmade, as if people had got up in a hurry, but there was no sign of a struggle. Later stories claimed that half-eaten meals and warm cups of tea were found on the table. Historians note that this was not true. These details were invented later. The real scene was quieter and stranger, a home left behind in the middle of the ocean.
===
IMG: two-ships-sailing-together-atlantic-grey | two wooden sailing ships sailing side by side across the grey Atlantic Ocean in 1872, one following the other, white sails filled with wind, soft cloudy light
HEAD: Captain Morehouse decided to sail the abandoned ship to Gibraltar and claim a reward for saving her.
BODY: Under the laws of the sea, sailors who rescued an abandoned ship and brought it safely to port could claim a reward. This was called salvage. The reward was a share of the value of the ship and its cargo. Captain Morehouse knew that the Mary Celeste and her cargo were worth a large sum of money. Historians note that he decided to divide his crew. Oliver Deveau and two other sailors would sail the Mary Celeste, while Morehouse and the rest of the crew sailed the Dei Gratia. Both ships were short of hands, which made sailing difficult and tiring. Even so, the plan worked. The two ships headed for Gibraltar, about six hundred miles away, at the entrance to the Mediterranean Sea. They sailed through stormy weather, sometimes losing sight of each other. The Dei Gratia arrived on the twelfth of December 1872, and the Mary Celeste followed the next morning.
===
IMG: empty-ship-moonlight-calm-sea-night | an empty two-masted sailing ship drifting alone on a calm dark sea under a full moon, moonlight shining on the water and on the loose sails, quiet night
HEAD: The story of the Mary Celeste has become one of the most famous mysteries of the sea.
BODY: The Mary Celeste was not the first ship to be found abandoned at sea, and she would not be the last. Yet her story became more famous than almost any other. Historians note that several things made it special. The ship was in good condition. The weather had not been extreme. There was no sign of violence, and the people on board included a woman and a small child. Even so, no one ever discovered what had happened to the ten people who left the ship. No bodies were found, and the lifeboat was never seen again. Over the following years, the mystery attracted writers, newspapers and investigators. Many theories were put forward, some sensible and some fantastic. The story was retold so often that facts and legends became mixed together. Today, the name Mary Celeste is used for any place that has been suddenly and mysteriously abandoned. The real story is calmer, but no less puzzling.

TOPIC: A ship built in Nova Scotia
===
IMG: spencers-island-shipyard-nova-scotia-1861 | a small wooden shipyard on the shore of a quiet bay in Nova Scotia in 1861 with the half-built hull of a sailing ship on wooden supports, shipwrights with axes, forests and hills behind, soft morning light
HEAD: The Mary Celeste was built in 1861 in a small shipyard on Spencer's Island, in Nova Scotia.
BODY: The ship that became the Mary Celeste was built in 1861 at Spencer's Island, a small village on the Bay of Fundy, in Nova Scotia. At that time, Nova Scotia was a British colony, and it later became part of Canada. Historians note that shipbuilding was a major industry in Nova Scotia in the nineteenth century. The region had vast forests, skilled workers and long traditions of sailing. Hundreds of wooden ships were built there every year and sold around the world. The ship was built by a group of local men, led by Joshua Dewis. She was made of local wood, including birch, beech, maple and spruce. Even so, she was not a large ship. She was a brigantine, a two-masted sailing vessel, about thirty metres long. When she was launched in May 1861, she was given the name Amazon. A monument at Spencer's Island today marks the place where she was built.
===
IMG: shipwrights-hammering-hull-planks-oak | shipwrights in shirtsleeves and caps hammering wooden planks onto the curved ribs of a large wooden ship's hull in a seaside shipyard in Nova Scotia in 1861, wood shavings and timber piles around, warm daylight
HEAD: Building a wooden ship took many months of skilled work by carpenters and blacksmiths.
BODY: Building a wooden sailing ship was a complex task. First, the shipwrights laid the keel, the long backbone of the ship. Then they added the ribs, curved timbers that gave the hull its shape. Planks were fixed to the ribs with wooden pegs and iron nails, and the gaps were filled with oakum, loose fibres soaked in tar, to make the hull watertight. Historians note that every part of the work was done by hand, using axes, saws, adzes and hammers. Blacksmiths made the iron fittings, and sailmakers sewed the sails from heavy canvas. Riggers fitted the masts and ropes. Even so, a small community could build a ship in about a year. Many people in the village took part, and the launch was a great celebration. When a new ship slid down the slipway into the water for the first time, the whole village gathered to watch and cheer. Families often owned shares in the ships they built.
===
IMG: brigantine-sails-diagram-two-masts-sea | a two-masted brigantine sailing ship under full sail on a blue sea, square sails on the front mast and a large fore-and-aft sail on the rear mast, white clouds, bright daylight
HEAD: The Mary Celeste was a brigantine, a ship with two masts and a mix of square and triangular sails.
BODY: A brigantine is a sailing ship with two masts. On the front mast, called the foremast, it carries square sails, which hang across the ship. On the rear mast, called the mainmast, it carries a large fore-and-aft sail, which runs along the length of the ship. Historians note that brigantines were popular in the nineteenth century for trade. They were fast, easy to handle and needed a smaller crew than larger ships. A brigantine like the Mary Celeste could be sailed by about eight men. Even so, the work was hard. The square sails had to be raised, lowered and adjusted by climbing the rigging, sometimes high above the deck in strong winds. The fore-and-aft sail could be handled from the deck. Brigantines carried all sorts of goods across the oceans, from timber and fish to grain, sugar and alcohol. They were the workhorses of the sea in the age of sail.
===
IMG: captain-ill-cabin-first-voyage-1861 | a sick sea captain lying in a narrow bunk in a small wooden ship's cabin, a worried sailor beside him holding a cup, an oil lamp swinging, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a low wooden ceiling, dim warm light
HEAD: The ship's first captain fell ill and died on her very first voyage.
BODY: The Amazon's first captain was Robert McLellan, one of the men who had built her. In June 1861, she set out on her first voyage, carrying timber. Historians note that soon after leaving port, McLellan fell ill. He was taken back to his home, where he died. Some sailors were superstitious and believed that a ship whose captain died on her first voyage would be unlucky. Even so, the Amazon continued to sail. Her next captain was John Nutting Parker, who sailed her successfully for several years. She carried cargoes across the Atlantic and to the Caribbean. Over time, the story of her unlucky start became part of her legend. Later writers claimed that the ship had been cursed from the beginning. Historians do not believe in curses, but they note that the Amazon did have a series of accidents and changes of owner in her early years, which helped create her reputation as a troubled ship.
===
IMG: ship-aground-rocky-shore-storm-cape-breton | a wooden two-masted sailing ship driven aground on a rocky shore during a storm, waves breaking around her hull, dark clouds and pouring rain, Cape Breton coast, 1867
HEAD: In 1867, a storm drove the Amazon ashore on the coast of Cape Breton Island.
BODY: In October 1867, the Amazon was caught in a severe storm off Cape Breton Island, in Nova Scotia. The wind drove her ashore, and she was badly damaged. Historians note that her owners decided she was not worth repairing, and they sold her as a wreck. Even so, the ship was not finished. She was bought by a man named Alexander McBean, who repaired her. Soon afterwards, she was sold again, this time to American owners in New York. Under American ownership, she was registered as an American ship and given a new name: Mary Celeste. The reason for the name is not known for certain. Some believe it was chosen in honour of a family member of one of the owners. The ship was later rebuilt and made larger. Her hull was lengthened, and a new deck and cabin were added. By 1872, she was almost a new ship, ready for her most famous voyage.
===
IMG: new-york-shipping-office-owners-ledger | ship owners in dark frock coats and top hats examining a ledger and a model of a sailing ship in a wood-panelled shipping office in New York in 1872, maps on the wall, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm light
HEAD: The Mary Celeste's main owner was James Winchester, a ship owner in New York.
BODY: By 1872, the Mary Celeste was owned by a group of people, as was common at the time. The largest shareholder was James H. Winchester, a ship owner and merchant in New York. Historians note that shares in ships were bought and sold like shares in companies today. Each owner received a portion of the ship's profits. Captain Benjamin Briggs also bought a share in the Mary Celeste, which gave him a personal interest in her success. Even so, owning ships was risky. Storms, accidents and changes in trade could cause great losses. To protect themselves, owners insured their ships and cargoes. Winchester's company managed the ship's affairs, finding cargoes and hiring crews. In 1872, the Mary Celeste had just been refitted at a large cost. Winchester and his partners hoped that she would earn good money on her voyages. Her next voyage, to Genoa in Italy, was expected to be a simple and profitable one.

TOPIC: Captain Briggs and his family
===
IMG: sea-captain-portrait-new-england-1870 | a calm bearded sea captain in a dark wool coat standing on the deck of a wooden sailing ship in a New England harbour around 1870, other ships and wooden houses behind, soft morning light
HEAD: Benjamin Briggs was an experienced sea captain from a seafaring family in Massachusetts.
BODY: Benjamin Spooner Briggs was born in 1835 in Wareham, Massachusetts, in New England. His father was a sea captain, and four of his five brothers went to sea. Historians note that seafaring was a family tradition in many New England towns, where boys often began their careers as young sailors. Briggs became a captain while still young and commanded several ships. He was known as an honest, sober and religious man. He did not drink alcohol and was respected by his crews. Even so, his life was marked by losses. His brother Oliver, also a sea captain, was lost at sea only a few weeks after the Mary Celeste sailed. When Briggs took command of the Mary Celeste in 1872, he was thirty-seven years old. He had plenty of experience. Historians agree that he was a capable and careful captain, who would not have abandoned his ship without a serious reason.
===
IMG: young-wife-portrait-parlour-1870 | a young woman in a dark Victorian dress with a lace collar sitting at a small organ in a modest New England parlour around 1870, sheet music and a vase of flowers, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft window light
HEAD: Sarah Briggs, the captain's wife, loved music and often travelled with her husband at sea.
BODY: Sarah Elizabeth Cobb was the daughter of a minister in Marion, Massachusetts. She and Benjamin Briggs had known each other since childhood. They married in 1862. Historians note that it was quite common for captains' wives to sail with their husbands on long voyages. Sarah had already travelled with Benjamin on earlier voyages and was used to life at sea. She loved music and brought a melodeon, a small organ, on board the Mary Celeste. She also brought her sewing machine and books. Even so, life at sea for a woman was not always easy. The cabin was small, the ship moved constantly, and storms could be frightening. Yet many wives found it better than being separated from their husbands for months. Sarah's letters to her family survive. They describe her life with warmth and humour. Her last letter, written in New York before the voyage, is one of the most moving documents of the story.
===
IMG: little-girl-toys-ship-cabin | a little girl of two years old in a white Victorian dress playing with a wooden toy on the floor of a small ship's cabin, a bunk and a round window behind, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a low wooden ceiling, warm light
HEAD: The Briggs's two-year-old daughter, Sophia, sailed with her parents on the Mary Celeste.
BODY: Benjamin and Sarah Briggs had two children. Their son, Arthur, was born in 1865. Their daughter, Sophia Matilda, was born in 1870. When the Mary Celeste left New York in November 1872, Sophia was two years old, and she sailed with her parents. Historians note that a small child on board was not unusual on family voyages. The crew would have known her well. She played in the cabin and on the deck when the weather was calm. Even so, the presence of a young child makes the mystery especially touching. Many people have wondered what could have made her parents take her into a small open boat on the ocean. Mrs Briggs's letter mentioned that Sophia was excited about the trip. Arthur, who was seven years old, stayed at home with his grandmother in Marion, so that he could go to school. He never saw his parents or his sister again.
===
IMG: new-england-town-marion-harbour-1870 | a quiet New England seaside town around 1870 with white wooden houses, a church steeple, small boats in the harbour and elm trees along the street, soft autumn light
HEAD: The Briggs family's home was in the quiet seaside town of Marion, in Massachusetts.
BODY: Marion is a small town on Buzzards Bay, on the coast of Massachusetts. In the nineteenth century, it was home to many sailors and sea captains. Historians note that towns like Marion depended on the sea for their living. Men worked on whaling ships, merchant ships and fishing boats, and women ran the homes and farms while their husbands were away for months or years. Captain Briggs and his wife had a house in Marion, near the homes of their relatives. The town was quiet and peaceful, with white wooden houses, churches and gardens. Even so, it was a place used to loss. Many families had lost sons, husbands or fathers at sea. When news of the Mary Celeste reached Marion, it brought great sorrow. Arthur Briggs grew up there with his grandparents. A memorial to the family can still be seen in the cemetery at Marion, though their bodies were never found.
===
IMG: woman-writing-letter-cabin-lamp-1872 | a young woman in a Victorian dress writing a letter with a pen at a small table in a ship's cabin by the light of an oil lamp, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a low wooden ceiling, warm lamplight
HEAD: Before the voyage, Sarah Briggs wrote a cheerful letter to her mother-in-law.
BODY: On the third of November 1872, while the Mary Celeste was being loaded in New York, Sarah Briggs wrote a letter to her mother-in-law in Marion. Historians have studied this letter carefully, because it is one of the last messages from the ship. In it, Sarah described the cabin, which had been freshly painted, and the preparations for the voyage. She wrote that Benjamin thought they had a good crew. She mentioned that Sophia was excited and liked to play with her things. Even so, the letter also showed her love for her son. She asked for news of Arthur and sent him her love. The letter is calm and cheerful, with no hint of worry. Captain Briggs also wrote to his mother, saying that the ship looked fine and that he hoped for a good passage. These letters are kept today in collections in Massachusetts. They give a warm picture of the family before the voyage.
===
IMG: family-hymn-evening-cabin-melodeon | a family singing hymns around a small organ in a lamplit ship's cabin at evening, the captain, his wife playing and a little girl on a stool, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a low wooden ceiling, warm lamplight
HEAD: On quiet evenings at sea, the Briggs family may have gathered around the melodeon to sing hymns.
BODY: Life on a merchant ship followed a steady routine, but there were also quiet moments. On calm evenings, when the day's work was done, the captain's family could relax in their cabin. Historians imagine that the Briggs family, who were religious, sang hymns together while Sarah played the melodeon. On Sundays, Captain Briggs may have held a short service for the crew, as many religious captains did. Even so, no record of these evenings survives from the Mary Celeste's last voyage. The family's letters from earlier voyages describe such moments. They talk of reading, sewing, playing music and watching the sea and the sky. Sarah also kept a small garden of plants in pots, which she took on voyages. For the family, the ship was a home as well as a place of work. Its small cabin was filled with their books, music and belongings, which the boarding party later found untouched.
===
IMG: memorial-stone-cemetery-marion-autumn | a simple old granite memorial stone in a quiet New England cemetery with autumn leaves on the grass, a wooden church and tall trees behind it, soft golden afternoon light
HEAD: The Briggs family's names were later carved on a memorial stone in their hometown.
BODY: After the Mary Celeste was found, the Briggs family's relatives waited for news. They hoped that the people from the ship might have been picked up by another ship or have reached land. Historians note that months passed without any word. Slowly, the families understood that their loved ones were lost. In the cemetery of Marion, a memorial stone was later put up for Captain Briggs, Sarah and Sophia. It records their names and the year of their deaths at sea. Even so, the memorial does not mark a grave, since their bodies were never found. Arthur Briggs, the son who had stayed at home, grew up and lived a long life. He never knew what had happened to his family. In later years, he sometimes spoke to writers about the mystery. He did not believe the sensational stories that had been told. He believed his father would only have left the ship if he felt it was in danger.

TOPIC: The crew and the cargo
===
IMG: first-mate-portrait-deck-1872 | a serious young first mate with a short beard in a dark wool coat and cap standing by the rail of a sailing ship in New York harbour in 1872, ropes and rigging behind, soft daylight
HEAD: The first mate of the Mary Celeste, Albert Richardson, was a trusted and experienced sailor.
BODY: Albert G. Richardson was the first mate of the Mary Celeste. He was born in Maine and had served in the Union army during the American Civil War. After the war, he became a sailor and rose to the rank of first mate. Historians note that the first mate was the captain's second in command. He supervised the crew, managed the cargo and took charge of the ship when the captain was resting. Richardson was married to a niece of James Winchester, the ship's main owner. He was well regarded, and Captain Briggs trusted him. Even so, little is known about his personality. His wife later said that he was a good and gentle man. The second mate was Andrew Gilling, a young man from New York of Danish background. He also had a good reputation. Together, the two mates were responsible for the daily running of the ship and the watches.
===
IMG: german-sailors-crew-deck-rigging | four young sailors in rough wool sweaters and caps working together on the deck of a sailing ship, hauling on ropes and checking the rigging, New York harbour behind, 1872, soft daylight
HEAD: Four young sailors from Germany made up most of the ordinary crew.
BODY: The Mary Celeste had four ordinary seamen, all from Germany. Two of them were brothers, Volkert and Boz Lorenzen. The others were Arian Martens and Gottlieb Goodschaad. Historians note that they came from islands and coastal areas in northern Germany, where many young men went to sea. They were described as peaceful and reliable sailors, with good records. Even so, little is known about them as individuals. Their families in Germany later received the sad news of their loss. One of the Lorenzen brothers had lost his possessions in an earlier shipwreck, and he had joined the Mary Celeste with only a few belongings. Some later stories suggested that the German sailors had mutinied and killed the captain. Historians reject this idea, since there was no sign of violence on board and the sailors had left their belongings behind. The four men worked hard, handling the sails and keeping watch, day and night.
===
IMG: ship-cook-galley-stove-pots | a ship's cook in a white apron stirring a large pot on a small iron stove in a cramped wooden galley of a sailing ship, pots and pans hanging, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a low wooden ceiling, warm firelight
HEAD: The ship's cook and steward, Edward Head, had just married before the voyage.
BODY: Edward William Head was the steward and cook of the Mary Celeste. He was born in New York and had recently married. Historians note that the cook was an important person on a ship. He prepared meals for the whole crew in a small kitchen on deck, called the galley. He cooked on a small iron stove that burned coal or wood. The food was simple: salted beef and pork, ship's biscuits, potatoes, beans, rice, coffee and tea. When possible, fresh food was bought in port. Even so, the quality of the food mattered greatly to sailors. A good cook made long voyages easier to bear. The steward also looked after the captain's cabin and served the officers' meals. Head had been recommended to Captain Briggs, and the captain was pleased with him. His young wife in New York waited for him to return. She, too, never learned what had become of her husband.
===
IMG: wooden-barrels-alcohol-hold-lantern | rows of wooden barrels stacked tightly in the dark hold of a wooden sailing ship, lit by a single lantern, ropes and timber beams around them, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a low wooden ceiling, dim warm light
HEAD: The Mary Celeste carried a cargo of 1,701 barrels of alcohol, bound for Genoa in Italy.
BODY: For her voyage in November 1872, the Mary Celeste was loaded with 1,701 barrels of alcohol. It was not alcohol for drinking. It was industrial alcohol, made unfit to drink, which was used to strengthen wines in Italy. The cargo was shipped by a company called Meissner, Ackermann and Company, and it was bound for the port of Genoa. Historians note that the cargo was valuable, worth about thirty-five thousand dollars at the time. It was insured, as was the ship. The barrels were made of red oak and stacked tightly in the hold. Even so, alcohol was a dangerous cargo. It could leak from the barrels, and its fumes could build up in the closed hold. The fumes could catch fire or even explode if they came near a flame. Later, when the cargo was unloaded in Genoa, nine of the barrels were found to be empty. This detail would become important in the search for an explanation.
===
IMG: dockworkers-rolling-barrels-pier-ship | dockworkers in caps and rolled sleeves rolling wooden barrels up a gangplank onto a two-masted sailing ship at a busy wooden pier in New York in 1872, other ships and warehouses behind, soft daylight
HEAD: The barrels were loaded at Pier 50 on the East River in New York in October 1872.
BODY: In October 1872, the Mary Celeste was tied up at Pier 50 on the East River, in New York. Dockworkers rolled the barrels of alcohol along the pier and up into the ship. Historians note that loading a ship was hard and careful work. The barrels had to be stacked tightly and secured, so that they would not move during the voyage. A cargo that shifted in a storm could cause a ship to tip over. Even so, the loading went well. Captain Briggs supervised the work and checked that everything was in order. The crew was hired and signed on. The ship was stocked with food, water, coal for the galley stove and spare sails and ropes. By early November, the Mary Celeste was ready to sail. The weather in New York was cold and grey, with the first signs of winter. Ships were leaving the harbour every day, heading across the Atlantic to Europe.
===
IMG: captain-mate-discussing-chart-cabin | a sea captain and his first mate studying a large sea chart spread on a table in a ship's cabin, dividers and a compass beside them, an oil lamp above, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a low wooden ceiling, warm lamplight
HEAD: Captain Briggs planned to sail across the Atlantic, past the Azores, and into the Mediterranean.
BODY: The route from New York to Genoa was a common one for merchant ships. It crossed the Atlantic Ocean eastward, passing near the Azores, a group of Portuguese islands in the middle of the ocean. Then it continued towards the Strait of Gibraltar, the narrow passage between Spain and Morocco, and into the Mediterranean Sea. Historians note that the voyage normally took about a month. Captain Briggs and his mates studied the charts and planned their course. They would use a sextant to measure the height of the sun at noon and a chronometer, an accurate clock, to work out their position. Even so, the voyage would be in winter, when the Atlantic could be stormy. Strong winds, rain and high waves were common. Briggs was an experienced captain and was used to these conditions. He expected a normal crossing. Nothing in his letters suggests that he was worried about the voyage ahead.
