On a grey December afternoon in 1872, sailors on the brigantine Dei Gratia saw a ship drifting across the Atlantic with no one at the wheel. Her name was the Mary Celeste. Every one of the ten people who had sailed on her was gone.

On the fourth of December 1872, sailors on the ship Dei Gratia saw a strange vessel drifting in the Atlantic. On the afternoon of the fourth of December 1872, the crew of the Canadian ship Dei Gratia noticed a sailing ship in the distance. It was moving oddly, changing direction with the wind, as if no one were steering. The ships were in the Atlantic Ocean, between the Azores islands and the coast of Portugal. Captain David Morehouse of the Dei Gratia looked through his telescope. He recognised the ship as the Mary Celeste, an American brigantine that had left New York a week before his own ship. Historians know about this moment from the sailors' testimony at a later court hearing. The Dei Gratia sailed closer and signalled, but there was no reply. No one could be seen on deck or at the wheel. Even so, the ship did not look wrecked. Her hull seemed sound, and some of her sails were still set. Morehouse decided to send a small boat to investigate.

[long-pause]

{img:02-three-sailors-rowing-small-boat-swell}

Three sailors from the Dei Gratia rowed across to the drifting ship to find out what was wrong. Captain Morehouse sent his first mate, Oliver Deveau, with the second mate, John Wright, and a seaman named John Johnson, to row across to the Mary Celeste. The sea was still rough after recent storms, and the small boat rose and fell on the long waves. Historians know from Deveau's testimony that the men climbed aboard and searched the ship from end to end. They called out, but no one answered. The deck was wet, and some ropes were hanging loose. Even so, there was no sign of violence or of a fire. The cabin was empty, the crew's quarters were empty, and the lifeboat was gone. Deveau and Wright looked through every room and the hold. The Mary Celeste had been completely abandoned. Of the ten people who had sailed from New York, not one remained on board. The men rowed back to their own ship to report what they had found.

The ship was in good condition, with food and water for six months, yet her people were gone. One of the strangest things about the Mary Celeste was that she seemed perfectly able to sail. Deveau reported that the hull was sound and the masts were in good order. There was some water in the hold, and one of the pumps had been taken apart, but the ship was not sinking. Historians note that she still had about six months' supply of food and fresh water on board. The cargo was mostly untouched. The crew's sea chests, clothing and personal belongings were still in their quarters. Even so, some things were missing. The ship's lifeboat was gone, along with the captain's navigation instruments, a sextant and a chronometer, and most of the ship's papers. These clues suggested that the people on board had left in the lifeboat, in an orderly way, taking the instruments they would need to find land. Why they left a sound ship remains a mystery.

[long-pause]

{img:04-ship-log-slate-cabin-table-1872}

The last entry on the ship's log slate was written on the twenty-fifth of November 1872. On a sailing ship, the crew recorded the ship's position, course and weather on a slate, a small board of stone that could be written on with chalk. Later, these notes were copied into the ship's logbook. On the Mary Celeste, the boarding party found both. Historians know from the court records that the last entry on the slate was made at eight o'clock in the morning on the twenty-fifth of November 1872. It noted that the ship was about six miles from the island of Santa Maria, in the Azores. That was nine days before she was found. Even so, the ship had continued to drift for hundreds of miles after that last entry. The logbook contained nothing unusual. There was no mention of trouble, illness or danger. Whatever happened on board must have happened suddenly, after the last entry, and the crew never had time to write it down.

[long-pause]

{img:05-captains-cabin-belongings-sewing-machine}

In the captain's cabin, the boarding party found the belongings of a family. The captain's cabin of the Mary Celeste was where Captain Benjamin Briggs lived with his wife and young daughter. Deveau found the cabin wet, because the skylight had been left open and water had come in. Historians know from his testimony that the family's belongings were still there. There were clothes, books and a sewing machine. There was also a small organ called a melodeon, which Mrs Briggs liked to play. A child's toys and clothes were found as well. Even so, nothing seemed to have been disturbed by force. The beds were unmade, as if people had got up in a hurry, but there was no sign of a struggle. Later stories claimed that half-eaten meals and warm cups of tea were found on the table. Historians note that this was not true. These details were invented later. The real scene was quieter and stranger, a home left behind in the middle of the ocean.

[long-pause]

{img:06-two-ships-sailing-together-atlantic-grey}

Captain Morehouse decided to sail the abandoned ship to Gibraltar and claim a reward for saving her. Under the laws of the sea, sailors who rescued an abandoned ship and brought it safely to port could claim a reward. This was called salvage. The reward was a share of the value of the ship and its cargo. Captain Morehouse knew that the Mary Celeste and her cargo were worth a large sum of money. Historians note that he decided to divide his crew. Oliver Deveau and two other sailors would sail the Mary Celeste, while Morehouse and the rest of the crew sailed the Dei Gratia. Both ships were short of hands, which made sailing difficult and tiring. Even so, the plan worked. The two ships headed for Gibraltar, about six hundred miles away, at the entrance to the Mediterranean Sea. They sailed through stormy weather, sometimes losing sight of each other. The Dei Gratia arrived on the twelfth of December 1872, and the Mary Celeste followed the next morning.

[long-pause]

{img:07-empty-ship-moonlight-calm-sea-night}

The story of the Mary Celeste has become one of the most famous mysteries of the sea. The Mary Celeste was not the first ship to be found abandoned at sea, and she would not be the last. Yet her story became more famous than almost any other. Historians note that several things made it special. The ship was in good condition. The weather had not been extreme. There was no sign of violence, and the people on board included a woman and a small child. Even so, no one ever discovered what had happened to the ten people who left the ship. No bodies were found, and the lifeboat was never seen again. Over the following years, the mystery attracted writers, newspapers and investigators. Many theories were put forward, some sensible and some fantastic. The story was retold so often that facts and legends became mixed together. Today, the name Mary Celeste is used for any place that has been suddenly and mysteriously abandoned. The real story is calmer, but no less puzzling.

[long-pause]

{img:08-spencers-island-shipyard-nova-scotia-1861}

The Mary Celeste was built in 1861 in a small shipyard on Spencer's Island, in Nova Scotia. The ship that became the Mary Celeste was built in 1861 at Spencer's Island, a small village on the Bay of Fundy, in Nova Scotia. At that time, Nova Scotia was a British colony, and it later became part of Canada. Historians note that shipbuilding was a major industry in Nova Scotia in the nineteenth century. The region had vast forests, skilled workers and long traditions of sailing. Hundreds of wooden ships were built there every year and sold around the world. The ship was built by a group of local men, led by Joshua Dewis. She was made of local wood, including birch, beech, maple and spruce. Even so, she was not a large ship. She was a brigantine, a two-masted sailing vessel, about thirty metres long. When she was launched in May 1861, she was given the name Amazon. A monument at Spencer's Island today marks the place where she was built.

[long-pause]

{img:09-shipwrights-hammering-hull-planks-oak}

Building a wooden ship took many months of skilled work by carpenters and blacksmiths. Building a wooden sailing ship was a complex task. First, the shipwrights laid the keel, the long backbone of the ship. Then they added the ribs, curved timbers that gave the hull its shape. Planks were fixed to the ribs with wooden pegs and iron nails, and the gaps were filled with oakum, loose fibres soaked in tar, to make the hull watertight. Historians note that every part of the work was done by hand, using axes, saws, adzes and hammers. Blacksmiths made the iron fittings, and sailmakers sewed the sails from heavy canvas. Riggers fitted the masts and ropes. Even so, a small community could build a ship in about a year. Many people in the village took part, and the launch was a great celebration. When a new ship slid down the slipway into the water for the first time, the whole village gathered to watch and cheer. Families often owned shares in the ships they built.

[long-pause]

{img:10-brigantine-sails-diagram-two-masts-sea}

The Mary Celeste was a brigantine, a ship with two masts and a mix of square and triangular sails. A brigantine is a sailing ship with two masts. On the front mast, called the foremast, it carries square sails, which hang across the ship. On the rear mast, called the mainmast, it carries a large fore-and-aft sail, which runs along the length of the ship. Historians note that brigantines were popular in the nineteenth century for trade. They were fast, easy to handle and needed a smaller crew than larger ships. A brigantine like the Mary Celeste could be sailed by about eight men. Even so, the work was hard. The square sails had to be raised, lowered and adjusted by climbing the rigging, sometimes high above the deck in strong winds. The fore-and-aft sail could be handled from the deck. Brigantines carried all sorts of goods across the oceans, from timber and fish to grain, sugar and alcohol. They were the workhorses of the sea in the age of sail.

[long-pause]

{img:11-captain-ill-cabin-first-voyage-1861}

The ship's first captain fell ill and died on her very first voyage. The Amazon's first captain was Robert McLellan, one of the men who had built her. In June 1861, she set out on her first voyage, carrying timber. Historians note that soon after leaving port, McLellan fell ill. He was taken back to his home, where he died. Some sailors were superstitious and believed that a ship whose captain died on her first voyage would be unlucky. Even so, the Amazon continued to sail. Her next captain was John Nutting Parker, who sailed her successfully for several years. She carried cargoes across the Atlantic and to the Caribbean. Over time, the story of her unlucky start became part of her legend. Later writers claimed that the ship had been cursed from the beginning. Historians do not believe in curses, but they note that the Amazon did have a series of accidents and changes of owner in her early years, which helped create her reputation as a troubled ship.

[long-pause]

{img:12-ship-aground-rocky-shore-storm-cape-breton}

In 1867, a storm drove the Amazon ashore on the coast of Cape Breton Island. In October 1867, the Amazon was caught in a severe storm off Cape Breton Island, in Nova Scotia. The wind drove her ashore, and she was badly damaged. Historians note that her owners decided she was not worth repairing, and they sold her as a wreck. Even so, the ship was not finished. She was bought by a man named Alexander McBean, who repaired her. Soon afterwards, she was sold again, this time to American owners in New York. Under American ownership, she was registered as an American ship and given a new name: Mary Celeste. The reason for the name is not known for certain. Some believe it was chosen in honour of a family member of one of the owners. The ship was later rebuilt and made larger. Her hull was lengthened, and a new deck and cabin were added. By 1872, she was almost a new ship, ready for her most famous voyage.

[long-pause]

{img:13-new-york-shipping-office-owners-ledger}

The Mary Celeste's main owner was James Winchester, a ship owner in New York. By 1872, the Mary Celeste was owned by a group of people, as was common at the time. The largest shareholder was James H. Winchester, a ship owner and merchant in New York. Historians note that shares in ships were bought and sold like shares in companies today. Each owner received a portion of the ship's profits. Captain Benjamin Briggs also bought a share in the Mary Celeste, which gave him a personal interest in her success. Even so, owning ships was risky. Storms, accidents and changes in trade could cause great losses. To protect themselves, owners insured their ships and cargoes. Winchester's company managed the ship's affairs, finding cargoes and hiring crews. In 1872, the Mary Celeste had just been refitted at a large cost. Winchester and his partners hoped that she would earn good money on her voyages. Her next voyage, to Genoa in Italy, was expected to be a simple and profitable one.

{img:14-sea-captain-portrait-new-england-1870}

Benjamin Briggs was an experienced sea captain from a seafaring family in Massachusetts. Benjamin Spooner Briggs was born in 1835 in Wareham, Massachusetts, in New England. His father was a sea captain, and four of his five brothers went to sea. Historians note that seafaring was a family tradition in many New England towns, where boys often began their careers as young sailors. Briggs became a captain while still young and commanded several ships. He was known as an honest, sober and religious man. He did not drink alcohol and was respected by his crews. Even so, his life was marked by losses. His brother Oliver, also a sea captain, was lost at sea only a few weeks after the Mary Celeste sailed. When Briggs took command of the Mary Celeste in 1872, he was thirty-seven years old. He had plenty of experience. Historians agree that he was a capable and careful captain, who would not have abandoned his ship without a serious reason.

{img:15-young-wife-portrait-parlour-1870}

Sarah Briggs, the captain's wife, loved music and often travelled with her husband at sea. Sarah Elizabeth Cobb was the daughter of a minister in Marion, Massachusetts. She and Benjamin Briggs had known each other since childhood. They married in 1862. Historians note that it was quite common for captains' wives to sail with their husbands on long voyages. Sarah had already travelled with Benjamin on earlier voyages and was used to life at sea. She loved music and brought a melodeon, a small organ, on board the Mary Celeste. She also brought her sewing machine and books. Even so, life at sea for a woman was not always easy. The cabin was small, the ship moved constantly, and storms could be frightening. Yet many wives found it better than being separated from their husbands for months. Sarah's letters to her family survive. They describe her life with warmth and humour. Her last letter, written in New York before the voyage, is one of the most moving documents of the story.

{img:16-little-girl-toys-ship-cabin}

The Briggs's two-year-old daughter, Sophia, sailed with her parents on the Mary Celeste. Benjamin and Sarah Briggs had two children. Their son, Arthur, was born in 1865. Their daughter, Sophia Matilda, was born in 1870. When the Mary Celeste left New York in November 1872, Sophia was two years old, and she sailed with her parents. Historians note that a small child on board was not unusual on family voyages. The crew would have known her well. She played in the cabin and on the deck when the weather was calm. Even so, the presence of a young child makes the mystery especially touching. Many people have wondered what could have made her parents take her into a small open boat on the ocean. Mrs Briggs's letter mentioned that Sophia was excited about the trip. Arthur, who was seven years old, stayed at home with his grandmother in Marion, so that he could go to school. He never saw his parents or his sister again.

The Briggs family's home was in the quiet seaside town of Marion, in Massachusetts. Marion is a small town on Buzzards Bay, on the coast of Massachusetts. In the nineteenth century, it was home to many sailors and sea captains. Historians note that towns like Marion depended on the sea for their living. Men worked on whaling ships, merchant ships and fishing boats, and women ran the homes and farms while their husbands were away for months or years. Captain Briggs and his wife had a house in Marion, near the homes of their relatives. The town was quiet and peaceful, with white wooden houses, churches and gardens. Even so, it was a place used to loss. Many families had lost sons, husbands or fathers at sea. When news of the Mary Celeste reached Marion, it brought great sorrow. Arthur Briggs grew up there with his grandparents. A memorial to the family can still be seen in the cemetery at Marion, though their bodies were never found.

{img:18-woman-writing-letter-cabin-lamp-1872}

Before the voyage, Sarah Briggs wrote a cheerful letter to her mother-in-law. On the third of November 1872, while the Mary Celeste was being loaded in New York, Sarah Briggs wrote a letter to her mother-in-law in Marion. Historians have studied this letter carefully, because it is one of the last messages from the ship. In it, Sarah described the cabin, which had been freshly painted, and the preparations for the voyage. She wrote that Benjamin thought they had a good crew. She mentioned that Sophia was excited and liked to play with her things. Even so, the letter also showed her love for her son. She asked for news of Arthur and sent him her love. The letter is calm and cheerful, with no hint of worry. Captain Briggs also wrote to his mother, saying that the ship looked fine and that he hoped for a good passage. These letters are kept today in collections in Massachusetts. They give a warm picture of the family before the voyage.

{img:19-family-hymn-evening-cabin-melodeon}

On quiet evenings at sea, the Briggs family may have gathered around the melodeon to sing hymns. Life on a merchant ship followed a steady routine, but there were also quiet moments. On calm evenings, when the day's work was done, the captain's family could relax in their cabin. Historians imagine that the Briggs family, who were religious, sang hymns together while Sarah played the melodeon. On Sundays, Captain Briggs may have held a short service for the crew, as many religious captains did. Even so, no record of these evenings survives from the Mary Celeste's last voyage. The family's letters from earlier voyages describe such moments. They talk of reading, sewing, playing music and watching the sea and the sky. Sarah also kept a small garden of plants in pots, which she took on voyages. For the family, the ship was a home as well as a place of work. Its small cabin was filled with their books, music and belongings, which the boarding party later found untouched.

{img:20-memorial-stone-cemetery-marion-autumn}

The Briggs family's names were later carved on a memorial stone in their hometown. After the Mary Celeste was found, the Briggs family's relatives waited for news. They hoped that the people from the ship might have been picked up by another ship or have reached land. Historians note that months passed without any word. Slowly, the families understood that their loved ones were lost. In the cemetery of Marion, a memorial stone was later put up for Captain Briggs, Sarah and Sophia. It records their names and the year of their deaths at sea. Even so, the memorial does not mark a grave, since their bodies were never found. Arthur Briggs, the son who had stayed at home, grew up and lived a long life. He never knew what had happened to his family. In later years, he sometimes spoke to writers about the mystery. He did not believe the sensational stories that had been told. He believed his father would only have left the ship if he felt it was in danger.

{img:21-first-mate-portrait-deck-1872}

The first mate of the Mary Celeste, Albert Richardson, was a trusted and experienced sailor. Albert G. Richardson was the first mate of the Mary Celeste. He was born in Maine and had served in the Union army during the American Civil War. After the war, he became a sailor and rose to the rank of first mate. Historians note that the first mate was the captain's second in command. He supervised the crew, managed the cargo and took charge of the ship when the captain was resting. Richardson was married to a niece of James Winchester, the ship's main owner. He was well regarded, and Captain Briggs trusted him. Even so, little is known about his personality. His wife later said that he was a good and gentle man. The second mate was Andrew Gilling, a young man from New York of Danish background. He also had a good reputation. Together, the two mates were responsible for the daily running of the ship and the watches.

{img:22-german-sailors-crew-deck-rigging}

Four young sailors from Germany made up most of the ordinary crew. The Mary Celeste had four ordinary seamen, all from Germany. Two of them were brothers, Volkert and Boz Lorenzen. The others were Arian Martens and Gottlieb Goodschaad. Historians note that they came from islands and coastal areas in northern Germany, where many young men went to sea. They were described as peaceful and reliable sailors, with good records. Even so, little is known about them as individuals. Their families in Germany later received the sad news of their loss. One of the Lorenzen brothers had lost his possessions in an earlier shipwreck, and he had joined the Mary Celeste with only a few belongings. Some later stories suggested that the German sailors had mutinied and killed the captain. Historians reject this idea, since there was no sign of violence on board and the sailors had left their belongings behind. The four men worked hard, handling the sails and keeping watch, day and night.

[long-pause]

{img:23-ship-cook-galley-stove-pots}

The ship's cook and steward, Edward Head, had just married before the voyage. Edward William Head was the steward and cook of the Mary Celeste. He was born in New York and had recently married. Historians note that the cook was an important person on a ship. He prepared meals for the whole crew in a small kitchen on deck, called the galley. He cooked on a small iron stove that burned coal or wood. The food was simple: salted beef and pork, ship's biscuits, potatoes, beans, rice, coffee and tea. When possible, fresh food was bought in port. Even so, the quality of the food mattered greatly to sailors. A good cook made long voyages easier to bear. The steward also looked after the captain's cabin and served the officers' meals. Head had been recommended to Captain Briggs, and the captain was pleased with him. His young wife in New York waited for him to return. She, too, never learned what had become of her husband.

The Mary Celeste carried a cargo of 1,701 barrels of alcohol, bound for Genoa in Italy. For her voyage in November 1872, the Mary Celeste was loaded with 1,701 barrels of alcohol. It was not alcohol for drinking. It was industrial alcohol, made unfit to drink, which was used to strengthen wines in Italy. The cargo was shipped by a company called Meissner, Ackermann and Company, and it was bound for the port of Genoa. Historians note that the cargo was valuable, worth about thirty-five thousand dollars at the time. It was insured, as was the ship. The barrels were made of red oak and stacked tightly in the hold. Even so, alcohol was a dangerous cargo. It could leak from the barrels, and its fumes could build up in the closed hold. The fumes could catch fire or even explode if they came near a flame. Later, when the cargo was unloaded in Genoa, nine of the barrels were found to be empty. This detail would become important in the search for an explanation.

Dockworkers rolled the barrels of alcohol along the pier and up into the ship. Historians note that loading a ship was hard and careful work. The barrels had to be stacked tightly and secured, so that they would not move during the voyage. A cargo that shifted in a storm could cause a ship to tip over. Even so, the loading went well. Captain Briggs supervised the work and checked that everything was in order. The crew was hired and signed on. The ship was stocked with food, water, coal for the galley stove and spare sails and ropes. By early November, the Mary Celeste was ready to sail. The weather in New York was cold and grey, with the first signs of winter. Ships were leaving the harbour every day, heading across the Atlantic to Europe.

{img:26-captain-mate-discussing-chart-cabin}

Captain Briggs planned to sail across the Atlantic, past the Azores, and into the Mediterranean. The route from New York to Genoa was a common one for merchant ships. It crossed the Atlantic Ocean eastward, passing near the Azores, a group of Portuguese islands in the middle of the ocean. Then it continued towards the Strait of Gibraltar, the narrow passage between Spain and Morocco, and into the Mediterranean Sea. Historians note that the voyage normally took about a month. Captain Briggs and his mates studied the charts and planned their course. They would use a sextant to measure the height of the sun at noon and a chronometer, an accurate clock, to work out their position. Even so, the voyage would be in winter, when the Atlantic could be stormy. Strong winds, rain and high waves were common. Briggs was an experienced captain and was used to these conditions. He expected a normal crossing. Nothing in his letters suggests that he was worried about the voyage ahead.

{img:27-new-york-east-river-ships-masts-1872}

In 1872, New York was one of the busiest ports in the world, with ships arriving from every ocean. In the 1870s, New York was the largest city in the United States, with almost a million inhabitants. Its harbour was one of the busiest in the world. Historians note that thousands of ships arrived and departed every year, carrying goods and passengers to and from Europe, South America, the Caribbean and Asia. Along the East River and the Hudson River, piers stretched out into the water, crowded with sailing ships and steamships. The masts of the ships stood so close together that they looked like a forest. Even so, the age of sail was slowly coming to an end. Steamships were becoming faster and more reliable. Yet in 1872, many cargoes were still carried by sailing ships, which were cheaper to run. The waterfront was full of life, with sailors, merchants, dockworkers and shopkeepers. Taverns, boarding houses and shipping offices lined the streets near the water.

While the Mary Celeste was being loaded, the towers of the Brooklyn Bridge were rising over the East River. In 1872, a great engineering project was underway on the East River. Sailors in New York lived in boarding houses near the docks while they waited for a ship. When sailors arrived in New York, they usually stayed in boarding houses near the waterfront. Some boarding house keepers were honest, while others cheated the sailors, taking their wages or forcing them onto ships. Sailors were often signed on through shipping agents, who matched crews with ships. Even so, the crew of the Mary Celeste seems to have been chosen carefully. The voyage to Genoa would pay them a monthly wage, part of which they could receive in advance. Sailors' lives were hard and often lonely. They spent months at sea, far from their families, and their wages were low. Yet many loved the sea and could not imagine any other life.

A few piers away, another ship, the Dei Gratia, was preparing to sail to Europe as well. While the Mary Celeste was being loaded, another brigantine was also in New York. On a sailing ship, the day was divided into watches, and the ship's bell rang every half hour. Life on board a sailing ship followed a strict routine, organised around watches. The crew was divided into two groups, each led by one of the mates. Each group worked for four hours and then rested for four hours, day and night. Historians note that the ship's bell was rung every half hour to mark the passing of time. One bell meant the first half hour of a watch, two bells the first hour, and so on, up to eight bells at the end of the watch. Even so, the system had a clever twist. Two shorter watches in the early evening, called dog watches, made sure that the men did not work the same hours every day. The captain did not stand a watch, but he was always on call. On the Mary Celeste, the bell would have rung steadily through the days and nights of the voyage, marking the sailors' working hours across the ocean.

Sailors climbed high into the rigging to set and take in the square sails. Handling the sails was the most important and dangerous work on a sailing ship. On a brigantine like the Mary Celeste, the square sails on the foremast were set and taken in by sailors who climbed the rigging. Historians note that they stood on ropes called footropes, high above the deck, and leaned over the yards, the long wooden spars that held the sails. They had to work quickly, especially when the wind grew stronger. In storms, the ship rolled and pitched, and the men had to hold on tightly. Even so, experienced sailors moved through the rigging with great skill. They knew every rope and sail by name. Smaller sails could be handled from the deck. The fore-and-aft mainsail was raised and lowered with ropes and pulleys. The work never stopped. The sails had to be adjusted every time the wind changed direction or strength, by day and by night.

The ordinary sailors lived in a cramped space at the front of the ship called the forecastle. On a sailing ship, the ordinary sailors lived at the front of the ship, in a space called the forecastle, usually pronounced folk-sul. Historians note that it was small, crowded and often damp. Every day at noon, the captain measured the sun's height to find the ship's position. In 1872, there was no radio or satellite navigation. Ships found their position using the sun, the stars and careful calculations. Every day at noon, the captain or one of the mates measured the height of the sun above the horizon with a sextant, a brass instrument with mirrors and a telescope. Historians note that this measurement gave the ship's latitude, its position north or south. To find the longitude, its position east or west, the navigator also needed to know the exact time in Greenwich, England. This was given by a chronometer, a very accurate clock kept in a wooden box. Even so, navigation also relied on dead reckoning. This meant calculating the ship's position from its speed, course and the time that had passed. On the Mary Celeste, the sextant and chronometer were missing when she was found. This suggests that Captain Briggs took them with him when he left the ship.

Sailors' food was simple, but a cup of hot coffee in the morning was a great comfort. The food on a merchant ship in the 1870s was plain and filling. Even so, a good cook could make the food tasty. Coffee and tea were important, especially on cold nights and early mornings. The cook brought hot drinks to the men on watch. On the Mary Celeste, the food stores were found well stocked, with enough supplies for six months. The water casks were full. The supplies of food showed that the crew had not left the ship because they were hungry or thirsty.

In calm weather, the crew mended sails, spliced ropes and painted the ship. When the weather was calm, there was still plenty of work on a sailing ship. Historians note that captains kept their crews busy with maintenance. Sails were mended with heavy needles and a leather palm, a kind of thimble worn on the hand. Ropes were spliced, which meant joining two ropes together by weaving their strands. The deck was scrubbed, the brass was polished and the woodwork was painted. Even so, the work was not just about keeping busy. A well-maintained ship was safer and faster. Damaged sails or worn ropes could break in a storm, putting the ship and crew in danger. Sailors took pride in their skills and in the condition of their ship. On quiet afternoons, the steady work, the sound of the waves and the creak of the rigging made a peaceful scene. Many sailors later remembered these calm days at sea as the happiest part of their lives.

At night, one sailor stood at the wheel, steering by the compass and the stars. A ship at sea had to be steered at all times. During each watch, one sailor stood at the wheel at the back of the ship and kept her on course. Historians note that he steered by the compass, which was kept in a wooden stand called a binnacle, lit by a small lamp at night. He also watched the sails and the stars. Experienced helmsmen could feel the movement of the ship and the pressure of the wind, and they made small adjustments to keep her steady. Even so, steering at night could be lonely and tiring. The helmsman had to stay alert, watching for other ships, changes in the weather and the set of the sails. On clear nights, the sky was filled with stars, far brighter than on land. The only sounds were the waves, the wind in the rigging and the creaking of the ship's timbers as she sailed on through the darkness.

Even so, it also became an inquiry into what had happened to the ship and her people. Witnesses were called, including Deveau, Wright and other members of the Dei Gratia's crew. They described in detail how they had found the ship and what they had seen on board. The court also heard from experts who had examined the ship. The hearing lasted several months, with long breaks. The records of the court survive and are one of the most important sources for the story. They contain the sailors' own words, describing the empty ship.

[long-pause]

{img:66-attorney-general-portrait-office-1872}

Frederick Solly-Flood, the attorney general of Gibraltar, believed that a crime had been committed. Frederick Solly-Flood was the attorney general of Gibraltar, the chief legal officer of the government. He took a close interest in the Mary Celeste case. Historians note that he was suspicious from the start. He believed that the crew had been murdered, either by the sailors themselves, who might have drunk the alcohol and become violent, or by the crew of the Dei Gratia. He also suspected that the two captains might have plotted together to claim the salvage money. Even so, Solly-Flood had little evidence to support his theories. The alcohol was not fit to drink. There were no signs of a struggle, and the crew's belongings were untouched. Historians describe him as a proud and stubborn man, who was determined to find wrongdoing. His suspicions made the hearing longer and more difficult. He ordered several investigations of the ship, hoping to find proof of a crime, but nothing was ever found.

[long-pause]

{img:67-surveyor-examining-ship-hull-notebook}

Experts examined the ship from top to bottom, looking for signs of violence or damage. At the request of Solly-Flood, the Mary Celeste was examined several times by experts. Historians note that the main surveyor was John Austin, the surveyor of shipping in Gibraltar. He inspected the hull, the deck, the cabins and the hold. He also took a diver to examine the hull below the waterline. Even so, the experts found no evidence of collision, fire or violence. The hull was sound, and there was no sign that the ship had struck rocks or been in a battle. They noted the cuts at the bow but could not agree on their cause. They also found some marks on the rail and on the sword in the cabin, which Solly-Flood thought might be bloodstains. A doctor tested them. The marks were found not to be blood. The surveyors concluded that the Mary Celeste had been abandoned while in good condition, for a reason that could not be found.

[long-pause]

{img:68-captain-morehouse-witness-stand}

The crew of the Dei Gratia were questioned closely, and some officials suspected them of wrongdoing. Because of Solly-Flood's suspicions, the crew of the Dei Gratia were questioned closely in court. Historians note that they were asked about every detail of their voyage and their discovery. They were asked why they had sailed so close, whether they had known her captain and whether they had seen anything strange. Even so, their answers were consistent. They described what they had found and how they had sailed the ship to Gibraltar. There was no evidence that they had done anything wrong. Some officials suggested that the Dei Gratia's crew might have killed the people on the Mary Celeste to claim the salvage money. Historians have rejected this idea. The Dei Gratia's crew were honest sailors with good reputations. They had been sailing a separate course, and they could not have caught up with the Mary Celeste until long after the last log entry. The suspicion against them was unfair, and it affected the size of their reward.

[long-pause]

{img:69-salvage-award-papers-coins-table}

In March 1873, the court awarded the crew of the Dei Gratia a reward of 1,700 pounds. On the fourteenth of March 1873, the vice admiralty court in Gibraltar made its decision. It awarded the crew of the Dei Gratia a salvage reward of 1,700 pounds. Historians note that this was about one-fifth of the value of the ship and cargo. It was a considerable sum, but much less than many expected. In similar cases, salvors often received a third or even a half of the value. Even so, the court did not explain its decision in detail. Many historians believe that the low award reflected the suspicions raised by Solly-Flood. The court did not accuse the Dei Gratia's crew of any crime, but it seemed to doubt their story. The money was divided among the captain, the officers and the crew, according to the custom of the sea. Deveau and his two companions received extra shares for sailing the Mary Celeste to port. The crew felt that they had been treated unfairly.

[long-pause]

{img:70-unanswered-questions-judge-reading-file}

The court ended its work without discovering what had happened to the ten people on board. When the hearing in Gibraltar ended, the court had decided who should be paid and how much. Historians note that it had not answered the biggest question, the fate of the people of the Mary Celeste. The court found no evidence of a crime, but it also found no clear explanation for the abandonment. Even so, it was not the court's job to solve the mystery. Its main task had been to deal with the salvage claim. Solly-Flood sent reports to the British government and to the American consul, setting out his suspicions. The American government took an interest in the case, and some investigations were made in America. No new information was found. The missing lifeboat never reappeared. No bodies were washed ashore. No survivors came forward. The case was officially closed, but people did not stop wondering. Over the years, the mystery only grew larger.

[long-pause]

{img:71-american-consul-letter-desk-1873}

The American consul in Gibraltar sent reports home, and the families of the missing waited for news. The American consul in Gibraltar was Horatio Sprague, who represented the interests of American citizens and ships in the port. Historians note that he followed the case closely and sent detailed reports to the government in Washington. He believed that the crew of the Dei Gratia had acted honestly, and he defended them against Solly-Flood's suspicions. Sprague also sent news to the families of the missing people. He tried to answer their questions and returned the belongings found on board. Even so, he had no real answers to give. The families in Marion, New York and Germany waited for months, hoping that their loved ones might have been rescued. Sprague later corresponded with writers and investigators who were interested in the case. His reports are an important source for historians. They show the care and kindness with which he treated a difficult and sad situation, far from home.

[long-pause]

{img:72-alcohol-fumes-hold-barrels-lantern-haze}

One popular theory suggests that alcohol fumes in the hold made the captain fear an explosion. One of the most widely accepted theories about the Mary Celeste involves her cargo of alcohol. Historians note that nine barrels were found empty in Genoa, which means that alcohol had leaked into the hold. Alcohol gives off strong fumes, which can catch fire. According to this theory, the crew may have opened the hold and smelled strong fumes, or heard a small explosion or rumbling sound. Captain Briggs, who had never carried alcohol before, may have feared that the ship was about to explode. He ordered everyone into the lifeboat, intending to stay close by until the danger had passed. Even so, the theory has a problem. There were no signs of fire or explosion on the ship. In 2006, scientists at University College London carried out an experiment. They showed that alcohol vapour could burn with a cool flame that leaves no scorch marks, which made the theory more believable.

[long-pause]

{img:73-waterspout-ocean-dark-clouds}

Some people have suggested that a waterspout, a tornado over the sea, may have frightened the crew. A waterspout is a spinning column of air and water that forms over the sea, like a tornado over land. Waterspouts can be very powerful, and they can damage ships, tear sails and suck up water. Historians note that some writers have suggested that the Mary Celeste may have been struck by a waterspout. It could have damaged the sails, forced water into the hold and caused the instruments to give false readings. The crew might have believed that the ship was sinking and taken to the lifeboat. Even so, there is no clear evidence for this theory. A waterspout would probably have caused more damage than was found on the ship. Waterspouts are also relatively rare in that part of the Atlantic in winter. Yet the theory is not impossible. It shows how many different natural events might have frightened an experienced crew into leaving a ship that later turned out to be sound.

[long-pause]

{img:74-earthquake-sea-floor-waves-ship-trembling}

Another theory suggests that an undersea earthquake shook the ship near the Azores. The Azores lie on a boundary between great plates of the Earth's crust, and earthquakes are common there. Some earthquakes happen under the sea, and their shock can be felt by ships passing above. Historians note that sailors have reported feeling their ships shake violently during such seaquakes, as if they had struck rocks. Some writers have suggested that a seaquake near Santa Maria may have shaken the Mary Celeste, perhaps loosening barrels in the hold and releasing alcohol fumes. The crew may have thought the ship had been damaged and abandoned her. Even so, there are no records of a seaquake at that time and place. Seismographs, instruments that record earthquakes, were not yet in use. So the theory cannot be checked. It remains one of many possible explanations. What it shows is that the area where the Mary Celeste was last recorded was a place where unexpected natural events could happen.

[long-pause]

{img:75-pump-taken-apart-deck-tools}

A recent study suggested that a broken pump may have made the crew believe the ship was sinking. In 2007, a documentary investigation by the Smithsonian Channel examined the Mary Celeste mystery again. Historians and researchers looked at the evidence carefully. They noted that one of the ship's pumps had been taken apart. They also noted that the Mary Celeste had recently been carrying coal, and coal dust and debris could have blocked the pumps. According to this theory, the crew discovered water in the hold and tried to pump it out, but the pumps did not work properly. Captain Briggs may have believed that the ship was taking on water quickly. With the island of Santa Maria in sight, he might have decided to abandon ship and head for land. Even so, the theory has its limits. It cannot explain everything, and it cannot be proven. Yet many historians find it the most convincing explanation. It fits the evidence of the pump, the sounding rod and the last log entry near the island.

[long-pause]

{img:76-pirates-sailing-ship-horizon-myth}

Stories about pirates, mutiny and sea monsters have all been told, but there is no evidence for them. Over the years, many dramatic explanations have been offered for the Mary Celeste mystery. Some suggested that pirates attacked the ship and carried off the crew. Others said that the crew mutinied, killed the captain and his family and then escaped. Some writers even claimed that a giant squid or sea monster had snatched the people from the deck. Historians note that none of these theories fits the evidence. Pirates or mutineers would have taken the valuable cargo and personal belongings, which were left on board. There was no sign of a struggle or violence. Even so, these stories were popular, because they were exciting. Newspapers and magazines printed them, and readers enjoyed the thrill of a terrible secret. Historians prefer explanations based on the evidence, such as fear of an explosion or a sinking. Yet the dramatic theories show how the mystery captured people's imaginations, far beyond the facts.

[long-pause]

{img:77-lifeboat-drifting-alone-empty-sea}

Whatever the reason, the small boat and its passengers were never seen again. Most historians agree on what happened to the people of the Mary Celeste, even if they disagree about why. They believe that the ten people left the ship in the small yawl, perhaps near the island of Santa Maria. They may have tied the yawl to the ship with the long halyard rope, hoping to return if the danger passed. Then something went wrong. Perhaps the wind rose and the rope broke, and the ship sailed away from them. Even so, no one knows for certain. The yawl may have been swamped by waves, or carried out into the ocean. With a woman, a small child and eight men in a small boat, they would have had little chance in rough seas. The ship that they had left behind sailed on safely for another nine days. It is a sad and simple explanation. Many historians believe that this is most likely what happened.

[long-pause]

{img:78-harbour-sunset-calm-sea-memory}

In the end, the mystery may never be solved, but the people of the Mary Celeste are still remembered. More than one hundred and fifty years after the Mary Celeste was found, no one knows for certain what happened to the people on board. Historians have studied the court records, the letters and the ship's details. They have weighed all the theories carefully. Even so, the final answer remains out of reach. Without survivors or new evidence, it may never be found. Yet the people of the Mary Celeste are still remembered. Captain Briggs, Sarah and little Sophia, the mates Albert Richardson and Andrew Gilling, the cook Edward Head and the four German sailors, Volkert and Boz Lorenzen, Arian Martens and Gottlieb Goodschaad, were real people with families who loved them. Their loss was a real tragedy. Behind the famous mystery is a simple human story. Ten people set out across the ocean in November 1872, hoping for a safe voyage, and never came home.

[long-pause]

{img:79-victorian-writer-desk-lamp-manuscript-1884}

In 1884, a young doctor named Arthur Conan Doyle wrote a story that made the ship famous. In 1884, a young doctor named Arthur Conan Doyle wrote a short story about the Mary Celeste. He was not yet famous. His detective Sherlock Holmes would first appear three years later. The story was called J. Habakuk Jephson's Statement, and it was published in the Cornhill Magazine, a respected literary journal. Historians note that the story was written as if it were a true account by a survivor. In it, the ship was taken over by a passenger who wanted revenge. Even so, Conan Doyle changed many facts. He called the ship the Marie Celeste instead of the Mary Celeste. He also added invented details, such as warm cups of tea on the table and a lifeboat still on board. Many readers believed that the story was true. It made the Mary Celeste famous around the world. Even today, the wrong name, Marie Celeste, is often used.

[long-pause]

{img:80-victorian-readers-magazine-parlour-fire}

Readers in Britain and America were fascinated by the story, and many believed it was true. When Conan Doyle's story appeared in 1884, it caused a sensation. Readers in Britain and America discussed it eagerly. Historians note that some people wrote to newspapers, asking whether the story was true. Frederick Solly-Flood, the attorney general of Gibraltar, was angry and called it a fiction. The American consul, Horatio Sprague, also pointed out that it was not accurate. Even so, the story continued to be read and believed. It set a pattern for later tellings of the mystery. Over the following decades, many writers produced their own versions, each adding new details and theories. Some claimed to have found survivors or secret documents. Most of these stories were invented. Historians have had to work hard to separate the facts from the fiction. Yet the stories kept the Mary Celeste in people's memories. Without them, the ship might have been forgotten, like many other abandoned vessels.

[long-pause]

{img:81-mary-celeste-trading-voyages-caribbean}

After 1872, the Mary Celeste continued to sail for another twelve years, with many different owners. After her famous voyage, the Mary Celeste returned to ordinary work. Her owners sold her in 1873, and she changed hands many times in the following years. Historians note that she carried cargoes along the coasts of America, to the Caribbean and to Africa and the Indian Ocean. Even so, she had a bad reputation. Sailors were superstitious and believed that she was an unlucky ship. Some refused to sail on her. It became difficult to find crews, and her owners lost money. Several of her captains had problems. One fell ill and died, and others had accidents. Her owners struggled to make her profitable. Historians note that many old sailing ships had similar problems, as steamships took over more and more of the trade. Yet the Mary Celeste's reputation made things worse. She sailed on for twelve more years, through the warm waters of the Caribbean and beyond, but her best days were behind her.

[long-pause]

{img:82-ship-wrecked-coral-reef-haiti-1885}

In January 1885, her last captain deliberately wrecked the Mary Celeste on a reef off the coast of Haiti. In 1884, the Mary Celeste was bought by a group of businessmen in Boston. They loaded her with a cargo that they claimed was valuable, and they insured it for a large sum. Historians note that the cargo was actually worth very little. The ship's captain, Gilman Parker, sailed her to the Caribbean. On the third of January 1885, he ran her onto the Rochelais Reef, a coral reef off the coast of Haiti, on purpose. The ship was wrecked, and the crew went ashore. Even so, the plan failed. The insurance companies became suspicious and investigated. They found that the cargo was nearly worthless. Parker and the businessmen were charged with fraud. The case was complicated, and Parker was not convicted, but he died a few months later. Several of the businessmen were ruined. The Mary Celeste ended her life as part of a crime, wrecked on a reef in tropical waters.

[long-pause]

{img:83-old-wooden-timbers-coral-reef-clear-water}

In 2001, an expedition claimed to have found the wreck of the Mary Celeste off Haiti. In 2001, the American author and explorer Clive Cussler led an expedition to search for the wreck of the Mary Celeste. Historians note that Cussler was famous for his adventure novels and for founding an organisation that searched for historic shipwrecks. His team dived on the Rochelais Reef, off the coast of Haiti, and found the remains of an old wooden ship. They announced that they had found the Mary Celeste. Even so, later tests raised doubts. In 2005, scientists studied the wood from the wreck using tree-ring dating. They found that the wood came from trees that were still growing in the 1890s, many years after the Mary Celeste was wrecked. This meant that the wreck could not be hers. The search for the real wreck continues. Some believe that her remains still lie somewhere on the reef, broken up and covered by coral, waiting to be identified.

[long-pause]

{img:84-spencers-island-monument-shore-bay}

Today, a monument at Spencer's Island in Nova Scotia marks the place where the ship was built. At Spencer's Island, in Nova Scotia, a monument now stands near the place where the Amazon, later the Mary Celeste, was built in 1861. Historians note that the small community is proud of its connection to the famous ship. Visitors come to see the monument, walk along the shore and look out across the Bay of Fundy, which has some of the highest tides in the world. Even so, the shipyard itself has long disappeared. Spencer's Island is a quiet place today, with a small lighthouse and a few houses. In the nineteenth century, it was busy with shipbuilding, and hundreds of ships were launched from the shores of the Bay of Fundy. The Mary Celeste is now the most famous of them all. The people of the area remember her not as a ghost ship, but as a fine vessel built by local hands, whose story became one of the great puzzles of the sea.

[long-pause]

{img:85-calm-ocean-sunrise-sailing-ship-silhouette}

The name Mary Celeste is still used today for places that have been mysteriously abandoned. More than a century and a half after she was found drifting in the Atlantic, the Mary Celeste is still famous. Her name has become a byword in English for any place that has been suddenly and mysteriously abandoned. People speak of a deserted office or an empty town as being like the Mary Celeste. Historians note that her story has inspired books, films, plays and documentaries. It is still studied by historians, scientists and sailors. Even so, the true story is quieter than the legends. It is the story of a well-built ship, an experienced captain and a good crew, and of a decision made in a moment of fear or uncertainty. It is a reminder of how dangerous the sea could be in the age of sail, and how small human beings were in the middle of the vast ocean. The ship sailed on, while her people were lost.

[long-pause]

{img:86-old-ship-model-museum-case-soft-light}

Models, letters and records of the Mary Celeste are kept in museums and archives on both sides of the Atlantic. Today, the story of the Mary Celeste can be explored in museums and archives on both sides of the Atlantic. Historians note that the records of the court in Gibraltar are kept in the archives there. Letters from the Briggs family and the American consul are kept in collections in the United States. Maritime museums in Nova Scotia, Massachusetts and elsewhere display models of the ship and tell her story. Even so, the most important objects are lost. The lifeboat, the instruments and the people who carried them were never found. What remains are papers, models and memories. Visitors to these museums can read the sailors' testimony and see the faces of the people involved in old photographs. They can imagine the small brigantine sailing out of New York on a grey November day in 1872, carrying ten people, a family's music and a cargo of alcohol across the winter Atlantic, towards a mystery that has never been solved.
