{img:scrooby-village-dawn}
Tonight we are going about four hundred years back, to a small village in the flat green farmland of Nottinghamshire, in the middle of England.

[long-pause]

The village is called Scrooby.

[pause]

It is the year 1600, more or less, and the morning mist is lying low over the meadows along the River Ryton.

[pause]

There are perhaps forty or fifty houses here, with walls of timber and clay, and roofs of thatch that have turned grey with the years.

[pause]

Smoke rises slowly from the chimneys. A cart rolls along the muddy lane, pulled by a single brown horse, and somewhere a cockerel is calling.

[long-pause]

Scrooby is not an important place.

[pause]

Most of the people who live here are farmers and farm labourers. They plough the heavy soil with oxen, they sow barley and wheat and rye, and in the autumn they bring the harvest home on creaking wagons.

[pause]

They keep pigs and geese, and a cow or two if they are lucky, and in the evenings they sit by the fire and mend their tools.

[pause]

The days follow the seasons, the way they have done for hundreds of years.

[long-pause]

{img:scrooby-manor-house}
At the edge of the village stands an old manor house, with a moat around it and a great hall inside.

[pause]

It belongs to the Archbishop of York, and long ago, kings and queens stayed here on their journeys to the north.

[pause]

Now it is quieter. The great hall is cold and partly empty, and some of the rooms are falling into disrepair.

[pause]

But the house still has an important job. Scrooby lies on the Great North Road, the main road between London and Scotland, and the manor is a postal stage.

[pause]

Riders arrive here with letters and royal messages, change their tired horses for fresh ones, and gallop on.

[long-pause]

The man in charge of all this is the postmaster, and his name is William Brewster.

[pause]

He is in his thirties, a calm and thoughtful man with a short beard. As a young man he studied for a while at Cambridge, and then he worked for one of Queen Elizabeth's diplomats, travelling with him as far as the Netherlands.

[pause]

Now he has come home to Scrooby, where his father was postmaster before him.

[pause]

He lives in the manor house with his wife Mary and their children, and he keeps a small collection of books, which is unusual in a village like this.

[long-pause]

{img:brewster-reading-candle}
In the evenings, Brewster likes to read by candlelight.

[pause]

He reads the Bible, and books about the church, and he thinks a great deal about what he reads.

[pause]

In these years, religion is not a private matter in England. There is one official church, the Church of England, and everyone is expected to belong to it, to attend its services on Sunday, and to follow its rules.

[pause]

But some people feel that the church has kept too much of the old ceremony. They would like a simpler kind of worship, closer to what they read in the Bible, without bishops and without elaborate rituals.

[pause]

Most of these people stay inside the church and hope that it will slowly change.

[pause]

A smaller group goes further. They decide to leave the official church altogether, and to form their own little congregations.

[pause]

They are called Separatists, and in the eyes of the law, what they are doing is forbidden.

[long-pause]

{img:austerfield-young-bradford}
A few miles to the north of Scrooby lies another village, called Austerfield.

[pause]

There, in the year 1590, a boy named William Bradford was born.

[pause]

His family were farmers, quite comfortable ones, but William's life did not begin easily. His father died when he was still a baby, and his mother remarried and moved away, and the boy was raised by his grandfather and then by his uncles.

[pause]

He was often unwell as a child, and he spent long days indoors, reading.

[pause]

When he was about twelve years old, he began to walk over to the nearby village of Babworth on Sundays, to hear a preacher called Richard Clyfton.

[pause]

There he met William Brewster, and the older man took an interest in this quiet, serious boy. He lent him books, and talked with him, and slowly became something like a father to him.

[long-pause]

{img:secret-meeting-manor-hall}
Around the year 1606, a small group of Separatists began to meet in secret at the manor house in Scrooby.

[pause]

On Sundays, families came from the surrounding villages, walking along the lanes and across the fields, and gathered in Brewster's rooms.

[pause]

They prayed together, and read from the Bible, and sang psalms, and listened to a sermon.

[pause]

Their minister was Richard Clyfton, an older man with a long white beard, and alongside him was a younger preacher called John Robinson, who would become very important to them in the years ahead.

[pause]

Young William Bradford was there too, sitting among the families, listening carefully.

[long-pause]

But a secret like this was hard to keep in a small part of the country.

[pause]

People talked. The church authorities heard about the meetings, and some of the members were questioned, and fined, and watched.

[pause]

Their houses were visited. Some of them lost their work.

[pause]

And slowly, the little congregation began to talk about something that must have seemed almost impossible.

[pause]

They began to talk about leaving England.

[long-pause]

Across the North Sea, in the Netherlands, there was a country where people were allowed to worship more or less as they wished.

[pause]

It was a country of merchants and ships and busy towns, and it was not so far away.

[pause]

If they could only get there, they might live in peace.

[long-pause]

{img:boston-creek-night}
Leaving England was not simple.

[pause]

In those days, people were not allowed to leave the country without permission, and the Separatists knew that permission would never be given to them.

[pause]

So in the autumn of 1607, they made a quiet plan. They sold what they could not carry, packed their belongings, and travelled east to the coast of Lincolnshire, near the town of Boston.

[pause]

There they had arranged for a ship to carry them across the sea in secret.

[long-pause]

The first attempt did not go well.

[pause]

The captain they had hired gave them away to the authorities, and the whole group was taken back to Boston, where they spent some weeks under arrest before most of them were allowed to go home.

[pause]

They had lost money and possessions, and many of them now had no homes to go back to.

[pause]

But they did not give up.

[long-pause]

The following spring, in 1608, they tried again.

[pause]

This time they hired a Dutch ship, which was to meet them on a lonely stretch of shore near the mouth of the River Humber.

[pause]

The men walked there over land, and the women and children came down the river in a small boat with the luggage.

[pause]

It took a long time, and there were many delays, and the whole adventure was full of difficulties.

[pause]

But in the end, little by little, over the following months, the whole congregation found its way across the sea.

[long-pause]

{img:amsterdam-canal-1608}
At first, they settled in Amsterdam.

[pause]

To people from the quiet villages of Nottinghamshire, it must have looked like another world.

[pause]

Amsterdam was one of the busiest cities in Europe. Its canals were crowded with boats, and its quays were stacked with barrels and bales and crates from every corner of the world.

[pause]

There were tall narrow houses of brick, with stepped gables and big windows, leaning gently against each other along the water.

[pause]

There were bells ringing from the church towers, and carts rattling over the bridges, and the smell of fish and tar and fresh bread in the air.

[pause]

People spoke Dutch, of course, and the newcomers understood almost none of it.

[long-pause]

They stayed in Amsterdam for about a year.

[pause]

But there were already other English Separatist groups in the city, and those groups were quarrelling among themselves.

[pause]

John Robinson, who was now their pastor, did not want his people to be drawn into these arguments.

[pause]

So in 1609, they asked permission to move to another town, about a day's journey to the south.

[pause]

The town was called Leiden.

[long-pause]

{img:leiden-street-weavers}
Leiden was a university town, and a cloth town.

[pause]

It was full of weavers and spinners and dyers, and everywhere you went, you could hear the steady clack of looms coming from open doorways.

[pause]

The town was surrounded by water, with canals running through it and windmills turning on the old walls.

[pause]

Over the next few years, the English families settled in the narrow streets around the great Pieterskerk, the church of Saint Peter, with its tall roof rising over the houses.

[pause]

Some of them lived in small houses built around little courtyards. Others shared rooms in larger buildings.

[pause]

In 1611, John Robinson and some of his friends bought a large house close to the church, and in its garden they built a row of small cottages where poorer members of the congregation could live.

[pause]

The house itself became their meeting place, where they gathered on Sundays to pray and sing and listen.

[long-pause]

Life in Leiden was hard work.

[pause]

Most of the English had been farmers at home, and there were no farms for them here. They had to learn new trades, and the trades they could find were mostly in the cloth industry.

[pause]

They became wool combers, and weavers, and makers of serge and fustian. Others became carpenters, or tailors, or brewers' helpers.

[pause]

William Bradford, now a young man, worked as a maker of fustian, a heavy cloth of cotton and linen.

[pause]

He saved his money, and when he came of age he received a small inheritance from his family in England, and he bought a little house of his own.

[pause]

In 1613, he married a young woman from the congregation named Dorothy May.

[long-pause]

{img:brewster-printing-press}
William Brewster, the old postmaster of Scrooby, also had to find a new way of earning a living.

[pause]

At first he taught English to students at the university, who were curious about the language.

[pause]

Later, together with a younger man named Edward Winslow, he set up a small printing press in a narrow lane near the church.

[pause]

There they printed religious books, in English, which were then quietly carried across the sea and sold in England, where books like these could not be printed.

[pause]

It was patient, careful work. Every page was set by hand, letter by letter, with little pieces of metal type, and then inked and pressed onto the paper one sheet at a time.

[pause]

In the evenings, the printed sheets hung drying on lines across the room, like washing.

[long-pause]

{img:robinson-sermon-leiden}
John Robinson was the heart of the community.

[pause]

He was a gentle and learned man, and he became well known in Leiden. He even joined the university and took part in its debates.

[pause]

On Sundays, the congregation gathered in his house to hear him preach. He spoke calmly and at length, and the people listened, sitting close together on wooden benches, with the light coming in through the small leaded windows.

[pause]

They sang psalms together, without any instruments, in long slow melodies.

[pause]

Over the years, the congregation grew. New members arrived from England, children were born, and young people married.

[pause]

By around 1620, there were perhaps three hundred people in the community.

[pause]

For more than ten years, Leiden was their home.

[long-pause]

{img:leiden-children-canal}
But as the years went by, the families began to worry.

[pause]

Life in Leiden was safe, but it was also very hard. Many of them worked long hours at the looms, from early morning until late in the evening, and still they were poor.

[pause]

The older members were growing tired. Some of those who had come over from England as strong young people were now grey and stooped.

[pause]

And the children were changing.

[long-pause]

The children of the congregation had grown up in Leiden. They played along the canals with Dutch children, and spoke Dutch in the streets, and some of them were beginning to speak it better than English.

[pause]

Some of the young people were leaving the community, to become soldiers, or sailors, or simply to live as their Dutch neighbours did.

[pause]

The parents felt that, slowly, quietly, their little English community was dissolving into the town around it, like sugar in warm water.

[pause]

In another generation, they thought, there might be nothing left of it at all.

[long-pause]

There was another worry, too.

[pause]

For many years, the Netherlands had been at war with Spain. In 1609, the two countries had agreed to a pause, a truce of twelve years.

[pause]

That truce would end in 1621, and everyone expected that the fighting would begin again.

[pause]

If the Spanish armies returned, Leiden might be besieged, as it had been once before, within living memory.

[long-pause]

{img:robinson-house-discussion}
And so, in the evenings, in John Robinson's house, the members of the congregation began to talk about moving once more.

[pause]

This time, they spoke of going much further.

[pause]

They spoke of America.

[pause]

It was a bold idea, and many of them were frightened by it. America lay on the other side of a great ocean, and they knew very little about it.

[pause]

Some said it would be too dangerous, too far, too expensive. Others said that every great undertaking is difficult, and that with care and patience it could be done.

[pause]

They talked it over for a long time. Different places were suggested, including the hot lands of Guiana in South America, and the English colony of Virginia.

[pause]

In the end, they decided to seek permission to settle in the northern part of the lands claimed by the Virginia Company, near the mouth of the Hudson River.

[long-pause]

A plan like this needed money, a great deal of it.

[pause]

The families had very little, so they made an agreement with a group of investors in London, a company of merchants who were known as the Merchant Adventurers.

[pause]

Their leader was a man named Thomas Weston.

[pause]

The merchants would pay for the ships and supplies. In return, the settlers would work for seven years, and everything they produced, the fish and timber and furs, would go into a common stock, to be shared out between the settlers and the merchants at the end.

[pause]

The negotiations were long and difficult, and the terms changed several times, and not everyone was happy with them.

[pause]

But by the summer of 1620, everything was more or less ready.

[long-pause]

It was decided that not everyone would go at first.

[pause]

Only part of the congregation, those who were younger and stronger, or who most wanted to go, would sail in the first voyage. The others would follow later, when the new settlement was established.

[pause]

John Robinson, their beloved pastor, would stay behind in Leiden with the larger group.

[pause]

William Brewster, now in his fifties, would go with the travellers as their elder, to lead them in prayer and worship.

[pause]

William Bradford and his wife Dorothy would go too.

[pause]

They made the difficult decision to leave their little son John behind with family in Leiden, to come over later, when it was safer.

[long-pause]

{img:speedwell-delfshaven-quay}
For the journey, the congregation bought a small ship of their own, called the Speedwell.

[pause]

She was a little vessel of about sixty tons, and the plan was that she would carry them across the ocean, and then stay in America, to be used for fishing and trading along the coast.

[pause]

At the end of July in the year 1620, the travellers left Leiden on barges, gliding along the canals through the flat summer countryside, past windmills and grazing cows.

[pause]

They went to the little harbour town of Delfshaven, near Rotterdam, where the Speedwell lay waiting at the quay.

[pause]

Many of those who were staying behind came with them, to say goodbye.

[long-pause]

{img:delfshaven-farewell-prayer}
They spent the last night together.

[pause]

William Bradford wrote about it many years later. He remembered that there was little sleep that night, but friendly talk, and many quiet words between friends who knew they might not see each other again.

[pause]

The next morning, the wind was favourable, and it was time to go aboard.

[pause]

On the quay, John Robinson knelt down, and the whole company knelt with him, and he prayed for them, with tears.

[pause]

Then they embraced one another, and said their farewells.

[pause]

Bradford wrote that they had lived in Leiden for nearly twelve years, in a goodly and pleasant city. And he wrote one more line that would become famous.

[pause]

He wrote, "they knew they were pilgrims."

[long-pause]

{img:speedwell-leaving-harbour}
The Speedwell raised her sails and slowly moved away from the quay.

[pause]

As she did, the travellers fired a small farewell volley from their muskets, and a few small cannon on board answered with a boom that echoed over the water.

[pause]

On the quay, the people who were staying behind waved, and kept waving, until the little ship was far out on the river.

[pause]

Then she turned towards the sea, and towards England.

[long-pause]

{img:mayflower-thames-moored}
Meanwhile, in London, the merchants had hired a second, larger ship.

[pause]

Her name was the Mayflower.

[long-pause]

She was not a new ship, and she was not a grand one.

[pause]

She was an ordinary merchant ship, perhaps twelve years old, and for most of her working life she had carried cargo between England and the ports of France and Spain, and sometimes Norway.

[pause]

She had carried wool, and hats, and hemp, and fish. But most of all, she had carried wine.

[pause]

Barrel after barrel of French wine, loaded at the quays of Bordeaux and brought home to London.

[pause]

People who have written about her since like to imagine that her timbers still smelled faintly of it, a sweet and dusky smell, deep in the hold.

[long-pause]

She was what sailors called a carrack, or a merchantman, with a high, square castle at the stern and a smaller one at the bow.

[pause]

She had three masts. The two at the front carried big square sails, and the one at the back carried a sail shaped like a triangle.

[pause]

Her hull was made of oak, thick and strong, and painted dark, and she was about a hundred feet long, perhaps a little more.

[pause]

That is about the length of three or four buses placed end to end.

[pause]

She could carry about one hundred and eighty tons of cargo. For a merchant ship of her time, that made her of a good, middling size.

[pause]

But she had never been built to carry people.

[long-pause]

{img:captain-jones-cabin}
Her master, as the captain of a merchant ship was called, was a man named Christopher Jones.

[pause]

He was about fifty years old, and he came from Harwich, a port on the east coast of England, where he had grown up among ships and sailors.

[pause]

He was also one of the owners of the Mayflower. He had sailed her for years, and he knew her well.

[pause]

His home was now in Rotherhithe, a riverside village just downstream from London, on the south bank of the Thames, where he lived with his wife Josian and their children.

[pause]

Jones was an experienced seaman. He had sailed the coasts of Europe many times, but like most of his crew, he had never crossed the Atlantic Ocean.

[long-pause]

The crew were ordinary English sailors, perhaps thirty of them.

[pause]

There were the master's mates, who helped to command the ship and stood watch at night. There was a carpenter, a cooper to look after the barrels, a cook, a boatswain who looked after the sails and ropes, and a gunner.

[pause]

There was a ship's surgeon, a young man named Giles Heale.

[pause]

And there were the ordinary sailors, the men who climbed the rigging, hauled on the ropes, and worked the pumps.

[pause]

Most of them were rough, practical men, and they did not entirely understand the quiet, praying passengers who would soon be sharing their ship.

[long-pause]

{img:southampton-two-ships}
In the middle of July, the Mayflower sailed from London down the Thames and around the coast to Southampton, where she was to meet the Speedwell.

[pause]

On board she carried a group of passengers from London and the south of England, who had been gathered by the merchants.

[pause]

Some of them belonged to the same church tradition as the Leiden congregation. Others did not. They were craftsmen and labourers and their families, who simply wanted a new life on the other side of the ocean.

[pause]

At the end of July, the Speedwell arrived from Holland, and the two groups met at Southampton for the first time.

[pause]

They spent about a week there, loading supplies, settling accounts, and arguing with the merchants' agent about money.

[pause]

Then, on the fifth of August 1620, the two ships set sail together.

[long-pause]

{img:speedwell-leaking-dartmouth}
They did not get very far.

[pause]

After only a few days, the Speedwell began to leak.

[pause]

Water was coming in through her seams faster than her crew could pump it out.

[pause]

The two ships turned aside into the harbour of Dartmouth, a pretty port on the coast of Devon, where the Speedwell was taken out of the water and her hull was searched and repaired.

[pause]

This took more than a week.

[pause]

Then, towards the end of August, the two ships set out once again.

[long-pause]

This time they sailed perhaps three hundred miles out into the ocean, beyond the far western tip of England.

[pause]

And once again, the Speedwell began to leak.

[pause]

Her master declared that she could not possibly cross the Atlantic, and that if they went on, she would surely be lost.

[pause]

So once again, both ships turned around, and this time they made for the nearest large harbour, which was Plymouth.

[pause]

Years later, people came to believe that the Speedwell had been carrying too much sail, and that the strain had opened her seams. Some even thought that her crew had wanted to avoid the long voyage, and had not tried very hard.

[pause]

Whatever the reason, she would not be going to America.

[long-pause]

{img:plymouth-harbour-1620}
In Plymouth, a decision had to be made.

[pause]

All the supplies and as many of the passengers as possible would be moved from the Speedwell onto the Mayflower, and the Mayflower would sail on alone.

[pause]

There was not room for everyone. About twenty of the passengers stayed behind, some because they were ill or had lost heart, and some because there was simply no space for them.

[pause]

The Speedwell was sent back to London, where she was repaired and sold, and she sailed for many years afterwards without any trouble at all.

[pause]

For several days, the people of Plymouth watched the little boats going back and forth across the harbour, carrying chests and barrels and bundles from one ship to the other.

[pause]

The travellers were made welcome by the townspeople, and some of them were given food and rest in their houses.

[pause]

It was now early September. Summer was almost over, and the best time for crossing the ocean had already passed.

[long-pause]

{img:mayflower-leaving-plymouth}
On the sixth of September, 1620, the Mayflower left Plymouth.

[pause]

The weather was fine, and a steady, favourable wind was blowing, exactly the wind they needed.

[pause]

The sailors raised the anchor with the capstan, walking round and round, and the great sails dropped from the yards and filled, one after another.

[pause]

Slowly the ship moved out past the rocky headlands of Plymouth Sound, and into the open sea.

[pause]

Behind her, the green hills of Devon grew smaller and bluer, until at last they disappeared.

[long-pause]

On board, there were one hundred and two passengers.

[pause]

About half of them came from the Leiden congregation, and half were the people gathered in London and Southampton by the merchants.

[pause]

The Leiden people sometimes called themselves the Saints, in the old sense of the word, meaning simply the members of their church. The others they called the Strangers.

[pause]

But they were all going to the same place, and they would have to learn to live together.

[long-pause]

{img:families-on-deck}
There were families with small children, and young married couples, and single men looking for work and adventure.

[pause]

There was William Brewster, the elder, with his wife Mary and their two youngest sons, who had remarkable names. One was called Love, and the other was called Wrestling.

[pause]

There was William Bradford, with his wife Dorothy.

[pause]

There was John Carver, a deacon of the Leiden church, a respected man who had helped to organise the voyage, and his wife Katherine.

[pause]

And there was Edward Winslow, who had worked with Brewster at the printing press, with his wife Elizabeth.

[pause]

Among the Strangers was a man named Stephen Hopkins, with his wife Elizabeth and their children.

[pause]

Hopkins had travelled to America once before. Some years earlier, he had sailed for Virginia on a ship that was wrecked on the island of Bermuda, and he had lived there for many months before going on to Jamestown.

[pause]

He was one of the very few people on board who had ever seen the New World.

[long-pause]

{img:myles-standish-portrait}
There was also a soldier named Myles Standish.

[pause]

He was a short man with reddish hair, and he had fought with the English troops in the Netherlands. He was not a member of the congregation, but he had become friendly with them in Leiden, and they had hired him to look after their defence in the new land.

[pause]

He came with his wife, Rose.

[pause]

And there was a young man from Southampton named John Alden.

[pause]

He was a cooper, a maker of barrels, and he had been hired at Southampton to look after the ship's barrels on the voyage. He was free to stay in America or return home afterwards, as he pleased.

[pause]

He was about twenty-one years old, tall and strong.

[long-pause]

Among the passengers, too, was a young woman of about eighteen named Priscilla Mullins, travelling with her father and mother and her younger brother.

[pause]

Her father William was a shoemaker, and he had brought with him a large supply of shoes and boots to sell in the new colony.

[pause]

Many years later, a poet would write a famous story about John Alden and Priscilla Mullins. And indeed, a few years after the voyage, the two of them would marry, and have ten children, and live long lives in the new land.

[long-pause]

{img:children-tween-deck}
There were more than thirty children and young people on board.

[pause]

There were small children who still needed to be carried, and older boys and girls who helped their parents and looked after the little ones.

[pause]

There was a boy called Resolved White, who was about five. His mother Susanna was expecting another baby.

[pause]

There was a thirteen-year-old girl named Mary Chilton, travelling with her parents.

[pause]

And there were several children who were travelling without their parents at all, sent across the ocean as servants in other families, as was common in those days.

[pause]

Some of the families also had servants, young men and women who worked for them in return for their passage and their keep, and who would be free after a number of years.

[long-pause]

{img:ship-dogs-on-deck}
There were animals on board too.

[pause]

We know of at least two dogs. One was a large mastiff, a heavy, gentle guard dog, and the other was a little spaniel.

[pause]

There were probably chickens in wooden coops, and perhaps some goats and pigs, to give eggs and milk, and to be kept for the new settlement.

[pause]

There were no cows or horses. Those would come later, on other ships.

[long-pause]

And in the hold, packed tightly together, were all the things that one hundred people would need to start a new life.

[pause]

There were barrels of food and drink, and sacks of seed for planting.

[pause]

There were tools for building and farming: axes and saws, spades and hoes, hammers and nails.

[pause]

There were muskets and gunpowder, and a few small cannon.

[pause]

There were chests of clothes and bedding, and pots and pans, and spinning wheels, and books, and Bibles.

[pause]

And there were trading goods, beads and knives and cloth, to exchange for furs with the people who already lived in America.

[pause]

It was a great deal to fit into one small wooden ship.

[long-pause]

{img:tween-deck-lantern}
The passengers did not live in cabins, as travellers on a ship might today.

[pause]

Most of them lived between the decks, in a long, low space under the main deck, which sailors called the tween deck.

[pause]

It was the space where, on a warship, the cannon would stand. On the Mayflower, it had usually been filled with cargo.

[pause]

Now it was filled with people.

[long-pause]

The tween deck was about eighty feet long and about twenty-four feet wide at its widest point.

[pause]

That is roughly the size of a modern tennis court, but a little narrower, and with a curved shape, wider in the middle and narrower towards the ends.

[pause]

And it was low. There were only about five feet between the floor and the beams overhead.

[pause]

Most of the grown men could not stand up straight. They had to move about bent over, or sit on their chests and barrels, or lie down.

[long-pause]

{img:board-cabins-families}
To give themselves a little privacy, some of the families built small cabins out of thin boards, a bit like garden sheds, along the sides of the ship.

[pause]

Inside these little rooms, a whole family might sleep, packed closely together on mattresses filled with straw or wool.

[pause]

Others simply hung up blankets or pieces of canvas as curtains, to make a small corner of their own.

[pause]

Some of the men slept in hammocks, which sailors had begun to use in those years, slung between the beams.

[pause]

And some slept wherever they could find a space on the floor, rolled up in a blanket, with their heads on a bundle of clothes.

[long-pause]

{img:shallop-in-pieces}
In the middle of the tween deck lay something large and strange.

[pause]

It was a boat.

[pause]

The settlers had brought with them a shallop, a sturdy open boat about thirty feet long, which could be sailed or rowed. They would need it to explore the coast when they arrived.

[pause]

It was far too large to carry on the main deck, so the carpenters had taken it apart, and its pieces had been stored below.

[pause]

During the voyage, people used its planks and ribs as places to sit and sleep, and children climbed over them.

[long-pause]

There was not much light down here.

[pause]

There were a few small openings in the sides of the ship, gun ports and hatches, but in bad weather these had to be closed, and then the only light came from a few lanterns, hanging from the beams and swinging gently with the movement of the ship.

[pause]

The lanterns had to be watched carefully. Fire was the great fear on any wooden ship.

[pause]

At night it was very dark, and people found their way to their beds by touch, and by the sound of their families breathing.

[long-pause]

{img:hatch-light-tween-deck}
The air in the tween deck was thick and close.

[pause]

There were a hundred people living together in a small space, with their clothes and bedding and food, and there was no way to wash properly.

[pause]

On fine days, the hatches were opened, and fresh sea air came down the ladders, along with a square of grey daylight.

[pause]

Then people could climb up onto the main deck, a few at a time, to stretch their legs and feel the wind on their faces.

[pause]

But the main deck was the sailors' working space, and they did not want passengers getting in their way. So most of the time, the travellers stayed below.

[long-pause]

There were no toilets as we would know them.

[pause]

The passengers used buckets, which were emptied over the side of the ship. The sailors used a place at the very front of the ship, in the bow, which hung out over the waves.

[pause]

It was not comfortable, and it was not very private, but it was simply the way that everyone travelled by sea in those days.

[long-pause]

{img:great-cabin-stern-window}
At the back of the ship, under the high stern, was the great cabin.

[pause]

This was where Master Jones lived, and perhaps some of his officers. It had a row of small windows looking out over the sea behind the ship, and it was the brightest and most comfortable room on board.

[pause]

A few of the more important passengers may have had small cabins nearby, under the poop deck at the stern.

[pause]

At the very top of the ship, high above the waves, was the steering place, where the helmsman stood.

[pause]

The Mayflower did not have a wheel. She was steered with a long wooden pole called a whipstaff, which went down through the deck and was attached to the great tiller.

[pause]

The helmsman pushed the whipstaff to one side or the other, and slowly, heavily, the ship turned.

[pause]

He could not see the sails very well from his position, so another sailor stood on the deck above him, watching the wind and the sails, and calling down to tell him which way to steer.

[long-pause]

{img:ship-biscuit-salt-beef}
Every day on the Mayflower followed the same quiet pattern.

[pause]

The food was simple, and it was much the same from one day to the next.

[pause]

The most important part of it was ship's biscuit, a hard, dry bread made of flour and water and baked again and again until it would keep for months.

[pause]

It was so hard that people often soaked it in their drink or their soup before they tried to eat it.

[pause]

Then there was salted meat, beef and pork, packed in barrels of brine before the voyage. And there was dried, salted fish, and dried peas and beans, and oatmeal.

[pause]

There was cheese, and butter, and a little smoked bacon. There were onions and turnips while they lasted, and dried fruits and spices for the families who could afford them.

[long-pause]

To drink, there was beer.

[pause]

That may sound strange today, but on a long voyage, water kept in wooden barrels soon went stale and green. Beer kept much better, and it was usually light and weak, and everyone drank it, even the children.

[pause]

The richer passengers had brought a little wine and some stronger spirits, which they kept for special occasions and for those who were unwell.

[long-pause]

{img:sandbox-cooking-fire}
Cooking was not easy on a wooden ship.

[pause]

The passengers had no proper kitchen. Instead, they cooked over small fires lit in iron boxes, filled with a thick layer of sand, so that the burning coals would not touch the wooden deck.

[pause]

In fine weather, they could cook a hot meal. They boiled the salt meat in iron pots, with peas or oatmeal, to make a thick pottage, a kind of stew.

[pause]

The steam rose from the pots and mixed with the sea air, and for a little while the ship smelled like a farmhouse kitchen.

[pause]

But in rough weather, no fires were allowed at all, and then the passengers ate their biscuit and cheese cold, and drank their beer, and waited for better days.

[long-pause]

{img:sailor-turning-sandglass}
The sailors divided the day into watches.

[pause]

A watch lasted four hours, and the crew was divided into two groups, which took turns working and resting, all through the day and the night.

[pause]

Time was kept with a sandglass, a glass shaped like an hourglass that ran out every half hour.

[pause]

A young sailor stood beside it, and each time the sand ran out, he turned it over, and struck the ship's bell.

[pause]

One stroke after the first half hour, two strokes after the second, and so on, up to eight strokes at the end of the watch.

[pause]

The sound of that bell, ringing out over the waves, day and night, became part of the rhythm of life on board.

[long-pause]

The sailors were always busy.

[pause]

They climbed the rigging to set the sails or take them in. They pulled on ropes and coiled them neatly on the deck. They scrubbed the planks, and mended the sails with big needles and thread, and checked the ropes for wear.

[pause]

And twice a day, at least, they worked the pumps, because every wooden ship lets in a little water, and it had to be pumped out of the bottom of the hold and back into the sea.

[pause]

As they worked, they sometimes sang, slow songs and fast ones, to keep their pulling together.

[long-pause]

{img:navigator-cross-staff}
Finding their way across the ocean was a skill of its own.

[pause]

The master's mate, a man named John Clarke, had been to America before, and he was one of the ship's pilots.

[pause]

At midday, when the sun was at its highest, he would stand on the deck with an instrument called a cross-staff, or a backstaff, and measure the height of the sun above the horizon.

[pause]

From that, using his tables, he could work out how far north or south the ship was.

[pause]

To find out how fast they were going, the sailors used a log line. They threw a piece of wood over the stern, tied to a long rope with knots in it at regular distances, and counted how many knots ran out while a small sandglass emptied.

[pause]

That is where the word knot comes from, which sailors still use for the speed of a ship.

[pause]

All of this was written down every day, and marked on a chart, so that the master knew, as well as anyone could, where they were on the great grey ocean.

[long-pause]

{img:psalm-singing-below}
For the passengers, the days were long.

[pause]

The women looked after the children and the food, and mended clothes, and nursed those who were seasick. The men looked after their belongings, and talked, and helped where they could.

[pause]

In the first weeks, many people were seasick. The ship rolled and pitched with the waves, and those who had never been to sea lay on their beds, pale and miserable, waiting for it to pass.

[pause]

Slowly, most of them got used to the motion.

[long-pause]

The members of the congregation prayed together every morning and every evening.

[pause]

On Sundays, they held a longer service, with readings from the Bible and a sermon from William Brewster.

[pause]

They sang psalms from a book they had brought from Holland, a psalm book translated by a scholar named Henry Ainsworth, with the music printed beside the words.

[pause]

The sound of their singing, slow and steady, rose up through the hatches, and mixed with the creak of the timbers and the rush of the sea.

[long-pause]

The children played whatever games they could, in the small space between the decks.

[pause]

They played with dolls made of cloth or wood, and with tops and marbles, and with games of string. They listened to stories, and learned their letters, and recited verses from the Bible.

[pause]

And when the weather was fine and they were allowed up on deck, they looked out at the wide blue sea, and watched the sailors high up in the rigging, and sometimes, far away, saw the dark backs of whales, or a group of dolphins racing alongside the ship.

[long-pause]

{img:sunset-over-atlantic-deck}
Let us spend one ordinary night aboard the Mayflower, somewhere in the middle of the ocean, on a calm evening in early October.

[pause]

The sun is going down behind the ship's bow, far ahead in the west, where they are going.

[pause]

The sky is pale gold, and then pink, and the long low waves catch the light and roll it gently past the hull.

[pause]

On the main deck, the sailors of the evening watch are coiling ropes and checking the sails. The cook is putting out the last of the cooking fire, pouring the hot coals over the side, where they hiss and vanish into the sea.

[long-pause]

Below, in the tween deck, the families are getting ready for the night.

[pause]

Mothers are tucking small children into their blankets. Fathers are hanging up wet coats on wooden pegs, and stowing away the pots and the bowls, so that nothing will roll about if the sea grows rough in the night.

[pause]

A few people are still sitting together on a chest, talking quietly, their faces lit by a single lantern.

[pause]

Someone is reading aloud from a Bible, in a low voice, to a small group gathered close around.

[pause]

Then the evening prayer is said, and a psalm is sung, slowly and softly, and one by one the families go to their corners and lie down.

[long-pause]

{img:lantern-swinging-sleepers}
The lantern is turned down low.

[pause]

In the half-dark, the ship is full of small sounds.

[pause]

There is the creak of the timbers, a long, slow groaning, as the hull bends a little with each wave.

[pause]

There is the soft knocking of a loose block somewhere up in the rigging, and the hum of the wind in the ropes.

[pause]

There is the wash of the water against the planks, just on the other side of the wall, so close that a sleeper could almost reach out and touch it.

[pause]

And there are the sounds of a hundred people sleeping. A baby stirs and is hushed. Someone coughs, and turns over. A child murmurs in a dream.

[long-pause]

The mastiff lies by the ladder, with its head on its paws, watching the dark with one eye half open.

[pause]

Up on the deck, the helmsman leans on the whipstaff, and the sailor beside him watches the sails, pale shapes against the stars.

[pause]

The master's mate walks slowly up and down, his hands behind his back, looking out at the sea and the sky, as sailors on watch have always done.

[long-pause]

{img:sandglass-bell-night-watch}
At midnight, the watch changes.

[pause]

The ship's boy turns the sandglass for the last time, and strikes the bell eight times, and the sound rings out across the empty water.

[pause]

The men of the next watch climb up the ladders from their berths in the forecastle, yawning and pulling on their coats.

[pause]

They take over the helm, and the lookout, and the pumps, and the men who have finished go below, and fall asleep almost at once in their hammocks, swaying gently with the ship.

[pause]

The sandglass is turned again. The new watch begins.

[long-pause]

{img:mayflower-moonlit-sea}
Out on the ocean, the moon has risen.

[pause]

It lays a long silver path across the water, and the Mayflower sails slowly along it, her sails grey in the moonlight, her hull dark.

[pause]

From a distance, she would look very small, a tiny wooden ship alone in the middle of a vast, quiet sea, with not another sail anywhere in sight, and no land for a thousand miles in any direction.

[pause]

But inside her, there is warmth, and breathing, and the steady sleep of tired people.

[long-pause]

Towards morning, the wind freshens a little.

[pause]

The sailors on watch climb up to adjust the sails, their bare feet sure on the ropes.

[pause]

Down below, the sleepers feel the ship lean a little more, and the water rush a little faster along her sides, and they pull their blankets closer, and sleep on.

[long-pause]

{img:dawn-behind-ship}
At last, behind the ship, in the east, where England lies far away, the sky begins to grow pale.

[pause]

First grey, then a soft yellow, then gold, as the sun lifts out of the sea.

[pause]

The bell rings for the morning watch. The cook lights the fire again in its box of sand. The hatches are opened, and cold fresh air comes tumbling down the ladders, along with the smell of salt and the cries of a passing seabird.

[pause]

In the tween deck, people begin to stir, and stretch, and sit up, and rub their eyes.

[pause]

The children are the first to climb up the ladder, to see what the morning looks like.

[pause]

It looks very much like yesterday. Blue water, and white foam, and the wide sky, and the old ship moving on towards the west.

[pause]

Another day at sea has begun.

[long-pause]

{img:mayflower-grey-autumn-sea}
For the first weeks, the weather was kind.

[pause]

The wind was fair, and the Mayflower made good progress westwards, carried along on the long, easy swell of the ocean.

[pause]

But they had left late in the year, and as September turned into October, the weather began to change.

[pause]

The days grew shorter and cooler. The sky turned grey, and stayed grey. The wind shifted round to the west, which was the direction they wanted to go, and began to blow against them.

[pause]

And then the autumn gales arrived.

[long-pause]

On the North Atlantic, the autumn storms come one after another, rolling in from the west.

[pause]

The wind grew stronger and louder, whistling and moaning through the rigging. The waves grew higher, and the ship began to climb up one side of them, and slide down the other.

[pause]

Spray flew across the deck, and sometimes whole waves broke over the bow, and ran in white streams along the planks.

[long-pause]

{img:lying-ahull-storm}
When the wind became too strong, the sailors took in all the sails.

[pause]

They climbed high into the swaying rigging, and gathered the heavy wet canvas in their arms, and tied it to the yards.

[pause]

Then the ship simply lay in the water with bare masts, and let the wind and the waves carry her where they would.

[pause]

Sailors called this lying a-hull. It was the safest thing to do in a great storm, but it meant that for days at a time, the ship went nowhere at all, or was even pushed backwards.

[pause]

Down below, the passengers could only wait.

[pause]

The hatches were closed, and the lanterns swung, and everything that was not tied down slid across the floor and back again.

[pause]

Water dripped through the deck above and soaked their bedding. It was cold, and dark, and very hard to sleep.

[pause]

Parents held their children close, and talked to them quietly, and prayed.

[long-pause]

{img:cracked-beam-iron-screw}
During one of these storms, something alarming happened.

[pause]

One of the great wooden beams in the middle of the ship, which held up the main deck, bent and cracked.

[pause]

The sailors and passengers gathered round to look at it, and everyone was worried. Some of the sailors wondered aloud whether the ship was strong enough to go on, or whether they should turn back to England.

[pause]

The master and his officers came together to talk it over.

[pause]

Then one of the passengers remembered something.

[pause]

Among the things they had brought from Holland was a great iron screw, a kind of large jack, perhaps meant for building houses, or perhaps part of a printing press.

[pause]

They brought it up from the hold, and set it under the cracked beam, and slowly turned it until the beam was pushed back up into its place.

[pause]

Then the carpenter fixed a strong wooden post beneath it, to hold it firm.

[pause]

Master Jones inspected the work, and declared that the ship was sound below the waterline, and that they would go on.

[long-pause]

{img:howland-rope-rescue}
It was during another storm that a young man named John Howland came up onto the deck.

[pause]

He was a servant of John Carver, about twenty-eight years old, and perhaps he had grown tired of the dark, crowded space below, and wanted a breath of air.

[pause]

As he stood there, the ship rolled heavily in a great wave, and he lost his footing, and was thrown over the side, into the sea.

[pause]

But as he fell, his hands caught hold of a long rope, a halyard from the topsail, which was trailing over the side and into the water.

[pause]

He held on tightly.

[pause]

He was pulled under the water, many feet deep, but he did not let go.

[pause]

The sailors saw him, and hauled on the rope, and pulled him up close to the side of the ship. Then one of them reached down with a long boat hook, and caught hold of him, and together they lifted him back on board.

[long-pause]

He was soaked and cold and shaken, and he was ill for a while afterwards. But he recovered.

[pause]

John Howland lived a long life in the new land. He married another passenger from the Mayflower, a girl named Elizabeth Tilley, and they had ten children.

[pause]

It is said that today, millions of people are descended from that young man who held on to the rope.

[long-pause]

{img:storm-passing-evening-light}
And so the weeks went by.

[pause]

Storm followed storm, with quieter days in between.

[pause]

On the quiet days, the sailors set the sails again, and the ship moved on westwards, slowly, sometimes making good progress and sometimes very little.

[pause]

On those days, the hatches were opened, and the wet bedding was carried up and hung in the wind to dry, and the cooking fires were lit again.

[pause]

People came up on deck, a few at a time, and stood looking at the sea, which was now calm and grey and endless.

[pause]

And the ship's bell went on ringing, every half hour, marking the time.

[long-pause]

{img:hopkins-baby-oceanus}
Somewhere in the middle of the ocean, in the dark space between the decks, a baby was born.

[pause]

His mother was Elizabeth Hopkins, the wife of Stephen Hopkins, the man who had been to Virginia before.

[pause]

The women of the ship gathered around her, as women always did at a birth, to help and to comfort her. And in the lantern light, a little boy came into the world.

[pause]

His parents named him Oceanus, after the sea on which he had been born.

[pause]

For a while, the whole ship must have been talking about him. In the middle of that long, hard voyage, a new life had begun.

[long-pause]

There was sadness on the voyage too.

[pause]

Not long before the end, a young servant named William Butten fell ill, and did not recover.

[pause]

He was the only passenger who did not live to see the end of the crossing, and the others said prayers for him, and he was quietly buried at sea.

[long-pause]

{img:night-sky-over-mayflower}
By now, they had been at sea for many weeks.

[pause]

October had become November. The air was cold, and the passengers wrapped themselves in every piece of clothing they had.

[pause]

Their food was running low, and what remained was not very fresh. The biscuit had grown hard and dusty, and the beer was nearly gone.

[pause]

Some people were becoming unwell, weak and tired from the long days below deck, with too little fresh food and too little light.

[pause]

Everyone was longing for the voyage to end.

[long-pause]

On clear nights, when the sky was free of clouds, the stars came out over the ocean.

[pause]

There were more of them than anyone on land had ever seen, thousands upon thousands, spread across the sky from one horizon to the other, with the pale band of the Milky Way running through the middle.

[pause]

The sailor on watch could look up and find the Pole Star, low in the north, and know that they were still on their course.

[pause]

And below him, the dark ship moved on through the dark water, with a single lantern glowing at the stern.

[long-pause]

{img:seabirds-driftwood-near-land}
Then, little by little, the signs began to change.

[pause]

The sailors noticed that the water was a different colour, greener than before.

[pause]

Seabirds began to appear, circling around the masts and calling. Pieces of seaweed and driftwood floated past.

[pause]

The sailors threw a weighted line over the side, to measure the depth of the water, and found that the bottom was getting closer.

[pause]

Land could not be very far away.

[pause]

Everyone on board grew restless. People came up on deck whenever they could, and stood at the rail, staring westwards into the grey distance.

[long-pause]

{img:first-sight-cape-cod}
At daybreak on the ninth of November, 1620, the lookout high on the mast saw a long, low line on the horizon.

[pause]

It was land.

[pause]

As the light grew, they could see it more clearly. It was a coast of sandy cliffs and dunes, with trees growing thickly behind them.

[pause]

The passengers crowded onto the deck to look at it. After more than nine weeks at sea, here at last was the New World.

[pause]

William Bradford later wrote that they were not a little joyful.

[pause]

They fell on their knees, and gave thanks for their safe arrival.

[long-pause]

The master and his mates studied the coast and their charts, and decided that this was the land known as Cape Cod.

[pause]

Cape Cod is a long, curved arm of sand that reaches out into the ocean from the coast of what is now Massachusetts, a little like a bent arm with a clenched fist at the end.

[pause]

It had been given its name some years earlier by an English explorer, because of the great numbers of cod fish he had found in the waters there.

[long-pause]

{img:mayflower-turning-shoals}
But Cape Cod was not where they had planned to go.

[pause]

Their permission was to settle further south, near the Hudson River, in the northern part of Virginia, as it was then called.

[pause]

So, after some discussion, the Mayflower turned south, and began to sail down along the coast.

[pause]

For half a day, all went well. Then they came to a place where the sea was full of dangerous shoals, banks of sand hidden just under the surface, with the waves breaking white over them, and strong currents running between.

[pause]

The ship came close to running aground. The wind was dropping, and the water was rough, and the sailors were uneasy.

[pause]

Master Jones decided that it was too dangerous to go on.

[pause]

The Mayflower turned around once more, and sailed back north, around the tip of Cape Cod.

[pause]

On the eleventh of November, she came gently into a wide, calm harbour on the inside of the curve, sheltered from the ocean by the long arm of land.

[pause]

There the anchor was let go, and the great ship, after all her long weeks of travel, lay still.

[long-pause]

{img:mayflower-anchored-provincetown}
The harbour where they anchored is now the harbour of Provincetown, at the very tip of Cape Cod.

[pause]

In November of 1620, there was no town there. There were only sand dunes, and low hills covered with oak and pine and juniper, and long, quiet beaches.

[pause]

The water in the harbour was deep and calm, and full of fish and birds. Whales swam close to the ship, and the sailors watched them rise and blow, and wished they had harpoons.

[long-pause]

Before anyone went on shore, there was something that had to be settled.

[pause]

During the last days of the voyage, some of the passengers had been saying that, since they were not going to land in the place where they had permission to settle, no one would have the right to give them orders.

[pause]

Once they were on shore, these people said, they would do as they pleased.

[pause]

The leaders of the group were worried. A hundred people, alone on the edge of a great wilderness, with winter coming, would need to work together and agree on how they were to be governed.

[long-pause]

{img:great-cabin-signing}
So they drew up an agreement.

[pause]

It was a short document, only a few sentences long, and no one knows for certain who wrote it. It may have been William Brewster, the elder. It was signed in the great cabin of the Mayflower.

[pause]

In it, the signers promised to join together into what they called a civil body politic, a kind of community with its own government.

[pause]

They promised to make just and equal laws, as they were needed, for the good of the colony, and to obey them.

[pause]

It was a simple idea, but a powerful one. The people of the colony would make their own rules together, and everyone would agree to follow them.

[long-pause]

On the eleventh of November, the day they arrived in the harbour, the men gathered in the great cabin to sign it.

[pause]

One by one, they came forward, and dipped the quill pen in the ink, and wrote their names.

[pause]

John Carver signed first, and then William Bradford, and Edward Winslow, and William Brewster.

[pause]

Then Myles Standish, and John Alden, and Stephen Hopkins, and many others.

[pause]

Even some of the servants signed. Forty-one men in all put their names to the paper.

[pause]

In those days, women did not sign documents like this, and their names are not on it, though they would live under its rules just the same.

[long-pause]

Much later, people would call this agreement the Mayflower Compact, and many would see in it one of the early seeds of the idea of government by the consent of the people.

[pause]

But on that grey November day, it was simply a practical way of keeping a small, tired community together.

[long-pause]

{img:john-carver-chosen}
When the signing was done, the men chose a governor for the first year.

[pause]

They chose John Carver, the deacon from Leiden.

[pause]

He was one of the older men of the company, calm and respected, and he had spent a great deal of his own money on the voyage.

[pause]

It was his task now to lead them through whatever lay ahead.

[long-pause]

{img:first-shore-party-boat}
That same day, a small group of armed men went ashore in the ship's longboat, to look around.

[pause]

There were sixteen of them, led by Myles Standish.

[pause]

They rowed across the calm water, and their boat scraped onto the sand, and they climbed out and stood on the beach.

[pause]

For the first time in more than two months, they felt solid ground beneath their feet.

[pause]

They walked along the shore and into the woods, and gathered armfuls of juniper wood, which smelled sweet when it was burned, and brought it back to the ship for the fires.

[pause]

They found no houses and saw no people. The land was very quiet.

[long-pause]

{img:ship-at-anchor-dusk-dunes}
That evening, the passengers sat between the decks, and listened to the men's descriptions of what they had seen.

[pause]

There was good sand, they said, and woods of oak and pine and birch and holly, and a few small ponds.

[pause]

Out on the water, the Mayflower swung gently at her anchor, with the dunes of Cape Cod pale in the dusk around her.

[pause]

The long voyage was over. But their journey had not quite ended yet.

[long-pause]

{img:women-washing-shore}
Two days later, on a Monday, the women went ashore.

[pause]

They had not been able to wash their clothes properly for more than two months, and now, at last, there was fresh water on land.

[pause]

The men rowed them across to the beach in the longboat, with great bundles of linen and shirts and children's clothes.

[pause]

They found a pond of fresh water behind the dunes, and lit fires, and heated water in big pots, and scrubbed and rinsed and wrung out the clothes, and spread them on the bushes to dry in the cold November air.

[pause]

The children ran up and down the beach, and played in the sand, and gathered shells, after so many weeks below deck.

[pause]

People say that this was the beginning of a tradition in New England, of Monday being washing day. Whether that is true or not, it is a pleasant thing to imagine.

[long-pause]

While the women washed, the men took the pieces of the shallop out of the ship and carried them ashore, so that the carpenter could put the boat together again.

[pause]

But the pieces had been badly knocked about during the voyage, and the work would take a long time, more than two weeks.

[pause]

The settlers could not wait that long. They needed to find a place to build their homes before the deep winter set in.

[long-pause]

{img:exploring-party-dunes}
So, on the fifteenth of November, sixteen men set off on foot to explore the land, with Myles Standish in command.

[pause]

They wore helmets and armour, and carried muskets and swords. William Bradford went with them, and Stephen Hopkins.

[pause]

They walked along the beach and over the dunes, through low woods and thickets, where the branches caught at their clothes and the sand gave way under their feet.

[pause]

In the distance, they saw a few people with a dog, coming towards them. These were Native people, members of the Nauset, who lived on this part of Cape Cod.

[pause]

When the Nauset saw the strangers, they turned and went quickly away into the woods. The explorers followed their tracks for a while, but did not catch up with them.

[pause]

That night, the men camped in the woods, and built a fire, and kept watch in turns until morning.

[long-pause]

The next day, they found springs of fresh water, and sat down and drank from them. Bradford later wrote that they drank their first New England water with as much delight as they had ever drunk anything in all their lives.

[pause]

They found old fields where corn had been grown, and a few graves, and the remains of a small house.

[pause]

And on a low hill near the shore, they found something unexpected.

[long-pause]

{img:corn-hill-baskets}
Under a mound of sand, there was a great basket, buried in the ground, and inside it was corn.

[pause]

It was Indian corn, or maize, in many colours, some yellow, some red, and some speckled blue.

[pause]

The settlers had never seen anything like it. They were amazed by it.

[pause]

They knew that it belonged to someone, and that the people who had stored it there would come back for it. But they also knew that they had very little food, and almost no seed to plant that was suited to this land.

[pause]

So they took some of the corn with them, and they called the place Corn Hill.

[pause]

They told one another that they would pay the owners for it, when they found them. And many months later, they did, through a meeting with the Nauset.

[long-pause]

Over the next weeks, there were more journeys of exploration.

[pause]

Once the shallop was finally ready, groups of men sailed along the inside of Cape Cod, looking for a good harbour, with fresh water and land for planting.

[pause]

The weather was turning very cold now. Snow fell, and the spray from the waves froze on their coats, until, as one of them wrote, their clothes were like coats of iron.

[pause]

Some of the men who went on these trips came back ill and exhausted.

[long-pause]

{img:peregrine-white-cradle}
Meanwhile, on board the Mayflower, another baby was born.

[pause]

Susanna White, the mother of the little boy Resolved, gave birth to a son in the harbour of Cape Cod.

[pause]

His parents named him Peregrine, a word that means a traveller, or a pilgrim.

[pause]

He was the first English child born in New England, and he would grow up to be an old man, living into his eighties.

[pause]

A wicker cradle that is said to have been his has been kept to this day, in a museum in Plymouth.

[long-pause]

{img:shallop-entering-plymouth-bay}
Early in December, a small party set out in the shallop on a longer journey.

[pause]

Robert Coppin, one of the ship's pilots, had been to this coast before, and he remembered a good harbour across the bay, on the mainland.

[pause]

The weather was bad. The wind rose, and it began to rain and snow, and the sea grew rough. The shallop's rudder broke, and then her mast, and the men had to steer with oars.

[pause]

In the dark, they found their way into a sheltered harbour, and landed on a small island, and spent a cold, wet night there around a fire.

[pause]

The next day was a Saturday, and it was fine and sunny. They dried their clothes, and cleaned their muskets, and rested.

[pause]

And on Sunday, they stayed on the island, and kept the Sabbath, and prayed.

[long-pause]

On Monday, they crossed over to the mainland and explored it.

[pause]

They found a good harbour, deep enough for ships. They found fields that had been cleared and planted in years gone by, but were now empty.

[pause]

They found several clear brooks of fresh running water, and a high hill from which they could see far out over the bay.

[pause]

It seemed to them a good place to live.

[pause]

They sailed back across the bay to the Mayflower, with the news.

[pause]

The place had already been given a name on an English map, made a few years before by Captain John Smith of Virginia.

[pause]

It was called Plymouth.

[long-pause]

{img:mayflower-plymouth-harbour-december}
In the middle of December, the Mayflower sailed across the bay, and dropped anchor in Plymouth harbour.

[pause]

The harbour was shallow near the shore, so the ship had to stay more than a mile out, and everything had to be carried to land in the shallop and the longboat.

[pause]

The settlers looked at the land around them, and talked about where exactly to build.

[pause]

In the end, they chose a place where a stream of fresh water ran down into the harbour, beside a steep hill.

[pause]

On the hill, they could build a platform for their cannon, to protect the settlement. On the slope below, near the brook, they could build their houses.

[pause]

Many years later, people would call the brook the Town Brook, and the hill Burial Hill.

[long-pause]

{img:felling-trees-common-house}
On the twenty-fifth of December, the men went ashore and began to build.

[pause]

It was Christmas Day, but the congregation did not celebrate Christmas. They believed that no special holy days were mentioned in the Bible, and so it was simply another working day.

[pause]

They cut down trees in the woods with their axes, and dragged the logs to the building place, and sawed them into beams and planks.

[pause]

Their first building was a common house, about twenty feet square, where they could store their tools and supplies, and where the men could sleep while they worked.

[pause]

It had walls of timber and clay, and a roof of thatch made from the long grasses that grew along the shore.

[long-pause]

Then they began to lay out a street.

[pause]

It ran up from the shore, towards the hill, with plots of land on either side for the houses.

[pause]

The single men were asked to join one of the families, so that fewer houses would be needed, and each family was given a plot, larger or smaller according to its size.

[pause]

Then each family began to build its own house, small and simple, with one room, a fireplace, and perhaps a loft above for sleeping.

[long-pause]

{img:ship-winter-snow}
It was hard work, in the cold and the wet and the short winter days.

[pause]

There were storms of wind and rain and sleet, and days when no one could go ashore at all.

[pause]

The building went slowly. And so, for most of that winter, the women and children, and many of the men, went on living on board the Mayflower, out in the harbour, in the same cramped space where they had lived all through the voyage.

[pause]

Master Jones and his crew stayed with them. The sea was too stormy, and the crew too unwell, for the ship to sail home before spring.

[long-pause]

That first winter was very hard.

[pause]

The settlers were tired after the long voyage. They had had too little fresh food for many months. They had been cold and wet for weeks on end, and the houses on shore were not yet ready to shelter them.

[pause]

And so, one by one, many of them fell ill.

[pause]

There were days when only a handful of people were well enough to work, and look after all the others.

[pause]

William Bradford remembered later how those few who were well did everything for the sick. They fetched wood, and made fires, and cooked their food, and washed their clothes, willingly and without complaint.

[pause]

Among those who never seemed to fall ill, and who cared for the others day and night, were William Brewster, the elder, and Myles Standish, the soldier.

[long-pause]

{img:quiet-hill-winter-dusk}
By the time spring came, about half of the people who had sailed on the Mayflower had died.

[pause]

Among them was Dorothy Bradford, William Bradford's young wife, who had been lost from the ship while it lay in the harbour at Cape Cod, before they ever reached Plymouth.

[pause]

Rose Standish, the wife of the soldier, was gone too. So were Priscilla Mullins's father and mother and brother.

[pause]

Many of the sailors had died as well.

[pause]

Those who were lost were buried quietly on the hill above the settlement.

[pause]

The survivors mourned them, and prayed for them, and went on with their work, because there was nothing else they could do.

[long-pause]

{img:first-houses-smoke}
Little by little, the days grew longer.

[pause]

The sun rose higher, and the snow melted from the hillside, and the ice in the brook broke up and ran down to the sea.

[pause]

More of the houses were finished, and more families moved from the ship to the shore. Smoke rose from their chimneys in the cold morning air.

[pause]

Birds returned to the woods, and the first green shoots appeared in the clearings.

[pause]

Those who had lived through the winter came out into the sunshine, pale and thin, and began to think about the planting season ahead.

[long-pause]

{img:samoset-walks-in}
On a fine morning in the middle of March, 1621, something happened that no one in the little settlement expected.

[pause]

A tall man walked out of the woods and straight down the street, towards the houses.

[pause]

He was a Native man, with long black hair, wearing very little in spite of the cold, and carrying a bow and two arrows.

[pause]

The settlers stopped what they were doing and stared at him, and some of them moved to stop him going into the common house.

[pause]

Then he spoke to them, in English.

[pause]

He said, "Welcome, Englishmen."

[long-pause]

His name was Samoset.

[pause]

He was not from this part of the coast. He was a sachem, a leader, from the land further to the north and east, in what is now the state of Maine.

[pause]

Along that coast, English fishermen had been coming for years to catch cod in the summer, and from them he had learned some of their language.

[pause]

He sat down with the settlers, and they gave him something to eat and drink, biscuit and butter and cheese, and a piece of duck, and some pudding.

[pause]

Then he talked with them for the rest of the day, telling them about the land and the people who lived in it.

[long-pause]

He told them that the place where they had built their houses had once been a village called Patuxet.

[pause]

A few years earlier, a great sickness had come to this coast, perhaps brought by European sailors, and it had spread from village to village.

[pause]

Here at Patuxet, nearly everyone had been lost. That was why the fields were empty, and why the settlers had found the land cleared but unplanted.

[pause]

He told them that the nearest great leader was a man called Massasoit, the sachem of the Wampanoag people, who lived about forty miles away to the south-west.

[pause]

Samoset stayed the night in the settlement, in the house of Stephen Hopkins, and the next morning he went away, promising to return.

[long-pause]

{img:tisquantum-teaching}
A few days later, he came back, and this time he brought another man with him.

[pause]

This man's name was Tisquantum, though the settlers usually called him Squanto.

[pause]

He spoke English better than Samoset, and he had a remarkable story.

[pause]

He had been born at Patuxet, in this very place. Some years before, he had been taken away on an English ship, and carried across the ocean to Spain.

[pause]

From there, after a time, he had found his way to England, and he had lived in London, and learned the language, and at last he had found a ship to bring him home.

[pause]

But when he came back to Patuxet, he found his village empty, and his people gone.

[long-pause]

{img:massasoit-meeting}
Tisquantum brought news that Massasoit himself had come, and was waiting on the hill nearby, with about sixty of his men.

[pause]

After some careful messages back and forth, Edward Winslow went up the hill to greet him, carrying gifts, a pair of knives, a copper chain, and some biscuit and butter.

[pause]

Then Massasoit came down to the settlement with twenty of his men, and Myles Standish met him at the brook with a small group of musketeers, and led him to a house where a green rug and cushions had been laid out on the floor.

[pause]

Governor Carver came in with a drummer and a trumpeter, and the two leaders greeted each other.

[pause]

Massasoit was a tall, strong man in the prime of his life, with his face painted a deep red, and a chain of white bone beads around his neck.

[long-pause]

Through Tisquantum, the two leaders talked, and made an agreement of peace.

[pause]

They promised that neither of their peoples would harm the other. If anyone did, he would be handed over to be punished. And if anyone made war on one of them, the other would come to help.

[pause]

It was an agreement that both sides needed, and it held for more than fifty years.

[pause]

When the meeting was over, Massasoit returned to his people. But Tisquantum stayed with the settlers, and lived with them in Plymouth.

[long-pause]

He became their teacher, and their guide.

[pause]

He showed them where to catch eels in the mud of the streams, treading them out with his feet and catching them with his hands.

[pause]

He showed them where the best fishing places were, and which plants in the woods could be eaten, and how to find their way through the country.

[pause]

William Bradford wrote that he was a special instrument, sent for their good, beyond what they could have expected.

[long-pause]

{img:mayflower-departing-april}
At the beginning of April, the Mayflower was finally ready to sail home.

[pause]

She had been in America for almost five months. Her crew had been badly hit by the winter, just like the settlers, and Master Jones had waited until enough of his men were well again to sail her.

[pause]

He offered to take back any of the settlers who wanted to go home to England.

[pause]

Not one of them accepted.

[pause]

On the fifth of April, 1621, the people of Plymouth stood on the shore and watched the Mayflower raise her anchor and her sails, and move slowly out of the harbour.

[pause]

They watched her until she was a small speck on the sea, and then she was gone.

[pause]

She had a good, fast voyage home, with the wind behind her, and arrived back in England in early May, after only about a month at sea.

[long-pause]

Not long after the ship had left, Governor John Carver came in from the fields on a warm day, complaining of a pain in his head. He lay down, and he did not recover.

[pause]

His wife Katherine followed him a few weeks later.

[pause]

The settlers chose a new governor, and the man they chose was William Bradford, the quiet boy from Austerfield who had grown up reading books by the fire.

[pause]

He was thirty-one years old. He would be governor of Plymouth, on and off, for most of the rest of his life.

[long-pause]

{img:planting-corn-herring}
In April, it was time to plant.

[pause]

Tisquantum showed the settlers how to grow corn in the way his people had always done.

[pause]

In the spring, great numbers of small fish called alewives, a kind of herring, swam up the Town Brook to lay their eggs, so many that the water seemed to boil with them.

[pause]

The settlers caught them by the basketful.

[pause]

Then, in the old fields of Patuxet, they made little mounds of earth in rows, and in each mound they placed a few fish, to feed the soil, and on top of the fish, a few seeds of corn.

[pause]

The settlers planted about twenty acres of corn in this way.

[pause]

They also planted the wheat and barley and peas they had brought from England. The barley grew well, but the peas did not, perhaps because they had been planted too late, or perhaps because the summer sun was too strong for them.

[long-pause]

{img:winslow-white-wedding}
In May, there was a wedding in Plymouth.

[pause]

Edward Winslow, who had lost his wife Elizabeth in the winter, married Susanna White, whose husband had also died, and who now had two little boys, Resolved and the baby Peregrine.

[pause]

It was the first marriage in the colony. It was a quiet ceremony, held by Governor Bradford, because the congregation believed that marriage was a civil matter, not a religious one, just as it was in Holland.

[pause]

Little by little, the people who were left were finding ways to go on, and to build new families together.

[long-pause]

{img:plymouth-street-summer}
Through the long summer, the little settlement grew.

[pause]

There were seven houses now along the street, and four buildings for common use, and more were being built.

[pause]

The houses were small and simple, with walls of split timber and clay, and roofs of thatch. Each had a garden at the back, where the families grew herbs and vegetables.

[pause]

Smoke rose from the chimneys. Children fetched water from the brook. Chickens scratched in the dust of the street, and the two dogs from the Mayflower lay in the shade.

[pause]

At the top of the hill, the settlers had built a platform for their cannon, from which they could see far out over the bay.

[long-pause]

During the summer, Edward Winslow and Stephen Hopkins walked through the woods with Tisquantum to visit Massasoit at his village, to strengthen the friendship between them.

[pause]

They brought him a red coat with lace, as a gift, and spent a couple of nights with him, sleeping in his house and sharing what little food there was.

[pause]

Over the summer, other groups of Native people came to Plymouth to trade, bringing furs and corn, and the settlers began to learn the ways of the land and its people.

[long-pause]

{img:harvest-feast-1621}
In the autumn, the corn ripened.

[pause]

The tall plants turned golden and dry, and the settlers went out into the fields and picked the cobs, and carried them home in baskets, and hung them up to dry.

[pause]

It had been a good harvest. After the long hard winter, they would have enough to eat.

[pause]

And so, they decided to celebrate.

[long-pause]

Edward Winslow described it in a letter he wrote to a friend in England that December.

[pause]

He wrote, "Our harvest being gotten in, our governor sent four men on fowling, that so we might after a special manner rejoice together, after we had gathered the fruit of our labours."

[pause]

The four men went out with their muskets, and came back with enough ducks and geese and other birds to feed the whole settlement for almost a week.

[pause]

Then Massasoit arrived, with about ninety of his men.

[pause]

The celebration lasted three days. The Wampanoag men went out into the woods and came back with five deer, which they gave as gifts to the governor and to Myles Standish and to others.

[pause]

They ate together, outdoors, at long tables and around fires. There was venison and wild birds, and probably fish and eels and shellfish, and corn bread, and squash.

[pause]

There were games and contests, and the settlers showed off their muskets, and marched in their armour.

[long-pause]

People today often call this the first Thanksgiving.

[pause]

To the settlers, it was simply a harvest celebration, of the kind that English villages had always held at the end of the summer.

[pause]

But it was a moment of rest and plenty, after a very hard year. And it was a moment when two very different peoples sat down together and shared a meal.

[long-pause]

{img:fortune-arrives-november}
In November, a year after the Mayflower had first sighted Cape Cod, a ship appeared at the entrance to Plymouth harbour.

[pause]

At first, the settlers were afraid that it might be an enemy, perhaps a French ship. Then they saw it was English.

[pause]

It was a small ship called the Fortune, sent by the merchants in London, and on board were thirty-five new settlers.

[pause]

Some of them were friends and family from Leiden. Among them was Jonathan Brewster, the eldest son of William Brewster, who had stayed behind in Holland.

[pause]

There were joyful meetings on the shore, and many tears.

[pause]

The new settlers had brought very little food with them, so the winter that followed was a hungry one. But the little colony of Plymouth was growing.

[long-pause]

{img:plymouth-house-interior}
Let us look inside one of the little houses of Plymouth, a year or two after the landing.

[pause]

It is small, perhaps twenty feet long and sixteen wide, with a floor of beaten earth, swept clean every morning.

[pause]

The walls are made of boards split from the trunks of oak trees, and the gaps between them are filled with clay, and the inside is whitewashed with lime, to make the room a little brighter.

[pause]

There are only one or two small windows, without glass. They are covered with oiled paper or linen, which lets in a dim yellow light, and closed at night with wooden shutters.

[pause]

At one end of the room there is a big fireplace, made of stones and clay, with a wooden chimney plastered thickly with clay inside, so that it will not catch fire.

[long-pause]

The fire is the heart of the house.

[pause]

It is never allowed to go out. At night, the embers are covered with ashes, and in the morning they are uncovered and blown back into flame.

[pause]

Over it hangs an iron pot on a hook, where a stew of corn and beans and a little meat bubbles slowly all day.

[pause]

Beside it are a few other cooking things, brought over from England on the Mayflower: a brass kettle, an iron frying pan, a gridiron, and a long-handled spoon.

[long-pause]

{img:corn-pounding-mortar}
There is not much furniture.

[pause]

There is a table, made of boards laid on trestles, which can be taken apart and leaned against the wall when it is not in use.

[pause]

There are a few benches and stools, and perhaps one chair, for the master of the house.

[pause]

There are the wooden chests that the family brought across the ocean, which hold their clothes and linen and their most precious things. The chests are also used as seats, and sometimes as beds.

[pause]

In the corner stands a bed with a straw mattress and woollen blankets, and under it a smaller bed, on low wheels, that can be pulled out at night for the children.

[pause]

Up under the roof there is a loft, reached by a ladder, where the older children and the servants sleep, close to the warm thatch.

[long-pause]

Every day there is a great deal of work to be done.

[pause]

Water has to be carried from the brook in buckets. Wood has to be cut and stacked for the fire. The animals have to be fed.

[pause]

The corn has to be pounded into meal in a big wooden mortar, made from a hollowed-out log, with a heavy wooden pestle. It is slow work, and the sound of it, thump, thump, thump, can be heard all along the street in the mornings.

[pause]

From the meal, the women make a kind of porridge, called samp, and flat cakes of corn bread, baked on the hearth.

[long-pause]

{img:garden-herbs-plymouth}
Behind each house there is a garden, with a fence of wooden stakes to keep out the animals.

[pause]

In it the families grow the plants they have brought from England, and others they have learned about here.

[pause]

There are onions and cabbages, carrots and turnips and parsnips. There are herbs for cooking and for medicine, parsley and thyme and sage and rosemary.

[pause]

And there are the new plants of this land, which the settlers have learned to grow from their Wampanoag neighbours, the beans that climb up the corn stalks, and the big yellow squashes that sprawl over the ground.

[long-pause]

The men work in the fields, and go fishing in the bay, and hunt in the woods for deer and turkeys and ducks.

[pause]

They dig clams on the beaches at low tide, and catch lobsters among the rocks, some of them so large that one is enough for four people.

[pause]

In the evenings they mend their tools, and make shingles and barrel staves to send back to England, to pay their debt to the merchants.

[long-pause]

{img:sunday-meeting-house}
On Sundays, all work stops.

[pause]

In the morning, a drum is beaten, and the people come out of their houses and gather at the door of Captain Standish's house.

[pause]

Then they walk together up the hill, in a quiet procession, three by three, with the men carrying their muskets, to the fort at the top, which is also their meeting house.

[pause]

There they sit on benches, the men on one side and the women on the other, and the elder, William Brewster, leads the service.

[pause]

It lasts for most of the morning, and then again in the afternoon. There are prayers, and readings, and long sermons, and slow psalms sung in the old way, one line read out and then sung by everyone together.

[pause]

Through the open door, they can see the bay, blue and calm, stretching away towards the long low line of Cape Cod on the horizon.

[long-pause]

{img:plymouth-village-evening}
In the evening, the families eat their supper together, by the light of the fire.

[pause]

Afterwards, the children are put to bed, and the grown-ups sit for a while by the hearth, talking quietly about the day, and about the news, and about friends and family far away in Leiden and in England.

[pause]

Outside, the little street is dark and quiet. Smoke drifts from the chimneys, and somewhere a dog barks once, and is silent.

[pause]

Down at the shore, the small waves are breaking softly on the sand.

[pause]

And above the hill, the same stars are shining that the travellers saw from the deck of the Mayflower, on their long voyage across the sea.

[long-pause]

{img:rotherhithe-river-evening}
And what became of the Mayflower herself?

[pause]

After her long journey, she returned to her home on the Thames at Rotherhithe, and went back to her old work as a merchant ship.

[pause]

She made a voyage to France the same year, perhaps carrying salt, or perhaps wine once more.

[pause]

But Master Christopher Jones was not a young man any more, and the hard winter in Plymouth harbour had taken its toll on him.

[pause]

He died in March of 1622, less than a year after his return, and he was buried in the churchyard of Saint Mary's in Rotherhithe, close to the river.

[pause]

His wife Josian lived on in Rotherhithe with their children.

[long-pause]

After her master's death, the Mayflower lay quietly at her moorings in the river.

[pause]

In 1624, when the owners' affairs were settled, men came aboard to value her, and they wrote down that she was in ruins.

[pause]

She was old, and her timbers were worn. Most likely she was taken apart, and her wood was sold, as happened to most old ships in those days.

[pause]

There is a story that some of her beams were used to build a barn in the village of Jordans, in the green hills of Buckinghamshire, not far from London.

[pause]

The barn still stands, and it is known as the Mayflower Barn. No one can say for certain whether the story is true, but people still come to look at its old dark timbers, and wonder.

[long-pause]

{img:bradford-writing-history}
In Plymouth, the colony slowly grew.

[pause]

More ships arrived from England, bringing more settlers, and cows and goats, and tools and cloth. More houses were built, and more fields were cleared.

[pause]

The settlers paid back the merchants in London, though it took them many years. They traded furs, and fished, and farmed.

[pause]

William Brewster lived on in Plymouth as the elder of the church until he died in 1644, at the age of about seventy-eight.

[pause]

John Alden and Priscilla Mullins married, and lived a long life together, first in Plymouth and later in the town of Duxbury, a little to the north.

[pause]

John Alden became the last of the men who had signed the Mayflower Compact. He died in 1687, at a great age.

[long-pause]

William Bradford served as governor of Plymouth for more than thirty years.

[pause]

He married again, and had more children, and his son John came over from Leiden to join him.

[pause]

And in the evenings, in his house in Plymouth, he began to write a history of everything that had happened, from the secret meetings in Scrooby to the life of the colony.

[pause]

He called it Of Plymouth Plantation. He wrote it by hand, carefully, in a large book, over many years.

[pause]

Much of what we know about the Mayflower and her passengers comes from his pages.

[long-pause]

His manuscript has had its own long voyage.

[pause]

It was kept for many years in a church in Boston, and then, during the American Revolution, it disappeared.

[pause]

Almost eighty years later, it was found again, in the library of the Bishop of London, at Fulham Palace beside the Thames. No one knows quite how it got there.

[pause]

In 1897, it was returned to Massachusetts, and today it is kept in the State Library in Boston.

[long-pause]

{img:plymouth-rock-portico}
In the town of Plymouth today, beside the harbour, there is a large grey boulder, sheltered under a small stone building with columns, like a little temple.

[pause]

It is called Plymouth Rock, and it is said to be the place where the passengers first stepped ashore.

[pause]

The story began in 1741, more than a hundred and twenty years after the landing, when an old man named Thomas Faunce, who was ninety-four years old, asked to be carried down to the shore.

[pause]

He pointed to the rock, and said that his father, who had come to Plymouth only a few years after the Mayflower, had told him that this was the place.

[pause]

No one knows for certain if it is true. But the rock has become a symbol, and visitors come from all over the world to look at it.

[long-pause]

{img:mayflower-two-replica}
Not far from the rock, a ship lies at the quay.

[pause]

She is called Mayflower the Second, and she is a full-size copy of the original ship, built in the 1950s in the little port of Brixham, in Devon.

[pause]

In 1957, she sailed across the Atlantic, from Plymouth in England to Plymouth in Massachusetts, with a crew of volunteers, in fifty-four days.

[pause]

Today, visitors can climb aboard her, and stand on her decks, and go down into the low tween deck, and imagine what it was like to live there for more than two months with a hundred other people.

[pause]

Nearby, there is a living history museum, where the little village of 1627 has been rebuilt, with its thatched houses along the street, and its gardens and fences.

[pause]

And beside it, there is a homesite of the Wampanoag people, made by their descendants, who still live in this part of New England, and who tell their own story of those years.

[pause]

In Scrooby, the old manor house has mostly gone, but a farmhouse stands on its site, beside the quiet river.

[pause]

In Leiden, the great Pieterskerk still rises over the narrow streets, and a plaque on its wall remembers John Robinson, who stayed behind and never made the voyage. He died in Leiden in 1625, and he is buried there.

[pause]

And in Plymouth in England, on the old harbour front, there is a set of stone steps leading down to the water, called the Mayflower Steps, near the place where the travellers went aboard in September 1620.

It is said that today, around thirty million people around the world are descended from the passengers of the Mayflower.

[pause]

But perhaps the story is best remembered, not as a story of great events, but as a story of ordinary people.

[pause]

Farmers and weavers and printers, sailors and servants and children, who packed their chests, and climbed aboard an old wine ship, and set out across a great ocean, late in the year, towards a land they had never seen.

[long-pause]

Picture her one last time, somewhere in the middle of the Atlantic, on a quiet evening in the autumn of 1620.

[pause]

The storm has passed. The sea is long and grey and gentle, and the wind is soft in the sails.

[pause]

A lantern is glowing at the stern. Below the deck, in the warm half-dark, the families are lying close together, and the children are already asleep.

[pause]

Somewhere a sailor turns the sandglass, and the bell rings softly over the water.

And the old ship sails on, slowly, steadily, towards the west.
