{img:01-gettysburg-town-spring-hills}

Meet Tillie Pierce. She is fifteen, with dark hair pulled neatly back and a round, serious face. Her father is the butcher on Baltimore Street, in a small Pennsylvania town among apple orchards and wheat fields. On Saturdays she wraps meat in paper behind the counter. On summer evenings she sits on the front step with her sister, counting fireflies. She talks a little too much at school, and she looks out of the window a little too often. And she notices everything.

[pause]

Her town is called Gettysburg. In the summer of 1863, two great armies will come down its ten country roads, and its name will be known all over the world. Years later, as a grey-haired woman, Tillie will write down everything she saw. Thanks to her, and to a few neighbours like her, we can still walk those streets with them. So what happened to the families of Gettysburg, that summer and in the quiet autumn after it? Let's walk beside Tillie and find out, slowly and gently.

[pause]

First, a little note from me. These calm stories come out a few times every week. Should you like to hear the next one, you can subscribe, whenever it suits you. And if not, that's perfectly fine too.

[pause]

I'd also enjoy knowing where these words are finding you. Are you under a quilt in the countryside, or high up in a city flat? And how late is it there? You can tell me in the comments, in just a few words.

[pause]

Now let the day slip off you, like a coat at the end of a long walk. Rest your head. Let your breath grow long and slow, like a summer evening on a quiet street, when the swallows fly low and the lamps aren't yet lit.

[long-pause]

In 1863, Gettysburg had only about two thousand four hundred people, fewer than a big school today.

[pause]

It's the spring of 1863, in the south of Pennsylvania. Picture yourself walking into Pennsylvania on a soft spring morning, with the apple blossom all around you. The apple trees on the hillsides are in blossom, pink and white. The fields along the roads are green with young wheat and corn. Farmhouses stand among their barns and springhouses, with long lines of wooden fences between them. Cows graze in the meadows beside the creeks. A farmer's wagon creaks slowly along the road, loaded with sacks of grain for the mill. And in the middle of all this green, there's a small country town. It's called Gettysburg. About two thousand four hundred people live here. That's fewer than in many big schools today. The houses are neat, of brick and wood, along a few straight streets. A church spire or two rises above the rooftops. The town was laid out about eighty years earlier by a man named James Gettys. Would you have guessed, walking in, that one day the whole world would know this name? He drew the streets and sold the plots, and the town still carries his name.

[long-pause]

{img:02-ten-roads-countryside-aerial}

Ten roads met in Gettysburg, like the spokes of a wagon wheel meeting at the hub.

[pause]

What makes Gettysburg a little unusual is its roads. If you looked at a map, you'd see them meet like the spokes of a wheel. No fewer than ten of them come together here, from every direction. On a map, they look like the spokes of a wheel meeting at the hub. There's the Chambersburg Pike, coming in from the mountains to the west. There's the road to Baltimore, running south. There are roads to Harrisburg and York and Hanover, to the north and east. And there are roads to Emmitsburg and Taneytown, just a few miles away in Maryland. Farmers bring their produce into town along these roads. Merchants send their goods out along them. Stagecoaches and wagons pass through every day, raising a little dust in dry weather. Since 1858, there has also been a small railway, coming in from Hanover in the east. For a town of this size, Gettysburg is very well connected. Remember those roads. Keep them in mind, if you can. You'll see why later. A little later, they will matter a great deal.

[long-pause]

{img:03-seminary-ridge-cupola-view}

From the cupola on the seminary roof, you could see for miles, all the way to the blue mountains.

[pause]

Around the town, the land rolls gently up and down in long, low ridges. Let's climb up and look. You go up the narrow wooden stairs, and the steps creak under your feet. They are covered with fields and woods and orchards. To the west, there's a ridge with a Lutheran seminary on top. It's a fine brick building with a little white cupola on the roof. From up there, a person can see for miles across the countryside. To the south, just outside the town, there's a hill with a cemetery on it. From there, a long and gentle ridge runs south for about two miles. At its end stand two rocky, wooded hills. The local people call them the Round Tops. Further off, to the west and north, the blue line of the South Mountains rises along the horizon. If you stood in the cupola on a clear evening, you'd see it all at once. The town below. The ridges and the farms. Stand here a moment. And the mountains, turning purple as the sun went down behind them.

[long-pause]

{img:04-german-family-farmhouse-porch-evening}

Some older people in Gettysburg still spoke German at home, two or three generations after their families arrived.

[pause]

The people of Gettysburg are mostly farmers and craftsmen. Now, who are your neighbours here? You'll soon find out. There are shopkeepers and teachers, ministers and lawyers too. Many of their families came here from Germany or Scotland or Ireland, two or three generations ago. Many still have German names, like Weikert, Trostle and Shriver. Some of the older people still speak German at home, by the stove in the evening. The local German is a mix of old country words and English ones. Visitors from the cities sometimes smile at it. These are hardworking, churchgoing people. They know their neighbours, and they know their neighbours' business too. Nothing much stays secret in a town of this size. If a new bonnet appears in church on Sunday, everyone has talked about it by Monday. And on Sunday mornings, the church bells ring out across the rooftops, one after another, calling them to worship. I find that rather comforting, don't you? It's a peaceful, ordinary, pleasant place, much like hundreds of other small towns across the country.

[long-pause]

{img:05-diamond-square-courthouse-morning}

The square in the middle of Gettysburg wasn't round or square at all. Everyone called it the Diamond.

[pause]

In the middle of the town, there's an open square. You might think a town square would be square. Not here. Everyone calls it the Diamond. In the mornings, it's the busiest place in Gettysburg. Farmers' wagons stand around it, piled high with butter and eggs, potatoes and cabbages, and chickens in wooden crates. The shopkeepers open their doors and set their goods out on the pavement. The bank opens its heavy door. Men stand on the corners in their dark coats and hats. They read the newspapers and talk about the weather, and the crops. Gettysburg is the county seat of Adams County. So it has a courthouse, a jail, a bank, a few hotels and taverns, a newspaper office and a good many shops. On court days, lawyers hurry across the square with papers under their arms. Farmers come in to pay their taxes, or to settle an argument about a fence. And the talk on the corners always comes back, sooner or later, to one subject. The war.

[long-pause]

{img:06-newspaper-reading-hotel-lobby-lamp}

For two years, most people in Gettysburg knew the war mainly from newspapers and letters.

[pause]

For two years now, the United States has been divided. You'd read the news over breakfast, and then go on with your day. In the spring of 1861, eleven states in the South left the Union. They formed a new country of their own and called it the Confederate States of America. The government in Washington, under President Abraham Lincoln, refused to let them go. And so the war began. Since then, there have been great battles in Virginia and Tennessee, in Maryland and Mississippi. Hundreds of thousands of young men have gone off to fight, on both sides. In Gettysburg, the men follow it all in the newspapers. They read them aloud in the hotel lobbies and the stores. They argue about the generals and the politicians. Some of them pin maps to the walls and move little marks around as the armies move. Yet for most of the time, the war happens far away. It's something you read about in the evening, by the lamp, before you go to bed.

[long-pause]

{img:07-recruitment-young-men-train-station}

When the young men of Gettysburg left for the army, a brass band played them off at the station.

[pause]

Many young men from Gettysburg have gone to the war. You'd stand on the platform with your family, waving your handkerchief as the train pulled away. They marched off in their new blue uniforms, with flags flying and a brass band playing. Their mothers and sisters and sweethearts waved from the station platform until the train was out of sight. Some of them have already come home, wounded or ill. Some haven't come home at all. In a small town, everyone knows which families have an empty chair at the table. The ones who stay behind do what they can. They pray for their sons and brothers on Sundays. They knit socks and roll bandages for the soldiers. And then they go back to their own work, their own fields, their own lives. Gettysburg is only about ten miles from the Maryland border. Once or twice, there have been rumours that the Southern army might come north. Each time, people hid their horses and their valuables, and waited. And each time, nothing happened at all.

[long-pause]

{img:08-brick-townhouse-street-green-shutters}

The rumours have died down again, and the town has gone back to its work. Let's open a front door on one of those brick houses together. Shall we see how you'd have spent an ordinary day here?

[pause]

Houses in Gettysburg were built right up against the pavement, with long gardens hidden behind them.

[pause]

A house in Gettysburg in 1863 is usually built right up against the pavement. You'd step straight from the pavement into the front room. The front door opens straight onto the street. Behind it, a long, narrow garden stretches away out of sight. Many of the houses are of red brick, two storeys high, with green shutters and a small attic under the roof. Others are of wood, painted white, or of grey stone. At the back, there's a yard with a well or a pump. There's a woodshed and a little stable for a horse and a cow. There may be a pigsty and a chicken house too. And there's always a vegetable garden, with beans and cabbages, onions and potatoes in neat rows. So even in the middle of town, a family can feel half like a farm family. In the evening, you can hear cows lowing and hens settling down, behind the brick houses of the main streets. If you peeked through the gate, you'd see rows of beans and a pear tree or two. The town and the countryside are never very far apart here.

[long-pause]

{img:09-mother-cookstove-breakfast-children}

A Gettysburg breakfast could include ham, sausage, fried potatoes, eggs and apple butter, all at once.

[pause]

The day begins early. You'd smell it all the way out in the street. The mother of the house is up before dawn. She lights the fire in the iron cookstove in the kitchen. She fetches water from the pump in the yard and puts the kettle on. Then she starts the breakfast. There's fried ham or sausage, and fried potatoes. There are eggs, bread and butter, and apple butter, thick and dark and sweet. And there's strong coffee. The children are woken and washed and dressed. The older girls help with the cooking and with the little ones. The boys go out to feed the chickens, milk the cow and bring in wood for the stove. Then the whole family sits down together around the kitchen table. The father says grace, and they eat. It's a big breakfast, because the work of the day is hard. You'd hardly need lunch after that, would you? Most of these people will be on their feet until the sun goes down, and then a little longer.

[long-pause]

{img:10-carriage-workshop-painter-gold-lines}

Gettysburg was famous for its carriages, and painters added thin gold lines by hand.

[pause]

After breakfast, the father goes off to work. You'd watch a painter's hand, perfectly steady, drawing a thin gold line. Many men in Gettysburg work at a trade. There are blacksmiths and harness makers, tanners and shoemakers, carpenters and masons. There are tailors and hatters, bakers and butchers. But the town is best known for its carriages. In the carriage shops on Chambersburg Street and York Street, the hammers and saws go on all day. The wheelwrights shape spokes and rims from seasoned hickory. The smiths fit iron tyres onto the wheels while they are still hot. And the painters work in a separate room, where there's no dust. There, they brush on layer after layer of glossy black paint. Then they take a very fine brush and pick out thin lines of gold or red along the sides. It takes a steady hand and a great deal of patience. You'd want to run your fingers along those lines, but you wouldn't dare. Gettysburg buggies are sold far away, even in the South, and people say they are among the finest anywhere.

[long-pause]

{img:11-washday-iron-kettle-backyard-line}

In 1863, Monday was washing day, and doing the laundry took the whole day.

[pause]

The women's work is mostly in and around the house, and there's a great deal of it. You'd wake to the smell of soap and steam rising from the yard. Each day of the week has its own job. Monday is washing day. A great iron kettle of water boils over a fire in the yard. The clothes are scrubbed on a washboard, rinsed, wrung out by hand and hung on a line to dry. It's long, heavy work, and it takes the whole day. Tuesday is ironing day. The heavy irons are heated on the stove, and used one after another while the others warm up again. Then there's baking and mending, cleaning and churning butter, and work in the garden. In summer, there's fruit to be preserved for the winter. Cherries and peaches go into jars. Apples are sliced and dried. Cabbages are shredded and packed into big stone crocks in the cellar, to turn slowly into sauerkraut. Could you have done that every week? By autumn, the cellar shelves are full.

[long-pause]

{img:12-general-store-counter-1863}

The general store sold patent medicines that promised to cure almost every illness known.

[pause]

Every few days, the women walk to the stores on the Diamond or along Baltimore Street. You'd find shelves full of little bottles with grand promises on their labels. The general store sells almost everything. There's coffee and tea, sugar and salt, molasses and spices. There are bolts of cloth, thread and buttons, needles and pins. There are pots and pans, lamp oil, candles, soap and nails. And on a high shelf stand rows of patent medicines, in little glass bottles. Their labels promise to cure nearly every illness known to humanity, from headaches to weak nerves. Some of them are mostly sugar and alcohol. The storekeeper weighs out the sugar and coffee on brass scales. He writes the price in a big ledger, because many families pay only once a month, or after the harvest. Since the war began, some things have become scarce and expensive, especially coffee and cotton cloth. Some people mix roasted grain into their coffee to make it last. You'd probably buy a bottle, just in case. But in a farming town like this one, there's always plenty to eat.

[long-pause]

{img:13-church-sunday-families-1863}

A town of only 2,400 people had churches for Lutherans, Presbyterians, Methodists, Catholics and more.

[pause]

On Saturday evenings, people take baths. On a Sunday morning, you'd hear bells ringing from every direction, one after another. They take turns in a tin tub in front of the kitchen stove, with water heated on the fire. The youngest go first, while the water is still clean and warm. On Sunday, everyone dresses in their best clothes and walks to church. For a small town, Gettysburg has a great many churches. There are Lutherans and Presbyterians, Methodists and Catholics, German Reformed and Episcopalians. There's also a church for the town's Black families, who have lived in Gettysburg for generations. After the service, families stand outside in the sunshine and talk with their neighbours. Then they walk home to a big Sunday dinner. There's roast chicken and potatoes, and pie for afterwards. The afternoon is for resting. Some people visit relatives. Some read the Bible aloud. Some take a slow walk out along one of the country roads, between the fields. Nobody works on a Sunday, if they can possibly help it.

[long-pause]

{img:14-children-swimming-creek-summer-trees}

In summer, the boys of Gettysburg went swimming in Rock Creek, just east of town.

[pause]

Children in Gettysburg go to school, at least for a few years. You'd hear their splashing and shouting from across the fields. There are public schools for the younger children. For families who can afford it, there are private academies too. There's a seminary for young ladies, where girls learn grammar and French. And on the hill north of town there's a college, where young men study Latin and Greek. When school is out, the children play in the streets and the fields. The boys play ball and marbles. They go fishing in the creeks and climb the trees in the orchards. The girls play with dolls and skip rope, and pick wildflowers along the lanes. In winter, there's sledding down the snowy hills. In summer, there's swimming in Rock Creek, on the east side of town. The water is cool and brown under the trees. Imagine the shouting and splashing on a hot afternoon, while the dragonflies dart over the water and the cows watch from the bank.

[long-pause]

{img:15-market-day-wagons-sunbonnets-diamond}

On market day, a boy might sell you cherries by the quart, straight from the farm.

[pause]

Market days bring the whole county into town. You'd hand over a few coins and carry your cherries home in a paper twist. Early in the morning, the farm wagons come rolling in along the ten roads, one after another. Soon the Diamond is crowded with them. The farmers' wives sit on the wagon seats in their sunbonnets. At their feet are baskets of eggs, crocks of butter and bunches of herbs. The townswomen walk slowly from wagon to wagon, choosing and bargaining. There are fresh strawberries in June, and sweet corn and tomatoes in August. In autumn, there are apples and cider and pumpkins. There are live chickens in crates, sides of smoked ham and great wheels of cheese. A farmer sells honey in the comb from a wooden tub. A boy sells cherries by the quart. By midday, the wagons are empty. The farmers go off to the taverns for their dinner, and to talk about the weather and the price of wheat. Wouldn't you love a bowl of those right now? Then comes the long drive home, in the afternoon sun.

[long-pause]

{img:16-front-step-evening-neighbours}

On summer evenings, half the town seemed to sit outside on its front steps.

[pause]

In summer, the whole town smells of the countryside. You'd pull out a chair and nod to your neighbours as they passed. There's the sweet smell of cut hay from the meadows at the edge of town. There's the smell of baking bread from the kitchens. And there's the sharp smell of the tanneries down by the creek, which nobody likes very much. In the evenings, the families sit together in the parlour. The father reads the newspaper aloud by the light of an oil lamp. The mother knits or sews. Sometimes someone plays the parlour organ, and everyone sings. But on warm nights, people go outside. They sit on the steps in front of their houses and call out to their neighbours across the street. They watch the swallows over the rooftops. They watch the fireflies rising in the gardens. And they watch the long golden light fade slowly behind the ridge to the west. You'd hear your name called from three porches at once. Then the lamps go out one by one, and the town goes to sleep.

[long-pause]

{img:17-tillie-pierce-girl-1863}

In one of those houses, a girl is still sitting at her window, watching the last fireflies. Come and sit with her for a while, and you'll soon see why she matters.

[pause]

Much of what we know about Gettysburg in 1863 comes from a fifteen-year-old girl.

[pause]

On Baltimore Street, in the middle of the town, there lives a girl named Tillie Pierce. You've already met her, at the very beginning of our story. Her full name is Matilda, but nobody ever calls her that. She is fifteen years old in the spring of 1863. She has dark hair, parted in the middle and pulled back neatly behind her ears. She has a round, serious face and bright, watchful eyes. She is curious about everything, and she notices a great deal. Many years later, she will write down what she saw that summer, in a little book. Its long title is At Gettysburg, or What a Girl Saw and Heard of the Battle. It's because of Tillie that we know so much about this summer. Through her eyes, we can see the soldiers and the farm kitchens and the people of the town. Historians still read her book today. So when you picture Gettysburg in 1863, you might picture it from her window, as she looked out at the street below.

[long-pause]

{img:18-butcher-shop-family-brick-house}

Tillie's father was the town butcher, and on Saturdays she helped him in the shop.

[pause]

Tillie's father, James Pierce, is a butcher. You'd hear the chop of the cleaver from the back of the shop. The family lives in a brick house on the corner of Baltimore Street and Breckenridge Street. It's at the southern end of town, on the road that leads out toward the cemetery on the hill. The shop is at the front of the house. It's a cool room, with a scrubbed wooden counter, a great wooden chopping block and hooks along the ceiling. On Saturdays, Tillie helps her father there. He cuts and weighs the meat in his long white apron. Tillie wraps it in paper, takes the money and counts out the change carefully. The customers are all people she knows. The minister's wife always wants the best beef for Sunday. The old widow from across the street buys only a little. Tillie's father always gives her a little extra, without saying a word about it. I've always liked her for that. And the boys from the college come in for sausages for their suppers.

[long-pause]

{img:19-young-ladies-seminary-classroom-desks}

Tillie liked her lessons, but she also liked to talk with her friends and look out of the window.

[pause]

On weekdays, Tillie goes to school at a seminary for young ladies. You might recognise a little of yourself in her. It's run by a Mrs. Eyster, a few streets away. The girls study reading and grammar, history and geography. They learn arithmetic and French, and a little music and drawing. They sit in neat rows at wooden desks, in their long dresses, with their hair braided. When the teacher calls on them, they stand and recite their lessons. Tillie is a good student. But perhaps she isn't always a perfectly quiet one. She likes to talk with her friends. And she likes to look out of the window, at the wagons and riders going past. After school, there's work at home. Tillie helps her mother in the kitchen, and with the washing and mending. She feeds the chickens and collects the eggs. She runs errands to the Diamond with a basket on her arm. And of course she stops to talk with the neighbours on the way.

[long-pause]

{img:20-baltimore-street-1863-houses}

From her window on Baltimore Street, Tillie could watch the road that ran all the way to the city of Baltimore.

[pause]

Baltimore Street is one of the main streets of Gettysburg. Sit beside her for a moment, and you'll see wagons rolling slowly past. It runs south from the Diamond, past rows of brick houses and shops. Then it climbs out of town toward the cemetery on the hill. Beyond the cemetery, it becomes the Baltimore Pike. That road leads all the way to the city of Baltimore, about fifty miles away. Every day, wagons and carriages and riders pass along it. Farmers come in from the country. Travellers go out. A peddler walks by with pots and pans tied to his pack, clanking softly. From the windows of the Pierce house, Tillie can watch them all. Tillie's mother, Margaret, is a kind and capable woman who keeps the household running smoothly. Her father is a quiet, steady man, respected in the town. Tillie has an older sister, Maggie, and two older brothers. One of them, James, has gone to the army. The other, William, is still at home, helping their father with the animals.

[long-pause]

{img:21-shriver-house-mother-daughters}

A neighbour of Tillie's had a bowling alley and a saloon built in the basement of his new house.

[pause]

A little way up the street lives the Shriver family. You'd hear the rumble of the balls under the floorboards. Mrs. Henrietta Shriver is a young mother with two little daughters. Everyone calls her Hettie. Her husband, George, built a fine new brick house on Baltimore Street. In the basement, he put a saloon and a bowling alley. He hoped they'd bring in a good income. But George has gone off to join the army. So Hettie and the girls are living in the new house alone. Hettie's parents, the Weikerts, have a farm a few miles south of town. It lies out on the Taneytown Road, at the foot of the hill called Little Round Top. Hettie often goes out to visit them, and sometimes Tillie goes with her. It's a pleasant walk on a summer day, past the cemetery and along the ridge. The Weikert farmhouse has a well with cold, clear water. Tillie always remembers how good it tastes after the dusty road.

[long-pause]

{img:22-young-seamstress-sewing-by-window}

The most famous young woman of Gettysburg was probably not really called Jennie at all.

[pause]

Further up Baltimore Street, near the edge of town, there's a small brick house. You've probably heard her name, but you may not know her real one. A young woman named Jennie Wade sometimes stays there with her married sister. Her real first name is Mary Virginia, and at home people call her Ginnie. The name Jennie will come later, most likely from a newspaper that got it wrong. And that's the name that stays. She is twenty years old. She lives with her mother and earns money as a seamstress, sewing clothes for other families. She sits by the window for the light, with her needle going in and out, hour after hour. She is engaged to a young man named Jack Skelly. They grew up together in this town. Jack is away in the army now. Jennie is waiting for him to come home, so that they can be married. Isn't it funny how a name can change in the telling? Like many young women in Gettysburg, she reads his letters again and again, until the paper is soft.

[long-pause]

{img:23-cemetery-hill-path-girls-sunday-view}

On Sunday afternoons, the young people of Gettysburg liked to walk in the cemetery for the view.

[pause]

On Sunday afternoons, if the weather is fine, Tillie and her friends walk out along the Baltimore Pike to the cemetery on the hill. You might think a cemetery is a gloomy place for a walk. But not in those days. That may sound strange to us. But in the 1860s, many towns had cemeteries laid out like parks, with trees and winding paths. People went there to walk and to enjoy the view. The girls stroll along the shady paths, reading the names on the old stones. At the top of the hill, they stop to look out. From there, they can see the whole of Gettysburg, with its church spires and rooftops. They can see the long ridges stretching away to the south and the west. In the afternoon light, everything is green and gold. The keeper's wife is often working in the garden by the gatehouse. She waves to them as they pass. Then the girls walk home slowly, arm in arm, laughing about something one of the boys said in church.

[long-pause]

{img:24-girl-window-candle-night}

The war seemed very far away on a summer evening, with honeysuckle drifting over the garden walls.

[pause]

On summer evenings, Baltimore Street is full of life. You'd breathe in the sweet smell of honeysuckle as the light faded. The children play in the street until it's too dark to see. The older people sit out on their front steps, fanning themselves and talking. Girls walk arm in arm along the pavement. A young man plays a fiddle on a doorstep. Somewhere a dog barks, and someone calls a child in for bed. Tillie sits on the step with her sister, watching the people pass. She has heard a good deal of talk about the war. Her brother is in it, and her friends' brothers too. She knows there have been great battles far away. But it all seems a long way off, on an evening like this. The swallows wheel over the rooftops. The smell of honeysuckle drifts over the garden walls. At bedtime, Tillie climbs the stairs with a candle, says her prayers and lies down. Through the open window, she hears the crickets, and a horse going slowly home along the street.

[long-pause]

{img:25-pennsylvania-college-hill}

While Tillie sleeps, the town's two tall cupolas stand pale in the moonlight, one to the north and one to the west. Shall we walk up there and look around?

[pause]

The little college on the hill north of Gettysburg is still teaching students today.

[pause]

On the northern edge of Gettysburg, on a gentle rise among tall trees, stands Pennsylvania College. You could still walk across its lawns today. It was founded about thirty years earlier, in 1832. It's a handsome building of brick, painted pale, with tall white columns at the front. On the roof, there's a little cupola. About a hundred and fifty young men study here, in their dark coats and waistcoats. Some live in small rooms in the college building. Others lodge with families in the town and walk up the hill every morning. They study Latin and Greek, mathematics and science. They have debating clubs, where they argue about great questions late into the evening. The students are a lively part of town life. They buy sausages at the butcher's, and they sing under windows on summer nights. You may still visit the college today. You'd hear a bell calling the students to class. It's now called Gettysburg College, and its old main building still stands on the same rise, among the trees.

[long-pause]

{img:26-professor-thermometer-garden}

A professor wrote down the weather three times a day, and kept doing it right through the battle.

[pause]

One of the professors at the college is a man named Michael Jacobs. You'd see him stepping out three times a day to read his thermometer. He teaches mathematics and natural science. He is a careful, methodical man, with a long face and a neat beard. And he has a habit that will become very valuable to history. Every day, three times a day, he goes out to look at his instruments. At seven in the morning, two in the afternoon and nine in the evening, he writes down the weather. The temperature. The direction of the wind. The clouds. Whether it's raining or dry. He has been doing this for years, faithfully, in a little book. And he will go on doing it all through the summer of 1863, whatever happens around him. Thanks to Professor Jacobs, we know exactly how warm it was in Gettysburg on every day of that summer. Can you imagine keeping a habit so faithfully, through everything? I love that little detail. He did, and his notes are still read today.

[long-pause]

{img:27-lutheran-seminary-gardens-students-walking}

Young men at the seminary on the ridge preached practice sermons to one another.

[pause]

To the west of town, on the ridge above the fields, stands the Lutheran Theological Seminary. You'd hear them practising in empty rooms, a little nervous. It was founded in 1826, and it's the oldest Lutheran seminary in America. The main building is long and made of brick, four storeys high, with a white wooden cupola on top. Here, young men study to become ministers in the Lutheran church. They read Hebrew and Greek and theology. They preach practice sermons to one another, and their teachers tell them kindly what to do better. In their free hours, they walk in the gardens and the woods along the ridge. The view from the cupola is wonderful. On a clear day, you can see the whole town spread out below, and the farms and woods and orchards all around. Away to the west, there's the long blue line of the mountains. Because of this building, the people of Gettysburg call the ridge Seminary Ridge. You'd probably try not to laugh if you heard one of them stumble. The name will soon be known far beyond the town.

[long-pause]

{img:28-cemetery-gatehouse-brick-arch}

The cemetery gatehouse in Gettysburg had an arch in the middle and a small home on each side.

[pause]

On the southern edge of town, on a hill above the Baltimore Pike, there's a cemetery. You'd walk through the arch and up the gentle path. It's called Evergreen Cemetery. It was opened only about nine years earlier, in 1854. It's a peaceful, green place, with tall trees and winding paths and white gravestones. At the entrance, beside the road, stands a handsome brick gatehouse. It has an arched passage through the middle, wide enough for a wagon. On either side are rooms where the cemetery keeper lives. The keeper of the cemetery is a man named Peter Thorn. But Peter, like so many men in Gettysburg, has gone off to the army. So the work of the cemetery is being done by his wife, Elizabeth. You can still see the gatehouse today, with its arch, on the same hill. If you stood in front of it on a summer evening, you'd hear the same thing Elizabeth heard. Birds settling in the trees, and the wind in the long grass.

[long-pause]

{img:29-woman-gardening-cemetery-paths}

While her husband was in the army, Elizabeth Thorn ran the whole cemetery herself.

[pause]

Elizabeth Thorn came to America from Germany as a young woman. You'd find her busy from morning until night, with her children close by. She is about thirty in 1863. She is strong and hardworking, with three young sons, and she is expecting another child in the autumn. Her elderly parents live with her in the gatehouse, and her father helps with the heavy work. It isn't an easy job. Elizabeth looks after the grounds, the paths and the gates. She keeps the records of who is buried where, in a big book. When someone in the town passes away, she and her father prepare the grave and help with the burial. Then she writes the name carefully in the cemetery book. In between, she cooks and cleans for her family and tends her garden. Her boys play among the trees as if it were the finest playground in the world. From the gatehouse windows, she can look over the whole town in one direction, and over the long ridge to the south in the other.

[long-pause]

{img:30-cemetery-ridge-farms-wheat-orchard}

A free Black farmer named Abraham Bryan owned a small farm on the ridge south of town.

[pause]

The hill with the cemetery on it's called Cemetery Hill. You'd see his small house among the fields on the ridge. From the top, the land falls away gently on all sides. To the east, there's another wooded hill, called Culp's Hill. To the south, the long, low ridge runs on for two miles or so. People call it Cemetery Ridge. Along it, there are farms, as there are everywhere around Gettysburg. There's the farm of Abraham Bryan, a free Black farmer. He owns a small house, a barn and a few acres of land on the ridge. There's the little white farmhouse of a widow named Lydia Leister, who lives there with her children. Further south, there are the farms of the Trostles and the Weikerts, with their big stone barns and orchards. In the fields, the wheat is growing tall. In a peach orchard beside the Emmitsburg Road, the little green peaches are beginning to swell on the branches.

[long-pause]

{img:31-round-tops-wooded-hills-dusk}

The rocky valley called the Devil's Den was a favourite place for family picnics.

[pause]

At the southern end of the ridge, two rocky hills rise out of the fields. You'd spread out your blanket among the great grey boulders. The bigger one is called Big Round Top. The smaller one, a little closer to town, is Little Round Top. Both are covered in trees, with great grey boulders scattered over their slopes. At their feet, a little stream winds through a valley full of rocks. Some of the boulders there are as big as a house. The local people call that valley the Devil's Den. It sounds a little frightening, but it's really a favourite place for picnics. On summer Sundays, families come out in their wagons. The children climb on the rocks and hide between them. The parents spread a cloth in the shade and unpack bread, cold chicken and cherries. In the evening, the light falls long and golden across the fields. The cows walk slowly home to be milked. Can you picture the families laughing among the rocks? And on the hill, Elizabeth Thorn closes the iron gates of the cemetery and lights her lamp.

[long-pause]

{img:32-family-reading-letter-parlour-lamp}

The gates are closed and the lamp is lit on the hill. Down in the town, though, the post has just come in. Can you hear the doors opening along the street?

[pause]

For most families in Gettysburg, the war arrived in the form of letters.

[pause]

Every few days, the post comes in on the train from Hanover. You'd wait at the window for the postman, every single day. The postmaster sorts it into the little wooden boxes at the post office. Mothers and wives come in to ask whether there's anything for them. When there's a letter from a son or a husband, they hurry home with it. The whole family gathers in the parlour to hear it read aloud. The letters are full of ordinary things. The weather, which is too hot, or too cold, or too wet. The food, which is mostly hard crackers and salt pork and coffee, and never enough. There are long marches, blisters and mud. There's a new friend in the regiment, or a good night's sleep in a dry tent. Often there's a request for warm socks, a new shirt or a pot of jam. The letters about battles are shorter and quieter. And at the end, there's always the same line. Love to everyone, and I hope to be home soon.

[long-pause]

{img:33-sewing-circle-women-open-windows}

Girls in Gettysburg made soft lint from old linen, to send to the army hospitals.

[pause]

The women of Gettysburg do what they can for the soldiers. You'd sit in a circle with them, picking at old linen. They meet in each other's parlours and in the churches. There, they sew shirts and knit socks, and roll bandages from old linen sheets. They pack boxes of food and clothes and send them off to the army. They raise money for the hospitals with suppers and little fairs. On summer afternoons, the circle of women sits by the open windows. Their needles flash in the light. They talk quietly about the news and their families. The younger girls sit beside them, picking old linen apart into soft threads. This is called lint, and the army doctors use it to dress wounds. It's slow, fiddly work, but even small hands can do it. The little children play on the rug at their feet, with a wooden horse or a rag doll. Now and then, someone reads out a line from a soldier's letter, and the others stop sewing to listen.

[long-pause]

{img:34-old-man-cobbler-workshop}

A seventy-year-old shoemaker was furious when the army told him he was too old to fight.

[pause]

The men who have stayed at home talk about the war all the time. You can almost hear him grumbling about it. Some of the older ones say they'd go and fight themselves, if only they were younger. One of them is an old man named John Burns. He is about sixty-nine years old. He is short and stubborn, with white hair, a craggy face and strong opinions. He is a shoemaker by trade, and for some years he has been the town constable. As a young man, he fought in the War of 1812, against Britain, and he is very proud of it. When this war began in 1861, he tried to join the army. They told him he was too old. He wasn't pleased at all. He has been grumbling about it ever since, to anyone who will listen. His old musket hangs on the wall at home. Some people in town smile about him. But nobody doubts that he means every word he says.

[long-pause]

{img:35-quiet-farm-lane-evening-north}

For the Black families of Gettysburg, a Southern army in Pennsylvania meant a very real danger.

[pause]

There are other worries in Gettysburg too. If you'd lived in their homes, you'd have listened to every rumour with great care. The town is close to the border between North and South. Just across the state line, in Maryland, there are families who support the Confederacy. And for the Black families of Gettysburg, the nearness of the border is a real danger. Before the war, people escaping from slavery often passed through Gettysburg on their way north. Friends in the town helped them along, quietly, at night. And there were always men who came looking for them, to take them back. If the Southern army comes into Pennsylvania, free Black people might be seized and carried south. Everyone knows it can happen. So many Black families in Gettysburg have already made plans to leave quickly, if they must. A wagon stands ready. A bundle of clothes waits by the door. It's a hard way to live. You watch the road, and you listen to every rumour, and you hope the warning comes in time.

[long-pause]

{img:36-general-lee-grey-horse-portrait-calm}

The most famous general in the South had a grey horse named Traveller.

[pause]

In the late spring of 1863, the news from Virginia isn't good. You'd have known him at once, sitting straight and calm on his grey horse. In early May, at a place called Chancellorsville, the Southern army has won a great victory. It has beaten the Northern army, which is called the Army of the Potomac. The Southern army is led by General Robert E. Lee. He is the most famous soldier in the Confederacy. He is about fifty-six years old, a dignified, grey-bearded man from an old Virginia family. He is calm and courteous, and his soldiers love him greatly. He rides a grey horse named Traveller, and the two of them are known to every soldier in his army. After his victory, people in the North wonder what he will do next. Soon there are rumours. Lee is planning to march north, people say, into Maryland and Pennsylvania. He wants to carry the war into the North for the first time, and perhaps make the North tired of fighting.

[long-pause]

{img:37-storekeeper-packing-crates-railway}

When the rumours grew strong, the Gettysburg bank quietly sent its money away to Philadelphia.

[pause]

In Gettysburg, the rumours grow stronger week by week. You'd notice the bank manager looking a little worried. People say the Southern army is already moving through the valleys west of the mountains. They say it's taking horses and cattle and food from the farms. Some say it's coming toward Harrisburg. Others say Philadelphia, or Baltimore. Nobody knows for sure. But the storekeepers begin to pack up their goods in crates. They send them away by train, for safety. The bank sends its money to Philadelphia. Farmers begin to drive their horses away over the hills, to hide them in the woods. A good horse is worth a great deal, and a farm can't work without one. Some families bury their silver spoons in the garden. Others hide them in the chimney or under a loose floorboard. In the evenings, people stand at their gates and ask every traveller what he has seen. What would you have hidden away, if you'd lived there? And every traveller seems to have a different story.

[long-pause]

{img:38-june-fields-wheat-golden-farmhouse}

Even as the rumours grew, the cherries ripened and the children climbed the trees to pick them.

[pause]

And still, the summer comes on, warm and green and beautiful. You'd hear the children laughing up in the branches. The wheat in the fields is turning golden. The cherries in the orchards are ripe, and the children climb the trees to pick them. They come down with red-stained fingers and red-stained mouths. The hay is being cut and stacked in the meadows. The days are long, and the evenings are full of fireflies. On the hill above the town, Elizabeth Thorn tends the cemetery. At the college, Professor Jacobs reads his thermometer. On Baltimore Street, Tillie Pierce and her friends sit on the step in the evening. They talk about the rumours, half excited and half afraid. Will the Rebels really come? What do they look like? Will they take our horses? Then their mothers call them in, and they go to bed. And somewhere beyond the mountains to the west, two great armies are moving slowly north, closer every day.

[long-pause]

{img:39-rainy-june-afternoon-street}

For a few more days, the summer goes on as before. Then, one grey and drizzly Friday afternoon, you'd have heard a shout from the end of the street.

[pause]

On the day the first Southern soldiers arrived, Tillie was sitting at her school desk.

[pause]

It's the afternoon of Friday, the twenty-sixth of June, 1863. You'd hear the shout and see the whole classroom turn to the window. It's raining in Gettysburg. It's a grey, drizzly day, and the streets are muddy. Tillie Pierce is at school, in Mrs. Eyster's seminary, bending over her lessons. Then someone outside begins to shout. The Rebels are coming! The teacher closes the books. She tells the girls to go straight home, as quickly as they can. Tillie runs home along the wet pavement, holding her skirts up out of the mud. Her heart is pounding. When she gets there, she climbs up to look out of the window. And then she sees them. First comes a great crowd of men on horseback, riding into town from the west. Then come the foot soldiers, many of them, in long, ragged columns. They wear grey and brown clothes. Some of them are barefoot, splashing through the mud. They are soldiers of the Southern army, under a general named Jubal Early.

[long-pause]

{img:40-confederate-soldiers-march-rain-town}

The Southern general asked Gettysburg for a thousand pairs of shoes and five hundred hats.

[pause]

The soldiers gather in the Diamond, in the rain. You might think a town could easily find that many. It couldn't. Their general sends a message to the town council. He asks for food and supplies. He wants flour and bacon, sugar and coffee, salt and onions. He wants a thousand pairs of shoes and five hundred hats. Or else, he says, the town can pay him a large sum of money instead. The council members reply, politely, that the town doesn't have such things to give. The storekeepers have sent most of their goods away. The bank has sent away its money. The general isn't pleased. But there isn't much he can do about it. His soldiers take what they can find in the stores and the cellars. Many of them are tired and hungry, and their clothes are worn thin. Some people in town notice how young they look. A few of them even say thank you, very politely, for a cup of water.

[long-pause]

{img:41-horse-led-from-stable-yard-rain}

One of the horses the soldiers took that day belonged to Tillie's family.

[pause]

One of the horses the soldiers take belongs to the Pierce family. You can imagine how you'd feel, watching your own horse led away. Tillie watches from the window as two soldiers lead it out of the yard behind her house. Her father stands in the doorway. He can do nothing to stop them. Tillie is indignant. It's the first time she has seen the war with her own eyes, and she doesn't like it at all. Many years later, she will still remember exactly how she felt. Other families lose horses too, and hams from their cellars, and bread from their kitchens. That night, the soldiers camp on the edge of town. In the morning, they march away again, eastward, toward the town of York. The people of Gettysburg come out of their houses. They look at each other and let out a long breath. Their first visit from the Southern army is over. It has been frightening, and a little strange. But nobody has been hurt, and the town still stands.

[long-pause]

{img:42-townspeople-talking-diamond-square}

After the first visit, some townspeople said the Rebels had been rather polite.

[pause]

For a few days, things are quiet. You'd hear them talking about it over the garden fences. People talk of nothing else, of course. They tell each other what they saw, and what the soldiers said, and what was taken from whom. One family lost a horse. Another lost a ham. Someone's boots were taken right off the doorstep. Some people say the Rebels weren't so very frightening after all. They were polite enough, they say, and very tired, and very poorly dressed. Others aren't so sure. They noticed how many there were, and how hungry they looked. And the rumours keep coming. The main Southern army, people say, is close now, just beyond the mountains. And the Northern army is coming up from Maryland to meet it. The town council meets. The ministers pray. Families check their cellars and their food. Everyone looks a little more often along the roads, toward the hills. Something is coming. Nobody can say exactly what.

[long-pause]

{img:43-union-cavalry-riding-through-town-cheering}

When the Union cavalry rode in, Tillie and her school friends stood on a corner and sang to them.

[pause]

Then, on the morning of Tuesday, the thirtieth of June, there are soldiers on the roads again. You'd see their blue coats coming up the road in a long line. This time they come from the south, along the Emmitsburg Road. And they are wearing blue. They are Union cavalry, about three thousand men on horseback. They ride into Gettysburg in a long, dusty column, with their sabres jingling and their flags flying. The people of the town come running out of their houses to greet them. It's a joyful scene. The women and girls stand on the pavements, waving their handkerchiefs and cheering. Some bring out bread and cakes and cups of water. The children run alongside the horses. Everyone feels much safer now. Tillie and her school friends stand at the corner of Washington Street. As the cavalrymen ride by, the girls sing to them. They sing the patriotic songs they have learned at school. The soldiers smile and touch their caps. Can you hear them singing? Tillie will remember that moment for the rest of her life.

[long-pause]

{img:44-cavalry-general-ridge-looking-west}

A quiet cavalry general looked at the ridges around Gettysburg and decided to stay.

[pause]

The cavalry are led by a general named John Buford. You'd see him riding slowly along the ridges, looking carefully at everything. He is a quiet, weathered man of about thirty-seven, from Kentucky. He has a drooping moustache and a calm, thoughtful manner. He is an experienced cavalry officer. And he doesn't like what he sees. He rides up onto the ridge by the seminary and looks out to the west, toward the mountains. His scouts have told him that a large Southern force is camped only a few miles away, along the Chambersburg Pike. He studies the roads and the ridges carefully. Remember the ten roads, coming together like spokes of a wheel? Buford understands at once what they mean. Armies will come here from every direction. And whoever holds the high ground around this town will have a great advantage. So he decides that his men will stay and hold the ridges, until the rest of the Union army can arrive. It's a quiet decision, made on horseback, in the evening light.

[long-pause]

{img:45-cavalry-campfires-fields-dusk}

The night before the battle, some families in Gettysburg invited soldiers home for supper.

[pause]

That evening, Buford's men make camp on the ridges west and north of town. You'd see soldiers sitting at kitchen tables, eating a quiet supper. Their campfires flicker across the fields in the dusk. Their horses graze in the meadows. A few of the soldiers walk into town to buy bread or pies. Some of the families invite them in for supper. They sit at kitchen tables, these young men from New York and Indiana and Illinois. They eat ham and fried potatoes and talk about their own homes far away. At nine o'clock, Professor Jacobs goes out to read his thermometer, as he always does. He writes the temperature in his book. On Baltimore Street, Tillie goes to bed full of excitement. She is thinking about the soldiers, and the songs, and the way the men smiled at the girls. It's a warm, still night. The stars are out. And in the camps along the ridges, the cavalrymen lie down beside their horses, waiting for the morning.

[long-pause]

{img:46-army-column-marching-dusty-road}

Gettysburg is sleeping now. But out in the dark countryside, along every road from the south and the west, you'd hear the tramp of thousands of feet.

[pause]

Together, the two armies had more than sixty times as many men as Gettysburg had people.

[pause]

While Gettysburg sleeps, two great armies are moving toward it. Try to picture that many people, and you'll find you can't. They are enormous. The Union army, the Army of the Potomac, has about ninety thousand men. The Southern army, the Army of Northern Virginia, has about seventy-five thousand. Together, they are more than sixty times the population of the little town that lies between them. On the roads, the armies stretch out for miles. There are columns of soldiers marching four abreast. They carry their rifles on their shoulders and their blanket rolls slung across their chests. There are long lines of cannons and wagons, pulled by teams of horses, rumbling over the stones. There are herds of cattle too, walking with the army, to be its meat. And there are officers on horseback, riding up and down the lines. The dust rises in great clouds over the roads. It settles on faces and uniforms, until everyone is the same pale brown colour, whatever side he is on.

[long-pause]

{img:47-soldiers-crowding-farm-pump-heat}

Marching soldiers broke ranks at every farm pump they passed, to fill their canteens.

[pause]

It's hot this summer. You'd hear the pump handles squeaking all along the road. Very hot. The soldiers march fifteen or twenty miles a day, sometimes more. They carry heavy loads. Their feet are blistered, and their shoes are worn through. They are thirsty all the time. Whenever the column passes a farm with a well or a pump, the men break ranks. They crowd around it, filling their tin canteens and splashing water on their faces. The officers shout at them to get back in line. But the men drink first. The farm families come out to watch them pass. Some bring out buckets of water, or bread, or milk. Children stand on the fences and stare at the endless lines. The soldiers thank them, and some of them wave, and they march on. At the end of the day, some of the men are so tired that they fall asleep the moment they stop. You'd see the farm wives bringing out cups and buckets for them. They lie down beside the road in the dust, still wearing their packs.

[long-pause]

{img:48-evening-camp-coffee-fires-harmonica}

For the soldiers, the most important moment of the day was making coffee.

[pause]

In the evening, the armies make camp in the fields beside the road. You'd smell the coffee in every camp at dawn. The men stack their rifles in little pyramids and light small fires. Then they make coffee in tin cups. For most of them, this is the most important moment of the whole day. They fry salt pork in their mess tins and break hard crackers into it. The crackers are so hard that the men call them teeth dullers. They take off their shoes and rub their aching feet. Then they sit around the fires and talk. They talk about home and their families, and about the girls they left behind. Someone takes out a harmonica or a fiddle and plays a sad old song. Someone writes a letter by the light of the fire, with the paper on his knee. Someone falls asleep sitting up. Isn't it lovely how something so small can matter so much? Imagine hundreds of little fires across the dark fields, and the soft sound of voices, all the way to the hills.

[long-pause]

{img:49-young-soldiers-faces-campfire-night}

Soldiers on opposite sides sometimes traded coffee for tobacco between the lines.

[pause]

Most of the soldiers on both sides are young men. You might think enemies never spoke. But sometimes they did. Many are in their late teens or early twenties. They are farm boys, mostly, and clerks and labourers, students and craftsmen. Some have been in the army for two years and have fought in many battles. Others are new and have never seen a battle at all. In many ways, the soldiers in blue and the soldiers in grey are very much alike. They come from the same kind of homes. They speak the same language. They sing many of the same songs, and they pray to the same God. In quiet times, when the armies were camped close together beside a river, they sometimes called across to each other. They even traded. The Southern soldiers had tobacco, and the Northern soldiers had coffee. So they'd swap, sending little bundles across the water on tiny rafts. The next day, of course, they were enemies again.

[long-pause]

{img:50-general-meade-tent-maps}

The Union general was woken in the night with news, and at first thought he was being arrested.

[pause]

The Union army has a new commander. You can picture him, sitting up in bed, still half asleep. Just three days earlier, on the twenty-eighth of June, President Lincoln gave the command to General George Gordon Meade. Meade is a tall, thin, serious man of forty-seven, from Philadelphia. He has a grey beard, large spectacles and a sharp temper. His soldiers call him, not very kindly, a goggle-eyed old snapping turtle. But he is a careful, sensible and experienced officer. And he is fighting to defend his own home state. He was woken in the middle of the night in his tent to be told the news. An officer from Washington stood beside his bed. Meade thought at first that he was being arrested. Instead, he was given command of the whole army, with a battle coming in a matter of days. It wasn't a job he had asked for. He wrote to his wife that he was moving at once, and that he hoped to do his duty.

[long-pause]

{img:51-professor-colonel-writing-letter-tent}

One Union colonel had been a language professor, and could read Latin, Greek and Hebrew.

[pause]

Among the many thousands of soldiers marching north, there's a man named Joshua Lawrence Chamberlain. You might not expect a scholar on a battlefield. Before the war, he was a professor at Bowdoin College, in the state of Maine, far to the north. He taught languages and the art of speaking. He could read Latin and Greek and Hebrew, and several other languages besides. He was married, with two young children, and he loved music and books. In 1862, he asked the college for leave to travel and study in Europe. Instead, he joined the army. Now he is thirty-four years old and a colonel. He commands a regiment called the 20th Maine. It has about three hundred and eighty men. Most of them are farmers and fishermen and lumbermen, from the coast and the forests of Maine. They are used to hard work and long winters. Chamberlain writes to his wife, Fanny, whenever he can. He tells her about the country and the men, and how much he misses home.

[long-pause]

{img:52-night-camp-tents-stars-hills}

Neither General Lee nor General Meade had planned to fight a battle at Gettysburg.

[pause]

On the other side, there are men like James Longstreet, one of Lee's most important generals. You might think a great battle is always planned. This one wasn't. He is a big, bearded, quiet man from Georgia, solid and stubborn. Lee calls him his old war horse. In the winter of 1862, three of his young children were lost to fever in a single week. He doesn't talk about it. But his friends say he has become quieter and more serious since then. On the night of the thirtieth of June, the armies are spread out across the countryside, under the summer stars. Some are only a few miles from Gettysburg. Others are a day's march or more away. Neither Lee nor Meade has planned to fight at Gettysburg. Neither knows exactly where the other's army is. But the ten roads are drawing them both in, like water running down into a valley. The fires die down to embers. A whip-poor-will calls from the edge of the woods. And the short summer night slips by.

[long-pause]

{img:53-misty-dawn-fields-west-of-town}

Before the sun is up, the bugles sound in the camps. The men roll up their blankets and drink their coffee. Let's stay close to the townspeople as the days unfold, gently.

[pause]

On the first morning of the battle, the professor's thermometer showed a mild seventy-two degrees.

[pause]

The morning of Wednesday, the first of July, begins quietly in Gettysburg. You'd step outside into a mild, pleasant morning. There's a light mist over the fields and the sound of birds in the orchards. At seven o'clock, Professor Jacobs goes out to read his thermometer, as usual. It's seventy-two degrees, with light clouds and a gentle breeze from the south. A warm summer day. West of town, along the Chambersburg Pike, Buford's cavalrymen are watching the road. A little after seven, they see what they have been waiting for. A long column of Southern soldiers is coming down the road from the west, toward the town. A Union officer raises his carbine and fires a single shot. It's said to be the first shot of the battle of Gettysburg. Several men later claimed that they fired it. Nobody can be completely sure. But that small sound, on a misty morning, is where the story of the battle begins.

[long-pause]

{img:54-townspeople-rooftops-watching-distant-smoke}

Some boys climbed onto the rooftops of Gettysburg to watch, until soldiers told them to come down.

[pause]

In the town, people hear firing in the distance, coming from the west. You can imagine their mothers calling them down. At first it's only a scattered popping, far away, like someone shooting at birds. Then it grows louder and steadier. Then there's a deep, heavy boom, and another, as the cannons begin. People come out of their houses and stand in the streets. They look toward the west. Little white clouds of smoke are rising over the ridges. Some of the boys climb up onto the roofs to see better. Some older people climb into the cupolas of the college and the courthouse. Then the soldiers tell them all to come down and go indoors. On Baltimore Street, Tillie's mother makes a decision. Mrs. Shriver is going out to her parents' farm, three miles south of town, at the foot of the Round Tops. It will be safer there, she says, away from the fighting. Tillie's mother asks her to take Tillie along.

[long-pause]

{img:55-girl-woman-children-walking-south-road}

Tillie was sent to a farm for safety, without knowing that great fighting would come right there.

[pause]

And so, a little before noon, Tillie sets off. You'd see her climbing into a wagon with a small bundle on her lap. She walks with Mrs. Shriver and her two little girls, south out of town. They go along the road past the cemetery, away from the sound of the guns. The road is quiet out here. The wheat is golden in the fields, and the sun is hot. They reach the Weikert farm in the early afternoon. Tillie doesn't know it yet. But she is walking straight toward the place where some of the greatest fighting of the whole battle will take place. Nobody could have guessed it that morning. In town, meanwhile, an unexpected volunteer goes out to the battle. Old John Burns, the shoemaker, takes his musket from the wall. He puts on his tall hat and his best coat, and walks out toward the fighting. He finds some Union soldiers in a wood west of the seminary and asks to join them. They look at him in amazement. Can you imagine how she felt, so far from home? Then they let him stay.

[long-pause]

{img:56-church-turned-hospital-women-water}

On the first day, the churches of Gettysburg were already turned into hospitals.

[pause]

In the afternoon, the town begins to fill with wounded soldiers. You'd see pews pushed aside to make room. They come in along the roads, walking or limping, or carried by their friends, or lying in wagons. The doctors set up hospitals in the churches and the courthouse. They use the college and the seminary too, and many private houses. Christ Lutheran Church on Chambersburg Street fills with men lying on the pews and the floor. The Catholic church fills up. The courthouse fills up. Everywhere, the people of Gettysburg open their doors. The women come out with water and bandages and bread. They have never done anything like this before. But they simply begin. Old John Burns, meanwhile, will be wounded that day. But he will recover, and become famous all over the country, as the old man of Gettysburg who went out to fight. Years later, people will still come to his door, just to shake his hand.

[long-pause]

{img:57-family-cellar-candle-preserves-jars}

During the fighting, families in Gettysburg sat in their cellars among the jars of preserves.

[pause]

By late afternoon, the Union soldiers are being pushed back. You'd sit on a step in the cool dark, among the shelves. They retreat through the town, through the streets and alleys and back gardens. They head for the hill with the cemetery on it. For a little while, the streets are full of running men and shouting. The people of the town stay inside. They go down into their cellars with the doors locked and the shutters closed. In hundreds of houses, families sit close together in the dark. They sit among the potatoes and the jars of preserves, by the light of a single candle. The children are frightened. The mothers hold them close and hum to them softly. The fathers sit by the cellar steps, listening. Outside, the noise rolls over the town. And then, slowly, slowly, it begins to move away, to the south. By evening, it's quiet again. The families climb the cellar steps and look out at a town that's suddenly full of strangers.

[long-pause]

{img:58-cemetery-hill-gatehouse-soldiers-evening}

On the first night, the cemetery keeper's wife baked bread for the generals until late at night.

[pause]

By evening, the Union soldiers have gathered on Cemetery Hill and Culp's Hill, south of town. You'd smell fresh bread drifting from her little kitchen. They are digging in along the ridge, behind stone walls and piles of fence rails. The Southern soldiers hold the town itself, and the ridges to the west and north. On Cemetery Hill, the gatehouse is suddenly in the middle of the Union army. Elizabeth Thorn does what she knows. She bakes bread and makes coffee for the generals and the soldiers, until late into the night. Her boys and her parents sleep. An officer asks her to show him the roads around the hill. So she walks out with him in the dusk, pointing them out one by one. Remember, she is expecting a baby. Yet she walks the roads calmly, as she has done a thousand times. Later, a general will remember her as a brave woman, who knew every lane and field around her hill.

[long-pause]

{img:59-farmhouse-well-water-soldiers-night}

Tillie spent the evening carrying buckets of water to soldiers marching past the farm.

[pause]

That night, Tillie is at the Weikert farm, at the foot of Little Round Top. You'd feel the weight of the bucket pulling at your arm. The house is full of soldiers coming and going. The yard is full of horses. The Weikert family and their guests are busy all evening. They bake bread. They give out water from the well to the soldiers passing by. There are hundreds and hundreds of them, marching up from the south to join the army on the ridge. Tillie carries buckets of water out to the road. She fills the soldiers' cups. They thank her, and march on into the dark. The next day, she will keep doing the same. One young soldier shows her a picture of his mother. Another asks her to write to his family, if anything happens to him. She promises that she will. Late at night, she lies down on the farmhouse floor to sleep, tired out. Outside, the sound of marching feet still goes by on the road.

[long-pause]

{img:60-officer-on-rocky-hilltop-telescope}

A young engineer climbed Little Round Top and saw that nobody was there to hold it.

[pause]

Thursday, the second of July, begins quietly. Stand beside him on the rocks, and you'll see the whole valley below you. For most of the morning, there's very little fighting. The two armies are getting ready. Between the two ridges lie the farm fields, with wheat and oats and corn, orchards and meadows. It's about a mile across, from one ridge to the other. Behind the Weikert farm rises Little Round Top, steep and rocky. That morning, it's almost empty. There are only a few signalmen at the top, waving their flags to send messages. In the early afternoon, a young engineer officer named Gouverneur Warren rides up to look around. He looks west across the fields. Among the trees on the far side, he sees sunlight glinting on rifles. He realizes, with a jolt, what it means. If the other side takes this hill, they can fire along the whole Union line. And nobody is there to stop them. Warren sends messengers galloping off in every direction, to find soldiers who can come.

[long-pause]

{img:61-rocky-wooded-slope-boulders-afternoon}

The professor from Maine and his regiment held the very end of the Union line.

[pause]

At about four o'clock in the afternoon, the great attack begins. You'll remember him, the man who knew Latin, Greek and Hebrew. Longstreet's soldiers come forward from the west, across the fields and through the woods. The fighting spreads across the Peach Orchard and the Wheatfield, and the rocky Devil's Den. At the Weikert farm, the soldiers tell the family to leave at once. Tillie and Mrs. Shriver and the little girls hurry away to another farmhouse, further from the fighting. On the slopes of Little Round Top, the soldiers Warren called for arrive just in time. Among them is Colonel Chamberlain, the professor from Maine, with the 20th Maine. They are placed at the very end of the Union line, among the boulders and trees. For about an hour and a half, his men hold the slope. When they have almost no ammunition left, Chamberlain orders them forward, down the hill, and the attackers fall back. The end of the Union line is safe. Years later, Chamberlain will be governor of Maine.

[long-pause]

{img:62-small-farmhouse-lamplit-generals-night}

On the second night, the Union generals crowded into a widow's tiny farmhouse to decide what to do.

[pause]

As the sun goes down, the fighting slowly dies away. You'd see their shadows on the walls of that tiny room, in the candlelight. By nightfall, the Southern soldiers have taken the Peach Orchard and the Devil's Den. But the Union line on the ridge has held, all the way from Cemetery Hill to Little Round Top. Across the dark fields, small lights move slowly back and forth. They are the lanterns of the stretcher-bearers, searching for the wounded and carrying them to the barns and farmhouses behind the lines. That night, the Union generals meet in the little white farmhouse of the widow Lydia Leister. General Meade has made his headquarters there. They crowd into the tiny front room, around a small table, by the light of a single candle. Should they stay, or should they leave? One by one, the generals give their opinions. They will stay, they agree. And at the farmhouse where Tillie has found shelter, she lies awake for a long time, listening to the silence.

[long-pause]

{img:63-hot-morning-baltimore-street-quiet}

The candle in the little farmhouse is blown out at last. A few hours later, the sun rises hot and still. Can you feel the heat already, so early in the day?

[pause]

The third day of the battle was the hottest, at eighty-seven degrees in the afternoon.

[pause]

Friday, the third of July, dawns hot and still over Gettysburg. You'd feel the heat even in the shade. At seven o'clock, Professor Jacobs writes in his book. Seventy-three degrees, and clouds. By two in the afternoon, it will be eighty-seven, the hottest day of the battle. Early in the morning, there's fighting again on Culp's Hill, east of the cemetery. The sound echoes through the streets of the town. Then, by late morning, that too dies away. The town is strangely quiet. For two days now, the Southern soldiers have been in the streets. They sit on the doorsteps, cleaning their rifles. They cook small breakfasts over fires in the alleys. Some knock on doors and ask, politely, for bread or water. The people of the town stay indoors, with their shutters closed. They peer out through the cracks. Nobody knows how long this will last. They only know that the sun is very hot, and that they are very tired.

[long-pause]

{img:64-small-brick-house-kitchen-bread-dough}

Jennie Wade was baking bread for the soldiers when the battle reached her sister's kitchen.

[pause]

On Baltimore Street, near the southern edge of town, Jennie Wade is at her sister's house. You'd smell the bread rising in that small, warm kitchen. Her sister Georgia has just had a baby, a few days before the battle began. Jennie and her mother have come to help look after them. The little brick house stands right between the two armies. That morning, Jennie is in the kitchen, kneading dough to make bread for the Union soldiers. She has been baking for them for two days, handing out loaves at the door. At about half past eight, a stray bullet passes through two wooden doors. Jennie is struck, and she passes away at once, quietly, in the kitchen. She is twenty years old. She is the only person living in the town who lost her life in the battle. The soldiers wrap her gently in a quilt. Far away in Virginia, her Jack is lying in a hospital, wounded in an earlier battle. He too will pass away, never knowing what happened to her.

[long-pause]

{img:65-hot-noon-ridge-waiting-soldiers-shade}

Just before the great cannonade, some Union generals sat down to a lunch of stewed chicken.

[pause]

By late morning, a great stillness settles over the battlefield. You'd hear the clink of their tin plates in the quiet midday heat. The soldiers on both ridges lie in the hot sun, waiting. Some of them sleep. Some write letters. Some eat what little food they have. The fields between the ridges lie empty and quiet, shimmering in the heat. Bees buzz in the clover. A bird sings in a tree. On Cemetery Ridge, a Union officer later remembered a strange, peaceful lunch. The generals sat down together in the shade and ate stewed chicken, potatoes and bread, with coffee. They had found an old rooster at a farm nearby. He was tough, the officer wrote, but they were glad of him. It's the calm before the last great storm. At about one o'clock in the afternoon, two cannons fire, one after the other, from Seminary Ridge. It's a signal. And then, all along the ridge, more than a hundred and fifty cannons begin to fire at once.

[long-pause]

{img:66-white-smoke-cloud-over-fields-distant}

The cannonade at Gettysburg could be heard many miles away, like distant thunder.

[pause]

The sound is like nothing anyone in Gettysburg has ever heard. If you'd been miles away, you'd have looked up at a clear sky and wondered about the thunder. People hear it many miles away, rolling across the hills like distant thunder. In the town, the windows rattle and the dishes shake on the shelves. The ground trembles. Great clouds of white smoke roll across the fields and hide the sun. In the cellars, the families sit in the dark and hold each other, and wait. On Cemetery Hill, the soldiers lie flat behind their stone walls. Then the Union cannons answer back. For nearly two hours, the noise goes on and on. Then, slowly, it fades away. The smoke begins to drift off across the fields. Out of the woods on Seminary Ridge, a long line of soldiers comes into view. There are about twelve thousand of them, in long grey lines, a mile wide, with their flags above them. Many of them belong to a general named George Pickett. So this will become known as Pickett's Charge.

[long-pause]

{img:67-general-lee-on-horse-meeting-soldiers}

After the charge, General Lee told his returning soldiers that it was all his own fault.

[pause]

The long grey lines march steadily across the open fields, over the fences and across the Emmitsburg Road. You'd hear his voice, quiet and steady, as the men came back. On Cemetery Ridge, the Union soldiers watch them come. Some of them say afterwards that it was the most magnificent thing they ever saw. The march across the fields takes a little under an hour. When it's over, the long grey lines have been turned back. A few men reach the stone wall on Cemetery Ridge, near a little clump of trees, and for a few minutes they cross it. But then they are stopped. The rest turn and walk slowly back across the fields toward their own ridge, helping their wounded friends along. General Lee rides out to meet them. He speaks to them gently. It's all my fault, he tells them. I am the one who has lost this fight. He doesn't blame his men. For a proud general, it's a rare and honest thing to say.

[long-pause]

{img:68-rain-falling-town-dawn-july}

On the morning of the Fourth of July, the people of Gettysburg opened their doors to find the Southern army gone.

[pause]

As the sun goes down on the third of July, the battlefield falls silent at last. You'd open your door slowly and look out at a quiet street. In the town, the people come up out of their cellars and stand at their doors. The great battle of Gettysburg is over. It's the largest battle ever fought in North America. In the early hours of the fourth of July, it begins to rain. At first it's only a light, warm drizzle. By the afternoon, it's pouring down in sheets. Professor Jacobs writes in his book that it rained, and measures how much. It's Independence Day. In other years, there would be a parade in Gettysburg, with flags and a brass band. This year, there's only the rain. And in the morning, people notice that something has changed. The Southern soldiers are gone from the streets. In the night, they quietly pulled back to the ridge in the west. People open their shutters and look out. Then, slowly, they step outside.

[long-pause]

{img:69-wagon-train-rain-night-mountain-road}

When Lee's army left, its line of wagons and ambulances stretched for about seventeen miles.

[pause]

Soon, Union soldiers come down from Cemetery Hill into the town. You'd see the wagons going by for hours and hours in the rain. The people of Gettysburg come out to greet them. Some of them cry. Some laugh. Some simply stand in the rain and say nothing at all. All day long, the two armies stay on their ridges, watching each other across the rainy fields. Neither side attacks. Both are exhausted. The soldiers huddle under their oilcloth blankets and make coffee over fires that sputter in the rain. That evening, Lee's army begins to leave. It goes slowly westward, back toward the mountains and Virginia. First goes the long train of wagons and ambulances carrying the wounded. It's about seventeen miles long, creaking along the muddy roads through the night. Then go the cannons, and then the soldiers, with their heads down. On the same day, far away on the Mississippi River, the city of Vicksburg surrenders to General Grant. Many people begin to feel, for the first time, that the end of the war is coming.

[long-pause]

{img:70-mother-daughter-reunion-doorstep}

When Tillie finally came home, her mother didn't recognize her at first.

[pause]

Tillie is still with Mrs. You'd see her walking slowly up Baltimore Street, tired and dusty. Shriver and her daughters, at a farmhouse south of the battlefield. For several more days, they can't go home. The roads are full of soldiers and wagons. Tillie helps wherever she can. She carries water and rolls bandages. She sits with lonely soldiers and writes letters to their families for them. At last, on the seventh of July, she sets off for home. The countryside isn't the one she left. The fences are gone, burned for firewood. The wheat fields are trampled flat. Tillie walks with her eyes down, holding her skirts out of the mud. When she reaches Baltimore Street, her mother is standing at the door. At first, her mother doesn't recognize her, she is so tired and pale and muddy. Then she does. She runs down the steps and throws her arms around her. The whole family is safe. Tillie sits at the kitchen table and eats bread and butter. Can you picture her mother's face? It's the best thing she has ever tasted.

[long-pause]

{img:71-night-town-lamps-every-window}

That night, the rain stops and the stars come out. And in almost every window in Gettysburg, you'd see a lamp still burning.

[pause]

After the battle, Gettysburg had about eight wounded soldiers for every person who lived there.

[pause]

When the two armies leave, they leave behind more than twenty thousand wounded men, from both sides. You'd find soldiers being cared for in almost every house. And the town has only about two thousand four hundred people. That's about eight wounded soldiers for every person in Gettysburg. They are everywhere. They lie in the churches and the schools, in the college and the seminary. They lie in the courthouse and the warehouses, in barns and in nearly every private house. So the people of Gettysburg take care of them. The women make soup and bake bread from morning till night. They bring water, wash the soldiers' faces and sit beside them. They fan them in the heat. They write letters for those who can't write, and read to them from the Bible. In the evening, they sing to them softly. The men carry stretchers and help the doctors. The children run errands. And they make no difference between blue and grey. A wounded man is a wounded man.

[long-pause]

{img:72-young-teacher-sitting-with-soldier}

A young teacher who had never nursed anyone became one of Gettysburg's most faithful nurses.

[pause]

One of the young women of the town is named Sallie Myers. You might expect a nurse to have years of training. She had none. She is about twenty-one, a schoolteacher, with a gentle face and dark hair. On the first day of the battle, the wounded were carried into the Catholic church on High Street. Sallie went in to help. She had never nursed anyone before, and she was frightened. She has stayed ever since. She brings the men water and food. She washes their faces. She holds their hands, and talks to them, and listens as they talk about their homes and families. One soldier, a young sergeant from Pennsylvania, she takes into her own house. Her family looks after him there. At night, when she can, Sallie writes in her diary. Many years later, her words will help us understand what those weeks were like. Picture her walking home late along the dark street, tired, with a basket on her arm. Then, early the next morning, going back again.

[long-pause]

{img:73-supply-train-unloading-station-volunteers}

Trains full of food, bandages and volunteers poured into Gettysburg from the cities of the North.

[pause]

Soon, help begins to arrive from outside. You'd hear the whistle and see the volunteers climbing down. Trains come into Gettysburg from Baltimore, Philadelphia and Harrisburg. They are loaded with doctors and nurses, food and bandages and medicine. They bring clean sheets, shirts and blankets. Much of it's sent by two great aid societies of the North, the Sanitary Commission and the Christian Commission. Churches and families all over the country send help too. Children in faraway towns have collected pennies to buy lemons and crackers. Volunteers arrive as well. Some are men and women who simply want to help. Others have come to search for their own sons and husbands and brothers among the wounded. The little railway station is crowded with people, day and night. They step off the trains and look around, a little lost. Then a boy from the town offers to show them the way. You'd help carry the boxes up the street to the churches. Everyone in Gettysburg, it seems, has become a guide, a cook or a nurse.

[long-pause]

{img:74-white-tent-hospital-rows-trees}

A great tent hospital with hundreds of white tents stood on a hill east of Gettysburg.

[pause]

In the middle of July, the army sets up a great tent hospital on a hill east of town, beside the York Pike. You'd see the white tents from far off, like a field of sails. It's called Camp Letterman, after the army's chief medical officer, Jonathan Letterman. There are hundreds of white canvas tents, set out in long, neat rows among the trees. There's a kitchen and a bakery, and a store. Slowly, the wounded are moved there from the churches and houses of the town. It's a calm and orderly place. The tents are clean and airy. The nurses walk up and down the rows with their baskets and buckets. In the evenings, a band sometimes plays. The soldiers who can walk sit outside their tents in the long golden light. They write home, play cards and talk. Letterman's way of organising care was new, and it helped many men get well. By the end of November, nearly all of them have been sent home or on to other hospitals. Then Camp Letterman is closed.

[long-pause]

{img:75-gatehouse-woman-baby-autumn-window}

That autumn, the cemetery keeper's wife gave birth to a healthy baby girl named Rose.

[pause]

Up on Cemetery Hill, Elizabeth Thorn is hard at work. You'd hear a baby crying softly in the little gatehouse. When she came back to the gatehouse after the battle, she found it badly damaged. Her furniture was broken and her bedding was gone. But there was work to be done. In the hot July days, though she was expecting a child herself, Elizabeth and her old father set to work. They laid to rest the soldiers who had fallen on and around the hill. It was hard, sad work, and she never forgot it. Many years later, she told her story to a newspaper, so that people would know what the women of Gettysburg had done. In the autumn, she gave birth to a healthy baby girl. She named her Rose. Imagine the little gatehouse in October, with the leaves turning red and gold on the trees outside. A baby sleeps in a cradle by the stove. And her mother sits beside her for a moment, resting at last.

[long-pause]

{img:76-farmer-rebuilding-fence-late-summer}

After the battle, farmers around Gettysburg had to rebuild miles of fences by hand.

[pause]

Little by little, life in Gettysburg goes back to something like normal. You'd see them working along the field edges with new rails. The farmers go out into their fields and gather what they can of the harvest. They rebuild their fences, rail by rail. Around Gettysburg, there are miles and miles of them to put back. The shops reopen. The storekeepers bring their crates back from Philadelphia. The bank opens its doors again. The schools open in the autumn. Tillie goes back to Mrs. Eyster's seminary and sits at her desk, and learns her lessons as before. But the town will never be quite the same. Every family has a story to tell about those days. Every house has its marks. And out on the ridges and in the fields, in hundreds of simple places, lie the soldiers who didn't go home. Often they are marked only with a piece of wood, with a name written on it in pencil. Many people in Gettysburg feel that this isn't right.

[long-pause]

{img:77-david-wills-house-diamond}

One evening, in a big brick house on the corner of the Diamond, a lamp burns late. A young lawyer is writing at his desk. What do you think he's planning?

[pause]

The idea of a national cemetery at Gettysburg came from a young lawyer in the town.

[pause]

David Wills is a young lawyer, about thirty-two years old. You'd see the lamp in his window burning late into the night. He lives in a large brick house on the corner of the Diamond, in the very centre of Gettysburg. He is an energetic, practical and well-organised man, with a high forehead and a neat beard. He knows everyone in town, and everyone knows him. In the summer of 1863, the governor of Pennsylvania asks him to look after the affairs of the Pennsylvania soldiers who fell in the battle. Wills takes the job very seriously. He walks out over the battlefield and sees the simple graves scattered across the fields and woods. Every day, letters come to him from families all over the North. They ask where their sons and husbands are, and whether they can be brought home. He reads them all, by the lamp, late at night. And then he writes to the governor with an idea. There should be a national cemetery here, at Gettysburg, where all the Union soldiers can rest together with honour.

[long-pause]

{img:78-landscape-designer-plans-hilltop}

The graves in the new cemetery were laid out in curved rows, like ripples in a pond.

[pause]

The governor agrees, and so do the governors of the other Northern states. If you walk there today, you'll see the curved rows for yourself. Wills buys seventeen acres of land on Cemetery Hill, right next to Evergreen Cemetery. From there, the ground looks out over the fields and ridges where the battle was fought. To design the new cemetery, the states choose a man named William Saunders. He is a gardener and landscape designer, who works for the government in Washington. Saunders is a thoughtful, quiet Scotsman who loves trees and plants and gardens. He walks over the hill many times, studying the shape of the land. Then he decides on a simple and beautiful plan. The graves will lie in great curved rows, in a half circle. They will look like the ripples spreading out from a stone dropped into water. The soldiers of each state will lie together. And at the highest point in the middle, there will be a monument. It's a calm, gentle shape, made to look peaceful.

[long-pause]

{img:79-autumn-trees-cemetery-hill-workers}

In the new cemetery, every grave was made the same, whether it was a general's or a private's.

[pause]

Saunders made one more important choice. You'd walk along the rows and see that no stone stood taller than another. Every grave would be the same. There would be no grand stones for the officers and small ones for the privates. All of them would be equal, side by side, as they were on the battlefield. In the autumn, the work begins. The soldiers are brought to the new cemetery, carefully and respectfully, one by one. They are laid to rest in the curved rows on the hillside. Wherever their names are known, the names are written down, so that they can be remembered. Many have no names that anyone knows. They rest among their comrades, with a small marker that says only that they are unknown. The work goes on through the autumn and into the winter, slowly and quietly. The leaves turn red and gold on the trees, and then fall softly over the hill. Doesn't that feel right? Families from far away come to stand at the edge of the rows, and look, and pray.

[long-pause]

{img:80-elderly-statesman-library-books-lamp}

The main speaker at Gettysburg was famous for speeches that could last for hours.

[pause]

Wills and the committee decide there should be a ceremony to dedicate the new cemetery. You'd need to be patient if you came to hear him. They plan it for the nineteenth of November. They invite the most famous speaker in America, Edward Everett, to give the main address. Everett is a distinguished old gentleman from Massachusetts, nearly seventy years old. He has been a congressman, a governor and a senator. He has been president of Harvard College and Secretary of State. He is famous for his long, beautiful speeches, which can go on for hours. People travel great distances to hear him. In fact, the ceremony was first planned for October. But Everett said he needed more time to prepare. So the date was moved to November, to suit him. He studies the reports of the battle carefully. He reads letters from the generals. He plans to walk over the battlefield himself before he speaks, so that every detail is right.

[long-pause]

{img:81-lincoln-writing-by-window-evening}

President Lincoln was only invited to say a few appropriate remarks at Gettysburg.

[pause]

On the second of November, David Wills writes another letter. You might think the President would be the main speaker. He wasn't. It goes to the President of the United States, Abraham Lincoln. He invites Lincoln to come to Gettysburg, and, after Mr. Everett's speech, to say a few appropriate remarks. The committee doesn't really expect him to come. He is very busy, and the invitation is rather late. But Lincoln accepts. In Gettysburg, the news causes great excitement. The town begins to prepare. Flags and bunting are hung from the windows. A wooden platform is built at the new cemetery. Wills invites the President to stay in his own house. Lincoln is fifty-four years old, very tall and thin, with a lined face and deep-set grey eyes. He has been President for nearly three years, through the hardest time in his country's history. He has been asked for only a few words. So he decides to say only a few, but to choose them very carefully.

[long-pause]

{img:82-presidential-train-countryside-november}

In Washington, the President folds his sheet of paper and puts it in his coat pocket. Come with me now to a cool November day in Gettysburg.

[pause]

Lincoln's train journey to Gettysburg took about six hours.

[pause]

On Wednesday, the eighteenth of November, 1863, a special train leaves Washington at noon, heading north. You'd sit by the window and watch the fields roll past. It has four carriages. In one of them, sitting by the window, is President Lincoln. With him are his two private secretaries, John Nicolay and John Hay. There are a few members of his cabinet, some foreign diplomats, a military band and a guard of soldiers. The journey takes about six hours. The train rolls north through the Maryland countryside, past bare brown fields and leafless woods. Little farms send smoke up from their chimneys. At a place called Hanover Junction, it changes tracks and turns west, along a small branch line. At the stations along the way, people gather to see the President pass. At Hanover, a crowd calls out for Lincoln to speak. He steps onto the platform at the back of the carriage and says a few friendly words. A little girl hands him some flowers. He bends down to thank her.

[long-pause]

{img:83-dinner-table-lamplight-guests-1863}

So many visitors came to Gettysburg for the ceremony that some walked the streets all night.

[pause]

The train arrives at Gettysburg at dusk. You'd hear voices in the streets long after midnight. The little station is crowded with people, waiting in the cold. As the President steps down, tall and dark in his black coat and tall black hat, a murmur goes through the crowd. David Wills is there to meet him, with Edward Everett. They walk together up Carlisle Street to the Diamond, a few hundred yards away, with the crowd following behind. That evening, Mrs. Wills gives a dinner for about two dozen guests, in the warm, lamplit dining room. The President is quiet, but friendly. He listens more than he talks. When he does speak, he often tells a story from his young days in Illinois, and the others laugh. Outside, the town is overflowing. Thousands of visitors have come, many more than the town can hold. People sleep in churches and parlours, on floors and in chairs. Some can't find anywhere at all, and walk the streets all night.

[long-pause]

{img:84-crowd-serenade-house-night-band}

When a crowd asked Lincoln for a speech the night before, he joked his way out of it.

[pause]

After dinner, a crowd gathers outside the Wills house with a band. You'd hear the crowd laughing under the window. They begin to serenade the President. They play patriotic songs, and then they call out for Lincoln to speak. At last he comes to the door. He thanks them for coming. Then he tells them, with a smile, that he has no speech ready tonight. In his position, he says, it's rather important not to say any foolish things. A voice in the crowd calls out: If you can help it! And the President answers. It very often happens, he says, that the only way to help it's to say nothing at all. The crowd laughs and cheers, and lets him go back inside. Later, he goes up to his room on the second floor. It has a large bed, a fireplace and a small writing table. He sits down by the lamp with his sheet of paper. He changes a word here and there, and reads it over again.

[long-pause]

{img:85-telegram-reading-candlelight}

The night before his speech, Lincoln received a telegram that his young son was feeling better.

[pause]

Before he goes to bed, a telegram arrives from Washington. You can picture him reading it twice, by the lamp. His young son, Tad, has been ill with a fever. Lincoln has been worried about him. His wife Mary was very upset that he should leave home at such a time. Only a year and a half earlier, another of their sons, Willie, passed away of a fever, at the age of eleven. Lincoln still wears a black band on his hat in Willie's memory. But the telegram brings good news. Tad is better. Lincoln reads it, folds it up and puts it away. Then he puts out the lamp and lies down to sleep. Outside, the town is still awake late into the night. People walk through the streets, talking and singing. It's a cold, clear November night, and the stars are bright. On Cemetery Hill, Elizabeth Thorn rocks her baby beside the stove. Can you imagine his relief? And beside the gatehouse, the soldiers rest in their long curved rows, under the stars.

[long-pause]

{img:86-lincoln-on-horseback-procession-street}

Lincoln rode to the cemetery on a horse so small that his feet nearly touched the ground.

[pause]

Thursday, the nineteenth of November, dawns cool and grey. You'd probably smile at the sight. Then, as the morning goes on, the clouds clear away and the sun comes out. It's a beautiful late autumn day, mild and still. At about ten o'clock, the procession forms in the Diamond. There are soldiers and bands, governors and generals, members of Congress and wounded veterans. The President comes out in his black suit, white gloves and tall black hat. He mounts a horse that has been brought for him. He is so tall, and the horse is so small, that his feet nearly touch the ground. The procession moves slowly along Baltimore Street toward Cemetery Hill. The people line the pavements and stand on their doorsteps. Tillie Pierce stands outside her house with her family. The President rides past so close that she could almost touch him. At the top of the hill, perhaps fifteen thousand people stand around the wooden platform, among the fresh graves.

[long-pause]

{img:87-elderly-orator-speaking-crowd}

Edward Everett spoke for two hours at Gettysburg, without any notes.

[pause]

The ceremony begins with music and a long prayer. You'd need a comfortable place to stand. Then Edward Everett rises to speak. He speaks for two hours, without notes, in a rich and beautiful voice that carries over the crowd. He tells the whole story of the battle, day by day. He describes the first shots on the ridge west of town. He describes the long grey lines crossing the fields on the third day. He speaks of the ancient Greeks, and how they honoured their dead after their great battles. He speaks of the meaning of the war, and the future of the country. It's a fine speech, and the crowd listens patiently. But it's very long. The people standing at the back grow tired. Some of them wander off to look at the graves and the battlefield. When Everett sits down at last, a choir sings a hymn. Then Ward Lamon, the President's old friend, stands and announces: The President of the United States.

[long-pause]

{img:88-lincoln-standing-platform-paper-crowd}

The Gettysburg Address took about two minutes and had only about two hundred and seventy words.

[pause]

Abraham Lincoln rises slowly from his chair. You might think a famous speech must be long. This one was very short. He puts on his spectacles. He takes the sheet of paper from his coat pocket and steps to the edge of the platform. The crowd grows quiet. He speaks in a high, clear voice, with the country accent of Kentucky and Indiana, where he grew up. He begins: Four score and seven years ago, our fathers brought forth on this continent a new nation, conceived in liberty. He says the world will little note nor long remember what is said here, but it can never forget what the soldiers did here. And he ends with the hope that government of the people, by the people, for the people, shall not perish from the earth. Then it's over. The whole speech has taken about two minutes. Some people didn't even realise he had begun before he finished. The photographer, still setting up his camera, missed the moment entirely. Then the applause comes, warm and long.

[long-pause]

{img:89-lincoln-reading-letter-train-window}

Everett wrote to Lincoln that he wished he had come as close to the point in two hours as Lincoln did in two minutes.

[pause]

Lincoln himself isn't sure the speech was a success. You'll like this one, I think. He tells a friend afterwards that he is afraid it fell flat. But the next day, Edward Everett writes him a letter. He'd be glad, he says, if he could flatter himself that he came as near to the central idea of the occasion in two hours as Lincoln did in two minutes. Lincoln writes back kindly. He is glad, he says, that what little he said wasn't entirely a failure. That evening, old John Burns walks arm in arm with the President to a church service. Then Lincoln goes back to the station and takes the train home. He is tired, and he doesn't feel well. On the way home, he lies down with a damp cloth over his eyes. A few days later, it turns out he has caught a mild form of smallpox. He spends several weeks resting in bed. Five copies of the speech in his own handwriting still exist today.

[long-pause]

{img:90-older-woman-writing-memoir-parlour}

The train rolls south through the darkness, past the sleeping farms. Behind it, Gettysburg settles down to rest. So what became of the town, and of the people you've met tonight?

[pause]

Tillie Pierce grew up, married a lawyer, and wrote down her memories more than twenty years later.

[pause]

The war went on for another year and a half after that November day. You can picture her, older now, writing by lamplight. It ended in the spring of 1865, when General Lee surrendered to General Grant at a little village in Virginia called Appomattox Court House. Slavery was ended throughout the United States. Just a few days later, President Lincoln lost his life in Washington, and the whole country mourned him. The people of Gettysburg went on with their lives. Tillie Pierce grew up and married a lawyer named Horace Alleman. She moved to the town of Selinsgrove, in central Pennsylvania. In 1889, she wrote down her memories of the battle in her small book. She lived until 1914, and she never forgot those days. Imagine her in her parlour, a grey-haired woman, writing by the lamp. She remembers the girls singing on the corner, and the soldiers at the well. And she writes it all down, so that we can still read it today.

[long-pause]

{img:91-old-gatehouse-couple-autumn-evening}

Elizabeth Thorn cared for the cemetery for many years, and she is buried on the hill she looked after.

[pause]

Elizabeth Thorn's husband, Peter, came home safely from the war. If you visit, you can stand beside her grave on that same hill. Together they looked after the cemetery for many more years. Elizabeth lived to be seventy-five. She is buried in Evergreen Cemetery, on the hill she cared for so long. Today, there's a statue of her near the gatehouse. It shows her as she was in that summer, a young woman expecting a baby, looking out over the hill. John Burns became a hero. People came from far away to shake his hand, and he enjoyed it very much. Professor Jacobs wrote one of the first books about the battle, using his weather notes. Sallie Myers married and later wrote about her memories too. Hettie Shriver's husband George never came home. But Hettie and her girls lived on. The Weikert farm, the Leister house and the Trostle barn all stood. And every summer, the cherries ripened again in the orchards, as if nothing had changed.

[long-pause]

{img:92-veterans-reunion-1913-handshake}

Fifty years after the battle, old soldiers from both sides shook hands across the same stone wall.

[pause]

Fifty years after the battle, in the summer of 1913, something remarkable happened at Gettysburg. You'd see old men in faded uniforms, leaning on canes. More than fifty thousand old soldiers came back to the battlefield, from both North and South. They came for a great reunion. Most of them were in their seventies now, white-haired and stooped. Some came with canes. Some came with their grandchildren. They camped together in a huge town of tents, on the fields where they had once faced each other. They ate together in great dining tents, and told each other stories. On the anniversary of the third day, the old men who had made the long march walked slowly across the fields once more. They walked toward the stone wall on Cemetery Ridge. Waiting on the other side of the wall were the old soldiers who had held it. This time, they reached across the wall, and shook hands. There were photographs, and there were tears. Many of them said it was the best day of their lives.

[long-pause]

{img:93-gettysburg-battlefield-today-monuments}

There are more than thirteen hundred monuments on the battlefield at Gettysburg today.

[pause]

Today, the battlefield of Gettysburg is a national park. You could walk for days and still not see them all. The fields and ridges and woods look very much as they did in 1863. The farms are still there. There's the Trostle barn, the Weikert farmhouse and the little white house of the widow Leister. The stone walls still run along Cemetery Ridge. The fences have been rebuilt in the old style. In summer, wheat still grows in the fields between the ridges. All over the battlefield, there are monuments. There are more than thirteen hundred of them. There are statues of generals on horseback and granite soldiers standing on guard. There are stone towers and simple markers. Each regiment has its own, placed where its men stood. On Little Round Top, the boulders still lie among the trees. On the southern slope, where Chamberlain's men stood, there's a small, simple monument to the 20th Maine. Visitors sit on the rocks at the top and watch the sun go down behind the South Mountains.

[long-pause]

{img:94-national-cemetery-curved-rows-trees-evening}

Every year on the nineteenth of November, people gather in Gettysburg to read Lincoln's words aloud.

[pause]

The town of Gettysburg is still a small town, with about seven thousand people. If you're there, you'll hear those short words again, read out slowly. Many of the old buildings are still there. The Wills house on the Diamond is now a museum. Visitors can see the room where Lincoln stayed. The Shriver house on Baltimore Street has been restored, just as it was in 1863. The little house where Jennie Wade was baking bread still stands. And on the hill, the old gatehouse of Evergreen Cemetery is still there, with its arched passage. The Soldiers' National Cemetery is a peaceful place. The graves lie in their great curved rows, just as William Saunders planned them, under tall old trees. The small stones are all the same, plain and low. In the middle stands a tall column with a statue of Liberty on top. Near the entrance, there's a bronze tablet with Lincoln's words. Would you like to stand there one day and listen? And every year, on the nineteenth of November, people gather here, and someone reads them aloud.

[long-pause]

{img:95-battlefield-dusk-deer-fields-fireflies}

On summer nights, fireflies still rise over the fields at Gettysburg, just as they did in 1863.

[pause]

In the evening, when the visitors have gone, the battlefield grows quiet. Close your eyes, and you'll see them too. The light goes golden over the fields and the ridges, and then fades slowly to grey. Deer come out of the woods to graze in the meadows. They step carefully between the monuments, lifting their heads now and then to listen. The swallows swoop low over the wheat. In the farmhouses along the ridge, lights come on in the windows. Somewhere, a dog barks once and is quiet. And on summer nights, the fireflies rise out of the long grass in their thousands. They drift slowly above the fields, blinking on and off, as they did on those July evenings in 1863. Tillie Pierce once watched them from her front step on Baltimore Street. Elizabeth Thorn saw them from her gatehouse window. Now they float over the stone walls and the quiet rows on the hill. If you stood there tonight, you'd hear only crickets, and the wind in the grass.

[long-pause]

And so we say goodbye to Gettysburg, a small town among the orchards, under a sky full of stars.

[pause]

On Baltimore Street, the old brick houses stand quiet in the dark. The cupola on Seminary Ridge is only a pale shape against the night. Up on the hill, the gatehouse arch is still, and the curved rows lie peacefully under the old trees. Out in the fields, the wheat bends softly in the warm wind. A deer steps out from the woods, and the fireflies blink slowly above the grass.

[pause]

Are you still listening? There's another gentle story from history, waiting on the screen for you. And if you found some rest along these quiet roads, you can subscribe, so the next one finds you easily.

[pause]

Let the ten roads lead you home now. The lamp is out, the step is empty, and the night is soft and still.

[pause]

Sleep well, and good night.
