In the summer of 1863, a quiet farming town in southern Pennsylvania found itself at the centre of the American Civil War. Its name was Gettysburg. For three days in July, two great armies fought on the hills around it. Afterwards, its people cared for thousands of wounded men, and that November, a president came to speak a few short words. In this story, we will follow the town through that year.

In 1863, Gettysburg was a small country town of about two thousand four hundred people. Gettysburg stood in the rolling farmland of southern Pennsylvania, a few miles north of the Maryland border. In spring, the apple orchards on the hillsides blossomed pink and white. Fields of young wheat and corn stretched along the roads. Stone barns and wooden fences divided the land into neat farms. The town itself was modest. Its houses were built of brick and wood along a few straight streets. One or two church spires rose above the rooftops. About two thousand four hundred people lived there, fewer than attend many large schools today. The town had been laid out in the 1780s by a man named James Gettys. He divided the land into plots and sold them to settlers, and the town kept his name. For most of its history, Gettysburg was not a place where great events happened. It was a market town, where farmers sold their produce and bought what they needed. Yet its quiet appearance hid one important feature, in the roads that led into it.

[long-pause]

{img:02-ten-roads-countryside-aerial}

Ten roads met at Gettysburg, like the spokes of a wheel meeting at its hub. For a small town, Gettysburg was unusually well connected. Roads arrived from almost every direction. The Chambersburg Pike came in from the mountains to the west. Other roads led south toward Baltimore, and north and east toward Harrisburg, York and Hanover. Smaller roads ran south to Emmitsburg and Taneytown in Maryland. Farmers used these roads to bring their produce to market. Merchants sent goods out along them. Stagecoaches and wagons passed through every day, raising dust in dry weather. In 1858, a small railway line also arrived from Hanover in the east. In peaceful times, this network simply made Gettysburg a useful place to trade. In wartime, it meant something very different. An army on the move needed roads for its men, its wagons and its guns. A town where ten roads met was a natural place for marching columns to come together. Nobody planned a battle there. Yet the map itself made it likely that armies crossing the region would meet near this crossroads.

[long-pause]

{img:03-seminary-ridge-cupola-view}

From the cupola of the Lutheran seminary, a person could see for miles across the countryside. The land around Gettysburg rose and fell in long, low ridges. West of the town stood a Lutheran seminary, a college where young men trained to become ministers. It was a fine brick building with a small white cupola on its roof. The ridge it stood on became known as Seminary Ridge. South of the town was a hill with a cemetery, called Cemetery Hill. From there, a long and gentle ridge ran south for about two miles. At its far end rose two rocky, wooded hills, known as the Round Tops. Further away, the blue line of the South Mountains marked the western horizon. To the people of Gettysburg, these were simply familiar parts of the landscape. Students walked along the ridges. Families visited the cemetery on Sundays. Farmers worked the fields between them. To an army, however, ridges and hills offered height, cover and a view of approaching enemies. In the summer of 1863, the quiet shapes of the land would decide where soldiers stood and where they fought.

[long-pause]

{img:04-german-family-farmhouse-porch-evening}

Many families in Gettysburg had German roots, and some older residents still spoke German at home. Most people in the town were farmers, craftsmen and shopkeepers. There were also teachers, ministers, doctors and lawyers. Many families had arrived from Germany, Scotland or Ireland two or three generations earlier. German names were common, such as Weikert, Trostle and Shriver. Some older residents still spoke a local form of German by the stove in the evening. This Pennsylvania German mixed old country words with English ones. It was a living reminder of the settlers who had first farmed these valleys. Church life was central to the community. On Sunday mornings, bells rang out across the rooftops, calling families to worship. In a town of this size, people knew their neighbours well. News travelled quickly, whether it concerned a wedding, an illness or a new bonnet seen in church. Life followed the rhythm of the seasons and the working week. In many ways, Gettysburg was an ordinary American town, much like hundreds of others. That ordinariness is part of what makes its later story so striking.

[long-pause]

{img:05-diamond-square-courthouse-morning}

The central square of Gettysburg was known to everyone as the Diamond. At the heart of the town lay an open square where the main roads met. Local people called it the Diamond, a name used for town squares in several parts of Pennsylvania. In the mornings, it was the busiest place in Gettysburg. Farmers' wagons stood around it, loaded with butter, eggs, potatoes, cabbages and chickens in wooden crates. Shopkeepers opened their doors and set goods out on the pavement. Men in dark coats and hats gathered on the corners to read newspapers and talk about the weather and the crops. Gettysburg was the county seat of Adams County. It therefore had a courthouse, a jail, a bank, several hotels and taverns, a newspaper office and many shops. On court days, lawyers hurried across the square with papers under their arms. Farmers came to pay taxes or settle disputes. The Diamond was the town's meeting place, market and news centre all at once. By 1863, nearly every conversation there turned to the war.

[long-pause]

{img:06-newspaper-reading-hotel-lobby-lamp}

For two years, most people in Gettysburg knew the war mainly from newspapers and letters. The Civil War had begun in the spring of 1861. Eleven Southern states had left the Union and formed the Confederate States of America. The government in Washington, under President Abraham Lincoln, refused to accept their departure. Since then, great battles had been fought in Virginia, Tennessee, Maryland and Mississippi. Hundreds of thousands of young men had gone to fight on both sides. In Gettysburg, people followed events closely through newspapers. Men read them aloud in hotel lobbies and stores. They argued about generals and politicians. Some pinned maps to the walls and marked the movements of the armies. Letters from soldiers brought the war closer, with news of marches, camps and losses. Yet for most residents, the fighting still felt distant. The great armies had clashed far to the south. The war was something read about by lamplight in the evening. That sense of distance was common across the North. Few towns imagined that the war might one day arrive in their own streets.

[long-pause]

{img:07-recruitment-young-men-train-station}

When the young men of Gettysburg left for the army, a brass band played them off at the station. Like towns across the North, Gettysburg sent many of its young men to war. They marched away in new blue uniforms, with flags flying and bands playing. Mothers, sisters and sweethearts waved from the station platform until the train disappeared. By 1863, some had already come home wounded or ill. Others had not come home at all. In a small town, everyone knew which families had lost a son or a husband. Those who stayed behind supported the war effort in practical ways. Women knitted socks and rolled bandages for the soldiers. Churches held prayer meetings and collected supplies. Then people returned to their own work, fields and households. Gettysburg lay only about ten miles from the Maryland border. More than once, rumours spread that Confederate cavalry might ride north. Each time, families hid their horses and valuables and waited anxiously. Each time, the danger seemed to pass. These alarms became almost routine. Few expected that the next warning would be real.

[long-pause]

{img:08-brick-townhouse-street-green-shutters}

Houses in Gettysburg were often built right up against the pavement, with long gardens behind them. In Gettysburg, as in many Pennsylvania towns, houses usually stood directly on the street. The front door opened straight onto the pavement. Behind the house, a long, narrow garden stretched back from the road. Many homes were built of red brick, two storeys high, with green shutters and small attic windows. Others were of painted wood or grey stone. At the back stood a well or pump, a woodshed and often a small stable for a horse and a cow. Many families also kept pigs and chickens. Every household had a vegetable garden, with beans, cabbages, onions and potatoes in neat rows. This arrangement meant that town families lived partly like farmers. In the evening, cows lowed and hens settled behind the brick houses of the main streets. Town and countryside were never far apart. The layout also mattered later. During the battle, soldiers moved through these yards, gardens and alleys, and the walls of the houses became part of the fighting.

[long-pause]

{img:09-mother-cookstove-breakfast-children}

A Gettysburg breakfast could include ham, sausage, fried potatoes, eggs and apple butter, all at once. Domestic life in a Pennsylvania town began early. Women rose before dawn to light the fire in the iron cookstove. Water was fetched from the pump in the yard, and the kettle was set to boil. Breakfast was a substantial meal. It might include fried ham or sausage, fried potatoes, eggs, bread and butter. Apple butter, thick and dark, was a regional favourite, made by slowly cooking apples for many hours. Strong coffee was usual, at least before wartime shortages made it expensive. Children were woken, washed and dressed. Older girls helped with cooking and younger children. Boys fed the chickens, milked the cow and carried in wood. Families often ate together and said grace before the meal. Such breakfasts reflected the demands of daily life. Most people worked on their feet from dawn until dark, whether in workshops, kitchens or fields. A heavy morning meal was simply a practical necessity, not a luxury.

[long-pause]

{img:10-carriage-workshop-painter-gold-lines}

Gettysburg was known for its carriages, and painters added thin gold lines to them by hand. Many men in Gettysburg worked at a trade. There were blacksmiths, harness makers, tanners, shoemakers, carpenters and masons. There were tailors, hatters, bakers and butchers. Yet the town's best-known industry was carriage making. Several carriage works operated along Chambersburg Street, York Street and elsewhere. Wheelwrights shaped spokes and rims from seasoned hickory. Smiths fitted iron tyres onto the wheels while the metal was still hot, so that it shrank tightly as it cooled. Painters worked in separate rooms, kept free of dust. They applied layer after layer of glossy paint. Then, with very fine brushes, they added thin decorative lines of gold or red along the bodies and wheels. This work required a steady hand and great patience. Before the war, Gettysburg carriages were sold widely, including in the South. The war disrupted that trade. Yet the industry showed how skilled craftsmanship could give even a small town a wide reputation.

[long-pause]

{img:11-washday-iron-kettle-backyard-line}

In 1863, Monday was washing day, and doing the laundry took the whole day. Housework in the 1860s followed a regular weekly pattern. Monday was washing day. A large iron kettle of water was heated over a fire in the yard. Clothes were scrubbed on a washboard, rinsed, wrung out by hand and hung on a line to dry. The work was long and physically heavy. Tuesday was usually ironing day. Heavy irons were heated on the stove and used in turn while the others warmed again. Other days brought baking, mending, cleaning, churning butter and garden work. Summer and autumn were for preserving food. Cherries and peaches went into jars. Apples were sliced and dried. Cabbage was shredded and packed into stone crocks to ferment into sauerkraut, a favourite in German Pennsylvania. By autumn, cellar shelves were full. Diaries kept by women of the period describe this routine in detail. They show how much of a household's comfort depended on work that was rarely recorded in official histories.

[long-pause]

{img:12-general-store-counter-1863}

General stores in the 1860s sold patent medicines that promised to cure almost every illness. The general store was one of the most important places in any small town. It sold coffee, tea, sugar, salt, molasses and spices. It stocked cloth, thread, buttons, needles and pins. There were pots and pans, lamp oil, candles, soap and nails. On high shelves stood rows of patent medicines in small glass bottles. Their labels promised cures for almost every complaint, from headaches to weak nerves. Many contained little more than sugar, alcohol or opium. There were no laws yet requiring honest labels. Storekeepers weighed goods on brass scales and recorded purchases in large ledgers. Many families paid only once a month or after the harvest. Credit was a normal part of rural life. During the war, some goods became scarce and expensive, especially coffee and cotton cloth. People mixed roasted grain into their coffee to make it last. Yet in a farming district like Adams County, food itself was generally plentiful.

[long-pause]

{img:13-church-sunday-families-1863}

A town of only 2,400 people had churches for Lutherans, Presbyterians, Methodists, Catholics and more. Religion was central to life in Gettysburg. For a small town, it had a remarkable number of churches. There were Lutheran, Presbyterian, Methodist, Catholic, German Reformed and Episcopal congregations. There was also a church for the town's Black community, whose families had lived in Gettysburg for generations. Saturday evening was often bath night. Family members took turns in a tin tub in front of the kitchen stove, with water heated on the fire. On Sunday, people dressed in their best clothes and walked to church. After the service, neighbours talked outside in the sunshine. Then families went home to a large Sunday dinner, often roast chicken, potatoes and pie. The afternoon was kept for rest. Some visited relatives, some read the Bible aloud, and some walked out along the country roads. Work was avoided whenever possible. These church buildings later took on another role. During and after the battle, many of them were turned into hospitals.

[long-pause]

{img:14-children-swimming-creek-summer-trees}

In summer, the boys of Gettysburg went swimming in Rock Creek, just east of town. Most children in Gettysburg attended school for at least a few years. Public schools served younger pupils. Families who could afford it sent children to private academies. There was a seminary for young ladies, where girls studied grammar and French. On the hill north of town stood a college, where young men studied Latin and Greek. Outside school, children played in the streets and fields. Boys played ball and marbles, fished in the creeks and climbed trees in the orchards. Girls played with dolls, skipped rope and picked wildflowers along the lanes. In winter, there was sledding on the snowy hills. In summer, Rock Creek on the east side of town was a favourite swimming place. Its water ran cool and brown beneath the trees. Memoirs written by people who grew up in Gettysburg often recall these simple pleasures. They describe a small-town childhood that felt safe and ordinary, until the summer of 1863 changed it forever.

[long-pause]

{img:15-market-day-wagons-sunbonnets-diamond}

On market days, farm families from across Adams County filled the streets of Gettysburg. Market days brought the surrounding countryside into town. Early in the morning, farm wagons rolled in along the roads, one after another. Soon the Diamond was crowded with them. Farm wives sat on wagon seats in sunbonnets, with baskets of eggs, crocks of butter and bunches of herbs at their feet. Townswomen moved from wagon to wagon, choosing and bargaining. The produce changed with the seasons. June brought strawberries. August brought sweet corn and tomatoes. Autumn brought apples, cider and pumpkins. Live chickens, smoked hams, cheeses and honey in the comb were also for sale. Boys sold cherries by the quart. By midday, many wagons were empty. Farmers went to the taverns for dinner and talked about the weather and the price of wheat. Then they drove home in the afternoon sun. Markets like this tied the town and countryside together. They were the economic heartbeat of a farming county.

[long-pause]

{img:16-front-step-evening-neighbours}

On summer evenings, much of Gettysburg seemed to sit outside on its front steps. In summer, the town smelled strongly of the countryside. The scent of cut hay drifted in from nearby meadows. Kitchens smelled of baking bread. The tanneries near the creek gave off a sharper smell that few people enjoyed. In the evenings, families gathered in their parlours. Fathers read newspapers aloud by the light of oil lamps. Mothers knitted or sewed. Some families owned a parlour organ, and everyone sang together. On warm nights, however, people went outside. They sat on the steps in front of their houses and called to neighbours across the street. Swallows wheeled above the rooftops. Fireflies rose from the gardens. The long golden light faded behind the ridge to the west. Then lamps went out one by one, and the town went to sleep. Such evenings were common in small American towns before electric light and air conditioning. They made the street itself into a shared living room for the whole neighbourhood.

[long-pause]

{img:17-tillie-pierce-girl-1863}

Much of what is known about everyday life in Gettysburg in 1863 comes from a fifteen-year-old girl. Matilda Pierce, known as Tillie, lived on Baltimore Street in the middle of town. She was fifteen in the spring of 1863. Photographs show a serious young woman with dark hair parted in the middle and pulled neatly back. She was curious and observant. In 1889, she published a book about that summer. Its title was At Gettysburg, or What a Girl Saw and Heard of the Battle. In it, she described her family, her neighbours, the soldiers and the long days that followed the fighting. Her account is one of the best-known civilian memories of the battle. Historians still use it today. Like all memoirs written many years later, it must be read with some care. Memories fade and change over time. Yet her book preserves details that official reports ignored. It shows the battle not from a general's headquarters, but from a family kitchen and a front window. That perspective has made it valuable for more than a century.

[long-pause]

{img:18-butcher-shop-family-brick-house}

Tillie Pierce's father was the town butcher, and the family lived above the shop on Baltimore Street. James Pierce was a butcher. His family lived in a brick house at the corner of Baltimore Street and Breckenridge Street. It stood at the southern end of town, on the road leading out toward the cemetery on the hill. The shop occupied the front of the house. It had a scrubbed wooden counter, a heavy chopping block and hooks along the ceiling. Butchers in small towns were well known to everyone. Customers included ministers' wives, widows and students from the college. Children often helped in family businesses, wrapping meat, taking money and counting change. The Pierce house still stands today on Baltimore Street. It has been restored and is open to visitors. Its location placed the family close to some of the most dramatic events of the battle. Baltimore Street became a key route for retreating and advancing soldiers. An ordinary family shop therefore found itself on one of the busiest roads in the war's most famous battle.

[long-pause]

{img:19-young-ladies-seminary-classroom-desks}

Girls in Gettysburg could attend a seminary for young ladies, studying grammar, history and French. Education for girls was expanding in mid-nineteenth-century America. In Gettysburg, a young ladies' seminary run by Mrs Eyster offered lessons a few streets from the Diamond. Pupils studied reading, grammar, history and geography. They learned arithmetic and French, with some music and drawing. They sat in rows at wooden desks, in long dresses with braided hair. When called upon, they stood and recited their lessons aloud. Recitation was the standard teaching method of the time. Pupils memorised passages and repeated them before the class. Tillie Pierce attended this seminary. In her memoir, she recalled that she and her classmates were in school on the afternoon when the first Confederate soldiers appeared in town. Outside school, girls helped with housework, washing and mending. They fed chickens, collected eggs and ran errands. Female seminaries like this one were common in Northern towns. They prepared girls for running households, and sometimes for teaching, one of the few professions then open to women.

[long-pause]

{img:20-baltimore-street-1863-houses}

Baltimore Street ran south from the town square and became the road to the city of Baltimore. Baltimore Street was one of the main streets of Gettysburg. It ran south from the Diamond past rows of brick houses and shops. Then it climbed out of town toward Cemetery Hill. Beyond the cemetery, it became the Baltimore Pike, a road leading all the way to Baltimore, about fifty miles away. Every day, wagons, carriages and riders passed along it. Farmers came into town, and travellers went out. Peddlers walked by with pots and pans tied to their packs. Families living along the street could watch the traffic from their windows. The Pierce family was one of them. Tillie had an older sister and two older brothers. One brother, James, was serving in the army. The other, William, worked with their father. During the battle, Baltimore Street lay along a crucial line between the armies. Houses at its southern end stood almost on the front line. Some still carry bullet marks in their brick walls today.

[long-pause]

{img:21-shriver-house-mother-daughters}

One house on Baltimore Street had a saloon and a bowling alley built into its basement. George Shriver had built a new brick house on Baltimore Street shortly before the war. In the basement, he installed a saloon and a two-lane bowling alley, hoping they would provide a good income. Then he joined the army. His wife, Henrietta, known as Hettie, remained in the house with their two young daughters. Hettie's parents, the Weikerts, owned a farm a few miles south of town. It lay on the Taneytown Road, near the foot of the hill called Little Round Top. During the battle, Hettie took her daughters to her parents' farm, believing it would be safer. Tillie Pierce went with them. The decision turned out to be mistaken, because the farm lay close to some of the heaviest fighting. Meanwhile, Confederate sharpshooters occupied the Shriver house in town. The house has since been restored and is open to the public. Its basement bowling alley is a reminder of ordinary hopes interrupted by war.

[long-pause]

{img:22-young-seamstress-sewing-by-window}

Jennie Wade, the only civilian killed during the battle, was probably never called Jennie in her lifetime. Mary Virginia Wade was twenty years old in 1863. Her family and friends called her Ginnie. The name Jennie seems to have come from newspapers after her death, and it has remained ever since. She lived with her mother and worked as a seamstress, sewing clothes for other families. Like many young women in town, she had a sweetheart in the army. His name was Johnston Skelly, known as Jack, and they had grown up together in Gettysburg. During the battle, Ginnie was staying at her sister's house on Baltimore Street, helping her sister, who had just given birth. On the morning of the third of July, a stray bullet passed through two doors and struck her while she was baking bread. She was the only civilian known to have been killed directly by the fighting. Her story became one of the best-known personal stories of the battle. The house where she died is now a museum.

[long-pause]

{img:23-cemetery-hill-path-girls-sunday-view}

In the 1860s, young people in Gettysburg often walked in the cemetery on Sunday afternoons. To modern ears, a walk in a cemetery may sound unusual. Yet in the mid-nineteenth century, many American towns laid out cemeteries like parks. These rural cemeteries had trees, lawns and winding paths. People visited them to walk, rest and enjoy the view. Evergreen Cemetery in Gettysburg was one of these. On fine Sunday afternoons, young people walked out along the Baltimore Pike to its gates. They strolled along shady paths and read the names on the stones. From the top of the hill, they could see the whole town, with its church spires and rooftops. They could also see the long ridges running south and west. The rural cemetery movement reflected new ideas about death and memory. Graves were placed in peaceful natural settings rather than crowded churchyards. In 1863, Cemetery Hill gained a very different meaning. Its height made it one of the most important positions on the entire battlefield.

[long-pause]

{img:24-girl-window-candle-night}

Before July 1863, the war still felt far away to many families in Gettysburg. Summer evenings on the streets of Gettysburg were lively and familiar. Children played until it was too dark to see. Older people sat on front steps, fanning themselves and talking. Young women walked arm in arm along the pavements. Fiddles played on doorsteps, and dogs barked in the yards. Almost every family had a relative or friend in the army. People knew that great battles had been fought far to the south. Yet on calm evenings, the war seemed distant. Swallows wheeled over the rooftops. Honeysuckle scented the garden walls. Candles were carried up the stairs at bedtime. Through open windows came the sound of crickets and a horse moving slowly home along the street. Civilian diaries and memoirs often describe this sense of normal life just before the battle. They show how suddenly ordinary routines could be broken. In late June 1863, rumours of approaching armies grew stronger. Within days, the quiet town would be transformed.

[long-pause]

{img:25-pennsylvania-college-hill}

The college on the hill north of Gettysburg, founded in 1832, still teaches students today. On the northern edge of Gettysburg stood Pennsylvania College. It had been founded in 1832 by Lutherans who wanted a college for their region. Its main building was a handsome brick structure with tall white columns and a small cupola on the roof. In 1863, around a hundred and fifty young men studied there. Some lived in the college building, while others lodged with families in town. They studied Latin, Greek, mathematics and natural science. Debating societies met in the evenings to argue about the great questions of the day. Students played an active part in town life. When Confederate forces threatened Pennsylvania in 1863, some students joined a local emergency regiment. During the battle, the college building was used as a hospital. Today, the institution is called Gettysburg College. Its original main building, now known as Pennsylvania Hall, still stands on the same rise among the trees. It is one of the oldest buildings associated with the battle.

[long-pause]

{img:26-professor-thermometer-garden}

A professor in Gettysburg recorded the weather three times a day, right through the battle. Michael Jacobs taught mathematics and natural science at Pennsylvania College. He was careful and methodical by nature. For years, he had kept daily weather records. At seven in the morning, two in the afternoon and nine in the evening, he noted the temperature, the wind, the clouds and any rain. He continued this habit throughout the summer of 1863, even while armies fought around the town. His notes survive. They show, for example, that the third of July was warm and humid, with temperatures in the high eighties Fahrenheit. They also record heavy rain on the fourth of July, after the battle had ended. Jacobs later wrote one of the first accounts of the battle, published within months. His weather records are still used by historians and battlefield guides. They help to explain conditions that soldiers described in letters, such as heat, thirst and mud. A quiet scientific habit thus preserved a precise record of one of history's most famous weeks.

[long-pause]

{img:27-lutheran-seminary-gardens-students-walking}

The Lutheran seminary on the ridge west of Gettysburg was founded in 1826, and it is the oldest of its kind in America. West of town, on the ridge above the fields, stood the Lutheran Theological Seminary. It had been founded in 1826 and was the oldest continuously operating Lutheran seminary in the United States. Its main building was long, brick and four storeys high, crowned by a white wooden cupola. Here, young men trained to become Lutheran ministers. They studied Hebrew, Greek and theology. They preached practice sermons to one another and received guidance from their teachers. In free hours, they walked in the gardens and woods along the ridge. The building gave its name to Seminary Ridge. On the first day of the battle, Union cavalry officers used the cupola to watch Confederate troops approaching from the west. Later, the building became a hospital for wounded soldiers of both sides. Today, the main building houses a museum about the seminary and the battle. Visitors can climb to the cupola and look out over the same landscape.

[long-pause]

{img:28-cemetery-gatehouse-brick-arch}

The gatehouse of Evergreen Cemetery had an arch in the middle and a small home on each side. Evergreen Cemetery opened on the southern edge of Gettysburg in 1854. It was a peaceful place, with trees, winding paths and white gravestones. At its entrance beside the Baltimore Pike stood a handsome brick gatehouse. An arched passage ran through the middle, wide enough for a wagon. Rooms on either side served as the home of the cemetery keeper. In 1863, the keeper was Peter Thorn. Like many local men, he had joined the army. His wife, Elizabeth, took over his duties. The gatehouse stood on Cemetery Hill, which became one of the most important positions of the battle. Union artillery and infantry gathered around it. Shells struck the area during the fighting. The building survived with damage. Today, it still stands on the same hill, beside the cemetery and close to the Soldiers' National Cemetery created after the battle. A monument nearby honours the civilian women of Gettysburg, modelled on Elizabeth Thorn.

[long-pause]

{img:29-woman-gardening-cemetery-paths}

While her husband was in the army, Elizabeth Thorn managed Evergreen Cemetery herself. Elizabeth Thorn had come to America from Germany as a young woman. In 1863, she was about thirty years old. She had three young sons and was expecting another child. Her elderly parents lived with her in the gatehouse, and her father helped with heavy work. Managing a cemetery was demanding. Elizabeth cared for the grounds, paths and gates. She kept the burial records in a large book. When someone died in town, she and her father prepared the grave and helped with the burial. After the battle, her work became overwhelming. In the heat of July, she and her father buried around a hundred soldiers in the cemetery grounds. She later said that this labour, done while pregnant, damaged her health for years. Her daughter, born that autumn, was frail. Elizabeth Thorn's story became better known in the late twentieth century. It is now one of the most cited examples of civilian endurance during and after the battle.

[long-pause]

{img:30-cemetery-ridge-farms-wheat-orchard}

A free Black farmer named Abraham Bryan owned a small farm on the ridge south of town. From Cemetery Hill, the land sloped gently in several directions. To the east rose wooded Culp's Hill. To the south, a long, low ridge ran for about two miles. It was called Cemetery Ridge. Farms lined it, as they did all around Gettysburg. Abraham Bryan, a free Black farmer, owned a small house, barn and orchard there. Pennsylvania was a free state, but Gettysburg lay close to slave-holding Maryland. Black residents knew that Confederate armies sometimes seized free Black people and sent them south into slavery. Many fled when the armies approached in 1863, and Bryan was among them. A widow named Lydia Leister lived in a small farmhouse nearby. It later became the headquarters of the Union commander, General George Meade. Further south lay the farms of the Trostle and Weikert families. Wheat grew tall in the fields, and young peaches ripened in an orchard beside the Emmitsburg Road. These farms would soon give their names to famous parts of the battlefield.

[long-pause]

{img:31-round-tops-wooded-hills-dusk}

Before the battle, the rocky valley called the Devil's Den was a popular place for picnics. At the southern end of Cemetery Ridge rose two rocky, wooded hills. The larger was Big Round Top. The smaller, closer to town, was Little Round Top. Great grey boulders were scattered over their slopes. At their feet, a small stream wound through a valley filled with enormous rocks, some as large as a house. Local people called it the Devil's Den. Despite its name, it was a favourite picnic spot. On summer Sundays, families came out by wagon. Children climbed and hid among the boulders, while parents rested in the shade. The origins of the name are uncertain. Local stories spoke of a large snake once seen there. On the second day of the battle, these peaceful places saw some of the fiercest fighting. Confederate sharpshooters took cover among the rocks of Devil's Den. Union troops fought desperately to hold Little Round Top. Today, the boulders remain much as they were, visited by many thousands of people each year.

[long-pause]

{img:32-family-reading-letter-parlour-lamp}

For most families in Gettysburg, the war arrived in the form of letters. During the Civil War, letters were the main link between soldiers and their families. Mail arrived in Gettysburg by train from Hanover. The postmaster sorted it into small wooden boxes at the post office. Mothers and wives came in regularly to ask whether anything had arrived. A letter from a son or a husband was often read aloud to the whole family in the parlour. Most letters described ordinary things. They complained about the weather and the food, which was mostly hard crackers, salt pork and coffee. They mentioned long marches, blisters and mud. They spoke of new friends in the regiment. Many asked for socks, shirts or a jar of jam from home. Letters about battles were usually shorter. Historians have collected tens of thousands of these letters. They are among the richest sources for understanding how ordinary soldiers experienced the war. Nearly all of them end in the same way, with love to the family and a hope to be home soon.

[long-pause]

{img:33-sewing-circle-women-open-windows}

Girls in Civil War towns picked old linen into soft lint, which army doctors used to dress wounds. Women across the North organised to support the soldiers. In Gettysburg, they met in parlours and churches. They sewed shirts, knitted socks and rolled bandages from old linen sheets. They packed boxes of food and clothing for the army. They raised money for hospitals with suppers and small fairs. Younger girls had a special task. They picked old linen apart into soft threads, called lint. Army doctors packed wounds with it. The work was slow and fiddly, but even small hands could do it. Nobody yet understood that used linen could carry infection. Medical science would only learn about germs in the following decades. Much of this local work fed into a large national organisation called the United States Sanitary Commission. It collected supplies from thousands of communities and sent them to the armies. Women did most of the work. Their efforts became one of the largest volunteer movements in American history up to that time.

[long-pause]

{img:34-old-man-cobbler-workshop}

John Burns, a shoemaker in his late sixties, had been told he was too old to join the army. Many older men in Gettysburg followed the war closely. One of them was John Burns. In 1863, he was about sixty-nine years old. He was short and stubborn, with white hair and firm opinions. He worked as a shoemaker and had served for some years as the town constable. As a young man, he had fought in the War of 1812 against Britain. When the Civil War began, he tried to enlist. He was rejected because of his age. According to several accounts, he complained about this to anyone who would listen. On the first day of the battle, he took his old musket and walked out to join Union soldiers west of town. He fought alongside them and was wounded several times. He survived, and became a national celebrity. President Lincoln met him when he visited Gettysburg in November 1863. A statue of John Burns now stands on the battlefield. His story shows how deeply the war could stir even those it had tried to leave behind.

[long-pause]

{img:35-quiet-farm-lane-evening-north}

For the Black families of Gettysburg, a Confederate army in Pennsylvania meant a very real danger. Gettysburg lay only a few miles from the border with Maryland, a slave state. Before the war, people escaping from slavery often passed through the area on their way north. Some local residents helped them quietly, as part of the Underground Railroad. Slave catchers also came looking for them. In 1863, Black residents of Gettysburg faced a particular threat. When Confederate forces entered Pennsylvania, they seized free Black people and sent them south into slavery. Several hundred are believed to have been taken during the campaign. Many Black families in Gettysburg therefore left town as the armies approached. They fled to safer places further north and east, or hid in the countryside. Some returned after the battle to find their homes damaged or looted. Historians have only recently given this part of the story the attention it deserves. It shows that the Gettysburg campaign held very different dangers for different people in the same town.

[long-pause]

{img:36-general-lee-grey-horse-portrait-calm}

General Robert E. Lee, the most famous commander of the South, rode a grey horse named Traveller. In May 1863, the Confederate army won a major victory at Chancellorsville in Virginia. It defeated the Union's main eastern army, the Army of the Potomac. The Confederate force was commanded by General Robert E. Lee. He was about fifty-six years old, a dignified, grey-bearded man from an old Virginia family. He was known for his calm manner and courtesy, and his soldiers admired him greatly. He rode a grey horse named Traveller, which became almost as famous as he was. After Chancellorsville, Lee decided to move north. He hoped to take the war into Union territory for the first time on a large scale. He wanted to gather supplies from Pennsylvania's rich farms. He also hoped a victory in the North might weaken the Union's will to keep fighting. In June, his army began marching through the valleys west of the mountains. Rumours of its approach spread quickly through southern Pennsylvania.

[long-pause]

{img:37-storekeeper-packing-crates-railway}

As rumours grew in June 1863, the Gettysburg bank quietly sent its money away to Philadelphia. Throughout June 1863, rumours reached Gettysburg almost daily. People said the Confederate army was moving through the valleys west of the mountains. They said it was taking horses, cattle and food from farms. Some said it was heading for Harrisburg, others for Philadelphia or Baltimore. Nobody knew for certain. Many residents took precautions. Storekeepers packed goods into crates and sent them away by train. The bank sent its money to Philadelphia for safekeeping. Farmers drove their horses over the hills to hide them in the woods. A good horse was worth a great deal, and a farm could hardly function without one. Families buried silverware in their gardens or hid it in chimneys and under floorboards. In the evenings, people stood at their gates and questioned every traveller. Each seemed to bring a different story. Diaries from the time show how exhausting this uncertainty was. Waiting for an unknown danger could be harder than facing a known one.

[long-pause]

{img:38-june-fields-wheat-golden-farmhouse}

In late June 1863, the wheat was ripening and the cherries were ready to pick around Gettysburg. Even as rumours grew, the summer went on. The wheat in the fields turned golden. Cherries ripened in the orchards, and children climbed the trees to pick them. Hay was cut and stacked in the meadows. The days were long and the evenings full of fireflies. Farmers began to plan the harvest, which normally started in early July. Many of those fields would never be harvested that year. Within days, they would be trampled by soldiers, horses and wagons. Fences would be torn down for firewood. Crops would be destroyed across thousands of acres. Accounts written by local residents often contrast these peaceful June days with what followed. They describe ordinary routines continuing almost to the last moment. Meanwhile, beyond the mountains to the west, two great armies were moving slowly north. Neither yet knew exactly where the other was. Both were drawing closer to the little crossroads town every day.

[long-pause]

{img:39-rainy-june-afternoon-street}

On the twenty-sixth of June, 1863, the first Confederate soldiers marched into Gettysburg. Friday, the twenty-sixth of June, was a grey and rainy day in Gettysburg. The streets were muddy. Children were in school. That afternoon, word spread that Confederate troops were approaching. Teachers sent pupils home. Tillie Pierce later described running through the rain and watching from her window. She saw a large body of horsemen riding in from the west, followed by columns of infantry. Many of the soldiers were dressed in worn grey and brown clothing. Some had no shoes. They belonged to a division commanded by General Jubal Early. A small force of local militia, hastily raised, had tried to stop them west of town. It was quickly scattered. Early's men occupied Gettysburg for only one night. Their visit was brief and caused little damage. Yet it was the first time most residents had seen enemy soldiers. For many, the war suddenly became real, standing in their own streets in the rain.

[long-pause]

{img:40-confederate-soldiers-march-rain-town}

General Jubal Early demanded a thousand pairs of shoes and five hundred hats from the town of Gettysburg. Once his soldiers had gathered in the Diamond, General Early sent a written demand to the town council. He asked for large quantities of flour, bacon, sugar, coffee, salt and onions. He also demanded a thousand pairs of shoes and five hundred hats. As an alternative, the town could pay five thousand dollars. The council replied that it could not meet these demands. Storekeepers had already sent most of their goods away, and the bank had removed its money. Early did not press the matter. His division was moving on toward York, and he had little time. Soldiers took what they could find in shops and cellars. Many were tired, hungry and poorly clothed. Several residents later remarked on how young some of them looked. Some even thanked people politely for a drink of water. Early's demands were not unusual. Confederate commanders made similar requests of many Pennsylvania towns during the campaign, often with more success.

[long-pause]

{img:41-horse-led-from-stable-yard-rain}

Confederate soldiers took horses from many Gettysburg families, including the Pierce family. Horses were among the most valuable things an army could seize. Cavalry needed fresh mounts, and wagons and artillery needed teams to pull them. During their brief stay in Gettysburg, Confederate soldiers took horses from many households. Tillie Pierce later recalled watching soldiers lead her family's horse out of the yard. Her father could do nothing to stop them. She remembered feeling deeply indignant, and she described the scene in her book many years later. Other families lost hams from their cellars and bread from their kitchens. That night, the soldiers camped on the edge of town. The next morning, they marched east toward York. Residents came out of their houses and compared their losses. Nobody had been hurt, and the town still stood. It had been a frightening but brief encounter. Yet the loss of horses mattered greatly in a farming community. Without them, fields could not be ploughed or harvests carried home.

[long-pause]

{img:42-townspeople-talking-diamond-square}

After the first Confederate visit, some townspeople described the soldiers as tired and rather polite. For several days after Early's departure, Gettysburg was quiet. Residents talked about little else. They shared stories about what the soldiers had said and what they had taken. One family had lost a horse, another a ham. Someone's boots had been taken from a doorstep. Some people concluded that the Confederates were not as frightening as they had feared. They described them as tired, ragged and reasonably well behaved. Others were less reassured. They had noticed the numbers and the hunger of the soldiers. Rumours continued to arrive. The main Confederate army was said to be close, just beyond the mountains. A Union army was reported moving up from Maryland. Town officials met, ministers prayed and families checked their stores of food. Everyone watched the roads more closely. In hindsight, these few quiet days were the last moments of normal life in Gettysburg for many weeks to come.

[long-pause]

{img:43-union-cavalry-riding-through-town-cheering}

When Union cavalry rode into Gettysburg on the thirtieth of June, local girls sang patriotic songs to welcome them. On the morning of Tuesday, the thirtieth of June, soldiers appeared on the roads again. This time they came from the south, along the Emmitsburg Road, and they wore blue. They were Union cavalry, around three thousand men on horseback. They rode into town in a long, dusty column, with sabres jingling and flags flying. Residents poured out of their houses to greet them. Women and girls waved handkerchiefs and cheered from the pavements. Some brought out bread, cakes and cups of water. Children ran alongside the horses. Tillie Pierce and her schoolmates stood at a street corner and sang patriotic songs as the cavalry passed. Several soldiers later recalled this welcome in their own letters and memoirs. After the anxiety of the previous week, people felt safer. Union troops were finally there. Few in town realised that this arrival meant not safety, but the beginning of a great battle around their homes.

[long-pause]

{img:44-cavalry-general-ridge-looking-west}

Union cavalry general John Buford decided to hold the ridges west of Gettysburg until the army arrived. The Union cavalry was commanded by General John Buford. He was a quiet, weathered officer of about thirty-seven, from Kentucky, with a drooping moustache and long experience. On arriving, he rode up to Seminary Ridge and looked west toward the mountains. His scouts reported a large Confederate force camped a few miles away along the Chambersburg Pike. Buford studied the roads and the ridges carefully. He understood the importance of the town's road network. Armies would come here from several directions. Whoever held the high ground nearby would have a great advantage. He therefore decided to hold the ridges west and north of town with his cavalry. His aim was to delay the Confederates until Union infantry could arrive. Historians often regard this as one of the key decisions of the battle. It helped ensure that Union forces would eventually occupy the strong positions south of town. It was a small decision, made calmly on horseback, with very large consequences.

[long-pause]

{img:45-cavalry-campfires-fields-dusk}

On the night before the battle, some Gettysburg families invited Union soldiers home for supper. On the evening of the thirtieth of June, Buford's cavalry camped on the ridges west and north of town. Their campfires flickered across the fields in the dusk. Horses grazed in the meadows. Some soldiers walked into town to buy bread or pies. A number of families invited them in for supper. Young men from New York, Indiana and Illinois sat at kitchen tables, eating ham and fried potatoes and talking about their homes. At nine o'clock, Professor Jacobs went out to read his thermometer, as he did every evening, and recorded the temperature. The night was warm and still. Stars filled the sky. Many residents went to bed with a sense of excitement rather than fear. Union soldiers were present, and the town felt protected. Accounts from that evening are calm and almost cheerful. They make the contrast with the following morning even sharper. By dawn, the first shots of the battle would be fired west of town.

[long-pause]

{img:46-army-column-marching-dusty-road}

Together, the two armies at Gettysburg had more than sixty times as many men as the town had residents. Two very large armies were converging on Gettysburg. The Union Army of the Potomac had about ninety thousand men. The Confederate Army of Northern Virginia had about seventy-five thousand. Together, they numbered more than sixty times the population of the town. On the march, each army stretched for many miles along the roads. Infantry marched in columns, rifles on their shoulders and blanket rolls across their chests. Long lines of cannons and supply wagons followed, pulled by teams of horses and mules. Herds of cattle walked with the armies to provide meat. Officers rode up and down the lines. Clouds of dust rose over the roads and settled on faces and uniforms. Soldiers of both sides often became the same pale brown colour. The logistics were enormous. Each army needed tons of food, ammunition and fodder every day. Moving such forces required careful planning, and it was impossible to keep their presence secret from the people they passed.

[long-pause]

{img:47-soldiers-crowding-farm-pump-heat}

Marching soldiers often broke ranks at farm pumps to fill their canteens. The summer of 1863 was hot. Soldiers marched fifteen to twenty miles a day, sometimes more, carrying heavy loads. Blisters and worn-out shoes were common. Thirst was a constant problem. Whenever a column passed a farm with a well or pump, men broke ranks to fill their tin canteens and splash water on their faces. Officers shouted at them to return to the line, often without much success. Farm families came out to watch. Some brought buckets of water, bread or milk. Children sat on fences, staring at the endless lines of soldiers. Many soldiers later described these moments of kindness in their letters. In the evenings, some were so exhausted that they fell asleep the moment they halted, still wearing their packs. Forced marches in heat caused many soldiers to collapse along the way. Some units arrived at Gettysburg after marching through the night. They went into battle already tired, thirsty and footsore.

[long-pause]

{img:48-evening-camp-coffee-fires-harmonica}

For Civil War soldiers, making coffee was often the most important moment of the day. In the evening, the armies camped in fields beside the roads. Soldiers stacked their rifles in small pyramids and lit fires. Then they boiled coffee in tin cups. Many veterans later said coffee mattered more to them than almost anything else. Union soldiers received a generous coffee ration, and historians have found that the word coffee appears in their diaries more often than words like rifle or war. Supper usually consisted of salt pork fried in a mess tin, with hard crackers broken into it. These crackers, known as hardtack, were so tough that soldiers joked about them constantly. Men took off their shoes and rubbed their aching feet. They talked about home, families and sweethearts. Someone might play a harmonica or fiddle. Others wrote letters by firelight. Hundreds of small fires spread across the dark fields. The scene was repeated in camps of both armies, with very similar routines on either side.

[long-pause]

{img:49-young-soldiers-faces-campfire-night}

During quiet times, Union and Confederate soldiers sometimes traded coffee for tobacco across the lines. Most soldiers on both sides were young men in their late teens or early twenties. Many were farm boys, clerks, labourers, students or craftsmen. Some had served for two years and fought in many battles. Others had never seen combat. In many ways, soldiers in blue and grey were remarkably similar. They came from similar homes, spoke the same language and sang many of the same songs. They often shared the same religious faith. During quiet periods, when armies camped near each other along rivers, soldiers sometimes called across to one another. Informal trading was common. Confederates often had tobacco, while Union soldiers had coffee. Small bundles were floated across rivers on tiny rafts. Officers usually disapproved, but the practice continued. Such exchanges reveal an important truth about the war. Soldiers could hold strong convictions and still recognise each other as fellow human beings. The next day, however, they could be fighting again.

[long-pause]

{img:50-general-meade-tent-maps}

General George Meade was given command of the Union army only three days before the battle began. On the twenty-eighth of June, 1863, President Lincoln replaced the commander of the Army of the Potomac. The new commander was General George Gordon Meade. He was forty-seven, tall, thin and serious, from Philadelphia. He wore spectacles, had a grey beard and was known for a sharp temper. His soldiers nicknamed him a goggle-eyed old snapping turtle. Yet he was careful, sensible and experienced. He was also defending his home state. According to his own account, he was woken in his tent in the middle of the night by an officer from Washington. At first, he thought he was being arrested. Instead, he was handed command of the entire army, with a major battle only days away. He had not sought the appointment. In a letter to his wife, he wrote that he would move at once and try to do his duty. Few generals have taken command in more difficult circumstances, with so little time to prepare.

[long-pause]

{img:51-professor-colonel-writing-letter-tent}

Union colonel Joshua Lawrence Chamberlain had been a professor of languages before the war. Before the Civil War, Joshua Lawrence Chamberlain taught at Bowdoin College in Maine. He was a professor of rhetoric and languages. He could read Latin, Greek and Hebrew, among several other languages. He was married, with two young children, and loved music and books. In 1862, the college offered him leave to travel and study in Europe. Instead, he joined the army. By the summer of 1863, he was thirty-four years old and a colonel. He commanded the 20th Maine, a regiment of about three hundred and eighty men. Most were farmers, fishermen and lumbermen from the coast and forests of Maine. Chamberlain wrote regularly to his wife, Fanny, describing the country and his men. At Gettysburg, his regiment defended the southern end of the Union line on Little Round Top. Chamberlain later received the Medal of Honor. After the war, he became governor of Maine and president of Bowdoin College.

[long-pause]

{img:52-night-camp-tents-stars-hills}

Neither General Lee nor General Meade had planned to fight a battle at Gettysburg. On the Confederate side, one of Lee's most important commanders was General James Longstreet. He was a large, bearded and quiet man, originally from Georgia, solid and stubborn by nature. Lee called him his old war horse. In early 1862, three of Longstreet's young children had died of fever within a single week. Friends said he became quieter and more serious afterwards. On the night of the thirtieth of June, both armies were spread across the countryside. Some units were only a few miles from Gettysburg. Others were a day's march away. Neither commanding general had chosen Gettysburg as a battlefield. Neither knew exactly where all the enemy's forces were. Lee had ordered his army to concentrate in the area, while Meade was preparing a defensive line further south. The road network drew both armies toward the town. Historians often describe Gettysburg as a battle that began almost by accident, and grew larger than anyone intended.

[long-pause]

{img:53-misty-dawn-fields-west-of-town}

On the first morning of the battle, the professor's thermometer showed a mild seventy-two degrees. Wednesday, the first of July, 1863, began quietly in Gettysburg. A light mist lay over the fields, and birds sang in the orchards. At seven o'clock, Professor Michael Jacobs recorded a temperature of seventy-two degrees Fahrenheit, with light clouds and a gentle southern breeze. West of town, along the Chambersburg Pike, Union cavalrymen were watching the road. A little after seven, they saw a long column of Confederate infantry approaching from the west. A Union lieutenant fired a shot at the distant column. This is traditionally remembered as the first shot of the battle. Several men later claimed the honour, and historians cannot be completely certain who fired first. What is certain is that the fighting grew quickly from that moment. Within hours, thousands of soldiers were engaged along the ridges west of town. The largest battle ever fought in North America began on a calm summer morning, with a single shot in the mist.

[long-pause]

{img:54-townspeople-rooftops-watching-distant-smoke}

On the first day of the battle, some residents of Gettysburg climbed onto rooftops to watch the fighting. As the morning went on, residents heard firing to the west. At first it was scattered and distant. Then it grew louder and steadier, and the deep boom of cannons joined in. People gathered in the streets and looked toward the ridges, where white clouds of smoke were rising. Some boys climbed onto rooftops for a better view. Older residents went up into the cupolas of the college and the courthouse. Soldiers soon ordered them down and told them to stay indoors. Many families had to decide quickly whether to stay or leave. Some went to relatives in the countryside, believing it would be safer. The Pierce family sent their daughter Tillie south of town with their neighbour Hettie Shriver and her two young girls. They were going to the Weikert farm near the Round Tops. Such choices were made with very little information. Nobody yet knew where the fighting would spread over the following days.

[long-pause]

{img:55-girl-woman-children-walking-south-road}

Tillie Pierce was sent to a farm for safety, near where some of the heaviest fighting would take place. Shortly before noon on the first of July, Tillie Pierce set off on foot with Hettie Shriver and her daughters. They walked south past the cemetery, away from the sound of the guns. The road was quiet, the wheat golden and the sun hot. They reached the Weikert farm in the early afternoon. Nobody could have known that the farm lay close to ground where some of the fiercest fighting of the second day would occur. Meanwhile, back in town, John Burns took his old musket from the wall. He put on his tall hat and best coat and walked west toward the fighting. Near the seminary, he found Union soldiers in a wood and asked to join them. They were astonished, but they let him stay. Burns fought alongside them for several hours and was wounded three times. These two journeys, one away from danger and one toward it, show how differently ordinary people responded when the battle reached their town.

[long-pause]

{img:56-church-turned-hospital-women-water}

On the first day of the battle, churches in Gettysburg were already being turned into hospitals. By the afternoon of the first of July, wounded soldiers were streaming into Gettysburg. Some walked or limped. Others were carried by comrades or brought in wagons. Army surgeons set up hospitals in churches, the courthouse, the college, the seminary and many private homes. Christ Lutheran Church on Chambersburg Street filled with men lying on pews and floors. Other churches quickly followed. Residents opened their doors. Women brought water, bandages and bread. Few had any medical training, but they began to help immediately. Church buildings were especially useful because they had large open interiors. Pews were sometimes covered with boards to make beds. In the following weeks, nearly every public building in town became a hospital. Many still carry reminders of this period. Some churches display plaques or preserved bloodstains from the wounded who lay there. These buildings, made for worship, became places where townspeople and soldiers met in the hardest circumstances of their lives.

[long-pause]

{img:57-family-cellar-candle-preserves-jars}

During the fighting, many families in Gettysburg sheltered in their cellars among jars of preserves. Late in the afternoon of the first day, Union forces were pushed back through the town. Soldiers ran through streets, alleys and back gardens toward Cemetery Hill south of town. For a short time, the streets were filled with shouting and confusion. Residents stayed indoors. Many families went down into their cellars and locked the doors. They sat among potatoes and jars of preserves, often by the light of a single candle. Children were frightened, and parents tried to comfort them. Civilian accounts describe the sounds of running feet, gunfire and shouting overhead. Some Union soldiers hid in cellars and attics to avoid capture. A few remained hidden for days. By evening, the fighting had moved south of town. When families came up from their cellars, they found Confederate soldiers occupying the streets. For the next two days, Gettysburg lay between the lines, with residents living in a town held by one army and watched by another.

[long-pause]

{img:58-cemetery-hill-gatehouse-soldiers-evening}

On the first night of the battle, the cemetery keeper's wife baked bread for Union soldiers until late. By the evening of the first of July, Union forces had gathered on Cemetery Hill and Culp's Hill. They dug in behind stone walls and piles of fence rails. Confederate forces held the town and the ridges to the west and north. The Evergreen Cemetery gatehouse now stood in the middle of the Union position. Elizabeth Thorn, pregnant and caring for her family, baked bread and made coffee for officers and soldiers. According to her later account, an officer asked her to show him the roads around the hill. She walked out with him in the dusk, pointing out the routes one by one. Union generals later used the gatehouse as a headquarters for a time. Elizabeth's family left the area during the heavy fighting of the next days. When they returned, they found their home badly damaged. Her local knowledge was a small but practical contribution. It shows how civilians sometimes became part of military decisions without ever intending to.

[long-pause]

{img:59-farmhouse-well-water-soldiers-night}

At the Weikert farm, Tillie Pierce carried water to Union soldiers marching past. On the night of the first of July, the Weikert farm near Little Round Top was crowded with soldiers. Union troops were marching north along the Taneytown Road to join the army on Cemetery Ridge. The family and their guests baked bread and drew water from the well for the passing men. Tillie Pierce carried buckets of water to the road and filled soldiers' cups. In her book, she described how grateful the men were. Some showed her photographs of their families. Some asked her to write to their relatives if anything happened to them. That night, she slept on the farmhouse floor, exhausted, while troops continued marching past outside. The next day, the farm lay close to the fighting around the Round Tops and the Wheatfield. The house became a field hospital. Wounded soldiers filled its rooms and yard. Tillie's account of these hours is one of the most vivid civilian descriptions of the battle from behind the Union lines.

[long-pause]

{img:60-officer-on-rocky-hilltop-telescope}

On the second day, a Union engineer discovered that Little Round Top was almost undefended. Thursday, the second of July, began quietly. For most of the morning, the armies prepared rather than fought. Between Seminary Ridge and Cemetery Ridge lay about a mile of farm fields, orchards and meadows. At the southern end of the Union line rose Little Round Top. That morning, it was almost empty, apart from a few signalmen sending messages with flags. In the afternoon, General Gouverneur Warren, the army's chief engineer, rode up to inspect the position. Looking west, he noticed sunlight glinting on rifles among the trees. He realised that Confederate forces were preparing to attack. If they captured the hill, they could fire along the entire Union line. Warren immediately sent messengers to find troops who could defend it. Reinforcements arrived just in time. A statue of Warren now stands on the summit, looking west. His quick observation is often cited as one of the decisive moments of the battle.

[long-pause]

{img:61-rocky-wooded-slope-boulders-afternoon}

On Little Round Top, the 20th Maine held the very end of the Union line. At about four o'clock on the second of July, Confederate forces under General Longstreet launched a major attack. The fighting spread through the Peach Orchard, the Wheatfield and Devil's Den. At the Weikert farm, soldiers told the family and their guests to leave immediately. Tillie Pierce and the Shrivers hurried to another farmhouse further from the fighting. On Little Round Top, Union reinforcements arrived just in time. Among them was the 20th Maine under Colonel Joshua Chamberlain. His regiment was placed at the extreme end of the Union line, among boulders and trees. For about an hour and a half, they held their position against repeated attacks. Running low on ammunition, Chamberlain ordered a downhill bayonet charge. The attacking Confederates fell back. Historians still debate how decisive this action was. Yet it became one of the best-known episodes of the battle, made famous again in the twentieth century through books and films.

[long-pause]

{img:62-small-farmhouse-lamplit-generals-night}

On the night of the second of July, Union generals met in a widow's small farmhouse to decide whether to stay. As evening fell on the second of July, the fighting gradually died down. Confederate troops had captured the Peach Orchard and Devil's Den. Yet the Union line along Cemetery Ridge held from Cemetery Hill to Little Round Top. Across the dark fields, lanterns moved slowly back and forth. Stretcher-bearers were searching for wounded men and carrying them to barns and farmhouses behind the lines. General Meade had made his headquarters in the small farmhouse of a widow, Lydia Leister. That night, his generals crowded into its tiny front room around a table lit by candles. Meade asked whether the army should stay and fight or withdraw. One by one, the generals gave their opinions. They agreed to stay and defend their position. This meeting became known as the council of war. The Leister house still stands on the battlefield. It is a modest building for such an important decision.

[long-pause]

{img:63-hot-morning-baltimore-street-quiet}

The third day of the battle was the hottest, at eighty-seven degrees in the afternoon. Friday, the third of July, dawned hot and still. At seven o'clock, Professor Jacobs recorded seventy-three degrees and cloudy skies. By two in the afternoon, the temperature reached eighty-seven, the hottest reading of the battle. Early in the morning, heavy fighting resumed on Culp's Hill, east of the cemetery. The noise echoed through the streets of the town. By late morning, that fighting had ended. Gettysburg grew strangely quiet. Confederate soldiers had occupied the town for two days. They sat on doorsteps cleaning rifles and cooked small meals in alleys. Some knocked on doors and politely asked for bread or water. Residents mostly stayed indoors with shutters closed, peering through cracks. Food and water were running low in many houses. Civilian accounts describe exhaustion and uncertainty. Nobody knew how long the battle would last or which army would remain. The heat made everything harder for soldiers, residents and the wounded alike.

[long-pause]

{img:64-small-brick-house-kitchen-bread-dough}

Jennie Wade was baking bread when a stray bullet struck her on the third of July. Mary Virginia Wade was staying at her sister's house on Baltimore Street during the battle. Her sister Georgia had recently given birth. Ginnie and her mother had come to help. The small brick house stood between the two armies, and it was struck many times by bullets. On the morning of the third of July, Ginnie was in the kitchen kneading dough. She had been baking bread for Union soldiers during the battle. At about half past eight, a bullet passed through two wooden doors and struck her. She died instantly. She was twenty years old. She is the only civilian known to have been killed directly by the fighting at Gettysburg. Her fiancé, Johnston Skelly, had been wounded earlier in Virginia. He died a few days later in a hospital, never learning of her death. The house is now a museum. Her story became one of the most widely told personal stories of the battle.

[long-pause]

{img:65-hot-noon-ridge-waiting-soldiers-shade}

Just before the great bombardment on the third day, some Union officers ate a lunch of stewed chicken. By late morning on the third of July, a long stillness settled over the battlefield. Soldiers on both ridges lay in the hot sun, waiting. Some slept, some wrote letters, and some ate what little food they had. The empty fields between the ridges shimmered in the heat. An officer named Frank Haskell later described a lunch on Cemetery Ridge. Several Union generals sat together in the shade and ate stewed chicken, potatoes, bread and coffee. The chicken came from an old rooster found at a nearby farm. Haskell wrote that it was tough, but welcome. His long letter about the battle became one of its most famous eyewitness accounts. The calm did not last. At about one o'clock, two signal guns fired from Seminary Ridge. Moments later, Confederate artillery all along the ridge opened fire. Union guns soon replied. The largest artillery bombardment of the war had begun.

[long-pause]

{img:66-white-smoke-cloud-over-fields-distant}

The cannonade at Gettysburg on the third of July could be heard many miles away. Around one hundred and fifty Confederate cannons opened fire on the Union lines. More than a hundred Union guns answered. The noise was enormous. Reports suggest it was heard many miles away, rolling across the countryside like distant thunder. In town, windows rattled and dishes shook on shelves. The ground trembled. Clouds of white smoke drifted across the fields and dimmed the sun. Families sheltered again in their cellars. On Cemetery Ridge, Union soldiers pressed themselves behind stone walls. The bombardment lasted for more than an hour, some accounts say nearly two. Many Confederate shells flew too high and landed behind the Union front line. When the firing died down, a long line of Confederate infantry stepped out from the woods on Seminary Ridge. About twelve thousand men advanced across open fields in lines almost a mile wide. Many belonged to the division of General George Pickett. The attack became known as Pickett's Charge.

[long-pause]

{img:67-general-lee-on-horse-meeting-soldiers}

After Pickett's Charge failed, General Lee told his returning soldiers that it was all his fault. The Confederate lines advanced steadily across the fields, crossing fences and the Emmitsburg Road. Union soldiers on Cemetery Ridge watched them come. Many later said it was one of the most impressive sights they had ever seen. As the attackers drew closer, Union artillery and rifle fire tore into their ranks. A small number reached the stone wall near a clump of trees on Cemetery Ridge. For a few minutes, they broke through. Then they were stopped. The survivors fell back across the fields toward Seminary Ridge, helping wounded comrades along. Losses were very heavy. More than half of the attackers were killed, wounded or captured. General Lee rode out to meet the returning men. According to witnesses, he told them that the failure was entirely his fault. He did not blame his soldiers. Historians still argue about his decision to order the attack. Yet his acceptance of responsibility has often been noted as a rare act of honesty by a commander.

[long-pause]

{img:68-rain-falling-town-dawn-july}

On the morning of the Fourth of July, residents of Gettysburg found that the Confederate soldiers had left the town. As the sun set on the third of July, the battlefield finally fell silent. Residents emerged from their cellars, uncertain what would happen next. In the early hours of the fourth of July, rain began to fall. At first it was a light drizzle. By afternoon, it was pouring. Professor Jacobs recorded the rainfall in his notebook. It was Independence Day. In other years, the town would have celebrated with flags and a band. In 1863, there was only rain. During the night, Confederate forces had withdrawn from the town to Seminary Ridge. In the morning, residents opened their shutters and saw empty streets. Slowly, they stepped outside. Union soldiers came down from Cemetery Hill into the town. Some residents cried, some laughed, and some simply stood silently in the rain. After three days of battle, the town was once again in Union hands. Its people now faced a very different challenge.

[long-pause]

{img:69-wagon-train-rain-night-mountain-road}

When Lee's army retreated, its train of wagons and ambulances stretched for about seventeen miles. On the fourth of July, both armies remained on their ridges, watching each other through the rain. Neither attacked. Both were exhausted after three days of fighting. Soldiers huddled under oilcloth blankets and tried to make coffee over sputtering fires. That evening, Lee's army began its retreat westward toward the mountains and Virginia. First came a vast wagon train carrying wounded soldiers. It stretched for roughly seventeen miles along muddy roads. Accounts describe the suffering of the wounded as wagons jolted through the rain all night. Then came the artillery, followed by the infantry. On the same day, far away on the Mississippi River, the city of Vicksburg surrendered to Union forces under General Ulysses S. Grant. The two events together marked a turning point in the war. The Confederacy never again invaded the North on such a scale. Many people in the North began to believe, for the first time, that victory was possible.

[long-pause]

{img:70-mother-daughter-reunion-doorstep}

When Tillie Pierce returned home after the battle, her mother did not recognise her at first. Tillie Pierce spent several more days at a farmhouse south of the battlefield. The roads were crowded with soldiers, wagons and ambulances, making travel difficult. She helped wherever she could, carrying water, rolling bandages and writing letters for wounded soldiers. On the seventh of July, she finally walked home. The countryside had been transformed. Fences had been torn down for firewood. Wheat fields had been trampled flat. Dead horses lay in the fields. When she reached Baltimore Street, her mother was standing at the door. According to Tillie's account, her mother did not recognise her at first, because she was so tired, pale and muddy. Then she ran down the steps and embraced her. The whole family had survived. Tillie later wrote that the bread and butter she ate at the kitchen table that day tasted better than anything she had ever eaten. Her account captures the relief felt by many families after the battle.

[long-pause]

{img:71-night-town-lamps-every-window}

After the battle, Gettysburg had about eight wounded soldiers for every person who lived there. When the armies left, more than twenty thousand wounded soldiers remained in and around Gettysburg. The town had only about two thousand four hundred residents. That meant roughly eight wounded men for every person living there. They filled churches, schools, the college, the seminary, the courthouse, warehouses, barns and nearly every private house. Townspeople cared for them as best they could. Women made soup and baked bread from morning until night. They brought water, washed faces and sat beside the wounded. They fanned them in the heat and wrote letters for men who could not write. They read to them and sometimes sang softly in the evenings. Men carried stretchers and assisted surgeons. Children ran errands. Many residents later noted that they cared for soldiers from both sides. Wounded Confederates were treated alongside Union soldiers. For several weeks, the entire town became a hospital, and its residents became nurses.

[long-pause]

{img:72-young-teacher-sitting-with-soldier}

A young schoolteacher named Sallie Myers became one of Gettysburg's most devoted volunteer nurses. Sarah Myers, known as Sallie, was about twenty-one in 1863 and worked as a schoolteacher. On the first day of the battle, wounded soldiers were carried into St Francis Xavier Catholic Church on High Street. Sallie went inside to help. She had no nursing experience and later admitted she was frightened. Yet she stayed. She brought water and food, washed faces, held hands and listened as soldiers spoke about home. Her family took a wounded sergeant from Pennsylvania into their own house. Sallie cared for him there until he died. She kept a diary during these weeks. Her writings later became an important source for historians. They describe exhaustion, grief and moments of unexpected friendship. Many young women in Gettysburg did similar work, often without recognition. Sallie Myers is remembered partly because she wrote down what she saw. Her diary preserves the experience of an ordinary civilian thrust into an extraordinary role.

[long-pause]

{img:73-supply-train-unloading-station-volunteers}

After the battle, trains full of food, bandages and volunteers arrived in Gettysburg from across the North. Help soon arrived from outside. Trains came into Gettysburg from Baltimore, Philadelphia and Harrisburg. They brought doctors, nurses, food, bandages and medicine. They carried clean sheets, shirts and blankets. Much of this aid came from two large Northern relief organisations, the United States Sanitary Commission and the United States Christian Commission. Churches and communities across the country also sent supplies. Children in distant towns collected money to buy lemons and crackers for the wounded. Volunteers came too. Some simply wanted to help. Others came searching for sons, husbands or brothers among the wounded and the dead. The small railway station was crowded day and night. Local residents guided newcomers through the town and helped them find hospitals. The relief effort at Gettysburg was one of the largest of the war. It showed how civilian organisations had become essential to caring for soldiers on a scale never seen before in America.

[long-pause]

{img:74-white-tent-hospital-rows-trees}

A large tent hospital called Camp Letterman was set up on a hill east of Gettysburg after the battle. In mid-July 1863, the army established a general hospital east of town beside the York Pike. It was named Camp Letterman, after Jonathan Letterman, the medical director of the Army of the Potomac. Hundreds of white canvas tents stood in long, neat rows among the trees. There were kitchens, a bakery and supply stores. The site was chosen partly because it lay near the railway, making it easier to bring supplies and move patients. Gradually, the wounded were transferred there from churches, houses and barns. The camp was orderly and well ventilated. Nurses moved along the rows with baskets and buckets. Letterman had already introduced new systems for evacuating and treating the wounded. His methods improved survival and influenced military medicine for generations. By late November, most patients had been sent home or to other hospitals. Camp Letterman closed shortly afterwards. Today, little remains of the site, but its importance in medical history is widely recognised.

[long-pause]

{img:75-gatehouse-woman-baby-autumn-window}

In November 1863, the cemetery keeper's wife gave birth to a daughter named Rose. When Elizabeth Thorn returned to the Evergreen Cemetery gatehouse after the battle, she found it badly damaged. Furniture had been broken and bedding taken. Yet there was urgent work to do. In the hot days of July, she and her elderly father buried around a hundred soldiers who had died on and around Cemetery Hill. She was six months pregnant at the time. The work was physically exhausting and emotionally painful. Elizabeth later said that it affected her health for years. In 1905, she described her experiences to a newspaper, wanting people to understand what civilian women had endured. In November 1863, she gave birth to a daughter. She named her Rose Meade Thorn, after the Union commander. The girl was frail as a child, but she survived. Elizabeth's story was largely overlooked for many decades. Today, she is often cited as one of the clearest examples of the hidden labour of women during and after the battle.

[long-pause]

{img:76-farmer-rebuilding-fence-late-summer}

After the battle, farmers around Gettysburg had to rebuild miles of fences by hand. Gradually, life in Gettysburg returned to something like normal. Farmers went back to their fields and gathered what remained of the harvest. They rebuilt fences rail by rail. Thousands of fence rails had been burned for firewood or used to build defences. Many farms had lost crops, livestock and buildings. Some families later submitted damage claims to the government, which survive as useful historical records. Shops reopened, and storekeepers brought back their goods from Philadelphia. The bank resumed business. Schools reopened in the autumn. Yet the town was permanently changed. Every family had stories of those days. Many houses carried bullet holes and shell damage. Across the fields and ridges, thousands of soldiers lay in hastily dug graves, often marked only by wooden boards with names written in pencil. Some were not marked at all. Many residents felt deeply troubled by this situation. They believed the soldiers deserved a proper and lasting resting place.

[long-pause]

{img:77-david-wills-house-diamond}

The idea of a national cemetery at Gettysburg came from a young local lawyer, David Wills. David Wills was a lawyer of about thirty-two. He lived in a large brick house on the Diamond, in the centre of Gettysburg. He was energetic, practical and well organised. After the battle, the governor of Pennsylvania asked him to oversee matters relating to Pennsylvania's dead soldiers. Wills walked across the battlefield and saw graves scattered across fields and woods. Letters arrived daily from families across the North. They asked where their loved ones were buried and whether their bodies could be brought home. Wills proposed creating a national cemetery at Gettysburg, where Union soldiers could be buried together with honour. The governor supported the idea, and other Northern states agreed to share the cost. It was a new kind of project. National cemeteries were still uncommon at the time. The Soldiers' National Cemetery at Gettysburg became a model for many that followed. The Wills house still stands on the square and is now a museum.

[long-pause]

{img:78-landscape-designer-plans-hilltop}

The graves in the Soldiers' National Cemetery were laid out in curved rows, like ripples in a pond. Wills purchased about seventeen acres on Cemetery Hill, next to Evergreen Cemetery. The land overlooked many of the fields and ridges where the battle had been fought. To design the new cemetery, the states chose William Saunders. He was a Scottish-born landscape gardener working for the government in Washington. Saunders walked the hill many times, studying its shape. He then created a simple and elegant plan. The graves would be arranged in curved rows forming a semicircle. The rows resembled ripples spreading from a stone dropped into water. Soldiers from each state would be buried together in their own section. At the centre, on the highest point, a monument would stand. Saunders wanted the design to feel calm and dignified. He used trees and open lawns rather than elaborate decoration. His plan still shapes the cemetery today. It is often admired as one of the most thoughtful memorial landscapes of its time.

[long-pause]

{img:79-autumn-trees-cemetery-hill-workers}

In the national cemetery, every grave was made the same, whether it held an officer or a private. Saunders made another important decision. All graves would be equal. There would be no large monuments for officers and small stones for ordinary soldiers. Every man would rest beside his comrades on the same terms. In the autumn of 1863, reburials began. Bodies were carefully moved from temporary graves across the battlefield into the new cemetery. Workers recorded names whenever they could be identified. Many soldiers could not be identified at all. Their graves were marked as unknown. Records from the reburial work show how difficult and painful the process was. It continued through the autumn and into the following year. More than three thousand Union soldiers were eventually buried there. Confederate dead were not included and remained in field graves for years. Many were later moved to cemeteries in the South. The principle of equal graves became a lasting tradition in American military cemeteries. It expressed the idea that every soldier's sacrifice deserved the same respect.

[long-pause]

{img:80-elderly-statesman-library-books-lamp}

The main speaker at the Gettysburg ceremony was famous for speeches that could last for hours. Wills and the organising committee planned a ceremony to dedicate the new cemetery. They invited Edward Everett to deliver the main address. Everett was one of the most celebrated speakers in America. He was nearly seventy years old and came from Massachusetts. He had been a congressman, governor, senator, president of Harvard College and Secretary of State. He was known for long, carefully crafted speeches that often lasted hours. The ceremony was originally planned for October. Everett said he needed more time to prepare, so the date was moved to the nineteenth of November. He studied battle reports and letters from generals. He planned to visit the battlefield personally before speaking. Speeches like his were a popular form of public event in the nineteenth century. Audiences expected length, learning and classical references. Everett's address at Gettysburg was praised at the time. Yet it was soon overshadowed by a far shorter speech delivered after it.

[long-pause]

{img:81-lincoln-writing-by-window-evening}

President Lincoln was invited to Gettysburg only to add a few appropriate remarks. On the second of November, 1863, David Wills wrote to President Abraham Lincoln. He invited him to attend the ceremony and, after Everett's oration, to offer a few appropriate remarks. The invitation came relatively late. Lincoln was extremely busy, and the committee was not sure he would come. Yet he accepted. Gettysburg prepared eagerly. Flags and bunting were hung from windows. A wooden platform was built at the new cemetery. Wills invited the President to stay in his own house on the Diamond. Lincoln was fifty-four years old, very tall and thin, with a deeply lined face. He had led the country for nearly three years through civil war. Historians know he worked on the speech in Washington and probably revised it in Gettysburg. Several handwritten versions survive. He had been asked for only a few words. He chose to say very little, but to make every word count.

[long-pause]

{img:82-presidential-train-countryside-november}

Lincoln's train journey from Washington to Gettysburg took about six hours. On Wednesday, the eighteenth of November, 1863, a special train left Washington around noon. President Lincoln travelled with his private secretaries, John Nicolay and John Hay. Members of his cabinet, foreign diplomats, a military band and a guard of soldiers accompanied him. The journey took about six hours. The train passed through the Maryland countryside, past bare fields and leafless woods. At Hanover Junction, it switched to a branch line heading west toward Gettysburg. Crowds gathered at stations along the route to see the President. At Hanover, people called for him to speak. He stepped onto the rear platform and offered a few friendly words. According to one account, a young girl gave him flowers, and he bent down to thank her. A popular story later claimed that Lincoln wrote his speech on the back of an envelope during this journey. Historians consider this unlikely. He had already prepared much of it before leaving Washington.

[long-pause]

{img:83-dinner-table-lamplight-guests-1863}

So many visitors came to Gettysburg for the ceremony that some walked the streets all night. Lincoln's train reached Gettysburg at dusk. A crowd waited at the small station in the cold. David Wills and Edward Everett met the President and walked with him up Carlisle Street to the Wills house on the Diamond. That evening, the Wills family hosted a dinner for about two dozen guests. Accounts describe Lincoln as quiet but friendly. He listened more than he spoke, though he sometimes told stories from his early life in Illinois. Outside, the town was overflowing. Thousands of visitors had arrived, far more than Gettysburg could accommodate. People slept in churches, parlours, hallways and chairs. Some could find no shelter at all and wandered the streets through the night. Hotels and private homes were packed. Many visitors had travelled long distances, some to visit the graves of relatives. For a town of two and a half thousand people, the arrival of perhaps fifteen thousand visitors was an extraordinary event.

[long-pause]

{img:84-crowd-serenade-house-night-band}

When a crowd called for a speech the night before, Lincoln politely joked his way out of it. After dinner, a crowd gathered outside the Wills house with a band. They played patriotic songs and called for the President to speak. Eventually Lincoln appeared at the door. He thanked them for coming. Then, according to newspaper reports, he explained that he had no speech prepared. In his position, he said, it was important not to say foolish things. Someone in the crowd shouted that he should avoid them if he could. Lincoln replied that the only way to help it was often to say nothing at all. The crowd laughed and cheered and let him go back inside. Later, he went upstairs to his room on the second floor. It had a large bed, a fireplace and a small writing table. Historians believe he worked further on his remarks there. The room has been restored and can be visited today. The exchange at the door shows the humour Lincoln often used to manage public attention.

[long-pause]

{img:85-telegram-reading-candlelight}

The night before his speech, Lincoln received a telegram saying that his sick son was feeling better. Before going to bed, Lincoln received a telegram from Washington. His youngest son, Tad, had been ill with a fever. Lincoln had been worried about him. His wife Mary had been very anxious and had not wanted him to leave. Their concern was understandable. In February 1862, their son Willie had died of typhoid fever at the age of eleven. Lincoln still wore a black mourning band on his hat in Willie's memory. The telegram brought good news. Tad was improving. Outside, the town remained busy late into the night. Visitors walked through the streets talking and singing. The night was cold and clear, with bright stars. These personal details are often overlooked in accounts of the Gettysburg Address. Yet they remind us that Lincoln spoke about national loss while carrying his own grief. The speech he gave the next day was written by a man who knew personally what it meant to lose a child.

[long-pause]

{img:86-lincoln-on-horseback-procession-street}

Lincoln rode to the cemetery on a horse so small that his feet nearly touched the ground. Thursday, the nineteenth of November, began cool and grey. As the morning went on, the clouds cleared and the sun came out. It became a mild and still autumn day. At about ten o'clock, a procession formed in the Diamond. It included soldiers, bands, governors, generals, members of Congress and wounded veterans. Lincoln appeared in a black suit, white gloves and his tall black hat. He mounted a horse provided for him. Several witnesses noted that he was so tall and the horse so small that his feet nearly touched the ground. The procession moved slowly along Baltimore Street toward Cemetery Hill. Residents lined the streets and stood on doorsteps. Tillie Pierce later recalled standing outside her house as the President passed close by. At the cemetery, an estimated fifteen thousand people gathered around the wooden platform. Many stood among the newly dug graves, waiting for the ceremony to begin.

[long-pause]

{img:87-elderly-orator-speaking-crowd}

Edward Everett spoke for about two hours at the Gettysburg ceremony, entirely from memory. The ceremony began with music and a long prayer. Then Edward Everett rose to speak. He delivered his address from memory, in a rich and carefully trained voice. It lasted about two hours. He described the battle in detail, day by day, from the first shots west of town to the great attack on the third day. He compared the occasion with the funeral ceremonies of ancient Athens, where citizens honoured those who died in war. He discussed the causes of the conflict and the future of the nation. The speech was thoughtful and polished. Many listeners admired it. Yet it was long, and some people at the back of the crowd grew restless. A few wandered away to look at the graves and the battlefield. When Everett finished, a choir sang a hymn written for the occasion. Then Lincoln's friend Ward Hill Lamon, who was in charge of arrangements, announced the President of the United States.

[long-pause]

{img:88-lincoln-standing-platform-paper-crowd}

The Gettysburg Address took about two minutes and contained only around two hundred and seventy words. Lincoln rose and stepped forward with a sheet of paper. He spoke in a high, clear voice, shaped by his youth in Kentucky and Indiana. He began with the words that became famous: four score and seven years ago. He spoke of a nation conceived in liberty and dedicated to the idea that all men are created equal. He said that the world would little note what was said there, but could never forget what the soldiers had done. He ended with the hope that government of the people, by the people, for the people, would not perish from the earth. The speech lasted about two minutes. Some listeners barely realised it had begun before it ended. A photographer setting up his camera reportedly missed the moment entirely. Only one known photograph shows Lincoln on the platform, small and partly hidden in the crowd. Applause followed. Newspapers reported the speech in very different ways, depending on their politics.

[long-pause]

{img:89-lincoln-reading-letter-train-window}

Edward Everett wrote that Lincoln had captured the meaning of the day in two minutes better than he had in two hours. Lincoln was not sure his speech had succeeded. According to some accounts, he told a friend he feared it had fallen flat. The next day, however, Edward Everett wrote him a gracious letter. He said he wished he had come as close to the central idea of the occasion in two hours as the President had in two minutes. Lincoln replied kindly, saying he was glad his brief remarks had not been a complete failure. That evening, John Burns walked with Lincoln to a church service in town. Afterwards, the President boarded his train back to Washington. He felt unwell on the journey and lay down with a damp cloth over his eyes. Within days, doctors found that he had a mild form of smallpox. He spent several weeks recovering. Five handwritten copies of the Gettysburg Address survive today. Each differs slightly, showing how Lincoln continued to refine his words even after he had spoken them.

[long-pause]

{img:90-older-woman-writing-memoir-parlour}

Tillie Pierce married, moved away and published her memories of the battle in 1889. The Civil War continued for another year and a half after the Gettysburg Address. In April 1865, General Lee surrendered to General Grant at Appomattox Court House in Virginia. Slavery was abolished throughout the United States by constitutional amendment later that year. Just days after Lee's surrender, President Lincoln was assassinated in Washington, and the nation mourned him. Life in Gettysburg continued. Tillie Pierce grew up and married a lawyer named Horace Alleman. They settled in Selinsgrove, in central Pennsylvania. In 1889, more than twenty-five years after the battle, she published her memoir. She lived until 1914. Her book was written long after the events it describes. Historians therefore read it alongside letters and diaries from 1863. Yet her account remains one of the most valuable civilian perspectives on the battle. It shows the experience of a young girl who watched history unfold from a kitchen, a farmyard and a front step.

[long-pause]

{img:91-old-gatehouse-couple-autumn-evening}

Elizabeth Thorn cared for Evergreen Cemetery for many years, and she is buried on the hill she looked after. Peter Thorn returned safely from the war. Together, he and Elizabeth continued to care for Evergreen Cemetery for many years. Elizabeth lived until 1907 and was buried in the cemetery she had tended. In 2002, a memorial to the civilian women of Gettysburg was unveiled near the gatehouse. Its statue was modelled on Elizabeth Thorn, shown pregnant and weary, with a shovel at her side. Other residents also lived on. John Burns became a celebrated figure, visited by admirers from far away. Professor Michael Jacobs published one of the earliest histories of the battle, using his weather records. Sallie Myers later wrote about her experiences. George Shriver, Hettie's husband, died as a prisoner of war and never returned home. Hettie raised her daughters alone. Many farms and buildings associated with the battle still stand. Their survival allows visitors today to connect individual stories with the places where they happened.

[long-pause]

{img:92-veterans-reunion-1913-handshake}

Fifty years after the battle, veterans from both sides shook hands across the same stone wall. In the summer of 1913, a great reunion was held at Gettysburg to mark the fiftieth anniversary of the battle. More than fifty thousand veterans attended from both the North and the South. Most were in their seventies, white-haired and frail. Some walked with canes, and some came with grandchildren. They stayed in a vast tent camp built on the old battlefield. They ate together in large dining tents and shared stories. On the anniversary of the third day, Confederate veterans who had taken part in Pickett's Charge walked slowly across the fields once more. Union veterans waited for them at the stone wall on Cemetery Ridge. Instead of fighting, they shook hands across the wall. Photographs and newspaper reports captured the moment. Many participants described it as deeply moving. Historians note that such reunions also tended to leave out the experiences of Black Americans. Even so, the scene became a powerful symbol of reconciliation.

[long-pause]

{img:93-gettysburg-battlefield-today-monuments}

There are more than thirteen hundred monuments on the battlefield at Gettysburg today. Today, much of the battlefield is protected as Gettysburg National Military Park. The fields, ridges and woods look much as they did in 1863. Farms associated with the battle still stand, including the Trostle barn, the Weikert farmhouse and the Leister house. Stone walls still run along Cemetery Ridge. Fences have been rebuilt in historical styles. In summer, wheat still grows between the ridges. Across the park stand more than thirteen hundred monuments, markers and memorials. There are statues of generals on horseback, granite soldiers and simple stone markers. Many regiments placed their monuments where they fought. The park also works to restore orchards and woodlots to their 1863 appearance. On Little Round Top, boulders still lie among the trees. A modest monument marks the position of the 20th Maine. Each year, around a million people visit the park. Many sit on the rocks at sunset and look west toward the South Mountains.

[long-pause]

{img:94-national-cemetery-curved-rows-trees-evening}

Every year on the nineteenth of November, people gather in Gettysburg to remember Lincoln's address. Gettysburg remains a small town, with roughly seven thousand residents. Many historic buildings survive. The Wills house on the Diamond is now a museum, where visitors can see the room where Lincoln stayed. The Shriver house on Baltimore Street has been restored to its 1863 appearance. The small house where Ginnie Wade was killed still stands. The gatehouse of Evergreen Cemetery remains on the hill. The Soldiers' National Cemetery is a quiet, peaceful place. Graves still lie in the curved rows designed by William Saunders, beneath tall old trees. The small stones are plain and equal. At the centre stands the Soldiers' National Monument, a tall column topped by a figure representing Liberty. Near the entrance, a bronze tablet carries the words of the Gettysburg Address. Every year on the nineteenth of November, a ceremony known as Dedication Day is held there. The address is read aloud, and new citizens are sometimes sworn in.

[long-pause]

{img:95-battlefield-dusk-deer-fields-fireflies}

On summer nights, fireflies still rise over the fields at Gettysburg, as they did in 1863. In the evening, when most visitors have left, the battlefield grows quiet. Golden light spreads across the fields and ridges before fading slowly to grey. White-tailed deer emerge from the woods to graze in the meadows. They move carefully between the monuments, lifting their heads to listen. Swallows swoop low over the wheat. Lights appear in farmhouse windows along the ridges. On summer nights, fireflies rise from the long grass in their thousands. They drift above the fields, blinking softly, as they did in July 1863. Civilian memoirs from that summer mention fireflies on the evenings before the battle. The insects were part of ordinary summer life in rural Pennsylvania. Today, they offer a quiet link between past and present. The park's careful preservation of the landscape means that many evening scenes resemble those of 1863. Only the sounds have changed, replaced now by crickets, wind and the occasional distant car.
