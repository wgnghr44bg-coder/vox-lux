Tonight we are going about a hundred and sixty years back, to a small country town among the farms and orchards of southern Pennsylvania.

--

It is the spring of 1863, and the apple trees on the hillsides are in blossom.

Along the roads into town, the fields are green with young wheat and corn, and the farmhouses stand among their barns and springhouses and long lines of wooden fences. Cows graze in the meadows beside the creeks. A farmer's wagon creaks slowly along the road, loaded with sacks of grain for the mill.

If quiet stories like this one help you drift off, you are welcome to subscribe. And now, let us begin.

--

The town is called Gettysburg.

It is not a large place. About two thousand four hundred people live here, in neat brick and wooden houses along a few straight streets, with a church spire or two rising above the rooftops, and a square in the middle, which everyone calls the Diamond.

It is the county seat of Adams County, and so it has a courthouse, a jail, a bank, a few hotels and taverns, a newspaper office, and a good many shops.

--

{img:ten-roads-countryside-aerial}
What makes Gettysburg a little unusual is its roads.

No fewer than ten roads come together here, from every direction, like the spokes of a wheel meeting at the hub. There is the Chambersburg Pike, coming in from the mountains to the west. There is the road to Baltimore, running south, and the roads to Harrisburg and York and Hanover, running north and east. There are roads to Emmitsburg and Taneytown, in Maryland, just a few miles away across the state line.

Farmers bring their produce into Gettysburg along these roads, and merchants send their goods out along them. Stagecoaches and wagons pass through every day.

--

Around the town, the land rolls gently up and down in long, low ridges, covered with fields and woods and orchards.

To the west, there is a ridge with a Lutheran seminary on top, a fine brick building with a little white cupola, from which a person can see for miles across the countryside. To the south, just outside the town, there is a hill with a cemetery on it, and a long, gentle ridge that runs on southward from there, toward two rocky, wooded hills that the local people call the Round Tops.

{img:seminary-ridge-cupola-view}
Further off, to the west and north, the blue line of the South Mountains rises along the horizon.

--

It is a peaceful, ordinary, pleasant place.

The people of Gettysburg are mostly farmers and craftsmen, shopkeepers and teachers, ministers and lawyers. Many of their families came to this part of Pennsylvania from Germany or Scotland or Ireland, two or three generations ago, and they still have German names, and some of the older people still speak German at home.

They are hardworking, churchgoing people. They know their neighbours, and they know their neighbours' business, and on Sundays, the church bells ring out across the rooftops, calling them to worship.

--

{img:diamond-square-courthouse-morning}
In the mornings, the Diamond is the busiest place in town.

Farmers' wagons stand around the square, piled high with butter and eggs, potatoes and cabbages, chickens in wooden crates. The shopkeepers open their doors and set out their goods on the pavement. The bank opens. Men stand on the corners, reading the newspapers and talking about the weather, and the crops, and the war.

Because there is a war.

--

For two years now, the United States has been divided.

In the spring of 1861, eleven states in the South left the Union and formed a new country of their own, which they called the Confederate States of America. The United States government, under President Abraham Lincoln, refused to let them go. And so the war began.

Since then, there have been great battles in Virginia and Tennessee, in Maryland and Mississippi, and hundreds of thousands of young men have gone off to fight, on both sides.

--

{img:recruitment-young-men-train-station}
Many young men from Gettysburg have gone too.

They marched off in their new blue uniforms, with flags flying and a brass band playing, while their mothers and sisters and sweethearts waved from the station platform. Some have already come home, wounded or ill. Some have not come home at all.

But for most of the time, the war is something that happens far away. The people of Gettysburg read about it in the newspapers, and pray for their sons and brothers, and knit socks and roll bandages for the soldiers. And then they go back to their own work, their own fields, their own lives.

--

Gettysburg is only about ten miles from the Maryland border, and Maryland is only a short distance from Virginia, where most of the great battles of the war have been fought.

Once or twice, there have been rumours that the Southern army might come north into Pennsylvania. Each time, people have hidden their horses and their valuables, and waited, and nothing has happened.

{img:orchard-blossom-farmhouse-evening}
And so, in the spring of 1863, the people of Gettysburg go about their business much as usual. The children go to school. The farmers plant their corn. The apple trees blossom on the hillsides, and the evenings grow long and warm.

Nobody imagines that, before the summer is half over, the name of their quiet little town will be known in every corner of the world.
