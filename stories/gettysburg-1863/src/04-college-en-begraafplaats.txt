On the northern edge of Gettysburg, on a gentle rise among tall trees, stands Pennsylvania College.

It is a handsome building of brick, painted pale, with tall white columns at the front and a little cupola on the roof. About a hundred and fifty young men study here, in their dark coats and waistcoats, and live in small rooms in the college building or lodge with families in the town.

--

One of the professors at the college is a man named Michael Jacobs.

Professor Jacobs teaches mathematics and natural science. He is a careful, methodical man, with a long face and a neat beard, and he has a habit that will become very valuable to history.

{img:professor-thermometer-garden}
Every day, three times a day, at seven in the morning, two in the afternoon and nine in the evening, he goes out to look at his instruments, and writes down the weather. The temperature. The direction of the wind. The clouds. Whether it is raining or dry.

He has been doing this for years, faithfully, and he will go on doing it all through the summer of 1863, whatever happens around him.

--

To the west of town, on the ridge that rises above the fields, stands the Lutheran Theological Seminary.

It is a long brick building, four storeys high, with a white wooden cupola on top. Here, young men study to become ministers in the Lutheran church. They read Hebrew and Greek and theology, and preach practice sermons to one another, and walk in the gardens and the woods along the ridge in their free hours.

The view from the cupola is wonderful. On a clear day, a person can see the whole town spread out below, and the farms and woods and orchards all around, and away to the west, the long blue line of the mountains.

People call this ridge Seminary Ridge.

--

On the southern edge of town, on a hill above the Baltimore Pike, there is a cemetery.

It is called Evergreen Cemetery, and it is a peaceful, green place, with tall trees and winding paths and white gravestones, where the families of Gettysburg bury their dead. People like to walk there on Sunday afternoons, among the trees and the flowers, and look out over the town.

At the entrance, beside the road, stands a handsome brick gatehouse, with an arched passage through the middle, and rooms on either side where the cemetery keeper lives.

--

{img:cemetery-gatehouse-brick-arch}
The keeper of the cemetery is a man named Peter Thorn. But Peter, like so many men in Gettysburg, has gone off to the army.

So the work of the cemetery is being done by his wife, Elizabeth.

Elizabeth Thorn came to America from Germany as a young woman. She is about thirty in 1863, a strong, hardworking woman, with three young sons, and she is expecting another child in the autumn. Her elderly parents live with her in the gatehouse, and her father helps with the heavy work.

--

It is not an easy job.

Elizabeth looks after the grounds, and the paths, and the gates. She keeps the records of who is buried where. When someone in the town dies, she and her father dig the grave, and help with the burial, and then she writes the name carefully in the cemetery book.

{img:woman-gardening-cemetery-paths}
In between, she cooks and cleans for her family, tends her garden, and looks after her boys, who play among the trees and the gravestones as if it were the finest playground in the world.

From the windows of the gatehouse, she can look out over the whole town in one direction, and over the long ridge running south toward the Round Tops in the other.

--

The hill where the cemetery stands is called Cemetery Hill.

From the top of it, the land falls away gently on all sides. To the north is the town. To the east, there is another wooded hill, called Culp's Hill, and beyond it, the valley of Rock Creek. To the south, the long, low ridge runs away for two miles or so, with farms and orchards and a little wheatfield along it, until it reaches the Round Tops.

People call that ridge Cemetery Ridge.

--

Along Cemetery Ridge, there are farms, as there are everywhere around Gettysburg.

There is the farm of Abraham Bryan, a free Black farmer, who owns a small house and a barn and a few acres of land on the ridge. There is the little white farmhouse of a widow named Lydia Leister, who lives there with her children. Further south, there are the farms of the Trostles and the Weikerts, with their big stone barns and their orchards.

In the fields, the wheat is growing tall. In the orchards, the cherries are ripening. In a peach orchard beside the Emmitsburg Road, the little green peaches are beginning to swell on the branches.

--

It is a beautiful, quiet piece of country.

On a summer evening, the light falls long and golden across the fields, and the shadows of the trees stretch out over the grass. The cows walk slowly home to be milked. A farmer's wife calls her children in for supper. On the hill, Elizabeth Thorn closes the iron gates of the cemetery and goes inside, and lights the lamp.

{img:round-tops-wooded-hills-dusk}
And away to the south, the two rocky hills, the Round Tops, stand dark against the evening sky, covered in trees, with great boulders scattered over their slopes, and a little stream winding past their feet through a valley full of rocks.

The local people call that valley the Devil's Den. It is a favourite place for picnics.
