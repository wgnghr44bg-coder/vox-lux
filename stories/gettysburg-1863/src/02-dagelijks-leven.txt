A house in Gettysburg in 1863 is usually built right up against the pavement, with a front door opening onto the street, and a long garden stretching away behind.

Many of the houses are of red brick, two storeys high, with green shutters and a small attic under the roof. Others are of wood, painted white, or of stone. At the back, there is a yard with a well or a pump, a woodshed, a little stable for a horse and a cow, perhaps a pigsty and a chicken house, and a vegetable garden.

--

The day begins early.

The mother of the house is up before dawn, lighting the fire in the iron cookstove in the kitchen. She fetches water from the pump in the yard, puts the kettle on, and starts the breakfast: fried ham or sausage, fried potatoes, eggs, bread and butter, apple butter, and strong coffee.

{img:mother-cookstove-breakfast-children}
The children are woken and washed and dressed. The older girls help with the cooking and the younger children. The boys go out to feed the chickens, milk the cow, and bring in wood for the stove.

Then the family sits down together around the kitchen table, and the father says grace, and they eat.

--

After breakfast, the father goes off to work.

Many of the men in Gettysburg work at a trade. There are carriage makers, who build fine buggies and wagons and sell them all over the country. There are blacksmiths and harness makers, tanners and shoemakers, carpenters and masons, tailors and hatters, bakers and butchers.

In the carriage shops on Chambersburg Street and York Street, the sound of hammers and saws goes on all day. The wheelwrights shape spokes and rims from seasoned hickory. The painters, in a separate room where there is no dust, brush on layer after layer of glossy black paint, and pick out thin lines of gold or red along the sides.

--

The women's work is mostly in and around the house, and there is a great deal of it.

On Monday, there is washing, a long, heavy job, with a great iron kettle of water boiling in the yard, and the clothes scrubbed on a washboard, rinsed, wrung out by hand, and hung on a line to dry. On Tuesday, there is ironing, with heavy irons heated on the stove. Then there is baking, and mending, and cleaning, and churning butter, and working in the garden.

In the summer, there are fruits and vegetables to be preserved for the winter: cherries and peaches, beans and tomatoes, apples to be dried, cabbages to be made into sauerkraut in big stone crocks in the cellar.

--

{img:general-store-counter-1863}
Every few days, the women walk to the stores on the Diamond or along Baltimore Street.

The general store sells almost everything. Coffee and tea, sugar and salt, molasses, spices, bolts of cloth, thread and buttons, needles and pins, pots and pans, lamp oil, candles, soap, nails, and patent medicines in little glass bottles that promise to cure every illness known to humanity.

Since the war began, some things have become scarce and expensive, especially coffee and cotton cloth. But in a farming town like Gettysburg, there is always plenty to eat.

--

On Saturday evenings, people take baths, one after another, in a tin tub in front of the kitchen stove, with water heated on the fire.

On Sunday, everyone dresses in their best clothes and goes to church.

{img:church-sunday-families-1863}
Gettysburg has many churches: Lutheran and Presbyterian, Methodist and Catholic, German Reformed and Episcopal, and a church for the town's Black families, who have lived in Gettysburg for generations. After the service, families stand outside in the sunshine talking with their neighbours, and then walk home to a big Sunday dinner of roast chicken and potatoes and pie.

The afternoon is for resting, visiting, reading the Bible, or taking a slow walk out along one of the country roads.

--

Children in Gettysburg go to school, at least for a few years.

There are public schools for the younger children, and for those whose families can afford it, private academies. There is a seminary for young ladies, and there is a college on the hill north of town, Pennsylvania College, where young men study Latin and Greek and mathematics and science.

When school is out, the children play in the streets and the fields. The boys play ball and marbles, go fishing in the creeks, and climb trees in the orchards. The girls play with dolls, skip rope, and pick wildflowers. In winter, there is sledding on the hills. In summer, there is swimming in Rock Creek, on the east side of town.

--

Market days bring the whole county into town.

Early in the morning, the farm wagons come rolling in along the ten roads, one after another, until the Diamond is crowded with them. The farmers' wives sit on the wagon seats in their sunbonnets, with baskets of eggs and crocks of butter and bunches of herbs at their feet, and the townswomen walk slowly from wagon to wagon, choosing and bargaining.

There are fresh strawberries in June, sweet corn and tomatoes in August, apples and cider and pumpkins in the autumn. There are live chickens in crates, and sides of smoked ham, and great wheels of cheese. A farmer sells honey in the comb from a wooden tub. A boy sells cherries by the quart.

By midday, the wagons are empty, and the farmers go off to the taverns for their dinner, and to talk with one another about the weather and the price of wheat, before the long drive home in the afternoon sun.

--

In the summer, the whole town smells of the countryside.

There is the sweet smell of cut hay from the meadows at the edge of town, and the smell of baking bread from the kitchens, and the sharp smell of the tanneries down by the creek, which nobody likes very much. In the evenings, when the wind comes from the west, it brings the smell of the orchards and the woods along the ridge.

--

In the evenings, families sit together in the parlour or on the front step.

The father reads the newspaper aloud by the light of an oil lamp. The mother knits, or sews. The children do their lessons, or play quietly. Sometimes a neighbour drops in. Sometimes someone plays the piano, or the parlour organ, and everyone sings.

{img:front-step-evening-neighbours}
On warm summer evenings, people sit out on the steps in front of their houses, calling out to their neighbours across the street, watching the swallows over the rooftops and the fireflies in the gardens, and the long golden light slowly fading behind the ridge to the west.

Then the lamps are put out one by one, and the town goes to sleep.
