David Wills is a young lawyer, about thirty-two years old, who lives in a large brick house on the corner of the Diamond, in the very centre of Gettysburg.

He is an energetic, practical, well-organized man, with a high forehead and a neat beard. He knows everyone in the town, and everyone knows him. And in the summer of 1863, he is asked by the governor of Pennsylvania to look after the affairs of the soldiers from Pennsylvania who fell in the battle.

--

Wills takes the job very seriously.

He walks out over the battlefield, and sees the places where the soldiers lie, scattered across the fields and the woods, in the orchards and along the stone walls. He reads the letters that come to him every day from families all over the North, asking where their sons and husbands are, and whether they can be brought home.

{img:lawyer-study-letters-desk-lamp}
And he writes to the governor with an idea. There should be a cemetery here, he says, at Gettysburg, a national cemetery, where all the Union soldiers who fell in the battle can be gathered together and laid to rest side by side, with honour.

--

The governor agrees, and so do the governors of the other Northern states whose soldiers fought at Gettysburg.

Wills buys seventeen acres of land on Cemetery Hill, right next to Evergreen Cemetery, on the slope where some of the hardest fighting took place. From there, the ground looks out over the fields and the ridges where the battle was fought.

To design the new cemetery, the states choose a man named William Saunders, a gardener and landscape designer who works for the government in Washington.

--

Saunders is a thoughtful, quiet Scotsman, who loves trees and plants and gardens.

He walks over the hill many times, studying the shape of the land, and he decides on a simple and beautiful plan. The graves will be laid out in great curved rows, in a half circle, like the ripples spreading out from a stone dropped into water. The soldiers of each state will lie together, in their own section. In the centre, at the highest point, there will be a monument.

{img:landscape-designer-plans-hilltop}
And every grave will be the same. There will be no grand stones for the officers and small ones for the privates. All of them will be equal, side by side, as they were on the battlefield.

--

In the autumn, the work begins.

The soldiers are brought to the new cemetery, carefully and respectfully, one by one, and laid to rest in the curved rows on the hillside. Wherever their names are known, the names are written down, so that they can be remembered. Many of them have no names that anyone knows. They are buried among their comrades, with a small marker that says only that they are unknown.

{img:autumn-trees-cemetery-hill-workers}
The work goes on through the autumn and into the winter, slowly and quietly.

--

Meanwhile, Wills and the committee decide that there should be a ceremony, to dedicate the new cemetery.

They plan it for the nineteenth of November. They invite the most famous speaker in America, Edward Everett, to give the main address.

Everett is a distinguished old gentleman from Massachusetts, nearly seventy years old, who has been a congressman, a governor, a senator, the president of Harvard College, and the Secretary of State. He is famous for his long, beautiful speeches, which can go on for hours, and which people travel great distances to hear.

--

Then, a little later, the committee sends another invitation.

On the second of November, David Wills writes a letter to the President of the United States, Abraham Lincoln, inviting him to come to Gettysburg, and, after Mr. Everett's oration, to say a few appropriate remarks, to formally set apart these grounds to their sacred use.

The committee does not really expect the President to come. He is very busy, with the war, and with everything else. And the invitation has come rather late.

But Lincoln accepts.

--

In Gettysburg, the news causes great excitement.

The President is coming, people tell each other. Abraham Lincoln himself is coming to Gettysburg.

{img:town-preparing-flags-november}
The town begins to prepare. The hotels are full, and still more visitors are expected, thousands of them, from all over the country. Every family offers rooms to guests. The streets are swept and cleaned. Flags and bunting are hung from the windows. A wooden platform is built at the new cemetery, for the speakers to stand on.

David Wills invites the President to stay in his own house, on the Diamond, and Lincoln accepts that too.

--

In the White House in Washington, President Lincoln thinks about what he will say.

He is fifty-four years old, very tall and thin, with a lined, weathered face, a dark beard, and deep-set grey eyes that are often sad, but that light up when he tells a story or a joke. He has been President for nearly three years, through the hardest time in his country's history. He is tired, and the war has worn him down.

{img:lincoln-writing-by-window-evening}
He has been asked to say only a few words. And so he decides to say only a few, but to choose them very carefully.

He begins to write them down, on a sheet of paper, in his clear, careful handwriting, sitting at his desk by the window, with the autumn light fading over the city.
