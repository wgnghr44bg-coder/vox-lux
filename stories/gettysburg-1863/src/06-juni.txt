On the afternoon of Friday, the twenty-sixth of June, 1863, it is raining in Gettysburg.

It is a grey, drizzly day, and the streets are muddy. Tillie Pierce is at school, in Mrs. Eyster's seminary, bending over her lessons, when someone outside begins to shout that the Rebels are coming.

The teacher closes the books and tells the girls to go straight home.

--

Tillie runs home along the wet pavement, with her skirts held up out of the mud.

When she gets there, she climbs up to look out of the window. And then she sees them: a great crowd of men on horseback, riding into town along the Chambersburg Pike from the west, and then foot soldiers, many of them, marching in long, ragged columns, in grey and brown clothes, some of them barefoot, splashing through the mud.

{img:confederate-soldiers-march-rain-town}
They are the soldiers of the Confederate army, under a general named Jubal Early.

--

The soldiers do not stay long.

They gather in the Diamond, in the rain, and their general sends a message to the town council, asking for food and supplies: flour and bacon, sugar and coffee, salt, onions, a thousand pairs of shoes, five hundred hats, or else a large sum of money.

The council members reply, politely, that the town does not have such things to give. The storekeepers have sent most of their goods away. The bank has sent away its money.

The general is not pleased, but there is not much he can do. His soldiers take what they can find in the stores and the cellars, and a few horses from the stables.

--

One of the horses they take belongs to the Pierce family.

Tillie watches from the window as two soldiers lead it out of the yard behind her house. Her father, standing in the doorway, can do nothing to stop them. Tillie is indignant. It is the first time she has seen the war with her own eyes, and she does not like it.

That night, the soldiers camp on the edge of town. In the morning, they march away again, eastward, toward York.

The people of Gettysburg come out of their houses, look at each other, and let out a long breath.

--

For a few days, things are quiet.

People talk of nothing else, of course. They tell each other what they saw, what the soldiers said, what was taken from whom. Some of them say that the Rebels were not so very frightening after all, that they were polite enough, and very tired, and very poorly dressed.

{img:townspeople-talking-diamond-square}
But the rumours keep coming. The main Southern army, people say, is close, just beyond the mountains. And the Northern army is coming up from Maryland to meet it.

--

Then, on the morning of Tuesday, the thirtieth of June, there are soldiers on the roads again.

This time, they come from the south, along the Emmitsburg Road, and they are wearing blue.

They are Union cavalry, about three thousand men on horseback, under a general named John Buford. They ride into Gettysburg in a long, dusty column, with their sabres jingling and their flags flying, and the people of the town come running out of their houses to greet them.

--

{img:union-cavalry-riding-through-town-cheering}
It is a joyful scene.

The women and girls stand on the pavements, waving their handkerchiefs and cheering. Some of them bring out bread and cakes and cups of water for the soldiers. The children run alongside the horses. Everyone feels much safer now.

Tillie Pierce and her friends from school stand at the corner of Washington Street, and as the cavalrymen ride by, they sing to them, the patriotic songs they have learned at school. The soldiers smile, and touch their caps, and some of them call out their thanks.

Tillie will remember that moment for the rest of her life.

--

General Buford is a quiet, weathered man of about thirty-seven, from Kentucky, with a drooping moustache and a calm, thoughtful manner. He is an experienced cavalry officer, and he does not like what he sees.

He rides up onto the ridge by the seminary and looks out to the west, toward the mountains. His scouts have told him that a large Southern force is camped just a few miles away, along the Chambersburg Pike.

He studies the roads and the ridges carefully. He knows that whoever holds the high ground around this town will have a great advantage, if there is a battle here. And he decides that his men will stay, and hold it, until the rest of the Union army can arrive.

--

That evening, Buford's men make camp on the ridges west and north of town.

Their campfires flicker across the fields in the dusk. Their horses graze in the meadows. A few of the soldiers walk into town to buy food, or to talk with the people, and some of the families invite them in for supper.

{img:cavalry-campfires-fields-dusk}
Professor Jacobs, at the college, goes out at nine o'clock to read his thermometer, as he always does, and writes down the temperature in his book.

In the house on Baltimore Street, Tillie Pierce goes to bed full of excitement, thinking about the soldiers and the songs.

It is a warm, still night. The stars are out. And in the camps along the ridges, the cavalrymen sleep beside their horses, waiting for the morning.
