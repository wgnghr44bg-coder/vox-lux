In the early hours of the fourth of July, it begins to rain.

At first it is only a light, warm drizzle, falling softly on the roofs of Gettysburg and on the fields beyond. Then, as the day goes on, it grows heavier and heavier, until by the afternoon it is pouring down in sheets, drumming on the roofs and running in streams down the streets.

Professor Jacobs, at the college, writes in his book that it rained, and he measures how much.

--

It is Independence Day, the birthday of the United States, eighty-seven years after the Declaration of Independence was signed in Philadelphia.

In other years, there would be a parade in Gettysburg today, with flags and a brass band, and speeches in the Diamond, and picnics in the meadows, and fireworks in the evening.

This year, there is only the rain.

--

{img:townspeople-opening-doors-morning-rain}
In the morning, the people of Gettysburg begin to notice that something has changed.

The Confederate soldiers who have been in the streets for three days are gone. During the night, they have quietly pulled back out of the town, to the ridge in the west. People open their doors and their shutters and look out cautiously. Then, slowly, they come out into the wet streets.

Soon, Union soldiers come down from Cemetery Hill into the town. And the people of Gettysburg come out of their houses to greet them, and some of them cry, and some of them laugh, and some of them simply stand in the rain and say nothing at all.

--

All day long, the two armies stay where they are, on their ridges, watching each other across the rainy fields.

Neither side attacks. Both are exhausted.

On the ridges, the soldiers huddle under their oilcloth blankets and their little tents, trying to keep dry, making coffee over fires that sputter and smoke in the rain. Some of them simply lie in the mud and sleep, too tired to care.

--

That evening, in the rain and the darkness, General Lee's army begins to leave.

It goes slowly, westward, back along the roads toward the mountains, and Maryland, and Virginia. First goes the long train of wagons and ambulances carrying the wounded, so long that it stretches for seventeen miles along the muddy roads, through the night. Then go the cannons and the supply wagons, and then the soldiers, marching in the rain, with their heads down.

{img:wagon-train-rain-night-mountain-road}
All night long, the wheels creak and the horses splash through the mud, and the rain falls and falls.

--

On the same day, far away in the west, another great event has taken place.

On the banks of the Mississippi River, in the state of Mississippi, the city of Vicksburg has surrendered to the Union army of General Ulysses S. Grant, after a long siege of nearly seven weeks.

News travels slowly in 1863. It will take several days for the people of Gettysburg to hear of it. But when they do, they will understand that, on the same Independence Day, the war has changed. Nobody can know yet how long it will go on. But many people begin to feel, for the first time, that the end is coming.

--

{img:farmhouse-returning-family-rain}
Out in the countryside, Tillie Pierce is still with Mrs. Shriver and her daughters, at a farmhouse south of the battlefield.

For several more days, they cannot go home. The roads are full of soldiers and wagons, and the fields are full of the wounded. Tillie helps wherever she can, carrying water, rolling bandages, sitting with the soldiers who are lonely and ill, and writing letters to their families for them.

At last, on the seventh of July, she sets off for home, walking back along the muddy road toward the town.

--

The walk home is long and slow.

The countryside she passes through is not the countryside she left a week before. The fences are gone, pulled down and burned for firewood. The wheat fields are trampled flat. The orchards are broken. Everywhere there are signs of the battle, and the soldiers are busy, quietly, with the work of caring for the wounded and laying the fallen to rest.

Tillie walks with her eyes down, holding her skirts out of the mud, and does not look too closely. She is fifteen years old, and she has seen too much already.

--

When she reaches Baltimore Street at last, her mother is standing at the door.

Her mother does not recognize her at first, because she is so tired and pale and muddy. Then she does, and she runs down the steps and throws her arms around her, and they stand there together in the street for a long time.

{img:mother-daughter-reunion-doorstep}
The whole family is safe. The house is still standing, though there are marks of bullets on the walls, and soldiers have been in and out of it for days.

Tillie goes inside, and washes her face, and sits down at the kitchen table, and eats a slice of her mother's bread with butter. It is the best thing she has ever tasted.

--

That night, the rain stops, and the clouds break apart, and the stars come out over Gettysburg.

The town is very quiet. In almost every house, there are wounded soldiers, lying on beds and on the floors, being cared for by the families. In the churches and the schools and the barns, the doctors and the nurses are still at work.

{img:night-town-lamps-every-window}
And in almost every window in the town, a lamp is burning, all night long.
