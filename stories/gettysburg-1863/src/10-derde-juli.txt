Friday, the third of July, dawns hot and still over Gettysburg.

At seven o'clock, Professor Jacobs writes in his book: seventy-three degrees, and clouds. By two in the afternoon, it will be eighty-seven, the hottest day of the battle.

Early in the morning, there is fighting again on Culp's Hill, east of the cemetery, and the sound of it echoes through the streets of the town. Then, by late morning, that too dies away.

--

On Baltimore Street, near the southern edge of town, Jennie Wade is at her sister's house.

Her sister Georgia has just had a baby, a few days before the battle began, and Jennie and her mother have come to help look after her. The little brick house stands right between the two armies, with the Union soldiers on Cemetery Hill just above it, and the Confederates in the houses of the town just below.

{img:small-brick-house-kitchen-bread-dough}
That morning, Jennie is in the kitchen, kneading dough to make bread for the soldiers. She has been baking for them for two days, and handing out the loaves to the Union men who come to the door.

At about half past eight, a stray bullet passes through two wooden doors, and Jennie is struck, and she dies at once, quietly, in the kitchen, with her hands still in the dough.

She is twenty years old. She is the only person who lived in Gettysburg to lose her life in the battle.

--

The news spreads slowly, from house to house.

Her mother and her sister are carried down into the cellar. The soldiers wrap Jennie in a quilt and lay her gently in the cellar beside them.

Far away, in Virginia, her young man Jack Skelly has been wounded in an earlier battle, and is lying in a hospital. He will never hear the news, because he too will pass away, not long after, without knowing what happened to her.

--

By late morning, a great stillness has settled over the battlefield.

The soldiers on both ridges lie in the hot sun, waiting. Some of them sleep. Some write letters. Some eat what little food they have. The fields between the ridges lie empty and quiet, shimmering in the heat. Bees buzz in the clover. A bird sings in a tree.

{img:hot-noon-ridge-waiting-soldiers-shade}
On Cemetery Ridge, a Union officer later remembers, the generals sit down together in the shade for a lunch of stewed chicken, potatoes and bread, and coffee.

It is the calm before the last great storm.

--

At about one o'clock in the afternoon, two cannons fire, one after the other, from the Confederate line on Seminary Ridge.

It is a signal.

And then, all along the ridge, more than a hundred and fifty cannons begin to fire at once.

--

The sound is like nothing anyone has ever heard before.

It is so loud that people hear it many, many miles away, rolling across the hills like distant thunder. In the town, the windows rattle and the dishes shake on the shelves. The ground trembles. Great clouds of white smoke roll across the fields and hide the sun.

{img:white-smoke-cloud-over-fields-distant}
In the cellars of Gettysburg, the families sit in the dark and hold each other, and wait. On Cemetery Hill, the soldiers lie flat behind their stone walls. The Union cannons answer back, and for nearly two hours, the noise goes on and on.

Then, slowly, it fades away, and the smoke begins to drift off across the fields.

--

Out of the woods on Seminary Ridge, a long line of soldiers comes into view.

There are about twelve thousand of them, in long grey lines, a mile wide, with their flags flying above them, marching forward across the open fields toward the Union line on Cemetery Ridge.

Many of them are the men of a general named George Pickett, and so this moment will become known as Pickett's Charge.

They march steadily, in perfect order, across the fields and the fences and the Emmitsburg Road, as if they were on parade. On Cemetery Ridge, the Union soldiers watch them come, and some of them say, afterwards, that it was the most magnificent thing they had ever seen.

--

The march across the fields takes a little under an hour.

When it is over, the long grey lines have been turned back. A few of the men have reached the stone wall on Cemetery Ridge, near a little clump of trees, and for a few minutes, they cross it. But then they are stopped, and the rest of the men turn and walk slowly back across the fields, toward their own ridge, helping their wounded friends along.

{img:general-lee-on-horse-meeting-soldiers}
General Lee rides out to meet them as they come back. He speaks to them gently. It is all my fault, he tells them. It is I who have lost this fight.

--

As the sun goes down on the third of July, the battlefield falls silent at last.

Along the ridges, and in the fields between them, the soldiers lie down to rest, exhausted. The surgeons work on into the night. The lanterns move slowly back and forth across the dark fields.

{img:sunset-quiet-ridge-stone-wall}
In the town, the people come up out of their cellars, one by one, and stand at their doors, and look out at the darkening sky.

The great battle of Gettysburg is over. Over the three days, about fifty thousand men on both sides have been killed, or wounded, or are missing. It is the largest battle ever fought in North America.

The night is warm and heavy, and in the west, over the mountains, the clouds are gathering.
