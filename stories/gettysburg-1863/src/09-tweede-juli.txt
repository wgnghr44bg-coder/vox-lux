On the morning of Thursday, the second of July, the town of Gettysburg is strangely quiet.

The Confederate soldiers are in the streets now. They stand at the corners and sit on the doorsteps, resting, cleaning their rifles, cooking their breakfast over small fires in the alleys. Some of them knock on doors and ask, politely, for bread or water. Some of them simply go into empty houses and take what they need.

The people of the town stay indoors, with their shutters closed, peering out through the cracks.

--

Professor Jacobs reads his thermometer at seven o'clock. It is seventy-four degrees, cloudy, with a light wind.

For most of the morning, there is very little fighting. The two armies are getting ready. Along the ridges south of town, the Union soldiers are digging in, building low walls of stones and logs and fence rails. On the ridge to the west, the Confederates are moving into position, behind the trees along Seminary Ridge.

{img:two-ridges-fields-between-summer}
Between the two ridges lie the farm fields: wheat and oats and corn, orchards and meadows, a few farmhouses and barns, with wooden fences running across them. It is perhaps a mile across, from one ridge to the other.

--

Out at the Weikert farm, Tillie Pierce wakes up early.

The farm is busy from the first light. Soldiers are everywhere, in the yard, in the orchard, along the road. Officers ride up to the house and ride away again. The family bakes bread all morning, and Tillie carries water to the soldiers, and talks to them, and listens to their stories.

One young soldier shows her a picture of his mother. Another asks her to write to his family, if anything happens to him. She promises that she will.

--

Behind the Weikert farm, rising steep and rocky above the fields, is Little Round Top.

On that morning, it is almost empty. There are a few signalmen at the top, waving their flags to send messages across the battlefield, but no soldiers to defend it.

In the early afternoon, a young engineer officer on the Union side, a general named Gouverneur Warren, rides up to the top of the hill to look around. He looks out to the west, across the fields, and sees the glint of sunlight on rifles and bayonets, among the trees on the far side.

{img:officer-on-rocky-hilltop-telescope}
He realizes, with a jolt, that if the Confederates take this hill, they will be able to fire down along the whole Union line. And there is no one here to stop them.

--

Warren sends messengers galloping off in every direction, to find soldiers who can come and hold the hill.

At about four o'clock in the afternoon, the great attack begins.

From the west, the Confederate soldiers of General Longstreet come forward, across the fields and through the woods, toward the southern end of the Union line. The fighting spreads across the Peach Orchard, and the Wheatfield, and the rocky valley of the Devil's Den, and up the slopes of the Round Tops.

--

At the Weikert farm, the noise is tremendous.

The family and their guests are told by the soldiers that they must leave at once, because the farm is now in the middle of the battle. Tillie and Mrs. Shriver and the little girls hurry away along the road, further south and east, to another farmhouse, away from the fighting.

{img:women-hurrying-along-fence-road-smoke}
Behind them, the smoke rises over the hills, and the sound rolls across the countryside like a great storm.

--

On the slopes of Little Round Top, the soldiers who answered Warren's call arrive just in time.

Among them is Colonel Joshua Chamberlain, the professor from Maine, with his regiment, the 20th Maine. They are placed at the very end of the Union line, on the southern slope of the hill, among the boulders and the trees. Chamberlain is told that he must hold this position at all costs.

For about an hour and a half, his men hold the slope, against attack after attack, coming up the hill through the trees. When they have almost no ammunition left, Chamberlain orders his men forward, down the slope, and the attackers fall back.

The hill is held. The end of the Union line is safe.

Years later, Chamberlain will receive the Medal of Honor for that afternoon. He will become a general, and governor of Maine, and president of his old college.

--

All through the late afternoon and evening, the fighting goes on, in the Wheatfield and the Peach Orchard and along the ridge.

Then, as the sun goes down, it slowly dies away. By nightfall, the Confederates have taken the Peach Orchard and the Devil's Den. But the Union line on the ridge has held, from Cemetery Hill all the way south to Little Round Top.

--

{img:night-battlefield-lanterns-fields}
When night falls, the fields between the ridges are quiet again, under the stars.

Across the darkness, small lights move slowly back and forth. They are the lanterns of the soldiers and the surgeons and the stretcher-bearers, searching for the wounded, carrying them back to the field hospitals in the barns and farmhouses behind the lines.

At the Trostle farm, at the Weikert farm, at farms all along the ridge, the houses and barns are filled with wounded men. The surgeons work all night, by the light of candles and lanterns.

--

That night, the Union generals meet in the little white farmhouse of the widow Lydia Leister, on the Taneytown Road, behind the centre of their line, where General Meade has made his headquarters.

{img:small-farmhouse-lamplit-generals-night}
They crowd into the tiny front room, around a small table, by the light of a single candle, and discuss what to do next. Should they stay and fight, or should they retreat?

One by one, the generals give their opinions. They will stay, they agree. They will wait for whatever comes tomorrow.

Outside, the night is warm and close. And at the farmhouse where Tillie Pierce has found shelter, she lies awake for a long time, listening to the silence after the storm.
