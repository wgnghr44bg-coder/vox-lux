In the weeks after the battle, Gettysburg becomes one great hospital.

When the two armies leave, they leave behind more than twenty thousand wounded men, from both sides, in a town of only two thousand four hundred people. They are everywhere: in the churches, the schools, the college, the seminary, the courthouse, the warehouses, the barns, and in nearly every private house.

--

The people of Gettysburg take care of them.

The women make soup and bake bread, from morning till night. They bring water, and wash the soldiers' faces, and sit beside them, and fan them in the heat. They write letters for those who cannot write. They read to them from the Bible. They sing to them softly in the evening.

{img:woman-feeding-soup-wounded-soldier-house}
The men of the town carry stretchers, and dig, and help the doctors. The children run errands, carrying messages and buckets of water and baskets of food.

They make no difference between the soldiers in blue and the soldiers in grey. A wounded man is a wounded man.

--

One of the young women of the town is named Sallie Myers.

She is twenty-one or twenty-two years old, a schoolteacher, with a gentle face and dark hair. When the first wounded men were carried into the Catholic church on High Street, during the first day of the battle, she went in to help, although she had never nursed anyone before, and she was frightened.

She has stayed ever since. She brings the men water and food. She washes their faces. She holds their hands, and talks to them, and listens to them talk about their homes and their families. One soldier, a young sergeant from Pennsylvania, she takes into her own house, and her family looks after him there.

She writes in her diary at night, when she can, and many years later, her words will help us to understand what those weeks were like.

--

Soon, help begins to arrive from outside.

Trains come into Gettysburg from Baltimore and Philadelphia and Harrisburg, loaded with doctors and nurses, with food and bandages and medicines, with clean sheets and shirts and blankets. They are sent by aid societies in the cities of the North, the Sanitary Commission and the Christian Commission, and by churches and families all over the country.

{img:supply-train-unloading-station-volunteers}
Volunteers arrive too, men and women who have come to help, and to search for their own sons and husbands and brothers among the wounded. The little railway station is crowded with people, day and night.

--

In the middle of July, the army sets up a great tent hospital on a hill east of the town, beside the York Pike.

It is called Camp Letterman, after the army's chief medical officer, Jonathan Letterman. There are hundreds of white canvas tents, set out in long, neat rows, among the trees, with a kitchen and a bakery, a store, and a cemetery nearby. The wounded men are moved there, slowly, from the churches and houses of the town.

{img:white-tent-hospital-rows-trees}
It is a calm, orderly place. The tents are clean and airy. The nurses walk up and down the rows, with their baskets and their buckets. In the evenings, a band sometimes plays, and the soldiers who can walk sit outside their tents in the long golden light.

By the end of November, nearly all of the wounded have been well enough to be sent home or to other hospitals, and Camp Letterman is closed.

--

For the people of Gettysburg, the days in July and August are long and tiring.

The women are up before dawn, baking bread and boiling soup, and they go to bed long after dark. The children are given jobs too. Little girls carry cups of water and plates of food from bed to bed. Boys run to the stores for supplies, or to the station to meet the trains.

There is very little time to rest. But in the evenings, when the heat goes out of the day, some of the families sit for a little while on their front steps, as they used to do, and look up at the stars, and talk quietly with their neighbours about everything that has happened.

Then they go back inside, to sit with the soldiers through the night.

--

Up on Cemetery Hill, Elizabeth Thorn is hard at work.

When she came back to the gatehouse after the battle, with her family, she found it badly damaged, and the cemetery full of signs of the fighting. Her furniture was broken, and her bedding gone.

But there was work to be done. And so, in the hot July days, though she was expecting a child herself in a few months' time, Elizabeth and her elderly father set about laying to rest the soldiers who had fallen on and around the hill. It was hard, sad work, and she never forgot it.

In the autumn, she gave birth to a healthy baby girl, whom she named Rose.

--

Little by little, life in Gettysburg goes back to something like normal.

The fences are rebuilt. The farmers go out into their fields and gather what they can of the harvest. The shops reopen. The schools open again in the autumn. Tillie Pierce goes back to Mrs. Eyster's seminary, and sits at her desk, and learns her lessons as before.

{img:farmer-rebuilding-fence-late-summer}
But the town will never be quite the same.

Every family has a story to tell about those days. Every house has its marks. And out on the ridges and in the fields around the town, in hundreds of shallow places, often marked only with a piece of wood with a name written on it in pencil, lie the soldiers who did not go home.

--

Many people in Gettysburg feel that this is not right.

Those young men gave their lives here, they say, far from their families. They deserve a proper resting place, a place where they can lie together in peace, with their names remembered, and where their families can come to visit them.

{img:lawyer-writing-letter-study-lamp}
And one man in the town decides to do something about it.

His name is David Wills.
