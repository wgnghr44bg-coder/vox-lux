While Gettysburg sleeps, the two great armies are moving toward it, along every road from the south and the west.

They are enormous. The Union army, the Army of the Potomac, has about ninety thousand men. The Confederate army, which is called the Army of Northern Virginia, has about seventy-five thousand. Together, they are more than sixty times the population of the little town that lies between them.

--

On the roads, the armies stretch out for miles.

There are columns of infantry, marching four abreast, with their rifles on their shoulders and their blanket rolls slung across their chests. There are long lines of cannons and ammunition wagons, pulled by teams of horses, rumbling over the stones. There are supply wagons, with white canvas covers, loaded with food and tents and medicine. There are ambulances, and herds of cattle, and officers on horseback, riding up and down the lines.

{img:supply-wagons-white-covers-road}
The dust rises in great clouds over the roads, so thick that the men at the back of the column can barely see the men in front of them. It settles on their faces and their uniforms, until everyone is the same pale brown colour.

--

It is hot, this summer. Very hot.

The soldiers march for fifteen or twenty miles a day, sometimes more, carrying heavy loads. Their feet are blistered. Their shoes are worn through. They are thirsty all the time, and whenever the column passes a farm with a well or a pump, the men break ranks and crowd around it, filling their canteens.

The farm families come out to watch them pass. Some bring out buckets of water, or bread, or milk. The soldiers thank them, and march on.

--

In the evening, the armies make camp in the fields beside the road.

The men pile their rifles in little stacks, light small fires, and make coffee in tin cups, the most important thing of the whole day. They fry salt pork in their mess tins and break hard crackers into it. They take off their shoes and rub their aching feet.

Then they sit around the fires and talk, about home, about their families, about the girls they left behind. Someone takes out a harmonica, or a fiddle, and plays a sad old song. Someone writes a letter by the light of the fire. Someone falls asleep sitting up.

--

Most of the soldiers on both sides are young men, in their late teens or early twenties.

They are farm boys, mostly, and clerks and labourers and students and craftsmen. Some of them have been in the army for two years and have fought in many battles. Others are new, and have never seen a battle at all.

{img:young-soldiers-faces-campfire-night}
In many ways, they are very much alike, the soldiers in blue and the soldiers in grey. They come from the same kind of homes, they speak the same language, they sing many of the same songs, and they pray to the same God. In quiet moments, when the armies are camped close together, they have sometimes called out to each other across the lines, and traded coffee for tobacco.

--

The Union army has a new commander.

Just three days ago, on the twenty-eighth of June, President Lincoln gave the command of the Army of the Potomac to General George Gordon Meade.

{img:general-meade-tent-maps}
Meade is a tall, thin, serious man of forty-seven, from Philadelphia, with a grey beard, large spectacles, and a sharp temper. His soldiers call him, not very kindly, a goggle-eyed old snapping turtle. But he is a careful, sensible, experienced officer, and he is fighting to defend his own home state.

He was woken in the middle of the night in his tent to be told the news. He thought at first that he was being arrested.

--

Among the many thousands of soldiers marching north, there is a man named Joshua Lawrence Chamberlain.

Before the war, Chamberlain was a professor at Bowdoin College, in the state of Maine, far to the north. He taught languages and rhetoric, and he could read Latin and Greek and Hebrew, and several other languages besides. He was married, with two young children, and he loved music and books.

In 1862, he asked the college for a leave of absence, to travel and study in Europe. Instead, he joined the army. Now he is thirty-four years old, a colonel, in command of a regiment called the 20th Maine, about three hundred and eighty men, most of them farmers and fishermen and lumbermen from the coast and the forests of Maine.

He writes to his wife Fanny whenever he can.

--

On the other side, there are men like James Longstreet, one of Lee's most important generals.

Longstreet is a big, bearded, quiet man from Georgia, solid and stubborn, whom Lee calls his old war horse. Just a year and a half ago, three of his young children died of fever in a single week, in the winter of 1862. He has never really recovered from it. He does not talk about it, but his friends say that he has become quieter and more serious since then.

--

{img:night-camp-tents-stars-hills}
On the night of the thirtieth of June, the armies are spread out across the countryside, in camps along the roads, under the summer stars.

Some of them are only a few miles from Gettysburg. Others are a day's march or more away. Neither General Lee nor General Meade has planned to fight at Gettysburg. Neither of them knows exactly where the other's army is.

But the ten roads of Gettysburg are drawing them both in, like water running down into a valley.

--

In the camps, the soldiers lie down on the ground beside their fires, wrapped in their blankets, with their heads on their knapsacks.

The fires die down to glowing embers. The sentries walk slowly up and down, watching the dark. The horses shift and stamp in their lines. Somewhere, a whip-poor-will calls, over and over, from the edge of the woods.

The night is warm and short. And in a few hours, before the sun is up, the bugles will sound, and the men will roll up their blankets and drink their coffee, and set off once more along the roads, toward the little town in the hills.
