The war went on for another year and a half after that November day.

It ended in the spring of 1865, when General Lee surrendered to General Grant at a little village in Virginia called Appomattox Court House. Slavery was ended throughout the United States. And just a few days later, President Lincoln lost his life in Washington, and the whole country mourned him.

--

The people of Gettysburg went on with their lives.

Tillie Pierce grew up, and married a lawyer named Horace Alleman, and moved to the town of Selinsgrove, in central Pennsylvania. In 1889, she wrote down her memories of the battle, in a small book called At Gettysburg, or What a Girl Saw and Heard of the Battle. She lived until 1914, and she never forgot those days.

{img:older-woman-writing-memoir-parlour}
Elizabeth Thorn stayed on at the gatehouse with her husband Peter, who came home safely from the war, and they looked after the cemetery for many more years. She lived to be seventy-five, and she is buried in Evergreen Cemetery, on the hill she cared for.

John Burns became a hero, and people came from far away to shake his hand. Professor Jacobs wrote one of the first books about the battle, using his weather notes. Sallie Myers married, and later wrote about her memories too.

--

Fifty years after the battle, in the summer of 1913, something remarkable happened at Gettysburg.

More than fifty thousand old soldiers, from both North and South, came back to the battlefield for a great reunion. Most of them were in their seventies now, white-haired and stooped. They camped together in a huge town of tents on the fields where they had once fought.

On the anniversary of the third day, the old soldiers who had made the long march across the fields walked slowly toward the stone wall on Cemetery Ridge once more. And waiting for them, on the other side of the wall, were the old soldiers who had held it.

This time, they reached across the wall, and shook hands.

--

{img:gettysburg-battlefield-today-monuments}
Today, the battlefield of Gettysburg is a national park.

The fields and the ridges and the woods are very much as they were in 1863. The farms are still there, the Trostle barn and the Weikert farmhouse, and the little white house of the widow Leister. The stone walls still run along Cemetery Ridge. The fences have been rebuilt, in the old style, and in summer the wheat still grows in the fields between the ridges.

All over the battlefield, there are monuments, more than thirteen hundred of them: statues of generals on horseback, granite soldiers standing on guard, stone towers and simple markers. Each regiment has its own, placed where its men stood.

--

On Little Round Top, the boulders are still scattered over the slopes among the trees, and the view from the top is still wide and beautiful, out over the Wheatfield and the Peach Orchard and the Devil's Den.

{img:little-round-top-view-sunset-today}
On a quiet evening, visitors sit on the rocks at the top and watch the sun go down behind the South Mountains, as the shadows stretch out across the fields where so much happened, so long ago.

On the southern slope, where Chamberlain's men stood, there is a small, simple monument to the 20th Maine.

--

The town of Gettysburg is still a small town, with about seven thousand people.

Many of the old buildings are still there. The Wills house on the Diamond is now a museum, and visitors can see the room where Lincoln stayed. The Shriver house on Baltimore Street has been restored, just as it was in 1863. The little brick house where Jennie Wade was baking bread still stands. And on the hill, the old gatehouse of Evergreen Cemetery is still there, with its arched passage, just as Elizabeth Thorn knew it.

Every year, on the nineteenth of November, people gather in the national cemetery to remember the day that Lincoln spoke there, and someone reads his words aloud.

--

The Soldiers' National Cemetery is a peaceful place.

The graves lie in their great curved rows on the hillside, just as William Saunders planned them, under tall old trees, with the soldiers of each state together. The small stones are all the same, plain and low, set into the grass. In the middle stands the monument, a tall column with a statue of Liberty on top.

{img:national-cemetery-curved-rows-trees-evening}
Near the entrance, there is a bronze tablet with the words of the Gettysburg Address. And close by, there is a memorial marking the place where, it is believed, President Lincoln stood that day, on the wooden platform, holding his sheet of paper.

--

In the evening, when the visitors have gone, the battlefield grows quiet.

The light goes golden over the fields and the ridges, and then fades to grey. Deer come out of the woods to graze in the meadows. The swallows swoop low over the wheat. In the farmhouses along the ridge, lights come on in the windows.

{img:battlefield-dusk-deer-fields-fireflies}
On summer nights, the fireflies rise out of the long grass in their thousands, drifting slowly above the fields, as they did on those July evenings in 1863.

--

So we leave Gettysburg for tonight, a quiet town among the farms and orchards of Pennsylvania, with the stars coming out over the ridges and the long curved rows on the hill.
