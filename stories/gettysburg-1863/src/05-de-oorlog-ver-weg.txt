For the families of Gettysburg, the war arrives mostly in the form of letters.

Every few days, the post comes in on the train from Hanover, and the postmaster sorts it into the little wooden boxes at the post office. Mothers and wives come to ask whether there is anything for them. When there is a letter from a son or a husband in the army, they hurry home with it, and the whole family gathers in the parlour to hear it read aloud.

--

The letters are full of ordinary things.

The weather, which is too hot, or too cold, or too wet. The food, which is mostly hard crackers and salt pork and coffee, and never enough. The long marches, the blisters, the mud. A new friend in the regiment. A good night's sleep in a dry tent. A request for a pair of warm socks, or a new shirt, or a pot of jam.

{img:soldier-writing-letter-camp}
Sometimes, there is news of a battle. The letters about battles are shorter, and quieter. The soldiers do not like to say too much about what they have seen, so as not to worry their families at home.

At the end, there is always the same thing: love to everyone, and I hope to be home soon.

--

The women of Gettysburg do what they can for the soldiers.

They meet in each other's parlours and in the churches, to sew shirts and knit socks and roll bandages from old linen sheets. They pack boxes of food and clothes and send them off to the army. They raise money for the hospitals.

On summer afternoons, the circle of women sits by the open windows, their needles flashing, talking quietly about the news and their families, while the younger girls make lint for dressing wounds, and the little children play on the rug at their feet.

--

The men who have stayed at home talk about the war constantly.

They read the newspapers aloud in the hotel lobbies and the stores, and argue about the generals and the politicians. They follow the movements of the armies on maps pinned to the walls. They wait for the telegraph to bring the latest news.

Some of the older men say that they would go and fight themselves, if only they were younger.

--

{img:old-man-cobbler-workshop}
One of them is an old man named John Burns.

John Burns is about sixty-nine years old, a short, stubborn, white-haired man with a craggy face and strong opinions. He is a shoemaker by trade, and for some years he has been the town constable. As a young man, he fought in the war against Britain in 1812, and he is very proud of it.

When the war began in 1861, he tried to join the army, but they told him he was too old. He was not pleased. He has been grumbling about it ever since, to anyone who will listen.

--

There are other worries in Gettysburg, too.

The town is close to the border between North and South. Just across the state line, in Maryland, there are families who support the Confederacy. And for the Black families of Gettysburg, the nearness of the border is a real danger. Before the war, people escaping from slavery in the South often passed through Gettysburg on their way north, helped by friends in the town, and there were always men who came looking for them.

{img:quiet-farm-lane-evening-north}
If the Southern army comes into Pennsylvania, the Black families of Gettysburg know that they may be in great danger, and many of them have already made plans to leave quickly, if they must.

--

In the late spring of 1863, the news from Virginia is not good.

In early May, at a place called Chancellorsville, the Southern army, led by General Robert E. Lee, has won a great victory over the Northern army, which is called the Army of the Potomac.

General Lee is the most famous soldier in the Confederacy. He is about fifty-six years old, a dignified, grey-bearded man from an old Virginia family, calm and courteous, and greatly loved by his soldiers.

After his victory, people in the North begin to wonder what he will do next. And soon there are rumours that he is planning to march north, into Maryland and Pennsylvania, to carry the war into the North for the first time.

--

In Gettysburg, the rumours grow stronger week by week.

People say that the Southern army is already moving through the valleys west of the mountains. That they are taking horses and cattle and food from the farms. That they are coming toward Harrisburg, or toward Philadelphia, or toward Baltimore.

Nobody knows for sure. But the storekeepers begin to pack up their goods and send them away by train, for safety. The bank sends its money to Philadelphia. Farmers begin to drive their horses away over the hills, to hide them in the woods.

--

{img:june-fields-wheat-golden-farmhouse}
And still, the summer comes on, warm and green and beautiful.

The wheat in the fields is turning golden. The cherries in the orchards are ripe, and the children climb the trees to pick them. The hay is being cut and stacked in the meadows. The days are long, and the evenings are full of fireflies.

On the hill above the town, Elizabeth Thorn tends the cemetery. In the college, Professor Jacobs reads his thermometer. On Baltimore Street, Tillie Pierce and her friends sit on the step in the evening and talk about the rumours, half excited and half afraid.

And somewhere beyond the mountains to the west, the two great armies are moving north.
