The morning of Wednesday, the first of July, 1863, begins quietly in Gettysburg, with a light mist over the fields and the sound of birds in the orchards.

At seven o'clock, Professor Jacobs goes out to read his thermometer, as usual. It is seventy-two degrees, with light clouds and a gentle breeze from the south. A warm summer day.

--

West of town, along the Chambersburg Pike, General Buford's cavalrymen are watching the road.

At a little after seven, they see what they have been waiting for: a long column of Confederate infantry coming down the road from the west, toward the town.

A Union officer raises his carbine and fires, a single shot. It is said to be the first shot of the battle of Gettysburg.

--

{img:distant-ridge-smoke-morning-farms}
In the town, people hear the sound of firing in the distance, coming from the west.

At first it is only a scattered popping, far away, like someone shooting at birds. Then it grows louder and steadier, and then there is a deep, heavy boom, and another, and another, as the cannons begin to fire.

People come out of their houses and stand in the streets, looking toward the west, where little white clouds of smoke are rising over the ridges. Some of the boys climb up onto the roofs to see better. Some of the older people go up into the cupola of the seminary, and the college, and the courthouse, until the soldiers tell them to come down.

--

On Baltimore Street, Tillie Pierce's mother makes a decision.

Mrs. Shriver, from up the street, is going out to her parents' farm, the Weikert farm, three miles south of town, on the Taneytown Road, at the foot of the Round Tops. It will be safer there, she says, away from the fighting.

Tillie's mother asks Mrs. Shriver to take Tillie with her. And so, a little before noon, Tillie sets off with Mrs. Shriver and her two little girls, walking south out of town, along the road past the cemetery, away from the sound of the guns.

Tillie does not know it, but she is walking straight toward the place where the greatest fighting of the whole battle will take place.

--

Out on the ridges west of town, Buford's men hold on through the morning, until the Union infantry begins to arrive, marching up the roads from the south at the double.

More and more men arrive on both sides, all through the day. The fighting spreads along the ridges to the west, and then to the north of town, across the farm fields and the orchards and the woods.

{img:seminary-cupola-officers-watching-smoke}
In the middle of the day, old John Burns, the shoemaker and constable, takes his old musket from the wall, puts on his tall hat and his best coat, and walks out of town toward the fighting. He finds some Union soldiers in a wood west of the seminary, and asks if he can join them. They look at him in amazement, and then they let him stay.

He will be wounded three times that day. But he will survive, and become famous.

--

In the afternoon, the town begins to fill with wounded soldiers.

They come in along the roads, walking, limping, carried by their friends, or lying in wagons. Doctors set up hospitals in the churches, the courthouse, the college, the seminary, and in many private houses. The women of the town come out with water and bandages and bread.

{img:church-turned-hospital-women-water}
The Christ Lutheran Church on Chambersburg Street fills with men lying on the pews and the floor. The Catholic church fills up. The courthouse fills up. Everywhere, the people of Gettysburg open their doors.

--

By late afternoon, the Union soldiers are being pushed back.

From the west and the north, the Confederate lines are coming forward, and the Union men are retreating, back through the town, through the streets and the alleys and the back gardens, toward the hill with the cemetery on it, south of town.

For a little while, the streets of Gettysburg are full of running men, and shouting, and confusion. The people of the town stay inside, in their cellars, with the doors locked and the shutters closed, listening to the sound of feet running past, and wondering what is happening.

--

In hundreds of houses all over the town, families sit huddled together in the dark of their cellars, among the potatoes and the jars of preserves, by the light of a single candle.

The children are frightened, and the mothers hold them close and hum to them softly. The fathers sit by the cellar steps, listening. Outside, the noise rolls over the town, and then slowly, slowly, it begins to move away, to the south.

--

By evening, it is quiet again.

The Union soldiers have gathered on Cemetery Hill and Culp's Hill, south of town, and are digging in along the ridge, behind stone walls and piles of fence rails. The Confederates hold the town itself, and the ridges to the west and the north.

{img:cemetery-hill-gatehouse-soldiers-evening}
On Cemetery Hill, the gatehouse of Evergreen Cemetery is suddenly in the middle of the Union army. Elizabeth Thorn cooks bread and coffee for the generals and the soldiers until late into the night, while her boys and her parents sleep. One of the officers asks her to show him the roads around the hill, and she walks out with him in the dusk, pointing them out one by one.

--

That night, Tillie Pierce is at the Weikert farm, at the foot of Little Round Top.

The house is full of soldiers coming and going, and the yard is full of horses. The Weikert family and their guests are busy all evening, baking bread and giving out water from the well to the soldiers who pass, hundreds and hundreds of them, marching up from the south to join the army on the ridge.

{img:farmhouse-well-water-soldiers-night}
Tillie carries buckets of water out to the road, and fills the soldiers' cups, and the soldiers thank her, and march on into the dark.

Late at night, she lies down on the floor of the farmhouse to sleep, tired out, with the sound of marching feet still going by outside on the road.
