On Wednesday, the eighteenth of November, 1863, a special train leaves Washington at noon, heading north.

It has four carriages. In one of them, sitting by the window, is President Lincoln. With him are his two private secretaries, John Nicolay and John Hay, a few members of his cabinet, some foreign diplomats, a military band, and a guard of soldiers.

--

The journey takes about six hours.

The train rolls north through the Maryland countryside, past bare brown fields and leafless woods, and little farms with smoke rising from their chimneys. It changes tracks at a place called Hanover Junction, in Pennsylvania, and then goes west, along a small branch line, through the town of Hanover, toward Gettysburg.

{img:railway-station-crowd-waving-flags}
At the stations along the way, people have gathered to see the President pass. At Hanover, a crowd stands on the platform, and they call out for Lincoln to come out and speak. He steps out onto the platform at the back of the carriage, and says a few friendly words, and the people cheer.

A little girl hands him a bunch of flowers. He bends down and thanks her.

--

The train arrives at Gettysburg station at dusk.

The little station is crowded with people, waiting in the cold to see the President. As he steps down from the train, tall and dark in his black coat and his tall black hat, there is a great murmur, and the people press forward to see him.

David Wills is there to meet him, with Edward Everett, who arrived two days ago. They shake hands, and walk together up Carlisle Street toward the Diamond, a few hundred yards away, with the crowd following behind them.

--

The Wills house is a large, comfortable brick house, three storeys high, on the corner of the square.

That evening, Mrs. Wills gives a dinner for the President and the other important guests. There are about two dozen people around the long table, in the warm, lamplit dining room. The President is quiet, but friendly and courteous, and he talks easily with the people beside him.

{img:dinner-table-lamplight-guests-1863}
Outside, in the Diamond, the town is full of people. Thousands of visitors have come to Gettysburg for the ceremony, many more than the town can possibly hold. The hotels are overflowing. People are sleeping in churches, in parlours, on floors, in chairs. Some cannot find anywhere at all, and walk the streets all night.

--

After dinner, a crowd gathers outside the Wills house, with a band, and they begin to serenade the President.

They play patriotic songs, and then they call out for Lincoln to come and speak to them.

At last, he comes to the door. He thanks them for coming. He tells them, with a smile, that he does not have a speech ready for tonight, and that in his position, it is somewhat important that he should not say any foolish things.

{img:crowd-serenade-house-night-band}
A voice in the crowd calls out: If you can help it!

And the President answers: It very often happens that the only way to help it is to say nothing at all.

The crowd laughs, and cheers, and lets him go back inside.

--

Inside the house, the guests talk for a while in the parlour.

Edward Everett is there, white-haired and dignified, talking in his polished way about the battlefield, which he has spent the last two days exploring on foot and on horseback, with a guide, so that he can describe it properly in his speech tomorrow. The governor of Pennsylvania is there, and the governors of other states, and generals in their dress uniforms.

The President listens more than he talks. When he does speak, he often tells a story, about his days as a young man on the frontier in Illinois, or about a lawyer he once knew, and the others laugh. He has a gift for making people feel at ease.

--

Later that evening, Lincoln goes up to his room on the second floor of the Wills house.

It is a comfortable room, with a large bed, a fireplace, a wardrobe and a small writing table. He takes out the sheet of paper on which he has been writing his speech, and sits down at the table, and works on it for a while, by the light of the lamp.

He changes a word here and there. He reads it over. He asks for a few more sheets of paper, and writes it out again.

At one point, he goes next door to talk with William Seward, his Secretary of State, who is staying in the house beside the Wills house. They talk for a while. Then Lincoln comes back, and goes on working.

--

Before he goes to bed, a telegram arrives, with news from Washington.

His young son, Tad, has been ill with a fever, and Lincoln has been worried about him, and his wife Mary was very upset that he should leave home at such a time. Only a year and a half earlier, another of their sons, Willie, passed away of a fever, at the age of eleven.

{img:telegram-reading-candlelight}
But the telegram brings good news. Tad is better.

Lincoln reads it, and folds it up, and puts it away. Then he puts out the lamp, and lies down to sleep.

--

Outside, the town of Gettysburg is still awake late into the night.

People walk through the streets, talking and singing. The bands play on the corners. The windows of the houses glow with lamplight. It is a cold, clear November night, and the stars are bright.

{img:cold-november-night-town-square-stars}
On Baltimore Street, Tillie Pierce lies in her bed, too excited to sleep, thinking of tomorrow. On Cemetery Hill, Elizabeth Thorn rocks her new baby, Rose, beside the stove in the gatehouse.

And out on the hillside, beside the gatehouse, the soldiers rest in their long curved rows, under the stars, waiting for the morning.
