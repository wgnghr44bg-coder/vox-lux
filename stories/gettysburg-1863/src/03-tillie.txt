On Baltimore Street, in the middle of the town, there lives a girl named Tillie Pierce.

She is fifteen years old in the spring of 1863. She has dark hair, parted in the middle and pulled back neatly behind her ears, a round, serious face, and bright, observant eyes. She is curious about everything, and she notices a great deal.

Many years later, she will write down everything she saw that summer, in a little book. And it is because of Tillie that we know so much about what it was like to be a young girl in Gettysburg in 1863.

--

Tillie's father, James Pierce, is a butcher.

The family lives in a brick house on the corner of Baltimore Street and Breckenridge Street, at the southern end of town, on the road that leads out toward the cemetery on the hill. Behind the house, there is a yard and a stable, where the family keeps a horse, and where her father keeps the animals he will sell as meat.

{img:butcher-shop-family-brick-house}
Tillie has an older sister, Maggie, and two older brothers. One brother, James, has gone off to the army. The other, William, is still at home, helping their father.

--

On weekdays, Tillie goes to school at a seminary for young ladies, run by a Mrs. Eyster, a few streets away.

The girls study reading and grammar, history and geography, arithmetic, French, and a little music and drawing. They sit in neat rows at wooden desks, in their long dresses with their hair braided, and recite their lessons when they are called upon.

Tillie is a good student, though perhaps not always a perfectly quiet one. She likes to talk with her friends, and she likes to look out of the window.

--

At home, there is work to do.

Tillie helps her mother in the kitchen, and with the washing and the mending. She feeds the chickens and collects the eggs. She runs errands to the stores on the Diamond, carrying a basket on her arm, and stops to talk with the neighbours on the way.

Her mother, Margaret, is a kind, capable woman, who keeps the household running smoothly. Her father is a quiet, steady man, respected in the town. They are a close, affectionate family.

--

{img:baltimore-street-1863-houses}
Baltimore Street is one of the main streets of Gettysburg.

It runs south from the Diamond, past rows of brick houses and shops, and out of town toward the cemetery on the hill. Beyond the cemetery, it becomes the Baltimore Pike, the road that leads all the way to the city of Baltimore, about fifty miles away.

Every day, wagons and carriages and riders pass along it. Farmers come in from the country, and travellers go out. From the windows of the Pierce house, Tillie can watch them all.

--

A little way up the street, on the other side, lives the McCreary family, and further along, the Shrivers.

Mrs. Henrietta Shriver, whom everyone calls Hettie, is a young mother with two little daughters. Her husband, George, has built a fine new brick house on Baltimore Street, with a saloon and a bowling alley in the basement, which he hoped would bring in a good income. But George has gone off to join the army, and Hettie and the girls are living there alone.

{img:shriver-house-mother-daughters}
Hettie's parents, the Weikerts, have a farm a few miles south of town, out on the Taneytown Road, at the foot of the hill called Little Round Top. Hettie often goes out to visit them, and sometimes Tillie goes with her.

--

Further up Baltimore Street, near the edge of town, there is a small brick house where a young woman named Jennie Wade sometimes stays with her married sister.

Jennie is twenty years old. She lives with her mother, and earns money as a seamstress, sewing clothes for other families. She is engaged to a young man named Jack Skelly, who is away in the army, and she is waiting for him to come home so that they can be married.

--

On Saturdays, Tillie helps her father in the shop.

The butcher's shop is at the front of the house, a cool room with a scrubbed wooden counter, a great wooden chopping block, and hooks along the ceiling. Her father, in his long white apron, cuts and weighs the meat for the customers, and Tillie wraps it in paper and takes the money, and counts out the change carefully into their hands.

The customers are all people she knows. The minister's wife, who always wants the best cut of beef for Sunday. The old widow from across the street, who buys only a little, and to whom her father always gives a little extra, without saying anything. The boys from the college, who want sausages for their suppers.

--

On Sunday afternoons, if the weather is fine, Tillie and her friends walk out along the Baltimore Pike to the cemetery on the hill.

They stroll along the shady paths between the graves, reading the names on the old stones, and stop at the top of the hill to look out over the town and the countryside. From there, they can see the whole of Gettysburg, with its church spires and its rooftops, and the long ridges stretching away to the south and west, green and gold in the afternoon light.

Mrs. Thorn, the keeper's wife, is often working in the garden by the gatehouse, and she waves to them as they pass.

--

On summer evenings, Baltimore Street is full of life.

The children play in the street until it is too dark to see, and the older people sit out on their front steps, fanning themselves and talking. Girls walk arm in arm along the pavement, laughing. A young man plays a fiddle on a doorstep. Somewhere, a dog barks, and someone calls a child in for bed.

Tillie sits on the step with her sister, watching the people pass, and the fireflies rising in the gardens.

--

She has heard a good deal of talk about the war. Her brother is in it, and her friends' brothers too. She knows that there have been great battles far away, and that many men have been lost.

But it all seems a long way off, on a summer evening in Gettysburg, with the swallows wheeling over the rooftops, and the smell of honeysuckle drifting over the garden walls.

{img:girl-window-candle-night}
At bedtime, Tillie climbs the stairs with a candle, says her prayers, and lies down in the room she shares with her sister. Through the open window, she can hear the crickets in the yard, and the slow clop of a horse going home late along Baltimore Street.

And then, like the rest of the town, she sleeps.
