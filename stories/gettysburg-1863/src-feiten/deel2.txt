TOPIC: Letters, rumours and an old constable
===
IMG: family-reading-letter-parlour-lamp | a family of the 1860s gathered in a small parlour in the evening, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, a mother reading a letter aloud by an oil lamp, children listening on a sofa, an old clock on the shelf
HEAD: For most families in Gettysburg, the war arrived in the form of letters.
BODY: During the Civil War, letters were the main link between soldiers and their families. Mail arrived in Gettysburg by train from Hanover. The postmaster sorted it into small wooden boxes at the post office. Mothers and wives came in regularly to ask whether anything had arrived. A letter from a son or a husband was often read aloud to the whole family in the parlour. Most letters described ordinary things. They complained about the weather and the food, which was mostly hard crackers, salt pork and coffee. They mentioned long marches, blisters and mud. They spoke of new friends in the regiment. Many asked for socks, shirts or a jar of jam from home. Letters about battles were usually shorter. Historians have collected tens of thousands of these letters. They are among the richest sources for understanding how ordinary soldiers experienced the war. Nearly all of them end in the same way, with love to the family and a hope to be home soon.
===
IMG: sewing-circle-women-open-windows | a group of women in long dresses sitting in a circle in a sunny parlour by open windows, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, sewing shirts and knitting socks, young girls rolling bandages, small children playing on a rug
HEAD: Girls in Civil War towns picked old linen into soft lint, which army doctors used to dress wounds.
BODY: Women across the North organised to support the soldiers. In Gettysburg, they met in parlours and churches. They sewed shirts, knitted socks and rolled bandages from old linen sheets. They packed boxes of food and clothing for the army. They raised money for hospitals with suppers and small fairs. Younger girls had a special task. They picked old linen apart into soft threads, called lint. Army doctors packed wounds with it. The work was slow and fiddly, but even small hands could do it. Nobody yet understood that used linen could carry infection. Medical science would only learn about germs in the following decades. Much of this local work fed into a large national organisation called the United States Sanitary Commission. It collected supplies from thousands of communities and sent them to the armies. Women did most of the work. Their efforts became one of the largest volunteer movements in American history up to that time.
===
IMG: old-man-cobbler-workshop | a short white-haired old man with a craggy face working on a boot at a wooden bench in a small cobbler's workshop, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, leather and tools on the walls, an old musket hanging above the door, lamplight
HEAD: John Burns, a shoemaker in his late sixties, had been told he was too old to join the army.
BODY: Many older men in Gettysburg followed the war closely. One of them was John Burns. In 1863, he was about sixty-nine years old. He was short and stubborn, with white hair and firm opinions. He worked as a shoemaker and had served for some years as the town constable. As a young man, he had fought in the War of 1812 against Britain. When the Civil War began, he tried to enlist. He was rejected because of his age. According to several accounts, he complained about this to anyone who would listen. On the first day of the battle, he took his old musket and walked out to join Union soldiers west of town. He fought alongside them and was wounded several times. He survived, and became a national celebrity. President Lincoln met him when he visited Gettysburg in November 1863. A statue of John Burns now stands on the battlefield. His story shows how deeply the war could stir even those it had tried to leave behind.
===
IMG: quiet-farm-lane-evening-north | a quiet country lane between wooden fences and fields at dusk, a small farmhouse with one lit window, a covered wagon standing ready in the yard, a family with bundles near the door, a dark blue evening sky
HEAD: For the Black families of Gettysburg, a Confederate army in Pennsylvania meant a very real danger.
BODY: Gettysburg lay only a few miles from the border with Maryland, a slave state. Before the war, people escaping from slavery often passed through the area on their way north. Some local residents helped them quietly, as part of the Underground Railroad. Slave catchers also came looking for them. In 1863, Black residents of Gettysburg faced a particular threat. When Confederate forces entered Pennsylvania, they seized free Black people and sent them south into slavery. Several hundred are believed to have been taken during the campaign. Many Black families in Gettysburg therefore left town as the armies approached. They fled to safer places further north and east, or hid in the countryside. Some returned after the battle to find their homes damaged or looted. Historians have only recently given this part of the story the attention it deserves. It shows that the Gettysburg campaign held very different dangers for different people in the same town.
===
IMG: general-lee-grey-horse-portrait-calm | a dignified grey-bearded general of the 1860s in a plain grey coat sitting calmly on a grey horse under a large oak tree, a quiet green field behind him, soft morning light
HEAD: General Robert E. Lee, the most famous commander of the South, rode a grey horse named Traveller.
BODY: In May 1863, the Confederate army won a major victory at Chancellorsville in Virginia. It defeated the Union's main eastern army, the Army of the Potomac. The Confederate force was commanded by General Robert E. Lee. He was about fifty-six years old, a dignified, grey-bearded man from an old Virginia family. He was known for his calm manner and courtesy, and his soldiers admired him greatly. He rode a grey horse named Traveller, which became almost as famous as he was. After Chancellorsville, Lee decided to move north. He hoped to take the war into Union territory for the first time on a large scale. He wanted to gather supplies from Pennsylvania's rich farms. He also hoped a victory in the North might weaken the Union's will to keep fighting. In June, his army began marching through the valleys west of the mountains. Rumours of its approach spread quickly through southern Pennsylvania.
===
IMG: storekeeper-packing-crates-railway | a shopkeeper and his son packing goods into wooden crates on a small railway platform in the 1860s, barrels and boxes on a handcart, a small steam train waiting, a quiet country town behind, afternoon light
HEAD: As rumours grew in June 1863, the Gettysburg bank quietly sent its money away to Philadelphia.
BODY: Throughout June 1863, rumours reached Gettysburg almost daily. People said the Confederate army was moving through the valleys west of the mountains. They said it was taking horses, cattle and food from farms. Some said it was heading for Harrisburg, others for Philadelphia or Baltimore. Nobody knew for certain. Many residents took precautions. Storekeepers packed goods into crates and sent them away by train. The bank sent its money to Philadelphia for safekeeping. Farmers drove their horses over the hills to hide them in the woods. A good horse was worth a great deal, and a farm could hardly function without one. Families buried silverware in their gardens or hid it in chimneys and under floorboards. In the evenings, people stood at their gates and questioned every traveller. Each seemed to bring a different story. Diaries from the time show how exhausting this uncertainty was. Waiting for an unknown danger could be harder than facing a known one.
===
IMG: june-fields-wheat-golden-farmhouse | golden wheat fields ripening in early summer around a stone farmhouse with a red barn, children picking cherries in an orchard, haystacks in a meadow, long warm evening light and fireflies starting to rise
HEAD: In late June 1863, the wheat was ripening and the cherries were ready to pick around Gettysburg.
BODY: Even as rumours grew, the summer went on. The wheat in the fields turned golden. Cherries ripened in the orchards, and children climbed the trees to pick them. Hay was cut and stacked in the meadows. The days were long and the evenings full of fireflies. Farmers began to plan the harvest, which normally started in early July. Many of those fields would never be harvested that year. Within days, they would be trampled by soldiers, horses and wagons. Fences would be torn down for firewood. Crops would be destroyed across thousands of acres. Accounts written by local residents often contrast these peaceful June days with what followed. They describe ordinary routines continuing almost to the last moment. Meanwhile, beyond the mountains to the west, two great armies were moving slowly north. Neither yet knew exactly where the other was. Both were drawing closer to the little crossroads town every day.

TOPIC: Soldiers in the rain
===
IMG: rainy-june-afternoon-street | a quiet small town street of brick houses on a grey rainy afternoon in the 1860s, a muddy road, a girl in a long dress hurrying along the wet brick pavement holding up her skirts, soft grey light
HEAD: On the twenty-sixth of June, 1863, the first Confederate soldiers marched into Gettysburg.
BODY: Friday, the twenty-sixth of June, was a grey and rainy day in Gettysburg. The streets were muddy. Children were in school. That afternoon, word spread that Confederate troops were approaching. Teachers sent pupils home. Tillie Pierce later described running through the rain and watching from her window. She saw a large body of horsemen riding in from the west, followed by columns of infantry. Many of the soldiers were dressed in worn grey and brown clothing. Some had no shoes. They belonged to a division commanded by General Jubal Early. A small force of local militia, hastily raised, had tried to stop them west of town. It was quickly scattered. Early's men occupied Gettysburg for only one night. Their visit was brief and caused little damage. Yet it was the first time most residents had seen enemy soldiers. For many, the war suddenly became real, standing in their own streets in the rain.
===
IMG: confederate-soldiers-march-rain-town | tired soldiers of the 1860s in worn grey and brown clothes and slouch hats resting in a muddy town square in light rain, some sitting on a wooden step, rifles leaning against a fence, brick houses with closed shutters, grey light
HEAD: General Jubal Early demanded a thousand pairs of shoes and five hundred hats from the town of Gettysburg.
BODY: Once his soldiers had gathered in the Diamond, General Early sent a written demand to the town council. He asked for large quantities of flour, bacon, sugar, coffee, salt and onions. He also demanded a thousand pairs of shoes and five hundred hats. As an alternative, the town could pay five thousand dollars. The council replied that it could not meet these demands. Storekeepers had already sent most of their goods away, and the bank had removed its money. Early did not press the matter. His division was moving on toward York, and he had little time. Soldiers took what they could find in shops and cellars. Many were tired, hungry and poorly clothed. Several residents later remarked on how young some of them looked. Some even thanked people politely for a drink of water. Early's demands were not unusual. Confederate commanders made similar requests of many Pennsylvania towns during the campaign, often with more success.
===
IMG: horse-led-from-stable-yard-rain | a brown farm horse being led out of a small backyard stable by two soldiers in grey in light rain, a man in a white apron standing in a doorway watching, a girl's face at an upstairs window, wet cobbles, grey light
HEAD: Confederate soldiers took horses from many Gettysburg families, including the Pierce family.
BODY: Horses were among the most valuable things an army could seize. Cavalry needed fresh mounts, and wagons and artillery needed teams to pull them. During their brief stay in Gettysburg, Confederate soldiers took horses from many households. Tillie Pierce later recalled watching soldiers lead her family's horse out of the yard. Her father could do nothing to stop them. She remembered feeling deeply indignant, and she described the scene in her book many years later. Other families lost hams from their cellars and bread from their kitchens. That night, the soldiers camped on the edge of town. The next morning, they marched east toward York. Residents came out of their houses and compared their losses. Nobody had been hurt, and the town still stood. It had been a frightening but brief encounter. Yet the loss of horses mattered greatly in a farming community. Without them, fields could not be ploughed or harvests carried home.
===
IMG: townspeople-talking-diamond-square | townspeople of the 1860s standing in small groups in a town square after rain, men in dark coats and women in bonnets talking, puddles on the ground, the sun breaking through clouds over brick buildings
HEAD: After the first Confederate visit, some townspeople described the soldiers as tired and rather polite.
BODY: For several days after Early's departure, Gettysburg was quiet. Residents talked about little else. They shared stories about what the soldiers had said and what they had taken. One family had lost a horse, another a ham. Someone's boots had been taken from a doorstep. Some people concluded that the Confederates were not as frightening as they had feared. They described them as tired, ragged and reasonably well behaved. Others were less reassured. They had noticed the numbers and the hunger of the soldiers. Rumours continued to arrive. The main Confederate army was said to be close, just beyond the mountains. A Union army was reported moving up from Maryland. Town officials met, ministers prayed and families checked their stores of food. Everyone watched the roads more closely. In hindsight, these few quiet days were the last moments of normal life in Gettysburg for many weeks to come.
===
IMG: union-cavalry-riding-through-town-cheering | a long column of cavalrymen in blue uniforms riding slowly through a small brick town in the 1860s, women and girls on the pavements waving white handkerchiefs, children running beside the horses, flags, warm morning sun
HEAD: When Union cavalry rode into Gettysburg on the thirtieth of June, local girls sang patriotic songs to welcome them.
BODY: On the morning of Tuesday, the thirtieth of June, soldiers appeared on the roads again. This time they came from the south, along the Emmitsburg Road, and they wore blue. They were Union cavalry, around three thousand men on horseback. They rode into town in a long, dusty column, with sabres jingling and flags flying. Residents poured out of their houses to greet them. Women and girls waved handkerchiefs and cheered from the pavements. Some brought out bread, cakes and cups of water. Children ran alongside the horses. Tillie Pierce and her schoolmates stood at a street corner and sang patriotic songs as the cavalry passed. Several soldiers later recalled this welcome in their own letters and memoirs. After the anxiety of the previous week, people felt safer. Union troops were finally there. Few in town realised that this arrival meant not safety, but the beginning of a great battle around their homes.
===
IMG: cavalry-general-ridge-looking-west | a quiet cavalry officer with a drooping moustache on a brown horse at the top of a gentle wooded ridge, looking west across fields toward distant blue mountains through field glasses, a brick building with a cupola behind him, evening light
HEAD: Union cavalry general John Buford decided to hold the ridges west of Gettysburg until the army arrived.
BODY: The Union cavalry was commanded by General John Buford. He was a quiet, weathered officer of about thirty-seven, from Kentucky, with a drooping moustache and long experience. On arriving, he rode up to Seminary Ridge and looked west toward the mountains. His scouts reported a large Confederate force camped a few miles away along the Chambersburg Pike. Buford studied the roads and the ridges carefully. He understood the importance of the town's road network. Armies would come here from several directions. Whoever held the high ground nearby would have a great advantage. He therefore decided to hold the ridges west and north of town with his cavalry. His aim was to delay the Confederates until Union infantry could arrive. Historians often regard this as one of the key decisions of the battle. It helped ensure that Union forces would eventually occupy the strong positions south of town. It was a small decision, made calmly on horseback, with very large consequences.
===
IMG: cavalry-campfires-fields-dusk | small campfires flickering across quiet fields at dusk, horses grazing in a meadow, cavalrymen in blue sitting by the fires, a gentle ridge and dark trees behind, the first stars in a deep blue sky
HEAD: On the night before the battle, some Gettysburg families invited Union soldiers home for supper.
BODY: On the evening of the thirtieth of June, Buford's cavalry camped on the ridges west and north of town. Their campfires flickered across the fields in the dusk. Horses grazed in the meadows. Some soldiers walked into town to buy bread or pies. A number of families invited them in for supper. Young men from New York, Indiana and Illinois sat at kitchen tables, eating ham and fried potatoes and talking about their homes. At nine o'clock, Professor Jacobs went out to read his thermometer, as he did every evening, and recorded the temperature. The night was warm and still. Stars filled the sky. Many residents went to bed with a sense of excitement rather than fear. Union soldiers were present, and the town felt protected. Accounts from that evening are calm and almost cheerful. They make the contrast with the following morning even sharper. By dawn, the first shots of the battle would be fired west of town.

TOPIC: Two armies on the roads
===
IMG: army-column-marching-dusty-road | a very long column of soldiers in blue marching four abreast along a dusty country road between wheat fields, rifles on their shoulders and blanket rolls across their chests, dust rising golden in the low evening sun
HEAD: Together, the two armies at Gettysburg had more than sixty times as many men as the town had residents.
BODY: Two very large armies were converging on Gettysburg. The Union Army of the Potomac had about ninety thousand men. The Confederate Army of Northern Virginia had about seventy-five thousand. Together, they numbered more than sixty times the population of the town. On the march, each army stretched for many miles along the roads. Infantry marched in columns, rifles on their shoulders and blanket rolls across their chests. Long lines of cannons and supply wagons followed, pulled by teams of horses and mules. Herds of cattle walked with the armies to provide meat. Officers rode up and down the lines. Clouds of dust rose over the roads and settled on faces and uniforms. Soldiers of both sides often became the same pale brown colour. The logistics were enormous. Each army needed tons of food, ammunition and fodder every day. Moving such forces required careful planning, and it was impossible to keep their presence secret from the people they passed.
===
IMG: soldiers-crowding-farm-pump-heat | thirsty soldiers in dusty uniforms crowding around an iron pump in a farmyard on a hot summer day, filling tin canteens, a farm woman handing out a bucket of water, a stone barn behind, bright hazy sunlight
HEAD: Marching soldiers often broke ranks at farm pumps to fill their canteens.
BODY: The summer of 1863 was hot. Soldiers marched fifteen to twenty miles a day, sometimes more, carrying heavy loads. Blisters and worn-out shoes were common. Thirst was a constant problem. Whenever a column passed a farm with a well or pump, men broke ranks to fill their tin canteens and splash water on their faces. Officers shouted at them to return to the line, often without much success. Farm families came out to watch. Some brought buckets of water, bread or milk. Children sat on fences, staring at the endless lines of soldiers. Many soldiers later described these moments of kindness in their letters. In the evenings, some were so exhausted that they fell asleep the moment they halted, still wearing their packs. Forced marches in heat caused many soldiers to collapse along the way. Some units arrived at Gettysburg after marching through the night. They went into battle already tired, thirsty and footsore.
===
IMG: evening-camp-coffee-fires-harmonica | soldiers of the 1860s sitting around small campfires in a field at dusk, making coffee in tin cups, one playing a harmonica, another writing a letter on his knee, rifles stacked in little pyramids, a soft orange sky
HEAD: For Civil War soldiers, making coffee was often the most important moment of the day.
BODY: In the evening, the armies camped in fields beside the roads. Soldiers stacked their rifles in small pyramids and lit fires. Then they boiled coffee in tin cups. Many veterans later said coffee mattered more to them than almost anything else. Union soldiers received a generous coffee ration, and historians have found that the word coffee appears in their diaries more often than words like rifle or war. Supper usually consisted of salt pork fried in a mess tin, with hard crackers broken into it. These crackers, known as hardtack, were so tough that soldiers joked about them constantly. Men took off their shoes and rubbed their aching feet. They talked about home, families and sweethearts. Someone might play a harmonica or fiddle. Others wrote letters by firelight. Hundreds of small fires spread across the dark fields. The scene was repeated in camps of both armies, with very similar routines on either side.
===
IMG: young-soldiers-faces-campfire-night | the faces of young soldiers in their late teens lit by a campfire at night, some in blue caps, some in grey slouch hats, quiet and thoughtful, one holding a tin cup, dark trees behind them
HEAD: During quiet times, Union and Confederate soldiers sometimes traded coffee for tobacco across the lines.
BODY: Most soldiers on both sides were young men in their late teens or early twenties. Many were farm boys, clerks, labourers, students or craftsmen. Some had served for two years and fought in many battles. Others had never seen combat. In many ways, soldiers in blue and grey were remarkably similar. They came from similar homes, spoke the same language and sang many of the same songs. They often shared the same religious faith. During quiet periods, when armies camped near each other along rivers, soldiers sometimes called across to one another. Informal trading was common. Confederates often had tobacco, while Union soldiers had coffee. Small bundles were floated across rivers on tiny rafts. Officers usually disapproved, but the practice continued. Such exchanges reveal an important truth about the war. Soldiers could hold strong convictions and still recognise each other as fellow human beings. The next day, however, they could be fighting again.
===
IMG: general-meade-tent-maps | a tall thin general of the 1860s with a grey beard and large spectacles studying maps by candlelight in a canvas tent at night, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, a camp table and folding chair
HEAD: General George Meade was given command of the Union army only three days before the battle began.
BODY: On the twenty-eighth of June, 1863, President Lincoln replaced the commander of the Army of the Potomac. The new commander was General George Gordon Meade. He was forty-seven, tall, thin and serious, from Philadelphia. He wore spectacles, had a grey beard and was known for a sharp temper. His soldiers nicknamed him a goggle-eyed old snapping turtle. Yet he was careful, sensible and experienced. He was also defending his home state. According to his own account, he was woken in his tent in the middle of the night by an officer from Washington. At first, he thought he was being arrested. Instead, he was handed command of the entire army, with a major battle only days away. He had not sought the appointment. In a letter to his wife, he wrote that he would move at once and try to do his duty. Few generals have taken command in more difficult circumstances, with so little time to prepare.
===
IMG: professor-colonel-writing-letter-tent | a thoughtful man of about thirty-four with a moustache, in a blue officer's coat, writing a letter by candlelight in a small canvas tent at night, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, a few books beside him
HEAD: Union colonel Joshua Lawrence Chamberlain had been a professor of languages before the war.
BODY: Before the Civil War, Joshua Lawrence Chamberlain taught at Bowdoin College in Maine. He was a professor of rhetoric and languages. He could read Latin, Greek and Hebrew, among several other languages. He was married, with two young children, and loved music and books. In 1862, the college offered him leave to travel and study in Europe. Instead, he joined the army. By the summer of 1863, he was thirty-four years old and a colonel. He commanded the 20th Maine, a regiment of about three hundred and eighty men. Most were farmers, fishermen and lumbermen from the coast and forests of Maine. Chamberlain wrote regularly to his wife, Fanny, describing the country and his men. At Gettysburg, his regiment defended the southern end of the Union line on Little Round Top. Chamberlain later received the Medal of Honor. After the war, he became governor of Maine and president of Bowdoin College.

IMG: night-camp-tents-stars-hills | a quiet army camp at night with rows of small white tents in a meadow below dark wooded hills, glowing embers of campfires, a sentry walking slowly, horses tied in a line, a sky full of summer stars
HEAD: Neither General Lee nor General Meade had planned to fight a battle at Gettysburg.
BODY: On the Confederate side, one of Lee's most important commanders was General James Longstreet. He was a large, bearded and quiet man, originally from Georgia, solid and stubborn by nature. Lee called him his old war horse. In early 1862, three of Longstreet's young children had died of fever within a single week. Friends said he became quieter and more serious afterwards. On the night of the thirtieth of June, both armies were spread across the countryside. Some units were only a few miles from Gettysburg. Others were a day's march away. Neither commanding general had chosen Gettysburg as a battlefield. Neither knew exactly where all the enemy's forces were. Lee had ordered his army to concentrate in the area, while Meade was preparing a defensive line further south. The road network drew both armies toward the town. Historians often describe Gettysburg as a battle that began almost by accident, and grew larger than anyone intended.
