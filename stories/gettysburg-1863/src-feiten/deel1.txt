TOPIC: A crossroads town in the hills
===
IMG: gettysburg-town-spring-hills | a small country town of red brick houses and white church spires among green rolling hills, apple trees in pink and white blossom on the slopes, farm fields and wooden fences around it, soft evening light
HEAD: In 1863, Gettysburg was a small country town of about two thousand four hundred people.
BODY: Gettysburg stood in the rolling farmland of southern Pennsylvania, a few miles north of the Maryland border. In spring, the apple orchards on the hillsides blossomed pink and white. Fields of young wheat and corn stretched along the roads. Stone barns and wooden fences divided the land into neat farms. The town itself was modest. Its houses were built of brick and wood along a few straight streets. One or two church spires rose above the rooftops. About two thousand four hundred people lived there, fewer than attend many large schools today. The town had been laid out in the 1780s by a man named James Gettys. He divided the land into plots and sold them to settlers, and the town kept his name. For most of its history, Gettysburg was not a place where great events happened. It was a market town, where farmers sold their produce and bought what they needed. Yet its quiet appearance hid one important feature, in the roads that led into it.
===
IMG: ten-roads-countryside-aerial | a wide view over a green countryside with many dirt roads meeting at a small town in the distance, like the spokes of a wheel, farms and woods between them, soft golden afternoon light
HEAD: Ten roads met at Gettysburg, like the spokes of a wheel meeting at its hub.
BODY: For a small town, Gettysburg was unusually well connected. Roads arrived from almost every direction. The Chambersburg Pike came in from the mountains to the west. Other roads led south toward Baltimore, and north and east toward Harrisburg, York and Hanover. Smaller roads ran south to Emmitsburg and Taneytown in Maryland. Farmers used these roads to bring their produce to market. Merchants sent goods out along them. Stagecoaches and wagons passed through every day, raising dust in dry weather. In 1858, a small railway line also arrived from Hanover in the east. In peaceful times, this network simply made Gettysburg a useful place to trade. In wartime, it meant something very different. An army on the move needed roads for its men, its wagons and its guns. A town where ten roads met was a natural place for marching columns to come together. Nobody planned a battle there. Yet the map itself made it likely that armies crossing the region would meet near this crossroads.
===
IMG: seminary-ridge-cupola-view | a long brick building with a small white wooden cupola on top of a gentle wooded ridge, looking out over fields, orchards and a small town, a blue line of mountains far away, warm evening light
HEAD: From the cupola of the Lutheran seminary, a person could see for miles across the countryside.
BODY: The land around Gettysburg rose and fell in long, low ridges. West of the town stood a Lutheran seminary, a college where young men trained to become ministers. It was a fine brick building with a small white cupola on its roof. The ridge it stood on became known as Seminary Ridge. South of the town was a hill with a cemetery, called Cemetery Hill. From there, a long and gentle ridge ran south for about two miles. At its far end rose two rocky, wooded hills, known as the Round Tops. Further away, the blue line of the South Mountains marked the western horizon. To the people of Gettysburg, these were simply familiar parts of the landscape. Students walked along the ridges. Families visited the cemetery on Sundays. Farmers worked the fields between them. To an army, however, ridges and hills offered height, cover and a view of approaching enemies. In the summer of 1863, the quiet shapes of the land would decide where soldiers stood and where they fought.
===
IMG: german-family-farmhouse-porch-evening | a stone farmhouse with a wide wooden porch, an older couple sitting on a bench in plain dark clothes, a big stone barn behind, chickens in the yard, warm low evening sun
HEAD: Many families in Gettysburg had German roots, and some older residents still spoke German at home.
BODY: Most people in the town were farmers, craftsmen and shopkeepers. There were also teachers, ministers, doctors and lawyers. Many families had arrived from Germany, Scotland or Ireland two or three generations earlier. German names were common, such as Weikert, Trostle and Shriver. Some older residents still spoke a local form of German by the stove in the evening. This Pennsylvania German mixed old country words with English ones. It was a living reminder of the settlers who had first farmed these valleys. Church life was central to the community. On Sunday mornings, bells rang out across the rooftops, calling families to worship. In a town of this size, people knew their neighbours well. News travelled quickly, whether it concerned a wedding, an illness or a new bonnet seen in church. Life followed the rhythm of the seasons and the working week. In many ways, Gettysburg was an ordinary American town, much like hundreds of others. That ordinariness is part of what makes its later story so striking.
===
IMG: diamond-square-courthouse-morning | a small town square surrounded by red brick shops and houses, farm wagons with baskets of eggs and vegetables standing around it, men in dark coats and hats talking on a corner, soft morning light
HEAD: The central square of Gettysburg was known to everyone as the Diamond.
BODY: At the heart of the town lay an open square where the main roads met. Local people called it the Diamond, a name used for town squares in several parts of Pennsylvania. In the mornings, it was the busiest place in Gettysburg. Farmers' wagons stood around it, loaded with butter, eggs, potatoes, cabbages and chickens in wooden crates. Shopkeepers opened their doors and set goods out on the pavement. Men in dark coats and hats gathered on the corners to read newspapers and talk about the weather and the crops. Gettysburg was the county seat of Adams County. It therefore had a courthouse, a jail, a bank, several hotels and taverns, a newspaper office and many shops. On court days, lawyers hurried across the square with papers under their arms. Farmers came to pay taxes or settle disputes. The Diamond was the town's meeting place, market and news centre all at once. By 1863, nearly every conversation there turned to the war.
===
IMG: newspaper-reading-hotel-lobby-lamp | the plain lobby of a small country hotel in the 1860s, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, men in waistcoats reading newspapers by an oil lamp, a map pinned to the wall, wooden chairs and a stove
HEAD: For two years, most people in Gettysburg knew the war mainly from newspapers and letters.
BODY: The Civil War had begun in the spring of 1861. Eleven Southern states had left the Union and formed the Confederate States of America. The government in Washington, under President Abraham Lincoln, refused to accept their departure. Since then, great battles had been fought in Virginia, Tennessee, Maryland and Mississippi. Hundreds of thousands of young men had gone to fight on both sides. In Gettysburg, people followed events closely through newspapers. Men read them aloud in hotel lobbies and stores. They argued about generals and politicians. Some pinned maps to the walls and marked the movements of the armies. Letters from soldiers brought the war closer, with news of marches, camps and losses. Yet for most residents, the fighting still felt distant. The great armies had clashed far to the south. The war was something read about by lamplight in the evening. That sense of distance was common across the North. Few towns imagined that the war might one day arrive in their own streets.
===
IMG: recruitment-young-men-train-station | a small country railway station in the 1860s, young men in new blue uniforms waving from the platform, women in long dresses and bonnets waving handkerchiefs, a brass band with shining instruments, soft afternoon light
HEAD: When the young men of Gettysburg left for the army, a brass band played them off at the station.
BODY: Like towns across the North, Gettysburg sent many of its young men to war. They marched away in new blue uniforms, with flags flying and bands playing. Mothers, sisters and sweethearts waved from the station platform until the train disappeared. By 1863, some had already come home wounded or ill. Others had not come home at all. In a small town, everyone knew which families had lost a son or a husband. Those who stayed behind supported the war effort in practical ways. Women knitted socks and rolled bandages for the soldiers. Churches held prayer meetings and collected supplies. Then people returned to their own work, fields and households. Gettysburg lay only about ten miles from the Maryland border. More than once, rumours spread that Confederate cavalry might ride north. Each time, families hid their horses and valuables and waited anxiously. Each time, the danger seemed to pass. These alarms became almost routine. Few expected that the next warning would be real.

TOPIC: Brick houses and busy days
===
IMG: brick-townhouse-street-green-shutters | a quiet street of two storey red brick houses built right up against the brick pavement, green wooden shutters, small attic windows under the roof, a horse and buggy passing, warm late afternoon light
HEAD: Houses in Gettysburg were often built right up against the pavement, with long gardens behind them.
BODY: In Gettysburg, as in many Pennsylvania towns, houses usually stood directly on the street. The front door opened straight onto the pavement. Behind the house, a long, narrow garden stretched back from the road. Many homes were built of red brick, two storeys high, with green shutters and small attic windows. Others were of painted wood or grey stone. At the back stood a well or pump, a woodshed and often a small stable for a horse and a cow. Many families also kept pigs and chickens. Every household had a vegetable garden, with beans, cabbages, onions and potatoes in neat rows. This arrangement meant that town families lived partly like farmers. In the evening, cows lowed and hens settled behind the brick houses of the main streets. Town and countryside were never far apart. The layout also mattered later. During the battle, soldiers moved through these yards, gardens and alleys, and the walls of the houses became part of the fighting.
===
IMG: mother-cookstove-breakfast-children | a farmhouse kitchen before dawn, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, a woman in a long dress and apron frying ham on an iron cookstove, children sitting at a wooden table, an oil lamp glowing, a coffee pot steaming
HEAD: A Gettysburg breakfast could include ham, sausage, fried potatoes, eggs and apple butter, all at once.
BODY: Domestic life in a Pennsylvania town began early. Women rose before dawn to light the fire in the iron cookstove. Water was fetched from the pump in the yard, and the kettle was set to boil. Breakfast was a substantial meal. It might include fried ham or sausage, fried potatoes, eggs, bread and butter. Apple butter, thick and dark, was a regional favourite, made by slowly cooking apples for many hours. Strong coffee was usual, at least before wartime shortages made it expensive. Children were woken, washed and dressed. Older girls helped with cooking and younger children. Boys fed the chickens, milked the cow and carried in wood. Families often ate together and said grace before the meal. Such breakfasts reflected the demands of daily life. Most people worked on their feet from dawn until dark, whether in workshops, kitchens or fields. A heavy morning meal was simply a practical necessity, not a luxury.
===
IMG: carriage-workshop-painter-gold-lines | a long wooden workshop with a half-finished black buggy, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, a craftsman painting a thin gold line along its side with a fine brush, wooden wheels leaning on the wall, light from tall windows
HEAD: Gettysburg was known for its carriages, and painters added thin gold lines to them by hand.
BODY: Many men in Gettysburg worked at a trade. There were blacksmiths, harness makers, tanners, shoemakers, carpenters and masons. There were tailors, hatters, bakers and butchers. Yet the town's best-known industry was carriage making. Several carriage works operated along Chambersburg Street, York Street and elsewhere. Wheelwrights shaped spokes and rims from seasoned hickory. Smiths fitted iron tyres onto the wheels while the metal was still hot, so that it shrank tightly as it cooled. Painters worked in separate rooms, kept free of dust. They applied layer after layer of glossy paint. Then, with very fine brushes, they added thin decorative lines of gold or red along the bodies and wheels. This work required a steady hand and great patience. Before the war, Gettysburg carriages were sold widely, including in the South. The war disrupted that trade. Yet the industry showed how skilled craftsmanship could give even a small town a wide reputation.

IMG: washday-iron-kettle-backyard-line | a backyard behind a brick house, a large iron kettle steaming over a small fire, a woman in a long dress scrubbing clothes on a washboard in a wooden tub, white sheets hanging on a line, soft morning light
HEAD: In 1863, Monday was washing day, and doing the laundry took the whole day.
BODY: Housework in the 1860s followed a regular weekly pattern. Monday was washing day. A large iron kettle of water was heated over a fire in the yard. Clothes were scrubbed on a washboard, rinsed, wrung out by hand and hung on a line to dry. The work was long and physically heavy. Tuesday was usually ironing day. Heavy irons were heated on the stove and used in turn while the others warmed again. Other days brought baking, mending, cleaning, churning butter and garden work. Summer and autumn were for preserving food. Cherries and peaches went into jars. Apples were sliced and dried. Cabbage was shredded and packed into stone crocks to ferment into sauerkraut, a favourite in German Pennsylvania. By autumn, cellar shelves were full. Diaries kept by women of the period describe this routine in detail. They show how much of a household's comfort depended on work that was rarely recorded in official histories.
===
IMG: general-store-counter-1863 | the inside of a country general store in the 1860s, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, a long wooden counter, shelves of jars, bolts of cloth, barrels of flour and sugar, small glass medicine bottles, an oil lamp hanging, warm light
HEAD: General stores in the 1860s sold patent medicines that promised to cure almost every illness.
BODY: The general store was one of the most important places in any small town. It sold coffee, tea, sugar, salt, molasses and spices. It stocked cloth, thread, buttons, needles and pins. There were pots and pans, lamp oil, candles, soap and nails. On high shelves stood rows of patent medicines in small glass bottles. Their labels promised cures for almost every complaint, from headaches to weak nerves. Many contained little more than sugar, alcohol or opium. There were no laws yet requiring honest labels. Storekeepers weighed goods on brass scales and recorded purchases in large ledgers. Many families paid only once a month or after the harvest. Credit was a normal part of rural life. During the war, some goods became scarce and expensive, especially coffee and cotton cloth. People mixed roasted grain into their coffee to make it last. Yet in a farming district like Adams County, food itself was generally plentiful.
===
IMG: church-sunday-families-1863 | families in their best dark Sunday clothes and bonnets standing outside a small white country church in the sunshine, talking, children holding their parents' hands, a tall tree beside the church, soft light
HEAD: A town of only 2,400 people had churches for Lutherans, Presbyterians, Methodists, Catholics and more.
BODY: Religion was central to life in Gettysburg. For a small town, it had a remarkable number of churches. There were Lutheran, Presbyterian, Methodist, Catholic, German Reformed and Episcopal congregations. There was also a church for the town's Black community, whose families had lived in Gettysburg for generations. Saturday evening was often bath night. Family members took turns in a tin tub in front of the kitchen stove, with water heated on the fire. On Sunday, people dressed in their best clothes and walked to church. After the service, neighbours talked outside in the sunshine. Then families went home to a large Sunday dinner, often roast chicken, potatoes and pie. The afternoon was kept for rest. Some visited relatives, some read the Bible aloud, and some walked out along the country roads. Work was avoided whenever possible. These church buildings later took on another role. During and after the battle, many of them were turned into hospitals.
===
IMG: children-swimming-creek-summer-trees | a shallow creek with clear water running over flat stones, shaded by tall trees, boys wading and splashing in the water, a fishing line on the bank, a quiet green meadow beyond, golden summer afternoon light
HEAD: In summer, the boys of Gettysburg went swimming in Rock Creek, just east of town.
BODY: Most children in Gettysburg attended school for at least a few years. Public schools served younger pupils. Families who could afford it sent children to private academies. There was a seminary for young ladies, where girls studied grammar and French. On the hill north of town stood a college, where young men studied Latin and Greek. Outside school, children played in the streets and fields. Boys played ball and marbles, fished in the creeks and climbed trees in the orchards. Girls played with dolls, skipped rope and picked wildflowers along the lanes. In winter, there was sledding on the snowy hills. In summer, Rock Creek on the east side of town was a favourite swimming place. Its water ran cool and brown beneath the trees. Memoirs written by people who grew up in Gettysburg often recall these simple pleasures. They describe a small-town childhood that felt safe and ordinary, until the summer of 1863 changed it forever.

IMG: market-day-wagons-sunbonnets-diamond | a small town square full of farm wagons on market day, farm women in sunbonnets sitting on wagon seats with baskets of eggs and crocks of butter, townswomen choosing vegetables, fresh strawberries in baskets, morning light
HEAD: On market days, farm families from across Adams County filled the streets of Gettysburg.
BODY: Market days brought the surrounding countryside into town. Early in the morning, farm wagons rolled in along the roads, one after another. Soon the Diamond was crowded with them. Farm wives sat on wagon seats in sunbonnets, with baskets of eggs, crocks of butter and bunches of herbs at their feet. Townswomen moved from wagon to wagon, choosing and bargaining. The produce changed with the seasons. June brought strawberries. August brought sweet corn and tomatoes. Autumn brought apples, cider and pumpkins. Live chickens, smoked hams, cheeses and honey in the comb were also for sale. Boys sold cherries by the quart. By midday, many wagons were empty. Farmers went to the taverns for dinner and talked about the weather and the price of wheat. Then they drove home in the afternoon sun. Markets like this tied the town and countryside together. They were the economic heartbeat of a farming county.

IMG: front-step-evening-neighbours | a row of brick houses on a quiet street at dusk, families sitting on their front steps, an old man fanning himself, children playing in the street, swallows in the sky, fireflies in the small gardens, warm fading golden light
HEAD: On summer evenings, much of Gettysburg seemed to sit outside on its front steps.
BODY: In summer, the town smelled strongly of the countryside. The scent of cut hay drifted in from nearby meadows. Kitchens smelled of baking bread. The tanneries near the creek gave off a sharper smell that few people enjoyed. In the evenings, families gathered in their parlours. Fathers read newspapers aloud by the light of oil lamps. Mothers knitted or sewed. Some families owned a parlour organ, and everyone sang together. On warm nights, however, people went outside. They sat on the steps in front of their houses and called to neighbours across the street. Swallows wheeled above the rooftops. Fireflies rose from the gardens. The long golden light faded behind the ridge to the west. Then lamps went out one by one, and the town went to sleep. Such evenings were common in small American towns before electric light and air conditioning. They made the street itself into a shared living room for the whole neighbourhood.

TOPIC: A butcher's daughter on Baltimore Street
===
IMG: tillie-pierce-girl-1863 | a fifteen year old girl in a long dress of the 1860s with dark hair parted in the middle and pulled neatly back, sitting by a window with a book on her lap, bright curious eyes, soft evening light from the window
HEAD: Much of what is known about everyday life in Gettysburg in 1863 comes from a fifteen-year-old girl.
BODY: Matilda Pierce, known as Tillie, lived on Baltimore Street in the middle of town. She was fifteen in the spring of 1863. Photographs show a serious young woman with dark hair parted in the middle and pulled neatly back. She was curious and observant. In 1889, she published a book about that summer. Its title was At Gettysburg, or What a Girl Saw and Heard of the Battle. In it, she described her family, her neighbours, the soldiers and the long days that followed the fighting. Her account is one of the best-known civilian memories of the battle. Historians still use it today. Like all memoirs written many years later, it must be read with some care. Memories fade and change over time. Yet her book preserves details that official reports ignored. It shows the battle not from a general's headquarters, but from a family kitchen and a front window. That perspective has made it valuable for more than a century.

IMG: butcher-shop-family-brick-house | a brick corner house of the 1860s with a small butcher's shop at the front, a scrubbed wooden counter and a chopping block inside, a man in a long white apron, a girl wrapping meat in paper, warm light through the doorway
HEAD: Tillie Pierce's father was the town butcher, and the family lived above the shop on Baltimore Street.
BODY: James Pierce was a butcher. His family lived in a brick house at the corner of Baltimore Street and Breckenridge Street. It stood at the southern end of town, on the road leading out toward the cemetery on the hill. The shop occupied the front of the house. It had a scrubbed wooden counter, a heavy chopping block and hooks along the ceiling. Butchers in small towns were well known to everyone. Customers included ministers' wives, widows and students from the college. Children often helped in family businesses, wrapping meat, taking money and counting change. The Pierce house still stands today on Baltimore Street. It has been restored and is open to visitors. Its location placed the family close to some of the most dramatic events of the battle. Baltimore Street became a key route for retreating and advancing soldiers. An ordinary family shop therefore found itself on one of the busiest roads in the war's most famous battle.
===
IMG: young-ladies-seminary-classroom-desks | a small classroom of the 1860s, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, girls in long dresses with braided hair sitting at wooden desks in neat rows, a teacher by a blackboard, a globe on a table, light from a tall window
HEAD: Girls in Gettysburg could attend a seminary for young ladies, studying grammar, history and French.
BODY: Education for girls was expanding in mid-nineteenth-century America. In Gettysburg, a young ladies' seminary run by Mrs Eyster offered lessons a few streets from the Diamond. Pupils studied reading, grammar, history and geography. They learned arithmetic and French, with some music and drawing. They sat in rows at wooden desks, in long dresses with braided hair. When called upon, they stood and recited their lessons aloud. Recitation was the standard teaching method of the time. Pupils memorised passages and repeated them before the class. Tillie Pierce attended this seminary. In her memoir, she recalled that she and her classmates were in school on the afternoon when the first Confederate soldiers appeared in town. Outside school, girls helped with housework, washing and mending. They fed chickens, collected eggs and ran errands. Female seminaries like this one were common in Northern towns. They prepared girls for running households, and sometimes for teaching, one of the few professions then open to women.

IMG: baltimore-street-1863-houses | a long straight street of brick houses and small shops in the 1860s, a horse and wagon on the dirt road, a man on horseback, a gentle green hill with trees at the end of the street, soft afternoon light
HEAD: Baltimore Street ran south from the town square and became the road to the city of Baltimore.
BODY: Baltimore Street was one of the main streets of Gettysburg. It ran south from the Diamond past rows of brick houses and shops. Then it climbed out of town toward Cemetery Hill. Beyond the cemetery, it became the Baltimore Pike, a road leading all the way to Baltimore, about fifty miles away. Every day, wagons, carriages and riders passed along it. Farmers came into town, and travellers went out. Peddlers walked by with pots and pans tied to their packs. Families living along the street could watch the traffic from their windows. The Pierce family was one of them. Tillie had an older sister and two older brothers. One brother, James, was serving in the army. The other, William, worked with their father. During the battle, Baltimore Street lay along a crucial line between the armies. Houses at its southern end stood almost on the front line. Some still carry bullet marks in their brick walls today.

IMG: shriver-house-mother-daughters | a new two storey brick house on a town street in the 1860s, a young mother in a long dress standing at the front door with two small daughters in pinafores, flower pots on the step, soft evening light
HEAD: One house on Baltimore Street had a saloon and a bowling alley built into its basement.
BODY: George Shriver had built a new brick house on Baltimore Street shortly before the war. In the basement, he installed a saloon and a two-lane bowling alley, hoping they would provide a good income. Then he joined the army. His wife, Henrietta, known as Hettie, remained in the house with their two young daughters. Hettie's parents, the Weikerts, owned a farm a few miles south of town. It lay on the Taneytown Road, near the foot of the hill called Little Round Top. During the battle, Hettie took her daughters to her parents' farm, believing it would be safer. Tillie Pierce went with them. The decision turned out to be mistaken, because the farm lay close to some of the heaviest fighting. Meanwhile, Confederate sharpshooters occupied the Shriver house in town. The house has since been restored and is open to the public. Its basement bowling alley is a reminder of ordinary hopes interrupted by war.

IMG: young-seamstress-sewing-by-window | a young woman of the 1860s with dark hair sewing a dress by a window in a small plain room, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, spools of thread on a table, soft afternoon light
HEAD: Jennie Wade, the only civilian killed during the battle, was probably never called Jennie in her lifetime.
BODY: Mary Virginia Wade was twenty years old in 1863. Her family and friends called her Ginnie. The name Jennie seems to have come from newspapers after her death, and it has remained ever since. She lived with her mother and worked as a seamstress, sewing clothes for other families. Like many young women in town, she had a sweetheart in the army. His name was Johnston Skelly, known as Jack, and they had grown up together in Gettysburg. During the battle, Ginnie was staying at her sister's house on Baltimore Street, helping her sister, who had just given birth. On the morning of the third of July, a stray bullet passed through two doors and struck her while she was baking bread. She was the only civilian known to have been killed directly by the fighting. Her story became one of the best-known personal stories of the battle. The house where she died is now a museum.

IMG: cemetery-hill-path-girls-sunday-view | two girls in long summer dresses and bonnets walking along a shady path among old white gravestones on a green hilltop, a small town with church spires below, long ridges and fields beyond, golden afternoon light
HEAD: In the 1860s, young people in Gettysburg often walked in the cemetery on Sunday afternoons.
BODY: To modern ears, a walk in a cemetery may sound unusual. Yet in the mid-nineteenth century, many American towns laid out cemeteries like parks. These rural cemeteries had trees, lawns and winding paths. People visited them to walk, rest and enjoy the view. Evergreen Cemetery in Gettysburg was one of these. On fine Sunday afternoons, young people walked out along the Baltimore Pike to its gates. They strolled along shady paths and read the names on the stones. From the top of the hill, they could see the whole town, with its church spires and rooftops. They could also see the long ridges running south and west. The rural cemetery movement reflected new ideas about death and memory. Graves were placed in peaceful natural settings rather than crowded churchyards. In 1863, Cemetery Hill gained a very different meaning. Its height made it one of the most important positions on the entire battlefield.

IMG: girl-window-candle-night | a girl in a white nightgown sitting by an open window in a small bedroom at night, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, a candle on the windowsill, a quiet town street outside under a dark blue sky, fireflies in the garden below
HEAD: Before July 1863, the war still felt far away to many families in Gettysburg.
BODY: Summer evenings on the streets of Gettysburg were lively and familiar. Children played until it was too dark to see. Older people sat on front steps, fanning themselves and talking. Young women walked arm in arm along the pavements. Fiddles played on doorsteps, and dogs barked in the yards. Almost every family had a relative or friend in the army. People knew that great battles had been fought far to the south. Yet on calm evenings, the war seemed distant. Swallows wheeled over the rooftops. Honeysuckle scented the garden walls. Candles were carried up the stairs at bedtime. Through open windows came the sound of crickets and a horse moving slowly home along the street. Civilian diaries and memoirs often describe this sense of normal life just before the battle. They show how suddenly ordinary routines could be broken. In late June 1863, rumours of approaching armies grew stronger. Within days, the quiet town would be transformed.

TOPIC: The college, the seminary and the hill
===
IMG: pennsylvania-college-hill | a handsome pale brick college building with tall white columns at the front and a small cupola on the roof, standing among tall trees on a gentle rise, young men in dark coats walking on the lawn, soft morning light
HEAD: The college on the hill north of Gettysburg, founded in 1832, still teaches students today.
BODY: On the northern edge of Gettysburg stood Pennsylvania College. It had been founded in 1832 by Lutherans who wanted a college for their region. Its main building was a handsome brick structure with tall white columns and a small cupola on the roof. In 1863, around a hundred and fifty young men studied there. Some lived in the college building, while others lodged with families in town. They studied Latin, Greek, mathematics and natural science. Debating societies met in the evenings to argue about the great questions of the day. Students played an active part in town life. When Confederate forces threatened Pennsylvania in 1863, some students joined a local emergency regiment. During the battle, the college building was used as a hospital. Today, the institution is called Gettysburg College. Its original main building, now known as Pennsylvania Hall, still stands on the same rise among the trees. It is one of the oldest buildings associated with the battle.
===
IMG: professor-thermometer-garden | a bearded professor in a dark coat of the 1860s standing in a small garden beside a wooden box with a thermometer, writing in a notebook, the brick wall of a college behind him, calm early morning light
HEAD: A professor in Gettysburg recorded the weather three times a day, right through the battle.
BODY: Michael Jacobs taught mathematics and natural science at Pennsylvania College. He was careful and methodical by nature. For years, he had kept daily weather records. At seven in the morning, two in the afternoon and nine in the evening, he noted the temperature, the wind, the clouds and any rain. He continued this habit throughout the summer of 1863, even while armies fought around the town. His notes survive. They show, for example, that the third of July was warm and humid, with temperatures in the high eighties Fahrenheit. They also record heavy rain on the fourth of July, after the battle had ended. Jacobs later wrote one of the first accounts of the battle, published within months. His weather records are still used by historians and battlefield guides. They help to explain conditions that soldiers described in letters, such as heat, thirst and mud. A quiet scientific habit thus preserved a precise record of one of history's most famous weeks.
===
IMG: lutheran-seminary-gardens-students-walking | a long four storey brick building with a white wooden cupola on top of a wooded ridge, young men in dark coats walking in quiet gardens with books in their hands, tall oak trees, soft golden evening light
HEAD: The Lutheran seminary on the ridge west of Gettysburg was founded in 1826, and it is the oldest of its kind in America.
BODY: West of town, on the ridge above the fields, stood the Lutheran Theological Seminary. It had been founded in 1826 and was the oldest continuously operating Lutheran seminary in the United States. Its main building was long, brick and four storeys high, crowned by a white wooden cupola. Here, young men trained to become Lutheran ministers. They studied Hebrew, Greek and theology. They preached practice sermons to one another and received guidance from their teachers. In free hours, they walked in the gardens and woods along the ridge. The building gave its name to Seminary Ridge. On the first day of the battle, Union cavalry officers used the cupola to watch Confederate troops approaching from the west. Later, the building became a hospital for wounded soldiers of both sides. Today, the main building houses a museum about the seminary and the battle. Visitors can climb to the cupola and look out over the same landscape.

HEAD: The gatehouse of Evergreen Cemetery had an arch in the middle and a small home on each side.
BODY: Evergreen Cemetery opened on the southern edge of Gettysburg in 1854. It was a peaceful place, with trees, winding paths and white gravestones. At its entrance beside the Baltimore Pike stood a handsome brick gatehouse. An arched passage ran through the middle, wide enough for a wagon. Rooms on either side served as the home of the cemetery keeper. In 1863, the keeper was Peter Thorn. Like many local men, he had joined the army. His wife, Elizabeth, took over his duties. The gatehouse stood on Cemetery Hill, which became one of the most important positions of the battle. Union artillery and infantry gathered around it. Shells struck the area during the fighting. The building survived with damage. Today, it still stands on the same hill, beside the cemetery and close to the Soldiers' National Cemetery created after the battle. A monument nearby honours the civilian women of Gettysburg, modelled on Elizabeth Thorn.

HEAD: While her husband was in the army, Elizabeth Thorn managed Evergreen Cemetery herself.
BODY: Elizabeth Thorn had come to America from Germany as a young woman. In 1863, she was about thirty years old. She had three young sons and was expecting another child. Her elderly parents lived with her in the gatehouse, and her father helped with heavy work. Managing a cemetery was demanding. Elizabeth cared for the grounds, paths and gates. She kept the burial records in a large book. When someone died in town, she and her father prepared the grave and helped with the burial. After the battle, her work became overwhelming. In the heat of July, she and her father buried around a hundred soldiers in the cemetery grounds. She later said that this labour, done while pregnant, damaged her health for years. Her daughter, born that autumn, was frail. Elizabeth Thorn's story became better known in the late twentieth century. It is now one of the most cited examples of civilian endurance during and after the battle.

HEAD: A free Black farmer named Abraham Bryan owned a small farm on the ridge south of town.
BODY: From Cemetery Hill, the land sloped gently in several directions. To the east rose wooded Culp's Hill. To the south, a long, low ridge ran for about two miles. It was called Cemetery Ridge. Farms lined it, as they did all around Gettysburg. Abraham Bryan, a free Black farmer, owned a small house, barn and orchard there. Pennsylvania was a free state, but Gettysburg lay close to slave-holding Maryland. Black residents knew that Confederate armies sometimes seized free Black people and sent them south into slavery. Many fled when the armies approached in 1863, and Bryan was among them. A widow named Lydia Leister lived in a small farmhouse nearby. It later became the headquarters of the Union commander, General George Meade. Further south lay the farms of the Trostle and Weikert families. Wheat grew tall in the fields, and young peaches ripened in an orchard beside the Emmitsburg Road. These farms would soon give their names to famous parts of the battlefield.

HEAD: Before the battle, the rocky valley called the Devil's Den was a popular place for picnics.
BODY: At the southern end of Cemetery Ridge rose two rocky, wooded hills. The larger was Big Round Top. The smaller, closer to town, was Little Round Top. Great grey boulders were scattered over their slopes. At their feet, a small stream wound through a valley filled with enormous rocks, some as large as a house. Local people called it the Devil's Den. Despite its name, it was a favourite picnic spot. On summer Sundays, families came out by wagon. Children climbed and hid among the boulders, while parents rested in the shade. The origins of the name are uncertain. Local stories spoke of a large snake once seen there. On the second day of the battle, these peaceful places saw some of the fiercest fighting. Confederate sharpshooters took cover among the rocks of Devil's Den. Union troops fought desperately to hold Little Round Top. Today, the boulders remain much as they were, visited by many thousands of people each year.
