BODY: It is the spring of 1863, in the south of Pennsylvania. The apple trees on the hillsides are in blossom, pink and white. The fields along the roads are green with young wheat and corn. Farmhouses stand among their barns and springhouses, with long lines of wooden fences between them. Cows graze in the meadows beside the creeks. About two thousand four hundred people live here. That is fewer than in many big schools today. The houses are neat, of brick and wood, along a few straight streets. A church spire or two rises above the rooftops. The town was laid out about eighty years earlier by a man named James Gettys. He drew the streets and sold the plots, and the town still carries his name.

HEAD: Ten roads met in Gettysburg, like the spokes of a wagon wheel meeting at the hub.
BODY: What makes Gettysburg a little unusual is its roads. No fewer than ten of them come together here, from every direction. On a map, they look like the spokes of a wheel meeting at the hub. There is the Chambersburg Pike, coming in from the mountains to the west. There is the road to Baltimore, running south. There are roads to Harrisburg and York and Hanover, to the north and east. And there are roads to Emmitsburg and Taneytown, just a few miles away in Maryland. Farmers bring their produce into town along these roads. Merchants send their goods out along them. Stagecoaches and wagons pass through every day, raising a little dust in dry weather. Since 1858, there has also been a small railway, coming in from Hanover in the east. For a town of this size, Gettysburg is very well connected. Remember those roads. A little later, they will matter a great deal.

BODY: The people of Gettysburg are mostly farmers and craftsmen. There are shopkeepers and teachers, ministers and lawyers too. Some of the older people still speak German at home, by the stove in the evening. The local German is a mix of old country words and English ones. Visitors from the cities sometimes smile at it. If a new bonnet appears in church on Sunday, everyone has talked about it by Monday. And on Sunday mornings, the church bells ring out across the rooftops, one after another, calling them to worship. It is a peaceful, ordinary, pleasant place, much like hundreds of other small towns across the country.

On court days, lawyers hurry across the square with papers under their arms. Farmers come in to pay their taxes, or to settle an argument about a fence. And the talk on the corners always comes back, sooner or later, to one subject. The war.

HEAD: For two years, most people in Gettysburg knew the war mainly from newspapers and letters.
BODY: For two years now, the United States has been divided. In the spring of 1861, eleven states in the South left the Union. They formed a new country of their own and called it the Confederate States of America. The government in Washington, under President Abraham Lincoln, refused to let them go. And so the war began. Since then, there have been great battles in Virginia and Tennessee, in Maryland and Mississippi. Hundreds of thousands of young men have gone off to fight, on both sides. In Gettysburg, the men follow it all in the newspapers. They read them aloud in the hotel lobbies and the stores. They argue about the generals and the politicians. It is something you read about in the evening, by the lamp, before you go to bed.

Once or twice, there have been rumours that the Southern army might come north. Each time, people hid their horses and their valuables, and waited. And each time, nothing happened at all.

Most of these people will be on their feet until the sun goes down, and then a little longer.

BODY: On Baltimore Street, in the middle of the town, there lives a girl named Tillie Pierce. Her full name is Matilda, but nobody ever calls her that. She is fifteen years old in the spring of 1863. So when you picture Gettysburg in 1863, you might picture it from her window, as she looked out at the street below.

BODY: One of the professors at the college is a man named Michael Jacobs. He teaches mathematics and natural science. He is a careful, methodical man, with a long face and a neat beard. BODY: To the west of town, on the ridge above the fields, stands the Lutheran Theological Seminary. It was founded in 1826, and it is the oldest Lutheran seminary in America. The main building is long and made of brick, four storeys high, with a white wooden cupola on top. Here, young men study to become ministers in the Lutheran church. They read Hebrew and Greek and theology. They preach practice sermons to one another, and their teachers tell them kindly what to do better. In their free hours, they walk in the gardens and the woods along the ridge. The view from the cupola is wonderful. On a clear day, you can see the whole town spread out below, and the farms and woods and orchards all around. Away to the west, there is the long blue line of the mountains. Because of this building, the people of Gettysburg call the ridge Seminary Ridge. The name will soon be known far beyond the town.

IMG: cemetery-gatehouse-brick-arch | a handsome brick gatehouse with an arched passage through the middle and small rooms on either side, at the entrance to a green hilltop cemetery with tall trees and white gravestones, a dirt road in front, warm evening light
HEAD: The cemetery gatehouse in Gettysburg had an arch in the middle and a small home on each side.
BODY: On the southern edge of town, on a hill above the Baltimore Pike, there is a cemetery. It is called Evergreen Cemetery. It was opened only about nine years earlier, in 1854. It is a peaceful, green place, with tall trees and winding paths and white gravestones. At the entrance, beside the road, stands a handsome brick gatehouse. It has an arched passage through the middle, wide enough for a wagon. On either side are rooms where the cemetery keeper lives. The keeper of the cemetery is a man named Peter Thorn. But Peter, like so many men in Gettysburg, has gone off to the army. So the work of the cemetery is being done by his wife, Elizabeth. You can still see the gatehouse today, with its arch, on the same hill. If you stood in front of it on a summer evening, you would hear the same thing Elizabeth heard. Birds settling in the trees, and the wind in the long grass.

IMG: woman-gardening-cemetery-paths | a strong young woman in a long work dress and apron tending flowers beside a gravel path among trees and white gravestones, three small boys playing nearby, a brick gatehouse behind, soft golden afternoon light
HEAD: While her husband was in the army, Elizabeth Thorn ran the whole cemetery herself.
BODY: Elizabeth Thorn came to America from Germany as a young woman. She is about thirty in 1863. She is strong and hardworking, with three young sons, and she is expecting another child in the autumn. Her elderly parents live with her in the gatehouse, and her father helps with the heavy work. It is not an easy job. Elizabeth looks after the grounds, the paths and the gates. She keeps the records of who is buried where, in a big book. When someone in the town passes away, she and her father prepare the grave and help with the burial. Then she writes the name carefully in the cemetery book. In between, she cooks and cleans for her family and tends her garden. Her boys play among the trees as if it were the finest playground in the world. From the gatehouse windows, she can look over the whole town in one direction, and over the long ridge to the south in the other.

HEAD: A free Black farmer named Abraham Bryan owned a small farm on the ridge south of town.
BODY: The hill with the cemetery on it is called Cemetery Hill. From the top, the land falls away gently on all sides. To the east, there is another wooded hill, called Culp's Hill. To the south, the long, low ridge runs on for two miles or so. People call it Cemetery Ridge. Along it, there are farms, as there are everywhere around Gettysburg. There is the farm of Abraham Bryan, a free Black farmer. He owns a small house, a barn and a few acres of land on the ridge. There is the little white farmhouse of a widow named Lydia Leister, who lives there with her children. Further south, there are the farms of the Trostles and the Weikerts, with their big stone barns and orchards. In the fields, the wheat is growing tall. In a peach orchard beside the Emmitsburg Road, the little green peaches are beginning to swell on the branches.

HEAD: The rocky valley called the Devil's Den was a favourite place for family picnics.
BODY: At the southern end of the ridge, two rocky hills rise out of the fields. The bigger one is called Big Round Top. The smaller one, a little closer to town, is Little Round Top. Both are covered in trees, with great grey boulders scattered over their slopes. At their feet, a little stream winds through a valley full of rocks. Some of the boulders there are as big as a house. The local people call that valley the Devil's Den. It sounds a little frightening, but it is really a favourite place for picnics. On summer Sundays, families come out in their wagons. The children climb on the rocks and hide between them. The parents spread a cloth in the shade and unpack bread, cold chicken and cherries. In the evening, the light falls long and golden across the fields. The cows walk slowly home to be milked. And on the hill, Elizabeth Thorn closes the iron gates of the cemetery and lights her lamp.
