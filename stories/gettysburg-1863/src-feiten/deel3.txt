TOPIC: The first two days of July
===
IMG: misty-dawn-fields-west-of-town | a light morning mist lying over green fields, wooden fences and orchards west of a small town, a gentle ridge with trees, birds in the sky, soft pale dawn light
HEAD: On the first morning of the battle, the professor's thermometer showed a mild seventy-two degrees.
BODY: Wednesday, the first of July, 1863, began quietly in Gettysburg. A light mist lay over the fields, and birds sang in the orchards. At seven o'clock, Professor Michael Jacobs recorded a temperature of seventy-two degrees Fahrenheit, with light clouds and a gentle southern breeze. West of town, along the Chambersburg Pike, Union cavalrymen were watching the road. A little after seven, they saw a long column of Confederate infantry approaching from the west. A Union lieutenant fired a shot at the distant column. This is traditionally remembered as the first shot of the battle. Several men later claimed the honour, and scholars cannot be completely certain who fired first. What is certain is that the fighting grew quickly from that moment. Within hours, thousands of soldiers were engaged along the ridges west of town. The largest battle ever fought in North America began on a calm summer morning, with a single shot in the mist.
===
IMG: townspeople-rooftops-watching-distant-smoke | townspeople of the 1860s standing in a street and on a rooftop looking west, small white clouds of smoke rising far away over a green ridge, brick houses and a church spire, bright morning light
HEAD: On the first day of the battle, some townsfolk of Gettysburg climbed onto rooftops to watch the fighting.
BODY: As the morning went on, townsfolk heard firing to the west. At first it was scattered and distant. Then it grew louder and steadier, and the deep boom of cannons joined in. People gathered in the streets and looked toward the ridges, where white clouds of smoke were rising. Some boys climbed onto rooftops for a better view. Older townsfolk went up into the cupolas of the college and the courthouse. Soldiers soon ordered them down and told them to stay indoors. Many families had to decide quickly whether to stay or leave. Some went to relatives in the farmland, believing it would be safer. The Pierce family sent their daughter Tillie south of town with their neighbour Hettie Shriver and her two young girls. They were going to the Weikert farm near the Round Tops. Such choices were made with very little information. No one yet knew where the fighting would spread over the following days.
===
IMG: girl-woman-children-walking-south-road | a girl of fifteen in a long dress walking along a quiet country road with a young mother and two small girls, past a hill with trees and a brick gatehouse, fields and a rocky wooded hill ahead, bright summer midday light
HEAD: Tillie Pierce was sent to a farm for safety, near where some of the heaviest fighting would take place.
BODY: Shortly before noon on the first of July, Tillie Pierce set off on foot with Hettie Shriver and her daughters. They walked south past the cemetery, away from the sound of the guns. The road was quiet, the wheat golden and the sun hot. They reached the Weikert farm in the early afternoon. No one could have known that the farm lay close to ground where some of the fiercest fighting of the second day would occur. Meanwhile, back in town, John Burns took his old musket from the wall. He put on his tall hat and best coat and walked west toward the fighting. Near the seminary, he found Union soldiers in a wood and asked to join them. They were astonished, but they let him stay. Burns fought alongside them for a few hours and was wounded three times. These two journeys, one away from danger and one toward it, show how differently common people responded when the battle reached their town.

IMG: church-turned-hospital-women-water | the inside of a simple brick church in the 1860s turned into a quiet hospital, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soldiers resting on the wooden pews and on blankets, women in long dresses carrying water jugs and bandages, soft light through tall windows
HEAD: On the first day of the battle, churches in Gettysburg were already being turned into hospitals.
BODY: By the afternoon of the first of July, wounded soldiers were streaming into Gettysburg. Some walked or limped. Others were carried by comrades or brought in wagons. Army surgeons set up hospitals in churches, the courthouse, the college, the seminary and many private homes. Christ Lutheran Church on Chambersburg Street filled with men lying on pews and floors. Other churches quickly followed. Townsfolk opened their doors. Women brought water, bandages and bread. Few had any medical training, but they began to help immediately. Church buildings were especially useful because they had large open interiors. Pews were at times covered with boards to make beds. In the following weeks, nearly every public building in town became a hospital. Many still carry reminders of this period. Some churches display plaques or preserved bloodstains from the wounded who lay there. These buildings, made for worship, became places where townsfolk and soldiers met in the hardest circumstances of their lives.

IMG: family-cellar-candle-preserves-jars | a family of the 1860s sitting quietly together in a stone cellar by the light of a single candle, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, shelves of preserve jars and sacks of potatoes, a mother holding a small child close
HEAD: During the fighting, many families in Gettysburg sheltered in their cellars among jars of preserves.
BODY: Late in the afternoon of the first day, Union forces were pushed back through the town. Soldiers ran through streets, alleys and back gardens toward Cemetery Hill south of town. For a short time, the streets were filled with shouting and confusion. Townsfolk stayed indoors. Many families went down into their cellars and locked the doors. They sat among potatoes and jars of preserves, often by the light of a single candle. Children were frightened, and parents tried to comfort them. Local accounts describe the sounds of running feet, gunfire and shouting overhead. Some Union soldiers hid in cellars and attics to avoid capture. A few remained hidden for days. By evening, the fighting had moved south of town. When families came up from their cellars, they found Confederate soldiers occupying the streets. For the next two days, Gettysburg lay between the lines, with townsfolk living in a town held by one army and watched by another.

IMG: cemetery-hill-gatehouse-soldiers-evening | a brick gatehouse with an arched passage on a hilltop at dusk, Union soldiers resting by low stone walls, an officer talking with a strong young woman in an apron at the door, lamplight in a window, a soft purple evening sky
HEAD: On the first night of the battle, the cemetery keeper's wife baked bread for Union soldiers until late.
BODY: By the evening of the first of July, Union forces had gathered on Cemetery Hill and Culp's Hill. They dug in behind stone walls and piles of fence rails. Confederate forces held the town and the ridges to the west and north. The Evergreen Cemetery gatehouse now stood in the middle of the Union position. Elizabeth Thorn, pregnant and caring for her family, baked bread and made coffee for officers and soldiers. According to her later account, an officer asked her to show him the roads around the hill. She walked out with him in the dusk, pointing out the routes one by one. Union generals later used the gatehouse as a headquarters for a time. Elizabeth's family left the area during the heavy fighting of the next days. When they returned, they found their home badly damaged. Her local knowledge was a small but practical contribution. It shows how townsfolk at times became part of military decisions without ever intending to.

IMG: farmhouse-well-water-soldiers-night | a girl in a long dress handing a cup of water to soldiers marching past a farmhouse at night, a wooden bucket from a stone well, lanterns glowing on the porch, a long line of soldiers on the dark road
HEAD: At the Weikert farm, Tillie Pierce carried water to Union soldiers marching past.
BODY: On the night of the first of July, the Weikert farm near Little Round Top was crowded with soldiers. Union troops were marching north along the Taneytown Road to join the army on Cemetery Ridge. The family and their guests baked bread and drew water from the well for the passing men. Tillie Pierce carried buckets of water to the road and filled soldiers' cups. In her book, she described how grateful the men were. Some showed her photographs of their families. Some asked her to write to their relatives if anything happened to them. That night, she slept on the farmhouse floor, exhausted, while troops continued marching past outside. The next day, the farm lay close to the fighting around the Round Tops and the Wheatfield. The house became a field hospital. Wounded soldiers filled its rooms and yard. Tillie's account of these hours is one of the most vivid local descriptions of the battle from behind the Union lines.

IMG: officer-on-rocky-hilltop-telescope | a young officer standing among large grey boulders on a rocky wooded hilltop, looking west through field glasses across wide summer fields toward a distant wooded ridge, a signal flag beside him, bright afternoon light
HEAD: On the second day, a Union engineer discovered that Little Round Top was almost undefended.
BODY: Thursday, the second of July, began quietly. For most of the morning, the armies prepared rather than fought. Between Seminary Ridge and Cemetery Ridge lay about a mile of farm fields, orchards and meadows. At the southern end of the Union line rose Little Round Top. That morning, it was almost empty, apart from a few signalmen sending messages with flags. In the afternoon, General Gouverneur Warren, the army's chief engineer, rode up to inspect the position. Looking west, he noticed sunlight glinting on rifles among the trees. He realised that Confederate forces were preparing to attack. If they captured the hill, they could fire along the entire Union line. Warren immediately sent messengers to find troops who could defend it. Reinforcements arrived just in time. A statue of Warren now stands on the summit, looking west. His quick observation is often cited as one of the decisive moments of the battle.

IMG: rocky-wooded-slope-boulders-afternoon | a steep wooded hillside covered with great grey boulders and oak trees, soldiers in blue kneeling behind the rocks, a thin haze of smoke drifting through the trees in the distance, hazy late afternoon light
HEAD: On Little Round Top, the 20th Maine held the very end of the Union line.
BODY: At about four o'clock on the second of July, Confederate forces under General Longstreet launched a major attack. The fighting spread through the Peach Orchard, the Wheatfield and Devil's Den. At the Weikert farm, soldiers told the family and their guests to leave immediately. Tillie Pierce and the Shrivers hurried to another farmhouse further from the fighting. On Little Round Top, Union reinforcements arrived just in time. Among them was the 20th Maine under Colonel Joshua Chamberlain. His regiment was placed at the extreme end of the Union line, among boulders and trees. For about an hour and a half, they held their position against repeated attacks. Running low on ammunition, Chamberlain ordered a downhill bayonet charge. The attacking Confederates fell back. Scholars still debate how decisive this action was. Yet it became one of the best-known episodes of the battle, made famous again in the twentieth century through books and films.

IMG: small-farmhouse-lamplit-generals-night | the tiny front room of a small white farmhouse at night, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, a group of generals in blue coats crowded around a small wooden table lit by a single candle, maps spread out, quiet and serious
HEAD: On the night of the second of July, Union generals met in a widow's small farmhouse to decide whether to stay.
BODY: As evening fell on the second of July, the fighting gradually died down. Confederate troops had captured the Peach Orchard and Devil's Den. Yet the Union line along Cemetery Ridge held from Cemetery Hill to Little Round Top. Across the dark fields, lanterns moved slowly back and forth. Stretcher-bearers were searching for wounded men and carrying them to barns and farmhouses behind the lines. General Meade had made his headquarters in the small farmhouse of a widow, Lydia Leister. That night, his generals crowded into its tiny front room around a table lit by candles. Meade asked whether the army should stay and fight or withdraw. One by one, the generals gave their opinions. They agreed to stay and defend their position. This meeting became known as the council of war. The Leister house still stands on the battlefield. It is a modest building for such a key decision.

TOPIC: The third day, and a rainy Fourth of July
===
IMG: hot-morning-baltimore-street-quiet | a quiet street of brick houses on a hot hazy summer morning in the 1860s, closed shutters, an empty dirt road, a few soldiers resting in the shade of a doorway, a green hill at the end of the street, warm hazy light
HEAD: The third day of the battle was the hottest, at eighty-seven degrees in the afternoon.
BODY: Friday, the third of July, dawned hot and still. At seven o'clock, Professor Jacobs recorded seventy-three degrees and cloudy skies. By two in the afternoon, the temperature reached eighty-seven, the hottest reading of the battle. Early in the morning, heavy fighting resumed on Culp's Hill, east of the cemetery. The noise echoed through the streets of the town. By late morning, that fighting had ended. Gettysburg grew strangely quiet. Confederate soldiers had occupied the town for two days. They sat on doorsteps cleaning rifles and cooked small meals in alleys. Some knocked on doors and politely asked for bread or water. Townsfolk mostly stayed indoors with shutters closed, peering through cracks. Food and water were running low in many houses. Local accounts describe exhaustion and uncertainty. No one knew how long the battle would last or which army would remain. The heat made everything harder for soldiers, townsfolk and the wounded alike.
===
IMG: small-brick-house-kitchen-bread-dough | a small simple kitchen of the 1860s in early morning light, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, a wooden table with a bowl of bread dough and a dusting of flour, a few loaves cooling, an iron stove, a window with soft light
HEAD: Jennie Wade was baking bread when a stray bullet struck her on the third of July.
BODY: Mary Virginia Wade was staying at her sister's house on Baltimore Street during the battle. Her sister Georgia had recently given birth. Ginnie and her mother had come to help. The small brick house stood between the two armies, and it was struck many times by bullets. On the morning of the third of July, Ginnie was in the kitchen kneading dough. She had been baking bread for Union soldiers during the battle. At about half past eight, a bullet passed through two wooden doors and struck her. She died instantly. She was twenty years old. She is the only civilian known to have been killed directly by the fighting at Gettysburg. Her fiancé, Johnston Skelly, had been wounded earlier in Virginia. He died a few days later in a hospital, never learning of her death. The house is now a museum. Her story became one of the most widely told personal stories of the battle.
===
IMG: hot-noon-ridge-waiting-soldiers-shade | soldiers resting quietly in the shade of trees along a low stone wall on a ridge at noon, fields shimmering in the summer heat below, bees over clover, a few officers sitting on the grass eating, hazy bright light
HEAD: Just before the great bombardment on the third day, some Union officers ate a lunch of stewed chicken.
BODY: By late morning on the third of July, a long stillness settled over the battlefield. Soldiers on both ridges lay in the hot sun, waiting. Some slept, some wrote letters, and some ate what little food they had. The empty fields between the ridges shimmered in the heat. An officer named Frank Haskell later described a lunch on Cemetery Ridge. Several Union generals sat together in the shade and ate stewed chicken, potatoes, bread and coffee. The chicken came from an old rooster found at a nearby farm. Haskell wrote that it was tough, but welcome. His long letter about the battle became one of its most famous eyewitness accounts. The calm did not last. At about one o'clock, two signal guns fired from Seminary Ridge. Moments later, Confederate artillery all along the ridge opened fire. Union guns soon replied. The largest artillery bombardment of the war had begun.
===
IMG: white-smoke-cloud-over-fields-distant | a great white cloud of smoke drifting slowly over wide summer fields and a distant ridge, seen from far away, a quiet farmhouse and trees in the foreground, the sun a pale disc behind the haze
HEAD: The cannonade at Gettysburg on the third of July could be heard many miles away.
BODY: Around one hundred and fifty Confederate cannons opened fire on the Union lines. More than a hundred Union guns answered. The noise was enormous. Reports suggest it was heard many miles away, rolling across the farmland like distant thunder. In town, windows rattled and dishes shook on shelves. The ground trembled. Clouds of white smoke drifted across the fields and dimmed the sun. Families sheltered again in their cellars. On Cemetery Ridge, Union soldiers pressed themselves behind stone walls. The bombardment lasted for more than an hour, some accounts say nearly two. Many Confederate shells flew too high and landed behind the Union front line. When the firing died down, a long line of Confederate infantry stepped out from the woods on Seminary Ridge. About twelve thousand men advanced across open fields in lines almost a mile wide. Many belonged to the division of General George Pickett. The attack became known as Pickett's Charge.
===
IMG: general-lee-on-horse-meeting-soldiers | a grey-bearded general on a grey horse speaking gently to tired soldiers walking back across a field at the edge of a wood, late afternoon light, a quiet and sad mood, smoke thinning in the distance
HEAD: After Pickett's Charge failed, General Lee told his returning soldiers that it was all his fault.
BODY: The Confederate lines advanced steadily across the fields, crossing fences and the Emmitsburg Road. Union soldiers on Cemetery Ridge watched them come. Many later said it was one of the most impressive sights they had ever seen. As the attackers drew closer, Union artillery and rifle fire tore into their ranks. A small number reached the stone wall near a clump of trees on Cemetery Ridge. For a few minutes, they broke through. Then they were stopped. The survivors fell back across the fields toward Seminary Ridge, helping wounded comrades along. Losses were very heavy. More than half of the attackers were killed, wounded or captured. General Lee rode out to meet the returning men. According to witnesses, he told them that the failure was entirely his fault. He did not blame his soldiers. Scholars still argue about his decision to order the attack. Yet his acceptance of responsibility has often been noted as a rare act of honesty by a commander.
===
IMG: rain-falling-town-dawn-july | soft summer rain falling on the wet roofs and muddy street of a quiet brick town at dawn, water running in the gutters, people cautiously opening their front doors and shutters, grey gentle light
HEAD: On the morning of the Fourth of July, townsfolk of Gettysburg found that the Confederate soldiers had left the town.
BODY: As the sun set on the third of July, the battlefield finally fell silent. Townsfolk emerged from their cellars, uncertain what would happen next. In the early hours of the fourth of July, rain began to fall. At first it was a light drizzle. By afternoon, it was pouring. Professor Jacobs recorded the rainfall in his notebook. It was Independence Day. In other years, the town would have celebrated with flags and a band. In 1863, there was only rain. During the night, Confederate forces had withdrawn from the town to Seminary Ridge. In the morning, townsfolk opened their shutters and saw empty streets. Slowly, they stepped outside. Union soldiers came down from Cemetery Hill into the town. Some townsfolk cried, some laughed, and some simply stood silently in the rain. After three days of battle, the town was once again in Union hands. Its people now faced a very different challenge.
===
IMG: wagon-train-rain-night-mountain-road | a very long line of covered wagons and ambulances moving slowly along a muddy mountain road at night in heavy rain, horses splashing through puddles, a few lanterns swinging, dark wooded hills
HEAD: When Lee's army retreated, its train of wagons and ambulances stretched for about seventeen miles.
BODY: On the fourth of July, both armies remained on their ridges, watching each other through the rain. Neither attacked. Both were exhausted after three days of fighting. Soldiers huddled under oilcloth blankets and tried to make coffee over sputtering fires. That evening, Lee's army began its retreat westward toward the mountains and Virginia. First came a vast wagon train carrying wounded soldiers. It stretched for roughly seventeen miles along muddy roads. Accounts describe the suffering of the wounded as wagons jolted through the rain all night. Then came the artillery, followed by the infantry. On the same day, far away on the Mississippi River, the city of Vicksburg surrendered to Union forces under General Ulysses S. Grant. The two events together marked a turning point in the war. The Confederacy never again invaded the North on such a scale. Many people in the North began to believe, for the first time, that victory was possible.

IMG: mother-daughter-reunion-doorstep | a mother in a long dress and apron hugging a tired muddy girl on the front step of a brick house, a quiet wet street, soft evening light after rain, the sky clearing
HEAD: When Tillie Pierce returned home after the battle, her mother did not recognise her at first.
BODY: Tillie Pierce spent several more days at a farmhouse south of the battlefield. The roads were crowded with soldiers, wagons and ambulances, making travel difficult. She helped wherever she could, carrying water, rolling bandages and writing letters for wounded soldiers. On the seventh of July, she finally walked home. The farmland had been transformed. Fences had been torn down for firewood. Wheat fields had been trampled flat. Dead horses lay in the fields. When she reached Baltimore Street, her mother was standing at the door. According to Tillie's account, her mother did not recognise her at first, because she was so tired, pale and muddy. Then she ran down the steps and embraced her. The whole family had survived. Tillie later wrote that the bread and butter she ate at the kitchen table that day tasted better than anything she had ever eaten. Her account captures the relief felt by many families after the battle.

TOPIC: A town that became a hospital
===
IMG: night-town-lamps-every-window | a small town of brick houses at night after rain, a lamp glowing warmly in almost every window, wet streets shining, a clear sky full of stars above the rooftops and church spire, quiet and peaceful
HEAD: After the battle, Gettysburg had about eight wounded soldiers for each person who lived there.
BODY: When the armies left, more than twenty thousand wounded soldiers remained in and around Gettysburg. The town had only about two thousand four hundred townsfolk. That meant roughly eight wounded men for each person living there. They filled churches, schools, the college, the seminary, the courthouse, warehouses, barns and nearly every private house. Townsfolk cared for them as best they could. Women made soup and baked bread from morning until night. They brought water, washed faces and sat beside the wounded. They fanned them in the heat and wrote letters for men who could not write. They read to them and at times sang softly in the evenings. Men carried stretchers and assisted surgeons. Children ran errands. Many townsfolk later noted that they cared for soldiers from both sides. Wounded Confederates were treated alongside Union soldiers. For a few weeks, the entire town became a hospital, and its townsfolk became nurses.
===
IMG: young-teacher-sitting-with-soldier | a young schoolteacher of the 1860s with dark hair and a gentle face sitting beside a resting soldier on a cot, holding a cup of water, in a quiet church with tall windows, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft light
HEAD: A young schoolteacher named Sallie Myers became one of Gettysburg's most devoted volunteer nurses.
BODY: Sarah Myers, known as Sallie, was about twenty-one in 1863 and worked as a schoolteacher. On the first day of the battle, wounded soldiers were carried into St Francis Xavier Catholic Church on High Street. Sallie went inside to help. She had no nursing experience and later admitted she was frightened. Yet she stayed. She brought water and food, washed faces, held hands and listened as soldiers spoke about home. Her family took a wounded sergeant from Pennsylvania into their own house. Sallie cared for him there until he died. She kept a diary during these weeks. Her writings later became a key source for scholars. They describe exhaustion, grief and moments of unexpected friendship. Many young women in Gettysburg did similar work, often without recognition. Sallie Myers is remembered partly because she wrote down what she saw. Her diary preserves the experience of a normal civilian thrust into an extraordinary role.
===
IMG: supply-train-unloading-station-volunteers | a small country railway station in the 1860s crowded with people, volunteers in long coats and women in bonnets unloading crates and barrels of supplies from a steam train, piles of blankets on the platform, afternoon light
HEAD: After the battle, trains full of food, bandages and volunteers arrived in Gettysburg from across the North.
BODY: Help soon arrived from outside. Trains came into Gettysburg from Baltimore, Philadelphia and Harrisburg. They brought doctors, nurses, food, bandages and medicine. They carried clean sheets, shirts and blankets. Much of this aid came from two large Northern relief organisations, the United States Sanitary Commission and the United States Christian Commission. Churches and communities across the country also sent supplies. Children in distant towns collected money to buy lemons and crackers for the wounded. Volunteers came too. Some simply wanted to help. Others came searching for sons, husbands or brothers among the wounded and the dead. The small railway station was crowded day and night. Local townsfolk guided newcomers through the town and helped them find hospitals. The relief effort at Gettysburg was one of the largest of the war. It showed how civilian organisations had become essential to caring for soldiers on a scale never seen before in America.
===
IMG: white-tent-hospital-rows-trees | hundreds of white canvas hospital tents set out in long neat rows among tall trees on a gentle hill, nurses in long dresses walking between them with baskets, soldiers sitting outside their tents, long golden evening light
HEAD: A large tent hospital called Camp Letterman was set up on a hill east of Gettysburg after the battle.
BODY: In mid-July 1863, the army established a general hospital east of town beside the York Pike. It was named Camp Letterman, after Jonathan Letterman, the medical director of the Army of the Potomac. Hundreds of white canvas tents stood in long, neat rows among the trees. There were kitchens, a bakery and supply stores. The site was chosen partly because it lay near the railway, making it easier to bring supplies and move patients. Gradually, the wounded were transferred there from churches, houses and barns. The camp was orderly and well ventilated. Nurses moved along the rows with baskets and buckets. Letterman had already introduced new systems for evacuating and treating the wounded. His methods improved survival and influenced military medicine for generations. By late November, most patients had been sent home or to other hospitals. Camp Letterman closed shortly afterwards. Today, little remains of the site, but its importance in medical history is widely recognised.
===
IMG: gatehouse-woman-baby-autumn-window | a strong young woman in a long dress holding a newborn baby wrapped in a shawl by the window of a small brick gatehouse room, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, autumn trees outside, warm soft light
HEAD: In November 1863, the cemetery keeper's wife gave birth to a daughter named Rose.
BODY: When Elizabeth Thorn returned to the Evergreen Cemetery gatehouse after the battle, she found it badly damaged. Furniture had been broken and bedding taken. Yet there was urgent work to do. In the hot days of July, she and her elderly father buried around a hundred soldiers who had died on and around Cemetery Hill. She was six months pregnant at the time. The work was physically exhausting and emotionally painful. Elizabeth later said that it affected her health for years. In 1905, she described her experiences to a newspaper, wanting people to understand what local women had endured. In November 1863, she gave birth to a daughter. She named her Rose Meade Thorn, after the Union commander. The girl was frail as a child, but she survived. Elizabeth's story was largely overlooked for many decades. Today, she is often cited as one of the clearest examples of the hidden labour of women during and after the battle.
===
IMG: farmer-rebuilding-fence-late-summer | a farmer in shirt sleeves and a straw hat rebuilding a split rail wooden fence beside a field in late summer, his son handing him a rail, a farmhouse and orchard behind, soft golden afternoon light
HEAD: After the battle, farmers around Gettysburg had to rebuild miles of fences by hand.
BODY: Gradually, life in Gettysburg returned to something like normal. Farmers went back to their fields and gathered what remained of the harvest. They rebuilt fences rail by rail. Thousands of fence rails had been burned for firewood or used to build defences. Many farms had lost crops, livestock and buildings. Some families later submitted damage claims to the government, which survive as useful historical records. Shops reopened, and storekeepers brought back their goods from Philadelphia. The bank resumed business. Schools reopened in the autumn. Yet the town was permanently changed. Every family had stories of those days. Many houses carried bullet holes and shell damage. Across the fields and ridges, thousands of soldiers lay in hastily dug graves, often marked only by wooden boards with names written in pencil. Some were not marked at all. Many townsfolk felt deeply troubled by this situation. They believed the soldiers deserved a proper and lasting resting place.

TOPIC: A resting place on Cemetery Hill
===
IMG: david-wills-house-diamond | a large three storey red brick house on the corner of a small town square in the 1860s, tall windows with shutters, a lamp glowing in an upstairs window, a quiet street, soft evening light
HEAD: The idea of a national cemetery at Gettysburg came from a young local lawyer, David Wills.
BODY: David Wills was a lawyer of about thirty-two. He lived in a large brick house on the Diamond, in the centre of Gettysburg. He was energetic, practical and well organised. After the battle, the governor of Pennsylvania asked him to oversee matters relating to Pennsylvania's dead soldiers. Wills walked across the battlefield and saw graves scattered across fields and woods. Letters arrived daily from families across the North. They asked where their loved ones were buried and whether their bodies could be brought home. Wills proposed creating a national cemetery at Gettysburg, where Union soldiers could be buried together with honour. The governor supported the idea, and other Northern states agreed to share the cost. It was a new kind of project. National cemeteries were still uncommon at the time. The Soldiers' National Cemetery at Gettysburg became a model for many that followed. The Wills house still stands on the square and is now a museum.
===
IMG: landscape-designer-plans-hilltop | a thoughtful man with a beard in a dark coat standing on a green hilltop in late summer with a large paper plan in his hands, looking out over fields and ridges, a brick gatehouse and trees nearby, soft golden light
HEAD: The graves in the Soldiers' National Cemetery were laid out in curved rows, like ripples in a pond.
BODY: Wills purchased about seventeen acres on Cemetery Hill, next to Evergreen Cemetery. The land overlooked many of the fields and ridges where the battle had been fought. To design the new cemetery, the states chose William Saunders. He was a Scottish-born landscape gardener working for the government in Washington. Saunders walked the hill many times, studying its shape. He then created a simple and elegant plan. The graves would be arranged in curved rows forming a semicircle. The rows resembled ripples spreading from a stone dropped into water. Soldiers from each state would be buried together in their own section. At the centre, on the highest point, a monument would stand. Saunders wanted the design to feel calm and dignified. He used trees and open lawns rather than elaborate decoration. His plan still shapes the cemetery today. It is often admired as one of the most thoughtful memorial landscapes of its time.
===
IMG: autumn-trees-cemetery-hill-workers | workers in plain clothes quietly preparing curved rows on a green hillside in autumn, trees with red and gold leaves, a brick gatehouse in the distance, soft grey autumn light, a calm and respectful mood
HEAD: In the national cemetery, each grave was made the same, whether it held an officer or a private.
BODY: Saunders made another key decision. All graves would be equal. There would be no large monuments for officers and small stones for common soldiers. Each man would rest beside his comrades on the same terms. In the autumn of 1863, reburials began. Bodies were carefully moved from temporary graves across the battlefield into the new cemetery. Workers recorded names whenever they could be identified. Many soldiers could not be identified at all. Their graves were marked as unknown. Records from the reburial work show how difficult and painful the process was. It continued through the autumn and into the following year. More than three thousand Union soldiers were eventually buried there. Confederate dead were not included and remained in field graves for years. Many were later moved to cemeteries in the South. The principle of equal graves became a lasting tradition in American military cemeteries. It expressed the idea that each soldier's sacrifice deserved the same respect.

IMG: elderly-statesman-library-books-lamp | a dignified white-haired gentleman of about seventy in a dark frock coat writing at a large desk in a library full of books, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, a green-shaded oil lamp, autumn evening
HEAD: The main speaker at the Gettysburg ceremony was famous for speeches that could last for hours.
BODY: Wills and the organising committee planned a ceremony to dedicate the new cemetery. They invited Edward Everett to deliver the main address. Everett was one of the most celebrated speakers in America. He was nearly seventy years old and came from Massachusetts. He had been a congressman, governor, senator, president of Harvard College and Secretary of State. He was known for long, carefully crafted speeches that often lasted hours. The ceremony was originally planned for October. Everett said he needed more time to prepare, so the date was moved to the nineteenth of November. He studied battle reports and letters from generals. He planned to visit the battlefield personally before speaking. Speeches like his were a popular form of public event in the nineteenth century. Audiences expected length, learning and classical references. Everett's address at Gettysburg was praised at the time. Yet it was soon overshadowed by a far shorter speech delivered after it.

HEAD: President Lincoln was invited to Gettysburg only to add a few appropriate remarks.
BODY: On the second of November, 1863, David Wills wrote to President Abraham Lincoln. He invited him to attend the ceremony and, after Everett's oration, to offer a few appropriate remarks. The invitation came relatively late. Lincoln was extremely busy, and the committee was not sure he would come. Yet he accepted. Gettysburg prepared eagerly. Flags and bunting were hung from windows. A wooden platform was built at the new cemetery. Wills invited the President to stay in his own house on the Diamond. Lincoln was fifty-four years old, very tall and thin, with a deeply lined face. He had led the country for nearly three years through civil war. Scholars know he worked on the speech in Washington and probably revised it in Gettysburg. Several handwritten versions survive. He had been asked for only a few words. He chose to say very little, but to make each word count.

TOPIC: Two minutes on a November afternoon

IMG: presidential-train-countryside-november | a small steam train of the 1860s with four wooden carriages crossing a quiet countryside in November, bare brown fields and leafless trees, smoke rising from farmhouse chimneys, soft grey autumn light
HEAD: Lincoln's train journey from Washington to Gettysburg took about six hours.
BODY: On Wednesday, the eighteenth of November, 1863, a special train left Washington around noon. President Lincoln travelled with his private secretaries, John Nicolay and John Hay. Members of his cabinet, foreign diplomats, a military band and a guard of soldiers accompanied him. The journey took about six hours. The train passed through the Maryland farmland, past bare fields and leafless woods. At Hanover Junction, it switched to a branch line heading west toward Gettysburg. Crowds gathered at stations along the route to see the President. At Hanover, people called for him to speak. He stepped onto the rear platform and offered a few friendly words. According to one account, a young girl gave him flowers, and he bent down to thank her. A popular story later claimed that Lincoln wrote his speech on the back of an envelope during this journey. Scholars consider this unlikely. He had already prepared much of it before leaving Washington.

IMG: dinner-table-lamplight-guests-1863 | a long dining table in a warm lamplit dining room of the 1860s, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, about two dozen guests in dark evening clothes, a very tall man listening quietly, silver dishes and candles
HEAD: So many visitors came to Gettysburg for the ceremony that some walked the streets all night.
BODY: Lincoln's train reached Gettysburg at dusk. A crowd waited at the small station in the cold. David Wills and Edward Everett met the President and walked with him up Carlisle Street to the Wills house on the Diamond. That evening, the Wills family hosted a dinner for about two dozen guests. Accounts describe Lincoln as quiet but friendly. He listened more than he spoke, though he at times told stories from his early life in Illinois. Outside, the town was overflowing. Thousands of visitors had arrived, far more than Gettysburg could accommodate. People slept in churches, parlours, hallways and chairs. Some could find no shelter at all and wandered the streets through the night. Hotels and private homes were packed. Many visitors had travelled long distances, some to visit the graves of relatives. For a town of two and a half thousand people, the arrival of perhaps fifteen thousand visitors was an extraordinary event.

HEAD: When a crowd called for a speech the night before, Lincoln politely joked his way out of it.
BODY: After dinner, a crowd gathered outside the Wills house with a band. They played patriotic songs and called for the President to speak. Eventually Lincoln appeared at the door. He thanked them for coming. Then, according to newspaper reports, he explained that he had no speech prepared. In his position, he said, it was key not to say foolish things. Someone in the crowd shouted that he should avoid them if he could. Lincoln replied that the only way to help it was often to say nothing at all. The crowd laughed and cheered and let him go back inside. Later, he went upstairs to his room on the second floor. It had a large bed, a fireplace and a small writing table. Scholars believe he worked further on his remarks there. The room has been restored and can be visited today. The exchange at the door shows the humour Lincoln often used to manage public attention.

HEAD: The night before his speech, Lincoln received a telegram saying that his sick son was feeling better.
BODY: Before going to bed, Lincoln received a telegram from Washington. His youngest son, Tad, had been ill with a fever. Lincoln had been worried about him. His wife Mary had been very anxious and had not wanted him to leave. Their concern was understandable. In February 1862, their son Willie had died of typhoid fever at the age of eleven. Lincoln still wore a black mourning band on his hat in Willie's memory. The telegram brought good news. Tad was improving. Outside, the town remained busy late into the night. Visitors walked through the streets talking and singing. The night was cold and clear, with bright stars. These personal details are often overlooked in accounts of the Gettysburg Address. Yet they remind us that Lincoln spoke about national loss while carrying his own grief. The speech he gave the next day was written by a man who knew personally what it meant to lose a child.

IMG: lincoln-on-horseback-procession-street | a very tall man in a black suit, white gloves and a tall black hat riding a small horse in a slow procession down a town street of brick houses, soldiers and a band ahead, people watching from doorsteps, clear November sunshine
HEAD: Lincoln rode to the cemetery on a horse so small that his feet nearly touched the ground.
BODY: Thursday, the nineteenth of November, began cool and grey. As the morning went on, the clouds cleared and the sun came out. It became a mild and still autumn day. At about ten o'clock, a procession formed in the Diamond. It included soldiers, bands, governors, generals, members of Congress and wounded veterans. Lincoln appeared in a black suit, white gloves and his tall black hat. He mounted a horse provided for him. Several witnesses noted that he was so tall and the horse so small that his feet nearly touched the ground. The procession moved slowly along Baltimore Street toward Cemetery Hill. Townsfolk lined the streets and stood on doorsteps. Tillie Pierce later recalled standing outside her house as the President passed close by. At the cemetery, an estimated fifteen thousand people gathered around the wooden platform. Many stood among the newly dug graves, waiting for the ceremony to begin.

IMG: elderly-orator-speaking-crowd | a white-haired elderly speaker in a dark coat speaking from a wooden platform draped with flags to a large quiet crowd on a hillside in autumn, bare trees, a wide view of fields and ridges behind, soft November sunlight
HEAD: Edward Everett spoke for about two hours at the Gettysburg ceremony, entirely from memory.
BODY: The ceremony began with music and a long prayer. Then Edward Everett rose to speak. He delivered his address from memory, in a rich and carefully trained voice. It lasted about two hours. He described the battle in detail, day by day, from the first shots west of town to the great attack on the third day. He compared the occasion with the funeral ceremonies of ancient Athens, where citizens honoured those who died in war. He discussed the causes of the conflict and the future of the nation. The speech was thoughtful and polished. Many listeners admired it. Yet it was long, and some people at the back of the crowd grew restless. A few wandered away to look at the graves and the battlefield. When Everett finished, a choir sang a hymn written for the occasion. Then Lincoln's friend Ward Hill Lamon, who was in charge of arrangements, announced the President of the United States.

HEAD: The Gettysburg Address took about two minutes and contained only around two hundred and seventy words.
BODY: Lincoln rose and stepped forward with a sheet of paper. He spoke in a high, clear voice, shaped by his youth in Kentucky and Indiana. He began with the words that became famous: four score and seven years ago. He spoke of a nation conceived in liberty and dedicated to the idea that all men are created equal. He said that the world would little note what was said there, but could never forget what the soldiers had done. He ended with the hope that government of the people, by the people, for the people, would not perish from the earth. The speech lasted about two minutes. Some listeners barely realised it had begun before it ended. A photographer setting up his camera reportedly missed the moment entirely. Only one known photograph shows Lincoln on the platform, small and partly hidden in the crowd. Applause followed. Newspapers reported the speech in very different ways, depending on their politics.

HEAD: Edward Everett wrote that Lincoln had captured the meaning of the day in two minutes better than he had in two hours.
BODY: Lincoln was not sure his speech had succeeded. According to some accounts, he told a friend he feared it had fallen flat. The next day, however, Edward Everett wrote him a gracious letter. He said he wished he had come as close to the central idea of the occasion in two hours as the President had in two minutes. Lincoln replied kindly, saying he was glad his brief remarks had not been a complete failure. That evening, John Burns walked with Lincoln to a church service in town. Afterwards, the President boarded his train back to Washington. He felt unwell on the journey and lay down with a damp cloth over his eyes. Within days, doctors found that he had a mild form of smallpox. He spent a few weeks recovering. Five handwritten copies of the Gettysburg Address survive today. Each differs slightly, showing how Lincoln continued to refine his words even after he had spoken them.

IMG: older-woman-writing-memoir-parlour | a gentle older woman with grey hair in a dark dress of the 1880s writing in a notebook at a small table in a cosy parlour, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, an oil lamp, a framed photograph on the wall
HEAD: Tillie Pierce married, moved away and published her memories of the battle in 1889.
BODY: The Civil War continued for another year and a half after the Gettysburg Address. In April 1865, General Lee surrendered to General Grant at Appomattox Court House in Virginia. Slavery was abolished throughout the United States by constitutional amendment later that year. Just days after Lee's surrender, President Lincoln was assassinated in Washington, and the nation mourned him. Life in Gettysburg continued. Tillie Pierce grew up and married a lawyer named Horace Alleman. They settled in Selinsgrove, in central Pennsylvania. In 1889, more than twenty-five years after the battle, she published her memoir. She lived until 1914. Her book was written long after the events it describes. Scholars therefore read it alongside letters and diaries from 1863. Yet her account remains one of the most valuable local perspectives on the battle. It shows the experience of a young girl who watched history unfold from a kitchen, a farmyard and a front step.

HEAD: Elizabeth Thorn cared for Evergreen Cemetery for many years, and she is buried on the hill she looked after.
BODY: Peter Thorn returned safely from the war. Together, he and Elizabeth continued to care for Evergreen Cemetery for many years. Elizabeth lived until 1907 and was buried in the cemetery she had tended. In 2002, a memorial to the local women of Gettysburg was unveiled near the gatehouse. Its statue was modelled on Elizabeth Thorn, shown pregnant and weary, with a shovel at her side. Other townsfolk also lived on. John Burns became a celebrated figure, visited by admirers from far away. Professor Michael Jacobs published one of the earliest histories of the battle, using his weather records. Sallie Myers later wrote about her experiences. George Shriver, Hettie's husband, died as a prisoner of war and never returned home. Hettie raised her daughters alone. Many farms and buildings associated with the battle still stand. Their survival allows visitors today to connect individual stories with the places where they happened.

HEAD: Fifty years after the battle, veterans from both sides shook hands across the same stone wall.
BODY: In the summer of 1913, a great reunion was held at Gettysburg to mark the fiftieth anniversary of the battle. More than fifty thousand veterans attended from both the North and the South. Most were in their seventies, white-haired and frail. Some walked with canes, and some came with grandchildren. They stayed in a vast tent camp built on the old battlefield. They ate together in large dining tents and shared stories. On the anniversary of the third day, Confederate veterans who had taken part in Pickett's Charge walked slowly across the fields once more. Union veterans waited for them at the stone wall on Cemetery Ridge. Instead of fighting, they shook hands across the wall. Photographs and newspaper reports captured the moment. Many participants described it as deeply moving. Scholars note that such reunions also tended to leave out the experiences of Black Americans. Even so, the scene became a powerful symbol of reconciliation.

IMG: gettysburg-battlefield-today-monuments | a wide peaceful view over green fields, wooden fences and stone walls with granite monuments and statues standing among them, an old stone barn and farmhouse, wooded hills in the distance, soft golden evening light
HEAD: There are more than thirteen hundred monuments on the battlefield at Gettysburg today.
BODY: Today, much of the battlefield is protected as Gettysburg National Military Park. The fields, ridges and woods look much as they did in 1863. Farms associated with the battle still stand, including the Trostle barn, the Weikert farmhouse and the Leister house. Stone walls still run along Cemetery Ridge. Fences have been rebuilt in historical styles. In summer, wheat still grows between the ridges. Across the park stand more than thirteen hundred monuments, markers and memorials. There are statues of generals on horseback, granite soldiers and simple stone markers. Many regiments placed their monuments where they fought. The park also works to restore orchards and woodlots to their 1863 appearance. On Little Round Top, boulders still lie among the trees. A modest monument marks the position of the 20th Maine. Each year, around a million people visit the park. Many sit on the rocks at sunset and look west toward the South Mountains.

IMG: national-cemetery-curved-rows-trees-evening | a peaceful cemetery on a green hillside with small plain gravestones in long curved rows, tall old trees, a tall white column with a statue on top in the middle, soft golden evening light, quiet and calm
HEAD: Each year on the nineteenth of November, people gather in Gettysburg to remember Lincoln's address.
BODY: Gettysburg remains a small town, with roughly seven thousand townsfolk. Many historic buildings survive. The Wills house on the Diamond is now a museum, where visitors can see the room where Lincoln stayed. The Shriver house on Baltimore Street has been restored to its 1863 appearance. The small house where Ginnie Wade was killed still stands. The gatehouse of Evergreen Cemetery remains on the hill. The Soldiers' National Cemetery is a quiet, peaceful place. Graves still lie in the curved rows designed by William Saunders, beneath tall old trees. The small stones are plain and equal. At the centre stands the Soldiers' National Monument, a tall column topped by a figure representing Liberty. Near the entrance, a bronze tablet carries the words of the Gettysburg Address. Each year on the nineteenth of November, a ceremony known as Dedication Day is held there. The address is read aloud, and new citizens are at times sworn in.

HEAD: On summer nights, fireflies still rise over the fields at Gettysburg, as they did in 1863.
BODY: In the evening, when most visitors have left, the battlefield grows quiet. Golden light spreads across the fields and ridges before fading slowly to grey. White-tailed deer emerge from the woods to graze in the meadows. They move carefully between the monuments, lifting their heads to listen. Swallows swoop low over the wheat. Lights appear in farmhouse windows along the ridges. On summer nights, fireflies rise from the long grass in their thousands. They drift above the fields, blinking softly, as they did in July 1863. Civilian memoirs from that summer mention fireflies on the evenings before the battle. The insects were part of normal summer life in rural Pennsylvania. Today, they offer a quiet link between past and present. The park's careful preservation of the landscape means that many evening scenes resemble those of 1863. Only the sounds have changed, replaced now by crickets, wind and the occasional distant car.
