In 1789, France is ruled by a king. His name is Louis the Sixteenth. He is thirty-four years old, a large, shy and kind man. He loves hunting and working with locks and clocks in his own little workshop. He became king when he was only twenty. He is not a bad man. But he finds it very hard to make difficult decisions.

His wife is the queen, Marie Antoinette. She came from Austria when she was only fourteen, to marry Louis. She is graceful and lively and loves beautiful things. The king and the queen do not live in Paris. They live in a huge palace at Versailles, about twenty kilometres to the west. Versailles is one of the grandest palaces in the world. French society is divided into three groups, called the three estates. The first estate is the clergy, the priests and bishops of the Church. The second estate is the nobility, the lords and ladies with old family names. And the third estate is everyone else. The farmers and the craftsmen, the shopkeepers and the lawyers, the workers and the merchants. The third estate is almost everyone in France. But it has the least say in how the country is run.

[long-pause]

The nobles and the clergy pay very few taxes. The third estate pays most of them. Farmers must also pay taxes to their lord and to the Church. Many people feel this is not fair. And in recent years, life has become harder. France has spent a lot of money on wars, including helping the Americans in their war for independence. Now the king has very little money left, and large debts to pay.

To make things worse, the harvest of 1788 was very bad. There was a terrible hailstorm in the summer, which flattened the wheat in many places. Then came one of the coldest winters anyone could remember. The river Seine froze over. The price of grain went up and up. And the price of bread, the most important food for ordinary people, went up too.

[long-pause]

So the king decided to call a great meeting. It is called the Estates-General. Representatives of all three estates would come together at Versailles, to talk about the problems of France. The last time such a meeting had been held was in 1614, long ago. All over France, people chose their representatives. And they wrote down their complaints and hopes in little books, to send to the king.

In village churches and town halls, people gathered to write these books. They wrote about the high taxes and the unfair rules. They wrote about the hunting rights of the lords, whose animals ate the farmers' crops. They wrote about the price of bread. And many of them wrote, at the end, words of hope and trust in the king. They believed that if the king only knew how they suffered, he would make things right.

In the countryside, most people are farmers. They live in small villages of stone houses, around a church and a little square. They grow wheat and rye, vines and vegetables. They keep a cow or a few goats, and some chickens. Life is hard, and money is short. Many people in France in 1789 also read the books of new thinkers, called the philosophers of the Enlightenment. These writers said that people should use their reason, and question old ideas. They wrote about freedom and fairness, and about the rights that all people share. Their books were read in cafés and salons all over Paris. And their ideas slowly spread, even to the workshops of the faubourg.

[pause]

At Versailles, life goes on as it has for a hundred years. There are grand dinners and balls, hunts and card games. Courtiers in silk and powdered wigs walk in the gardens among the fountains. The queen plays with her children in her little village. The king goes hunting in the forests. And far away, in Paris and the countryside, people wonder if the king knows how they live.

Let us walk through the streets of Paris in the summer of 1789.

The streets of Paris are narrow and crowded and muddy. Most of them have no pavements. A gutter of dirty water runs down the middle. The houses are tall, five or six storeys high, built of pale stone or plaster. The rich live on the lower floors, in big rooms with high ceilings. The higher you go, the poorer the people. Around the old city, there are the faubourgs, the neighbourhoods outside the old walls. They are newer and poorer and very busy. Around the whole city there is a new wall, built only a few years ago. It is not a wall for defence. It is a wall for taxes. At each gate, there are tax offices. Everything that comes into Paris must pay a tax: wine and meat, wood and flour. The people of Paris hate this wall. It makes everything more expensive. But for all its mud and crowds, Paris is a beautiful and exciting city. There are wonderful gardens, like the Tuileries beside the Louvre, where people walk on summer evenings. And in the summer of 1789, those ideas are about to change everything.

At night, Paris is darker than it is today. There are oil lamps hanging on ropes across some of the streets. They give a soft yellow light, but they leave many shadows. On Sundays, Paris is quieter. The shops are closed, and the church bells ring for mass. Families put on their best clothes and walk to church together. Let us meet a family who lives in the Faubourg Saint-Antoine, in the east of Paris.

The family is not rich, but they are not the poorest either. Antoine is a skilled craftsman with his own workshop. Marguerite earns a little extra with her sewing. On Sundays, the family goes to mass at the church of Sainte-Marguerite, in the faubourg. After mass, they stand outside in the square and talk with their neighbours. It is the one day when Antoine does not work, and he enjoys it very much.

The family has two rooms. The main room has a fireplace, a wooden table and some chairs that Antoine made himself. There is a big wooden cupboard for clothes and dishes. There is no bathroom either. People wash with a bowl of water in their rooms. On hot summer days, some people bathe in the Seine, in places set aside for swimming. The toilets are little sheds in the courtyard, shared by everyone. At night, people use pots, which are emptied in the morning. Paris in 1789 is not a very clean city. People are used to the smells.

The family's window looks out onto the street. From there, Marguerite can see everything that happens. She sees the neighbours going to market, and the carts rumbling past. The owner of the house, the wine merchant, is not a bad man. He collects the rent every three months, and he grumbles when it is late. The Faubourg Saint-Antoine is one of the busiest neighbourhoods in all of Paris. It is the neighbourhood of the furniture makers.

[pause]

For more than a hundred years, carpenters and cabinet makers have worked here. Long ago, the king gave them special freedoms. Here, they can work without belonging to the strict guilds of the old city. So craftsmen came from all over France, and even from Germany and Holland, to work in the faubourg. Today, there are hundreds of workshops, side by side, along the main street and in the little courtyards behind it.

[long-pause]

All day long, the street is full of the sounds of work. There is the rasp of saws and the knock of hammers. There is the soft shush of planes, shaving thin curls of wood. The smell of fresh oak and walnut, glue and varnish drifts out of every doorway. Apprentices carry planks and finished chairs through the street. Carts bring in wood from the river and take away finished furniture to the rich houses of the city.

Some of the craftsmen are very skilled. They make beautiful furniture for the nobles and the rich merchants. They make tables with patterns of different coloured woods, set into the surface like a picture. They make cupboards with curved legs and brass handles. Some of their furniture even goes to the palace of Versailles. Other craftsmen make simple, strong furniture for ordinary people. Antoine is one of these.

The people of the faubourg are proud and independent. They work hard and they look after each other. When someone is ill, the neighbours bring food. When someone has no work, the others help out. There are many little wine shops, where the workers meet in the evenings. They drink a glass of wine, play cards and talk. They talk about work and wages, about the price of bread, and more and more, about politics.

In the spring of 1789, there was trouble in the faubourg. A rich wallpaper maker called Réveillon was said to have spoken about cutting the workers' wages. He may not have meant it that way. But the rumour spread quickly, in a time when bread was so expensive. There were angry crowds in the streets for two days. It was a sad and frightening time for the neighbourhood. Antoine kept his family indoors. Since then, everyone has been a little more watchful.

At the end of the main street stands the Bastille. It is an old castle, built four hundred years ago to guard the eastern gate of Paris. Now it is a prison. It has eight tall round towers, joined by walls thirty metres high. There is a moat around it, and a drawbridge. Very few prisoners are kept there now, only a handful. But for the people of Paris, the Bastille is a symbol. It stands for the power of the king to put people in prison without a trial.

The children of the faubourg grow up in the shadow of the Bastille. They play in the streets around it. They look up at its dark towers and wonder who is inside. There are old stories about a mysterious prisoner in an iron mask, long ago. Paul and his friends dare each other to run up to the moat and touch the old stones. But the guards shout at them, and they run away, laughing. For them, the Bastille is just part of the neighbourhood. They do not know that very soon, it will be famous all over the world.

In the courtyards behind the main street, there are wood stores and drying sheds. Planks of oak and walnut are stacked in high piles, with little gaps between them for the air. They must dry for several years before they can be used. Antoine knows each kind of wood by its smell and its colour. He can tell, just by tapping a plank, whether it is dry enough to use.

[pause]

On Saturday evenings, the faubourg comes alive. The workers get their week's wages and go to the wine shops. There is music and dancing in some of the courtyards. Street sellers sell hot sausages and fried potatoes. The children are allowed to stay up a little later. And the whole neighbourhood seems to breathe out, after a long week of work.

Let us spend an ordinary day with the family, in the middle of June.

[pause]

Marguerite wakes first, as the light comes in through the shutters. She gets up quietly and lights a small fire in the fireplace. She puts a pot of water on to heat. Then she goes down the creaking stairs to the courtyard, to fetch water from the well. The water carrier's wife is there too, already filling her buckets. The two women talk quietly for a moment about the price of bread.

Breakfast is simple. There is bread, dipped in a little warm milk or soup. Sometimes there is coffee, which has become popular in Paris. It is very weak and mixed with a lot of milk. Antoine eats quickly and goes down to his workshop. He has an order for a big oak table, for a shopkeeper in the city. He wants to finish it this week.

Upstairs, Marguerite sits down at the window with her sewing. She has a pile of linen shirts to finish for the shop. Louise sits beside her and sews the buttons on. Mémé knits in her chair. They talk while they work, about the neighbours and the news, about the weather and the price of things. Outside, the street is busy and noisy. Peddlers call out as they pass. Old clothes! Paul runs errands all morning. He takes a finished chair to a customer in the next street. He carries a message to the wood seller by the river. He buys onions and salt for his mother from the little shop on the corner. Everywhere he goes, he hears people talking about Versailles. The meeting of the Estates-General is going on there. People are saying that the third estate is standing up to the king. Paul does not understand it all. But he can tell that something important is happening.

At midday, the family eats together. There is soup of vegetables and bread, with a little piece of cheese. Then everyone goes back to work. In the afternoon, the heat of summer fills the narrow street. The workshops keep their doors wide open. Dogs lie panting in the shade. The water carrier does good business, going up and down the stairs with his buckets.

In the evening, when the light begins to fade, Antoine closes the doors of the workshop. He washes the sawdust from his hands and arms at the well. The family eats supper together by candlelight. Then there is a knock at the door, and Étienne comes in, with a newspaper in his hand and ink on his fingers. Everyone sits down and listens, as he reads the latest news from Versailles. Mémé shakes her head and says it will all come to nothing. Antoine says nothing, but he listens very carefully. And Louise listens most carefully of all.

In the afternoon, Mémé takes a short nap in her chair. Louise puts down her sewing and reads a little, from an old almanac Étienne gave her. An almanac is a little book with the calendar of the year, and the phases of the moon. It has advice for farmers, and little stories and jokes. Louise has read it so many times that she knows most of it by heart.

Before bed, the family says a short prayer together. Then Marguerite tucks the children in and blows out the candle. Through the open window, they can hear the sounds of the street. A cart rolls past, its wheels clattering. Someone laughs in the wine shop. A mother calls a child. And slowly, the sounds grow fewer, and the children fall asleep.

In Paris in 1789, the most important thing in life is bread.

[pause]

Ordinary people in Paris eat bread with every meal. A worker might eat a kilo and a half of bread every day. Bread is dipped in soup, eaten with cheese or onions, or simply eaten on its own. It is the main food of the poor. When bread is cheap, people are content. When bread is expensive, people are worried and hungry.

[long-pause]

In the summer of 1789, bread is very expensive. Because of the bad harvest last year, there is not enough grain. A four-pound loaf of bread now costs more than half of what a worker earns in a day. For a family with children, that is a lot. Many families have to spend almost all their money on bread. There is little left for anything else.

[pause]

{img:french-baker-1789-oven-long-loaves-paddle-flour-early-morning}
Every morning, long before dawn, people line up outside the bakeries. They want to be sure of getting their bread before it runs out. Women stand in the queues with their baskets, wrapped in shawls against the morning chill. They talk and complain and worry together. Sometimes there is not enough bread for everyone. Then the last people in the queue go home with empty baskets.

[long-pause]

In the family, it is Marguerite who goes for the bread, usually with Louise. They get up very early, while it is still dark. They walk to the baker's shop at the corner and wait in line. The baker is a big man with floury arms, called Monsieur Lebrun. He is a good baker, and an honest one. He does his best to make enough bread for everyone. But his flour is expensive too, and sometimes he cannot get enough.

[pause]

{img:mother-daughter-waiting-bread-line-dawn-street-shawls-baskets}
While they wait, Louise listens to the women talking. Some of them say that the bakers are hiding flour, to keep the prices high. Some of them say that rich people are buying all the grain. Some of them say that it is the fault of the king and queen. Marguerite does not join in. She just holds Louise's hand and waits patiently. When they finally reach the counter, they buy a big round loaf. Monsieur Lebrun gives Louise a little roll as well, for free. She thanks him with a big smile.

[long-pause]

At home, Marguerite cuts the loaf carefully, so that it will last the whole day. She saves the crusts to put in the soup. Nothing is wasted. Mémé says that when she was a girl, bread was cheaper. But she also remembers years when it was even worse. Bread is like the weather, she says. Sometimes good, sometimes bad. You just have to wait for better days.

[pause]

{img:family-sharing-bread-loaf-table-mother-cutting-children-watching}
But many people in Paris are tired of waiting. They are hungry, and they are worried. They hear that things are changing at Versailles. They hope that the meeting will make bread cheaper and life fairer. All through June and July, the price of bread goes on rising. And it will reach its highest point on the fourteenth of July. Nobody knows that yet. But everyone can feel that something is about to happen.

[pause]

The bakers of Paris work very hard. They start in the middle of the night, kneading the dough and heating the ovens. They often sleep only a few hours. Their shops are hot and full of flour dust. Monsieur Lebrun has two apprentices who help him. In the summer, they work in their shirtsleeves, with sweat running down their faces. And still, there is never quite enough bread.

[pause]

Sometimes, when there is not enough bread, Marguerite makes a thick soup of vegetables and potatoes instead. She adds a little bacon if she has it, and herbs from the market. The children do not mind. They like their mother's soup. Mémé says that a good soup is worth more than a fine dinner. And she always asks for a second bowl.

[long-pause]

{img:les-halles-market-1789-paris-stalls-vegetables-crowds-morning}
In the middle of Paris, near the old church of Saint-Eustache, there is the great market of Les Halles. It is the biggest market in Paris. And it is the place where the whole city comes to buy its food.

[pause]

Every night, long before dawn, farmers come into Paris from the countryside. They come with carts full of vegetables and fruit, butter and eggs. They come through the city gates, pay the tax, and drive to Les Halles. There, they set up their stalls under the old wooden roofs, or in the open air. By sunrise, the market is full and busy.

[long-pause]

There are vegetables of every kind. There are cabbages and carrots, leeks and onions, turnips and lettuces. There are beans and peas in summer, and pumpkins in autumn. There are strawberries and cherries, apples and pears, in their seasons. There are big baskets of eggs and great blocks of butter, wrapped in leaves. There are cheeses from all over France, soft and hard, white and yellow.

[pause]

{img:market-women-1789-les-halles-fish-stall-baskets-shouting-cheerful}
There is a fish market too, where the fish arrive from the coast in carts, packed in seaweed and straw. And there are the famous market women, called the dames de la Halle. They are strong, loud and clever. They sell fish and vegetables and fruit, and they know everyone. They are famous for their sharp tongues and their quick jokes. Even the king and queen respect them. On special occasions, they send flowers to the queen and receive gifts in return.

[long-pause]

Once a week, Marguerite goes to Les Halles to buy food for the week. It is a long walk from the faubourg, about half an hour. She takes Louise with her, and a big basket. They walk through the narrow streets, along the river and past the town hall. When they arrive, the market is crowded and noisy. Louise holds her mother's hand tightly, so she will not get lost.

[pause]

{img:mother-daughter-buying-vegetables-market-stall-1789-basket-bargaining}
Marguerite buys cabbages and carrots, onions and a few potatoes. Potatoes are quite new in France. Not long ago, many people thought they were only for animals. But a clever scientist called Parmentier has been telling people that they are good to eat. Now more and more people are buying them. They are cheaper than bread, and very filling. Marguerite boils them in the soup.

[long-pause]

She buys a little cheese and a few eggs too, and a bunch of herbs for the soup. She bargains with the market women, politely but firmly. They laugh and argue, and in the end they agree on a fair price. One of the market women, a big woman with red cheeks, gives Louise a cherry to taste. It is sweet and juicy. Louise eats it slowly, and saves the stone in her pocket.

[pause]

{img:saint-eustache-church-les-halles-evening-market-closing-1789}
On the way home, they stop at the church of Saint-Eustache. It is a huge church, tall and light, right next to the market. Marguerite lights a small candle and says a prayer. She prays for her family, and for good harvests, and for cheaper bread. Louise looks up at the tall windows and the painted ceiling. Then they walk home slowly, with the heavy basket between them, as the market begins to close for the day.

[pause]

Around Les Halles, there are little eating houses where the market workers eat. They open very early in the morning, while it is still dark. They serve hot soup and bread, and a glass of wine. The porters and the farmers come in, cold and tired, and warm themselves. They talk about the weather and the prices. Then they go back out into the busy market.

[pause]

The porters of Les Halles are famous for their strength. They carry enormous loads on their backs and heads: sacks of flour, baskets of fish, sides of meat. They wear big hats with wide brims, to protect their heads. Paul thinks they are the strongest men in the world. He tries to carry a sack of potatoes the way they do, and falls over. Everybody laughs, including Paul.

[pause]

By midday, the market begins to empty. The farmers sell the last of their vegetables cheaply. Poor women come to collect the leaves and scraps that are left behind. The stall holders pack up their baskets and their carts. Then they drive slowly out of the city, back to their villages, to start again the next night.

[long-pause]

{img:seine-river-1789-washerwomen-boats-quays-notre-dame-summer-morning}
The river Seine is the heart of Paris. It brings the city its food and its wood, its wine and its water. And in summer, it is one of the liveliest places in the city.

[pause]

All along the banks of the river, there are quays, stone walls where boats can tie up. Barges come down the river from the countryside, loaded with grain and wine and firewood. Others come up from the sea, with goods from all over the world. Porters carry the cargo up the steps onto the quays. There are piles of wood and barrels and sacks everywhere.

[long-pause]

On the river itself, there are big floating wooden boats, used as laundries. They are called bateaux-lavoirs. Inside, there are long rows of washerwomen, kneeling at the edge of the water. They scrub clothes on wooden boards with soap and brushes. They beat them with wooden paddles to get the dirt out. Then they rinse them in the river. All day long, you can hear the slap, slap of their paddles on the wet cloth.

[pause]

{img:floating-laundry-boat-washerwomen-scrubbing-paddles-seine-sunlight}
The washerwomen are strong and cheerful, and they sing while they work. Some of them work for rich families, washing their fine linen. Others take in washing from anyone who can pay. Marguerite sometimes brings her washing here, when she has too much to do at home. She knows several of the washerwomen. They laugh and gossip with her while they work.

[long-pause]

Along the river, there are also many people who make their living from the water. There are fishermen, who fish for carp and pike with long rods and nets. There are boatmen, who ferry people across the river for a small coin. There are men who collect wood floating down the river, and sell it for firewood. And there are the water carriers, who fill their buckets at the river and carry them all over the city.

[pause]

{img:bouquinistes-1789-booksellers-stalls-quay-seine-readers-browsing}
On some of the quays, there are booksellers with little stalls and boxes of books. They sell old books and new books, almanacs and songs, maps and prints. People stop and browse, turning the pages. Louise loves these stalls. When she goes to the river with her mother, she always stops to look at the books. Sometimes the old bookseller lets her read a few pages, standing at the stall. He knows she cannot afford to buy anything. But he likes to see a child who loves books.

[long-pause]

In the hot summer evenings, the river is cool and pleasant. People come down to the banks to walk and to sit in the shade of the trees. Some people swim in the river, in the places where swimming is allowed. Young men dive from the boats and splash and shout. Children paddle in the shallow water at the edges. Paul loves to go swimming with his friends. His mother worries, but his father says the boy needs to learn.

[pause]

{img:summer-evening-seine-people-walking-quays-swimmers-sunset-1789}
On Sunday evenings, the whole family sometimes walks down to the river together. They walk along the quays and look at the boats. They cross the Pont Neuf and watch the street singers and the jugglers. They buy a little paper cone of roasted nuts and share it. The sun sets behind the towers of Notre-Dame, and the river turns gold. And for a little while, they forget about the price of bread and the news from Versailles.

[pause]

Big rafts of logs come floating down the Seine from the forests far upstream. They are tied together with ropes, and men stand on them, steering with long poles. The wood is for the fires of Paris, for cooking and for heating. When the rafts arrive, the logs are pulled out of the water and stacked on the quays to dry. Paul loves to watch the raftsmen jumping from log to log.

[pause]

In the evening, the fishermen sit on the quays with their rods, hoping for a carp for supper. Old men sit beside them, watching and giving advice. Lovers walk slowly along the river, arm in arm. The lamplighters come along the quays, lighting the oil lamps. And the river flows on, dark and quiet, reflecting the little lights.

[long-pause]

{img:estates-general-opening-versailles-may-1789-great-hall-deputies-king}
While the family goes about its daily life in Paris, important things are happening at Versailles.

[pause]

The meeting of the Estates-General opened on the fifth of May, 1789. There was a grand ceremony. The deputies of the three estates walked in a great procession through the streets of Versailles. The clergy wore their church robes. The nobles wore silk and lace and feathered hats. And the deputies of the third estate wore plain black clothes, as they had been told to. Crowds of people watched them pass and cheered for the third estate.

[long-pause]

But very soon, there was a problem. How would the voting work? In the old way, each estate would vote separately, as one group. That would mean the clergy and the nobles together would always outvote the third estate, two against one. But the third estate had as many deputies as the other two together. So they wanted everyone to vote together, one person one vote. The arguments went on for weeks.

[pause]

{img:deputies-third-estate-black-coats-arguing-meeting-hall-versailles}
In the middle of June, the deputies of the third estate made a bold decision. They declared that they were the National Assembly. They said they represented the whole people of France. Some priests and a few nobles came to join them. It was a very big step. Nothing like it had ever happened in France before.

[long-pause]

On the twentieth of June, something dramatic happened. The deputies arrived at their meeting hall, and found the doors locked. Soldiers were guarding them. The king had ordered the hall to be closed, perhaps for a royal ceremony. It was raining. The deputies did not know where to go. Then someone suggested a large indoor tennis court nearby. So they all went there.

[pause]

{img:tennis-court-oath-deputies-raising-hands-indoor-court-rain-june-1789}
In the tennis court, the deputies stood together and made a solemn promise. They swore that they would not separate until they had written a constitution for France. A constitution is a set of rules that says how a country should be governed. They raised their hands and swore the oath, all together. One deputy was too ill to stand. He stayed seated, and everyone respected that. This moment became known as the Tennis Court Oath.

[long-pause]

A few days later, the king ordered all three estates to meet together. The National Assembly had won the first round. In Paris, people celebrated. They lit candles in their windows. They cheered in the streets and the cafés. Étienne came running home with the news and told everyone in the house. Antoine smiled and shook his hand. Mémé said that she would believe it when bread was cheaper.

[pause]

{img:paris-houses-candles-in-windows-celebrating-night-june-1789}
But the king was also moving soldiers. Thousands of soldiers began to arrive around Paris and Versailles. Many of them were foreign soldiers, from Switzerland and Germany, who served the king of France. They camped in the fields outside the city. People in Paris began to worry. Why were so many soldiers coming? Did the king mean to close the National Assembly? Nobody knew. And as July began, the worry grew, day by day.

[pause]

At Versailles, crowds of ordinary people come every day to watch the meetings of the deputies. They sit in the galleries and listen to the speeches. They cheer when they like what they hear, and boo when they do not. Some of them walk all the way from Paris, twenty kilometres, just to watch. Then they walk home again in the evening and tell everyone what they have heard.

[pause]

The deputies of the third estate come from all over France. Many of them are lawyers, and some are merchants or doctors. They live in rented rooms in the town of Versailles. In the evenings, they meet in little clubs to talk and plan. Some of them have never been to Paris or Versailles before. They write long letters home, to their wives and friends, about everything they see.

[long-pause]

{img:palais-royal-gardens-1789-arcades-cafes-crowds-trees-summer-evening}
In the middle of Paris, near the Louvre, there is a famous place called the Palais-Royal. In the summer of 1789, it is the most exciting place in the whole city.

[pause]

The Palais-Royal is a palace with a big garden in the middle. Around the garden, there are long buildings with covered walkways, called arcades. Under the arcades, there are shops and cafés, restaurants and bookshops, theatres and gaming rooms. The palace belongs to the Duke of Orléans, a cousin of the king. And the duke does not allow the king's police inside. So people feel free to say whatever they like there.

[long-pause]

All day and all night, the Palais-Royal is crowded with people. There are rich young men in fine clothes, and workers in their aprons. There are students and lawyers, soldiers and shopkeepers. There are women selling flowers, and boys selling newspapers. People walk up and down under the trees, talking and arguing and laughing. They gather in groups around anyone who is making a speech.

[pause]

{img:cafe-de-foy-1789-interior-people-reading-newspapers-coffee-arguing}
The cafés are the centre of everything. The most famous is the Café de Foy. Inside, there are tables and mirrors and chandeliers. People sit there for hours, drinking coffee and lemonade, reading newspapers and arguing about politics. Sometimes someone climbs onto a table and makes a speech. Everyone listens, and then everyone argues.

[long-pause]

In the summer of 1789, there are new newspapers and pamphlets every day. A pamphlet is a little booklet, a few pages long, printed quickly and sold cheaply. Before, the king's officials checked everything before it was printed. Now, there are so many pamphlets that nobody can check them all. They are full of new ideas: about freedom, about equality, about the rights of the people. People buy them and read them, and pass them from hand to hand.

[pause]

{img:pamphlet-sellers-palais-royal-arcade-crowd-reading-1789-afternoon}
One Sunday afternoon, Étienne takes Louise and Paul to the Palais-Royal. Their mother says yes, as long as they hold hands and stay close. The children have never seen anything like it. There are so many people, so much noise and colour. They see a man selling ices, and a juggler, and a woman with a parrot on her shoulder. They see crowds gathered around a young man on a chair, shouting about freedom.

[long-pause]

Étienne buys them each a little glass of lemonade at a café. It is cold and sweet, and they drink it slowly, sitting at a little table under the arcade. Louise watches the people walking by. She sees a little bookshop across the way, with books in the window. She wishes she could go in. Étienne sees her looking. Perhaps one day, he says, when I am a master printer, I will print a book just for you.

[pause]

{img:children-lemonade-arcade-cafe-table-palais-royal-young-man-smiling}
On the way home, they walk through the Tuileries gardens, beside the Louvre. The garden is calm and green, with long avenues of trees and big round ponds. Families are walking in the evening sun. Children are sailing little boats on the ponds. It seems so peaceful. But Étienne says that something is going to happen soon. Everyone in the Palais-Royal says so. Louise does not know what he means. But she holds Paul's hand a little tighter all the way home.

[pause]

The Palais-Royal is also famous for its little cannon. It stands in the middle of the garden, and it is fired every day at noon. But it is not fired by a person. A glass lens focuses the sun's rays onto the little cannon, and the heat lights the gunpowder. At exactly midday, if the sun is shining, there is a small bang. People set their watches by it.

[pause]

Many writers and thinkers come to the Palais-Royal to talk and to listen. Some of them will become famous in the years to come. Others will be forgotten. But in the summer of 1789, they are all young and full of ideas. They sit in the cafés late into the night, talking about a new France, where everyone would be free and equal.

[long-pause]

{img:paris-printing-shop-1789-wooden-press-workers-pulling-lever-paper}
Let us visit the printing shop where Étienne works, near the river on the left bank.

[pause]

It is a long room with a low ceiling, full of noise and the smell of ink. In the middle stand two big wooden printing presses. They look a little like great wooden frames, with a heavy screw in the middle and a long handle. Two men work at each press. One spreads black ink on the metal letters with leather balls. The other lays a sheet of damp paper on top and pulls the long handle. The press comes down with a thump, and when it goes up again, the page is printed.

[long-pause]

Étienne works at a sloping wooden table near the window, where the light is good. In front of him, there is a wooden case divided into many little boxes. Each box holds tiny metal letters, all the same letter. There are boxes for a and b and c, for capitals and small letters, for commas and full stops. Étienne picks out the letters one by one with his fingers, very quickly. He puts them into a little metal holder, to make words and lines. He has to do it backwards, because the print comes out the other way round.

[pause]

{img:young-typesetter-picking-metal-letters-type-case-window-light}
It takes many hours to set the letters for a page. A good typesetter can set about a thousand letters an hour. Étienne is getting faster every month. When the page is printed, all the letters must be cleaned and put back in their boxes again, ready for the next page. It is careful, patient work, and his fingers are always black with ink.

[long-pause]

In the summer of 1789, the printing shops of Paris are busier than ever. There are new newspapers starting every week. There are pamphlets and posters and songs. The presses work from morning until late at night. The printers are tired, but excited. They feel that they are part of something important. The words they print are read by thousands of people, all over the city.

[pause]

{img:apprentice-reading-newspaper-aloud-courtyard-neighbours-listening-evening}
Many people in Paris cannot read, or can read only a little. So someone often reads the newspaper aloud, for everyone to hear. In the wine shops, in the courtyards, at the street corners, people gather around a reader. In the family's house, the reader is Étienne. Every evening, he comes home with the latest news. And the neighbours come out to listen.

[long-pause]

He stands on the step of the courtyard, with a candle beside him. He reads slowly and clearly, so that everyone can follow. He reads about the National Assembly, and the speeches of the deputies. He reads about the soldiers gathering around Paris. He reads about new ideas, that all people are born free and equal. The neighbours listen in silence. Then they all start talking at once. Some are hopeful. Some are worried. Some say it is all nonsense.

[pause]

{img:girl-learning-to-set-type-letters-printer-apprentice-teaching-evening}
Louise always sits at the front, listening very closely. One evening, Étienne brings home a few old metal letters from the printing shop. He shows Louise how they work. He sets her name in letters, L, O, U, I, S, E, backwards in a little holder. Then he presses it on a piece of paper with some ink. And there it is: her name, printed, like in a real book. Louise looks at it for a long time. Then she folds the paper carefully and keeps it in her pocket.

[pause]

The master printer is an old man called Monsieur Didot. He has been printing books for forty years. He is strict about quality. Every page must be clean and straight, with no mistakes. He checks every sheet himself before it goes to the binder. Étienne is a little afraid of him. But he also admires him very much, and wants to be as good a printer one day.

[pause]

At the end of a long day, the printers wash their hands with soap and sand, to get the ink off. But the ink never comes off completely. Étienne's fingers are always a little grey. Louise teases him about it. He says that ink is the mark of an honest printer. And he says that one day, all of France will have ink on its fingers, because everyone will be reading.

[pause]

In the summer evenings, when the shop is closed, Étienne sometimes sits on the quay by the river with the other apprentices. They eat bread and cheese and talk about their dreams. One wants to have his own newspaper. Another wants to print beautiful books with pictures. Étienne just wants to be a good printer, and to marry a kind girl, and to live in a little house near the river.

[long-pause]

{img:paris-july-1789-hot-summer-street-crowds-talking-uneasy-calm}
At the beginning of July, the weather in Paris is hot and heavy. And the city is full of rumours.

[pause]

Every day, more soldiers arrive in the camps around Paris. People can see their tents in the fields outside the city. Some say there are twenty thousand of them, some say thirty thousand. Why are they there? The king says it is to keep order. But many people in Paris think the king wants to close the National Assembly. They are afraid that the hopes of the summer will come to nothing.

[long-pause]

The price of bread goes on rising. Now it is the highest it has been for many years. In the bread queues, the women are angry and worried. In the wine shops, the men talk late into the night. In the Palais-Royal, the crowds grow bigger every day. Even some of the king's own soldiers, the French Guards who live in Paris, say they are on the side of the people. They come to the Palais-Royal and are cheered by the crowds.

[pause]

{img:french-guards-soldiers-cheered-crowd-palais-royal-july-1789}
In the faubourg, life goes on, but everyone is on edge. Antoine works in his workshop, but he cannot keep his mind on his work. He goes to the door often to look down the street. Marguerite sews by the window, but she watches the street more than her needle. Mémé says her prayers more often than usual. And the children feel that something is not quite right, though nobody tells them what.

[long-pause]

On the hot evenings, the neighbours sit in the courtyard and talk in low voices. The water carrier says he has heard that the soldiers will march into the city. The lace maker says she has heard that the queen wants to punish Paris. Étienne says that the National Assembly will stand firm, whatever happens. Antoine listens to them all. Then he says that the best thing is to stay calm and look after each other. Everyone agrees with that.

[pause]

{img:neighbours-courtyard-night-candle-low-voices-july-heat-worried}
Louise cannot sleep in the heat. She lies on her bed by the open window and listens to the city. She can hear people talking in the street below, even late at night. She can hear dogs barking and carts rumbling. She can hear someone singing in a wine shop. She thinks about the soldiers in their tents, and the king in his palace, and the deputies in their meeting hall. She wonders what will happen. And at last, she falls asleep, as the sky begins to grow light.

[long-pause]

On Saturday the eleventh of July, the king does something that will change everything. He dismisses his chief minister, a banker from Geneva called Necker. Necker is very popular in Paris. People think he is on their side, and that he understands money and bread. Now the king has sent him away, quietly, out of France.

[pause]

{img:minister-necker-leaving-quietly-carriage-evening-road-july-1789}
The news does not reach Paris until the next day, Sunday. It is a hot and sunny morning. People are dressed in their Sunday clothes, walking in the gardens and along the river. And then, around midday, the news begins to spread. Necker has been dismissed. People stop and look at each other. What does it mean? Everyone in Paris is asking the same question. And the answer they give each other is not a happy one.

[pause]

In the heat, the children of the faubourg play in the fountains and the river. The women sit in the shade of the doorways, fanning themselves. The workshops start early in the morning and stop in the middle of the day, when it is too hot to work. Then they start again in the evening, when it is a little cooler. The whole city moves slowly in the summer heat.

[pause]

Through all these worries, ordinary life goes on. Marguerite sews her shirts and takes them to the shop. Antoine finishes his big oak table and delivers it to the customer, who is very pleased. Paul runs his errands. Louise reads her almanac. And every morning, the bread queues form again in the dark, outside Monsieur Lebrun's shop.

[long-pause]

{img:camille-desmoulins-standing-table-palais-royal-crowd-green-leaves-july-12}
On Sunday the twelfth of July, in the afternoon, the gardens of the Palais-Royal are packed with people. Everyone has heard the news about Necker. Everyone is upset and talking at once.

[pause]

A young lawyer and writer called Camille Desmoulins climbs up onto a table outside the Café de Foy. He is twenty-nine years old, thin, with long dark hair. He has a stammer, and usually he finds it hard to speak in public. But today, the words come out clearly. He tells the crowd that the dismissal of Necker is a warning. He tells them that they must be ready to defend themselves and the National Assembly.

[long-pause]

Then he does something that people remember. He pulls a leaf from a chestnut tree in the garden and pins it to his hat. Green is the colour of hope, he says. Let us all wear green. And everyone in the crowd starts pulling leaves from the trees. Within minutes, the trees of the Palais-Royal are bare. Thousands of people are wearing green leaves in their hats and on their coats.

[pause]

{img:crowd-pulling-chestnut-leaves-trees-pinning-hats-palais-royal-sunny}
That evening, the city is restless. Crowds walk through the streets, carrying busts of Necker, covered with black cloth. There is some trouble in the squares, with soldiers and crowds. People are afraid. Many shops close early. In the faubourg, Antoine closes the doors of his workshop and brings the family inside. They sit together in their room, listening to the noise in the streets.

[long-pause]

During the night, some people go to the tax gates in the wall around Paris, and set them on fire. They are tired of paying taxes on everything that comes into the city. The glow of the fires can be seen from many parts of the city. Louise looks out of the window and sees the orange light in the sky, far away. Her mother pulls her back gently and closes the shutters. Nobody in the house sleeps very much that night.

[pause]

{img:paris-night-july-1789-distant-glow-sky-family-window-shutters}
On Monday the thirteenth, the city is full of people looking for ways to defend themselves. In every neighbourhood, people gather and decide to form a citizens' guard, to keep order in the streets. Men come out with whatever they can find. The leaders of the city meet at the town hall. They try to organise things calmly. They want to keep Paris safe, both from the soldiers and from disorder.

[long-pause]

They decide that the citizens' guard should wear a sign, so people know who they are. At first, it is green, like the leaves. But green is also the colour of a brother of the king, who is not popular. So they change it. They choose blue and red, the old colours of the city of Paris. People make little rosettes of ribbon in these colours, called cockades, and pin them to their hats.

[pause]

{img:women-making-blue-red-ribbon-cockades-table-candles-evening}
In the family's house, Marguerite sits at the table and makes cockades for the neighbours. She has some blue and red ribbon in her sewing basket. Her quick fingers fold and stitch the ribbons into neat little rosettes. Louise helps her. The neighbours come in one by one to collect them. Antoine pins one to his hat. Étienne pins one to his coat. Even Paul gets a little one, which he wears very proudly. And outside, the city waits for what the next day will bring.

[pause]

In the house, the neighbours check on each other often during these days. The lace maker comes down to sit with Mémé, so the old woman will not be alone. The water carrier brings extra water for everyone. Antoine and the wine merchant take turns watching the street door at night. Even in worrying times, the people of the house look after each other.

[pause]

Mémé tells the children a story to calm them. She tells them about her village, long ago, and the river where she used to swim. She tells them about the cherry trees in the spring and the dances at the harvest. Her voice is soft and slow. And for a little while, the children forget the noise outside, and listen to the story of a quiet village far away.

[long-pause]

{img:july-14-1789-morning-paris-crowds-streets-cockades-hot-sunny}
Tuesday the fourteenth of July, 1789, begins as a hot summer morning. It will become one of the most famous days in history.

[pause]

Early in the morning, a large crowd goes to a great building on the west side of the city, called the Invalides. It is a home for old soldiers. There are many muskets stored there. The crowd takes the muskets, many thousands of them. But they have very little gunpowder. And people say there is a great store of gunpowder in the Bastille, the old fortress in the east.

[long-pause]

So, in the late morning, people begin to gather around the Bastille. They come from all over the city, and especially from the Faubourg Saint-Antoine. There are craftsmen and shopkeepers, workers and soldiers who have joined the people. The crowd grows bigger and bigger. They want the gunpowder. And they want the governor of the Bastille to remove the guns pointing at the faubourg.

[pause]

{img:crowd-gathering-before-bastille-towers-july-14-distance-hazy}
Inside the Bastille, there are only about a hundred defenders. Most of them are old soldiers, and a few Swiss guards. The governor is a man called de Launay. He talks with messengers from the town hall. The talks go on for hours. Nobody quite knows what to do. And outside, the crowd waits in the hot sun, growing more impatient.

[long-pause]

In the family's house, Antoine has told Marguerite and the children to stay inside. He goes down to the street, with the other men of the house, to see what is happening. Marguerite stands at the window with Mémé, holding Paul's hand. Louise stands beside them. From the window, they can see the crowds in the street, all moving toward the Bastille. They can hear shouting in the distance. And in the early afternoon, they hear the sound of guns.

[pause]

{img:mother-grandmother-children-window-watching-street-anxious-afternoon}
We will not go closer to the Bastille that afternoon. It was a hard and confusing day, and some people did not come home from it. We will stay with the family, at the window, waiting. The hours pass very slowly. Mémé says her prayers quietly, with her rosary beads in her hands. Marguerite does not say anything at all. She just watches the street, and waits for Antoine.

[long-pause]

In the late afternoon, around five o'clock, a great shout goes up from the direction of the Bastille. It spreads through the streets, from person to person, louder and louder. The Bastille has fallen! The Bastille is taken! The drawbridge has been lowered, and the people have gone inside. The old fortress belongs to Paris now.

[pause]

{img:father-returning-home-dusty-hug-family-doorway-evening-july-14}
A little later, Antoine comes home. He is dusty and tired, and his face is very serious. But he is not hurt. Marguerite runs down the stairs and holds him tightly. The children cling to his legs. Mémé cries with relief. Antoine sits down at the table and drinks a cup of water. He does not say much about what he saw. He only says that it was a terrible and a great day. And that he is very glad to be home.

[pause]

That night, the whole city is awake. People light candles in their windows. They gather in the streets and talk and sing. Nobody knows what will happen next. But everyone knows that something has changed forever.

[pause]

Later that evening, the neighbours gather in the courtyard. Nobody wants to be alone. They talk quietly about the day. Some of them were at the Bastille, and some were not. Some of them are happy, and some of them are sad. Étienne says that today, the people of Paris have shown that they are free. Antoine says that he hopes freedom will bring peace too.

[pause]

Many years later, people will argue about what happened at the Bastille that day. They will write books about it and paint pictures of it. Some will call it a great victory, and some will call it a sad day. For the family in the faubourg, it was both. It was a day of fear and a day of hope. And it was a day they would never forget.

[long-pause]

{img:workers-demolishing-bastille-stones-summer-1789-crowds-watching}
In the days after the fourteenth of July, Paris is a different city. People feel as if they are waking up from a long dream.

[pause]

On the fifteenth of July, the king goes to the National Assembly at Versailles. He tells the deputies that he will send the soldiers away from Paris. The deputies cheer. The next day, the king calls Necker back. In Paris, people celebrate in the streets. They ring the church bells and light candles in their windows.

[long-pause]

The people of Paris decide that the Bastille should be pulled down, stone by stone. Already on the fifteenth, workers begin the job. A clever builder called Palloy is put in charge of it. Hundreds of workers climb onto the walls with hammers and picks. They knock down the stones and carry them away. It will take months to pull it all down.

[pause]

{img:souvenir-bastille-stone-carved-small-model-family-table-1789}
Palloy has a clever idea. He takes some of the stones of the Bastille and carves them into little models of the fortress. He sends them to every part of France, as souvenirs. People also make little things from the stones and the iron of the Bastille: medals, paperweights, little boxes. Antoine picks up a small stone from the rubble and brings it home. He puts it on the shelf above the fireplace. One day, he says to the children, you will tell your grandchildren about this.

[long-pause]

On Friday the seventeenth of July, the king comes to Paris. It is a very special day. He comes in his carriage from Versailles, without his guards. All along the way, the people of Paris line the streets. Thousands of members of the new citizens' guard stand in long rows. They carry muskets and pikes and swords. They wear the blue and red cockade on their hats. The king passes slowly between them.

[pause]

{img:king-louis-xvi-carriage-arriving-paris-hotel-de-ville-crowds-july-17}
At the town hall, the new mayor of Paris welcomes the king. He gives him a cockade of blue and red, the colours of Paris. The king pins it to his hat. Then, a little later, white is added to the cockade, the old colour of the kings of France. So now there are three colours: blue, white and red. This is the beginning of the French flag, the tricolour, which is still the flag of France today.

[long-pause]

The king appears at the window of the town hall, wearing the cockade on his hat. The crowd below cheers loudly. Long live the king, they shout, and long live the nation! For a moment, everyone feels that the king and the people are together again. Antoine and Étienne are in the crowd. They come home in the evening, hoarse from shouting, and tell the family all about it.

[pause]

{img:tricolour-cockade-blue-white-red-pinned-hat-close-1789-sunlight}
In the faubourg, life slowly goes back to normal. The workshops open again. The bread queues are still long, and bread is still expensive. But people feel a new kind of hope. They talk about the future in a different way. Children play at storming the Bastille in the streets, with sticks for muskets. And at the end of the street, where the dark towers once stood, there is more and more sky to see, as the fortress comes down stone by stone.

[pause]

Inside the Bastille, the people found only seven prisoners. There were four men who had forged papers, two men who were unwell in their minds, and a nobleman whose family had asked for him to be kept there. They were led out and set free, to the cheers of the crowd. Some of them did not know what was happening. They were simply blinking in the bright July sunlight.

[pause]

In the following weeks, people from all over Paris come to see the ruins of the Bastille. Families bring picnics and watch the workers knocking down the walls. Street sellers sell little drawings of the fortress. Children climb on the piles of stones. It becomes one of the most popular places to visit in the whole city.

[long-pause]

{img:paris-children-1789-playing-street-hoop-ball-faubourg-afternoon}
What is life like for children in Paris in 1789?

[pause]

Children of working families start to help their parents very young. They fetch water and bread, run errands and look after younger brothers and sisters. At ten or twelve, many of them start an apprenticeship, to learn a trade. Boys learn to be carpenters and bakers, printers and shoemakers. Girls learn to sew and to make lace, to spin and to sell in the market.

[long-pause]

Some children go to a little school, run by the parish church. There, they learn to read and write, to count, and to say their prayers. The schools are small and simple, with one teacher and a few benches. Not all children go, and many go only for a few years. Louise went to the parish school for three years. She was one of the best readers in her class.

[pause]

{img:parish-school-1789-children-benches-teacher-slate-reading-lesson}
Paul went to the school too, but he found it hard to sit still. He would rather be running in the streets. Now he helps his father in the workshop in the mornings. He sweeps the shavings and sorts the nails. Antoine is teaching him to use a small plane, to make the wood smooth. Paul is proud when he makes his first smooth board. But he still likes the streets best.

[long-pause]

The children of Paris have many games. They roll hoops along the streets with sticks. They play ball games and tag and hide and seek. They spin tops, made of wood. They play at soldiers, with paper hats and wooden swords. Girls play with dolls made of cloth and wood, and skip with ropes. In the evening, they sit on the doorsteps and tell each other stories.

[pause]

{img:children-watching-puppet-show-street-paris-1789-laughing-crowd}
There are many things to see in the streets of Paris. There are street singers, who sing the latest songs and sell copies of the words. There are puppet shows, with funny little puppets that argue and fight. There are acrobats and jugglers, and men with dancing dogs. Paul loves the puppet shows best. He always stops to watch, even when he is supposed to be running an errand.

[long-pause]

In the summer of 1789, the children of Paris have a new favourite game. They play at storming the Bastille. They build a little fortress from boxes and planks. Some children are the defenders, and some are the attackers. The attackers always win. Then they all cheer together and pin paper cockades to their hats. Their mothers shake their heads. But they smile too.

[pause]

{img:girl-teaching-brother-letters-printed-name-paper-candle-evening}
In the evenings, Louise is teaching Paul to read. She uses the little paper with her name printed on it, and the old pages that Étienne brings home. She points to the letters, one by one. Paul is impatient at first. But Louise is patient, like her father. Slowly, Paul begins to read short words. One evening, he reads a whole line from a newspaper, all by himself. Louise claps her hands. Mémé says she never thought she would see the day. And Paul is so proud that he reads the same line again, five more times.

[pause]

Paul's best friend is a boy called Pierre, the son of the water carrier. They do everything together. They run errands, play in the streets, swim in the river and get into trouble. Pierre is learning to carry water like his father, with a little yoke and two small buckets. Paul sometimes helps him. In return, Pierre helps Paul sweep the workshop. They say they will be friends forever.

[pause]

On feast days, the children get little treats. There are gingerbread figures from the fairs, and sugared almonds and little cakes. Sometimes a puppet show comes to the square, or a man with a magic lantern. A magic lantern is a box with a lamp inside, which shows pictures on a white sheet. The children sit in the dark and watch the pictures in wonder. It is like magic to them.

[long-pause]

{img:national-assembly-august-1789-deputies-night-session-candles-versailles}
The summer goes on, and in Versailles, the National Assembly is busy. In August, the deputies do two very important things.

[pause]

On the night of the fourth of August, there is a long meeting in the Assembly. One after another, nobles and clergy stand up and give up their old privileges. They give up their special rights over the farmers, and their special taxes. They give up their hunting rights and their special courts. It goes on late into the night. The deputies are very moved. Some of them have tears in their eyes. In one night, much of the old order of France is swept away.

[long-pause]

Then, at the end of August, the Assembly writes a famous document. It is called the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen. It is only one page long, with seventeen short articles. It says that all people are born free and equal in their rights. It says that everyone should be equal before the law. It says that people should be free to speak and to write and to believe what they choose.

[pause]

{img:declaration-rights-of-man-printed-poster-wall-paris-people-reading}
The declaration is printed and posted on walls all over Paris. People stand in front of it and read it, or have it read to them. Étienne helps to print it at his shop. He brings home a copy and reads it aloud in the courtyard. The neighbours listen in silence. When he reaches the first article, all people are born and remain free and equal in rights, the water carrier wipes his eyes. Antoine nods slowly.

[long-pause]

Not everything in the declaration is perfect. It does not say much about women. It does not end slavery in the French colonies. Those changes will come much later, after many long struggles. But it is a very important beginning. It will inspire people in many other countries, for many years to come.

[pause]

{img:women-reading-declaration-street-corner-shawls-august-paris}
Louise asks her mother if the declaration is for women too. Marguerite thinks about it for a long time. I hope so, she says at last. Some women in Paris are already asking the same question. A few years later, a writer called Olympe de Gouges will write her own declaration, for the rights of women. Louise does not know that yet. But she keeps thinking about her question.

[long-pause]

In late August, the weather turns cooler, and the first rain comes. The harvest of 1789 is better than last year. Slowly, more grain arrives in Paris, and the price of bread begins to come down a little. The bread queues get a little shorter. Marguerite is able to buy a little meat for Sunday, for the first time in weeks.

[pause]

{img:family-sunday-meal-roast-meat-bread-wine-candles-august-1789-smiling}
That Sunday, the family has a special meal. There is a little roast meat, and fresh bread, and a jug of wine. Étienne is invited too. Antoine raises his glass. To freedom, he says, and to cheaper bread. Everyone laughs and drinks. Mémé says that cheaper bread is the most important part. And for once, nobody disagrees with her.

[pause]

In August, many Parisians go out of the city on Sundays, to the villages and the countryside nearby. They walk to the green hills of Montmartre, with its windmills, or to the woods to the west. They have picnics on the grass and drink wine from the little country inns. The children run in the fields and pick flowers. It is good to breathe the fresh air, away from the crowded streets.

[pause]

One Sunday in August, the family walks up to Montmartre. It is a long walk up the hill, past the windmills turning slowly in the breeze. At the top, they sit on the grass and look down at Paris, spread out below them. They can see the towers of Notre-Dame and the dome of the Invalides. They can see the river, shining in the sun. And they can see the faubourg, and the empty place where the Bastille once stood.

[long-pause]

{img:fete-de-la-federation-1790-champ-de-mars-huge-crowd-altar-flags}
The summer of 1789 was only the beginning. The French Revolution went on for ten years, and it changed France forever.

[pause]

One year after the Bastille, on the fourteenth of July 1790, there was a great celebration in Paris. It was called the Festival of the Federation. It was held on a huge open field called the Champ de Mars. For weeks before, thousands of Parisians worked together to prepare the field. Rich and poor, men and women, old and young, all dug the earth side by side. Even the king came to help for a little while.

[long-pause]

On the day itself, hundreds of thousands of people gathered on the field. It rained, but nobody minded. There was a great altar in the middle. Representatives came from all over France. The king swore to respect the new laws. Everyone cheered and sang and embraced. It was a day of great joy and hope. Many people said it was the happiest day of the revolution.

[pause]

{img:families-working-together-digging-champ-de-mars-1790-wheelbarrows}
But the years that followed were very hard. There were great quarrels between different groups. There was war with other countries. There were years of fear and suffering, when many people lost their lives. The king and the queen lost their lives too. We will not stay in those years tonight. We remember the people who suffered, on all sides, gently and with sorrow.

[long-pause]

In the end, a young general called Napoleon Bonaparte took power. He became emperor of France. He kept some of the changes of the revolution, like the new laws, which are still used in France today. But France was not free in the way the people of 1789 had hoped. It took many more years, and more changes, before France became the free republic it is today.

[pause]

{img:paris-1800s-later-new-wide-streets-gas-lamps-carriages-evening}
But the ideas of 1789 did not die. The idea that all people are born free and equal. The idea that the people should choose their own government. The idea that everyone should have the right to speak and to believe freely. These ideas spread all over the world. They inspired people in many countries, for hundreds of years. And they are still important today.

[long-pause]

What about the family in the Faubourg Saint-Antoine? We can only imagine. Perhaps Antoine went on making his tables and chairs, and Paul became a carpenter too. Perhaps Marguerite went on sewing by the window, and Mémé lived to a great age. Perhaps Étienne became a master printer, with his own shop near the river.

[pause]

{img:old-woman-louise-reading-book-grandchildren-paris-window-1840s}
And perhaps Louise grew up to work in a bookshop, surrounded by the books she loved. Perhaps one day, Étienne printed a book just for her, as he had promised. And perhaps, when she was an old woman, she sat by the window with her grandchildren. On the shelf above the fireplace, there was a small grey stone. And she told them about the summer of 1789, when she was twelve years old, and Paris changed forever.

[pause]

Many of the changes of the revolution are still part of life in France today. The country is divided into departments, which were created in 1790. The metric system, with metres and kilograms, was created during the revolution. It is now used almost everywhere in the world. And the motto of France, liberty, equality, fraternity, comes from those years too.

[pause]

The Faubourg Saint-Antoine remained a proud neighbourhood of craftsmen for a long time. Its people took part in many of the later events of the revolution. They were always ready to stand up for what they believed was fair. And for many years, people in Paris spoke of the faubourg with a mixture of respect and a little fear.

[long-pause]

{img:bastille-day-parade-champs-elysees-today-flags-crowds-sunny-today}
Today, the fourteenth of July is the national holiday of France. In France, people simply call it le quatorze juillet, the fourteenth of July. In other countries, it is often called Bastille Day.

[pause]

It became the national holiday in 1880, almost a hundred years after the fall of the Bastille. The French chose the date to remember two things. They remember the fourteenth of July 1789, when the people of Paris took the Bastille. And they remember the fourteenth of July 1790, the great Festival of the Federation, when all the French came together in peace and joy.

[long-pause]

On the morning of the fourteenth of July, there is a great parade in Paris. Soldiers march down the wide avenue of the Champs-Élysées, from the Arc de Triomphe to the Place de la Concorde. Planes fly overhead, trailing smoke in blue, white and red. The president of France watches from a stand. Crowds of people line the avenue, waving little flags.

[pause]

{img:planes-trailing-blue-white-red-smoke-paris-sky-today}
All over France, in every town and village, there are celebrations. There are little parades and bands playing. There are picnics and dances in the squares. In many places, there are dances on the evening of the thirteenth, held in the fire stations. They are called the firemen's balls. People of all ages dance under coloured lights, late into the warm summer night.

[long-pause]

And on the evening of the fourteenth, there are fireworks. In Paris, the biggest fireworks are at the Eiffel Tower. Hundreds of thousands of people gather on the Champ de Mars, the same field where the Festival of the Federation was held in 1790. They bring picnics and blankets and wait for the dark. When the fireworks begin, the whole sky above the Eiffel Tower lights up, in blue and white and red and gold.

[pause]

{img:eiffel-tower-fireworks-bastille-day-night-crowd-champ-de-mars-today}
Where the Bastille once stood, there is now a big open square, called the Place de la Bastille. There is no trace of the old fortress there anymore. Instead, in the middle of the square, there is a tall green column, with a golden figure on top. The figure is a winged spirit of freedom, holding a broken chain. If you look down at the pavement around the square, you can see a line of paving stones. They mark where the walls of the old fortress once stood.

[long-pause]

In the Faubourg Saint-Antoine, there are still furniture workshops and shops, though fewer than before. Some of the old courtyards still have their wooden doors and their old wells. Craftsmen still make chairs and tables and cupboards by hand. And on the fourteenth of July, the neighbours still hang flags from their windows, and come out into the street to celebrate.

[pause]

{img:place-de-la-bastille-today-july-column-golden-spirit-evening-today}
Every year, many French families remember the fourteenth of July in their own way. Some go to the parade, some go to the fireworks. Some have a picnic in the park with their friends. Some just sit on their balconies in the warm evening and watch the sky. And many of them think, at least for a moment, about the people of Paris in the summer of 1789. About their hopes, and their courage, and their dream of a fairer world.

[pause]

On the evening of the fourteenth, families sit on their balconies and in their gardens, waiting for the fireworks. Children are allowed to stay up late. Grandparents tell stories of other fourteenths of July, long ago. And when the first firework bursts in the sky, everyone looks up together, and says ooh, and aah, just as people have done for more than a hundred years.

[pause]

In many towns, there are also little ceremonies at the war memorials and town halls. The mayor gives a short speech. A band plays the national anthem, the Marseillaise. It was written a few years after 1789, as a marching song. Today it is sung at every fourteenth of July, by soldiers and children, old people and young, all together.

[long-pause]

{img:paris-today-seine-notre-dame-bridges-booksellers-summer-today}
Today, Paris is one of the most beautiful and most visited cities in the world. Many of the places our family knew are still there.

[pause]

The Seine still flows through the middle of the city. Notre-Dame still stands on its island, with its two great towers. It was badly damaged by a fire a few years ago, but it has been carefully rebuilt. The Pont Neuf still crosses the river, the oldest bridge in Paris. And along the quays, the booksellers still have their little green boxes of old books, as they did more than two hundred years ago.

[long-pause]

The Palais-Royal is still there too, with its quiet garden and its long arcades. It is much calmer now than in 1789. People sit on green chairs under the trees, reading or talking. Children play around the fountain. The old Café de Foy is gone. But you can still walk under the arcades, and imagine the crowds and the speeches of that hot summer.

[pause]

{img:palais-royal-garden-today-green-chairs-trees-fountain-calm-today}
The great market of Les Halles is gone. It was moved out of the city about fifty years ago. But the church of Saint-Eustache still stands there, tall and light. And in the streets around it, there are still little food shops and markets. You can buy bread and cheese, fruit and vegetables, just as Marguerite and Louise did.

[long-pause]

And everywhere in Paris, there are bakeries. There are more than a thousand of them. Every morning, Parisians still line up to buy their bread. Now the most famous bread is the baguette, a long thin loaf with a golden crust. In 1789, it did not exist yet. But the love of fresh bread is the same. And the smell of bread still drifts through the streets of Paris every morning, as it did long ago.

[pause]

{img:paris-bakery-today-morning-queue-baguettes-golden-light-today}
In the museum of the history of Paris, called the Carnavalet, you can see many things from 1789. There are paintings of the fall of the Bastille. There are little models of the fortress, carved from its stones by Palloy. There are cockades of blue, white and red, and pamphlets and newspapers. There is even a key of the Bastille. And you can stand in front of them and imagine the people who held them, more than two hundred years ago.

[long-pause]

In the evenings, Parisians still love to walk along the Seine. They sit on the edge of the quays with their feet dangling over the water. They have picnics on the islands, with bread and cheese and wine. Boats glide past, full of people looking at the lights of the city. Music drifts from the cafés. And as the sun sets, the river turns gold, just as it did on the summer evenings of 1789.

[pause]

{img:paris-evening-today-people-sitting-quays-seine-sunset-picnic-today}
Paris has changed in so many ways. There are cars and metro trains and tall buildings. There are lights everywhere at night, and millions of visitors. But in its heart, it is still the same city. A city of narrow streets and tall houses, of cafés and bookshops and bakeries. A city of ideas and arguments and hope. And on the fourteenth of July, it still remembers the summer when its people changed the world.

[pause]

The Faubourg Saint-Antoine is still a lively neighbourhood. Its old courtyards have been turned into little shops and studios and cafés. Some still have furniture workshops, where craftsmen restore old tables and chairs. The church of Sainte-Marguerite is still there, quiet and old. And in the evenings, people still sit outside the cafés, talking and laughing, just as the workers did in the wine shops of 1789.

[pause]

Near the Place de la Bastille, there is a little harbour now, where pleasure boats are tied up. It is on an old canal that leads to the Seine. People walk along it in the evenings and look at the boats. Children feed the ducks. And nearby, there is a great modern opera house, where music fills the night, on the very place where the people of 1789 once gathered.

[long-pause]

{img:paris-summer-evening-1789-faubourg-street-sunset-candles-windows}
So we leave Paris tonight, in the summer of 1789, as the evening falls.

[pause]

The sun is setting over the rooftops of the city. The sky turns gold, then pink, then a deep soft blue. On the river, the boats are tied up for the night. The washerwomen have gone home. The booksellers on the quays are closing their boxes. And all over the city, candles are being lit in the windows, one by one.

[long-pause]

In the Faubourg Saint-Antoine, the workshops are closed for the day. The street is quiet. A few neighbours are sitting on their doorsteps, enjoying the cool evening air. Children are playing a last game before bedtime. A cat walks slowly along the top of a wall. And at the end of the street, where the dark towers of the Bastille once stood, there is now open sky, with the first stars coming out.

[pause]

{img:courtyard-neighbours-evening-candle-wine-jug-singing-quiet-1789}
In the courtyard of the old house, the neighbours are sitting together. The water carrier is singing a song from his mountains, softly. The lace maker is nodding along. Étienne is reading a newspaper by the light of a candle. Antoine sits beside him, smoking a little pipe, listening. And Marguerite comes down with a jug of wine and some cups, and sits down with them.
