Let us walk through the streets of Paris in the summer of 1789.

The oldest part of the city is on two islands in the middle of the Seine. On the larger island stands the cathedral of Notre-Dame and the old royal palace, now the law courts. Narrow old streets wind between them. Bridges connect the islands to both banks of the river. Some of the bridges still have houses built on them, tall and narrow, leaning over the water.

The most famous bridge is the Pont Neuf, which means the new bridge. In fact, it is the oldest bridge in Paris, almost two hundred years old. It is wide and busy, and it has no houses on it. Instead, it is full of people. There are street sellers and singers, tooth pullers and fortune tellers. There are booksellers with their little stalls, and dog washers and shoe shiners. People say that if you stand on the Pont Neuf long enough, you will meet everyone in Paris.

--

On the right bank of the river, there are the great markets, the grand squares and the palace of the Louvre. There are the elegant streets where the rich live, with fine shops selling silk and lace, hats and gloves. On the left bank, there is the old university quarter, full of students and booksellers and printers. There are churches and convents with walled gardens.

{img:narrow-paris-street-1789-mud-gutter-shop-signs-water-carrier}
The streets of Paris are narrow and crowded and muddy. Most of them have no pavements. A gutter of dirty water runs down the middle. Carriages and carts rumble through, splashing mud on the people walking. Above the shops hang painted signs, showing what is sold there: a boot, a key, a loaf, a pair of scissors. Many people cannot read, so the pictures help them find what they need.

--

The houses are tall, five or six storeys high, built of pale stone or plaster. The rich live on the lower floors, in big rooms with high ceilings. The higher you go, the poorer the people. Right at the top, under the roof, there are tiny rooms for servants, students and poor workers. So rich and poor often live in the very same building, one above the other. They meet on the stairs every day.

{img:paris-tall-house-floors-rich-below-poor-attic-cross-section-1789}
Around the old city, there are the faubourgs, the neighbourhoods outside the old walls. They are newer and poorer and very busy. In the east is the Faubourg Saint-Antoine, the neighbourhood of the furniture makers. In the south is the Faubourg Saint-Marcel, where the tanners and the dyers work beside a little river. These are the neighbourhoods of the working people of Paris.

--

Around the whole city there is a new wall, built only a few years ago. It is not a wall for defence. It is a wall for taxes. At each gate, there are tax offices. Everything that comes into Paris must pay a tax: wine and meat, wood and flour. The people of Paris hate this wall. It makes everything more expensive. They have a joke about it: the wall that walls Paris makes Paris grumble.

{img:paris-customs-wall-barrier-gate-tax-office-carts-waiting-1789}
But for all its mud and crowds, Paris is a beautiful and exciting city. There are wonderful gardens, like the Tuileries beside the Louvre, where people walk on summer evenings. There are theatres and cafés, bookshops and coffee houses. There are new ideas everywhere, about freedom and fairness and the rights of people. And in the summer of 1789, those ideas are about to change everything.

At night, Paris is darker than it is today. There are oil lamps hanging on ropes across some of the streets. They give a soft yellow light, but they leave many shadows. People who walk at night often hire a boy with a lantern to light their way. These boys stand at the street corners, calling out: Lantern! Lantern! For a small coin, they walk you home and light the stairs to your door.

On Sundays, Paris is quieter. The shops are closed, and the church bells ring for mass. Families put on their best clothes and walk to church together. In the afternoon, they go walking in the gardens and along the boulevards, wide new streets with trees on either side. There are little cafés and theatres along the boulevards, and street performers. It is the best day of the week for many working families.
