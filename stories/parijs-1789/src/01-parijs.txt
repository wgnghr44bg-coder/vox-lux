Tonight we travel more than two hundred years back in time. We go to Paris, the great capital of France. The year is 1789, and it is early summer. And in a few weeks, something will happen here that the whole world will remember.

--

It is early morning in June, and the sky over Paris is pale and soft. A light mist lies on the river Seine, which winds slowly through the middle of the city. On an island in the river rise the two square towers of the cathedral of Notre-Dame. They have stood there for more than five hundred years. Boats are already moving on the water, carrying wood and wine and hay into the city.

Along the riverbanks, women are kneeling at the water's edge, washing clothes on flat stones. Men are rolling barrels up from the boats. A boy runs along the quay with a basket of bread on his head. Church bells ring out across the rooftops, one after another. Smoke rises from a thousand chimneys. And the smell of fresh bread and coffee drifts out from the bakeries and cafés, as the great city wakes up.

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

Paris in 1789 is one of the biggest cities in Europe. About six hundred thousand people live here. It is a city of narrow streets and tall stone houses, of churches and palaces, of markets and workshops. It is a city of rich and poor, living side by side. There are noble families in fine houses with gardens. And there are workers living in small rooms under the roofs, working long hours for a little bread.

This summer, Paris is full of talk and hope and worry. The king has called a great meeting, the first in a hundred and seventy-five years. People everywhere are talking about it. Will things change? Will life get better for ordinary people? And will there be enough bread to eat this summer? Bread is very expensive, and many families are hungry. Everyone is waiting to see what will happen.

{img:faubourg-saint-antoine-workshop-street-carpenters-1789-morning}
Tonight we will spend the summer of 1789 with one ordinary family in Paris. They live in the eastern part of the city, in a busy neighbourhood of craftsmen. We will meet a father who makes furniture, and a mother who sews for a living. We will meet their daughter, who loves to read, and their son, who is always running in the streets. We will meet a grandmother who remembers the old days, and a young neighbour who reads the newspapers aloud to everyone.

--

We will visit the markets and the riverbanks, the cafés and the gardens of the Palais-Royal. We will stand in line for bread, and listen to the news from Versailles. And in the middle of July, we will see how the people of Paris went to an old fortress called the Bastille. We will tell that part gently, from the streets nearby. And afterwards, we will see how Paris celebrated, and what came later.

{img:bastille-fortress-1789-eight-towers-faubourg-roofs-evening-light}
So let us begin in the east of Paris, on a summer morning in 1789. At the end of a wide street stands a great grey fortress, with eight round towers and thick walls. It is an old prison, called the Bastille. It looks dark and heavy above the little houses around it. But this morning, nobody is looking at it much. In a workshop nearby, a carpenter is opening his doors. And the smell of fresh wood shavings drifts out into the summer air.

In a little square, a woman opens the shutters of her café and puts out a few small tables. A man in a long apron sweeps the step of his shop. A flower seller arranges her roses and lilies in a basket. Pigeons strut across the cobblestones, looking for crumbs. And somewhere, a window opens, and a voice calls down to a child in the street to come home for breakfast.

The carpenter's name is Antoine. He stands for a moment in the doorway of his workshop, looking up at the morning sky. It will be hot today, he thinks. He rolls up his sleeves and picks up his plane. Upstairs, his wife is already at work with her needle by the window. And his children are still asleep, under a thin blanket, in the warm little room above the street.
