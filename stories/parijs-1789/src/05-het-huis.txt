The family lives in a tall old house in a narrow street. The house has five floors, and many families live in it.

On the ground floor, there is Antoine's workshop, with big wooden doors opening onto the street. Next to it, there is a little shop that sells wine. Behind them, there is a small courtyard, with a well and a few washing lines. A dark wooden staircase winds up through the house. It creaks with every step, and it smells of cooking and candle smoke.

--

On the first floor lives the owner of the house, a wine merchant, with his wife and children. They have the biggest rooms, with tall windows and a fireplace in every room. On the second floor lives Antoine's family. On the third floor there is a widow who makes lace, and a family of water carriers. On the fourth floor, there are two rooms for workers. And in the attic, under the sloping roof, there is Étienne's tiny room, with one little window looking out over the rooftops.

{img:simple-paris-room-1789-bed-curtains-table-fireplace-sewing-basket}
The family has two rooms. The main room has a fireplace, a wooden table and some chairs that Antoine made himself. There is a big wooden cupboard for clothes and dishes. There is Marguerite's sewing basket by the window, and Mémé's chair beside it. The second room is for sleeping. There is a big bed with curtains for Antoine and Marguerite, and a smaller bed for the children. Mémé sleeps in a little bed in the corner of the main room, near the fire.

--

There is no running water in the house. Water must be carried up from the well in the courtyard, or bought from the water carriers. The water carriers walk through the streets with two buckets hanging from a wooden yoke across their shoulders. They fill them at the fountains or from the river, and carry them up the stairs of the houses. They call out in the streets: À l'eau! Water! Water! Paul often helps the water carrier family on the third floor, and they give him a few coins.

{img:water-carrier-yoke-buckets-climbing-stairs-paris-house-1789}
There is no bathroom either. People wash with a bowl of water in their rooms. On hot summer days, some people bathe in the Seine, in places set aside for swimming. The toilets are little sheds in the courtyard, shared by everyone. At night, people use pots, which are emptied in the morning. Paris in 1789 is not a very clean city. People are used to the smells.

--

The family's window looks out onto the street. From there, Marguerite can see everything that happens. She sees the neighbours going to market, and the carts rumbling past. She sees the children playing and the dogs sleeping in the sun. She sees the peddlers with their trays and baskets, calling out what they sell. And at the end of the street, over the rooftops, she can see the grey towers of the Bastille.

{img:view-from-window-paris-street-1789-rooftops-bastille-towers-distance}
In the evenings, the neighbours often sit together in the courtyard, when the weather is warm. They bring out stools and chairs. They share a jug of wine and talk about the day. The children play around the well. The lace maker from the third floor tells stories about her youth. And the water carrier sings old songs from his village in the mountains. It is a crowded house. But it is a friendly one.

In winter, the house is cold. Wood is expensive, so the family burns only a small fire. They wear their warmest clothes indoors, and sit close together. Antoine brings up offcuts of wood from his workshop to burn. That helps a lot. The neighbours who have no workshop are glad when Antoine gives them a few pieces too. In a cold house, a little extra wood is a precious gift.

The owner of the house, the wine merchant, is not a bad man. He collects the rent every three months, and he grumbles when it is late. But he also lets the children play in the courtyard, and he gives them a sweet now and then. His wife sometimes buys shirts from Marguerite. And on feast days, he sends a bottle of wine up to each family in the house.
