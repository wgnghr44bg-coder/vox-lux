In 1789, France is ruled by a king. His name is Louis the Sixteenth. He is thirty-four years old, a large, shy and kind man. He loves hunting and working with locks and clocks in his own little workshop. He became king when he was only twenty. He is not a bad man. But he finds it very hard to make difficult decisions.

His wife is the queen, Marie Antoinette. She came from Austria when she was only fourteen, to marry Louis. She is graceful and lively and loves beautiful things. She has a little village built in the gardens of Versailles, where she can pretend to be a country girl. Many people in Paris do not like her. They say she spends too much money. Some of what they say is true, and much of it is unkind gossip.

--

The king and the queen do not live in Paris. They live in a huge palace at Versailles, about twenty kilometres to the west. Versailles is one of the grandest palaces in the world. It has hundreds of rooms, a great hall of mirrors, and enormous gardens with fountains and lakes. Thousands of nobles and servants live there with the king. For the people of Paris, Versailles feels very far away.

{img:hall-of-mirrors-versailles-candles-chandeliers-courtiers-evening}
French society is divided into three groups, called the three estates. The first estate is the clergy, the priests and bishops of the Church. The second estate is the nobility, the lords and ladies with old family names. And the third estate is everyone else. The farmers and the craftsmen, the shopkeepers and the lawyers, the workers and the merchants. The third estate is almost everyone in France. But it has the least say in how the country is run.

--

The nobles and the clergy pay very few taxes. The third estate pays most of them. Farmers must also pay taxes to their lord and to the Church. Many people feel this is not fair. And in recent years, life has become harder. France has spent a lot of money on wars, including helping the Americans in their war for independence. Now the king has very little money left, and large debts to pay.

{img:french-farmers-countryside-1789-wheat-field-poor-harvest-village}
To make things worse, the harvest of 1788 was very bad. There was a terrible hailstorm in the summer, which flattened the wheat in many places. Then came one of the coldest winters anyone could remember. The river Seine froze over. The price of grain went up and up. And the price of bread, the most important food for ordinary people, went up too.

--

So the king decided to call a great meeting. It is called the Estates-General. Representatives of all three estates would come together at Versailles, to talk about the problems of France. The last time such a meeting had been held was in 1614, long ago. All over France, people chose their representatives. And they wrote down their complaints and hopes in little books, to send to the king.

{img:villagers-writing-complaints-book-church-table-candles-1789}
In village churches and town halls, people gathered to write these books. They wrote about the high taxes and the unfair rules. They wrote about the hunting rights of the lords, whose animals ate the farmers' crops. They wrote about the price of bread. And many of them wrote, at the end, words of hope and trust in the king. They believed that if the king only knew how they suffered, he would make things right.

In the countryside, most people are farmers. They live in small villages of stone houses, around a church and a little square. They grow wheat and rye, vines and vegetables. They keep a cow or a few goats, and some chickens. Life is hard, and money is short. But in the evenings, they sit together outside their houses and talk, while the children play and the swallows fly over the fields.

Many people in France in 1789 also read the books of new thinkers, called the philosophers of the Enlightenment. These writers said that people should use their reason, and question old ideas. They wrote about freedom and fairness, and about the rights that all people share. Their books were read in cafés and salons all over Paris. And their ideas slowly spread, even to the workshops of the faubourg.

At Versailles, life goes on as it has for a hundred years. There are grand dinners and balls, hunts and card games. Courtiers in silk and powdered wigs walk in the gardens among the fountains. The queen plays with her children in her little village. The king goes hunting in the forests. And far away, in Paris and the countryside, people wonder if the king knows how they live.
