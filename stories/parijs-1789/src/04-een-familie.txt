Let us meet a family who lives in the Faubourg Saint-Antoine, in the east of Paris.

The father is called Antoine. He is a carpenter and furniture maker, about forty years old. He has a small workshop on the ground floor of a tall old house. He makes tables and chairs, chests and cupboards, from oak and walnut. He works with saws and planes and chisels, and his hands are strong and rough. He is a calm and thoughtful man. He does not talk much while he works. But in the evenings, he likes to sit with his neighbours and talk about the news.

The mother is called Marguerite. She is a seamstress, and she sews shirts and dresses for a shop in the city. She works at home, by the window, where the light is best. She can sew very fast, with tiny neat stitches. She also runs the household and manages the money, which is never quite enough. She is practical and warm, and she laughs easily, even in hard times.

--

They have two children. The daughter is called Louise, and she is twelve. She helps her mother with the sewing, and she is getting very good at it. But what she really loves is reading. She learned to read at the little school of the parish. Now she reads everything she can find: old almanacs, song sheets, and the little books sold on the Pont Neuf. She dreams of having a book of her own one day.

{img:girl-twelve-reading-small-book-window-light-paris-attic-room}
The son is called Paul, and he is nine. He is quick and cheerful and always on the move. He runs errands for his father and for the neighbours. He knows every street and every shop in the neighbourhood. He knows the names of the horses that pull the carts. He plays with the other boys in the street, and comes home with muddy knees and torn sleeves. His mother scolds him, and then mends his clothes again.

--

The grandmother lives with them too. She is Antoine's mother, and her name is Jeanne. Everyone calls her Mémé, which is a French word for granny. She is seventy-two years old, small and thin, with a white cap on her grey hair. She was born in a village outside Paris, and came to the city as a young woman. She sits by the fire in winter and by the window in summer, knitting stockings. She remembers the old king, Louis the Fifteenth. And she remembers the terrible winter of 1709, which her own mother told her about.

{img:old-french-grandmother-white-cap-knitting-window-sunlight-1789}
On the same floor as the family, in the little room next door, lives a young man called Étienne. He is eighteen, and he is an apprentice at a printing shop near the river. He works long hours, setting little metal letters into lines to print books and newspapers. He is thin and lively, with ink on his fingers. He brings home spoiled pages from the printing shop, which Louise loves to read. And he reads the news aloud to everyone in the evenings.

--

The family is not rich, but they are not the poorest either. Antoine is a skilled craftsman with his own workshop. Marguerite earns a little extra with her sewing. In good years, they have enough to eat and a little to spare. But this year is not a good year. Bread is very expensive. Sometimes Antoine has fewer orders. And Marguerite has to count every coin carefully.

{img:french-family-supper-1789-bread-soup-candle-small-room-evening}
In the evenings, the family eats together in their main room, on the second floor. There is soup with bread, and sometimes a little cheese or an egg. Meat is a rare treat, for Sundays or feast days. They eat by the light of one candle, because candles are expensive too. Then Étienne knocks at the door, with a newspaper under his arm. And everyone gathers round to hear the news from Versailles.

The family also has a cat, a striped grey cat called Minette. She lives mostly in the workshop, where she catches mice among the planks. She likes to sleep in the warm curls of wood shavings under Antoine's bench. In the evenings, she climbs the stairs and sits on Mémé's lap. Mémé strokes her and talks to her quietly, as if she were an old friend.

On Sundays, the family goes to mass at the church of Sainte-Marguerite, in the faubourg. After mass, they stand outside in the square and talk with their neighbours. The children run about with the other children. Then they go home for the Sunday meal, which is a little better than the meals of the week. It is the one day when Antoine does not work, and he enjoys it very much.
