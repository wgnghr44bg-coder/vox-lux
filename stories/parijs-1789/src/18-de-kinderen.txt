What is life like for children in Paris in 1789?

Children of working families start to help their parents very young. They fetch water and bread, run errands and look after younger brothers and sisters. At ten or twelve, many of them start an apprenticeship, to learn a trade. Boys learn to be carpenters and bakers, printers and shoemakers. Girls learn to sew and to make lace, to spin and to sell in the market.

--

Some children go to a little school, run by the parish church. There, they learn to read and write, to count, and to say their prayers. The schools are small and simple, with one teacher and a few benches. Not all children go, and many go only for a few years. Louise went to the parish school for three years. She was one of the best readers in her class.

{img:parish-school-1789-children-benches-teacher-slate-reading-lesson}
Paul went to the school too, but he found it hard to sit still. He would rather be running in the streets. Now he helps his father in the workshop in the mornings. He sweeps the shavings and sorts the nails. Antoine is teaching him to use a small plane, to make the wood smooth. Paul is proud when he makes his first smooth board. But he still likes the streets best.

--

The children of Paris have many games. They roll hoops along the streets with sticks. They play ball games and tag and hide and seek. They spin tops, made of wood. They play at soldiers, with paper hats and wooden swords. Girls play with dolls made of cloth and wood, and skip with ropes. In the evening, they sit on the doorsteps and tell each other stories.

{img:children-watching-puppet-show-street-paris-1789-laughing-crowd}
There are many things to see in the streets of Paris. There are street singers, who sing the latest songs and sell copies of the words. There are puppet shows, with funny little puppets that argue and fight. There are acrobats and jugglers, and men with dancing dogs. Paul loves the puppet shows best. He always stops to watch, even when he is supposed to be running an errand.

--

In the summer of 1789, the children of Paris have a new favourite game. They play at storming the Bastille. They build a little fortress from boxes and planks. Some children are the defenders, and some are the attackers. The attackers always win. Then they all cheer together and pin paper cockades to their hats. Their mothers shake their heads. But they smile too.

{img:girl-teaching-brother-letters-printed-name-paper-candle-evening}
In the evenings, Louise is teaching Paul to read. She uses the little paper with her name printed on it, and the old pages that Étienne brings home. She points to the letters, one by one. Paul is impatient at first. But Louise is patient, like her father. Slowly, Paul begins to read short words. One evening, he reads a whole line from a newspaper, all by himself. Louise claps her hands. Mémé says she never thought she would see the day. And Paul is so proud that he reads the same line again, five more times.

Paul's best friend is a boy called Pierre, the son of the water carrier. They do everything together. They run errands, play in the streets, swim in the river and get into trouble. Pierre is learning to carry water like his father, with a little yoke and two small buckets. Paul sometimes helps him. In return, Pierre helps Paul sweep the workshop. They say they will be friends forever.

On feast days, the children get little treats. There are gingerbread figures from the fairs, and sugared almonds and little cakes. Sometimes a puppet show comes to the square, or a man with a magic lantern. A magic lantern is a box with a lamp inside, which shows pictures on a white sheet. The children sit in the dark and watch the pictures in wonder. It is like magic to them.
