Tonight we go back to the summer of 1969. We go to America, in the month of July. And we look up at the moon.

[long-pause]

It is a warm summer night on the coast of Florida. The air is soft and smells of the sea. Crickets are singing in the long grass. Palm trees move gently in the breeze. And over the dark ocean, a big round moon is rising slowly, pale and silver. Its light makes a shining path across the water, all the way to the beach.

[pause]

People have looked up at the moon for as long as there have been people. They have told stories about it and sung songs about it. They have used it to count the months and to find their way at night. They have wondered what it is made of, and whether anyone lives there. And for thousands of years, they have dreamed of going there. In July 1969, three men are about to do it.

[pause]

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

{img:saturn-v-rocket-launch-pad-night-floodlights-florida-1969}
A few kilometres from the beach, a giant rocket is standing on a launch pad, lit by bright white lights. It is taller than a thirty-storey building. It is the biggest rocket ever built. In a few days, it will carry three astronauts away from the Earth, toward the moon. Their mission is called Apollo 11. And if everything goes well, two of them will walk on the moon.

[pause]

Tonight we will follow the journey of Apollo 11, from beginning to end. We will meet the three astronauts, and learn about their lives and their families. We will see the giant rocket, and the crowds who came to watch it go. We will follow the spacecraft across the dark space between the Earth and the moon. We will hear the voices from Mission Control in Houston. And we will watch, as the whole world watched, as the first person stepped onto the moon.

[pause]

{img:houston-suburb-1969-family-house-front-yard-evening-station-wagon}
We will also meet an ordinary family in Houston, Texas, where the mission is controlled. The father works as an engineer at Mission Control. The mother looks after the house and the children. Their daughter loves the stars and dreams of space. Their son builds model rockets in the garage. And their grandmother, who was born when there were not even aeroplanes, cannot quite believe what is happening.

[long-pause]

There will be some tense moments in this story, because the journey was difficult and new. Nobody had ever done it before. But mostly, it is a story of calm and careful people, working together. It is a story of courage and patience, of curiosity and wonder. And it is the story of how, for a few days in July 1969, the whole world looked up at the moon together.

[pause]

{img:family-backyard-looking-up-at-moon-summer-night-1969-telescope}
So let us begin in a backyard in Houston, on a warm summer evening in July 1969. A girl of twelve is standing on the grass, looking up at the moon through a small telescope. Her little brother is sitting beside her, asking a hundred questions. Their father is standing behind them, with his hands in his pockets, smiling. In a few days, he will be helping to send three men to that very moon. And the girl can hardly wait.

[pause]

On the beach, a few families have already set up little tents and camping chairs. They have come early, to get a good place to watch the launch. A father is cooking hot dogs on a little grill. Children are running along the edge of the waves with a flashlight. An old man sits in a folding chair, looking at the rocket across the water through a pair of binoculars. Nobody wants to miss a single moment.

[pause]

The moon has always been the closest of all the things in the sky. It is about three hundred and eighty-four thousand kilometres away. That sounds very far, but it is much closer than the sun or the planets. If you could drive a car to the moon, at the speed of a car on a motorway, it would take about six months. In July 1969, the astronauts will get there in three days.

[long-pause]

{img:sputnik-satellite-1957-small-silver-sphere-antennas-black-space}
How did people come to go to the moon? The story begins about twelve years before, in 1957.

[pause]

In those years, the world was divided. On one side were the United States and their friends. On the other side was the Soviet Union, a great country that included Russia. The two sides did not trust each other. They did not fight a real war. But they competed in everything: in weapons, in science, in sport, and in space. This time is called the Cold War.

[long-pause]

In October 1957, the Soviet Union launched a small silver ball into space. It was called Sputnik. It was the first man-made object to go around the Earth. It was only about the size of a beach ball. But it sent out little beeping radio signals that people all over the world could hear on their radios. People in America looked up at the night sky and felt surprised and a little worried. The race to space had begun.

[pause]

{img:yuri-gagarin-smiling-helmet-1961-first-man-in-space-calm}
In April 1961, the Soviet Union did something even more amazing. They sent the first person into space. His name was Yuri Gagarin. He was a young pilot with a big, friendly smile. He flew once around the whole Earth, in a little round spacecraft, in less than two hours. Then he came back down safely. He became a hero all over the world.

[long-pause]

A month later, the young American president, John F. Kennedy, made a famous speech. He said that America should land a man on the moon, and bring him safely back to Earth, before the end of the decade. That meant before the end of 1969. Many people thought it was impossible. In those days, America had only sent one person into space, for fifteen minutes. Nobody really knew how to get to the moon.

[pause]

{img:president-kennedy-speech-rice-stadium-1962-crowd-sunny-podium}
The next year, Kennedy gave another speech, in a big stadium in Houston. He said: We choose to go to the moon. We choose to go to the moon in this decade, not because it is easy, but because it is hard. The crowd cheered. And all over America, thousands of people began to work on the great project.

[long-pause]

The space agency of America is called NASA. In the years that followed, NASA grew very big. Four hundred thousand people worked on the Apollo program, in factories and offices and laboratories all over the country. There were engineers and scientists, builders and seamstresses, cooks and computer programmers. They built rockets and spacecraft, spacesuits and computers. They worked very long hours, often late into the night.

[pause]

{img:nasa-engineers-1960s-office-drawing-boards-slide-rules-white-shirts}
Step by step, they learned what they needed to know. First, they sent single astronauts into space, in a programme called Mercury. Then they sent two astronauts together, in a programme called Gemini. They learned how to stay in space for many days, how to walk outside a spacecraft, and how to meet another spacecraft in orbit. Then came Apollo. Apollo 8 flew around the moon at Christmas 1968, and came back safely. Apollo 10 went very close to the moon in May 1969. And now, at last, it was the turn of Apollo 11.

[pause]

In America, schools changed after Sputnik. Children were encouraged to study science and mathematics. New schools and laboratories were built. Young people dreamed of becoming scientists and engineers. Many of the young engineers who later worked at Mission Control were children when Sputnik flew. They remembered standing in their gardens at night, looking up at the sky, trying to see the little moving star.

[pause]

The space race also had sad moments. In January 1967, three American astronauts lost their lives in a fire, during a test on the ground. Their names were Gus Grissom, Ed White and Roger Chaffee. Some Soviet cosmonauts lost their lives too, in those years. The engineers worked very hard to make the spacecraft safer afterwards. And the astronauts of Apollo 11 never forgot the friends who had gone before them.

[long-pause]

{img:apollo-11-crew-portrait-armstrong-collins-aldrin-white-spacesuits-1969}
Three men were chosen to fly Apollo 11. Their names were Neil Armstrong, Buzz Aldrin and Michael Collins. They were all thirty-eight or thirty-nine years old. They had all been test pilots, flying new and experimental aeroplanes. And they had all flown in space once before.

[long-pause]

The commander was Neil Armstrong. He grew up in a small town in Ohio, in the middle of America. As a boy, he loved aeroplanes. He built model planes and read everything about flying. He got his pilot's licence when he was sixteen, before he even had a licence to drive a car. He became a navy pilot, and then a test pilot, flying the fastest planes in the world. He was a quiet, calm and modest man. He did not like to talk about himself. When things went wrong, he became even calmer.

[pause]

{img:young-neil-armstrong-ohio-boy-model-airplane-small-town-1940s}
The pilot of the lunar module, the little spacecraft that would land on the moon, was Buzz Aldrin. His real name was Edwin, but everyone called him Buzz. When he was little, his sister could not say brother. She said buzzer instead, and the name stuck. Buzz was a very clever man. He had studied at a great university and written a long study about how spacecraft could meet in space. He was strong and energetic, and he loved to explain things.

[long-pause]

The pilot of the command module, the spacecraft that would stay in orbit around the moon, was Michael Collins. He was born in Rome, in Italy, because his father was an American army officer working there. He was funny and friendly and liked to write. He loved gardening and fishing. During the mission, he would stay alone in the command module, circling the moon, while the other two went down to the surface. He would be the loneliest man in the universe, people said. But he did not feel lonely at all.

[pause]

{img:astronauts-training-simulator-cockpit-instruments-houston-1969}
For months before the mission, the three men trained very hard. They practised every part of the journey, again and again, in simulators. A simulator is a copy of the spacecraft, on the ground, with screens and controls. The trainers would make problems happen, to see how the astronauts would cope. The astronauts also studied the rocks of the moon with geologists. They practised walking in their heavy spacesuits. They learned every switch and every button by heart.

[long-pause]

Each of the astronauts had a family. Neil Armstrong was married to Janet, and they had two young sons. Buzz Aldrin was married to Joan, and they had three children. Michael Collins was married to Pat, and they had three children too. The families lived in the suburbs of Houston, near the space centre. They were proud of their husbands and fathers. But they also worried, of course. Nobody had ever done this before.

[pause]

{img:astronaut-family-1960s-living-room-children-mother-tv-houston}
In the evenings before the mission, the astronauts spent what time they could with their families. Neil Armstrong played with his boys in the garden. Michael Collins worked in his flower beds. Buzz Aldrin went running. They did not talk much about the dangers. They talked about ordinary things, about school and friends and summer plans. And then, in the second week of July, they flew to Florida, to get ready for the launch.

[pause]

The astronauts were also given special training in survival. In case their spacecraft came down in the wrong place, they had to know how to stay alive. They practised in the jungle and in the desert. They learned how to find water and food, how to make a shelter and how to signal for help. They hoped they would never need these skills. But they learned them carefully, just in case.

[pause]

The three men were colleagues and friends, but they were not very close. Each of them was quite private, in his own way. Collins later said they were amiable strangers. But they trusted each other completely. In a spacecraft, far from home, that trust was the most important thing of all. Each knew that the others would do their jobs perfectly.

[long-pause]

{img:houston-engineer-father-1969-short-sleeve-shirt-tie-briefcase-driveway}
Let us meet the family in Houston, who will follow the mission from very close by.

[pause]

The father is called Bill. He is about forty, with short hair and black-rimmed glasses. He is an engineer at NASA. He works in one of the back rooms of Mission Control, where experts help the main team. His job is to watch the systems of the spacecraft: the power, the oxygen, the water. He sits in front of a screen full of numbers, all day long. If something goes wrong, he must notice it at once and say what to do.

[pause]

The mother is called Carol. She looks after the house and the children. She also helps at the local school library twice a week. She is cheerful and organised, and she keeps the whole family running smoothly. She is used to Bill working long hours. During a mission, he sometimes does not come home for a whole day. She keeps his dinner warm in the oven and waits up for him.

[long-pause]

They have two children. The daughter is called Susan, and she is twelve. She loves the stars and the planets more than anything. She has a small telescope that she got for her birthday. She has a map of the moon on her bedroom wall, with all the craters and the seas marked on it. She knows the names of all the astronauts and all the missions. She wants to be an astronaut herself one day, even though there are no women astronauts yet.

[pause]

{img:girl-bedroom-1969-moon-map-wall-telescope-books-desk-lamp}
The son is called Danny, and he is eight. He loves rockets, especially the noise and the fire. He builds model rockets in the garage with his father, from cardboard tubes and balsa wood. Sometimes they launch them in the field behind the school. Some of them go very high. Some of them fall over and fizzle. Danny loves them all. He also loves his dog, a golden retriever called Rocket, of course.

[long-pause]

The grandmother is staying with them for the summer. She is Carol's mother, and her name is Ruth. She is seventy-five years old. She was born in 1894, on a farm in Kansas. When she was a girl, there were no cars in her village, no electric lights, no telephones. She remembers when she saw her first aeroplane, at a county fair, when she was a teenager. And now her son-in-law is helping to send men to the moon. She says she cannot quite believe it.

[pause]

{img:grandmother-1969-porch-rocking-chair-lemonade-evening-kansas-memories}
In the evenings, Grandma Ruth sits on the porch in a rocking chair, with a glass of lemonade. She tells the children stories about the farm. She tells them about the horses and the wheat fields, the big storms and the long winters. She tells them about the first time she saw a telephone, and the first time she rode in a car. The children love her stories. Danny cannot imagine a world without television.

[long-pause]

The family lives in a quiet street in a suburb called Clear Lake, near the space centre. Many of the neighbours work at NASA too. Some of the astronauts live nearby. Everyone knows that the mission is coming, and everyone is excited. Some families have put up flags in their front yards. The children of the street play at astronauts in the gardens, with cardboard boxes for spaceships.

[pause]

{img:clear-lake-street-1969-houses-lawns-flags-children-playing-astronauts}
The night before Bill leaves for his long shifts at Mission Control, the family has a barbecue in the backyard. Bill grills hamburgers and hot dogs. Carol makes potato salad and lemonade. Susan sets up her telescope on the lawn. When it gets dark, they all take turns to look at the moon. It is a thin crescent tonight, low in the west. Bill looks at it for a long time. Then he puts his arm around Susan's shoulders. In a few days, he says, there will be people up there. Susan does not say anything. She just looks and looks.

[pause]

The family has a black and white television in the living room, in a big wooden cabinet. It has a little round dial to change the channels, and two thin antennas on top, like rabbit ears. When the picture is fuzzy, Danny climbs up and moves the antennas around, until it gets better. In 1969, most families in America have a television. But colour television is still new and rare.

[pause]

On Sundays, the family goes to church in the morning, and then has a big lunch. In the afternoons, they sometimes drive to the beach at Galveston, about an hour away. The children swim in the warm sea and build sandcastles. Bill reads the newspaper under an umbrella. Carol and Grandma Ruth sit and talk. And on the way home, they stop for ice cream at a little roadside stand.

[long-pause]

{img:saturn-v-rocket-close-white-black-stages-gantry-tower-morning-sun}
The rocket that will carry Apollo 11 to the moon is called the Saturn V. It is one of the most amazing machines ever built.

[pause]

The Saturn V is a hundred and ten metres tall. That is taller than the Statue of Liberty, taller than a thirty-storey building. It is white, with black markings and the letters USA down its side. When it is full of fuel, it weighs almost three million kilograms. That is as heavy as about four hundred elephants. Or about thirty blue whales.

[long-pause]

The rocket is made of three parts, called stages, one on top of the other. The first stage is at the bottom. It is the biggest, with five huge engines. It burns for only two and a half minutes. In that time, it uses up an enormous amount of fuel, and lifts the rocket high into the sky. Then it falls away into the ocean. The second stage takes over, with five smaller engines, and carries the rocket almost into space. Then it falls away too. The third stage carries the astronauts into orbit around the Earth, and later sends them toward the moon.

[pause]

{img:saturn-v-stages-diagram-style-cutaway-engines-fuel-tanks-illustration}
On top of the third stage, there is the spacecraft itself. It is quite small compared with the rocket. It has three parts too. There is the command module, a little cone where the three astronauts will live. There is the service module, a cylinder with an engine, oxygen and power, joined to the command module. And there is the lunar module, the little spacecraft with legs that will land on the moon.

[long-pause]

The command module of Apollo 11 is called Columbia. It is about three metres high and four metres wide at the bottom. Inside, there is about as much space as inside a large car. The three astronauts will live in it for eight days. They will sleep, eat, work and look out of its little windows. It has hundreds of switches and dials and little lights. It is the only part that will come back to Earth.

[pause]

{img:command-module-columbia-interior-three-couches-switches-small-windows}
The lunar module is called Eagle. It looks a little strange, like a big golden spider. It has four long legs, a body covered with shiny gold foil, and little windows like eyes. It is very light and thin, because it only has to fly in space and on the moon, where there is no air. In some places, its walls are as thin as a few sheets of paper. On Earth, it could not even stand up under its own weight. But on the moon, where everything weighs much less, it will work perfectly.

[pause]

{img:lunar-module-eagle-gold-foil-four-legs-spider-shape-hangar-workers}
Thousands of people worked to build the Saturn V and the spacecraft. In factories in California and Alabama, New York and Louisiana, men and women built every part by hand, very carefully. Some women sewed the spacesuits and the parachutes, stitch by stitch. Others wove the computer memories, threading tiny wires through little rings by hand. Everything had to be perfect. Nobody wanted to make a mistake. Because three men's lives depended on it.

[pause]

The rocket is assembled in a huge building at Cape Kennedy, called the Vehicle Assembly Building. It is one of the biggest buildings in the world. It is so big that, on humid days, little clouds can form near the ceiling inside. When the rocket is ready, it is carried slowly out to the launch pad on a giant crawler. The crawler moves very slowly, slower than a person walking. It takes many hours to travel just a few kilometres.

[pause]

The fuel for the rocket is very cold. Some of it is liquid oxygen and liquid hydrogen, which must be kept at hundreds of degrees below zero. When the rocket is full of fuel, ice forms on its outside. Little pieces of ice fall off it as it stands on the pad. White clouds of vapour drift around it in the warm Florida air. It looks as if the rocket is breathing.

[pause]

The computer that will help to fly the spacecraft is very small for its time, about the size of a suitcase. But it is very slow and simple compared to computers today. It has less memory than a modern calculator. Its programs were written by a team of clever programmers. One of the leaders of the team was a young woman called Margaret Hamilton. Her careful work would help to save the landing on the moon.

[long-pause]

{img:families-camping-beach-florida-july-1969-cars-tents-waiting-launch}
In the days before the launch, people come from all over America, and all over the world, to watch.

[pause]

The launch will take place at a place called Cape Kennedy, on the east coast of Florida. Today it is called Cape Canaveral again. It is a flat, sandy land of beaches and swamps and orange groves, beside the Atlantic Ocean. The launch pads stand in the middle of it, far from any houses. On the roads and beaches around it, the crowds gather.

[long-pause]

People come by car, by bus and by plane. They come in campers and trailers. Some sleep in their cars, some in tents on the beach. The roads are packed with traffic for kilometres. The motels and hotels are all full. Altogether, about a million people come to watch the launch. It is the biggest crowd ever to see a rocket go up.

[pause]

{img:crowd-beach-cars-roof-binoculars-cameras-dawn-waiting-1969}
There are families with children and grandparents. There are students and teachers, farmers and businessmen. There are journalists from all over the world, with cameras and microphones. There are famous people too: politicians, film stars and writers. There is a former president, Lyndon Johnson. And there is the famous pilot Charles Lindbergh, who flew alone across the Atlantic Ocean forty-two years earlier.

[long-pause]

The night before the launch, the beaches look like a great festival. People have picnics and barbecues. Children play in the sand and swim in the sea. People sing songs and play guitars around little campfires. Others sit quietly on the roofs of their cars, looking across the water at the rocket. It is lit up by great searchlights, shining white against the dark sky. It looks like a great white candle.

[pause]

{img:rocket-lit-searchlights-across-water-night-campfires-beach-people}
Some of Bill's colleagues from NASA are there too, with their families. But Bill's family is staying in Houston. Bill must be ready at Mission Control. So they will watch on television, like most people in the world. Susan is a little disappointed. She would love to see the rocket with her own eyes. Bill promises her that one day, they will go to Florida together to see a launch. Susan makes him shake hands on it.

[long-pause]

Early on the morning of the sixteenth of July, the astronauts wake up at about four o'clock. They have a shower and a medical check. Then they eat a big breakfast together: steak and eggs, toast, orange juice and coffee. It is a tradition for astronauts on launch day. Then they put on their white spacesuits, with the help of the suit technicians. The suits are big and heavy. The astronauts carry little portable fans, to keep cool, as they walk out to the van.

[pause]

{img:astronauts-walking-to-van-white-suits-helmets-carrying-fans-dawn}
As they walk out of the building, there are people cheering and taking photographs. The astronauts wave. They climb into the van and drive slowly to the launch pad. There, a lift carries them up the tall tower beside the rocket, more than a hundred metres high. At the top, they walk across a little bridge to the spacecraft. One by one, they climb in and lie down on their couches. The hatch is closed. And now, they wait for the countdown.

[pause]

In the little towns near the Cape, the shops and restaurants are full. Some motels have put up signs welcoming the visitors and wishing the astronauts good luck. Children sell lemonade by the roadside. People have painted messages on their cars: Good luck, Apollo 11! and Moon or bust! Everyone is in a good mood, even when the traffic stops for hours.

[pause]

Many of the people on the beaches have travelled very far. Some families have driven for days, from the other side of the country. Some have come from Europe and Asia. They have spent their savings on the journey. They do not mind. They want to be able to say that they were there, on the day the rocket went to the moon.

[pause]

On the night before the launch, a group of poor people came to the gates of the space centre. They were led by a minister called Ralph Abernathy. They wanted to remind the country that many Americans were still poor and hungry. The head of NASA came out to meet them and talked with them kindly. He said he hoped that the spirit of the mission would help to solve problems on Earth too. They prayed together for the astronauts.

[long-pause]

{img:launch-control-room-1969-rows-consoles-engineers-countdown-windows}
At the launch control centre, a few kilometres from the rocket, hundreds of engineers are sitting at long rows of consoles. They are watching every part of the rocket, checking that everything is ready. The countdown has been going on for many hours. Now, on the morning of the sixteenth of July, it is coming to an end.

[pause]

In Houston, the family is gathered around the television in the living room. Carol has made breakfast, but nobody is very hungry. Grandma Ruth is sitting in the armchair with her hands folded. Danny is lying on the carpet in front of the television, with Rocket the dog beside him. Susan is sitting on the edge of the sofa, holding her breath. Bill is already at Mission Control, since very early in the morning.

[long-pause]

On the television, a calm voice reads out the countdown. It is the voice of the launch control commentator. Ten. Nine. Ignition sequence starts. Six. Five. Four. Three. Two. One. Zero. All engines running. Liftoff! We have a liftoff, thirty-two minutes past the hour. Liftoff on Apollo 11.

[pause]

{img:saturn-v-liftoff-flames-smoke-clouds-rising-blue-sky-july-16}
At the bottom of the rocket, a great cloud of fire and smoke bursts out. For a few seconds, the rocket seems to stand still. Then, very slowly, it begins to rise. It climbs past the tall tower, and up into the clear blue morning sky. It is so big and rises so slowly that it seems almost unreal.

[long-pause]

A few seconds later, the sound reaches the crowds on the beaches. It is a deep, rolling roar, like thunder that goes on and on. The ground shakes. The air shakes. People feel the sound in their chests. Birds fly up from the swamps. Some people cheer and shout. Some people cry. Some just stand still with their mouths open, watching the rocket climb higher and higher.

[pause]

{img:crowd-beach-watching-rocket-climb-pointing-cheering-morning-sky}
Inside the spacecraft, the astronauts feel the rocket shaking and pushing them back into their couches. It is noisy and bumpy, like riding in a train on a rough track. But they are calm. They have trained for this many times. Neil Armstrong keeps his eyes on the instruments. After two and a half minutes, the first stage falls away. There is a sudden jolt, and then the second stage engines start. And the ride becomes smoother.

[long-pause]

In the family's living room, everyone is on their feet. Danny is jumping up and down. Carol is holding Grandma Ruth's hand. Susan is watching the little white dot of the rocket getting smaller and smaller on the screen. Tears are running down her face, but she is smiling. Grandma Ruth shakes her head slowly. When I was a girl, she says, we had a horse and a buggy. And now look.

[pause]

{img:family-living-room-1969-tv-watching-launch-girl-tears-boy-jumping}
Twelve minutes after the launch, the spacecraft is in orbit around the Earth, about a hundred and ninety kilometres up. The astronauts are weightless now. Loose things float around the cabin. They look out of the windows and see the Earth below them, blue and white and beautiful. They check that everything is working properly. Then, after going one and a half times around the Earth, the third stage engine fires again. And Apollo 11 leaves the Earth behind, and begins its journey to the moon.

[pause]

Two days before, the family of Neil Armstrong had travelled to Florida to watch the launch. His wife Janet and his two sons watched from a boat, on a river near the Cape. Buzz Aldrin's family and Michael Collins's family watched from other places. Their children were very excited. Their wives were proud, and a little nervous. When the rocket rose into the sky, they held each other's hands tightly.

[pause]

After the launch, the crowds on the beaches slowly pack up their tents and chairs and go home. The roads are full of traffic for hours. But nobody complains very much. People talk about the launch all the way home. They talk about the roar and the fire and the shaking ground. And they look up, now and then, at the sky, where the rocket has disappeared.

[long-pause]

{img:apollo-spacecraft-coasting-black-space-earth-behind-blue-marble}
The journey to the moon takes about three days. The moon is almost four hundred thousand kilometres away. The spacecraft travels very fast, but there is a long way to go.

[pause]

Soon after leaving the Earth, there is an important job to do. The lunar module, Eagle, is packed away below the command module, inside the top of the rocket. Michael Collins must turn the command module around, and join it nose to nose with Eagle. Then he pulls Eagle out, and the rocket stage falls away. It is a delicate job, like threading a needle in space. Collins does it perfectly.

[long-pause]

Now the spacecraft is coasting through space, toward the moon. The engines are off. It is very quiet. Through the windows, the astronauts can see the Earth getting smaller and smaller behind them. At first, it fills the whole window. After a day, it is like a big blue and white ball. Later, it is so small that Armstrong can cover it with his thumb, held out at arm's length.

[pause]

{img:astronaut-thumb-covering-small-earth-window-spacecraft-black-sky}
To keep the spacecraft from getting too hot on one side and too cold on the other, it turns slowly, like a chicken on a spit. The astronauts call it the barbecue roll. As it turns, the sun and the Earth and the moon pass slowly across the windows, one after another.

[long-pause]

The astronauts have a small television camera with them. Every day, they send pictures back to Earth. People all over the world watch them on their televisions. They show the inside of the spacecraft, and the astronauts floating about. They show the Earth, far away, through the window. Buzz Aldrin does a little exercise, floating upside down. Michael Collins shows how they make their food.

[pause]

{img:astronauts-floating-cabin-camera-tv-broadcast-smiling-weightless}
The family in Houston watches every broadcast. Danny laughs when the astronauts float upside down. Susan wants to know exactly how they eat and how they sleep. Grandma Ruth says it looks very uncomfortable. Carol says it looks like a very small kitchen. They all agree that it looks very far away.

[long-pause]

In space, everything is a little different. There is no up and no down. Pencils and spoons float away if you let go of them. Water forms little floating balls instead of pouring. The astronauts must hold on to something when they work, or they drift across the cabin. They learn to push off gently from the walls, and float from one side to the other.

[pause]

{img:food-pouches-spoons-floating-capsule-astronaut-eating-water-gun}
The food is kept in little plastic bags. Some of it is dried, and the astronauts add water from a little water gun. Then they knead the bag and squeeze the food into their mouths, or eat it with a spoon. There is soup and spaghetti, bacon squares and fruit cocktail, sandwiches and little cubes of cake. There is coffee and orange drink. The astronauts say the food is not bad. But they miss real fresh food, like crisp apples and warm bread.

[pause]

On the second day, the astronauts have a little free time. Collins looks out of the window and takes photographs of the Earth. Aldrin works on his checklists. Armstrong talks with Houston. They also listen to music on a little tape recorder they have brought along. They play cheerful songs, and some quiet music too. The music sounds strange and lovely, floating in the silence of space.

[pause]

Every few hours, Houston gives the astronauts the latest news from Earth. They tell them about a big golf tournament and the baseball scores. They tell them about the weather in Houston and the crowds watching the mission. They also tell them that a Soviet spacecraft, called Luna 15, is flying to the moon at the same time. It is a robot spacecraft, with nobody on board. The two spacecraft will pass each other, far apart, near the moon.

[pause]

On the third day, the spacecraft passes an invisible line in space. Before that line, the Earth's gravity is stronger, and the spacecraft slows down a little as it travels. After the line, the moon's gravity is stronger, and the spacecraft begins to speed up again, falling toward the moon. The astronauts do not feel anything. But the numbers on their screens begin to change.

[long-pause]

{img:mission-control-houston-1969-rows-consoles-big-screens-engineers-headsets}
While the astronauts travel to the moon, hundreds of people on the ground are helping them. They work at Mission Control, in Houston, Texas.

[pause]

Mission Control is a big room, full of rows of desks called consoles. Each console has small screens, full of numbers and lines. There are buttons and switches and telephones. At the front of the room, there are big screens, showing a map of the Earth and the path of the spacecraft. The room is a little dim, and the air is full of cigarette smoke, as was common in those days.

[long-pause]

Each person in the room has a special job. Some watch the engines and the fuel. Some watch the oxygen and the water and the electricity. Some watch the computers and the radio. Some watch the path of the spacecraft and calculate where it will go. The leader of the team is called the flight director. He listens to everyone and makes the final decisions.

[pause]

{img:flight-director-console-headset-calm-focused-white-vest-mission-control}
Only one person in the room talks directly to the astronauts. He is called the capcom, which is short for capsule communicator. He is usually another astronaut. The astronauts trust his voice. During the landing on the moon, the capcom will be an astronaut called Charlie Duke. He has a slow, friendly voice from South Carolina.

[long-pause]

Behind the main room, there are back rooms, full of more experts. They help the people in the main room, like a team behind a team. Bill works in one of these back rooms. He sits at his console, wearing a headset, watching the numbers on his screen. He drinks a lot of coffee. Sometimes he talks quietly into his microphone to the man in the main room. Everything is calm and orderly. Everyone is very focused.

[pause]

{img:back-room-engineers-1969-headsets-paper-charts-coffee-cups-numbers}
The teams at Mission Control work in shifts, day and night, all through the mission. Bill works long shifts, ten or twelve hours at a time. Then he drives home through the quiet streets, eats a late supper and sleeps for a few hours. Carol waits up for him. He tells her a little about the day. Everything is going well, he says. Very well. Then he falls asleep the moment his head touches the pillow.

[long-pause]

The people at Mission Control are mostly young. Many of them are in their twenties. They are engineers and mathematicians, many of them from small towns all over America. They wear white shirts and narrow ties, and some of them wear glasses with black frames. They have worked very hard for years to get ready for this mission. They know every detail of the spacecraft. And they are very proud of their work.

[pause]

{img:engineer-driving-home-night-1969-car-quiet-street-moon-above}
On one of his drives home, Bill looks up through the windscreen at the moon. It is growing bigger every night. Somewhere between here and there, three men are floating in their little spacecraft. He thinks about them, and about their families. He thinks about all the numbers on his screen, and what each one means. Then he parks in his driveway and turns off the engine. For a moment, he sits in the quiet car, just looking up. Then he goes inside to his sleeping family.

[pause]

In the evenings, the families of the engineers sometimes bring food to the space centre. They bring sandwiches and cakes and big flasks of coffee. They leave them at the door, because nobody else is allowed inside. The engineers eat at their consoles, without looking away from their screens. Carol makes Bill a box of sandwiches every day, with an apple and a little note from the children.

[pause]

Sometimes, during quiet moments, the engineers at Mission Control step outside for a few minutes. They stand in the warm Texas night and look up at the sky. The moon is there, shining above the buildings of the space centre. It is strange, they say, to work all day on something so far away. And then to step outside and see it, hanging quietly in the sky.

[pause]

One of the most important tools at Mission Control is the slide rule. It is a kind of ruler with moving parts and numbers on it. Engineers use it to do quick calculations, without a computer. Almost every engineer in the room has one in his shirt pocket. They also use big computers, in another building. But those computers are much less powerful than a modern telephone.

[long-pause]

{img:astronauts-sleeping-bags-floating-tethered-cabin-dim-light-quiet}
What is daily life like for three men in a small spacecraft, on the way to the moon?

[pause]

The astronauts follow a schedule, planned carefully by Mission Control. There are times for work, times for meals and times for sleep. They talk to Houston often by radio. Every morning, the capcom wakes them up and reads them the news from Earth. He tells them about the baseball scores and the weather. Sometimes he tells them a joke, or reads them a funny story from the newspapers.

[long-pause]

To sleep, the astronauts use light sleeping bags. Two of them hang the bags under the couches, and float inside them. They zip themselves in, so they do not drift around. One of them always stays awake, on watch, in case something happens. It is very quiet in the spacecraft at night, except for the hum of the fans and the pumps. The astronauts say they sleep quite well, floating in the dark.

[pause]

{img:astronaut-looking-out-window-earth-crescent-black-space-thoughtful}
Keeping clean is not easy in space. There is no shower and no bath. The astronauts wash with wet cloths and little towels. They brush their teeth with special toothpaste that they can swallow. They shave with special razors and shaving cream. Everything must be done carefully, so that little drops of water and bits of hair do not float around the cabin.

[long-pause]

The astronauts have many jobs to do. They check the systems of the spacecraft. They take readings of the instruments and send them to Houston. They take photographs out of the windows. They fire the engine for a few seconds to correct their course, when Houston asks them to. And they prepare for the landing on the moon, going through the checklists again and again.

[pause]

{img:astronaut-reading-checklist-pencil-floating-instruments-panel}
They also have time to look out of the windows. Michael Collins later wrote about the view. The Earth was tiny and blue and beautiful, he said. It looked fragile, like something that needed to be looked after. The moon was growing bigger and bigger, grey and silver. And all around, there was the deep black of space, full of stars.

[long-pause]

On Earth, millions of people follow the journey every day. Newspapers print stories and drawings of the spacecraft. Television shows special programmes, with experts explaining every step. In schools and offices, people talk about nothing else. In the Netherlands and France, in Japan and India, in Brazil and Kenya, people follow the news. The whole world is watching three men in a little spacecraft.

[pause]

{img:people-around-world-1969-watching-tv-shop-window-crowd-street}
In the family's house in Houston, Susan keeps a diary of the mission. Every evening, she writes down what has happened that day. She draws pictures of the spacecraft and the moon. She cuts out articles from the newspaper and glues them into her book. Danny builds a new model rocket, painted white with black stripes, just like the Saturn V. And Grandma Ruth knits a little blanket, with a picture of the moon on it, for the baby that her neighbour is expecting. A moon baby, she says, will be lucky.

[pause]

The spacecraft also has a little toilet, though it is not very comfortable. There is no flushing water in space. So the astronauts use special bags and tubes. It is not very pleasant, and it takes a long time. The astronauts make jokes about it. Later, when people ask them about the journey, many people want to know about the toilet. The astronauts laugh and say it was the hardest part of the whole mission.

[pause]

During the long hours of travel, the astronauts sometimes talk about their families and their homes. Collins talks about his garden, and the roses he wants to plant. Aldrin talks about running and fishing. Armstrong talks about gliding, a kind of flying without an engine, which he loves. They talk about what they will do when they get home. A long holiday, they all agree.

[pause]

The astronauts also look after their health. They drink plenty of water and try to move around a little every day. A doctor at Mission Control watches their heartbeat and their breathing on his screen. He can tell when they are asleep, and when they are working hard. Everything looks good. The astronauts are healthy and well.

[long-pause]

{img:boy-girl-building-model-rocket-garage-1969-cardboard-paint-father}
For the children of America in the summer of 1969, space is the most exciting thing in the world.

[pause]

All over the country, children play at astronauts. They make helmets from goldfish bowls and spacesuits from silver paper. They build spacecraft from cardboard boxes in their gardens. They count down from ten and jump off the porch steps, shouting liftoff. Toy shops sell model rockets and astronaut dolls, moon maps and space games.

[long-pause]

In the family's garage, Danny and Susan are building a model of the lunar module. They use a cardboard box for the body, and drinking straws for the legs. They cover it with gold paper from a box of chocolates. They make a little ladder from matchsticks. It takes them three whole afternoons. When it is finished, they put it on the kitchen table, and everyone admires it.

[pause]

{img:children-playing-astronauts-backyard-cardboard-spaceship-goldfish-bowl-helmet}
In the evenings, if the sky is clear, Susan sets up her telescope on the lawn. She looks at the moon, which is getting a little bigger each night. She finds the Sea of Tranquillity on her map. That is where the astronauts will land. It is a wide, flat, dark grey area on the moon's face. People long ago thought these dark areas were seas, full of water. Now we know they are plains of old dark rock.

[long-pause]

Danny looks through the telescope too. He wants to see the astronauts. Susan explains that they are much too small to see, even with the best telescope in the world. Danny is disappointed. But then he sees the craters, round and grey, with long shadows. He has never seen them so clearly. He looks for a long time, without saying a word, which is very unusual for Danny.

[pause]

{img:girl-boy-telescope-lawn-night-moon-crescent-dog-lying-grass}
Many children write letters to the astronauts, or to NASA. They write about their dreams of space. They draw pictures of rockets and moons. They ask questions. How do you go to the toilet in space? What does the moon smell like? Will there be ice cream on the moon? NASA receives thousands of letters, and tries to answer some of them.

[long-pause]

At school, the teachers talk about the mission in class. They explain how the rocket works, and why there is no air on the moon. They show pictures and maps. Some classes make their own spacecraft and act out the journey. In the summer holidays, many towns hold space fairs, with talks and displays.

[pause]

{img:kitchen-table-model-lunar-module-gold-paper-family-admiring-evening}
Grandma Ruth watches the children's excitement with a smile. She tells them that when she was their age, people thought it would take hundreds of years to fly to the moon. Some said it could never be done. There was a famous book by a French writer, Jules Verne, about a trip to the moon in a great cannon shell. Everyone thought it was just a fantasy. And now, she says, it is really happening. She shakes her head. I have lived to see wonders, she says.

[pause]

In the town library, Carol sets up a special table about the moon. There are books about the moon and the planets, about rockets and astronauts. There are pictures of the craters and maps of the moon. Children come in every day to borrow the books. Some of them have never been to the library before. Carol is very pleased. She thinks the moon is bringing children to books.

[pause]

Danny and his friends launch their model rockets in the field behind the school. They count down together, loudly. Ten, nine, eight. Then Danny presses the button, and the little rocket shoots up into the sky with a whoosh. It goes up very high, and then a little parachute opens, and it floats gently down. The boys run across the field to catch it. Rocket the dog runs with them, barking.

[pause]

Grandma Ruth helps the children make a calendar of the mission. They draw a picture for each day, on a big sheet of paper on the kitchen wall. Day one: the launch, with a big rocket and lots of fire. Day two: the spacecraft floating in space. Day three: the Earth getting smaller. They fill in each day as the mission goes on. Danny colours in the fire very carefully, in orange and yellow.

[long-pause]

{img:moon-surface-close-craters-grey-spacecraft-window-view-orbit}
On the nineteenth of July, after three days of travel, Apollo 11 reaches the moon.

[pause]

As they come closer, the moon grows bigger and bigger in the windows. At first, it is a silver ball. Then it fills the whole window. The astronauts can see the craters and the mountains, the plains and the long shadows. It is grey and brown and silent. There is no air, no water, no clouds. Nothing moves.

[long-pause]

To go into orbit around the moon, the spacecraft must slow down. So the astronauts fire the big engine at the back of the service module. But they must do this behind the moon, on the far side. On the far side, the moon is between them and the Earth. So the radio cannot reach them. For about half an hour, nobody on Earth can hear them.

[pause]

{img:far-side-moon-dark-spacecraft-engine-firing-no-radio-silence}
At Mission Control, everyone waits in silence. If the engine works well, the spacecraft will come out from behind the moon at exactly the right time. If not, something has gone wrong. The minutes pass slowly. Bill sits at his console, watching the clock. Then the radio crackles. The voice of the astronauts comes through, clear and calm. The engine has worked perfectly. Apollo 11 is in orbit around the moon.

[long-pause]

For the next day, the spacecraft circles the moon, again and again. Each time around takes about two hours. The astronauts look down at the surface and take photographs. They look for the landing place, in the Sea of Tranquillity. They see it, flat and grey, with little craters scattered across it. It looks smooth from up here. But they know there are rocks and craters, big and small.

[pause]

{img:earthrise-over-moon-horizon-blue-earth-rising-grey-surface}
As they come round from the far side, they see something wonderful. The Earth rises over the edge of the moon, blue and white, against the black sky. It is small and bright and beautiful, like a jewel. On Apollo 8, the astronauts took a famous photograph of this moment, called Earthrise. Now the crew of Apollo 11 see it with their own eyes. Everyone they have ever known is on that little blue ball.

[long-pause]

On the morning of the twentieth of July, it is time. Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin float through the tunnel into the lunar module, Eagle. Michael Collins stays in Columbia. They close the hatches between them. Then Collins presses a switch, and the two spacecraft drift apart. Eagle, with its golden foil and its spider legs, floats away below Columbia.

[pause]

{img:lunar-module-eagle-separating-command-module-moon-below-sunlight}
Collins looks at Eagle through his window. You cats take it easy on the lunar surface, he says over the radio. Armstrong answers: See you later. Eagle turns slowly in the sunlight, showing all its sides, so Collins can check that everything is in order. It looks fine. The Eagle has wings, says Armstrong. Then Eagle drops slowly away, toward the moon. And Collins is alone in Columbia, circling above.

[pause]

As they orbit the moon, the astronauts look down at places with strange and beautiful names. The Sea of Crises. The Sea of Fertility. The Sea of Serenity. The Lake of Dreams. These names were given hundreds of years ago, by astronomers looking through the first telescopes. They thought the dark areas were seas of water. Now, at last, people are seeing them up close.

[pause]

In Houston, the family watches the news on television all day. The newsreaders show drawings and models of the spacecraft circling the moon. They explain what will happen next. Carol makes sandwiches for lunch, but nobody leaves the living room. Grandma Ruth knits and listens. Susan follows the path of the spacecraft on her moon map with her finger. And Danny asks, every few minutes, if they have landed yet.

[long-pause]

{img:eagle-descending-toward-moon-surface-engine-glow-craters-below}
On the afternoon of the twentieth of July, 1969, Eagle begins its descent to the moon. It is the most difficult and the most exciting part of the whole journey.

[pause]

Inside Eagle, Armstrong and Aldrin are standing up, side by side, in front of two small triangular windows. They are held in place by straps, like a little harness. There are no seats in the lunar module, to save weight. In front of them, there are panels full of switches, dials and little lights. And below them, through the windows, the grey surface of the moon is coming closer.

[long-pause]

The engine of Eagle fires, to slow it down. Eagle drops slowly toward the moon, tilted a little to one side. The computer is helping to fly it. Aldrin reads out the numbers from the computer: their height and their speed. Armstrong watches the surface through the window. At Mission Control, everyone is sitting very still, listening to every word.

[pause]

{img:inside-lunar-module-armstrong-aldrin-standing-small-window-switches}
Then, a few minutes into the descent, a little yellow light comes on. The computer is showing an alarm. It is called a twelve-oh-two alarm. Nobody is quite sure what it means. Armstrong asks Houston, calmly, what they should do. In Mission Control, a young engineer called Steve Bales looks at his screen. He is only twenty-six. He has studied this kind of alarm before. Behind him, in the back room, an expert called Jack Garman checks his notes. The computer is busy, but it is still working. They decide that it is safe to go on.

[long-pause]

We are go on that alarm, says the capcom. And Eagle keeps on going down. The alarm comes again, and again they say go. In the family's living room in Houston, everyone is sitting very still. Susan is holding Grandma Ruth's hand so tightly that it hurts. Danny is lying flat on the carpet. Carol has her hands over her mouth.

[pause]

{img:mission-control-tense-engineers-watching-screens-quiet-july-20-1969}
As Eagle comes lower, Armstrong looks out of the window at the landing place. And he sees that the computer is taking them toward a big crater, full of large rocks, some as big as cars. It is not a safe place to land. So Armstrong takes control himself. He flies Eagle by hand, like a helicopter, over the crater and the rocks. He looks for a smooth, flat place to land.

[long-pause]

Eagle flies on, over the grey surface, lower and lower. The fuel is running low. At Mission Control, someone starts calling out the time left. Sixty seconds. Armstrong keeps looking for a safe place. Aldrin keeps calling out the numbers, calmly. Forward. Down a little. Thirty seconds. The engine kicks up a cloud of grey dust from the surface. It is hard to see the ground.

[pause]

{img:lunar-module-landing-dust-cloud-surface-shadow-legs-touching}
Then Aldrin says: Contact light. One of the long probes under the legs has touched the moon. Armstrong turns off the engine. There is a moment of complete silence. Then Armstrong's calm voice comes over the radio. Houston, Tranquility Base here. The Eagle has landed.

[pause]

At Mission Control, there is a great breath of relief. Charlie Duke, the capcom, is so happy that his words tumble out. Roger, Tranquility, we copy you on the ground, he says. You got a bunch of guys about to turn blue. We are breathing again. Thanks a lot. In the back room, Bill takes off his glasses and wipes his eyes. And in the living room in Houston, the whole family jumps up and hugs each other, laughing and crying at the same time.

[pause]

Later, people learned that Eagle had landed with only about twenty-five seconds of fuel left for the landing. Armstrong had flown it calmly and carefully, as if he had all the time in the world. His heartbeat, measured by the doctors, went up a lot during those minutes. But his voice never changed. That was the kind of man he was. And that was why he was chosen.

[long-pause]

{img:lunar-module-on-moon-surface-tranquility-base-grey-plain-black-sky}
The Eagle is standing on the moon, in the Sea of Tranquillity. Outside its windows, there is a grey, flat plain, dotted with small craters and rocks. The sky above is completely black, even though the sun is shining. And low over the horizon, there is the Earth, blue and white, far away.

[pause]

The plan says that the astronauts should rest for a few hours before going outside. But they are too excited to sleep. So they ask Houston if they can go out sooner. Houston agrees. First, they must put on their backpacks and their helmets, and check everything very carefully. It takes several hours.

[long-pause]

All over the world, people are waiting in front of their televisions. In America, it is late on Sunday evening. In Europe, it is the middle of the night. In Australia, it is Monday afternoon. People have gathered in their homes, in town squares, in shops and in bars. In some places, families have stayed up all night. About six hundred million people are watching, the biggest television audience in history.

[pause]

{img:family-gathered-tv-late-night-1969-black-white-screen-grandmother-children}
In the family's living room in Houston, nobody is thinking of going to bed. Danny is in his pyjamas, but wide awake. Susan has her diary on her knees, ready to write. Carol has made coffee and hot chocolate. Grandma Ruth is sitting in the armchair, very straight. Bill is still at Mission Control. Neighbours have come over too, to watch together.

[long-pause]

At last, the hatch of Eagle opens. Neil Armstrong crawls out backwards, onto a little porch. He climbs slowly down the ladder on one of Eagle's legs. On the way down, he pulls a ring that switches on a television camera. Suddenly, on televisions all over the world, a grey, ghostly picture appears. It shows the ladder, and a white shape slowly coming down it.

[pause]

{img:armstrong-descending-ladder-lunar-module-ghostly-tv-image-1969}
Armstrong reaches the bottom of the ladder. He steps down onto the big round foot of Eagle's leg. He says the surface looks like a fine powder. Then he lifts his left foot, and puts it down on the moon. It is ten fifty-six in the evening, Houston time.

[long-pause]

And he speaks the words that will be remembered forever. That's one small step for man, one giant leap for mankind.

[pause]

For the first time in history, a human being is standing on another world.

[pause]

{img:first-bootprint-lunar-dust-close-grey-texture-sunlight}
In the family's living room, nobody says anything for a long moment. Then Grandma Ruth begins to cry quietly. Carol puts her arm around her. Susan is writing in her diary, with tears on her cheeks. She writes the date, and the time, and the words. Danny is staring at the screen with his mouth open. And all over the world, in millions of homes, people are doing the same. Looking at a grey, flickering picture, and knowing that they will remember this moment for the rest of their lives.

[pause]

In Europe, many families had set their alarm clocks for the middle of the night. In the Netherlands, it was about four o'clock in the morning. Children were woken up by their parents and wrapped in blankets on the sofa. They watched the grey pictures, half asleep, with their eyes wide. Many of them remember it as one of the first things they ever remember.

[pause]

In Mission Control, everyone is watching the big screen too. For a moment, the engineers stop working and just look. Then they turn back to their consoles. There is still a lot of work to do. In the back room, Bill watches the numbers on his screen, the oxygen and the water in the astronauts' backpacks. Everything is fine. He allows himself a small smile.

[pause]

Before going outside, Buzz Aldrin did something quiet and private. He took out a little kit that his church had given him. He read a few words from the Bible, and took communion, alone in the cabin of Eagle. He did not talk about it on the radio. He just asked everyone listening to pause for a moment, and to give thanks in their own way.

[long-pause]

{img:astronaut-standing-moon-surface-white-suit-gold-visor-reflection}
About twenty minutes later, Buzz Aldrin climbs down the ladder too. He stands beside Armstrong on the moon, and looks around. Beautiful view, he says. Magnificent desolation.

[pause]

Desolation means empty and lonely. And the moon is empty. There are no trees, no grass, no water, no animals. There is only grey dust and rocks, and craters and gentle hills. There is no air, so there is no wind and no sound. The sky is black, but the ground is lit by bright sunshine. The shadows are very sharp and very dark.

[long-pause]

On the moon, everything weighs only one sixth of what it weighs on Earth. So the astronauts feel very light, even in their heavy spacesuits and backpacks. They find that the best way to move is with little hops, like kangaroos. They bounce slowly across the surface, leaving footprints in the fine dust. Aldrin tries running, and jumping, and moving in different ways. He reports everything to Houston.

[pause]

{img:astronauts-hopping-moon-surface-dust-footprints-long-shadows}
The astronauts have a lot of work to do, and only about two and a half hours to do it. They collect rocks and dust, to bring back to Earth. They use little scoops and tongs, and put the samples in special boxes. They set up some scientific instruments. One measures moonquakes, the shaking of the moon. Another is a special mirror, which scientists on Earth can bounce laser light off, to measure the distance to the moon very exactly.

[long-pause]

They also put up an American flag. Because there is no wind on the moon, the flag would hang down limply. So it has a little rod along the top, to hold it out. The ground is hard just under the dust, and it is difficult to push the pole in. But they manage. Then they stand beside it, and Aldrin salutes. Armstrong takes a photograph.

[pause]

{img:flag-planted-moon-surface-astronaut-saluting-lunar-module-behind}
Then the astronauts receive a telephone call. It is from the President of the United States, Richard Nixon, in the White House. He speaks to them over the radio, and his voice is broadcast all over the world. He tells them that for one priceless moment, all the people on Earth are truly one. The astronauts thank him.

[long-pause]

On one of Eagle's legs, there is a little metal plaque. It shows the two halves of the Earth, and it has words engraved on it. Here men from the planet Earth first set foot upon the moon, July 1969 A.D. We came in peace for all mankind. The plaque will stay on the moon forever, on the part of Eagle that stays behind.

[pause]

{img:plaque-lunar-module-leg-engraved-words-earth-hemispheres-close}
The astronauts also leave some special things on the moon. There is a little silicon disc with messages from leaders of many countries, written in tiny letters. There is a little golden olive branch, a symbol of peace. And there are medals and patches, to honour astronauts from America and the Soviet Union who lost their lives in the years before. They are small things. But they show that the journey to the moon was for everyone.

[pause]

The astronauts also take many photographs on the moon. They have special cameras, fixed to the fronts of their suits. Most of the photographs are of Aldrin, because Armstrong is holding the camera most of the time. One of the most famous photographs shows Aldrin standing on the moon. In his gold visor, you can see the reflection of Armstrong, the lunar module and the grey surface around them.

[pause]

Armstrong and Aldrin find that the moon dust is very strange. It is fine like powder, but it sticks to their boots and their suits. It packs down hard when they step on it, and keeps the shape of their footprints perfectly. When they kick it, it flies up in long smooth arcs, and falls down again quickly, because there is no air to hold it up.

[long-pause]

{img:command-module-columbia-alone-orbiting-moon-sunlit-black-space}
While Armstrong and Aldrin walk on the moon, Michael Collins is alone in Columbia, circling high above.

[pause]

Every two hours, Columbia goes all the way around the moon. On the near side, Collins can talk to Houston on the radio. He listens to the news of his friends on the surface. He tries to see Eagle on the ground through his telescope, but it is too small to find. He keeps busy, checking the systems of the spacecraft and preparing for the return of his friends.

[long-pause]

Then, each time around, Columbia goes behind the moon, to the far side. And there, the radio goes silent. For about forty-eight minutes, Collins cannot talk to anyone. He cannot hear anyone. He is more alone than any person has ever been. On the other side of the moon, there are three billion people, and two friends. On this side, there is only Collins, and the stars.

[pause]

{img:far-side-of-moon-dark-stars-command-module-window-solitude-peace}
But Collins does not feel lonely. He later wrote that he felt a kind of awareness and joy. He liked the quiet. He looked out of the window at the stars, which were brighter than anything he had ever seen on Earth. He made himself a cup of hot coffee. He listened to the hum of the spacecraft. And he felt quite at peace.

[long-pause]

On the far side, the moon is dark, because the sun is behind it. Collins cannot see its surface at all. He can only see where it is, because it blocks out the stars. There is a great black shape in front of him, with no stars in it. Then, slowly, the edge of the moon begins to glow. The sun comes up over the moon's horizon, bright and dazzling. And Columbia comes round to the near side again, into the light, and the radio crackles back to life.

[pause]

{img:sunrise-over-lunar-horizon-bright-light-command-module-silhouette}
Collins was not jealous of his friends on the moon. He knew that his job was very important too. Without him, they could not get home. He had to be ready to help them, if anything went wrong with Eagle. He had practised many ways to rescue them. He hoped very much that he would not need to use any of them.

[long-pause]

In Houston, Bill thinks about Collins too. He thinks it must be a strange feeling, to be so far away, all alone. He mentions it to Carol when he comes home for a few hours of sleep. Carol says she thinks Collins must be a very calm man. Bill says that all the astronauts are calm. That is why they were chosen.

[pause]

{img:engineer-kitchen-early-morning-1969-coffee-wife-talking-tired-happy}
Later, Michael Collins wrote a book about his journey. It was one of the best books ever written by an astronaut. He wrote about the beauty of the Earth, seen from far away. He wrote that the Earth looked small and fragile and precious. He said that if world leaders could see the Earth from space, they might learn to get along better. And he wrote that he would not have changed places with anyone.

[pause]

While he is alone, Collins also talks to himself a little, and hums songs. He writes notes in his checklist. Later, people found that he wrote little jokes and comments in the margins. He also wrote, on the wall of the spacecraft, a message about the journey. It was later found, carefully preserved, when the spacecraft was put in a museum.

[pause]

On each orbit, when Columbia comes round from the far side, the Earth rises over the moon's horizon. Collins watches it every time. He never gets tired of the sight. The Earth looks so small and so far away, a little blue and white marble in the blackness. He thinks of his wife and children down there. And he thinks that everything he has ever loved is on that little ball.

[long-pause]

{img:astronauts-inside-lunar-module-resting-hammocks-cramped-moon-dust}
After about two and a half hours on the moon, Armstrong and Aldrin climb back up the ladder into Eagle. They bring with them two boxes of moon rocks and dust, about twenty-two kilograms altogether. They close the hatch and fill the cabin with air again. Then they take off their helmets and gloves.

[pause]

The moon dust gets everywhere. It is on their suits and their boots and their gloves. It has a strange smell, they say, a little like burnt gunpowder, or wet ashes in a fireplace. It is grey and very fine, like flour, and it sticks to everything.

[long-pause]

Now the astronauts must rest, before they leave the moon. They hang little hammocks across the cabin, and try to sleep. But it is not easy. The cabin is small and cold and noisy. There is light coming in through the windows. Armstrong lies with his head on top of the engine cover. They sleep only a little, perhaps a few hours.

[pause]

{img:eagle-ascent-stage-lifting-off-moon-descent-stage-left-behind-flag}
Then it is time to go. The top half of Eagle will fly up from the moon, and leave the bottom half behind, with its legs. There is only one engine for the ascent. It must work. There is no second chance. The astronauts go through their checklists carefully. While checking, Aldrin notices that a small switch has broken off. He finds a felt-tip pen and pushes it into the hole. It works. The switch is fixed.

[long-pause]

The engine fires. And the top part of Eagle lifts off from the moon, leaving the bottom part behind. It climbs quickly up into the black sky, and the moon falls away below. The flag waves a little in the blast of the engine. Then it is still again. On the surface, the footprints and the bottom half of Eagle remain, silent and alone, where they will stay for a very long time.

[pause]

{img:eagle-docking-with-columbia-orbit-moon-below-sunlight-careful}
A few hours later, Eagle meets Columbia, high above the moon. Collins sees the little spacecraft coming up toward him, glinting in the sunlight. He takes many photographs. Then the two spacecraft join together, carefully. Armstrong and Aldrin float back through the tunnel into Columbia. Collins is very glad to see them. He later said he wanted to kiss them on their foreheads, but he shook their hands instead.

[long-pause]

The astronauts move the boxes of moon rocks into Columbia. Then they let Eagle go, floating away into orbit around the moon. They do not need it anymore. And then they fire the big engine of the service module, again behind the moon, out of contact with Houston. It must work perfectly, to send them home. Everyone at Mission Control waits in silence once more.

[pause]

{img:columbia-leaving-moon-orbit-earth-small-blue-far-ahead-journey-home}
The spacecraft comes out from behind the moon right on time. Hey, Charlie boy, says Collins over the radio. The engine has worked perfectly. They are on their way home. At Mission Control, everyone smiles. In the back room, Bill leans back in his chair and closes his eyes for a moment. The hardest parts are over. Now there are only three days of travel, and the landing in the ocean.

[pause]

Back in Houston, Bill's long shift is ending. He drives home in the early morning, as the sky is getting light. The streets are empty and quiet. He parks in his driveway and goes in. Carol is asleep on the sofa, with the television still on. He covers her with a blanket and turns off the television. Then he goes up to look at the children. They are fast asleep. He smiles, and goes to bed.

[pause]

Before they leave the moon, Armstrong and Aldrin look out of the windows one more time. They look at the grey plain, the little craters and the long shadows. They look at the flag, and the instruments they have set up. They look at their own footprints in the dust. They know they will probably never come back. They want to remember it all.

[long-pause]

{img:astronauts-relaxing-cabin-journey-home-earth-growing-window-smiles}
The journey home takes about two and a half days. The spacecraft falls back toward the Earth, faster and faster, pulled by its gravity.

[pause]

The astronauts are tired, but happy. Now they have a little more time to rest and look out of the windows. They watch the moon getting smaller behind them, and the Earth getting bigger in front of them. They talk to Houston and make jokes. They eat their food from little bags. They sleep better than before.

[long-pause]

On the evening before they land, the astronauts make a last television broadcast to the people of Earth. Each of them says a few words. Collins talks about the rocket and all the people who built it. He thanks the people who worked so hard, all over the country. Aldrin talks about what the mission means, for all people everywhere. Armstrong thanks the many people who made it possible. Then he says good night from Apollo 11.

[pause]

{img:last-tv-broadcast-astronauts-floating-camera-earth-window-evening}
On Earth, the family watches the broadcast together. Bill is home for the evening, for once. They sit on the sofa, all squeezed together, with Rocket the dog at their feet. When the astronauts say good night, Danny waves at the television. Grandma Ruth laughs. Susan writes in her diary: Day 8. They are coming home. Tomorrow they land in the sea.

[long-pause]

On the morning of the twenty-fourth of July, Columbia separates from the service module. Only the little cone of the command module is left, with the three astronauts inside. It turns around, so its wide flat bottom faces forward. That bottom is covered with a thick heat shield. Because now comes the last difficult part of the journey: coming back through the air.

[pause]

{img:command-module-reentry-glowing-plasma-trail-atmosphere-dawn-ocean}
The command module hits the top of the Earth's air at enormous speed, about forty thousand kilometres an hour. The air in front of it gets very hot, and glows bright orange, like fire. The heat shield protects the astronauts inside. For a few minutes, the radio cannot get through the glowing air. At Mission Control, everyone waits again. Then the voices come back. And the command module slows down.

[long-pause]

Then the parachutes open. First, two small ones, to steady the spacecraft. Then three huge orange and white parachutes billow out above it. The command module drifts slowly down toward the blue Pacific Ocean. It is early morning, and the sun is rising. On a big ship nearby, called the Hornet, the sailors are watching. And so are millions of people on television.

[pause]

{img:three-parachutes-orange-white-command-module-descending-pacific-sunrise}
The command module splashes down in the ocean, at the end of its long journey. It turns upside down at first, but some balloons inflate and turn it the right way up again. Helicopters fly out from the Hornet. Divers jump into the water and fix a floating collar around the spacecraft. In Houston, Mission Control bursts into cheers and applause. People wave little flags and light cigars. On the big screen, there are the words of President Kennedy, from eight years before. And now, they have come true.

[pause]

On the Hornet, the sailors have been waiting for days. They have painted signs and cleaned the ship from top to bottom. The ship's band has practised its music. A special welcome has been prepared. And the cooks have made a big cake, decorated with a picture of the moon. When the command module splashes down, the whole ship cheers.

[pause]

As the Earth comes closer, the astronauts begin to tidy the cabin. They pack away their things and strap everything down. They put on their helmets and gloves again. They check the parachutes and the heat shield. They have done this in the simulator many times. But this time is real. And this time, at the end, they will be home.

[long-pause]

{img:astronauts-in-quarantine-trailer-window-president-talking-ship-deck}
When the astronauts come out of their spacecraft, they do something unusual. They put on special grey suits and masks, given to them by the divers. Why?

[pause]

Nobody knows for sure whether there are tiny germs on the moon. Scientists think it is very unlikely. But they want to be completely sure. If there were germs on the moon, they might be dangerous on Earth. So the astronauts must be kept apart from other people, for three weeks. This is called quarantine.

[long-pause]

The astronauts are lifted into a helicopter and flown to the Hornet. There, they walk straight into a special trailer, like a little caravan, sealed tight. Inside, there are beds and chairs, a little kitchen and a shower. A doctor and an engineer stay with them. The trailer has a big window. Through the window, President Nixon, who has come to the ship, talks to the astronauts and welcomes them home. He says that this is the greatest week since the beginning of the world.

[pause]

{img:quarantine-trailer-ship-deck-hornet-sailors-cheering-sunny-pacific}
The trailer is taken by ship and plane back to Houston. There, the astronauts move into a bigger quarantine building, at the space centre. They stay there for almost three weeks. They are examined by doctors every day. The moon rocks are kept there too, and studied by scientists. Some scientists even put moon dust on plants and in little tanks with mice, to see if it does them any harm. It does not.

[long-pause]

During the quarantine, the astronauts write reports and answer questions about the mission. They are also able to rest. They play cards and watch films. They talk to their families through a window, and by telephone. Neil Armstrong celebrates his thirty-ninth birthday in quarantine, on the fifth of August. The staff make him a big cake.

[pause]

{img:neil-armstrong-birthday-cake-quarantine-room-staff-window-smiles}
At home, the families wait. Janet Armstrong, Joan Aldrin and Pat Collins have been watching every moment of the mission. They have had reporters outside their houses for weeks. They are very happy, and very tired. Now they visit their husbands at the quarantine window. The children press their noses against the glass and wave.

[long-pause]

On the tenth of August, the quarantine ends. The astronauts walk out of the building, into the warm Texas evening. Their families are waiting for them. They hug their wives and children, at last. They drive home, and sleep in their own beds. After all the excitement, it is good to be home.

[pause]

{img:astronaut-family-reunion-driveway-evening-children-running-1969}
In the family's house in Clear Lake, there is a celebration too. Bill has a few days off at last. They have a big barbecue in the backyard, with all the neighbours. There are hamburgers and hot dogs, corn on the cob and watermelon. The children run through the sprinklers in the warm evening. And when the moon comes up over the trees, everyone stops and looks up at it, and raises a glass. To the moon, says Bill. And everybody says it with him.

[pause]

The scientists who study the moon rocks are very excited. Some of the rocks are dark and glassy. Some are full of tiny bubbles, from ancient volcanoes. Some are older than any rocks found on Earth. The scientists look at them through microscopes and test them in many ways. They will study them for years. Some pieces are sent to scientists in other countries too.

[pause]

The scientists also study the dust that the astronauts bring back on their suits. It is grey and very fine, with tiny glassy beads in it, like little marbles. The beads were made long ago, when meteorites crashed into the moon and melted the rock. Under the microscope, they shine in many colours. The scientists say they are very beautiful.

[long-pause]

{img:ticker-tape-parade-new-york-1969-astronauts-open-car-paper-falling}
On the thirteenth of August, the astronauts begin a great tour, to thank the people of America. It is one of the biggest celebrations the country has ever seen.

[pause]

On a single day, they fly to three cities. First, they go to New York. There, they ride in open cars through the streets of Manhattan. Millions of people line the streets to cheer them. From the windows of the tall buildings, people throw down paper, ripped into little pieces, and long streamers. It is called a ticker tape parade. The paper falls like snow, filling the air between the skyscrapers.

[long-pause]

In New York, they also visit the United Nations, where people from all the countries of the world meet. Then they fly to Chicago, for another parade. In the evening, they fly to Los Angeles, for a great dinner in their honour. It is a very long day. But the astronauts smile and wave the whole time.

[pause]

{img:chicago-parade-1969-crowds-astronauts-waving-confetti-sunny}
Then, a few weeks later, they set off on a journey around the world. In forty-five days, they visit twenty-four countries. They go to Mexico and Brazil, to Spain and France, to the Netherlands and Britain, to India and Japan and Australia, and many more. Everywhere they go, huge crowds come out to see them. People wave flags and cheer, and children give them flowers.

[long-pause]

The astronauts are surprised by what people say to them. Everywhere, people do not say: you did it. They say: we did it. People all over the world feel that the journey to the moon belongs to them too. It was a moment that belonged to everyone, wherever they lived. For a little while, the whole world felt like one family.

[pause]

{img:world-tour-astronauts-crowd-flags-many-countries-children-flowers}
In the Netherlands, the astronauts visit Amsterdam and The Hague. Crowds line the canals and the streets. Children wave little paper flags. Many Dutch people still remember that day, many years later. They remember standing on their fathers' shoulders, trying to see the men who had walked on the moon.

[long-pause]

In Houston, life slowly goes back to normal. Bill goes back to work at Mission Control. There is already another mission to prepare: Apollo 12, which will fly in November. Carol goes back to her work in the library. Grandma Ruth goes home to Kansas, at the end of the summer. Before she leaves, she gives Susan a present. It is the knitted blanket with the moon on it. I made it for the neighbour's baby, she says, but I made two. One for the baby, and one for my granddaughter, who will go to the moon one day.

[pause]

{img:grandmother-giving-knitted-moon-blanket-girl-porch-goodbye-1969}
Susan holds the blanket close. She does not know if she will ever go to the moon. But she knows that she will study the stars, and the planets, and everything about space. She will work as hard as the people at Mission Control. And she will never, ever forget the summer of 1969.

[pause]

In each country, the astronauts receive gifts and honours. They are given medals and keys to cities. Children sing songs for them and give them drawings. Old people come to shake their hands. In some places, the crowds are so big that the cars can hardly move. The astronauts are very tired by the end of the tour. But they say it was one of the most moving experiences of their lives.

[pause]

Back in Houston, there is a parade for the astronauts too, through the streets of the city. Bill takes the whole family. They stand on the pavement with thousands of other people, waving little flags. When the open cars come past, Danny shouts and waves as loudly as he can. Susan just watches, smiling. And she thinks she sees Michael Collins look straight at her, and wave.

[long-pause]

{img:apollo-12-astronauts-moon-surveyor-probe-later-mission-1969}
Apollo 11 was not the last journey to the moon. In the three and a half years that followed, there were six more missions.

[pause]

In November 1969, Apollo 12 landed on the moon, in a place called the Ocean of Storms. Its astronauts landed close to an old unmanned spacecraft, which had landed there two years before. They walked over to it and took some pieces back to Earth. Then came Apollo 13, in April 1970. Something went wrong on the way to the moon. An oxygen tank failed. The astronauts could not land. But with the help of Mission Control, they came home safely. It became one of the most famous rescue stories in history.

[long-pause]

Then there were Apollo 14, 15, 16 and 17. On the later missions, the astronauts stayed longer on the moon, up to three days. They drove around in a little electric car, called the lunar rover. They explored mountains and valleys and craters. They collected many more rocks. On Apollo 17, in December 1972, one of the astronauts was a geologist, a scientist who studies rocks. He was the first scientist to walk on the moon.

[pause]

{img:lunar-rover-electric-car-moon-mountains-astronaut-driving-later}
Altogether, twelve men walked on the moon, between 1969 and 1972. They brought back three hundred and eighty-two kilograms of moon rocks and dust. Scientists are still studying them today. The rocks have taught us that the moon is very old, about four and a half billion years. And they suggest that it may have been formed when a smaller planet crashed into the young Earth, long ago.

[long-pause]

After Apollo 17, nobody went back to the moon for a very long time. The missions were very expensive. And America and the other countries turned to other things in space. They built space stations, where astronauts could live and work for months. They sent robot spacecraft to all the planets. They built great telescopes in space, which can see the most distant stars and galaxies.

[pause]

{img:international-space-station-orbit-earth-blue-solar-panels-today}
Today, there is the International Space Station, high above the Earth. Astronauts from many countries live there together, including Americans and Russians, who were once rivals in the race to the moon. And now, more than fifty years after Apollo 11, people are planning to go back to the moon again. The new programme is called Artemis. Artemis was the twin sister of Apollo, in the old Greek stories. And this time, the first woman will walk on the moon.

[long-pause]

What happened to the three astronauts of Apollo 11? None of them ever flew in space again. Neil Armstrong became a teacher of engineering at a university in Ohio. He lived quietly with his family and did not like being famous. He passed away in 2012. Buzz Aldrin wrote books and talked about the future of space travel all his life. Michael Collins became the director of a great museum of air and space in Washington. He also passed away, in 2021.

[pause]

{img:air-and-space-museum-columbia-command-module-display-visitors-today}
And what about Susan, the girl with the telescope in Houston? We can only imagine. Perhaps she studied science at university, and became an astronomer. Perhaps she worked at NASA, like her father, in the same room at Mission Control. And perhaps, many years later, she sat in that room and helped to send a new spacecraft back to the moon. With a knitted blanket with a moon on it, folded on her chair.

[pause]

The last man to walk on the moon was Gene Cernan, the commander of Apollo 17. Before he climbed back into his spacecraft, he wrote the initials of his daughter in the moon dust. Then he said a few words. He said they were leaving as they came, with peace and hope for all mankind. That was in December 1972. Nobody has walked on the moon since then.

[pause]

Many children who watched Apollo 11 on television grew up to become scientists and engineers. Some became astronauts themselves. They say that watching the moon landing changed their lives. It made them want to explore, to learn and to build. And today, they are the ones teaching a new generation of children about space, and getting ready to go back to the moon.

[long-pause]

{img:moon-surface-tranquility-base-descent-stage-footprints-flag-still-today}
Today, more than fifty years later, the place where Apollo 11 landed is still there, on the moon, just as the astronauts left it.

[pause]

There is no wind and no rain on the moon. So nothing blows away and nothing washes away. The bottom half of Eagle still stands on its four legs, in the Sea of Tranquillity. The little plaque is still on its leg, with its words of peace. The instruments are still there, and the bags and tools the astronauts left behind. And the footprints of Armstrong and Aldrin are probably still there too, in the fine grey dust. They may stay there for millions of years.

[long-pause]

The flag is probably no longer standing. When Eagle took off, Aldrin saw it fall over in the blast of the engine. And over many years, the strong sunlight on the moon has probably made its colours fade to white. But the place is still there, and spacecraft orbiting the moon have taken photographs of it. In the pictures, you can see the bottom of Eagle, and the dark paths where the astronauts walked.

[pause]

{img:telescope-observatory-laser-beam-to-moon-night-scientists-today}
The special mirror that the astronauts left on the moon is still working. Scientists on Earth still shine laser beams at it, from big telescopes. The light travels to the moon, bounces off the mirror and comes back. By measuring how long the light takes, scientists can measure the distance to the moon very exactly, to within a few millimetres. They have found that the moon is slowly moving away from the Earth, by about four centimetres every year.

[long-pause]

In museums, you can see things from the Apollo 11 mission. The command module Columbia is in the National Air and Space Museum in Washington. You can stand next to it and look at the scorch marks on its heat shield. You can see how small it is, and imagine the three men inside it. There are moon rocks in museums too. In some museums, you are even allowed to touch a small piece of moon rock.

[pause]

{img:child-touching-moon-rock-museum-display-glass-wonder-today}
At the Kennedy Space Center in Florida, you can see a real Saturn V rocket. It lies on its side, in a huge hall, because it is too tall to stand up indoors. You can walk all along it, from the giant engines at the bottom to the little spacecraft at the top. It takes a long time to walk from one end to the other. People who see it say they cannot believe how big it is.

[long-pause]

In Houston, the old Mission Control room has been carefully restored, just as it was in 1969. The consoles and the screens, the chairs and the ashtrays, even the coffee cups are there. Visitors can sit behind a glass wall and look at it. It is quiet now. But you can imagine the people who once sat there, listening to every word, on the day the Eagle landed.

[pause]

{img:restored-mission-control-room-1969-consoles-empty-quiet-visitors-glass-today}
And every year on the twentieth of July, people all over the world remember the first landing on the moon. Some go outside and look up at the moon. Some watch the old black and white pictures again. And some tell their children and grandchildren where they were, on that summer night in 1969, when they saw a human being step onto another world.

[pause]

People still love to look at the moon through telescopes. On clear nights, amateur astronomers set up their telescopes in their gardens and in parks. They invite children and neighbours to look. Through a good telescope, you can see the Sea of Tranquillity very clearly. You cannot see the lunar module, because it is much too small. But you can see the place where it landed. And that is wonderful enough.

[pause]

Some of the moon rocks brought back by Apollo 11 were given as gifts to every country in the world. Each country received a few tiny pieces, set in a clear plastic ball, with its own flag. Many of these gifts are still kept in museums. In the Netherlands, there is one in a museum too. Visitors look at the little grey pieces of the moon, and think about the long journey they made.

[long-pause]

{img:houston-backyard-summer-night-1969-moon-high-family-lawn-chairs-quiet}
So we leave the summer of 1969 tonight, on a warm evening in July, as the night falls.

[pause]

In the backyard of the family's house in Houston, the air is warm and soft. Crickets are singing in the grass. Fireflies blink on and off among the bushes. The sprinkler has been turned off, and the lawn smells fresh and green. The family has brought their lawn chairs out onto the grass, to sit together in the cool of the evening.

[long-pause]

Bill is home from Mission Control for the night. He is sitting in his chair, with his feet up, and his eyes half closed. Carol sits beside him, with a glass of iced tea. Grandma Ruth is in her rocking chair, which the children have carried out from the porch. She is rocking slowly, humming an old song from her days on the farm in Kansas. Rocket the dog is lying on the grass at her feet, fast asleep.

[pause]

{img:boy-asleep-lawn-chair-model-rocket-hands-fireflies-summer-night}
Danny has fallen asleep in his chair, still holding his model rocket. His mouth is a little open, and he is snoring softly. Carol puts a light blanket over him. Susan is lying on her back on the grass, looking up at the sky. Her telescope stands beside her, but she is not using it now. She just wants to look.

[long-pause]

The moon is high in the sky tonight, bright and silver. Its light falls softly on the lawns and the roofs and the trees. Somewhere up there, on the Sea of Tranquillity, there are footprints in the dust. There is a little golden spacecraft leg, with a plaque that says we came in peace. And the silence of the moon is all around them, deep and complete.

[pause]

{img:girl-lying-grass-looking-up-moon-stars-telescope-beside-quiet}
Susan thinks about the three men who went there. She thinks about the thousands of people who helped them. She thinks about her father, sitting at his console, watching his numbers. She thinks about the six hundred million people who watched, all over the world, all looking up at the same moon. And she thinks that one day, perhaps, she will go there too.

[long-pause]

Grandma Ruth stops rocking and looks up at the moon. When I was a little girl, she says softly, I used to look at the moon from my bedroom window on the farm. My mother told me it was made of cheese. Everyone laughs quietly. And now, she says, men have walked on it. She shakes her head slowly. I have lived to see wonders. Then she closes her eyes, and goes on rocking, slowly, in the moonlight.

[pause]

The crickets go on singing. A car passes slowly in the street, and then it is quiet. The fireflies blink in the dark garden. And high above, the moon shines down on the sleeping houses of Houston, and on the whole wide world below.

[pause]

If you enjoyed this journey with Apollo 11 in July 1969, you are warmly invited to subscribe, so that another calm story from the past is always close by, whenever you need one.

[pause]

{img:moon-night-sky-earth-houses-silhouette-1969-stars-peaceful-calm}
There should be another quiet story waiting on the screen now, if you are still awake.

[pause]

Sleep well, and good night.
