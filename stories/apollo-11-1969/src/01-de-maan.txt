Tonight we go back to the summer of 1969. We go to America, in the month of July. And we look up at the moon.

--

It is a warm summer night on the coast of Florida. The air is soft and smells of the sea. Crickets are singing in the long grass. Palm trees move gently in the breeze. And over the dark ocean, a big round moon is rising slowly, pale and silver. Its light makes a shining path across the water, all the way to the beach.

People have looked up at the moon for as long as there have been people. They have told stories about it and sung songs about it. They have used it to count the months and to find their way at night. They have wondered what it is made of, and whether anyone lives there. And for thousands of years, they have dreamed of going there. In July 1969, three men are about to do it.

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

--

{img:saturn-v-rocket-launch-pad-night-floodlights-florida-1969}
A few kilometres from the beach, a giant rocket is standing on a launch pad, lit by bright white lights. It is taller than a thirty-storey building. It is the biggest rocket ever built. In a few days, it will carry three astronauts away from the Earth, toward the moon. Their mission is called Apollo 11. And if everything goes well, two of them will walk on the moon.

Tonight we will follow the journey of Apollo 11, from beginning to end. We will meet the three astronauts, and learn about their lives and their families. We will see the giant rocket, and the crowds who came to watch it go. We will follow the spacecraft across the dark space between the Earth and the moon. We will hear the voices from Mission Control in Houston. And we will watch, as the whole world watched, as the first person stepped onto the moon.

{img:houston-suburb-1969-family-house-front-yard-evening-station-wagon}
We will also meet an ordinary family in Houston, Texas, where the mission is controlled. The father works as an engineer at Mission Control. The mother looks after the house and the children. Their daughter loves the stars and dreams of space. Their son builds model rockets in the garage. And their grandmother, who was born when there were not even aeroplanes, cannot quite believe what is happening.

--

There will be some tense moments in this story, because the journey was difficult and new. Nobody had ever done it before. But mostly, it is a story of calm and careful people, working together. It is a story of courage and patience, of curiosity and wonder. And it is the story of how, for a few days in July 1969, the whole world looked up at the moon together.

{img:family-backyard-looking-up-at-moon-summer-night-1969-telescope}
So let us begin in a backyard in Houston, on a warm summer evening in July 1969. A girl of twelve is standing on the grass, looking up at the moon through a small telescope. Her little brother is sitting beside her, asking a hundred questions. Their father is standing behind them, with his hands in his pockets, smiling. In a few days, he will be helping to send three men to that very moon. And the girl can hardly wait.

On the beach, a few families have already set up little tents and camping chairs. They have come early, to get a good place to watch the launch. A father is cooking hot dogs on a little grill. Children are running along the edge of the waves with a flashlight. An old man sits in a folding chair, looking at the rocket across the water through a pair of binoculars. Nobody wants to miss a single moment.

The moon has always been the closest of all the things in the sky. It is about three hundred and eighty-four thousand kilometres away. That sounds very far, but it is much closer than the sun or the planets. If you could drive a car to the moon, at the speed of a car on a motorway, it would take about six months. In July 1969, the astronauts will get there in three days.
