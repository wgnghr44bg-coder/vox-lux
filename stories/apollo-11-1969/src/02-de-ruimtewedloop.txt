How did people come to go to the moon? The story begins about twelve years before, in 1957.

In those years, the world was divided. On one side were the United States and their friends. On the other side was the Soviet Union, a great country that included Russia. The two sides did not trust each other. They did not fight a real war. But they competed in everything: in weapons, in science, in sport, and in space. This time is called the Cold War.

--

In October 1957, the Soviet Union launched a small silver ball into space. It was called Sputnik. It was the first man-made object to go around the Earth. It was only about the size of a beach ball. But it sent out little beeping radio signals that people all over the world could hear on their radios. People in America looked up at the night sky and felt surprised and a little worried. The race to space had begun.

{img:yuri-gagarin-smiling-helmet-1961-first-man-in-space-calm}
In April 1961, the Soviet Union did something even more amazing. They sent the first person into space. His name was Yuri Gagarin. He was a young pilot with a big, friendly smile. He flew once around the whole Earth, in a little round spacecraft, in less than two hours. Then he came back down safely. He became a hero all over the world.

--

A month later, the young American president, John F. Kennedy, made a famous speech. He said that America should land a man on the moon, and bring him safely back to Earth, before the end of the decade. That meant before the end of 1969. Many people thought it was impossible. In those days, America had only sent one person into space, for fifteen minutes. Nobody really knew how to get to the moon.

{img:president-kennedy-speech-rice-stadium-1962-crowd-sunny-podium}
The next year, Kennedy gave another speech, in a big stadium in Houston. He said: We choose to go to the moon. We choose to go to the moon in this decade, not because it is easy, but because it is hard. The crowd cheered. And all over America, thousands of people began to work on the great project.

--

The space agency of America is called NASA. In the years that followed, NASA grew very big. Four hundred thousand people worked on the Apollo program, in factories and offices and laboratories all over the country. There were engineers and scientists, builders and seamstresses, cooks and computer programmers. They built rockets and spacecraft, spacesuits and computers. They worked very long hours, often late into the night.

{img:nasa-engineers-1960s-office-drawing-boards-slide-rules-white-shirts}
Step by step, they learned what they needed to know. First, they sent single astronauts into space, in a programme called Mercury. Then they sent two astronauts together, in a programme called Gemini. They learned how to stay in space for many days, how to walk outside a spacecraft, and how to meet another spacecraft in orbit. Then came Apollo. Apollo 8 flew around the moon at Christmas 1968, and came back safely. Apollo 10 went very close to the moon in May 1969. And now, at last, it was the turn of Apollo 11.

In America, schools changed after Sputnik. Children were encouraged to study science and mathematics. New schools and laboratories were built. Young people dreamed of becoming scientists and engineers. Many of the young engineers who later worked at Mission Control were children when Sputnik flew. They remembered standing in their gardens at night, looking up at the sky, trying to see the little moving star.

The space race also had sad moments. In January 1967, three American astronauts lost their lives in a fire, during a test on the ground. Their names were Gus Grissom, Ed White and Roger Chaffee. Some Soviet cosmonauts lost their lives too, in those years. The engineers worked very hard to make the spacecraft safer afterwards. And the astronauts of Apollo 11 never forgot the friends who had gone before them.
