Constantinople is protected by great walls. On the land side, to the west, there is a triple line of walls. They were built almost six hundred years before our story, by an emperor called Theodosius. There is a deep moat, then a low wall, then a high wall with nearly a hundred tall towers. They stretch for six kilometres, from the Sea of Marmara to the Golden Horn. Along the sea, there are more walls. No enemy has ever broken through them.

The walls have many gates. The grandest is called the Golden Gate. It is made of white marble, and long ago its doors were covered with gold. The emperors ride in through the Golden Gate when they come home from their victories. The people line the streets to cheer them.

--

Inside the walls, the city is a maze of streets and squares. The most important street is called the Mese, which means the middle street. It runs from the Golden Gate all the way to the great palace and Hagia Sophia. It is wide and paved with stone. On both sides, there are covered walkways with columns, called colonnades. Under the colonnades there are shops of every kind.

{img:forum-of-constantine-round-square-tall-column-statue-crowds-1000ad}
Along the Mese, there are several great squares, called forums. Each forum has a tall column in the middle, with a statue on top. The Forum of Constantine is round, and its column is made of purple stone. The Forum of Theodosius has a column carved with a spiral of pictures, showing old battles. In the forums, people meet and talk, buy and sell. Ceremonies and processions pass through them.

--

Away from the main streets, the city is quieter. There are narrow lanes winding up and down the hills. There are houses of brick and stone, two or three storeys high, with balconies of wood. There are small churches on every corner, with little domes. There are fountains where people fetch water. There are monasteries with walled gardens, and orchards and vegetable plots.

{img:narrow-byzantine-street-houses-wooden-balconies-small-church-dome-fountain}
There are seven hills in Constantinople, like in old Rome. From the tops of the hills, you can see the water all around. You can see the ships on the Golden Horn and the Bosphorus. On a clear day, you can see across the water to Asia. On the other side of the Bosphorus, there are more towns and villages, green hills and gardens.

--

The city has many harbours along its shores. Ships bring in grain from Egypt and Thrace, to feed the huge population. They bring in wine and olive oil, timber and stone. Fishermen bring in fish every morning, from the rich waters of the Bosphorus. There are great warehouses near the harbours, full of grain and goods.

{img:constantinople-harbour-quay-unloading-amphorae-grain-sacks-workers-sunny}
Every neighbourhood of the city has its own character. There is a quarter of the perfume makers, near the palace, so the emperor can smell their sweet scents. There is a quarter of the silversmiths, and one of the bronze workers. There are quarters where Italian merchants live, and quarters for Jewish families, Armenians and Syrians. People from many lands live side by side in the Queen of Cities. And all of them are proud to call it home.

At night, the city becomes quiet. The shops close their shutters, and the markets empty. Watchmen walk the streets with lanterns, keeping an eye on things. In the churches and monasteries, monks sing their night prayers. Oil lamps burn in front of icons on street corners. And the only sounds are the barking of dogs and the bells of the churches, ringing the hours of the night.

In spring and summer, the city is full of flowers. There are roses in the gardens of the monasteries, and lilies in the palace courtyards. Vines climb over the walls of the houses, with bunches of grapes in late summer. Fig trees grow in every little yard. And on the hillsides, there are orchards of almonds and cherries, white and pink with blossom in early spring.
