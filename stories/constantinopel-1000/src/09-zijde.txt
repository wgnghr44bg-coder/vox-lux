Silk is one of the great treasures of Constantinople. And it has a wonderful story.

For hundreds of years, silk came only from China, far away to the east. Nobody else knew how to make it. The Chinese kept the secret very carefully. Silk was carried along the long roads across Asia, by camel and by horse. It was very rare and very expensive. Only the very rich could buy it.

--

Then, about four hundred and fifty years before our story, something happened. The emperor Justinian was ruling in Constantinople. Two monks came to see him. They had been to the far east, they said, and they had learned the secret of silk. Silk is made by little worms, they told him, which eat the leaves of the mulberry tree. The emperor asked them to bring some of these worms back.

{img:two-monks-walking-long-road-hollow-walking-canes-mountains-east}
So the monks went back to the east. And a few years later, they returned. They had hidden silkworm eggs inside their hollow walking sticks, made of bamboo. From those few eggs, the silk industry of the empire began. Mulberry trees were planted. Silkworms were raised. And soon, Constantinople was making its own silk.

--

By the year 1000, the silk of Constantinople is famous all over the world. Some of the finest silk is made in the imperial workshops, inside the palace. Only the emperor's own weavers may make the very best kinds. There is silk dyed with a special purple colour, made from tiny sea snails. It takes thousands of snails to make a little dye. Only the emperor and his family may wear the deepest purple silk. It is called the imperial purple.

{img:dyers-vats-purple-dye-sea-snails-workers-cloth-drying-sunlight}
The silk weavers of Constantinople make wonderful patterns. They weave pictures of lions and eagles, elephants and griffins, in gold and silver thread. They weave patterns of flowers and vines and circles. Some of these silks were given as gifts to foreign kings. Pieces of them are still kept today, in the treasuries of old churches in Europe. They have lasted for a thousand years.

--

Grandmother Anna was once a weaver, but of linen, not silk. She tells Zoe about the silk weavers she knew when she was young. They worked in long rooms with big wooden looms, she says. They worked very slowly, thread by thread. One piece of fine silk with a pattern could take months to weave. The weavers were very skilled, and very proud of their work. Some of them could weave a lion so real that you thought it would roar.

{img:grandmother-showing-girl-weaving-small-loom-linen-thread-window}
Zoe asks her grandmother to teach her to weave. So, in the afternoons, Grandmother Anna sets up a little loom by the window. She shows Zoe how to put the threads on the loom. She shows her how to pass the shuttle back and forth, and how to press the threads together. Zoe's first piece is crooked and uneven. But her grandmother says it is very good for a first try. And she smiles, remembering her own first piece, long, long ago.

In the silk workshops of the city, many of the workers are women. They unwind the silk from the little cocoons, and spin it into thread. They dye the thread in big vats of colour. Some of them weave on the great looms. It is skilled and careful work. The best silk workers are honoured and well paid. And their silk is sent all over the world.

Zoe practises her weaving every afternoon. Slowly, her pieces get straighter and more even. After a few weeks, she weaves a little band of linen, with a simple pattern of blue stripes. She gives it to her mother, who ties it around her waist as a belt. Eirene wears it every day after that. And whenever a customer admires it, she says proudly that her daughter made it.
