Today, the city of Constantinople is called Istanbul. It is the biggest city in Turkey, with about fifteen million people. It is still a city on the edge of two worlds, with one part in Europe and one part in Asia.

The Bosphorus is still busy with ships. Big cargo ships sail slowly through the strait, from the Black Sea to the Mediterranean. Little ferries go back and forth between Europe and Asia, all day long. Fishermen still stand on the bridges over the Golden Horn, with their long fishing rods. And the seagulls still cry over the water.

--

Hagia Sophia still stands in the middle of the old city. It has been a church, then a mosque, then a museum, and now a mosque again. Visitors from all over the world come to see it. They walk through the great doors and look up at the dome. And, like the messengers from Kiev a thousand years ago, many of them say they have never seen anything like it.

{img:hagia-sophia-interior-today-dome-light-visitors-looking-up-today}
The great cistern near Hagia Sophia is open to visitors too. You can walk down the steps into the cool, dark, underground hall. Wooden walkways lead between the rows of columns, standing in the water. Soft lights shine on the columns and the water. Fish still swim between them. It is quiet and peaceful down there, far below the busy streets.

--

The Hippodrome is now a long, open square, with trees and benches. The old obelisk from Egypt still stands there, after more than fifteen hundred years. Nearby, there is a bronze column shaped like three twisted snakes, which is even older. Children play in the square, and families stroll in the evening. It is hard to imagine the roaring crowds and the thundering chariots of long ago.

{img:hippodrome-square-today-obelisk-trees-families-evening-istanbul-today}
In the old streets of Istanbul, there are still bakeries, everywhere. They still bake bread in big ovens early in the morning. The smell of fresh bread still drifts through the streets at dawn. People still buy round loaves and rolls on their way to work. And street sellers still sell rings of bread covered with sesame seeds, from little carts and trays.

--

The great covered market, the Grand Bazaar, was built later, by the Ottomans. But it carries on the old tradition of the markets of the Mese. It has thousands of little shops, under painted roofs. There are shops selling carpets and silk, gold and silver, spices and sweets. Merchants still sit in front of their shops, inviting customers in for a glass of tea. And people still bargain and laugh, as they did a thousand years ago.

{img:istanbul-bakery-today-dawn-fresh-bread-sesame-rings-street-seller-today}
In the evenings, the people of Istanbul still love to walk by the water. They sit in little cafés on the shores of the Bosphorus, drinking tea and watching the ships go by. The sun sets behind the domes and minarets of the old city, turning the sky gold and pink. The lights of the bridges come on, one by one. And the city on the edge of two worlds glows softly in the evening, as it has for many hundreds of years.

In the old neighbourhoods of Istanbul, there are still little churches from the time of the Byzantine Empire. Some are now mosques, and some are still churches. Some have been turned into museums. They are often hidden away in narrow streets, between ordinary houses. If you look carefully, you can see their old brick walls and little domes, still standing after a thousand years.
