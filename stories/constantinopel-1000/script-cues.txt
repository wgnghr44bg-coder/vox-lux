In the year 1000, Constantinople is the biggest and richest city in all of Europe. Perhaps three or four hundred thousand people live here. Constantinople is the capital of an empire that we call the Byzantine Empire. But the people who live there do not call it that. They call themselves Romans. In the year 1000, the empire of the Romans of Constantinople is large and strong. It stretches from the south of Italy in the west to the mountains of Armenia in the east. It includes Greece and the islands of the Aegean Sea. It includes most of what is now Turkey. And all of it is ruled from Constantinople.

[pause]

The emperor in the year 1000 is called Basil the Second. He is about forty-two years old. He has been emperor since he was a young man. He is a serious, hard-working man who does not care much for fine clothes or feasts. He spends much of his time away from the city, with his armies on the borders. He never married. People say he is married to the empire.

[long-pause]

The empire has a very well organised government. There are thousands of officials, who keep records and collect taxes. They wear special robes and carry titles that show their rank. Everything is written down carefully, on parchment and papyrus. There are laws for almost everything, from the price of bread to the work of the silk weavers. The government is complicated, slow and careful. Even today, we sometimes call a very complicated system byzantine.

The empire is also very Christian. There are churches and monasteries everywhere. The emperor is seen as God's chosen ruler on earth. The head of the church is the patriarch of Constantinople. He leads the great ceremonies in Hagia Sophia. Religion is part of every day of life. People pray in the morning and the evening. They cross themselves when they pass a church. And they have icons, holy pictures, in their homes.

The people of the empire speak Greek in daily life. But they still call themselves Romans, in Greek: Romaioi. Educated people read the old Greek writers, like Homer and Plato. Children learn to read from the Psalms and from Homer's poems. There are libraries and schools in the city. There are doctors who study the old Greek books of medicine. There are lawyers who study the old Roman laws.

Constantinople is also a great city for trade. Ships come here from all over the known world. They come from Italy and Egypt, from Syria and the Black Sea. Traders come from Kiev in the north, with furs and honey and wax. Merchants come from the east, with spices and silk and precious stones. Everyone wants to trade with the Queen of Cities.

[long-pause]

The golden coin of the empire is called the nomisma, or the solidus. It is made of pure gold, and it has the face of the emperor on it. It has been made in the same way for almost seven hundred years. People trust it everywhere, from England to India. It is sometimes called the dollar of the Middle Ages.

But for the ordinary people of Constantinople, the great empire seems far away. They care about the price of bread and the weather. They care about their families, their neighbours and their saints. They care about the next chariot race and the next feast day. And our family of bakers is just the same.

Life in the countryside of the empire is very different from life in the city. Most people are farmers, living in small villages. They grow wheat and barley, olives and grapes. They keep sheep and goats, and a few pigs and chickens. They pay taxes to the emperor in coins or in grain. And they go to their little village church every Sunday, to pray and to meet their neighbours.

[pause]

The empire has many enemies on its borders. But in the year 1000, the city itself is safe and peaceful. Its great walls protect it. Its people go about their daily lives, buying and selling, working and praying. The wars are far away, on the edges of the empire. And in the bakery near the Mese, the only battle is the daily battle to bake enough bread for everyone.

Constantinople is protected by great walls. On the land side, to the west, there is a triple line of walls. They were built almost six hundred years before our story, by an emperor called Theodosius. There is a deep moat, then a low wall, then a high wall with nearly a hundred tall towers. They stretch for six kilometres, from the Sea of Marmara to the Golden Horn. Along the sea, there are more walls. No enemy has ever broken through them.

[pause]

The walls have many gates. The grandest is called the Golden Gate. It is made of white marble, and long ago its doors were covered with gold. The emperors ride in through the Golden Gate when they come home from their victories. The people line the streets to cheer them.

[long-pause]

Inside the walls, the city is a maze of streets and squares. The most important street is called the Mese, which means the middle street. It runs from the Golden Gate all the way to the great palace and Hagia Sophia. It is wide and paved with stone. On both sides, there are covered walkways with columns, called colonnades. Under the colonnades there are shops of every kind.

[pause]

{img:forum-of-constantine-round-square-tall-column-statue-crowds-1000ad}
Along the Mese, there are several great squares, called forums. Each forum has a tall column in the middle, with a statue on top. The Forum of Constantine is round, and its column is made of purple stone. The Forum of Theodosius has a column carved with a spiral of pictures, showing old battles. In the forums, people meet and talk, buy and sell. Ceremonies and processions pass through them.

[long-pause]

Away from the main streets, the city is quieter. There are narrow lanes winding up and down the hills. There are houses of brick and stone, two or three storeys high, with balconies of wood. There are small churches on every corner, with little domes. There are fountains where people fetch water. There are monasteries with walled gardens, and orchards and vegetable plots.

There are seven hills in Constantinople, like in old Rome. From the tops of the hills, you can see the water all around. You can see the ships on the Golden Horn and the Bosphorus. On a clear day, you can see across the water to Asia. On the other side of the Bosphorus, there are more towns and villages, green hills and gardens.

[long-pause]

The city has many harbours along its shores. Ships bring in grain from Egypt and Thrace, to feed the huge population. They bring in wine and olive oil, timber and stone. Fishermen bring in fish every morning, from the rich waters of the Bosphorus. There are great warehouses near the harbours, full of grain and goods.

Every neighbourhood of the city has its own character. There is a quarter of the perfume makers, near the palace, so the emperor can smell their sweet scents. There is a quarter of the silversmiths, and one of the bronze workers. There are quarters where Italian merchants live, and quarters for Jewish families, Armenians and Syrians. People from many lands live side by side in the Queen of Cities. And all of them are proud to call it home.

At night, the city becomes quiet. The shops close their shutters, and the markets empty. Watchmen walk the streets with lanterns, keeping an eye on things. In the churches and monasteries, monks sing their night prayers. Oil lamps burn in front of icons on street corners. And the only sounds are the barking of dogs and the bells of the churches, ringing the hours of the night.

[pause]

In spring and summer, the city is full of flowers. There are roses in the gardens of the monasteries, and lilies in the palace courtyards. Vines climb over the walls of the houses, with bunches of grapes in late summer. Fig trees grow in every little yard. And on the hillsides, there are orchards of almonds and cherries, white and pink with blossom in early spring.

Let us meet a family who lives in a busy neighbourhood, a few streets away from the Mese.

[pause]

The father is called Nikolaos. He is a baker, and he is about forty years old. He is a big, strong man with a short beard and floury hands. He gets up every night, long before the sun, to bake bread for the neighbourhood. He has done this since he was a boy, working for his own father. He loves the smell of fresh bread and the warmth of the oven. And he loves to give a little warm roll to the children who come by in the morning.

[pause]

The mother is called Eirene, which means peace. She runs the front of the bakery. She sells the bread at the counter and takes the money. She keeps the accounts with little marks on a wax tablet. She knows every customer by name. She also runs the house and looks after the family. She is cheerful and quick, and she always has time for a chat.

[long-pause]

They have two children. The daughter is called Zoe, which means life. She is eleven years old, with long dark hair and serious eyes. She helps her mother at the counter in the mornings. She loves to go to church and look at the shining mosaics and the icons. She can sit for a long time, just looking at them. She wants to be a painter of icons one day, though she knows that is unusual for a girl.

[pause]

{img:byzantine-girl-looking-up-at-golden-mosaic-church-candles-wonder}
The son is called Leon, which means lion. He is nine years old, and he is never still. He runs errands for his father and delivers bread to the neighbours. He goes to a little school in the mornings, where he is learning to read and write. But what he really loves are the chariot races in the Hippodrome. He supports the Blues, one of the two great racing teams. He knows the names of all the famous charioteers and all the best horses.

[long-pause]

The grandmother lives with them too. She is Eirene's mother, and her name is Anna. She is nearly seventy, with white hair under a dark headscarf. She was once a weaver of linen. Now her eyes are not so good. But she still spins thread every afternoon, sitting by the window. She goes to church every morning, at dawn. And she knows all the saints and all their stories.

[pause]

{img:byzantine-grandmother-spinning-thread-window-headscarf-afternoon-light}
There are two workers in the bakery too. There is a young man called Basil, who helps Nikolaos with the dough and the ovens. And there is an old man called Stephanos, who grinds the flour and carries the sacks. They come every night, long before dawn, and work beside Nikolaos until the morning.

[long-pause]

The family has a neighbour who is very unusual. He is a tall man with long fair hair and a big blond beard. He comes from a cold country far to the north, beyond the sea. His name is Bjorn. He is a soldier in the emperor's special guard, the Varangian Guard. He lives in a house at the end of the street. He buys bread from Eirene every morning. At first, the children were a little afraid of him, because he is so big. But now they know he is gentle and kind.

[pause]

{img:tall-norse-varangian-guardsman-buying-bread-bakery-counter-children-smiling}
Bjorn speaks Greek slowly, with a funny accent. He tells the children stories about his homeland. He tells them about snow that lasts half the year, and about long dark winters. He tells them about forests and lakes, and about his long journey down the great rivers to Constantinople. Leon loves his stories. Zoe is not sure if all of them are true. But she listens anyway, every time.

[pause]

The family also has a cat, a thin grey cat called Psaraki, which means little fish. She got her name because she always waits by the door when Eirene comes home from the fish market. She catches mice in the bakehouse, where there are always crumbs. She sleeps on the warm bricks beside the ovens. She is always covered in a little flour, so she looks more white than grey.

[pause]

Every Sunday, the whole family goes to church together, in their best clothes. After church, they visit relatives or friends, or walk by the sea. Nikolaos does not bake on Sunday nights, so he can rest a little. It is the one day of the week when the whole family can be together for a long time. They all look forward to it.

[long-pause]

{img:byzantine-house-bakery-ground-floor-family-rooms-above-street-1000ad}
The family lives in a tall narrow house of brick and stone, three storeys high. The bakery is on the ground floor, at the front, opening onto the street. The family lives on the floors above.

[pause]

On the ground floor, there is the shop, with a wooden counter at the front. Behind it, there is the bakehouse, with two big ovens of brick. They are shaped like domes, with a small opening at the front. There are long wooden tables for kneading the dough. There are sacks of flour stacked against the walls. And everything is covered with a fine white dust of flour, even the cat.

[long-pause]

A narrow wooden staircase leads up to the family's rooms. On the first floor, there is the main room, where the family eats and spends the evenings. There is a wooden table and benches, and a few chests for clothes and linen. There is a small brazier, a metal bowl of burning charcoal, to keep warm in winter. And there is a little corner with icons, where the family prays.

[pause]

{img:byzantine-family-icon-corner-oil-lamp-icons-mother-praying-evening}
The icon corner is the most special place in the house. On the wall, there are three small icons, painted on wood. One shows the Virgin Mary holding the baby Jesus. One shows Saint Nicholas, the patron saint of Nikolaos. And one shows Saint Anna, the patron saint of the grandmother. In front of the icons, a little oil lamp burns all the time, day and night. Its small flame flickers softly. Every morning and every evening, the family stands in front of the icons and prays.

[long-pause]

On the second floor, there are the bedrooms. Nikolaos and Eirene sleep in one room, on a wooden bed with a mattress of wool. The children share another room. Grandmother Anna has a small room of her own, at the back, where it is quiet. The windows have wooden shutters. In summer, they keep them open at night, to let in the cool breeze from the sea.

[pause]

{img:wooden-balcony-byzantine-house-potted-herbs-view-domes-sea-evening}
At the front of the house, on the second floor, there is a little wooden balcony, sticking out over the street. Grandmother Anna has pots of herbs and flowers there. There is basil and mint, rosemary and little red roses. From the balcony, you can see the street below, busy with people. And between the rooftops, you can see the great dome of Hagia Sophia, and a little glimpse of the blue sea beyond.

[long-pause]

The house has no running water. Every day, someone must fetch water from the fountain at the end of the street. Usually it is Zoe or Leon. They carry it in big clay jars, one in each hand, and bring it back carefully. The water comes from far away, through great channels in the hills. It is clean and cold and good to drink.

[pause]

{img:children-fetching-water-street-fountain-clay-jars-neighbours-morning}
In the evenings, the family eats together at the table. There is always good bread, of course. There is soup of lentils or beans, cheese and olives, and vegetables from the market. On feast days, there is fish or a little meat. Nikolaos says a blessing before the meal. Then everyone eats and talks about the day. Leon talks about the races. Zoe talks about church. Grandmother Anna talks about the saints. And Eirene talks about the customers, and makes everyone laugh.

[pause]

In winter, the house can be cold. The wind blows from the north, across the Black Sea, and it brings rain and sometimes snow. The family gathers around the charcoal brazier in the evenings, with blankets over their knees. But the bakery is always warm, because of the ovens. On the coldest nights, Leon and Zoe like to sit in the bakehouse with their father, watching him work.

[pause]

In the mornings, Grandmother Anna waters the plants on the balcony. She talks to them as she works, as if they were old friends. The pigeons come and sit on the railing, hoping for crumbs. She always has a few in her pocket for them. Leon says the pigeons know her better than they know anyone else in the street. She laughs and says that is because she is the only one who feeds them.

[long-pause]

{img:byzantine-bakery-night-kneading-dough-lamplight-baker-workers-ovens-glow}
In Constantinople, bread is the most important food of all. And bakers have a very important job. The whole city depends on them.

[pause]

Nikolaos's day begins in the middle of the night. While the city sleeps, he gets up and goes down to the bakehouse. Basil and old Stephanos are already there. They light the ovens with bundles of dry wood. They mix flour with water, salt and a little old dough from yesterday. That helps the new dough to rise. Then they knead it on the long tables, pushing and folding and turning, for a long time.

[long-pause]

When the dough has risen, they shape it into loaves. There are big round loaves for families, and smaller rolls for single people. There are flat breads with a cross pressed into the top. On feast days, there are special breads with sesame seeds, or with a little honey. Nikolaos stamps some of the loaves with a little wooden stamp. It shows a cross and some letters. That is the mark of his bakery.

[pause]

{img:baker-sliding-loaves-into-domed-brick-oven-long-wooden-paddle-fire}
When the ovens are hot, the men sweep out the ashes. Then they slide the loaves inside, one by one, on a long flat wooden paddle. The oven door is closed. And soon, the most wonderful smell begins to fill the bakehouse, and the street outside. The smell of fresh bread. Neighbours who wake early smile when they smell it. They know that morning is coming.

[long-pause]

In Constantinople, the bakers are organised in a guild. A guild is a group of people who do the same work. There are guilds for many trades: the silk weavers and the perfume makers, the butchers and the fishmongers. Each guild has strict rules, written down in a book by the city's chief official, the Eparch. The book says exactly what each trade may do and may not do.

[pause]

{img:eparch-official-inspecting-bread-weights-bakery-counter-scales-morning}
For bakers, the rules are very important. The price of bread is fixed by the Eparch. The weight of each loaf is fixed too. Officials come round to check that the loaves are the right weight. A baker who cheats can be punished. But the bakers also have privileges. They do not have to serve in the army, or do other public work. Because the city must never be without bread.

[long-pause]

By dawn, the first loaves are ready. Nikolaos pulls them out of the oven with the long paddle. They are golden brown, with crispy crusts, and they smell wonderful. He stacks them on the shelves of the shop. Eirene comes down and opens the wooden shutters at the front. And the first customers are already waiting outside.

[pause]

{img:bakery-counter-dawn-customers-queue-mother-selling-warm-loaves-street}
There is a woman with a baby, and a workman on his way to the harbour. There is an old priest from the little church on the corner. And there is Bjorn, the tall guardsman, who always buys two big loaves. Eirene greets each one by name and asks about their families. Leon runs out with a basket of rolls to deliver to the neighbours. And Nikolaos, his work done for the night, goes upstairs to sleep for a few hours, with the smell of bread still in his beard.

[pause]

Bread in Constantinople comes in different kinds. There is fine white bread, made from the best wheat flour, for the rich. There is ordinary bread, a little darker, for most people. And there is coarse bread, made from barley or rough flour, for the poorest. Nikolaos makes all of them. He also gives his leftover bread to the poor at the end of each day. They come to the back door of the bakehouse, and he never sends them away empty-handed.

[pause]

Old Stephanos has worked in the bakery for more than thirty years. He worked for Nikolaos's father before him. He grinds the grain on a big millstone turned by a donkey. He carries the heavy sacks of flour on his back, even though he is old. He does not talk much. But he knows everything about bread. Nikolaos often asks his advice, and always listens to his answer.

[pause]

At the end of the morning, the bakehouse is quiet again. The ovens are cooling, and the floor is swept. Basil and Stephanos go home to sleep. Psaraki the cat curls up on a warm brick by the oven door. The smell of bread still hangs in the air. And upstairs, Nikolaos lies on his bed and closes his eyes, while the busy street goes on outside the shuttered window.

Let us follow Eirene and Zoe on an ordinary morning, after the busy hour at the bakery.

[pause]

When the first rush of customers is over, Basil takes over at the counter. Eirene puts on her outdoor cloak and a veil over her hair. Respectable women in Constantinople always cover their hair when they go out. She takes a big basket, and Zoe takes a small one. Together, they walk out into the busy street, toward the Mese.

The street is full of life. There are people everywhere, walking and talking and carrying things. There are porters carrying heavy loads on their backs. There are donkeys loaded with baskets of vegetables. There are priests and monks in black robes, and officials in coloured silk. There are soldiers and sailors, beggars and children. Somewhere, a man is shouting that he has fresh fish for sale.

First they go to the vegetable market, in a little square near their street. Farmers from outside the city bring their produce here every morning. There are cabbages and leeks, onions and garlic, beans and cucumbers. There are figs and grapes, apples and pears. Eirene chooses carefully, picking up each vegetable and looking at it closely. She bargains a little with the farmer's wife. They both laugh, and agree on a price.

Then they go to the fish market, down near the harbour. The fishermen have just come in with their catch. There are tuna and mackerel, red mullet and sardines. The Bosphorus is famous for its fish, which swim through the strait in great numbers. Eirene buys a few mackerel for supper. The fishmonger wraps them in big green leaves.

On the way back, they stop at a little shop that sells cheese and olives. The shopkeeper is an old friend of Eirene's. She gives Zoe a little piece of soft white cheese to taste. Zoe says thank you politely. Then they stop at a stall that sells spices, and Eirene buys a little pepper, for a special dish on Sunday. Pepper is expensive, so she buys only a tiny bag.

[long-pause]

On the way home, they pass a little church. Eirene and Zoe go inside for a moment. It is cool and dim inside, after the bright street. A few candles are burning in front of the icons. An old woman is kneeling in prayer. Eirene lights a candle and says a short prayer. Zoe looks up at the walls, which are painted with saints and angels. The gold behind them glimmers softly in the candlelight. She could stay here all day.

{img:small-byzantine-church-interior-candles-icons-woman-girl-praying-dim}
When they get home, it is almost midday. Nikolaos is waking up from his morning sleep. Grandmother Anna has been to church and back. Leon comes running in from school, hungry as always. The family eats a simple meal together: bread, cheese, olives and a few figs. Then Eirene goes back down to the shop. Zoe helps her grandmother with the spinning. And the afternoon goes slowly by, in the warm and busy house.

[pause]

Sometimes, on the way home, they stop to watch a street performer. There are jugglers who throw balls and knives into the air. There are tumblers who do somersaults and handstands. There are men with trained bears and monkeys, which dance to the music of a pipe. Zoe likes the monkeys best. They wear little hats and bow to the crowd, and then they hold out their hands for coins.

[pause]

In the afternoon, while Zoe spins with her grandmother, Eirene does the accounts of the bakery. She counts the coins from the morning's sales. She writes down how much flour and wood they have bought. She works out how much bread they need to bake tomorrow. She is very good with numbers. Nikolaos says the bakery would not last a week without her, and he is probably right.

[pause]

Every few weeks, Eirene goes to a special shop to buy olive oil. The oil comes in big clay jars from the south of the empire, from Greece and the islands. The shopkeeper pours it into Eirene's own jar with a long copper ladle. She uses it for cooking, for the lamps and for the icon corner. Good oil is important, she says. It makes the food taste better, and the lamps burn brighter.

The markets of Constantinople are among the richest in the world. Along the Mese, under the long colonnades, there are shops selling everything you can imagine.

[pause]

There are shops selling silk, in every colour of the rainbow. There are shops selling linen and wool, leather and furs. There are goldsmiths and silversmiths, making jewellery and cups and crosses. There are shops selling books, copied by hand on parchment. There are shops selling candles, and shops selling perfume. And there are money changers, sitting at their tables with their scales and their piles of coins.

[long-pause]

Each trade has its own place in the city. The silversmiths are all in one part of the Mese. The silk merchants are in another. The perfume makers are right next to the palace. The old book of the Eparch says this is so that the sweet smell of their perfumes will rise up to the emperor's windows. There are perfumes of rose and lavender, musk and amber, cinnamon and incense.

[pause]

{img:perfume-sellers-stalls-near-palace-glass-bottles-incense-sweet-smoke}
Merchants come to Constantinople from all over the world. There are Italians from Venice and Amalfi, with their fast ships. There are Arab merchants from Syria and Egypt, with spices and fine cloth. There are Jewish merchants, who trade all across the Mediterranean. And there are the Rus, traders from the north, from the city of Kiev. They come down the great rivers in long boats, bringing furs and honey, wax and amber.

[long-pause]

The Rus traders are allowed to stay in a special place outside the city walls. They may come into the city only through one gate, in small groups, without weapons. They are watched by an official. They may stay only for the summer. Then they must go home again before the winter. But while they are here, they buy silk and wine, spices and fine goods to take back north.

[pause]

{img:rus-traders-long-boats-unloading-furs-honey-barrels-quay-summer}
In the markets, there are many languages spoken. There is Greek and Latin, Arabic and Armenian, Hebrew and the language of the Rus. People shout and bargain and argue. They wave their hands and laugh. It is noisy and colourful and exciting. Leon loves to walk through the markets with his father. He listens to all the different languages and tries to copy the sounds.

[long-pause]

Eirene does not buy silk very often. It is very expensive. But once a year, before Easter, she buys a small piece of silk ribbon for Zoe and for herself. This year, she takes Zoe with her to choose. They walk slowly along the silk shops, looking at the colours. There is purple and gold, green and blue, red and saffron yellow.

[pause]

{img:mother-daughter-choosing-silk-ribbon-shop-colours-merchant-smiling}
Zoe chooses a ribbon of deep blue, with a little pattern of golden birds woven into it. She holds it up to the light, and the golden birds shine. Eirene chooses a ribbon of soft green. The silk merchant wraps them up in a piece of linen. Zoe holds the little package carefully all the way home. That evening, she shows it to her grandmother. Grandmother Anna looks at the little golden birds for a long time. They are very beautiful, she says. Just like you.

[pause]

Some of the shops along the Mese are very small, only a little room with a counter at the front. The shopkeeper lives in a room behind the shop, or upstairs. In the evening, he pulls down a wooden shutter over the front of the shop and locks it. Then he sits outside on a stool, talking with the other shopkeepers. They talk about business, and the weather, and the news from the palace.

[pause]

Along the Mese, there are also little stalls selling cooked food. There are grilled fish and roasted chestnuts, bean soup and boiled eggs. There are little cakes made with honey and sesame. Workers and porters stop to buy something hot to eat. Leon always begs his father for a honey cake when they walk past. And usually, his father gives in.

[long-pause]

{img:imperial-silk-workshop-looms-weavers-purple-gold-silk-palace-1000ad}
Silk is one of the great treasures of Constantinople. And it has a wonderful story.

[pause]

For hundreds of years, silk came only from China, far away to the east. Nobody else knew how to make it. The Chinese kept the secret very carefully. Silk was carried along the long roads across Asia, by camel and by horse. It was very rare and very expensive. Only the very rich could buy it.

[long-pause]

Then, about four hundred and fifty years before our story, something happened. The emperor Justinian was ruling in Constantinople. Two monks came to see him. They had been to the far east, they said, and they had learned the secret of silk. Silk is made by little worms, they told him, which eat the leaves of the mulberry tree. The emperor asked them to bring some of these worms back.

[pause]

{img:two-monks-walking-long-road-hollow-walking-canes-mountains-east}
So the monks went back to the east. And a few years later, they returned. They had hidden silkworm eggs inside their hollow walking sticks, made of bamboo. From those few eggs, the silk industry of the empire began. Mulberry trees were planted. Silkworms were raised. And soon, Constantinople was making its own silk.

[long-pause]

By the year 1000, the silk of Constantinople is famous all over the world. Some of the finest silk is made in the imperial workshops, inside the palace. Only the emperor's own weavers may make the very best kinds. There is silk dyed with a special purple colour, made from tiny sea snails. It takes thousands of snails to make a little dye. Only the emperor and his family may wear the deepest purple silk. It is called the imperial purple.

[pause]

{img:dyers-vats-purple-dye-sea-snails-workers-cloth-drying-sunlight}
The silk weavers of Constantinople make wonderful patterns. They weave pictures of lions and eagles, elephants and griffins, in gold and silver thread. They weave patterns of flowers and vines and circles. Some of these silks were given as gifts to foreign kings. Pieces of them are still kept today, in the treasuries of old churches in Europe. They have lasted for a thousand years.

[long-pause]

Grandmother Anna was once a weaver, but of linen, not silk. She tells Zoe about the silk weavers she knew when she was young. They worked in long rooms with big wooden looms, she says. They worked very slowly, thread by thread. One piece of fine silk with a pattern could take months to weave. The weavers were very skilled, and very proud of their work. Some of them could weave a lion so real that you thought it would roar.

[pause]

{img:grandmother-showing-girl-weaving-small-loom-linen-thread-window}
Zoe asks her grandmother to teach her to weave. So, in the afternoons, Grandmother Anna sets up a little loom by the window. She shows Zoe how to put the threads on the loom. She shows her how to pass the shuttle back and forth, and how to press the threads together. Zoe's first piece is crooked and uneven. But her grandmother says it is very good for a first try. And she smiles, remembering her own first piece, long, long ago.

[pause]

In the silk workshops of the city, many of the workers are women. They unwind the silk from the little cocoons, and spin it into thread. They dye the thread in big vats of colour. Some of them weave on the great looms. It is skilled and careful work. The best silk workers are honoured and well paid. And their silk is sent all over the world.

[pause]

Zoe practises her weaving every afternoon. Slowly, her pieces get straighter and more even. After a few weeks, she weaves a little band of linen, with a simple pattern of blue stripes. She gives it to her mother, who ties it around her waist as a belt. Eirene wears it every day after that. And whenever a customer admires it, she says proudly that her daughter made it.

[long-pause]

{img:hagia-sophia-exterior-1000ad-great-dome-buttresses-morning-light-square}
In the middle of Constantinople stands the most famous building of the whole empire. It is the great church of Hagia Sophia, the church of Holy Wisdom.

[pause]

Hagia Sophia was built about four hundred and fifty years before our story, by the emperor Justinian. It took only about five years to build, with ten thousand workers. When it was finished, Justinian walked inside and looked up at the great dome. People say he cried out: Solomon, I have outdone you! Solomon was the wise king of the Bible who built the great temple in Jerusalem.

[long-pause]

From the outside, Hagia Sophia is big and solid, made of brick and stone. But inside, it is like another world. You walk through great doors into an enormous space. And above you rises the dome, so high and so wide that it seems to float in the air. Around the bottom of the dome, there is a ring of forty windows. The light streams in through them. It makes the dome look as if it is hanging from heaven on a golden chain.

[pause]

{img:hagia-sophia-interior-dome-forty-windows-light-rays-gold-mosaics-vast}
The walls and the columns are covered with marble of many colours. There is green marble and red marble, white marble with grey veins and yellow marble. The marble was brought from all over the empire. The floors are covered with marble too, polished smooth by thousands of feet. And high up on the walls and in the dome, there are mosaics of gold, glittering in the light.

[long-pause]

On Sundays and feast days, Hagia Sophia is full of people. The patriarch leads the service, in robes of gold and white. Priests and deacons carry candles and incense. The choir sings beautiful, slow music, which echoes up into the dome. The smell of incense fills the great space. And thousands of oil lamps hang on chains from the ceiling, glowing softly.

[pause]

{img:hagia-sophia-service-patriarch-priests-incense-lamps-crowd-singing}
There is a famous story about Hagia Sophia. About fifteen years before our story, a prince of the Rus, from Kiev, wanted to choose a new religion for his people. He sent messengers to visit different countries. When his messengers came to Constantinople, they were taken to a service in Hagia Sophia. Afterwards, they went home and said: We did not know whether we were in heaven or on earth. We only know that God lives there among people. After that, the prince and his people became Christians.

[long-pause]

Zoe loves Hagia Sophia more than any other place in the world. Her family goes there on the great feast days. She stands with her mother and grandmother in the upper gallery, where the women stand. From there, she can look down on the huge space below. She can look up into the golden dome. She feels very small, and very happy.

[pause]

{img:women-upper-gallery-hagia-sophia-looking-down-girl-mother-grandmother}
One of her favourite mosaics is above a door. It shows the Virgin Mary sitting on a throne, with the baby Jesus on her lap. On one side of her stands the emperor Constantine, who founded the city. He is holding a little model of the city. On the other side stands the emperor Justinian, holding a little model of Hagia Sophia. They are giving their gifts to Mary. Zoe looks at the little models in their hands, and smiles. One day, she thinks, I will paint pictures like that.

[pause]

The great church is never really empty. Even in the middle of the day, there are always people praying in the quiet corners. There are old women with their prayer beads, and monks kneeling on the marble floor. There are travellers from far away, standing in wonder under the dome. And there are the keepers of the church, who fill the lamps with oil and sweep the floors.

[pause]

There is a famous column in Hagia Sophia, with a little hole in it, covered with bronze. People believe that the column is always a little damp, and that the damp brings healing. They put their thumb into the hole and turn their hand around, making a wish. Zoe always does this when she visits. She does not tell anyone what she wishes for. But it is always the same wish.

[long-pause]

{img:icon-painter-workshop-wooden-panels-gold-leaf-brushes-monk-painting}
In Constantinople, icons are everywhere. An icon is a holy picture, usually painted on a panel of wood. It shows Christ, the Virgin Mary, the saints or the angels. People pray in front of icons. They light candles and lamps before them. They kiss them and touch them. For the people of the empire, icons are a window between earth and heaven.

[pause]

There are icons in every church and every monastery. There are icons in every home, in the special icon corner. There are icons on the walls of shops and workshops. Soldiers carry little icons with them. Sailors have icons on their ships. And on great feast days, famous icons are carried through the streets in processions.

[long-pause]

Icons are painted by special painters, many of them monks. They work in quiet workshops in the monasteries and in the city. First, a carpenter makes a panel of good wood, smooth and flat. Then the painter covers it with linen and a layer of white chalk paste. He polishes it until it is as smooth as glass.

[pause]

{img:icon-painter-applying-gold-leaf-panel-soft-brush-careful-workshop-window}
Then the painter draws the picture, very carefully. He follows old patterns, so the saints always look the same. Saint Nicholas always has a round white beard. Saint George is always young, with curly hair. The background is covered with thin sheets of real gold, laid on very carefully with a soft brush. Then the painter paints the figures, using colours made from crushed stones and plants, mixed with egg yolk.

[long-pause]

Painting an icon is a holy work. The painter prays while he works. He fasts before he starts. He tries to keep his mind calm and peaceful. A good icon can take weeks or months to finish. When it is done, it is blessed by a priest. Then it is ready to be prayed in front of.

[pause]

{img:girl-watching-icon-painter-doorway-workshop-sunlight-colours-pots}
Not far from the family's house, there is a small monastery with an icon workshop. An old monk called Father Theophanes paints icons there. Zoe often stops by the door to watch him work. At first, he did not notice her. Then he began to smile at her. One day, he invited her in. He showed her his pots of colours: red earth and blue stone, green and yellow and white. He showed her how to mix them with egg yolk.

[long-pause]

Now Zoe visits Father Theophanes once a week, on her way back from the market. He lets her sit quietly in a corner and watch. Sometimes, he lets her help to grind the colours, with a little stone on a slab of marble. He tells her about the saints he is painting, and about their lives. He tells her that an icon must be painted with love, or it is not an icon at all.

[pause]

{img:old-monk-teaching-girl-grinding-colours-marble-slab-workshop-quiet}
One day, Father Theophanes gives Zoe a gift. It is a very small panel of wood, no bigger than her hand, covered with smooth white chalk. You can paint on this, he says, when you are ready. Paint something you love. Zoe takes it home and puts it in the chest under her bed. She does not dare to paint on it yet. But every evening, she takes it out and looks at it, and thinks about what she will paint.

[pause]

On the great feast days, a very famous icon of the Virgin Mary is carried through the streets of the city. People say it was painted by Saint Luke himself, long ago. The people of Constantinople believe it has protected the city many times. When it passes, people kneel down in the street and cross themselves. Mothers hold up their babies to see it. And the whole street goes quiet, as it passes by.

[pause]

Every family has its own favourite saints, and its own little stories about them. Grandmother Anna says that Saint Anna once helped her find a lost ring, when she was a young bride. Nikolaos says that Saint Nicholas watches over all bakers, because he once fed the hungry. The children are not sure about these stories. But they like them. And they always say goodnight to the icons before bed.

[long-pause]

{img:great-palace-constantinople-terraces-gardens-sea-walls-domes-1000ad}
Near Hagia Sophia, stretching down the hill to the sea, there is the Great Palace of the emperors. It is not one building, but many. There are halls and churches, courtyards and gardens, baths and barracks, all joined together by covered walkways. It is like a city within the city.

[pause]

The palace has been built and rebuilt by many emperors over the centuries. Its floors are covered with mosaics, showing scenes of hunting and country life. There are pictures of children playing with hoops, and of a boy feeding a donkey. There are pictures of tigers and elephants, and of a man milking a goat. Its walls are covered with marble and gold. Its gardens are full of fountains, flowers and fruit trees, looking out over the sea.

[long-pause]

The most famous room in the palace is the great throne room, called the Magnaura. Foreign visitors who come to see the emperor are brought here. And they are amazed by what they see. The emperor sits on a great golden throne. Beside the throne, there is a golden tree, with golden birds on its branches. And at the foot of the throne, there are golden lions.

[pause]

{img:golden-throne-room-mechanical-lions-golden-tree-birds-emperor-visitors}
When a visitor comes in and bows, something wonderful happens. The golden birds on the tree begin to sing. The golden lions open their mouths and roar, and swish their tails. And when the visitor looks up again, the emperor's throne has risen up high into the air, almost to the ceiling. The emperor is now wearing different clothes. The visitors do not know how it is done. It is all done by clever machines, with water and air and hidden ropes.

[long-pause]

A visitor from Italy wrote about this, about fifty years before our story. He said that he was not frightened, because he had been told about it beforehand. But he was very surprised. He bowed three times, as he had been told. And when he lifted his head, the emperor was up near the ceiling, dressed in new robes. The emperor did not speak to him directly. An official spoke for him. It was all very grand and very strange.

[pause]

{img:palace-ceremony-officials-silk-robes-procession-candles-golden-hall}
Life in the palace is full of ceremonies. There is a ceremony for every feast day and every important event. There are processions through the halls, with officials in coloured silk robes. Everyone has their place, and everyone knows exactly what to do. There is a great book that describes all these ceremonies, step by step. It was written by an emperor called Constantine the Seventh, about fifty years before our story.

[long-pause]

But the palace is also a home, for the emperor and his family and servants. There are thousands of people living and working there. There are cooks and bakers, gardeners and grooms, guards and secretaries. Nikolaos's cousin works in the palace bakery. He bakes fine white bread for the emperor's table. Sometimes, he brings a little of it home for the family to taste. It is very soft and very white. But Nikolaos says his own bread is better.

[pause]

{img:palace-gardens-terrace-sea-view-fountains-fruit-trees-evening-calm}
In the evenings, the palace gardens are quiet and beautiful. The fountains splash softly. The roses and the lilies smell sweet. The sea below is calm and blue, with the sun setting over the water. Peacocks walk slowly along the paths, their long tails trailing behind them. And from somewhere inside the palace, there is the sound of music, as the lamps are lit in the great halls.

[pause]

Some of the floor mosaics of the palace are very funny. One shows a boy being kicked by a donkey. Another shows a man trying to milk a goat that will not stand still. Another shows a bear eating apples from a tree. The emperors and their guests must have walked over these little pictures every day. Perhaps they smiled at them, just as we do today.

[pause]

The palace also has a great library, full of old books. There are books of history and law, medicine and astronomy. There are copies of the old Greek poets and thinkers. The scribes of the palace copy them out again and again, so they will not be lost. Some of the books are decorated with pictures in gold and bright colours. They are among the most precious treasures of the whole empire.

[long-pause]

{img:emperor-basil-ii-plain-armour-tent-maps-officers-serious-face-camp}
The emperor in the year 1000 is Basil the Second. He is one of the most hard-working emperors in the history of Constantinople.

[pause]

Basil became emperor when he was very young, together with his younger brother Constantine. For many years, older men ruled for them. There were difficult years, with rivals who wanted to take the throne. But Basil grew up strong and determined. When he was about thirty, he took charge of the empire himself. And he worked hard for it every day.

[long-pause]

Basil is not like many other emperors. He does not care for feasts and luxury. He dresses simply, in dark clothes. He eats simple food. He spends most of his time not in the beautiful palace, but in army camps on the borders of the empire. He lives in a tent, like his soldiers, and shares their hardships. The soldiers respect him for it.

[pause]

{img:emperor-riding-with-soldiers-mountain-road-plain-cloak-banners}
Basil is also very careful with money. He keeps the empire's treasury full. When he dies, many years later, the treasury will be fuller than it has ever been. There will be so much gold that new storerooms will have to be dug under the palace to hold it. Basil wants the empire to be strong and safe. And he believes that a full treasury helps to keep it that way.

[long-pause]

Basil is also known for something unusual. About ten years before our story, he made a friendship with the prince of Kiev, called Vladimir. Basil needed help, because some rivals were fighting him. Vladimir sent him six thousand soldiers from the north. In return, Basil agreed that his sister Anna could marry Vladimir. And Vladimir agreed to become a Christian, and his people with him.

[pause]

{img:byzantine-princess-anna-ship-leaving-for-kiev-sad-farewell-harbour}
This was a big decision for the princess Anna. In those days, the princesses of Constantinople almost never married foreign princes. Anna did not want to go to Kiev, so far away in the cold north. She cried when she said goodbye to her brothers. But she went. And in Kiev, she helped to build churches and to bring the faith and learning of Constantinople to the Rus. So she is remembered in Kiev as a good and wise princess.

[long-pause]

The six thousand soldiers from the north stayed in Constantinople. They became the emperor's personal guard. They are called the Varangian Guard. They are big, strong men from the lands of the Rus and from Scandinavia. They carry great axes and wear long cloaks. They are completely loyal to the emperor. And the emperor trusts them more than anyone.

[pause]

{img:varangian-guard-tall-norse-soldiers-axes-palace-gate-standing-guard}
The people of Constantinople have mixed feelings about the Varangians. Some of them find them frightening, because they are so big and so strange. Others find them funny, because they speak Greek badly and drink a lot of wine. Some people call them the emperor's wine-bags, behind their backs. But most people respect them. And when there is trouble in the city, the Varangians keep order calmly and firmly.

[pause]

Eirene's neighbour Bjorn is one of these guards. He has been in Constantinople for about five years now. He says the city still amazes him, every single day.

[pause]

Even though Basil is away so much, the people of Constantinople feel his presence. His name is said in the prayers in every church. His face is on every gold coin. When he comes home from his campaigns, there are great processions through the city. He rides through the Golden Gate and along the Mese, all the way to Hagia Sophia. The people line the streets and cheer. And then, a few weeks later, he is gone again, back to his army.

[pause]

Basil's younger brother, Constantine, is very different from him. Constantine loves feasts and music and hunting. He does not like hard work or wars. So he stays in the palace in Constantinople, and enjoys himself. He is officially emperor too, together with Basil. But everyone knows that Basil is the one who really rules. The two brothers get along well enough, as long as each one does what he likes best.

[long-pause]

{img:norse-guardsman-telling-story-children-bakery-step-evening-lamp}
Let us hear Bjorn's story, as he tells it to Leon and Zoe one evening, sitting on the step of the bakery.

[pause]

Bjorn was born in a little farming village in the north, in a land of forests and lakes and long cold winters. His family were farmers and fishermen. When he was young, he heard stories of the great city in the south, which his people call Miklagard. Miklagard means the great city. The stories said it was full of gold and wonders. Young men went there to serve the emperor and came home rich. Bjorn decided to go.

[long-pause]

He travelled with a group of other young men from his village. First, they sailed across the sea in a long wooden ship, to the lands of the Rus. Then they rowed up the rivers, through great forests, for many weeks. Where the rivers ended, they carried their ship over land on rollers, to the next river. Then they rowed down the great river Dnieper, toward the south.

[pause]

{img:viking-longship-river-dnieper-forest-banks-rowers-summer-mist}
On the Dnieper, there were dangerous rapids, where the river rushed over rocks. They had to get out and carry the ship and the goods along the bank. It took many days. At last, they reached the Black Sea. They sailed along the coast, and one morning, they came into the Bosphorus. And there, in front of them, was Constantinople. Bjorn says he will never forget that first sight. The walls and the towers, the domes and the palaces, shining in the morning sun.

[long-pause]

He joined the Varangian Guard and has served the emperor ever since. He has been on campaigns with the emperor, far away in the mountains. He has stood guard in the palace during great ceremonies. He has seen the golden throne and the singing birds. He has been inside Hagia Sophia. And he has been baptised as a Christian, in a little church near the palace.

[pause]

{img:varangian-guardsman-amazed-looking-up-hagia-sophia-dome-first-time}
But he misses his home. He misses the snow and the forests and the lakes. He misses his mother and his sisters. He misses the long summer evenings, when the sun hardly sets. He tells the children about the northern lights, green curtains of light in the winter sky. Leon and Zoe can hardly imagine it. Snow that lasts for half the year? Nights that last all day? It sounds like a fairy tale.

[long-pause]

Some of the Varangians have carved their names on the marble of Hagia Sophia. They carved them in runes, the old letters of the north. One of them can still be read today, on the balustrade of the upper gallery. It says something like: Halfdan carved these runes. Perhaps Halfdan was bored during a long service. Perhaps he wanted to leave a mark in the greatest church in the world. Bjorn says he knows a man called Halfdan, but he is not sure if it is the same one.

[pause]

{img:runes-carved-marble-balustrade-gallery-hagia-sophia-close-light}
One day, Bjorn says, he will go home, with gold in his pocket and stories to tell. He will build a farm by a lake, and marry a girl from his village. And in the long winter evenings, he will tell his children about Miklagard, the great city in the south. About the golden dome and the singing birds and the chariot races. And about a baker's family, who gave him the best bread in the world.

[pause]

In his little house at the end of the street, Bjorn has a few things from his homeland. There is a carved wooden box, a fur blanket, and a small silver hammer on a chain. The hammer was a lucky charm from his old religion. He does not wear it anymore, because he is a Christian now. But he keeps it, because his mother gave it to him. Sometimes he takes it out and holds it, and thinks of her.

[pause]

Bjorn has learned many things in Constantinople. He has learned to eat olives, which he did not like at first. He has learned to drink wine mixed with water, as the Romans do. He has learned a few prayers in Greek, and some of the names of the saints. And he has learned to bargain in the market, though Eirene says he still pays too much for everything.

[long-pause]

{img:hippodrome-constantinople-1000ad-huge-crowd-chariots-obelisk-sunny}
Next to the Great Palace, there is the Hippodrome. It is a huge stadium for chariot races. And it is the most exciting place in all of Constantinople.

[pause]

The Hippodrome is about four hundred and fifty metres long. It is shaped like a long U, with rows of stone seats rising up on all sides. It can hold about a hundred thousand people. Down the middle runs a long low wall, called the spina. On it stand statues and columns and an ancient obelisk from Egypt, covered with strange carvings. At one end, there are the starting gates for the chariots. And on one side, there is the emperor's box, connected directly to the palace.

[long-pause]

The races are run by four-horse chariots. Each chariot is light and small, made of wood, with two wheels. The charioteer stands in it, holding the reins of four horses. The chariots race seven times around the spina. The first to cross the finishing line wins. It is fast, and it can be dangerous. The crowds love it.

[pause]

{img:four-horse-chariots-racing-hippodrome-turn-dust-crowd-cheering}
There are two great racing teams: the Blues and the Greens. Each team has its own charioteers, horses, stables and colours. And each team has its own supporters, all over the city. The supporters wear the colours of their team, and they sit together in the Hippodrome. They sing songs and chant and cheer. Leon is a passionate supporter of the Blues. So is his father. Grandmother Anna is secretly a Green, but she does not say so too loudly.

[long-pause]

Today is a race day, and Nikolaos takes Leon and Zoe to the Hippodrome. They set off early, because the crowds are huge. They find seats high up, among the Blues. All around them, people are talking excitedly about the horses and the charioteers. There are sellers walking up and down with bread and nuts and fruit. There are musicians playing and acrobats tumbling in the arena before the races begin.

[pause]

{img:father-son-daughter-hippodrome-seats-blue-ribbons-excited-crowd}
Then the emperor's box fills with officials, and a hush falls over the crowd. The emperor is away with his army, so his brother Constantine is there in his place. He raises a white cloth, and lets it fall. The starting gates fly open. And the chariots burst out onto the track in a cloud of dust and noise.

[long-pause]

The crowd roars. The horses gallop at full speed, their manes flying. The charioteers lean forward, shouting to their horses. The chariots race down the long straight side, side by side. At the end of the spina, they must turn sharply. This is the most exciting moment. The chariots swing around the turn, their wheels skidding in the dust. A Blue chariot takes the turn tightly and pulls ahead. Leon jumps up and shouts at the top of his voice.

[pause]

{img:blue-chariot-crossing-finish-line-charioteer-arms-raised-crowd-joy}
Lap after lap, the race goes on. The Blue chariot stays in front, but a Green chariot is right behind it. On the last lap, the Green chariot comes closer and closer. Leon holds his breath. Zoe grabs her father's arm. And then the two chariots cross the line, almost together. For a moment, nobody knows who has won. Then the judges raise a blue flag. The Blues have won! Leon screams with joy and hugs his father. All around them, the Blues are cheering and singing. That night, Leon talks about the race at supper, at bedtime, and in his sleep.

[pause]

Between the races, there are other entertainments in the Hippodrome. There are acrobats and dancers, and men who walk on tightropes high above the ground. There are trained animals and musicians, and sometimes clowns. The crowd laughs and claps and throws coins. The sellers walk up and down with food and drink. And the children run about in the aisles, too excited to sit still.

[pause]

The charioteers are the great heroes of the city. People know their names and their stories. The best of them have statues built in their honour, along the spina of the Hippodrome. Leon's favourite charioteer is a young man called Porphyrios, who drives for the Blues. Leon has seen him win many races. He dreams that one day, he too will drive a chariot in the Hippodrome, with the crowd shouting his name.

[long-pause]

{img:golden-horn-1000ad-merchant-ships-quays-warehouses-busy-morning-sun}
The harbours of Constantinople are always busy. And the greatest of them is the Golden Horn.

[pause]

The Golden Horn is a long, narrow inlet of the sea, curving into the land on the north side of the city. It is deep and calm and sheltered from the winds. It is a perfect harbour. Ships of all sizes come here, from all over the world. Along its southern shore, there are quays and warehouses, workshops and markets. And on the other side, there are more settlements, where foreign merchants live.

[long-pause]

At the mouth of the Golden Horn, there is something very unusual. There is a great iron chain, which can be stretched across the water, from one side to the other. It hangs on floating wooden logs. In times of danger, it is pulled tight, and no enemy ships can get into the harbour. In times of peace, it lies on the bottom, and ships can come and go freely.

[pause]

{img:great-iron-chain-floating-logs-across-harbour-mouth-tower-calm-sea}
In the morning, the harbour is full of activity. Ships are being unloaded by teams of porters. There are big clay jars of wine and olive oil, sacks of grain and bales of cloth. There are timber and stone for building, and sheep and cattle for meat. The porters carry the goods on their backs, up the steep streets into the city. Officials stand on the quays, writing everything down and collecting taxes.

[long-pause]

Shipbuilders work along the shore, building new ships and repairing old ones. There are big merchant ships with round hulls and big sails. There are fast warships called dromons, with long rows of oars. The dromons carry a famous secret weapon, called Greek fire. Nobody outside the empire knows exactly how it is made. It is one of the best-kept secrets in history.

[pause]

{img:shipwrights-building-wooden-ship-hull-golden-horn-shore-tools-sun}
Leon loves to go down to the harbour with his father, to buy flour. The flour comes from big ships that bring grain from Egypt and from the lands around the Black Sea. The grain is ground into flour in mills by the water. Nikolaos buys his flour from an old miller who has been his friend for twenty years. While they talk, Leon watches the ships coming and going, and the sailors climbing the masts.

[long-pause]

Sometimes, Leon sees ships from very far away. Once, he saw a ship from Venice, with sailors in bright red caps. Once, he saw a long ship of the Rus, with a dragon's head carved on its prow. Once, he saw a ship from Egypt, unloading great jars of something sweet and sticky. It was sugar, the old miller told him. Sugar is made from a tall grass that grows in hot countries. It is very rare and very precious.

[pause]

{img:boy-on-quay-watching-ships-sails-sunset-golden-horn-father-miller}
In the evening, the harbour becomes quieter. The ships are tied up for the night, rocking gently on the water. The sailors go into the taverns along the shore, to eat and drink and sing. Fishermen sit on the quays, mending their nets. The sun sets behind the hills, and the water of the Golden Horn turns gold and red. That, people say, is why it is called the Golden Horn. And as darkness falls, little lamps are lit on the ships, one by one, reflected in the still water.

[pause]

On the other side of the Golden Horn, there is a town called Galata. Many foreign merchants live there, with their families and their warehouses. There are little boats that carry people across the water, back and forth, all day long. Leon has been across only once, with his father. He thought the view of the city from the other side was the most beautiful thing he had ever seen. All the domes and towers, rising up from the water.

[pause]

Along the shore of the Golden Horn, there are also many little taverns and food stalls for the sailors. They sell fried fish and bread, bean soup and wine. In the evenings, the sailors sit there and tell stories of their voyages. They talk about storms and pirates, faraway ports and strange customs. Leon loves to listen to them, when his father lets him stay for a while.

[long-pause]

{img:byzantine-school-boys-wax-tablets-teacher-psalms-small-room-1000ad}
What is life like for children in Constantinople, in the year 1000?

[pause]

Many boys, and some girls, go to a small school to learn to read and write. The school is often run by a priest or a teacher, in a small room near a church. The children sit on benches or on the floor. They write on wax tablets with a pointed stick, called a stylus. When they make a mistake, they smooth the wax with the flat end of the stylus, and try again.

[long-pause]

Leon goes to a little school near the church at the end of his street. The teacher is a young priest called Father Michael. He teaches the boys their letters, then words, then whole sentences. They learn to read from the Psalms, the old songs of the Bible. Later, they will read from Homer, the great poet of ancient Greece. Leon finds it hard to sit still. He would much rather be at the Hippodrome. But Father Michael is patient with him.

[pause]

{img:boy-struggling-letters-wax-tablet-stylus-patient-young-priest-helping}
Girls do not usually go to school, but many of them learn at home. Some of them learn to read very well. Grandmother Anna cannot read. But Eirene can read a little, and she is teaching Zoe in the evenings. Zoe learns quickly. She wants to be able to read the words written on the icons. Already, she can read the names of the saints, written in little letters beside their pictures.

[long-pause]

Children in Constantinople also help their families with work. Leon delivers bread to the neighbours every morning. He carries a big basket on his head, balancing it carefully. He knows every house and every customer. Some of the neighbours give him a little coin, or a fig, or a sweet. Zoe helps her mother at the counter and fetches water and spins thread with her grandmother.

[pause]

{img:children-playing-street-hoops-knucklebones-byzantine-neighbourhood-afternoon}
But there is time for play too. The children play in the streets and the little squares. They play with hoops, rolling them along with a stick. They play knucklebones, with little bones from sheep, throwing and catching them. They play with balls made of leather, stuffed with feathers. They play hide and seek among the columns of the colonnades. The girls have dolls made of wood and clay, with little dresses of cloth.

[long-pause]

Leon and his friends have their own little chariot races. They take turns being the horses and the charioteers. The horses run in front, holding a rope, and the charioteer runs behind, holding the end. They race around the little square, shouting and laughing. Leon always wants to be the charioteer for the Blues. His friend Theodore always wants to be the Greens. They argue about it every day. But they are the best of friends.

[pause]

{img:boys-playing-chariot-race-ropes-small-square-fountain-laughing}
In the evenings, the children sit with their grandmother and listen to stories. Grandmother Anna tells them about the saints. She tells them about Saint George, who was brave and kind. She tells them about Saint Nicholas, who secretly gave gold to poor families. She tells them about the holy icons that saved the city in times of trouble. The children listen in the lamplight, until their eyes grow heavy. And then they go to bed, with the stories still in their heads.

[pause]

On feast days, the children get little treats. There are sweet cakes and honey pastries, and dried figs stuffed with nuts. Sometimes their parents give them a small coin to spend at the market. Leon always spends his on a sweet. Zoe saves hers in a little clay pot under her bed. She is saving up for something special. She will not tell anyone what it is.

[pause]

Children in Constantinople also love animals. Many families have cats, which keep the mice away. Some have dogs, which guard the houses. Some have little birds in cages, which sing in the mornings. Leon would very much like a dog. But his mother says that a bakery is no place for a dog. So he makes do with Psaraki the cat, who sometimes lets him stroke her, and sometimes does not.

[pause]

Zoe's best friend is a girl called Maria, whose father is a candle maker. The two girls often sit together on the balcony, talking and spinning thread. Sometimes they help Maria's father in his workshop, dipping wicks into hot wax. The candles grow thicker with every dip. The girls make little candles of their own, and take them to church on Sundays to light in front of the icons.

[long-pause]

{img:aqueduct-of-valens-arches-constantinople-hills-1000ad-morning-light}
A city of three hundred thousand people needs a great deal of water. And Constantinople has some very clever ways of getting it.

[pause]

There are no big rivers in the city. So, long ago, the emperors built great channels to bring water from the hills and forests far away. Some of these channels are more than a hundred kilometres long. They run under the ground and over valleys on bridges. In the middle of the city, there is a great bridge of stone arches, called the Aqueduct of Valens. It carries the water across a valley, high above the houses.

[long-pause]

The water is stored in great cisterns. These are huge rooms under the ground, full of water. The biggest cistern is near Hagia Sophia. It is like an underground palace. Its roof is held up by more than three hundred columns of marble, standing in the water in long rows. It is dark and cool and quiet down there. You can hear water dripping, and see little fish swimming between the columns.

[pause]

{img:basilica-cistern-underground-columns-water-reflections-dim-lamps}
From the cisterns, the water flows through pipes to the fountains in the streets. Every neighbourhood has its own fountains. People come to them every day to fill their jars. The fountains are often decorated with carvings and statues. Some of them are very beautiful, with water flowing from the mouths of stone lions.

[long-pause]

The people of Constantinople also love their public baths. There are many baths in the city, some big and some small. They are like the old Roman baths, with hot rooms and warm rooms and cold pools. People go there to wash and to relax and to meet their friends. Men and women go at different times, or to different baths. Going to the bath is a pleasure, especially in winter.

[pause]

{img:byzantine-public-bath-warm-room-marble-steam-women-talking-soft-light}
Eirene and Grandmother Anna go to the bath once a week, on Saturday afternoons. They take Zoe with them. They take towels and little pots of oil, and a comb. At the bath, they wash in warm water and then sit in the warm room, chatting with the other women. They hear all the news of the neighbourhood. Then they rinse in cool water and rub their skin with sweet-smelling oil. They come home feeling fresh and clean and happy.

[long-pause]

Nikolaos and Leon go to the bath on Sunday mornings, before church. Leon likes to jump into the cold pool and splash the other boys. His father tells him to behave. But he is smiling too. In the warm room, the men talk about the races and the price of grain. They talk about the emperor and his wars, far away in the mountains. And they talk about the weather, of course, because everyone always does.

[pause]

{img:children-throwing-coins-street-fountain-lion-heads-wishing-sunny}
In the summer, when the weather is hot, the fountains are the favourite place for children. They splash each other with water and drink from the spouts. They float little boats made of wood and leaves in the basins. Leon and Theodore race their little boats from one side of the basin to the other. Zoe sits on the edge with her feet in the cool water. And in the shade nearby, the old women sit and watch them, fanning themselves, and remembering when they were young.

[pause]

The water channels and cisterns need constant care. There are special workers who look after them. They clean the channels of mud and leaves. They repair cracks in the walls. They check that the water is flowing freely. If something goes wrong, the fountains of the city could run dry. So these workers are very important, even though nobody ever sees them.

[pause]

In the hottest weeks of summer, the family sometimes goes to the seashore in the evening. They walk down through the city to a little beach of pebbles on the Sea of Marmara. The children paddle in the cool water and look for pretty shells. The grown-ups sit on the stones and watch the sun go down over the sea. Then they walk home slowly through the warm dusk, tired and happy.

[long-pause]

{img:holy-week-procession-constantinople-icons-candles-crowds-evening-1000ad}
The most important feast of the year in Constantinople is Easter. It is a time of great joy, after a long time of quiet preparation.

[pause]

For forty days before Easter, people keep a fast called Lent. They do not eat meat, eggs, cheese or butter. They eat bread and vegetables, beans and olives, and a little fish on some days. The churches are quiet and plain. People pray more and go to church more often. They give food and money to the poor.

[long-pause]

In the last week before Easter, called Holy Week, there are services in all the churches every day. On Thursday, people remember the last supper of Christ. On Friday, they remember his death. The churches are dark and sad. The icons are covered. People walk in slow processions through the streets, carrying candles. The bells do not ring. The whole city is quiet and solemn.

[pause]

{img:bakery-preparing-easter-bread-sweet-dough-red-eggs-family-helping}
In the bakery, Nikolaos is very busy in Holy Week. He is preparing the special Easter bread. It is a sweet bread, made with eggs and butter and a little honey, braided into a ring. The family helps to dye eggs bright red, by boiling them with onion skins and special roots. The red colour means joy and new life. The eggs will be pressed into the Easter bread, and shared with friends.

[long-pause]

On Saturday night, the whole family goes to church, in their best clothes. Zoe wears her blue silk ribbon with the golden birds. Grandmother Anna wears her best headscarf. Everyone carries an unlit candle. The church is completely dark and silent. The people stand and wait, in the darkness.

[pause]

{img:midnight-easter-church-candles-lit-one-by-one-faces-glowing-joy}
Then, at midnight, the priest comes out from behind the screen of icons, holding a single lit candle. He calls out: Christ is risen! And the people answer: He is truly risen! The priest lights the candles of the people nearest to him. They light the candles of the people next to them. And so the flame passes from candle to candle, until the whole church is full of light. The bells begin to ring, loud and joyful. Everyone is smiling and embracing.

[long-pause]

After the service, the family walks home through the streets, carrying their lit candles. The whole city is full of little flames, moving through the dark streets. People call out to each other: Christ is risen! And the answer comes back: He is truly risen! At home, they use the Easter flame to light the lamp in front of the icons. Then they break the fast, and eat the Easter bread and the red eggs, and a little cheese and meat.

[pause]

{img:family-easter-meal-red-eggs-sweet-bread-table-lamplight-night-joy}
The children play a game with the red eggs. Each one holds an egg, and they knock them together, end to end. The one whose egg does not crack is the winner. Leon wins against Zoe, and then Zoe wins against her father. Grandmother Anna's egg beats everyone. She laughs and says it is because she has been playing this game for seventy years. Bjorn comes by with a gift of wine, and they give him bread and red eggs. He knocks eggs with Leon, and loses, and laughs. And the joyful feast goes on until the sky begins to grow light.

[pause]

On Easter Monday, the whole city celebrates. There are processions with icons and banners, and music and dancing in the streets. Families visit each other, carrying red eggs and Easter bread. The emperor gives gifts to the poor and to his officials. And everyone greets each other with the joyful Easter words, again and again, all day long.

[pause]

In the days after Easter, Nikolaos bakes extra Easter bread for the poor of the neighbourhood. Eirene and the children carry it to the little church at the end of the street. The priest shares it out among the poor families. Zoe gives the first loaf to an old widow who lives alone. The old woman smiles and blesses her. And Zoe walks home feeling warm inside.

[long-pause]

{img:constantinople-1100s-walls-domes-later-centuries-ships-golden-horn}
Constantinople remained a great city for many more centuries after our story.

[pause]

The emperor Basil the Second ruled for another twenty-five years after the year 1000. He made the empire larger and stronger than it had been for hundreds of years. When he passed away, he was buried outside the city walls, at his own wish, among his soldiers. After him, there were good emperors and bad ones. There were times of peace and times of trouble.

[long-pause]

About two hundred years after our story, in 1204, there was a very sad time for the city. An army of knights from western Europe came to Constantinople. They were supposed to be going to the Holy Land. But instead, they turned against the city. They took many of its treasures away to the west. Some of those treasures can still be seen today, in churches in Italy and France. The four great bronze horses of the Hippodrome were taken to Venice. They stand there still, above the door of San Marco.

[pause]

{img:bronze-horses-hippodrome-being-shipped-away-sad-harbour-1204}
After some years, the Romans of Constantinople took back their city. But it was never quite as rich or as powerful again. The empire grew smaller and smaller. In the end, in 1453, a young Ottoman sultan called Mehmed came with a great army. After a long siege, the city became part of the Ottoman Empire. We will leave that moment gently, far away. It was the end of the old Roman empire, almost fifteen hundred years after it began.

[long-pause]

Under the Ottomans, the city became a great capital once again. It was called Istanbul. The sultans built beautiful mosques, with tall thin towers called minarets. Hagia Sophia became a mosque, and four minarets were built around it. New palaces and markets and fountains were built. People from all over the Ottoman world came to live there. And the city was once again one of the greatest in the world.

[pause]

{img:istanbul-ottoman-era-mosques-minarets-skyline-bosphorus-sunset-later}
But many things from the old city stayed. The great walls still stood, as they do today. The cisterns still held water. Hagia Sophia still rose above the city, with its great dome. The Golden Horn was still full of ships. And the people of the city went on baking bread and fishing in the Bosphorus, going to the baths and the markets, just as before.

[long-pause]

The learning of Constantinople also lived on. Over the centuries, scholars from the city had copied and kept the old Greek books, of Homer and Plato and many others. When the empire grew weak, many of these scholars travelled to Italy, bringing their books with them. In Italy, people began to read the old Greek writers again. This helped to start a great time of learning and art, which we call the Renaissance.

[pause]

{img:byzantine-scholar-arriving-italy-carrying-old-greek-books-library}
And the faith and art of Constantinople lived on too. They spread to the Rus, to Bulgaria and Serbia, to Romania and Georgia. In those lands, the churches still have domes and icons, golden mosaics and candles, like the churches of Constantinople. The people still sing the old hymns and keep the old feasts. When they celebrate Easter at midnight, with candles and joyful bells, they are doing what the family of Nikolaos did, a thousand years ago.

[pause]

Through all these changes, the people of the city went on living their ordinary lives. Bakers went on baking, and fishermen went on fishing. Children went on playing in the streets and the squares. Families went on gathering for meals and feasts. The city changed its rulers and its name. But its daily life, in many ways, went on much as before.

[long-pause]

{img:yenikapi-excavation-istanbul-ancient-ships-wooden-hulls-mud-archaeologists-later}
In our own time, archaeologists have found many traces of the old city of Constantinople, hidden under modern Istanbul.

[pause]

Some of the most exciting discoveries were made about twenty years ago. Workers were digging a new tunnel for trains under the city, near the old harbour on the Sea of Marmara. As they dug, they found something amazing. Under many metres of mud, there was an ancient harbour. And in it, there were the remains of more than thirty wooden ships.

[long-pause]

The ships had sunk in the harbour long ago, in storms, and had been covered by mud. The mud protected them from the air, so the wood did not rot. Archaeologists worked carefully for years to dig them out. They found merchant ships and warships, big and small. They found the cargo too: clay jars for wine and oil, cooking pots, ropes and tools. They even found the shoes of the sailors, and a little wooden comb.

[pause]

{img:archaeologists-cleaning-clay-amphorae-ship-timbers-lab-careful-later}
The archaeologists also found many other things in the old harbour. There were coins and lamps and pieces of glass. There were bones of animals, and seeds of fruits, like grapes and peaches and figs. There were even the footprints of people, pressed into the mud thousands of years ago. These little things tell us a great deal about daily life in the city. They tell us what people ate, what they wore and what they used every day.

[long-pause]

The great walls of Theodosius still stand today, across the western side of the city. They are old and crumbling in places, and plants grow in the cracks. But many of the towers are still there. You can walk along the walls and imagine the city as it once was. Some parts of the walls have been carefully repaired.

[pause]

{img:theodosian-walls-today-crumbling-towers-gardens-people-walking-today}
Under the old palace area, archaeologists have found beautiful mosaic floors. They show the scenes of daily life that we heard about earlier. There are children playing, and a man milking a goat, and animals of all kinds. They are now kept in a small museum, called the Mosaic Museum, near the Blue Mosque. Visitors can walk above them on wooden walkways, and look down at the bright little pictures.

[long-pause]

In the old church called Chora, in the west of the city, there are some of the most beautiful mosaics and wall paintings in the world. They were made a few hundred years after our story. They show the life of Mary and the life of Christ, with gold backgrounds and lovely colours. The faces of the people look gentle and alive. Looking at them, you can feel what the people of Constantinople felt, when they stood in their churches a long time ago.

[pause]

{img:chora-church-mosaics-golden-scenes-arches-soft-light-visitors-today}
Historians also study the old books written in Constantinople. There is the Book of the Eparch, with its rules for the bakers and the silk weavers. There is the Book of Ceremonies, describing the life of the palace. There are letters and poems, histories and lives of the saints. Through these books, we can still hear the voices of the people of the Queen of Cities.

[pause]

Among the things found in the old harbour, there were also little personal items. There was a wooden comb with fine teeth. There were leather shoes of children and grown-ups. There were little bronze crosses, worn on a string around the neck. There were game pieces and a little whistle. Each of these things belonged to a real person, long ago. Holding them, the archaeologists feel very close to the people of old Constantinople.

[long-pause]

{img:istanbul-today-hagia-sophia-blue-mosque-skyline-bosphorus-boats-today}
Today, the city of Constantinople is called Istanbul. It is the biggest city in Turkey, with about fifteen million people. It is still a city on the edge of two worlds, with one part in Europe and one part in Asia.

[pause]

The Bosphorus is still busy with ships. Big cargo ships sail slowly through the strait, from the Black Sea to the Mediterranean. Little ferries go back and forth between Europe and Asia, all day long. Fishermen still stand on the bridges over the Golden Horn, with their long fishing rods. And the seagulls still cry over the water.

[long-pause]

Hagia Sophia still stands in the middle of the old city. It has been a church, then a mosque, then a museum, and now a mosque again. Visitors from all over the world come to see it. They walk through the great doors and look up at the dome. And, like the messengers from Kiev a thousand years ago, many of them say they have never seen anything like it.

[pause]

{img:hagia-sophia-interior-today-dome-light-visitors-looking-up-today}
The great cistern near Hagia Sophia is open to visitors too. You can walk down the steps into the cool, dark, underground hall. Wooden walkways lead between the rows of columns, standing in the water. Soft lights shine on the columns and the water. Fish still swim between them. It is quiet and peaceful down there, far below the busy streets.

[long-pause]

The Hippodrome is now a long, open square, with trees and benches. The old obelisk from Egypt still stands there, after more than fifteen hundred years. Nearby, there is a bronze column shaped like three twisted snakes, which is even older. Children play in the square, and families stroll in the evening. It is hard to imagine the roaring crowds and the thundering chariots of long ago.

[pause]

{img:hippodrome-square-today-obelisk-trees-families-evening-istanbul-today}
In the old streets of Istanbul, there are still bakeries, everywhere. They still bake bread in big ovens early in the morning. The smell of fresh bread still drifts through the streets at dawn. People still buy round loaves and rolls on their way to work. And street sellers still sell rings of bread covered with sesame seeds, from little carts and trays.

[long-pause]

The great covered market, the Grand Bazaar, was built later, by the Ottomans. But it carries on the old tradition of the markets of the Mese. It has thousands of little shops, under painted roofs. There are shops selling carpets and silk, gold and silver, spices and sweets. Merchants still sit in front of their shops, inviting customers in for a glass of tea. And people still bargain and laugh, as they did a thousand years ago.

[pause]

{img:istanbul-bakery-today-dawn-fresh-bread-sesame-rings-street-seller-today}
In the evenings, the people of Istanbul still love to walk by the water. They sit in little cafés on the shores of the Bosphorus, drinking tea and watching the ships go by. The sun sets behind the domes and minarets of the old city, turning the sky gold and pink. The lights of the bridges come on, one by one. And the city on the edge of two worlds glows softly in the evening, as it has for many hundreds of years.

[pause]

In the old neighbourhoods of Istanbul, there are still little churches from the time of the Byzantine Empire. Some are now mosques, and some are still churches. Some have been turned into museums. They are often hidden away in narrow streets, between ordinary houses. If you look carefully, you can see their old brick walls and little domes, still standing after a thousand years.

[long-pause]

{img:constantinople-evening-1000ad-sunset-golden-horn-domes-ships-lamps}
So we leave Constantinople tonight, in the year 1000, as the evening falls.

[pause]

The sun is setting behind the hills to the west. The water of the Golden Horn turns gold and red, just as its name says. The great dome of Hagia Sophia glows softly in the last light. The ships in the harbour are tied up for the night. Their little lamps are being lit, one by one, and reflected in the still water. Seagulls settle on the rooftops. And the church bells ring out for evening prayer, all over the city.

[long-pause]

In the street near the Mese, the bakery is closed for the day. The ovens are cooling. Old Stephanos has gone home, and Basil is sweeping the last flour from the floor. Eirene is upstairs, lighting the lamps. Nikolaos is having a short sleep before his night's work begins. In a few hours, he will be down in the bakehouse again, kneading the dough for tomorrow.

[pause]

{img:byzantine-family-evening-upper-room-lamps-icons-grandmother-children}
Grandmother Anna is sitting by the window, with her spinning in her lap. She is watching the last light fade over the rooftops. Zoe is sitting beside her, holding the little white wooden panel from Father Theophanes. Tonight, at last, she has decided what she will paint on it. She will paint the dome of Hagia Sophia, with a little golden light above it. She smiles, and puts the panel safely away.

[long-pause]

Leon is lying on his bed, still talking about the chariot race to anyone who will listen. Nobody is listening anymore, but he does not mind. He is telling it to the ceiling now. The Blue chariot took the turn so tightly, he says to himself. And the Green chariot was right behind. And then, at the very end... But his eyes are closing. And soon he is asleep, dreaming of horses.

[pause]

{img:boy-asleep-small-bed-blue-ribbon-lamp-dim-room-quiet}
Down the street, Bjorn the Varangian is sitting on his doorstep, looking up at the first stars. He is thinking of the forests and the lakes, far away in the north. He is thinking of his mother and his sisters. Then he hears the bells of Hagia Sophia, ringing softly through the dusk. He smiles. This is home too, now, he thinks. At least for a while.

[long-pause]

In the family's house, Eirene gathers everyone in front of the icons for the evening prayer. The little oil lamp flickers in front of the Virgin Mary and Saint Nicholas and Saint Anna. They say the old prayers together, softly. Then Eirene kisses the children goodnight. Grandmother Anna makes the sign of the cross on their foreheads. And one by one, the lamps in the house go out.
