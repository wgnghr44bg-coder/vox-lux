One of the most unusual things about Alexander's army is that it travels with scholars. Historians, scientists, geographers and philosophers. Alexander was a student of Aristotle, and he wants to learn everything about the new lands he passes through. So he brings learned men with him, to write down what they see, to study the plants, the animals and the people, and to make maps.

--

The official historian of the expedition is a man called Callisthenes. He is a relative of Aristotle. He writes a history of Alexander's campaign, as it happens. He writes about the battles, the cities, the journeys and the deeds of the king. Copies of his writings are sent back to Greece, so that the Greeks at home can read about Alexander's adventures. It is a little like a newspaper, sent home from the war.

There are also botanists, who study plants. They collect specimens of every new plant they find. Leaves, flowers, seeds and roots. They draw them, describe them and dry them. Some of these were sent back to Aristotle, in Athens, and to his student Theophrastus, who wrote the first great book about plants. In it, he describes plants from Egypt, Persia and India, which Alexander's scholars had seen. The cotton plant. The banana. The banyan tree.

{img:botanist-collecting-plant-specimens-palm-grove-drawing-notebook}
Other scholars study animals. They describe elephants, crocodiles, hippos, ostriches, and many strange birds and fish. Some of these descriptions found their way into Aristotle's great book on animals. For the first time, the Greeks learned about many creatures they had only heard of in stories before. Some of the stories were true. Some were not. Slowly, the scholars sorted them out.

The scholars also study the peoples they meet. Their languages, their gods, their customs, their clothes and their food. They talk to priests, wise men and ordinary people, through interpreters. They write down what they learn. Some of what they wrote is still read today, and is one of our best sources about the ancient world of Persia, Mesopotamia and India.

--

{img:philosopher-and-young-man-discussing-under-palm-tree-evening-camp}
Nikias becomes friends with a young botanist, called Androsthenes. He is only a few years older than Nikias. He is always carrying a bag full of leaves and flowers, and his fingers are always green and sticky. He shows Nikias how to press a flower between two pieces of papyrus, so that it keeps its shape and colour. He tells him the names of trees and herbs, and what they are used for, as food or medicine.

In return, Nikias tells Androsthenes about the distances, and how many stadia it is from one place to another. Together, they make a little map, on a piece of papyrus. The river, the road, the camp, the villages. And around the edges, little drawings of plants and animals that they have seen. A palm tree. A crocodile. A strange bird with a long beak. It is not a very accurate map. But they are proud of it.

{img:lunar-eclipse-ancient-sky-red-moon-army-camp-people-looking-up}
The scholars also watch the sky. They study the stars, the planets and the moon, and keep careful records. Some of them have learned from the astronomers of Babylon, who have been watching the sky for centuries, and writing down everything they see. In a few months, something will happen in the sky that the whole army will remember. But that is for later in our journey.

--

In the evenings, Alexander sometimes invites the scholars to his tent for dinner. They talk about philosophy, about the gods, about the meaning of life, about the shape of the world. Alexander loves these conversations. He asks many questions. He argues and laughs. For a few hours, the great conqueror is just a curious young man, like a student again, sitting with his teachers in the garden of Mieza.

Androsthenes says that every plant has a story. Where it came from, how it travelled, who planted it, and who ate its fruit. He says that if you know the plants of a land, you know its people. Nikias thinks about this. He thinks that perhaps every road has a story too. And that if you know the distances of a land, you know how its people live, and how far they must walk to the market, the well and the temple.
