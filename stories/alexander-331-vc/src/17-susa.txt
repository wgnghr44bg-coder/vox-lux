In the late autumn, the army leaves Babylon and marches east, to the city of Susa. Susa is one of the capitals of the Persian kings. It lies on a plain, at the foot of the mountains of Persia, by a river. The journey takes about twenty days. The land is green and fertile, with fields of barley and groves of date palms. And at the end, there is another great city, waiting to welcome them.

--

Susa is very old too, even older than Babylon. People have lived there for more than five thousand years. The Persian kings built a great palace there, on a high platform. It has a huge audience hall, with tall stone columns, topped with carved bulls, back to back. The walls are decorated with glazed bricks in bright colours, showing rows of Persian archers, in long robes, with spears and bows. And lions and griffins, which are winged monsters with the head of an eagle and the body of a lion.

At Susa, Alexander finds an enormous treasure. The gold and silver of the Persian kings, collected over two hundred years. There is so much of it, that it takes thousands of mules and camels to carry it away. Alexander uses some of it to pay his soldiers, and to send gifts home to Greece. The soldiers receive bonuses. Nikias's uncle receives so much that he says he could buy three olive farms in Macedonia.

{img:persian-royal-treasury-gold-silver-coins-chests-vaulted-room-lamps}
At Susa, there is also something that the Greeks are very happy to find. Long ago, about one hundred and fifty years before, the Persians had attacked Athens, and carried away some famous bronze statues. Statues of two heroes of Athens, called the Tyrant Slayers. Alexander finds them at Susa. And he sends them back to Athens, as a gift. The Athenians are delighted. The statues stand in the market of Athens once again.

Alexander also finds the family of King Darius at Susa. His mother, his wife and his children. They had been captured after an earlier battle, and Alexander had treated them with great kindness and respect. He gave them their own servants, and allowed them to live in royal comfort. The mother of Darius, an old queen called Sisygambis, became very fond of Alexander. She called him her son. And he called her mother.

--

{img:royal-road-persian-stone-road-relay-stations-horses-messengers-mountains}
Near Susa, Nikias sees something that interests him very much, as a bematist. The Royal Road of the Persian kings. It is a great road, which runs for more than two thousand five hundred kilometres, from Susa all the way to Sardis, near the coast of the Aegean Sea. Along the road, every day's journey, there is a station, with fresh horses and riders. Royal messengers ride from station to station, passing the messages on, like a relay race.

The Greek historian Herodotus had written about these messengers, a hundred years before. He said that neither snow nor rain nor heat nor darkness stops them from completing their journey as fast as possible. Today, these words are carved on a great post office in New York. Baeton is very interested in the Royal Road. The Persians had already measured it, and put markers along it. He compares their measurements with his own. They are very close.

{img:bematist-checking-old-persian-road-marker-stone-measuring-cord-sunny}
At Susa, Alexander also sits on the throne of the Persian kings, for the first time. It is a golden throne, under a golden canopy. But Alexander is shorter than the Persian kings, and when he sits on the throne, his feet do not reach the floor. So a servant quickly brings a little table, for him to rest his feet on. An old Persian servant begins to cry, because the table was the one from which King Darius used to eat. Alexander is embarrassed, and almost has it taken away. But a friend says that it is a good sign. The table of the old king is now the footstool of the new one.

--

Nikias spends many evenings in Susa, writing up his notes. He has been with the army for a year now. He has walked from Phoenicia to Egypt, into the desert of Siwa, back through Palestine and Syria, across the Euphrates and the Tigris, to Babylon and to Susa. His wax tablets are full of numbers. He copies them carefully onto papyrus, so that they will not be lost. One day, he thinks, someone will make a map from them. A map of the whole world.
