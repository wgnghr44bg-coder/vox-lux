In September, the army reaches the second great river of Mesopotamia, the Tigris. It is narrower than the Euphrates, but much faster. Its water rushes along, cold and swirling, coming down from the mountains in the north. There is no bridge. And there is no time to build one. So the army must cross the river on foot, by wading through the water.

--

The scouts find a place where the river is shallow enough. But even there, the water comes up to the soldiers' chests. And the current is so strong that it can knock a man off his feet. So Alexander orders the horsemen to stand in a line in the river, upstream, to break the force of the current. And below them, the foot soldiers wade across, holding their weapons and their bags above their heads, and holding on to each other.

Nikias is frightened. He is not a strong swimmer. The water is cold and fast, and he can feel it pulling at his legs. His uncle is beside him, holding his arm firmly. Baeton is on the other side. Step by step, they wade through the rushing water. Nikias tries to count his steps, out of habit. But he loses count in the middle of the river. When they reach the other bank, he is soaking wet and shaking, but safe. Everyone is laughing with relief.

{img:soldiers-drying-clothes-riverbank-fires-evening-after-crossing-tigris}
It takes all day for the whole army to cross. On the far bank, the soldiers light fires to dry their clothes and warm themselves. They spread their cloaks and tunics on the bushes and the grass. The horses are rubbed down and given extra food. Everyone is tired, but cheerful. They have crossed the two great rivers. Now they are in the heart of the Persian Empire.

That night, something extraordinary happens. The army is camped by the river. The sky is clear, and the full moon rises over the eastern hills. Everyone is sitting around their fires, eating supper. And then, slowly, a dark shadow begins to creep across the face of the moon. Bit by bit, the bright moon disappears. And what remains turns a strange, dark red, like copper, or like blood.

--

{img:blood-red-moon-eclipse-over-river-camp-soldiers-pointing-fires}
It is an eclipse of the moon. Today, we know that it happens when the Earth passes between the sun and the moon, and the shadow of the Earth falls on the moon. It is not dangerous at all. But in those days, most people did not understand it. They thought it was a sign from the gods. And many of the soldiers are frightened. What does it mean? Is it a warning? Is something terrible going to happen?

Alexander calls for his seer, a wise man called Aristander. Aristander tells the king, and the whole army, that the eclipse is a good sign. The moon, he says, is the sign of the Persians. The sun is the sign of the Greeks. So the eclipse means that the Persians will be darkened, and the Greeks will win. The soldiers are relieved. Alexander makes sacrifices to the moon, the sun and the earth. And the camp calms down.

{img:bematist-and-old-teacher-watching-eclipse-calmly-explaining-shadow}
Nikias watches the eclipse with Baeton. He is a little frightened too. But Baeton is calm. He explains to Nikias that it is just the shadow of the Earth, falling on the moon. Aristotle himself had written about it. He had even used the shape of the shadow, which is always round, to show that the Earth must be round like a ball. Nikias looks at the dark red moon, and at the curve of the shadow, and suddenly he understands. The Earth is round. He is standing on a ball, in the middle of space.

--

This eclipse was real, and it was recorded at the time. The astronomers of Babylon, far to the south, saw it too, and wrote it down on a clay tablet, in their careful cuneiform writing. That tablet still exists today. Thanks to it, we know the exact date of the eclipse. It was the night of the twentieth of September, in the year 331 before Christ. And so, thanks to the moon, we know exactly where Nikias was, and what he saw, on that night, more than two thousand three hundred years ago.
