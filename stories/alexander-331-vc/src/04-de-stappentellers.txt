Being a bematist is not as easy as it sounds. You must walk at exactly the same pace, all day long, whether the road goes up or down, whether it is hot or cold, whether you are tired or rested. Every step must be the same length. And you must count every single one, without ever losing count. For hours. For days. For months.

--

Baeton teaches Nikias how to do it. First, he must learn to walk with an even step. Not too long, not too short. Baeton makes him walk up and down a measured line, again and again, until every step is exactly the same. Then he learns to count in a special way. Not one, two, three, up to a thousand. That is too hard. Instead, he counts to a hundred, and then moves a little bead on a string, or ties a knot in a cord. After ten beads, he has walked a thousand steps.

The Greeks measured long distances in a unit called the stadion. One stadion is about one hundred and eighty metres. About two hundred and fifty steps. So every two hundred and fifty steps, Nikias moves a bead. At the end of each day, he counts the beads, and writes the number of stadia on his wax tablet. In the evening, Baeton checks his numbers against his own. At first, they are quite different. But after a few weeks, they are almost the same.

{img:wax-tablet-stylus-numbers-distances-tent-lamp-evening-notes}
The bematists do not only count. They also write down everything they see along the way. Rivers and streams, and where they can be crossed. Mountains and passes. Towns and villages, and how many people live there. Wells and springs, where there is water. The kind of land, whether it is fertile or desert. What crops grow there, and what animals live there. All of this is very useful for the army, and for the king, who wants to know everything about his new lands.

Their notes were later used to make maps, and to write books about geography. The distances they measured were so accurate that scientists used them for hundreds of years. One of the greatest Greek geographers, Eratosthenes, used the numbers of the bematists to help him calculate the size of the whole Earth. And he got it almost right.

--

{img:two-bematists-resting-under-palm-tree-comparing-notes-wax-tablets}
At first, Nikias finds the work very boring. Walking and counting, walking and counting. His feet hurt. His mind wanders. He loses count, and has to guess. But slowly, he gets used to it. The counting becomes like breathing. He can count, and at the same time look around him, at the landscape, the sky, the people. And he begins to love it. Because every day, he sees new things. And every evening, he knows exactly how far he has come.

Baeton is a good teacher. He is quiet and patient, and he does not talk much. But when he does, he says interesting things. He tells Nikias about the stars, and how they move across the sky. About the shapes of mountains, and how rivers are formed. About the different peoples they meet, and their languages and customs. He says that a bematist must have eyes everywhere, not only in his feet.

{img:army-road-through-varied-landscape-hills-river-village-palm-trees-wide}
Nikias walks at the front of the army, with Baeton and the guides and the scouts. In the morning, when the army sets out, they walk ahead, so they can see the road before the dust of thousands of feet covers everything. Behind them, the long column of the army stretches back for kilometres. Horsemen, foot soldiers, wagons, mules, camels, and the great crowd of the baggage train. At the very end, there is a cloud of dust, rising into the sky.

--

In the evening, when the army stops and sets up camp, Nikias sits with Baeton outside their tent, writing his numbers on his wax tablet, by the light of his father's clay lamp. Then he writes a little note about the day. A river they crossed. A village where children came out to look at them. A strange bird he saw. And then he lies down on his blanket, and falls asleep at once, still counting in his dreams.
