Tonight we travel back more than two thousand three hundred years, to the year 331 before Christ. We go to Egypt, to the banks of the river Nile. It is early in the year, and the air is mild. And along the river, among the palm trees, there is a vast camp. Thousands of tents, thousands of horses, and tens of thousands of people. It is the army of Alexander the Great.

[long-pause]

It is early morning. The sun is rising over the desert, on the other side of the river. The sky turns from grey to pink to gold. Mist is drifting over the water. Herons stand in the shallows, very still. And in the camp, people are waking up. Fires are being lit. Horses are being watered at the river. Soldiers are stretching and yawning, talking in Greek and in many other languages.

[pause]

The camp is like a moving city. There are soldiers, of course. Foot soldiers with long spears, horsemen, archers and slingers. But there are also many other people. Cooks, blacksmiths, carpenters, doctors and servants. Merchants who sell food and wine. Wives and children of the soldiers. Scholars, writers and scientists. And a few special men who walk with a strange, careful step, counting under their breath.

[pause]

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

{img:young-greek-man-counting-steps-walking-road-measuring-desert-morning}
Tonight, we will travel with this army, not as a soldier, but as one of these special men. A young man called Nikias, seventeen years old, from the city of Pella, in Macedonia. He is a bematist, a step counter. His job is to walk with the army, and count every step, so that the distances can be written down. With his teacher, he is measuring the world.

[pause]

Alexander is twenty-five years old. He has been king of Macedonia for five years. He has already crossed into Asia, and won great victories against the Persian Empire, the biggest empire in the world. He has taken the coast of Asia Minor, Syria and Phoenicia. And now he has come to Egypt, where the people have welcomed him as a liberator, and even as a son of the gods.

[pause]

{img:alexander-young-king-curly-hair-riding-horse-camp-morning-calm-gaze}
In this year, 331, Alexander will do many remarkable things. He will found a new city by the sea, which will become one of the greatest cities in the world. He will travel deep into the desert, to visit a famous oracle. And then he will lead his army north and east, across the great rivers of Mesopotamia, to the ancient city of Babylon, and to the palaces of the Persian kings.

[long-pause]

There were battles on this journey, and we will see them only from very far away, like a storm on a distant horizon. Because most of the time, the life of the army was not fighting. It was walking. Day after day, week after week, along dusty roads, across deserts and rivers, through strange and wonderful lands. Setting up camp, cooking, sleeping, and walking again.

[pause]

{img:army-column-marching-desert-road-dust-camels-wagons-distant-mountains}
So let us begin, by the Nile, on a spring morning in 331 before Christ. Nikias is sitting outside his tent, rubbing his sore feet. His teacher is already standing, with his staff in his hand, looking at the road ahead. Today, they will walk again. And they will count every single step.

[pause]

Down by the river, a group of Egyptian boys are watching the soldiers with big eyes. They have never seen so many strangers. A soldier waves at them, and they wave back, laughing. An old fisherman pushes his little boat out into the river, as he does every morning, paying no attention at all to the army. To him, it is just another day on the Nile.

[long-pause]

{img:pella-macedonia-ancient-city-palace-hill-lake-mountains-4th-century-bc}
Alexander came from Macedonia, a kingdom in the north of Greece. It is a land of mountains, forests and rivers, with wide plains where horses graze. The Macedonians spoke a kind of Greek, and worshipped the Greek gods. But the people of the southern Greek cities, like Athens, often looked down on them, as rough mountain people, not quite real Greeks.

[long-pause]

The capital of Macedonia was a city called Pella. It stood on a hill, near a lake, with a view of the mountains. It was a beautiful city, with wide streets, a big market and a great palace. The houses of the rich had courtyards with columns, and floors decorated with wonderful pebble mosaics. Pictures made of thousands of little river pebbles, black, white and coloured, showing hunting scenes and gods.

[pause]

Alexander's father was King Philip the Second. He was a clever and powerful king. He made the Macedonian army the best in the world. He trained his foot soldiers to fight in a new way, with very long spears, called sarissas, almost six metres long. And he built up a strong cavalry, of noble horsemen, called the Companions. With this army, Philip made Macedonia the most powerful kingdom in Greece.

[pause]

{img:boy-alexander-taming-black-horse-bucephalus-facing-sun-crowd-watching}
Alexander was born in Pella in the year 356 before Christ. His mother was a princess called Olympias. She told him that he was descended from the hero Achilles, and from the god Zeus. Alexander grew up believing that he was destined for greatness. He loved the poems of Homer, about the Trojan War and the hero Achilles. He slept with a copy of the Iliad under his pillow, all his life.

[pause]

There is a famous story about Alexander as a boy. When he was about twelve, a merchant brought a beautiful black horse to King Philip, to sell. But the horse was wild, and nobody could ride it. Philip was about to send it away. But Alexander noticed something. The horse was afraid of its own shadow. So he turned the horse's head toward the sun, so that the shadow fell behind it. He calmed it with his voice, and then jumped on its back, and rode it. The horse was called Bucephalus. And it carried Alexander for many years.

[long-pause]

{img:aristotle-teaching-young-alexander-garden-mieza-trees-scroll-students}
When Alexander was thirteen, his father hired the greatest teacher in the world. His name was Aristotle. He was a philosopher, who was interested in everything. Plants, animals, the stars, politics, poetry, medicine. For three years, Aristotle taught Alexander and a group of noble boys, in a quiet place in the country, called Mieza, with gardens and caves and a spring. The boys learned philosophy, science, medicine and poetry.

[pause]

Aristotle gave Alexander a love of learning that stayed with him all his life. Even on his campaigns, far away, Alexander sent plants and animals back to his old teacher, for his studies. He brought scholars with his army. And he was always curious about the people, the animals and the plants of the lands he travelled through.

[pause]

{img:young-king-alexander-addressing-macedonian-soldiers-assembly-pella}
When Alexander was twenty, his father suddenly passed away, and Alexander became king. Many people thought he was too young. But Alexander quickly showed that he was not. Within two years, he had made all of Greece accept him as its leader. And then, in the spring of the year 334, he crossed the sea into Asia, with an army of about forty thousand men. He never came home again.

[long-pause]

Nikias was only a boy of thirteen when the army left Pella. He remembers it well. The soldiers marching through the streets, the crowds cheering, the king riding Bucephalus at the front. He wished he could go too. He never imagined that, three years later, he would be walking behind that same army, across the deserts of Egypt, counting his steps.

[long-pause]

{img:potters-workshop-pella-boy-shaping-clay-wheel-father-kiln-sunlight}
Nikias was born in Pella, the son of a potter. His father had a little workshop near the market, where he made jugs, bowls, cups and lamps, and sold them at a stall. Nikias grew up among the clay and the wheels and the kiln. He learned to shape a pot on the wheel, and to paint simple patterns on it. But he was never very good at it. His pots always came out a little crooked.

[long-pause]

What Nikias was good at was numbers. He loved to count. He counted the steps from his house to the market. He counted the pots on the shelves. He counted the stars at night, until he fell asleep. He could add and multiply big numbers in his head, faster than anyone. His father said that he should have been a merchant, or a tax collector. But Nikias wanted something more exciting.

[pause]

His uncle was a soldier in the army of Alexander. He had left with the army in 334. Every few months, a letter came from him, carried by messengers going back and forth between the army and Macedonia. The letters told about faraway places. The city of Troy, where the great heroes of Homer had fought. The rich cities of Asia Minor. The great king of Persia and his huge empire. Nikias read the letters again and again.

[pause]

{img:boy-reading-letter-from-army-uncle-courtyard-lamp-evening-pella}
In one of his letters, the uncle wrote about the bematists. These were the men who walked with the army and counted their steps, to measure the distances between places. They wrote everything down, and made maps. The king valued them highly, because he wanted to know exactly how big his new lands were, and how far it was from one place to another. The uncle wrote that the bematists needed a new apprentice, a boy who was good with numbers.

[pause]

Nikias begged his father to let him go. At first, his father said no. Nikias was only sixteen. Asia was far away and dangerous. But Nikias kept asking. And his uncle wrote again, saying that he would look after the boy. That the bematists did not fight. And that it was a great honour. In the end, his father agreed. His mother cried. But she packed his bag.

[long-pause]

{img:supply-convoy-leaving-macedonia-wagons-mules-young-man-waving-mother}
In the spring of 332, Nikias left Pella with a group of new soldiers and supplies, going east to join the army. He carried a bag with a spare tunic, a woollen cloak, a pair of sturdy sandals, a wax tablet for writing, a little bag of dried figs from his mother, and a clay lamp his father had made, a good one, which was not crooked at all.

[pause]

The journey took months. They crossed the sea to Asia, and then walked south, along the coast, through the green lands of Asia Minor, through the mountains, and down into Syria. They passed through cities whose names Nikias had only heard in his uncle's letters. And at last, in the autumn, they caught up with the army, in the land of Phoenicia, by the sea.

[pause]

{img:young-man-meeting-uncle-soldier-army-camp-embrace-tents-autumn}
His uncle was waiting for him. He was a big man, with a grey beard and a scar on his arm, and a warm laugh. He hugged Nikias so hard that he lifted him off the ground. Then he took him to meet the bematists. There were several of them, walking with different parts of the army. And the most famous of them all was a man called Baeton.

[long-pause]

Baeton was about forty-five years old. He was thin and wiry, with a brown, wrinkled face and very strong legs. He had been measuring distances for the army for years. He looked at Nikias for a long time, with his sharp grey eyes. Then he asked him a question. How many steps is it from here to that tree? Nikias looked at the tree, thought for a moment, and gave an answer. Baeton walked to the tree, counting. He came back, and nodded. You will do, he said.

[long-pause]

{img:bematist-walking-steady-pace-counting-dusty-road-army-behind-morning}
Being a bematist is not as easy as it sounds. You must walk at exactly the same pace, all day long, whether the road goes up or down, whether it is hot or cold, whether you are tired or rested. Every step must be the same length. And you must count every single one, without ever losing count. For hours. For days. For months.

[long-pause]

Baeton teaches Nikias how to do it. First, he must learn to walk with an even step. Not too long, not too short. Baeton makes him walk up and down a measured line, again and again, until every step is exactly the same. Then he learns to count in a special way. Not one, two, three, up to a thousand. That is too hard. Instead, he counts to a hundred, and then moves a little bead on a string, or ties a knot in a cord. After ten beads, he has walked a thousand steps.

[pause]

The Greeks measured long distances in a unit called the stadion. One stadion is about one hundred and eighty metres. About two hundred and fifty steps. So every two hundred and fifty steps, Nikias moves a bead. At the end of each day, he counts the beads, and writes the number of stadia on his wax tablet. In the evening, Baeton checks his numbers against his own. At first, they are quite different. But after a few weeks, they are almost the same.

[pause]

{img:wax-tablet-stylus-numbers-distances-tent-lamp-evening-notes}
The bematists do not only count. They also write down everything they see along the way. Rivers and streams, and where they can be crossed. Mountains and passes. Towns and villages, and how many people live there. Wells and springs, where there is water. The kind of land, whether it is fertile or desert. What crops grow there, and what animals live there. All of this is very useful for the army, and for the king, who wants to know everything about his new lands.

[pause]

Their notes were later used to make maps, and to write books about geography. The distances they measured were so accurate that scientists used them for hundreds of years. One of the greatest Greek geographers, Eratosthenes, used the numbers of the bematists to help him calculate the size of the whole Earth. And he got it almost right.

[long-pause]

{img:two-bematists-resting-under-palm-tree-comparing-notes-wax-tablets}
At first, Nikias finds the work very boring. Walking and counting, walking and counting. His feet hurt. His mind wanders. He loses count, and has to guess. But slowly, he gets used to it. The counting becomes like breathing. He can count, and at the same time look around him, at the landscape, the sky, the people. And he begins to love it. Because every day, he sees new things. And every evening, he knows exactly how far he has come.

[pause]

Baeton is a good teacher. He is quiet and patient, and he does not talk much. But when he does, he says interesting things. He tells Nikias about the stars, and how they move across the sky. About the shapes of mountains, and how rivers are formed. About the different peoples they meet, and their languages and customs. He says that a bematist must have eyes everywhere, not only in his feet.

[pause]

{img:army-road-through-varied-landscape-hills-river-village-palm-trees-wide}
Nikias walks at the front of the army, with Baeton and the guides and the scouts. In the morning, when the army sets out, they walk ahead, so they can see the road before the dust of thousands of feet covers everything. Behind them, the long column of the army stretches back for kilometres. Horsemen, foot soldiers, wagons, mules, camels, and the great crowd of the baggage train. At the very end, there is a cloud of dust, rising into the sky.

[long-pause]

In the evening, when the army stops and sets up camp, Nikias sits with Baeton outside their tent, writing his numbers on his wax tablet, by the light of his father's clay lamp. Then he writes a little note about the day. A river they crossed. A village where children came out to look at them. A strange bird he saw. And then he lies down on his blanket, and falls asleep at once, still counting in his dreams.

[long-pause]

{img:macedonian-phalanx-marching-sarissas-upright-like-forest-dust-road}
The army of Alexander is the most famous army of the ancient world. In 331, it has about forty or fifty thousand soldiers. They come from Macedonia, from the Greek cities, from Thrace and from other lands. And with them travel many thousands of other people, animals and wagons. Let us walk along the column, from the front to the back, and see who is there.

[long-pause]

At the heart of the army are the foot soldiers of the phalanx. They are Macedonians, strong farmers and shepherds from the mountains. Each of them carries a sarissa, the very long spear, almost six metres long, made of cornel wood with an iron point. When they march, they carry the sarissas upright, and the long column looks like a moving forest of tall, thin trees, swaying in the wind. They also carry a small round shield, a helmet, and a short sword.

[pause]

The phalanx soldiers are organised in groups of sixteen men, sixteen rows deep. They train for years to move together, as one. They can turn, advance and step back, all at the same moment, with their long spears pointing forward. When they march in formation, the ground shakes. But most of the time, on the march, they simply walk, in long lines, talking, singing and complaining about the dust and the heat.

[pause]

{img:companion-cavalry-riders-horses-macedonian-nobles-cloaks-riding}
The most famous part of the army are the Companions. They are the horsemen of the Macedonian nobles. They ride fine horses, and wear bright cloaks, helmets and armour. Alexander himself rides with them, on Bucephalus. They are called the Companions because they are the king's friends and companions, who eat and drink with him, and fight beside him. Many of them grew up with Alexander, as boys at the court of Pella.

[pause]

There are many other kinds of soldiers too. Light foot soldiers, with javelins and shields, who are fast and can climb mountains. Archers from Crete, famous for their skill with the bow. Slingers, who throw stones with leather slings. Horsemen from Thessaly, in Greece, famous for their horses. And soldiers from many other peoples, who have joined the army along the way.

[long-pause]

{img:baggage-train-wagons-women-children-merchants-mules-camels-dust}
Behind the soldiers comes the baggage train. It is enormous. There are wagons carrying tents, food, weapons and tools. There are siege engines, taken apart, for attacking city walls. There are carts full of treasure, gold and silver, taken from the Persian king. There are thousands of mules, horses, oxen and camels. And there are thousands of people who are not soldiers.

[pause]

There are the families of the soldiers. Many soldiers have married women from the lands they have passed through, and have children with them. So there are women and children walking with the army, riding on wagons, playing in the camp. There are merchants, selling food, wine, clothes and everything else. There are doctors, engineers, cooks and blacksmiths. There are entertainers, musicians and actors. It is a whole city on the move.

[pause]

{img:engineers-surveyors-army-measuring-plans-bridge-building-river}
There are also the specialists. Engineers who build bridges, roads and siege towers. Surveyors who plan the camps. Secretaries and clerks who write the king's letters and keep the records. And there are the scholars. Historians, who write about the campaign. Scientists, who study the plants, animals and stars of the new lands. Philosophers, who discuss with the king in the evenings. And the bematists, who measure the roads.

[long-pause]

Feeding all these people and animals is a huge task. An army this size needs hundreds of tonnes of food every day, and enormous amounts of water. So Alexander plans everything very carefully. He sends men ahead to buy food and collect it in stores along the route. He chooses roads that pass near rivers and wells. He often travels along the coast, so that ships can bring supplies. Without this careful planning, the army could never have travelled so far. Nikias, counting his steps at the front, is part of that planning.

[pause]

Every morning, the trumpet sounds, and the whole great camp begins to move. Tents are taken down and loaded onto the mules. Fires are put out. Children are lifted onto wagons. And slowly, the long column begins to move along the road, like a great snake, unrolling across the land. It takes hours for the last wagons to leave the place where the first soldiers slept.

[long-pause]

{img:memphis-egypt-331bc-ancient-temples-palm-groves-nile-pyramids-distance}
In the winter of 332, Alexander and his army arrived in Egypt. For much of the last two hundred years, Egypt had been ruled by the Persian kings. The Egyptians did not like the Persians. They had rebelled against them several times. So when Alexander arrived, the Persian governor surrendered without a fight. And the Egyptians welcomed Alexander as a liberator.

[long-pause]

Alexander went to the old capital of Egypt, the city of Memphis, near the pyramids. There, he made sacrifices to the Egyptian gods, especially to the Apis bull, a sacred bull which the Egyptians worshipped. The Persians had insulted the Egyptian gods. Alexander showed them respect. The Egyptian priests were very pleased. They recognised him as their new pharaoh, the king of Upper and Lower Egypt, and the son of the gods.

[pause]

Nikias is amazed by Egypt. Everything is so old. The temples are enormous, with huge stone columns, covered with carved and painted pictures and strange writing. The statues of the pharaohs are as tall as houses. And in the distance, on the edge of the desert, he sees the pyramids. They are already more than two thousand years old. They look like mountains made by people.

[pause]

{img:young-greek-man-staring-at-giza-pyramids-desert-sunset-awe}
One day, Baeton and Nikias walk out to the pyramids. Of course, Baeton wants to measure them. He paces out one side of the great pyramid, along its base, counting carefully. Then he does the next side, and the next. Nikias does the same. Their numbers are almost the same. Baeton writes them down, and shakes his head in wonder. How did they build this, he says, so long ago? Nikias has no answer.

[pause]

The Egyptians are very different from the Greeks. They shave their heads. They wear white linen clothes. They worship gods with the heads of animals. A falcon, a jackal, a cat, a crocodile. They write with little pictures, called hieroglyphs. And they believe in a life after death, for which they prepare all their lives. Nikias finds it all fascinating. He asks a young Egyptian scribe to teach him a few hieroglyphs. He learns the signs for water, for sun and for bread.

[long-pause]

{img:nile-river-boats-sails-farmers-fields-date-palms-evening-light}
Egypt is a land of the river. On both sides of the Nile, there is a narrow strip of green, rich, black soil, where the farmers grow wheat, barley, flax and vegetables. Beyond the green strip, there is the desert, yellow and empty, stretching away forever. Every year, in the summer, the Nile floods, and covers the fields with water and fresh mud. When the water goes down, the farmers plant their crops in the wet, rich soil.

[pause]

The river is full of life. Boats with tall white sails sail up and down, carrying grain, stone, people and animals. Fishermen cast their nets. Birds of all kinds live among the reeds. Ibises, herons, ducks and geese. And sometimes, in the water, there are crocodiles and hippopotamuses. Nikias sees a hippo for the first time, a huge grey animal, yawning in the river with its enormous mouth. He thinks it is the strangest animal he has ever seen.

[pause]

{img:soldiers-relaxing-nile-bank-swimming-fishing-winter-sunshine-camp}
For the soldiers, Egypt is a good place to rest. The winter is mild and sunny. There is plenty of food. Bread, fish, ducks, dates, figs and beer, which the Egyptians brew from barley. The soldiers swim in the river, mend their equipment, and rest their tired feet. There are festivals, with games, music and theatre. The king organises athletic contests and musical competitions, with famous performers who have come all the way from Greece.

[long-pause]

Nikias writes a letter home to his father, the potter in Pella. He tells him about the pyramids, the temples, the river and the hippo. He tells him about the Egyptian potters, who make beautiful blue pottery, glazed with a shining colour called faience. He asks a merchant going back to Greece to take the letter with him. And he hopes that one day, his father will read it, sitting in his workshop, among the crooked pots.

[long-pause]

{img:coast-egypt-331bc-limestone-ridge-lake-sea-island-pharos-small-fishing-village}
In the early part of the year 331, Alexander travelled along the coast of Egypt, west of the Nile. And there, he found a place that he liked very much. A narrow strip of land between the sea and a big lake. In front of it, there was a little island, called Pharos, which made a natural harbour. There was only a small fishing village there. But Alexander saw something more. He saw a great city.

[long-pause]

Alexander decided to found a new city on this spot. A Greek city, with a great harbour, which would connect Egypt with the whole Mediterranean world. He would call it Alexandria, after himself. He was very excited about it. He walked over the land with his architect, a man called Deinocrates, and showed him where everything should be. The market here. The temples there. The walls along this line. The harbour on that side.

[pause]

There is a famous story about the founding of Alexandria. To mark out the plan of the city, the builders usually used white chalk. But they had run out of chalk. So they used barley flour instead, which the soldiers had for their food. They sprinkled the flour along the lines of the streets and the walls. But then, suddenly, great flocks of birds came flying in from the lake and the sea, and ate up all the flour.

[pause]

{img:birds-eating-barley-flour-lines-city-plan-ground-architects-watching}
Alexander was worried. Was it a bad sign from the gods? He asked his seers, the men who interpreted the signs of the gods. And they said no, it was a very good sign. It meant that the city would be rich, and would feed people from all over the world. Alexander was relieved. And the seers were right. Alexandria became one of the richest cities in the world, and its harbour sent grain to many lands.

[pause]

Nikias and Baeton help to measure the land for the new city. They pace out the lines of the main streets, which are to be very wide, about thirty metres, wide enough for chariots to pass each other. The streets are laid out in a grid, all straight, crossing each other at right angles, like a chessboard. The main street runs from east to west, so that the cool sea wind from the north can blow through all the streets in summer.

[long-pause]

{img:young-bematist-pacing-out-street-line-new-city-sea-breeze-workers}
It is a special feeling, for Nikias, to walk on this empty land, and imagine the great city that will be built here. Today, there is only sand, scrubby bushes, the lake and the sea, and a few fishermen's huts. But one day, there will be palaces and temples, markets and theatres, and hundreds of thousands of people. And he, Nikias, the potter's son from Pella, helped to measure the very first streets.

[pause]

Alexander himself never saw his city finished. He left Egypt a few months later, and never came back alive. But his city grew, after he was gone. It became the capital of Egypt for almost a thousand years. It had the famous lighthouse on the island of Pharos, one of the Seven Wonders of the World. It had the greatest library of the ancient world, with hundreds of thousands of scrolls. And scholars from everywhere came to study there.

[pause]

{img:future-alexandria-vision-lighthouse-pharos-harbour-ships-library-later}
In fact, Alexander founded many cities called Alexandria, in many lands, as he travelled east. Perhaps twenty, or even more. Some of them became great cities. Some stayed small. Some have disappeared. But this first one, in Egypt, was always the greatest. Today, it is still a big city, with millions of people, on the same coast, by the same sea.

[long-pause]

On the last evening before they leave the place, Nikias walks along the beach in the sunset, with Baeton. The sea is calm and blue. The little island of Pharos is dark against the golden sky. Seagulls are crying. Baeton stops, and looks at the land behind them, where the lines of flour were sprinkled. He says that a city is like a person. It begins very small, and grows up, and has a long life, with good times and bad. And he says that he hopes this one will have a good life. It did.

[long-pause]

{img:desert-journey-siwa-camels-soldiers-sand-dunes-endless-heat-haze}
After founding Alexandria, Alexander decided to make a very strange and difficult journey. He wanted to visit a famous oracle, a place where people could ask the gods questions about the future. It was the oracle of the god Ammon, in the oasis of Siwa, deep in the Libyan desert. To get there, he had to cross more than three hundred kilometres of empty desert.

[long-pause]

The Greeks believed that the god Ammon was the same as their own god Zeus, the king of the gods. Many famous Greeks had visited the oracle at Siwa. The great hero Heracles, and Perseus, were said to have been there. Alexander wanted to follow in their footsteps. And he wanted to ask the god a question, perhaps about his own birth, and whether he was truly a son of Zeus.

[pause]

He took only a small group with him. Some of his Companions, some soldiers, guides who knew the desert, and a few others. Baeton was among them, to measure the way. And Baeton took Nikias. They followed the coast westward for some days, and then turned south, into the desert. Each person carried water. And there were camels, carrying more water in big skin bags.

[pause]

{img:sandstorm-distant-desert-small-group-travellers-sheltering-camels}
The desert is like nothing Nikias has ever seen. Sand and rock, as far as the eye can see, in every direction. No trees, no grass, no water. Only the burning sun during the day, and the freezing cold at night. The wind blows the sand into strange shapes, and wipes away the tracks. There is no road. Only the guides know the way, by the stars, and by the shapes of the dunes.

[pause]

The journey was very hard. After a few days, a strong wind started to blow, and covered the landmarks with sand. The guides were confused. They did not know which way to go. And the water was running low. Everyone was worried. But then, the old stories say, something wonderful happened. Rain began to fall. Real rain, in the middle of the desert. The travellers held up their hands and their cups, and drank the fresh water. And they were saved.

[long-pause]

{img:crows-flying-ahead-guiding-travellers-across-desert-dawn-legend}
Then, the stories say, two crows appeared in the sky, and flew ahead of the travellers, as if guiding them. When the travellers fell behind, the crows waited for them. And so they followed the crows across the desert, until they reached the oasis. Another story says it was two snakes that guided them. Whatever it was, Alexander and his companions believed that the gods were watching over them.

[pause]

Nikias, walking and counting, did not see any crows or snakes. But he did see the rain. And he never forgot the feeling of cool water falling on his face, in the middle of the burning desert. He wrote in his notes that night, simply, rain, in the desert, and everyone laughed and drank. Baeton read it, and smiled.

[pause]

{img:siwa-oasis-palm-groves-springs-mud-brick-town-temple-on-rock}
At last, after many days, they saw a line of green on the horizon. Palm trees. It was the oasis of Siwa. A green island in the sea of sand, with springs of fresh water, thousands of date palms, olive trees and little gardens. In the middle of the oasis, on a rocky hill, stood the temple of Ammon, and the town around it. After the desert, it seemed like paradise.

[long-pause]

Alexander went into the temple alone, to ask the god his question. Nobody else was allowed to go in. When he came out, his friends asked him what the god had said. Alexander only smiled, and said that he had heard what he wanted to hear. He never told anyone the question, or the answer. Some say the priest greeted him as son of Zeus. But nobody knows for sure. It is one of the little mysteries of history.

[long-pause]

{img:army-camp-evening-thousands-of-tents-campfires-smoke-sunset-plain}
Every evening, when the army stops after a long day's march, a great camp appears, as if by magic. Thousands of tents are put up in neat rows. Thousands of fires are lit. And for one night, a city of fifty thousand people springs up on an empty plain, or by a river, or among the palm trees. And the next morning, it disappears again.

[long-pause]

The surveyors choose the place for the camp, near water if possible. Then everyone knows where to go. The king's tent is in the middle. It is a big, beautiful tent, almost like a house, with many rooms, carpets and furniture, taken from the Persian king. Around it are the tents of the Companions and the officers. Then come the tents of the soldiers, row after row, each unit in its place. And around the edges, the baggage train, the merchants and the families.

[pause]

The soldiers sleep in tents made of leather or goat hair, about ten men to a tent. They sleep on the ground, on blankets and cloaks, or on a little straw if they can find it. Nikias shares a small tent with Baeton and two other bematists. It is crowded. One of the bematists snores so loudly that Nikias puts his fingers in his ears. But after a long day of walking, he sleeps anyway.

[pause]

{img:soldiers-cooking-around-fire-bread-porridge-pots-camp-evening-talk}
In the evening, every group of soldiers cooks its own supper, over a little fire. The main food is bread or porridge, made from wheat or barley. With it, they eat whatever they can get. Cheese, olives, onions, garlic, dried fish, beans, lentils and dates. Sometimes there is meat, when animals are bought or caught. They drink water, or wine mixed with water, or the local beer. And they always add salt, which is very precious.

[pause]

After supper, the camp is full of life. Soldiers sit around their fires, talking and laughing. They tell stories about home, about their adventures, about the strange places they have seen. They play dice and knucklebones. They sing old songs from Macedonia. Some of them dance. Some mend their clothes, or sharpen their weapons. Some just lie on their backs, looking at the stars.

[long-pause]

{img:merchants-market-in-army-camp-stalls-food-wine-trinkets-lanterns}
The merchants of the baggage train set up a little market in the camp. They sell food, wine, clothes, sandals, blankets and cooking pots. They buy things from the soldiers too. Souvenirs and treasures from the places they have passed through. There are also barbers, who cut hair and shave beards. And there are fortune tellers, who read the future in the stars, or in the flight of birds.

[pause]

The camp is noisy until late. But at last, the fires burn low, and the camp grows quiet. Guards walk between the tents, with torches. The horses stamp and snort in their lines. Somewhere, a baby cries, and a mother sings. And then, there is silence, except for the wind, and the crickets, and the soft breathing of fifty thousand people asleep.

[pause]

{img:quiet-army-camp-night-stars-moon-tents-guard-torch-silhouettes}
Nikias likes the camp at night. Sometimes, when he cannot sleep, he goes out of the tent and sits by the dying fire, looking up at the stars. They are so bright in the clear air of the desert and the plains. Baeton has taught him the names of the constellations. The Great Bear, Orion the hunter, the Pleiades, the seven sisters. They are the same stars he used to count in Pella, from the roof of his father's house.

[long-pause]

Sometimes his uncle comes and sits with him. He tells Nikias about the battles he has seen. But only a little, and only quietly, because he does not like to talk about them. He prefers to talk about Macedonia. About the mountains and the forests, about the wine harvest and the spring festivals. He says that when the war is over, he will go home, buy a little farm, and grow olives. Nikias says that he will come and help him. His uncle laughs, and ruffles his hair.

[pause]

In the morning, Nikias is woken by the trumpet, before sunrise. He crawls out of the tent, stretches, and splashes cold water on his face. The camp is grey and misty, and full of yawning people. He eats a piece of bread and a few dates, and ties on his sandals carefully. Then he takes his counting cord, and goes to find Baeton, who is already waiting at the edge of the camp, looking at the road.

[long-pause]

{img:ancient-greek-scholars-army-tent-scrolls-plants-specimens-lamp}
One of the most unusual things about Alexander's army is that it travels with scholars. Historians, scientists, geographers and philosophers. Alexander was a student of Aristotle, and he wants to learn everything about the new lands he passes through. So he brings learned men with him, to write down what they see, to study the plants, the animals and the people, and to make maps.

[long-pause]

The official historian of the expedition is a man called Callisthenes. He is a relative of Aristotle. He writes a history of Alexander's campaign, as it happens. He writes about the battles, the cities, the journeys and the deeds of the king. Copies of his writings are sent back to Greece, so that the Greeks at home can read about Alexander's adventures. It is a little like a newspaper, sent home from the war.

[pause]

There are also botanists, who study plants. They collect specimens of every new plant they find. Leaves, flowers, seeds and roots. They draw them, describe them and dry them. Some of these were sent back to Aristotle, in Athens, and to his student Theophrastus, who wrote the first great book about plants. In it, he describes plants from Egypt, Persia and India, which Alexander's scholars had seen. The cotton plant. The banana. The banyan tree.

[pause]

{img:botanist-collecting-plant-specimens-palm-grove-drawing-notebook}
Other scholars study animals. They describe elephants, crocodiles, hippos, ostriches, and many strange birds and fish. Some of these descriptions found their way into Aristotle's great book on animals. For the first time, the Greeks learned about many creatures they had only heard of in stories before. Some of the stories were true. Some were not. Slowly, the scholars sorted them out.

[pause]

The scholars also study the peoples they meet. Their languages, their gods, their customs, their clothes and their food. They talk to priests, wise men and ordinary people, through interpreters. They write down what they learn. Some of what they wrote is still read today, and is one of our best sources about the ancient world of Persia, Mesopotamia and India.

[long-pause]

{img:philosopher-and-young-man-discussing-under-palm-tree-evening-camp}
Nikias becomes friends with a young botanist, called Androsthenes. He is only a few years older than Nikias. He is always carrying a bag full of leaves and flowers, and his fingers are always green and sticky. He shows Nikias how to press a flower between two pieces of papyrus, so that it keeps its shape and colour. He tells him the names of trees and herbs, and what they are used for, as food or medicine.

[pause]

In return, Nikias tells Androsthenes about the distances, and how many stadia it is from one place to another. Together, they make a little map, on a piece of papyrus. The river, the road, the camp, the villages. And around the edges, little drawings of plants and animals that they have seen. A palm tree. A crocodile. A strange bird with a long beak. It is not a very accurate map. But they are proud of it.

[pause]

{img:lunar-eclipse-ancient-sky-red-moon-army-camp-people-looking-up}
The scholars also watch the sky. They study the stars, the planets and the moon, and keep careful records. Some of them have learned from the astronomers of Babylon, who have been watching the sky for centuries, and writing down everything they see. In a few months, something will happen in the sky that the whole army will remember. But that is for later in our journey.

[long-pause]

In the evenings, Alexander sometimes invites the scholars to his tent for dinner. They talk about philosophy, about the gods, about the meaning of life, about the shape of the world. Alexander loves these conversations. He asks many questions. He argues and laughs. For a few hours, the great conqueror is just a curious young man, like a student again, sitting with his teachers in the garden of Mieza.

[pause]

Androsthenes says that every plant has a story. Where it came from, how it travelled, who planted it, and who ate its fruit. He says that if you know the plants of a land, you know its people. Nikias thinks about this. He thinks that perhaps every road has a story too. And that if you know the distances of a land, you know how its people live, and how far they must walk to the market, the well and the temple.

[long-pause]

{img:alexander-the-great-portrait-young-face-tousled-hair-head-tilted-gentle}
What was Alexander really like? We have many statues and pictures of him, and many stories written about him. Some of these were written by people who knew him. Others were written hundreds of years later. They do not always agree. But together, they give us a picture of a remarkable young man.

[long-pause]

He was not very tall. But he was strong and fast, and full of energy. He had a thick mane of light brown hair, which he wore swept back from his forehead, like a lion. He was clean-shaven, which was unusual for a grown man in those days. Soon, many young men copied him. He held his head slightly tilted to one side, and had a habit of looking upward, as if he were dreaming of something far away. His eyes, some said, were different colours, one blue and one brown.

[pause]

He loved Homer, and especially the hero Achilles, whom he believed to be his ancestor. He carried a copy of the Iliad with him everywhere, kept in a precious box, which he had taken from the Persian king. He loved music and theatre, and sent for famous actors from Greece to perform for the army. He loved hunting. He loved his friends, and was fiercely loyal to them. His closest friend was Hephaestion, who had grown up with him.

[pause]

{img:alexander-sharing-water-with-soldiers-desert-helmet-pouring-legend}
Alexander had a way with his soldiers. He knew many of them by name. He shared their hardships, walking with them in the heat, sleeping on the ground, eating their food. There is a famous story, from later in his campaigns. The army was crossing a terrible desert, and everyone was dying of thirst. Some soldiers found a little water, and brought it to the king in a helmet. Alexander thanked them. And then he poured the water out on the sand, in front of everyone. He would not drink, he said, while his men were thirsty.

[pause]

He was also generous, sometimes extravagantly so. He gave gifts of gold, land and honours to his friends and his soldiers. When soldiers were in debt, he paid their debts. When soldiers wanted to go home, he gave them money for the journey. He cared for the sick and wounded. And he was often kind to the people he conquered, respecting their gods and their customs.

[long-pause]

{img:bucephalus-old-black-horse-white-star-forehead-resting-stable-camp}
But Alexander also had a darker side. He had a terrible temper, and could be very harsh. He drank too much wine, sometimes, at feasts with his friends. He could be proud and stubborn. As the years went by, and his power grew, he became more suspicious, and harder to live with. Like all people, he was a mixture of good and bad. And perhaps, being so young and so powerful, the bad grew stronger as time went on.

[pause]

In 331, Bucephalus is still with him. The great black horse is now more than twenty years old, which is quite old for a horse. He has a white star on his forehead. He is still strong, and he still allows nobody but Alexander to ride him. When he is saddled for battle, he kneels down, so that the king can get on his back more easily. Alexander loves him dearly. The grooms take great care of him.

[pause]

{img:young-bematist-seeing-king-walk-by-camp-morning-respectful-distance}
Nikias has seen the king several times, from a distance. Riding past on Bucephalus, at the head of the army. Walking through the camp, talking to the soldiers. Standing on a hill, looking at the land ahead. Once, at Alexandria, the king came to see the bematists at work, and asked Baeton some questions about the distances. Nikias stood behind Baeton, holding the measuring cord, not daring to breathe. The king glanced at him, and nodded. That was all.

[long-pause]

But Nikias will never forget it. The young king, only eight years older than himself, with his wild hair and his upward gaze. Who had already crossed half the world, and was planning to cross the other half. Nikias wonders what it must be like, to be Alexander. To carry so many people's lives, and so many dreams, on his shoulders. And he decides that he is quite happy, after all, just to count his steps.

[long-pause]

{img:army-leaving-egypt-spring-marching-north-coast-road-sea-palm-trees}
In the spring of 331, the army leaves Egypt. It marches north, back along the coast, through the land of Palestine and Phoenicia. The road runs between the sea on one side, and green hills on the other. There are olive groves, vineyards, little towns with white houses, and orchards full of blossom. After the desert of Siwa, Nikias thinks it is the most beautiful country in the world.

[long-pause]

At the city of Tyre, on the coast of Phoenicia, the army stops for a while. Tyre is a great trading city, famous for its purple dye, made from sea snails, and for its sailors, who have sailed all over the Mediterranean. Alexander holds a great festival there, with sacrifices to the gods, athletic games and theatre performances. Famous actors and musicians perform. The soldiers enjoy the rest, the music and the good food.

[pause]

Then, in the summer, the army turns east, away from the sea. It marches inland, through Syria, toward the great river Euphrates. The land becomes drier and hotter. The army marches early in the morning and late in the evening, and rests in the heat of the day. Nikias counts his steps across the dry plains, through little villages of mud brick, past wells where women are drawing water.

[pause]

{img:euphrates-river-wide-brown-water-pontoon-bridge-boats-army-crossing}
After many weeks, they reach the Euphrates, at a place called Thapsacus. It is one of the great rivers of the world. Wide, brown and slow, flowing south toward Babylon and the sea. There is no bridge. But Alexander's engineers have already been working for weeks. They have built two bridges of boats across the river. They tied boats side by side, all across the water, and laid wooden planks on top of them, to make a road.

[pause]

Crossing the bridges of boats is an adventure. The bridges sway and bob under the feet of thousands of soldiers, horses and wagons. The water flows past on both sides, swirling and brown. Nikias counts his steps across the bridge, very carefully. It is about seven hundred and fifty steps, he finds. Baeton finds seven hundred and forty-eight. They argue about it, all the way to the other side.

[long-pause]

{img:mesopotamian-plain-army-marching-north-east-villages-fields-mountains-distant}
On the other side of the Euphrates, they are in Mesopotamia. The land between the rivers. It is one of the oldest lands of civilisation in the world. People have been farming here, building cities and writing books for thousands of years. The Sumerians, the Babylonians, the Assyrians. Their old cities lie all around, some still living, some in ruins, as great mounds of earth on the plain.

[pause]

Alexander does not march straight to Babylon. Instead, he turns north, along the foothills of the mountains, where the land is greener, and there is more food and water for the army. It is a longer route. But it is safer, and kinder to the men and the animals. Nikias measures every step of it. The numbers grow and grow on his wax tablet. Thousands and thousands of stadia, all the way from Pella.

[pause]

{img:bematist-adding-numbers-wax-tablet-evening-fire-tired-proud}
One evening, Nikias adds up all the distances he has walked, since he left Pella. It is so far that he can hardly believe it. More than three thousand kilometres. He shows the number to Baeton. Baeton nods, and says that it is a good walk. Then he tells Nikias that the army has walked much further than that, since it left Macedonia. And that the king is not finished yet. Not by a long way.

[long-pause]

That night, Nikias writes a letter to his father. He tells him about the bridges of boats, the river Euphrates, and the old cities of Mesopotamia. He tells him the number of stadia he has walked. And he tells him that his feet are fine, his sandals are worn out, and that he has bought new ones from a merchant, which are very comfortable. He thinks his father will like to know that.

[long-pause]

{img:tigris-river-fast-current-soldiers-wading-across-holding-shields-overhead}
In September, the army reaches the second great river of Mesopotamia, the Tigris. It is narrower than the Euphrates, but much faster. Its water rushes along, cold and swirling, coming down from the mountains in the north. There is no bridge. And there is no time to build one. So the army must cross the river on foot, by wading through the water.

[long-pause]

The scouts find a place where the river is shallow enough. But even there, the water comes up to the soldiers' chests. And the current is so strong that it can knock a man off his feet. So Alexander orders the horsemen to stand in a line in the river, upstream, to break the force of the current. And below them, the foot soldiers wade across, holding their weapons and their bags above their heads, and holding on to each other.

[pause]

Nikias is frightened. He is not a strong swimmer. The water is cold and fast, and he can feel it pulling at his legs. His uncle is beside him, holding his arm firmly. Baeton is on the other side. Step by step, they wade through the rushing water. Nikias tries to count his steps, out of habit. But he loses count in the middle of the river. When they reach the other bank, he is soaking wet and shaking, but safe. Everyone is laughing with relief.

[pause]

{img:soldiers-drying-clothes-riverbank-fires-evening-after-crossing-tigris}
It takes all day for the whole army to cross. On the far bank, the soldiers light fires to dry their clothes and warm themselves. They spread their cloaks and tunics on the bushes and the grass. The horses are rubbed down and given extra food. Everyone is tired, but cheerful. They have crossed the two great rivers. Now they are in the heart of the Persian Empire.

[pause]

That night, something extraordinary happens. The army is camped by the river. The sky is clear, and the full moon rises over the eastern hills. Everyone is sitting around their fires, eating supper. And then, slowly, a dark shadow begins to creep across the face of the moon. Bit by bit, the bright moon disappears. And what remains turns a strange, dark red, like copper, or like blood.

[long-pause]

{img:blood-red-moon-eclipse-over-river-camp-soldiers-pointing-fires}
It is an eclipse of the moon. Today, we know that it happens when the Earth passes between the sun and the moon, and the shadow of the Earth falls on the moon. It is not dangerous at all. But in those days, most people did not understand it. They thought it was a sign from the gods. And many of the soldiers are frightened. What does it mean? Is it a warning? Is something terrible going to happen?

[pause]

Alexander calls for his seer, a wise man called Aristander. Aristander tells the king, and the whole army, that the eclipse is a good sign. The moon, he says, is the sign of the Persians. The sun is the sign of the Greeks. So the eclipse means that the Persians will be darkened, and the Greeks will win. The soldiers are relieved. Alexander makes sacrifices to the moon, the sun and the earth. And the camp calms down.

[pause]

{img:bematist-and-old-teacher-watching-eclipse-calmly-explaining-shadow}
Nikias watches the eclipse with Baeton. He is a little frightened too. But Baeton is calm. He explains to Nikias that it is just the shadow of the Earth, falling on the moon. Aristotle himself had written about it. He had even used the shape of the shadow, which is always round, to show that the Earth must be round like a ball. Nikias looks at the dark red moon, and at the curve of the shadow, and suddenly he understands. The Earth is round. He is standing on a ball, in the middle of space.

[long-pause]

This eclipse was real, and it was recorded at the time. The astronomers of Babylon, far to the south, saw it too, and wrote it down on a clay tablet, in their careful cuneiform writing. That tablet still exists today. Thanks to it, we know the exact date of the eclipse. It was the night of the twentieth of September, in the year 331 before Christ. And so, thanks to the moon, we know exactly where Nikias was, and what he saw, on that night, more than two thousand three hundred years ago.

[long-pause]

{img:wide-plain-gaugamela-distant-mountains-morning-mist-two-camps-far-apart}
A few days after the eclipse, the army comes to a wide, flat plain, near a village called Gaugamela, in the north of Mesopotamia. And there, waiting for them, is the army of the king of Persia, Darius the Third. It is enormous, much bigger than Alexander's army. Darius has chosen this flat plain on purpose, so that his chariots and his horsemen will have room to move.

[long-pause]

We will not go down to the battle. We will stay far away, on a low hill behind the camp, with Nikias, Baeton and the people of the baggage train. Because the bematists, the scholars, the merchants and the families do not fight. They wait in the camp, behind the army, and hope for the best. It is a long, anxious wait.

[pause]

The night before the battle is very quiet. The two armies are camped a few kilometres apart. From the hill, Nikias can see the fires of the Persian camp, spreading across the plain like a field of stars. There are so many of them. The Persian soldiers stay awake all night, in their armour, because Darius is afraid of a surprise attack. But Alexander tells his men to eat well, and sleep. And he himself sleeps so deeply that his friends have to wake him in the morning.

[pause]

{img:baggage-camp-people-waiting-anxiously-hill-dust-cloud-far-away}
In the morning, the armies march out onto the plain. From the hill, Nikias sees only a great cloud of dust, rising into the sky, far away. He hears a distant noise, like the roar of the sea. He sees flashes of sunlight on metal. He cannot tell what is happening. Nobody in the camp can. They wait, and pray, and wait. Some of the women hold their children close. Baeton sits on a rock, very still, with his eyes closed.

[pause]

The battle lasts most of the day. And at last, in the afternoon, a rider comes galloping back to the camp, waving his arms. The battle is won. Darius has fled. The Persian army has scattered. Alexander is victorious. A great shout goes up from the camp. People cry and laugh and hug each other. Nikias's uncle comes back that evening, dusty and exhausted, but safe. Nikias runs to him, and holds him for a long time.

[long-pause]

{img:evening-after-battle-quiet-camp-soldiers-resting-tired-sunset-peace}
That evening, the camp is very quiet. The soldiers are too tired to celebrate. They sit by their fires, eating and drinking in silence. Some of them have lost friends. The doctors are busy, caring for the wounded. The priests make sacrifices of thanks to the gods. And Alexander walks through the camp, visiting the wounded, talking to his men, thanking them.

[pause]

The battle of Gaugamela was one of the most important battles in history. After it, the Persian Empire, which had ruled the Middle East for more than two hundred years, came to an end. Darius fled to the east, into the mountains. He would never return. And Alexander became the ruler of the largest empire the world had ever seen, stretching from Greece to the borders of India.

[pause]

{img:peaceful-plain-next-morning-grass-flowers-mountains-birds-quiet}
The next morning, Nikias walks to the edge of the camp, and looks out over the plain. The dust has settled. The sky is clear and blue. The mountains in the north are sharp against the sky. Birds are singing in the grass. He does not want to look at the battlefield. He turns away, and looks at the road to the south. The road to Babylon.

[long-pause]

Baeton comes and stands beside him. He puts a hand on Nikias's shoulder. He says that wars are terrible things, and that he hopes Nikias will never have to see one up close. But that now, perhaps, there will be peace for a while. And that the road to Babylon is long and flat and easy. About a hundred and fifty stadia a day, he says. Shall we count them? And Nikias nods, and they set off together.

[long-pause]

{img:babylon-331bc-walls-ishtar-gate-blue-tiles-crowds-flowers-army-arriving}
About three weeks after the battle, the army arrives at Babylon. It is one of the oldest and most famous cities in the world. More than fifteen hundred years old. It was once the capital of a great empire, of King Hammurabi and King Nebuchadnezzar. And it is still a huge, rich, beautiful city, on the banks of the Euphrates.

[long-pause]

Alexander is worried that Babylon will resist him. He approaches the city with his army ready, just in case. But there is no need. The great gates open. And out come the people of Babylon, to welcome him. The Persian governor of the city comes out first, and surrenders. Then come the priests, the leaders of the city, and crowds of ordinary people. They bring gifts. Horses, cattle, gold and silver.

[pause]

The road into the city is decorated for the occasion. It is strewn with flowers and garlands. There are silver altars along the way, burning incense and perfumes, so that the air is sweet. Musicians play. Singers sing. Magicians and astrologers walk in the procession. Lions and leopards in cages are carried as gifts. It is a magnificent welcome. Nikias walks in the procession behind the army, with his mouth open, staring at everything.

[pause]

{img:ishtar-gate-glazed-blue-bricks-golden-lions-dragons-procession-street}
They enter the city through the Ishtar Gate. It is the most beautiful gate Nikias has ever seen. It is huge and tall, covered with glazed bricks of deep, shining blue. On the blue, there are pictures of animals, in gold and white. Bulls and dragons, in long rows, one above the other. And beyond the gate, the Processional Way, a long, wide street, with walls on both sides decorated with blue tiles and golden lions, walking in a long line.

[pause]

Babylon is enormous. It is surrounded by great double walls, so wide that, people say, a chariot with four horses can turn around on top of them. The river Euphrates flows right through the middle of the city. There are temples, palaces, gardens, markets and thousands of houses, with flat roofs, made of mud brick. Hundreds of thousands of people live here. It is far bigger than any city in Greece.

[long-pause]

{img:great-ziggurat-babylon-stepped-tower-etemenanki-temple-sky-sunset}
In the middle of the city, there is the great temple of the god Marduk, the chief god of Babylon. And beside it, there is a huge stepped tower, called a ziggurat. It is called Etemenanki, which means the house of the foundation of heaven and earth. It rises up in seven great steps, higher and higher, almost a hundred metres tall, with a little temple at the top. Some people think that it was the Tower of Babel, in the old story from the Bible.

[pause]

But the ziggurat is old, and parts of it are crumbling. Alexander decides to have it repaired, and to rebuild the temple of Marduk, to honour the gods of Babylon. He orders his soldiers to start clearing away the rubble. It is a huge job. In fact, it is never finished. But the people of Babylon are very grateful that the new king respects their gods.

[pause]

{img:palace-babylon-courtyard-fountains-gardens-terraces-trees-shade}
Alexander moves into the great palace of Nebuchadnezzar, by the river. It is a huge building, with many courtyards and rooms, decorated with blue tiles and carved stone. From its terraces, there is a wonderful view over the city and the river. And somewhere near the palace, perhaps, were the famous Hanging Gardens of Babylon, one of the Seven Wonders of the World. Gardens built on high terraces, full of trees and flowers, watered by machines that lifted water from the river.

[long-pause]

Nobody is quite sure today if the Hanging Gardens really existed in Babylon. Archaeologists have never found them. Some think they were somewhere else, in another city. But the soldiers of Alexander certainly saw beautiful gardens in Babylon, with palm trees, fruit trees and flowers, in the middle of the hot, dry plain. To men who had marched across deserts, they seemed like paradise.

[long-pause]

{img:babylon-street-market-mud-brick-houses-flat-roofs-people-donkeys-evening}
The army stays in Babylon for more than a month. It is the longest rest the soldiers have had for a long time. Alexander gives them money, and they spend it in the city. They eat and drink, buy clothes and souvenirs, go to the baths and the markets, and enjoy themselves. Many of them later remembered Babylon as the most wonderful city they ever saw.

[long-pause]

Nikias explores the city with his uncle and his friend Androsthenes, the young botanist. They walk through the busy streets, which are narrow and crowded, with tall mud-brick houses on both sides. The houses have no windows on the street, only doors. The windows look into inner courtyards, which are cool and shady. On the flat roofs, people sit in the evenings, to enjoy the breeze and look at the stars.

[pause]

The markets of Babylon are enormous. Everything is for sale. Dates, which grow everywhere around the city, in huge palm groves. Barley, onions, cucumbers, figs and pomegranates. Fish from the river. Wool and fine cloth. Pottery, glass and jewellery. Spices and incense from Arabia. Androsthenes buys some seeds of a palm tree, to send back to Greece. Nikias buys a little clay figure of a lion, for his father.

[pause]

{img:babylonian-astronomer-priest-clay-tablet-cuneiform-rooftop-night-stars}
But what interests Nikias most of all are the astronomers. The priests of Babylon have been watching the sky for centuries. Every night, they climb up onto the roofs of the temples, and watch the stars, the planets and the moon. And they write down everything they see, on clay tablets, in their wedge-shaped writing, called cuneiform. They have been doing this for hundreds of years, night after night. They know the sky better than anyone in the world.

[pause]

Baeton and Nikias meet one of these astronomers, an old priest with a long, curled beard, who speaks a little Greek. He shows them his clay tablets, covered with tiny wedge-shaped marks. He explains that they record the movements of the moon and the planets, and the eclipses. He even shows them the tablet where he recorded the eclipse of the moon, a few weeks before, which the army saw by the Tigris. Nikias is amazed. He saw the same eclipse, from far away, on the same night.

[long-pause]

{img:young-greek-and-old-babylonian-priest-comparing-notes-tablet-lamp}
The old astronomer tells them that the Babylonians can predict eclipses, years in advance. They have found the patterns in the sky, by watching for so long. He tells them about the twelve signs of the zodiac, the constellations that the sun passes through during the year, which the Babylonians invented. He tells them that the Babylonians divided the circle into three hundred and sixty degrees, and the hour into sixty minutes. Nikias realises that these people have known things for centuries that the Greeks are only just discovering.

[pause]

Later, Callisthenes, the historian, sent copies of the Babylonian astronomical records to Aristotle in Athens. So the knowledge of Babylon began to flow into the Greek world. Greek scientists learned from it, and built on it. And from them, it came down to us. Every time we look at a clock, and see sixty minutes in an hour, we are using the numbers of the old astronomers of Babylon.

[pause]

{img:rooftop-babylon-night-people-sleeping-under-stars-warm-breeze-quiet}
On warm nights, Nikias sleeps on the flat roof of the house where they are staying, like the people of Babylon do. He lies on a mat, wrapped in a light blanket, and looks up at the stars. The city is quiet around him. A dog barks somewhere. The palm trees rustle in the breeze. And from the top of a temple, not far away, he can see a small light, where an old astronomer is watching the sky, as his ancestors have done for a thousand years.

[long-pause]

Nikias thinks about the old man, and about all the nights he has spent watching the stars, writing down what he sees, so that people after him can learn. It is a little like counting steps, he thinks. Patient, careful work, day after day, night after night. Nobody notices it much. But in the end, it adds up to something great. A map of the world. Or a map of the sky.

[long-pause]

{img:susa-palace-apadana-columns-bull-capitals-courtyard-persian-glazed-archers}
In the late autumn, the army leaves Babylon and marches east, to the city of Susa. Susa is one of the capitals of the Persian kings. It lies on a plain, at the foot of the mountains of Persia, by a river. The journey takes about twenty days. The land is green and fertile, with fields of barley and groves of date palms. And at the end, there is another great city, waiting to welcome them.

[long-pause]

Susa is very old too, even older than Babylon. People have lived there for more than five thousand years. The Persian kings built a great palace there, on a high platform. It has a huge audience hall, with tall stone columns, topped with carved bulls, back to back. The walls are decorated with glazed bricks in bright colours, showing rows of Persian archers, in long robes, with spears and bows. And lions and griffins, which are winged monsters with the head of an eagle and the body of a lion.

[pause]

At Susa, Alexander finds an enormous treasure. The gold and silver of the Persian kings, collected over two hundred years. There is so much of it, that it takes thousands of mules and camels to carry it away. Alexander uses some of it to pay his soldiers, and to send gifts home to Greece. The soldiers receive bonuses. Nikias's uncle receives so much that he says he could buy three olive farms in Macedonia.

[pause]

{img:persian-royal-treasury-gold-silver-coins-chests-vaulted-room-lamps}
At Susa, there is also something that the Greeks are very happy to find. Long ago, about one hundred and fifty years before, the Persians had attacked Athens, and carried away some famous bronze statues. Statues of two heroes of Athens, called the Tyrant Slayers. Alexander finds them at Susa. And he sends them back to Athens, as a gift. The Athenians are delighted. The statues stand in the market of Athens once again.

[pause]

Alexander also finds the family of King Darius at Susa. His mother, his wife and his children. They had been captured after an earlier battle, and Alexander had treated them with great kindness and respect. He gave them their own servants, and allowed them to live in royal comfort. The mother of Darius, an old queen called Sisygambis, became very fond of Alexander. She called him her son. And he called her mother.

[long-pause]

{img:royal-road-persian-stone-road-relay-stations-horses-messengers-mountains}
Near Susa, Nikias sees something that interests him very much, as a bematist. The Royal Road of the Persian kings. It is a great road, which runs for more than two thousand five hundred kilometres, from Susa all the way to Sardis, near the coast of the Aegean Sea. Along the road, every day's journey, there is a station, with fresh horses and riders. Royal messengers ride from station to station, passing the messages on, like a relay race.

[pause]

The Greek historian Herodotus had written about these messengers, a hundred years before. He said that neither snow nor rain nor heat nor darkness stops them from completing their journey as fast as possible. Today, these words are carved on a great post office in New York. Baeton is very interested in the Royal Road. The Persians had already measured it, and put markers along it. He compares their measurements with his own. They are very close.

[pause]

{img:bematist-checking-old-persian-road-marker-stone-measuring-cord-sunny}
At Susa, Alexander also sits on the throne of the Persian kings, for the first time. It is a golden throne, under a golden canopy. But Alexander is shorter than the Persian kings, and when he sits on the throne, his feet do not reach the floor. So a servant quickly brings a little table, for him to rest his feet on. An old Persian servant begins to cry, because the table was the one from which King Darius used to eat. Alexander is embarrassed, and almost has it taken away. But a friend says that it is a good sign. The table of the old king is now the footstool of the new one.

[long-pause]

Nikias spends many evenings in Susa, writing up his notes. He has been with the army for a year now. He has walked from Phoenicia to Egypt, into the desert of Siwa, back through Palestine and Syria, across the Euphrates and the Tigris, to Babylon and to Susa. His wax tablets are full of numbers. He copies them carefully onto papyrus, so that they will not be lost. One day, he thinks, someone will make a map from them. A map of the whole world.

[long-pause]

{img:persepolis-terrace-grand-staircase-columns-mountains-behind-winter-sun}
In the winter, Alexander and his army march into the mountains of Persia itself, the homeland of the Persian kings. The road climbs up through steep valleys and high passes. It is cold. There is snow on the mountains. The soldiers wrap themselves in their cloaks and blankets. And after many days, they come down into a wide, high valley. And there, at the foot of a mountain, on a great stone terrace, stands Persepolis.

[long-pause]

Persepolis is the most magnificent palace of the Persian kings. It was begun by King Darius the Great, almost two hundred years before. It stands on a huge platform of stone, as high as a house, against the side of a mountain. To reach it, you climb a great double staircase, with steps so wide and shallow that horses can walk up them. At the top, there is a gate, guarded by huge stone bulls with the heads of men, and wings.

[pause]

On the platform, there are palaces, halls, treasuries and gardens. The greatest building is the audience hall, called the Apadana. It has seventy-two stone columns, each twenty metres tall, as tall as a seven-storey house. Their tops are carved into the shapes of bulls, lions and eagles. The roof was made of great cedar beams, brought from Lebanon. Here, the Persian king received visitors from all over his empire.

[pause]

{img:carved-relief-persepolis-stairs-tribute-bearers-many-nations-gifts-procession}
The most famous things at Persepolis are the carvings on the staircases. They show long processions of people, from all the lands of the Persian Empire, coming to bring gifts to the king, at the festival of the new year. There are Medes and Persians, Babylonians and Egyptians, Indians and Arabs, Greeks and Ethiopians, and many others. Each group wears its own clothes, and carries its own gifts. Gold and silver, cloth and jewellery, horses and camels, lions and antelopes. They are walking, calmly, in long lines, forever.

[pause]

Nikias walks along the staircases, looking at the carvings for a long time. The people in them look so real, with their beards, their hats, their robes and their faces. Some are holding hands. Some are turning to talk to the person behind them. Some are leading animals by the halter. They have been walking up these stairs, in stone, for almost two hundred years. And they will keep walking for more than two thousand more.

[long-pause]

{img:young-man-studying-stone-carvings-persepolis-winter-light-quiet-awe}
The army stays at Persepolis for several months, through the winter. It is a cold place, high in the mountains. But there is plenty of food, and the great treasury of Persepolis is even bigger than the one at Susa. Alexander and his Companions live in the palaces. The soldiers camp on the plain below. And Nikias and Baeton measure everything. The platform, the halls, the stairs and the columns.

[pause]

In the spring, before the army left, something sad happened at Persepolis. A fire broke out in the palaces, and much of them burned down. Some stories say it was an accident during a feast. Others say that Alexander ordered it, as a revenge for the Persian burning of Athens, a hundred and fifty years earlier. Some say he regretted it at once. Nobody knows exactly what happened. The great cedar roofs burned and fell. But the stone columns, the staircases and the carvings survived.

[pause]

{img:persepolis-ruins-columns-standing-mountains-sunset-ancient-stones-later}
Strangely, the fire helped to save some things. In the treasury and the offices of Persepolis, there were thousands of clay tablets, records of the Persian government. Lists of workers, payments, food rations. When the fire came, the clay tablets were baked hard, like pottery. And so they survived, buried in the ruins, for more than two thousand years, until archaeologists found them. They tell us how the workers of Persepolis were paid, and even that women workers sometimes received the same pay as men, and extra food when they had a baby.

[long-pause]

When the army leaves Persepolis in the spring, Nikias looks back from the road at the great terrace on the mountainside. The columns are blackened, but still standing. The stone bulls still guard the gate. And the long processions of stone people are still walking up the stairs, carrying their gifts, to a king who is no longer there. He thinks it is the saddest and most beautiful thing he has ever seen.

[long-pause]

{img:army-marching-east-high-mountains-snow-passes-afghanistan-long-column}
After Persepolis, Alexander does not go home. He goes further east. And Nikias goes with him. For seven more years, the army marches on, through the lands that are now Iran, Afghanistan, Uzbekistan, Pakistan and India. They cross deserts, climb mountains higher than any in Greece, and cross great rivers. And the bematists count every step of the way.

[long-pause]

We will follow these years only briefly, like looking at a long road from a high hill. The army crosses the mountains of the Hindu Kush, in the snow, through passes so high that the soldiers can hardly breathe. They reach the lands of Bactria and Sogdiana, with their great rivers and their fertile valleys, and their cities of mud brick. Alexander founds more cities, more Alexandrias, at the edge of the known world.

[pause]

In Bactria, Alexander marries a local princess, called Roxana. People say she was the most beautiful woman in Asia. Many of his officers marry local women too. Alexander wants the Macedonians and the Persians to live together, as one people. Not everyone in the army likes this idea. But Alexander is the king, and they follow him.

[pause]

{img:elephants-india-river-hydaspes-tropical-forest-monsoon-clouds-army-camp}
Then the army crosses the mountains into India. It is a completely different world. Huge rivers, swollen with the monsoon rains. Thick forests. Strange animals. Elephants, which the Greeks have never seen before, and which amaze them. Monkeys, peacocks, parrots and tigers. Cotton growing on trees. Bananas. Rice. Wise men, called gymnosophists, the naked philosophers, who sit in the forest and think about the meaning of life.

[pause]

Nikias is amazed by India. He measures the great rivers. The Indus, the Hydaspes, and others. He sees the elephants up close, and even rides on one, sitting behind an Indian driver, swaying high above the ground. Androsthenes, the botanist, is overjoyed. Every day, he finds new plants. He fills bag after bag with specimens. His fingers are never clean.

[long-pause]

{img:old-black-horse-memorial-city-founded-by-river-india-statue-dusk}
In India, Bucephalus, the great black horse, passes away. He is about thirty years old, very old for a horse. Alexander is heartbroken. He founds a city on the spot, by the river Hydaspes, and names it Bucephala, after his horse. Nikias helps to measure out its streets, as he once did at Alexandria. He thinks of the boy in Pella, long ago, who turned a frightened horse toward the sun.

[pause]

At the river Hyphasis, the soldiers at last say that they will go no further. They have been marching for eight years. They are tired, homesick and worn out. They want to go home. Alexander is very angry and sad. He shuts himself in his tent for three days. But in the end, he agrees. They will turn back. Nikias's uncle cries with joy when he hears the news. So do many others.

[pause]

{img:ships-sailing-down-indus-river-fleet-army-banks-tropical-evening}
The journey back is long and hard. Part of the army sails down the river Indus, in a great fleet of boats, to the sea. Then some sail along the coast, and some march through a terrible desert, called Gedrosia, where many people suffer from heat and thirst. Nikias walks through the desert, counting his steps, with his lips cracked and his feet burning. He survives. Baeton survives too, though he is older now, and very thin.

[long-pause]

At last, in the year 323, the army arrives back in Babylon. Alexander has great plans for the future. But a few months later, in June, he falls ill with a fever. And after some days, he passes away, in the palace of Nebuchadnezzar, in Babylon. He is only thirty-two years old. The whole army is in mourning. Nikias stands in the crowd outside the palace, with tears running down his face. He is twenty-five years old now. And he has walked with the king almost to the end of the world.

[long-pause]

{img:middle-aged-man-arriving-pella-road-home-old-father-waiting-potters-shop}
After Alexander's death, many soldiers went home. Nikias's uncle went back to Macedonia, with his savings, and bought a little olive farm, just as he had always dreamed. And Nikias went with him. He had been away for more than ten years. He had walked, by his own count, more than twenty thousand kilometres. He was twenty-six years old.

[long-pause]

When he arrives in Pella, he walks through the streets of his childhood. Everything looks smaller than he remembers. The market. The houses. The palace on the hill. He walks to the little workshop near the market, where his father used to make pots. And there, sitting at the wheel, with clay on his hands, is his father. Old and grey now, but still working. He looks up, and sees his son. And he stands up so fast that the pot on the wheel falls over.

[pause]

His father has kept every letter Nikias sent him. All of them. In a wooden box, under his bed. Some of them took two years to arrive. Some of them never arrived at all. But the ones that did, his father has read so many times that they are falling apart. Nikias gives his father the little clay lion from Babylon. His father puts it on the shelf in the workshop, next to the crooked pots that Nikias made as a boy.

[pause]

{img:father-son-sitting-in-workshop-clay-lion-on-shelf-letters-box-evening}
Nikias does not stay in Pella for long. Baeton has written a book about the measurements of the expedition. It is called The Stages of Alexander's Journey. And he asks Nikias to help him finish it, in Alexandria, in Egypt. The city they measured together, when it was only sand and flour and birds. Now it is growing into a real city, with streets, temples, houses and a harbour full of ships.

[pause]

Nikias goes to Alexandria. And he stays there for the rest of his life. He works with Baeton on the book, until the old man passes away, peacefully, at a great age. Then he works with other scholars, making maps from the numbers of the bematists. When the great Library of Alexandria is founded, some years later, Nikias's notes and maps are among the first things in it.

[long-pause]

{img:old-scholar-library-alexandria-scrolls-maps-young-students-listening}
He marries a woman from Alexandria, a Greek, whose family came from Macedonia too. They have children. He teaches his children to count their steps, and to look at the stars, and to measure things carefully. And in the evenings, he tells them stories about the long journey with Alexander. About the pyramids, the desert, the eclipse, Babylon, Persepolis and the elephants of India.

[pause]

As an old man, Nikias often walks along the main street of Alexandria, the great wide street that he paced out as a boy of seventeen. It is now lined with palaces and temples and colonnades. Crowds of people walk along it, speaking Greek, Egyptian, Hebrew, Persian and many other languages. Carts and chariots roll by. And at the end of the street, in the harbour, the great lighthouse is being built on the island of Pharos.

[pause]

{img:pharos-lighthouse-alexandria-tall-tower-fire-at-top-harbour-ships-dusk}
Sometimes, Nikias stops in the middle of the street, and counts the steps to the next corner, out of old habit. Two hundred and fifty steps, one stadion. Exactly the same as when he first measured it. The city has changed. The world has changed. But the steps are still the same. And Nikias smiles to himself, and walks on.

[long-pause]

He lives to be a very old man. And when he passes away, peacefully, in his bed, his children find among his things a little clay lamp. It is a simple lamp, made by a potter in Pella, long ago. It is not crooked at all. It is old and worn and blackened with soot. It has been carried all the way to India and back. And it still works.

[long-pause]

{img:hellenistic-world-greek-cities-asia-theatre-temple-columns-later}
After Alexander passed away, his great empire did not stay together. His son was born a few months after his death, and was only a baby. His generals quarrelled about who should rule. For many years, they fought each other. And in the end, the empire was divided into several kingdoms, each ruled by one of the generals and his family.

[long-pause]

One general, Ptolemy, took Egypt. He made Alexandria his capital. His family ruled Egypt for almost three hundred years. The last of them was the famous queen Cleopatra. Another general, Seleucus, took most of Asia, from Syria to Afghanistan. Another took Macedonia and Greece. These kingdoms lasted for about two or three hundred years, until the Romans came and conquered most of them.

[pause]

The time after Alexander is called the Hellenistic Age, from the word Hellas, which means Greece. During this time, Greek language and culture spread all over the Middle East and Central Asia. Greek cities were built in Egypt, Syria, Mesopotamia, Persia and even Afghanistan and India. They had theatres, gymnasiums, temples and markets, just like the cities of Greece. People from many different lands learned Greek, and mixed Greek ideas with their own.

[pause]

{img:ai-khanoum-greek-city-afghanistan-ruins-columns-river-mountains-later}
In the far east, in what is now Afghanistan, archaeologists have found a Greek city, by a river, called Ai Khanoum. It had a theatre, a gymnasium, a palace and temples, all in the Greek style. And in it, they found an inscription, with wise sayings from the temple of Delphi, in Greece, copied by a Greek philosopher who had travelled all that way. One of them says, as a child, be well behaved. As a young man, be self-controlled. In middle age, be just. As an old man, be wise. And at the end, die without sorrow.

[pause]

In India and Afghanistan, Greek art mixed with Indian art and with the faith of the Buddha. In a land called Gandhara, artists began to make statues of the Buddha, in a style that was partly Greek. With wavy hair, and robes with folds like Greek statues. Some of the earliest statues of the Buddha look a little like Greek gods. It is one of the most beautiful meetings of cultures in history.

[long-pause]

{img:library-of-alexandria-scholars-reading-scrolls-shelves-columns-later}
Alexandria became the greatest city of the Hellenistic world. Its library collected books from everywhere, perhaps hundreds of thousands of scrolls. Scholars came from all over the world to study there. Euclid wrote his famous book about geometry there. Eratosthenes measured the size of the Earth, using the distances of the bematists, and got it almost right. Archimedes studied there. The Hebrew Bible was translated into Greek there. It was the centre of learning of the ancient world.

[pause]

And Alexander became a legend. Stories about him spread everywhere, in many languages. In Persia, he was called Iskandar. In the Arab world, he was called al-Iskandar, or Dhul-Qarnayn, the two-horned one. In medieval Europe, there were romances about him, full of marvels. In the stories, he flew through the sky in a basket carried by griffins. He went down to the bottom of the sea in a glass barrel. He found the fountain of eternal life. He talked to trees that could speak.

[pause]

{img:medieval-manuscript-alexander-flying-griffins-basket-legend-illumination}
Kings and generals tried to copy Alexander for thousands of years. Julius Caesar is said to have cried, when he saw a statue of Alexander, because Alexander had conquered the world by the age at which Caesar had done almost nothing. Napoleon admired him. Many cities and many children were named after him. Alexander is still one of the most popular names in the world.

[long-pause]

But perhaps the most lasting thing that came from Alexander's journey was not the empire, or the legend. It was the meeting of peoples and ideas. Greeks and Persians, Egyptians and Indians, Babylonians and Bactrians, learning from each other. The knowledge of the Babylonian astronomers flowing into Greek science. Indian plants growing in Greek gardens. And the careful numbers of the bematists, showing for the first time how big the world really was.

[long-pause]

{img:pella-archaeological-site-today-mosaics-columns-visitors-greece-sunny}
Today, the places of Alexander's journey can still be visited. Many of them are in ruins. Some are still living cities. And all of them have stories to tell, if you know how to listen.

[long-pause]

In Macedonia, in the north of Greece, you can visit the ruins of Pella, where Alexander was born. There are the foundations of the houses, the streets and the market. And there are the beautiful pebble mosaics, still on the floors where they were made. One of them shows a lion hunt, with two young men, perhaps Alexander and his friend, attacking a lion with a spear and a sword. Another shows the god Dionysus, riding on a panther. They are made of thousands of tiny pebbles, and they are as beautiful today as they were then.

[pause]

Not far away, at a place called Vergina, archaeologists made a great discovery in 1977. Under a huge mound of earth, they found the royal tombs of the kings of Macedonia. One of them was probably the tomb of Philip the Second, Alexander's father. It was untouched, full of treasures. A golden box, with a star on its lid, containing the bones of the king. A golden wreath of oak leaves. Armour, weapons and silver vessels. Today, the tombs are inside a beautiful underground museum, under the mound.

[pause]

{img:vergina-royal-tomb-museum-golden-larnax-star-sun-dim-light-today}
In Egypt, Alexandria is a big, busy city today, with millions of people, on the same coast, by the same sea. Not much is left of the ancient city. But in 2002, a new great library was opened, called the Bibliotheca Alexandrina, in memory of the ancient library. It is a huge, round building, like a sun rising from the sea, with letters from all the alphabets of the world carved on its walls. And divers have found the remains of the ancient lighthouse and palaces, under the water of the harbour.

[pause]

The oasis of Siwa still exists, in the Western Desert of Egypt, with its palm groves and its springs. On a rocky hill, there are the ruins of the temple of the oracle of Ammon, where Alexander asked his question. Visitors climb up to the temple, and look out over the green palm trees and the yellow desert. And they wonder, like everyone, what Alexander asked, and what the god answered.

[long-pause]

{img:siwa-oasis-today-palm-groves-ruins-temple-oracle-hill-sunset}
In Iran, Persepolis still stands on its great terrace, at the foot of the mountain. The columns rise against the sky. The stone bulls still guard the gate. And the long processions of people from all the lands of the empire still walk up the stairs, carved in stone, carrying their gifts, as they have for two and a half thousand years. It is one of the most beautiful ancient sites in the world.

[pause]

In Iraq, the ruins of Babylon lie by the Euphrates, south of Baghdad. The walls and the palaces have mostly crumbled. But the Ishtar Gate, with its blue tiles and its golden animals, was carefully taken apart by archaeologists, about a hundred years ago, and rebuilt in a museum in Berlin. Visitors walk through it today, and stand in the deep blue light, among the dragons and the bulls, just as Nikias did.

[pause]

{img:ishtar-gate-reconstruction-museum-berlin-blue-tiles-visitors-today}
And in the British Museum, in London, there is a small clay tablet, covered with tiny wedge-shaped signs. It is a page from the astronomical diary of Babylon, for the year 331 before Christ. It records the eclipse of the moon on the night of the twentieth of September. And it also records, in a few short lines, the battle at Gaugamela, and the arrival of Alexander, the king of the world, at Babylon.

[long-pause]

The bematists' books are lost. Baeton's book survives only in a few quotations, in the works of later writers. But their numbers live on, hidden in the old geography books, and in the maps that were made from them. And every time someone measures the distance between two places, carefully and patiently, step by step, they are doing what Nikias and Baeton did, long ago, on the long road with Alexander.

[long-pause]

{img:army-camp-by-river-evening-331bc-palm-trees-sunset-smoke-tents-quiet}
So we leave the army of Alexander tonight, in the year 331 before Christ, as the evening falls. The army is camped by a great river, among the palm trees. The sun is setting over the plain, turning the water red and gold. The smoke of thousands of fires is rising into the still evening air. And the long day of walking is over.

[long-pause]

In the camp, the soldiers are cooking their suppers. The horses are being watered at the river. The merchants are closing their stalls. Children are playing between the tents. Somewhere, someone is playing a flute, a slow, sweet tune from the mountains of Macedonia. And in the middle of the camp, in the great tent of the king, the lamps are being lit, and the scholars are gathering for dinner and conversation.

[pause]

{img:young-bematist-writing-numbers-lamp-tent-doorway-evening-stars-appearing}
Outside a small tent, near the front of the camp, a young man is sitting on the ground, with a wax tablet on his knees. By the light of a little clay lamp, he is writing down the number of stadia that the army walked today. Beside him, an older man is rubbing his tired feet, and looking up at the first stars. Neither of them speaks. They do not need to. It was a good day's walk.

[long-pause]

The night comes softly over the camp. The stars come out, more and more of them, until the whole sky is full. The Great Bear. Orion the hunter. The seven sisters of the Pleiades. The same stars that shine over Pella, and over Egypt, and over Babylon, and over India. The same stars that the old astronomer is watching from the top of his temple, writing down what he sees.

[pause]

{img:moon-over-river-palm-silhouettes-army-tents-silver-light-calm-night}
The moon rises over the river, round and bright and silver. Its light shines on the water, on the palm trees, on the tents and on the sleeping horses. The fires burn low. The flute stops playing. The camp grows quiet. And in his tent, the young man lies down on his blanket, and closes his eyes, and begins to count. Not steps, this time. Just slow, quiet numbers, one after another, until he falls asleep.

[long-pause]

Tomorrow, the army will march on, toward lands that no Greek has ever seen. But tonight, everyone is resting. The river flows quietly past. The wind whispers in the palm trees. And under the great sky, full of stars, fifty thousand people are sleeping, far from home, dreaming of the road ahead, and of the places they have left behind.
