Tonight we are going more than two hundred years back, to the banks of a great brown river, at the edge of a wild and unknown continent, in the spring of the year 1804.

[long-pause]

It is early morning, and a thin mist lies over the water.

[pause]

The river is wide and fast and muddy, the colour of milky coffee, carrying whole trees along in its current, roots and all. Along its banks, there are tall cottonwood trees, their new leaves pale green, and willows, and thick tangles of grape vines and brush. Birds are singing in the woods. A heron stands motionless in the shallows. Somewhere, a woodpecker is drumming on a dead tree.

[pause]

If you enjoy calm journeys through history, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel. Now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

This is the Missouri River.

[pause]

It rises somewhere far away to the west, in mountains that no American has ever seen, and flows for more than two thousand miles across the great plains, until it joins the Mississippi River, just a few miles north of the little French town of Saint Louis.

[pause]

In 1804, Saint Louis is a small, busy frontier town of about a thousand people, mostly French, with houses of stone and timber along the river bank, and a church, and a few stores, and a fort on the hill. Fur traders come and go in their canoes, bringing furs down from the rivers of the north and the west. The language in the streets is French, and English, and the languages of the Native nations who come to trade.

[long-pause]

Until very recently, Saint Louis belonged to Spain, and then to France.

[pause]

But a few months ago, something extraordinary happened. The President of the United States, Thomas Jefferson, bought from the French Emperor, Napoleon, an enormous piece of land: all the country drained by the Missouri and its rivers, from the Mississippi to the Rocky Mountains, and from New Orleans in the south to the Canadian border in the north. It was called Louisiana, and it doubled the size of the United States, at a stroke.

[pause]

{img:flag-raising-ceremony-small-frontier-town-1804}
In March 1804, in a little ceremony in Saint Louis, the flag of France was lowered, and the flag of the United States, with its seventeen stars, was raised in its place.

[long-pause]

But nobody really knows what is out there.

[pause]

The maps of the time show the Missouri River running off to the west, and then a great blank space, with a few guesses, and some dotted lines. Somewhere out there, people believe, there are high mountains. Beyond the mountains, there is another great river, the Columbia, flowing to the Pacific Ocean. Some people believe that the two rivers might be connected, by a short walk across the mountains, so that a boat could travel almost all the way across the continent by water.

[pause]

{img:old-map-blank-west-candle-desk-1804}
Others say that there are woolly mammoths still living out there, and mountains of salt, and volcanoes, and tribes of Welsh-speaking Indians.

[pause]

Nobody knows. And so, President Jefferson has decided to find out.

[long-pause]

On the east bank of the Mississippi, across from the mouth of the Missouri, there is a little camp, on the banks of a small river called the Wood River, or, by the French, the Rivière Dubois.

[pause]

There, in a clearing in the woods, there are a few log huts, built during the winter, and a group of about forty men, getting ready for a great journey. Some of them are soldiers. Some of them are frontiersmen, hunters and woodsmen from Kentucky. Some are French boatmen, who know the river.

[pause]

They call themselves the Corps of Discovery. And they are about to travel up the Missouri River, into the unknown, all the way to the Pacific Ocean, and back.

[long-pause]

On the riverbank, the boats are waiting.

[pause]

There is a big keelboat, fifty-five feet long, with a mast and a square sail, and benches for twenty-two oarsmen, and a little cabin at the back, and a swivel gun at the front. And there are two smaller boats, long, wide, flat-bottomed canoes called pirogues, one painted red and one painted white.

[pause]

{img:keelboat-two-pirogues-riverbank-loaded-spring-1804}
They are loaded with everything the men will need for a journey of two or three years: barrels of flour and salt pork and corn, gunpowder and lead, tools and tents and medicines, scientific instruments, notebooks and ink, and great bales of presents for the Native nations they will meet along the way.

[pause]

Everything is ready. All they are waiting for now is the spring.

[long-pause]

{img:jefferson-study-monticello-maps-books-candle}
The journey was the dream of one man: Thomas Jefferson.

[pause]

Jefferson, the third President of the United States, the tall, red-haired Virginian who wrote the Declaration of Independence, is sixty years old in 1803. He is a man of extraordinary curiosity. He is interested in everything: science and music, architecture and farming, languages and fossils, plants and animals and the stars.

[long-pause]

And for many years, he has been fascinated by the West.

[pause]

He has never been there himself. In fact, he has never travelled more than about fifty miles west of his home, Monticello, in Virginia. But he has read every book and every map and every report about the lands beyond the Mississippi that he can find. His library at Monticello has more books about the American West than any other library in the world.

[pause]

He wants to know what is out there: what the rivers are like, and the mountains, and the plants, and the animals, and the people who live there. He wants to find a route across the continent, by water, for trade. And he wants the United States to claim the West, before the British, or the Spanish, or the Russians, can claim it.

[long-pause]

Twice before, he had tried to send expeditions to the West, and twice they had failed, before they even began.

[pause]

But now he is President, and he has the power, and the money, to do it properly. And in 1803, even before he bought Louisiana, he asked Congress, secretly, for two thousand five hundred dollars, to send a small party of soldiers up the Missouri River, to explore it, and to find a way across the mountains to the Pacific Ocean.

[pause]

{img:congress-chamber-1803-candles-quiet-session}
Congress agreed. And Jefferson chose the man who would lead the expedition. It was his own private secretary, a young army officer from Virginia, named Meriwether Lewis.

[long-pause]

Jefferson wrote Lewis a long letter of instructions, telling him everything he wanted him to do.

[pause]

He was to follow the Missouri River to its source, and find the best way across the mountains to the Pacific. He was to make careful maps of everything he saw, and take measurements of latitude and longitude, using the stars and the sun.

[pause]

He was to study the soil and the plants and the animals, the minerals and the climate, and write down everything about them, and collect specimens, and send them back. He was to learn about the Native nations he met: their names and their numbers, their languages and their customs, their food and their clothes, their homes and their medicines, and their trade. And he was to treat them in the most friendly and conciliatory manner, and to tell them that they now had a new father, the President in Washington, who wanted to be their friend.

[long-pause]

Jefferson wanted Lewis to write everything down.

[pause]

He told him to keep a journal, and to make sure that several of his men kept journals too, and to make copies, on paper made of birch bark if necessary, which would survive the damp, in case anything happened to the originals.

[pause]

{img:leather-bound-journals-quill-ink-bottle-field-desk}
Thanks to this, the journey of Lewis and Clark is one of the best recorded journeys in history. Lewis, and Clark, and four or five of their men, kept journals, every day, for more than two years. Together, they wrote more than a million words.

[long-pause]

Jefferson also wanted Lewis to learn science, before he left, so that he could understand what he saw.

[pause]

So, in the spring of 1803, Lewis went to Philadelphia, the greatest city of science in America, and studied with the most famous scientists of the day. He learned how to use a sextant and a chronometer, to find his position by the stars. He learned about plants, and how to preserve them, and about animals, and fossils. He learned about medicine, from Dr. Benjamin Rush, who gave him a chest of medicines, and many pills, and much advice.

[pause]

And he bought supplies: rifles and gunpowder, tents and blankets, tools and kettles, medicines and instruments, and presents for the Native nations: beads, mirrors, knives, needles, cloth, kettles, paint, tobacco, and silver medals with the face of President Jefferson on one side, and two hands clasped in friendship on the other.

[long-pause]

Then, at the end of the summer of 1803, Lewis set out from Pittsburgh, down the Ohio River, in a new keelboat, toward the Mississippi.

[pause]

{img:keelboat-ohio-river-autumn-forest-1803}
Along the way, in Kentucky, he picked up the man who would share the command of the expedition with him: his old friend, and former commanding officer in the army, William Clark.

[long-pause]

{img:meriwether-lewis-young-officer-portrait-frontier-coat}
Meriwether Lewis is twenty-nine years old in 1804.

[pause]

He is tall and slim and rather stiff, with a long, thin face, a high forehead, and dark hair tied back. He is serious and thoughtful, and sometimes moody and melancholy, given to long silences. But he is also brave, and determined, and very intelligent, and he loves the wild country, and the outdoors, and long, solitary walks.

[long-pause]

He was born in 1774, in Virginia, near Charlottesville, not far from Jefferson's home at Monticello.

[pause]

His father died when he was five, and his mother married again, and moved the family to Georgia, on the wild frontier, where young Meriwether learned to hunt and to fish, and to know the plants and the animals of the woods. His mother was a skilled herbalist, who knew the medicinal plants, and she taught him everything she knew.

[pause]

He joined the army as a young man, and served on the frontier, in the Ohio country. And in 1801, when Jefferson became President, he asked Lewis to come and be his private secretary, and live with him in the President's House in Washington. For two years, Lewis lived in the President's House, and talked with Jefferson every day, and learned from him, and became almost like a son to him.

[long-pause]

Lewis is a good naturalist, and a good writer.

[pause]

His journals are full of careful, beautiful descriptions of the plants and the animals and the landscapes he sees. He loves to walk alone along the riverbank, ahead of the boats, with his rifle and his dog, exploring, and collecting plants, and watching the birds and the animals.

[pause]

{img:lewis-walking-alone-riverbank-newfoundland-dog}
His dog goes everywhere with him. It is a big, black Newfoundland dog, named Seaman, which Lewis bought for twenty dollars in Pittsburgh. Seaman is a wonderful swimmer, and catches squirrels in the river, and guards the camp at night, barking at bears and buffalo. The men love him.

[long-pause]

William Clark is thirty-three years old in 1804.

[pause]

He is tall and strong and broad-shouldered, with red hair, and a friendly, open, freckled face. He is calm and cheerful and steady, practical and sensible, and very good with people. Where Lewis is moody and solitary, Clark is sociable and even-tempered. The men like him, and trust him.

[pause]

He comes from a large family in Virginia and Kentucky. His older brother, George Rogers Clark, was a famous hero of the War of Independence. William served in the army too, on the frontier, fighting in the wars against the Native nations of the Ohio country, and it was there that he met Lewis, who served under him for a while, and they became friends.

[long-pause]

Clark is a wonderful mapmaker.

[pause]

He has a gift for understanding landscapes, and rivers, and distances, and for drawing them. All through the journey, he makes careful maps of every bend of the river, and every creek and island and hill. When the journey is over, his great map of the West will be the most accurate ever made, and will be used for many years.

[pause]

{img:hand-drawn-river-map-compass-quill-candle-tent}
He is a less polished writer than Lewis. He spells words however he likes, and the same word in different ways on the same page. But his journals are lively and vivid and honest.

[long-pause]

When Jefferson chose Lewis to lead the expedition, Lewis asked if he could share the command with his old friend Clark.

[pause]

Jefferson agreed. And so the expedition had two captains, equal in command, who made every decision together. It was a very unusual arrangement, and many people thought it would not work. But it did. In more than two years, through all the dangers and difficulties of the journey, Lewis and Clark never had a serious quarrel.

[pause]

{img:two-captains-discussing-map-campfire-evening}
They complemented each other perfectly: Lewis, the scientist and naturalist and dreamer; Clark, the mapmaker and leader and practical man.

[long-pause]

With Clark came York.

[pause]

York is a tall, strong, powerful Black man, about the same age as Clark. He is enslaved: he has belonged to the Clark family since he was a boy, and grew up with William, as his servant and companion. He has no choice about coming on the journey.

[pause]

But on the journey, York will do everything that the other men do. He will hunt, and paddle, and carry loads, and help to build the forts, and nurse the sick. He will be the first Black man that many of the Native nations have ever seen, and they will be fascinated by him, and treat him with great respect. And at the end of the journey, in the Pacific, he will be given a vote, like everyone else, in an important decision.

[long-pause]

Together, through the winter of 1803 and 1804, at Camp Dubois, the two captains choose and train their men.

[pause]

{img:winter-camp-dubois-snow-log-huts-smoke-1804}
They need men who are strong and healthy, good hunters and good boatmen, brave, and able to work together, and to obey orders, and to live together for years, in the wilderness, without quarrelling. It is not easy to find them.

[long-pause]

{img:camp-dubois-winter-log-huts-woods-river-smoke}
The winter of 1803 and 1804 is a long one, at Camp Dubois.

[pause]

The camp stands in a clearing in the woods, on the east bank of the Mississippi, opposite the mouth of the Missouri. The men have built log huts to live in, with chimneys of sticks and mud, and fireplaces, and bunks. There is a storehouse, and a smithy, and a little hut for the captains.

[long-pause]

There are about forty men in the camp, that winter.

[pause]

Some of them are soldiers, volunteers from the army forts along the Ohio and the Mississippi. Some are young frontiersmen from Kentucky, chosen because they are good hunters and woodsmen: the Field brothers, Joseph and Reubin; George Shannon, who is only eighteen, the youngest of them all; John Colter, a tall, quiet hunter. There is George Drouillard, a skilled hunter and interpreter, half French and half Shawnee, who speaks several Native languages, and the sign language of the plains.

[pause]

There are three sergeants: Charles Floyd, John Ordway, and Nathaniel Pryor. There is Patrick Gass, a carpenter, short and tough. There is Pierre Cruzatte, a French boatman, who has only one eye, and is short-sighted in the other, but who knows the Missouri River well, and plays the fiddle beautifully.

[long-pause]

The men are young, mostly in their twenties, and full of energy.

[pause]

All through the winter, the captains train them, and drill them, and teach them to work together. They go hunting in the woods for deer and turkey. They practise shooting, and hold shooting matches, against the local farmers, and usually win. They build and repair the boats, and pack and repack the supplies.

[pause]

{img:men-shooting-match-target-clearing-winter-woods}
But they are also wild and restless, and sometimes they get into trouble. They fight, and drink whisky, and slip away to the nearby farms and taverns without permission. The captains have to be strict. Slowly, through the winter, the men learn to become a team.

[long-pause]

Captain Clark spends most of the winter at the camp, training the men, and preparing the boats.

[pause]

He studies the keelboat, and decides how to load it, so that it will be balanced in the water. He has lockers built along its sides, with lids that can be raised to make a kind of wall, to protect the men from arrows or bullets. He makes maps, from the reports of traders who have been up the Missouri.

[pause]

Captain Lewis spends much of the winter in Saint Louis, across the river, talking to the fur traders and the merchants, and buying more supplies, and collecting information about the river, and the Native nations along it.

[long-pause]

Every day, Clark writes down the weather in his journal: cold, snow, rain, ice in the river, a fine day.

[pause]

In January, the river freezes, and great sheets of ice float down the Mississippi, grinding and crashing against each other. In February, there is snow. In March, the ice begins to break up, and the river rises, and floods. The birds come back: the geese and the ducks and the swans, flying north in great flocks, honking, over the camp.

[pause]

{img:geese-flying-north-over-river-ice-breaking-march}
And in April, the trees begin to leaf, and the woods fill with flowers, and the men grow impatient to go.

[long-pause]

They pack the boats.

[pause]

They pack seven tons of supplies: barrels of flour and pork and corn and salt and lard and biscuits; bags of coffee and sugar; casks of whisky; kegs of gunpowder, sealed with wax in lead canisters, which can be melted down to make bullets when the powder is used; rifles and muskets and tools; tents and blankets; medicines; scientific instruments; and twenty-one bales of presents for the Native nations.

[pause]

They plan to hunt for most of their food along the way. But they must carry enough to keep them alive if the hunting is poor.

[long-pause]

On the evening of the thirteenth of May, 1804, everything is ready.

[pause]

The men sit around their fires, on the last night at Camp Dubois, talking quietly. Cruzatte plays his fiddle. Some of the men dance. Some write letters to their families, to be left behind and posted. Some just sit and look at the fire, and at the dark river, and think about what lies ahead.

[pause]

{img:last-night-camp-fiddle-campfire-men-dancing-spring}
Tomorrow, they will set out up the Missouri River, into country that no American has ever seen. They do not know how long they will be gone, or if they will ever come back.

[long-pause]

{img:keelboat-setting-out-rain-afternoon-missouri-may-1804}
On the afternoon of Monday, the fourteenth of May, 1804, in a gentle rain, the Corps of Discovery sets out.

[pause]

Captain Clark is in command, because Captain Lewis is still in Saint Louis, finishing his business. The men push the keelboat and the two pirogues out into the Mississippi, and cross the wide brown river, and turn into the mouth of the Missouri. A few people from the nearby farms stand on the bank to watch them go, and wave.

[long-pause]

Clark writes in his journal that evening: I set out at four o'clock, in the presence of many of the neighbouring inhabitants, and proceeded on under a gentle breeze, up the Missouri.

[pause]

They go only a few miles, that first afternoon, and camp on an island. It is a short beginning to a very long journey.

[pause]

A few days later, they reach the little French village of Saint Charles, on the north bank of the river, where the people welcome them with a ball, and the men dance with the French girls. And there, on the twentieth of May, Captain Lewis rides in from Saint Louis, and joins them. The next day, they set out again, up the river, all together.

[long-pause]

Travelling up the Missouri is very hard work.

[pause]

The river flows fast, against them, and its channel twists and turns, and is full of sandbars and snags: whole trees, which have fallen into the river, and lie hidden under the muddy water, with their branches pointing upstream, waiting to rip the bottom out of a boat. The banks are always falling in, as the river cuts away at them, with great crashes, sometimes just beside the boats.

[pause]

{img:men-towing-keelboat-rope-riverbank-mud}
When the wind is behind them, they can raise the sail. But most of the time, they must row, or push the boats along with long poles, walking along the narrow gangways on each side of the keelboat, pushing the poles into the river bed, and walking back toward the stern. And when the current is too strong, the men must get out, and walk along the bank, or wade in the water, pulling the boats with long ropes, like horses on a towpath.

[long-pause]

On a good day, they travel fifteen or twenty miles. On a bad day, only a few.

[pause]

The men work very hard, from dawn until dusk, in the heat and the rain, the mud and the mosquitoes. Their hands are blistered from the oars and the ropes. Their feet are cut by the rocks and the sharp sticks in the mud. Many of them suffer from boils, and upset stomachs, and sunstroke.

[pause]

But they are young and strong and cheerful, and they sing as they row, the old songs of the French boatmen, and the songs of Kentucky and Virginia.

[long-pause]

The country along the lower Missouri is beautiful.

[pause]

There are woods of cottonwood and sycamore and walnut and oak along the banks, and open meadows and prairies between them, covered with tall grass and wild flowers. There are steep bluffs of limestone, and caves, and clear creeks running into the river. There are wild grapes and raspberries and plums and mulberries, which the men pick and eat.

[pause]

{img:limestone-bluffs-prairie-flowers-missouri-river-june}
There are animals everywhere: deer, and turkeys, and geese, and ducks, and beaver, and bears, and, further up the river, elk. The hunters go out every day, along the banks, and bring back meat for the men's dinner.

[long-pause]

In June, they pass the last little settlements of white people along the river: a few farms, and a little village called La Charette, where an old frontiersman named Daniel Boone, the famous hunter and explorer of Kentucky, now lives, in his old age.

[pause]

After that, there are no more settlements. They are in the wilderness.

[pause]

They meet a few fur traders, coming down the river in canoes, loaded with furs and buffalo grease, who tell them about the country upstream, and the Native nations who live there. But otherwise, they are alone.

[long-pause]

In the evenings, they camp on the bank of the river, or on an island, or on a sandbar, where there are fewer mosquitoes.

[pause]

The men pull the boats up onto the bank, and set up the tents, and gather wood, and light fires, and cook their supper: venison or turkey or beef, with corn and flour, boiled or roasted over the fire. They clean their rifles, and mend their clothes and their moccasins, and the captains write their journals by the light of the fire.

[pause]

{img:evening-camp-sandbar-tents-fires-boats-pulled-up}
Then Cruzatte takes out his fiddle, and plays, and some of the men dance, on the sand, in the firelight, and the others clap and sing. And then they lie down, wrapped in their blankets, under the stars, with the river rushing past in the darkness, and the sentries watching, and the frogs and the whip-poor-wills calling from the woods.

[long-pause]

{img:dawn-camp-missouri-men-waking-mist-cottonwoods}
Let us spend an ordinary day with the Corps of Discovery, on the Missouri River, in the summer of 1804.

[pause]

The day begins at dawn, or even before. A sergeant goes from blanket to blanket, waking the men. They roll up their blankets, and pack their things, and eat a quick breakfast of cold meat from the night before, or hominy, boiled corn, and a little coffee, if there is any.

[long-pause]

Then they load the boats, and push off from the bank, as the sun rises over the river, and the mist lifts from the water.

[pause]

The captains take turns. One day, Clark stays in the keelboat, steering it, and directing the men, and making his maps of every bend in the river, with a compass, measuring the distances and the directions. And Lewis walks along the bank, with Seaman, his dog, collecting plants, and watching the birds and the animals, and writing his notes.

[pause]

The next day, they change places.

[long-pause]

The hunters go out early, on foot, or sometimes on horseback, along the riverbank, ahead of the boats.

[pause]

Drouillard is the best hunter of all. He can track any animal, and almost never misses. He and the other hunters bring back deer, and elk, and turkeys, and later, buffalo, and leave the meat hanging in a tree by the river, where the boats can pick it up as they pass.

[pause]

{img:hunter-deer-carcass-hanging-tree-riverbank-boat-approaching}
The men need a great deal of meat. Each man eats about nine pounds of meat a day, when they can get it, because the work is so hard. That is about one deer, or a quarter of a buffalo, for every four men, every day.

[long-pause]

At midday, they stop for a little while, and rest in the shade, and eat.

[pause]

Then they go on, through the long, hot afternoon. The sun beats down on the river, and the men row and pole and pull, and sweat, and swat at the mosquitoes and the gnats and the ticks, which are terrible in the summer. They tie handkerchiefs over their faces, and smear grease on their skin.

[pause]

Clark writes in his journal that the mosquitoes are so thick that he cannot keep them out of his eyes, nose, ears and throat.

[long-pause]

Along the way, the captains name things.

[pause]

Every creek and river and island and hill that they pass, that does not already have a name that they know, they give a name to. They name rivers after the men of the expedition, and after their friends and their families, and after the things that happen there. There is a Floyd's River, and a Shannon's Creek, and a Fourth of July Creek, and a Pryor's Creek, and an Ordway's Creek.

[pause]

{img:clark-writing-names-map-boat-stern-river-bend}
Some of the names are still used today. Most of them have been forgotten.

[long-pause]

The captains also write down everything new that they see.

[pause]

They describe more than a hundred and seventy plants, and more than a hundred animals, that no scientist has ever described before. They see a coyote, which they call a prairie wolf, and a jack rabbit, and a badger, and a pronghorn antelope, and a mule deer, with its great ears. They see magpies, and burrowing owls, and prairie dogs.

[pause]

{img:prairie-dog-town-burrows-mounds-grassland-sun}
They send specimens back to President Jefferson: skins and bones and horns, plants pressed between sheets of paper, seeds, minerals, and even, in the spring of 1805, a live prairie dog and four live magpies, in cages, which travel all the way down the river and across the country to Washington. Only the prairie dog and one magpie survive the journey. Jefferson is delighted with them.

[long-pause]

In the late afternoon, the captains look for a good place to camp for the night.

[pause]

The men pull the boats into the bank, and unload what they need for the night. They put up the tents, for the captains and the sergeants. The other men sleep in the open, or under little shelters of branches, or under the sail, stretched over poles.

[pause]

They cook supper, a great meal of meat, roasted or boiled, with corn or flour. They mend their clothes, and their moccasins, which wear out in a few days, and must be replaced, cut and sewn from deer skin. They clean their rifles, and check their powder.

[long-pause]

And as night falls, the captains sit by the fire, and write their journals.

[pause]

Lewis writes about the plants and animals he has seen that day, carefully, in his long, flowing hand. Clark writes about the river, and the distance they have travelled, and the weather, and the events of the day, in his rough, cheerful spelling. Sergeant Ordway and Sergeant Gass and Private Whitehouse write their journals too, in their own way.

[pause]

{img:captains-writing-journals-campfire-night-candle-stars}
Then the captains go to their tent, and the men lie down to sleep, and the fire burns low, and the river rushes past, and the stars wheel slowly over the wide, dark land.

[long-pause]

{img:july-fourth-1804-camp-river-evening-salute}
On the fourth of July, 1804, the Corps of Discovery celebrates Independence Day, on the banks of the Missouri River, in what is now Kansas.

[pause]

It is the first Independence Day ever celebrated west of the Mississippi. In the morning, they fire the swivel gun on the keelboat, as a salute. In the evening, they fire it again, and each man is given an extra ration of whisky.

[long-pause]

They name a creek Fourth of July Creek, and another Independence Creek.

[pause]

And Clark writes, that evening, about the beautiful prairie that they passed that day, one of the most beautiful he has ever seen, open and high, with a little creek winding through it, lined with trees, and the grass and flowers waving in the wind.

[pause]

The men are happy, and well. They have been on the river for seven weeks, and are now about five hundred miles from Saint Louis.

[long-pause]

As they go further up the river, the country begins to change.

[pause]

The woods along the banks grow thinner, and the prairies grow wider. The hills are lower and smoother, covered with tall grass. The sky seems to grow bigger. And they begin to see new animals: elk, in great herds, and antelope, and, in the distance, the first buffalo.

[pause]

{img:wide-open-prairie-big-sky-elk-herd-distance-river}
At the beginning of August, they meet the first of the Native nations, the Oto and the Missouria, at a place on the river bank that they call Council Bluff.

[long-pause]

The meeting is friendly.

[pause]

The captains put on their best uniforms, with their cocked hats and their swords, and set up a sail as an awning, for shade, and the men parade with their rifles. The chiefs come, with their people, and sit in a circle, and smoke the pipe, and listen while Lewis makes a long speech, telling them that they now have a new great father, the President in Washington, who wants to be their friend, and to trade with them, and wants them to live in peace with their neighbours.

[pause]

Then they give the chiefs presents: medals with the face of Jefferson, and flags, and clothes, and beads and paint and tobacco and kettles. The chiefs make speeches too, and ask for more presents, and for guns, and for traders to come. It is the first of many such meetings, all the way up the river.

[long-pause]

In August, there is a sad time.

[pause]

One of the sergeants, Charles Floyd, a young man of twenty-two, from Kentucky, well liked by everyone, falls ill. The captains do everything they can for him. But he grows weaker. And on the twentieth of August, he passes away, quietly, near the present city of Sioux City, in Iowa.

[pause]

{img:hilltop-cedar-post-grave-overlooking-river-sunset}
He is the only member of the Corps of Discovery to die during the whole journey. Today, doctors believe that he had appendicitis, which nobody, at that time, anywhere in the world, knew how to cure.

[pause]

The men bury him on the top of a high bluff, overlooking the river, with military honours, and Lewis reads a prayer, and they put up a cedar post on his grave, with his name carved on it. They name the bluff Floyd's Bluff, and a little river nearby Floyd's River.

[long-pause]

A few days later, they choose a new sergeant, by vote of the men. It is the first election held west of the Mississippi.

[pause]

The men choose Patrick Gass, the tough little carpenter. And they go on up the river.

[pause]

{img:men-voting-hands-raised-camp-riverbank-morning}
At the end of August, Lewis and Clark and some of the men walk out onto the prairie, to visit a strange, round hill, which the local people call Spirit Mound, and believe is the home of little spirits. From the top, they see a wonderful sight: the plains all around, for miles and miles, covered with great herds of buffalo, grazing.

[long-pause]

From now on, they see buffalo almost every day.

[pause]

Sometimes there are so many that they cover the whole plain, as far as the eye can see, a dark, moving mass of animals, grazing slowly, or moving together, with a sound like distant thunder. The men can hardly believe it. They estimate that they can see three thousand, five thousand, ten thousand at a time.

[pause]

The buffalo meat is delicious, the men say, better than beef. The hunters shoot them for food, and the men eat the tongues, and the humps, and the marrow from the bones, which they think the greatest treat of all.

[long-pause]

In September, they pass through the country of the Lakota, the great Sioux nation, who are the most powerful people on the Missouri.

[pause]

There is a tense meeting, near the mouth of the Bad River, where some of the young Lakota warriors seize the rope of one of the boats, and for a few moments, it seems that there might be fighting. But the chief, Black Buffalo, steps in, and calms everyone down, and the danger passes. The captains and the Lakota smoke the pipe, and share a feast, and watch the Lakota dance, and then the boats go on up the river.

[pause]

{img:river-boats-continuing-upstream-autumn-plains-calm}
As autumn comes, the leaves of the cottonwoods along the river turn yellow, and the nights grow cold. Great flocks of geese fly south over the boats, honking. And the captains know that they must find a place to spend the winter, before the river freezes.

[long-pause]

{img:mandan-earth-lodge-village-bluffs-river-autumn}
At the end of October, 1804, the Corps of Discovery reaches the villages of the Mandan and the Hidatsa people, on the upper Missouri, in what is now North Dakota.

[pause]

They have travelled about sixteen hundred miles up the river, in five and a half months.

[long-pause]

The Mandan villages are a wonderful sight.

[pause]

They stand on high bluffs above the river, surrounded by fences of wooden stakes. Inside, there are dozens of great round houses, called earth lodges, each one like a huge dome, covered with earth and grass, with a hole in the middle of the roof to let out the smoke.

[pause]

Around the villages, there are fields, where the Mandan women grow corn and beans and squash and sunflowers, in the rich soil of the river bottoms. The Mandan are farmers and traders. Their villages are the great trading centre of the northern plains. Native people come from hundreds of miles away to trade with them: horses and buffalo robes and dried meat from the plains, for corn and beans and squash. And British fur traders come down from Canada to trade too.

[long-pause]

The Mandan are friendly, and welcome the strangers.

[pause]

Their chief, Sheheke, whose name means Big White, because he is very large and pale, is a kind and generous man, who becomes a good friend of the captains. Another chief, Black Cat, is wise and thoughtful, and helps them in many ways.

[pause]

{img:mandan-chief-robe-friendly-meeting-captains-lodge}
The captains decide to spend the winter here, near the villages. They choose a place on the river bank, a few miles downstream, among the cottonwood trees, and the men begin to build a fort.

[long-pause]

They call it Fort Mandan.

[pause]

It is built of cottonwood logs, in the shape of a triangle, with two rows of huts, joined at one corner, and a high wall of logs along the third side, facing the river, with a gate. There are eight rooms, with fireplaces and chimneys, and lofts for storage. The men work hard, and finish it by the end of November, just in time.

[pause]

Because then the winter comes.

[long-pause]

The winter on the northern plains is the coldest that any of the men have ever known.

[pause]

In December, the river freezes solid, and the snow lies deep on the plains, and the wind blows from the north, across the open country, bitter and fierce. On some days, the temperature falls to forty degrees below zero. The men's breath freezes on their faces. Their feet and hands are frostbitten when they go out to hunt.

[pause]

{img:frozen-river-snowy-plains-bitter-cold-dawn-light}
But inside the fort, by the big fires, it is warm and snug. The men cook and eat and sleep and work, and play cards, and dance, and tell stories, and wait for the spring.

[long-pause]

The Mandan people visit the fort every day, and the men visit the villages.

[pause]

They trade with each other. The Mandan bring corn and beans and squash, and the men give them things in exchange. The expedition's blacksmith, John Shields, sets up his forge in the fort, and makes and repairs axes and hoes and kettles for the Mandan, in exchange for corn. The corn keeps the men alive through the winter.

[pause]

{img:blacksmith-forge-fort-trading-axes-corn-snow}
The men go to the villages to dance, and to watch the Mandan ceremonies. York, Clark's companion, is a great sensation in the villages. The Mandan have never seen a Black man before, and some of them try to rub the colour from his skin, to see if it is paint. York laughs, and dances, and performs feats of strength, and makes friends.

[long-pause]

On Christmas Day, 1804, the men celebrate in the fort.

[pause]

They fire the swivel gun, and run up the American flag, and the captains give them a glass of brandy, and they have a great Christmas dinner, of the best food they have. Then they clear the floor, and Cruzatte plays his fiddle, and they dance all day, and into the night.

[pause]

On New Year's Day, some of the men go to one of the Mandan villages, and dance there, to the music of a fiddle, a tambourine, and a horn, and the Mandan people laugh, and give them corn and buffalo robes.

[long-pause]

All winter, the captains work hard.

[pause]

They talk to the Mandan and the Hidatsa people, and to the French and British traders who live among them, and ask them about the country to the west: the rivers and the mountains, and the people who live there. Clark draws a great map, from what they tell him, showing the Missouri, and the Yellowstone, and the great falls of the Missouri, and the mountains beyond.

[pause]

{img:clark-drawing-large-map-fort-winter-lamplight}
Lewis writes a long report for President Jefferson, about everything they have seen and learned. And he packs up boxes of specimens, to send back: skins and skeletons, plants and seeds, minerals, and the live prairie dog, and the magpies.

[pause]

And among the people living near the villages, the captains meet a young woman, who will become one of the most famous members of the whole expedition.

[long-pause]

{img:young-shoshone-woman-fort-mandan-winter-robe}
Her name is Sacagawea.

[pause]

She is a young Shoshone woman, about sixteen years old, small and quiet, with dark eyes and long black hair. She is living in one of the Hidatsa villages, near the Mandan, with her husband, a French Canadian fur trader named Toussaint Charbonneau, who is much older than she is.

[long-pause]

She was not born here.

[pause]

She was born far away to the west, in the Rocky Mountains, among the Shoshone people, who live in the high valleys and the mountains, and hunt buffalo on the plains, and fish for salmon in the rivers. When she was about twelve years old, a raiding party of Hidatsa warriors attacked her people's camp, at a place called the Three Forks of the Missouri, and carried her away, with some other girls, hundreds of miles east, to the Hidatsa villages.

[pause]

There, she grew up, and learned the Hidatsa language, and was later sold, or won in a game, to Charbonneau, who took her as his wife.

[long-pause]

The captains hire Charbonneau as an interpreter, because he speaks French and Hidatsa.

[pause]

But they want Sacagawea to come too. Because she speaks Shoshone. And they know that, to cross the Rocky Mountains, they will need horses. And the people who live in the mountains, and have horses, are the Shoshone, Sacagawea's people.

[pause]

{img:charbonneau-sacagawea-captains-fort-mandan-meeting}
So, when the expedition sets out again in the spring, Sacagawea will come with them, as an interpreter, and as a guide, and as a link with her people.

[long-pause]

In February 1805, at Fort Mandan, Sacagawea gives birth to a baby boy.

[pause]

It is a long and difficult birth. A French trader tells Lewis that a little of the crushed rattle of a rattlesnake, mixed with water, will help. Lewis happens to have a rattlesnake rattle, and gives it to her. A few minutes later, the baby is born. Lewis writes that he does not know if it was the rattle that helped, but he is glad.

[pause]

The baby is named Jean Baptiste Charbonneau. But Clark, who becomes very fond of him, calls him Pomp, or Pompey, which, in the Shoshone language, means first born.

[long-pause]

So, when the expedition leaves Fort Mandan in the spring, there will be a young mother, and a newborn baby, among the soldiers and the hunters.

[pause]

Some people might think that a woman with a baby would slow the expedition down. But it is just the opposite. Sacagawea will prove to be strong, and calm, and brave, and very useful.

[pause]

She knows the plants of the plains and the mountains, and finds wild foods for the men to eat: roots, and berries, and wild artichokes, dug from the burrows of mice, and wild licorice, and currants. She carries her baby on her back, in a cradleboard, all the way to the Pacific and back.

[long-pause]

And the presence of a woman and a baby will tell the Native nations they meet that this is not a war party.

[pause]

As Clark will write later, a woman with a party of men is a token of peace. No war party, in that country, ever travelled with a woman and a baby.

[pause]

{img:canoes-river-woman-baby-men-paddling-calm-water}
Sacagawea will become the most famous woman in the history of the American West. There will be more statues of her, in the United States, than of almost any other woman. Her face will be on a golden dollar coin. Rivers and lakes and mountains will be named after her.

[pause]

But in the spring of 1805, she is simply a young mother, of sixteen or seventeen, with a baby on her back, setting out on a long journey toward the mountains where she was born.

[long-pause]

On the seventh of April, 1805, the ice on the river has broken up, and the Corps of Discovery is ready to go on.

[pause]

The big keelboat goes back down the river to Saint Louis, with a few men, carrying the reports and the maps and the specimens and the letters for President Jefferson, and the live prairie dog and the magpies.

[pause]

And the rest of the expedition, thirty-three people in all, including Sacagawea, and her baby, and her husband, and York, and Seaman the dog, set off up the river, in the two pirogues and six small canoes, carved from cottonwood trunks, toward the west, into country that no white man has ever seen.

[long-pause]

That evening, Lewis writes in his journal.

[pause]

He writes that their little fleet, although not quite so respectable as that of Columbus or Captain Cook, was still viewed by them with as much pleasure as those famous adventurers ever beheld theirs. He writes that they are about to penetrate a country, at least two thousand miles in width, on which the foot of civilized man had never trodden.

[pause]

{img:lewis-writing-journal-evening-campfire-hopeful}
And he writes that he believes this moment to be among the most happy of his life.

[long-pause]

{img:missouri-breaks-white-cliffs-river-spring-1805}
In the spring and early summer of 1805, the expedition travels up the Missouri, through some of the most beautiful country they have ever seen.

[pause]

They pass the mouth of the Yellowstone River, a great river coming in from the southwest, out of the mountains. They pass through badlands, of strange, eroded hills, coloured red and yellow and grey. And then, in what is now Montana, they pass through a place that Lewis describes as scenes of visionary enchantment: the White Cliffs of the Missouri.

[long-pause]

Here, the river flows between tall cliffs of white sandstone, carved by the wind and the rain into strange and wonderful shapes: towers and arches and pillars and statues and ruined castles, gleaming in the sun.

[pause]

Lewis writes that it is as if the cliffs had been built by the hand of some great architect, a thousand years ago. He walks along the cliffs for hours, gazing at them.

[pause]

They see more and more animals: great herds of buffalo and elk and antelope, and bighorn sheep on the cliffs, and beaver everywhere. And they meet grizzly bears for the first time: huge, powerful brown bears, which are very hard to frighten, and give the men some very anxious moments. Several times, men are chased by bears, and escape only by jumping into the river.

[long-pause]

At the beginning of June, they come to a fork in the river, where two great rivers meet, and they do not know which one is the Missouri.

[pause]

The men think it is the northern river, which is muddy, like the Missouri they know. The captains think it is the southern river, which is clear and fast. They spend a week exploring both. In the end, the men say that they will follow the captains wherever they go, even though they still think the captains are wrong.

[pause]

{img:two-rivers-fork-captains-standing-bluff-looking}
Lewis names the northern river the Marias, after his cousin, Maria Wood. And they go up the southern river, which is indeed the Missouri.

[long-pause]

A few days later, Lewis, walking ahead of the boats with a few men, hears a strange sound in the distance: a roaring, like thunder. And he sees, far away, a spray rising into the air, like a column of smoke.

[pause]

He walks toward it, for several miles, and comes to the edge of a cliff, and looks down. And there it is: the Great Falls of the Missouri.

[pause]

The whole river pours over a wall of rock, eighty feet high, in a great sheet of white water and foam and spray, with a roar that shakes the ground. Rainbows dance in the spray. Lewis sits down on a rock, and gazes at it, and writes that it is the grandest sight he has ever seen.

[long-pause]

But the falls are also a great problem.

[pause]

The boats cannot go up them. And when Lewis explores further, he finds that there is not just one waterfall, but five, one after another, along about ten miles of the river. The boats, and all the supplies, must be carried around them, overland, across the plains, eighteen miles.

[pause]

{img:men-hauling-canoe-on-wooden-wheels-across-prairie}
It takes them a month. The men build rough wagons, with wheels cut from the trunk of a cottonwood tree, and drag the canoes and the supplies across the rough, rocky prairie, which is full of prickly pear cactus, which pierces their moccasins and their feet. They are attacked by hailstorms, and thunderstorms, and grizzly bears, and mosquitoes. It is the hardest work of the whole journey.

[long-pause]

But at last, in the middle of July, the boats are back in the river, above the falls, and they go on.

[pause]

Soon, the mountains appear ahead of them, blue and distant, with snow on their peaks, even in summer. The river becomes narrower and faster and clearer, and winds between steep, rocky walls, through a great canyon, which Lewis calls the Gates of the Rocky Mountains.

[pause]

And at the end of July, they come to the Three Forks of the Missouri, where three rivers come together to form the great river. They name them the Jefferson, the Madison, and the Gallatin, after the President and two members of his government.

[long-pause]

And Sacagawea recognizes the place.

[pause]

This, she tells them, is where she was captured, as a little girl, five years ago, by the Hidatsa raiders. Her people's camp was here. She shows them the place where it happened.

[pause]

{img:three-forks-rivers-meeting-valley-mountains-quiet}
Lewis writes that she shows no sign of grief or joy, but is calm. They go on, up the Jefferson River, toward the mountains, and toward her people.

[long-pause]

{img:jefferson-river-canoes-poling-shallow-water-august}
In August 1805, the expedition is in trouble.

[pause]

They are going up the Jefferson River, toward the mountains. But the river is getting smaller and shallower and faster every day, with rapids and shoals, and the men must drag the canoes up it, wading in the cold water, slipping on the stones, pulling on ropes, hour after hour. The men are exhausted. Their feet are cut and bruised. And soon, they know, the river will be too small for the canoes at all.

[long-pause]

They need horses, to carry their baggage over the mountains. And they have not seen a single Shoshone.

[pause]

They have seen signs of them: old campfires, and tracks, and smoke in the distance. But the Shoshone are hiding. They have been attacked so often by raiding parties from the plains, the Blackfeet and the Hidatsa, who have guns, that they are very cautious, and keep away from strangers.

[pause]

One day, Sacagawea points to a great rock, standing out in the valley, shaped like the head of a swimming beaver. She tells them that she knows this place. It is near the summer camp of her people, on a river that flows to the west, over the mountains. They are close.

[long-pause]

So Lewis goes ahead, with three men, on foot, to find the Shoshone.

[pause]

He walks for days, up the valley, along a little stream, which grows smaller and smaller. And on the twelfth of August, 1805, he comes to the place where the stream begins, a little spring, coming out of the ground, at the foot of a hill.

[pause]

{img:tiny-spring-mountain-meadow-source-stream-lewis-kneeling}
One of the men, Hugh McNeal, stands with one foot on each side of the little stream, and thanks God that he has lived to bestride the mighty and heretofore deemed endless Missouri. They have reached the very source of the great river, that they have followed for three thousand miles.

[long-pause]

Then Lewis climbs to the top of the ridge, to the Continental Divide, the backbone of the continent.

[pause]

He hopes to see, on the other side, a great plain, with a river running down it to the Pacific. That is what everyone, even Jefferson, has believed: that there is only a short distance, perhaps half a day's walk, between the waters of the Missouri and the waters of the Columbia.

[pause]

But instead, he sees, stretching away to the west, as far as the eye can see, range after range of high mountains, their tops covered with snow.

[pause]

There is no easy way to the Pacific. The dream of an all-water route across the continent, that Jefferson has hoped for, is gone. Lewis goes down the other side of the ridge, and drinks from a little stream flowing west, the first water of the Columbia.

[long-pause]

The next day, Lewis meets the Shoshone.

[pause]

First, he comes upon three women, who are frightened, and sit down on the ground, with their heads bowed, expecting to be killed. Lewis gently raises them up, and paints their cheeks with red paint, which is a sign of peace among the Shoshone, and gives them presents of beads and mirrors.

[pause]

{img:shoshone-women-meeting-lewis-peace-paint-gifts}
Then sixty Shoshone warriors come galloping up on horseback, ready to fight. But the women show them the presents, and the chief comes forward, and embraces Lewis, and presses his cheek against Lewis's cheek, saying ah-hi-e, ah-hi-e, which means, I am much pleased, I am much rejoiced. All the warriors do the same, until Lewis is covered with grease and paint, and is heartily tired of the national hug, as he writes.

[long-pause]

The chief's name is Cameahwait.

[pause]

He is a tall, serious man, the leader of a small band of Shoshone, who are very poor. They have many horses, but few guns, and they are often hungry. They live in the mountains in summer, for fear of their enemies, eating berries and roots and fish, and go down to the plains only once a year, to hunt buffalo, in great danger.

[pause]

Lewis persuades Cameahwait and some of his people to come with him, back over the pass, to meet Clark and the boats, and the woman of their own people.

[long-pause]

On the seventeenth of August, they meet.

[pause]

Sacagawea is walking ahead with Clark, along the river, when she sees the Shoshone coming. She begins to dance, and to suck her fingers, which is a sign that these are her own people. A young woman runs out from among the Shoshone, and embraces her. They were girls together, and were both captured by the Hidatsa, five years ago. But this girl escaped, and found her way home.

[pause]

{img:sacagawea-embracing-childhood-friend-riverbank-joy}
Then the captains sit down in a tent, with Cameahwait, to talk, and they call Sacagawea in to interpret.

[long-pause]

She sits down, and begins to translate.

[pause]

And then she looks up at Cameahwait, and recognizes him. He is her brother.

[pause]

She jumps up, and runs to him, and throws her blanket over him, and weeps. Cameahwait is moved too. Then she sits down again, and tries to go on translating, but she keeps breaking into tears. Most of her family, he tells her, have passed away since she was taken. Only two brothers, and a little son of her older sister, are left.

[long-pause]

Of all the chances of the long journey, this is the strangest and the most wonderful.

[pause]

That, after five years, and thousands of miles, the young woman taken from her people as a child should come home with these strangers, and find that the chief of the very first band of Shoshone they meet is her own brother.

[pause]

{img:camp-fortunate-meadow-river-shoshone-expedition-together}
The captains call the place Camp Fortunate.

[long-pause]

{img:horse-trading-shoshone-camp-meadow-afternoon}
Now the captains must buy horses.

[pause]

The Shoshone have hundreds of fine horses, which they love, and are proud of. The captains trade for them, with things the Shoshone need: knives and axes, cloth, beads, and a few old guns, and an old uniform coat, and a pair of leggings. Clark buys one horse for a pistol, a hundred rounds of ammunition, and a knife. In the end, they get about twenty-nine horses, and a mule.

[long-pause]

Meanwhile, the men live among the Shoshone for a few weeks, and learn about their way of life.

[pause]

The Shoshone are wonderful horsemen. Lewis writes that he has never seen people ride so well. Even little children ride, tied onto the horse's back. The horses are handsome and fast, and many of them are spotted. The men watch the Shoshone hunt antelope on horseback, across the plain, in a long chase.

[pause]

The Shoshone fish for salmon in the rivers, with spears and traps made of willow, and dry the fish on racks in the sun. The women dig roots, and gather berries, and make clothes from the skins of deer and antelope and bighorn sheep, decorated with porcupine quills and beads.

[long-pause]

The Shoshone are very poor, and very generous.

[pause]

Even though they are hungry themselves, they share what little food they have with the strangers: dried berries, and a little salmon, and cakes made of roots. When one of the expedition's hunters kills a deer, the hungry Shoshone eat it all, every part. Lewis is moved, and sad, to see how hard their life is.

[pause]

{img:salmon-drying-racks-willow-river-camp-sunshine}
Cameahwait tells Lewis that if only his people had guns, they could live in the buffalo country, like the other nations, and eat as well as they do, instead of hiding in the mountains, and living on roots and berries. Lewis promises him that, one day, American traders will come, with guns, and his people will be able to defend themselves.

[long-pause]

On the eighteenth of August, while he is with the Shoshone, Lewis has his birthday. He is thirty-one years old.

[pause]

That evening, he writes a thoughtful page in his journal. He writes that he has lived thirty-one years, and that he fears he has done but little, very little, to further the happiness of the human race, or to advance the information of the succeeding generation. He resolves that, in future, he will live for mankind, as he has heretofore lived for himself.

[pause]

{img:lewis-alone-evening-tent-journal-mountain-dusk}
It is one of the few times, in all the journals, that Lewis writes about his own feelings. He is a man of great gifts, but also of dark moods, and he often feels that he has not done enough.

[long-pause]

Now the captains ask Cameahwait about the way to the west.

[pause]

Cameahwait draws a map on the ground, with a stick, and heaps of sand for the mountains. He tells them that the river to the west, the Salmon River, flows through a great canyon, between walls of rock, and is full of rapids, and no canoe can go down it. Clark goes ahead with a few men to see, and finds that Cameahwait is right.

[pause]

But there is another way. An old Shoshone man, who the men call Old Toby, knows a trail, to the north, over the mountains, which the Nez Perce people use, when they come over the mountains to hunt buffalo on the plains. It is a hard trail, he says. But it leads to the Nez Perce, and to the rivers that flow to the great water.

[long-pause]

So the captains hire Old Toby as a guide.

[pause]

They hide the canoes, by sinking them in a pond, and bury some of their supplies in a cache, a pit dug in the ground, to pick them up on the way home. And at the end of August, they say goodbye to Cameahwait and the Shoshone, and set off northward, with their horses, over the mountains.

[pause]

{img:pack-horses-line-climbing-mountain-trail-autumn}
Sacagawea goes with them. She could have stayed with her people. But she chooses to go on with her husband and her baby, and the strangers, to the end of the journey.

[long-pause]

{img:bitterroot-valley-wide-meadow-mountains-september}
In September 1805, the Corps of Discovery goes north, down the beautiful valley of the Bitterroot River, between high mountains.

[pause]

At a place they call Ross's Hole, they meet a large camp of Salish people, with hundreds of horses, who are very friendly, and give them more horses. The men find the Salish language very strange, full of soft, gurgling sounds, and some of them think, for a moment, that these people might be descended from the Welsh, because of the way they speak. They are not, of course.

[long-pause]

They come to a place by a creek where the Bitterroot valley meets a trail going west, over the mountains. They call the place Travelers' Rest. They rest there for two days, and hunt, and get ready.

[pause]

Then they turn west, onto the trail that will later be called the Lolo Trail. It is an old trail of the Nez Perce people, going up and down along the high, narrow ridges of the Bitterroot Mountains, through thick forests of pine and fir, for about a hundred and sixty miles.

[pause]

It is the hardest part of the whole journey.

[long-pause]

The trail is steep and narrow and rocky, and covered with fallen trees.

[pause]

The horses slip and stumble, and some of them fall down the steep hillsides, and roll over and over. One horse, carrying Clark's desk, rolls down a hill for a long way, and the desk is smashed. But the horse gets up, unhurt.

[pause]

{img:dense-fir-forest-fallen-logs-steep-hillside-trail}
The weather turns cold. On the sixteenth of September, it snows all day. The snow is so thick on the trees that every branch, as the men push past, drops a heap of snow on them, and they are soon wet through. Clark walks ahead, and writes that he has never been so wet and cold in his life, and fears that his feet will freeze in his thin moccasins.

[long-pause]

And there is nothing to eat.

[pause]

There is no game in these mountains: no deer, no elk, nothing but a few small birds. The men have a little flour, and some dried soup, called portable soup, which Lewis bought in Philadelphia, made of boiled-down meat and vegetables, like a hard jelly. They melt snow, and mix the soup with it. But it is not enough.

[pause]

They begin to eat their horses, one by one, and some candles, and a coyote, and a few grouse. They are hungry, and weak, and cold, and very tired. The mountains seem to go on forever, ridge after ridge, as far as they can see, all covered with snow and trees.

[long-pause]

Old Toby, the Shoshone guide, loses the trail once, in the snow, and they go down into a valley by mistake, and have to climb back up again. But he finds it again.

[pause]

They name one of their camps Hungry Creek, because they have nothing at all to eat there.

[pause]

At last, on the eighteenth of September, Clark goes ahead with six hunters, to find food, and the way down out of the mountains, leaving Lewis to bring on the rest of the party.

[long-pause]

On the twentieth of September, Clark comes down out of the mountains, onto a beautiful open plain, the Weippe Prairie, covered with grass, and full of the blue flowers of the camas plant.

[pause]

And there, he finds a village of the Nez Perce people.

[pause]

{img:weippe-prairie-blue-camas-flowers-open-meadow-sun}
The Nez Perce are surprised, and a little afraid, at first. They have never seen white men before. But they are kind, and they give Clark and his hunters food: dried salmon, and bread made from the roots of the camas, the blue flower, which they dig up, and bake, and pound, and make into flat cakes.

[long-pause]

There is a story, which the Nez Perce tell to this day.

[pause]

When the strangers came out of the mountains, some of the Nez Perce warriors thought of attacking them, to take their rifles and their goods. But an old woman, named Watkuweis, spoke up. Long ago, when she was young, she had been taken captive far to the east, and had been treated kindly by white people there, before she found her way home. Do them no hurt, she said.

[pause]

And the Nez Perce listened to her, and welcomed the strangers.

[long-pause]

Clark sends one of the hunters back with food, to meet Lewis and the others, who are still coming slowly through the mountains.

[pause]

On the twenty-second of September, Lewis and the main party come down out of the mountains too, onto the Weippe Prairie. They are thin, and weak, and exhausted, but they are alive. Every one of them has come through.

[pause]

{img:weary-men-horses-emerging-forest-onto-meadow-relief}
They have crossed the Bitterroot Mountains, in eleven days. It is the hardest thing they have ever done.

[long-pause]

{img:nez-perce-village-mat-lodges-river-autumn}
The Nez Perce are a great people, living in the valleys of the Clearwater River and the Snake River, in what is now Idaho.

[pause]

They call themselves the Nimiipuu, which means the real people. The name Nez Perce, which means pierced nose, was given to them by French traders, although very few of them ever pierced their noses.

[long-pause]

They live in long houses, made of poles, covered with mats of woven reeds, in villages along the rivers.

[pause]

In the spring and summer, they fish for salmon, which swim up the rivers in their millions, from the sea, and they dry the salmon, and store it for the winter. They dig camas roots on the prairies, and gather berries in the mountains, and every year, they cross the mountains to hunt buffalo on the plains.

[pause]

They are also famous for their horses. They breed them carefully, and have great herds of fine, fast, strong horses, many of them spotted, which will later be called Appaloosas, after the Palouse River, in their country.

[long-pause]

When the men of the expedition first eat the Nez Perce food, the dried salmon and the camas bread, after so many days of hunger, they eat too much, and are all ill for several days, with bad stomachs.

[pause]

But slowly, they recover, and grow stronger.

[pause]

{img:men-resting-recovering-shade-pine-trees-river-camp}
Lewis is so ill that he can hardly ride his horse. Clark gives the men medicine from his medicine chest, and they rest, by the Clearwater River, under the pines.

[long-pause]

The captains meet the Nez Perce chiefs.

[pause]

One of them is called Twisted Hair, an old man, who is friendly and helpful. He draws a map of the rivers for Clark, on a white elk skin, showing the Clearwater flowing into the Snake, and the Snake flowing into the great river, the Columbia, and the Columbia flowing to the sea, with great falls on it.

[pause]

Twisted Hair agrees to look after the expedition's horses for the winter, until they come back in the spring. The captains brand the horses, and cut their forelocks, so that they can recognize them, and leave them with the Nez Perce.

[long-pause]

Now they need canoes, to go down the rivers to the sea.

[pause]

So they go to a place on the Clearwater River, near where it meets another river, where there are tall ponderosa pine trees, and the men begin to make canoes, from the trunks of the pines.

[pause]

{img:men-burning-hollowing-pine-log-canoes-riverbank}
They are too weak to cut out the inside of the logs with axes, so they do it the Nez Perce way: they burn out the inside, with small fires, slowly, and then scrape out the charred wood. They make five canoes, in about ten days. They call the place Canoe Camp.

[long-pause]

On the seventh of October, 1805, they put the canoes into the Clearwater River, and set off downstream, toward the sea.

[pause]

Two Nez Perce chiefs, Twisted Hair and another named Tetoharsky, go with them for a while, as guides, and to tell the peoples further down the river that these strangers are friends.

[pause]

And Old Toby, the Shoshone guide, who led them over the mountains, leaves them now, without saying goodbye, and goes home, across the mountains. He does not even wait to be paid. The captains are sorry, because they owe him a great deal.

[long-pause]

For the first time in more than a year, the men are going downstream, with the current, instead of against it.

[pause]

After all the months of rowing and poling and pulling, up the long Missouri, and dragging the canoes up the shallow Jefferson, and climbing the mountains, now the river carries them along, swiftly, toward the west. The men are happy.

[pause]

{img:canoes-swift-current-river-canyon-golden-hills}
But the rivers ahead have many rapids, and the canoes are heavy and hard to steer. Several times, canoes hit rocks, and fill with water, and the men must swim, and dry their things on the bank. But they always go on.

[long-pause]

{img:snake-river-dry-golden-hills-canoes-october}
The canoes go down the Clearwater River, and into the Snake River, through a dry, brown country of bare hills, with no trees at all.

[pause]

The men have to buy firewood from the people living along the river, or use driftwood, or the dried stems of plants, to cook. They buy dried salmon, and roots, and dogs, from the villages, to eat. Many of the men grow to like dog meat, because it is better than nothing, although Clark never does.

[long-pause]

On the sixteenth of October, 1805, they come to the place where the Snake River flows into the Columbia.

[pause]

The Columbia is a great, wide river, rushing toward the sea. It is the river that Captain Robert Gray, an American sea captain, discovered from the ocean, in 1792, and named after his ship. And now, at last, the Corps of Discovery has reached it, from the land side, from the east.

[pause]

There are many people living along the river here, the Yakama and the Wanapum and other nations, in villages of mat houses, and they come to see the strangers, and dance, and sing. Hundreds of them gather around the camp.

[long-pause]

Everywhere along the river, they see salmon.

[pause]

It is autumn, and the salmon have finished their journey up the river, from the sea, to lay their eggs, and many of them lie on the banks and in the shallow water. The people along the river have caught millions of them, and dried them, and pounded them into a kind of meal, which they pack into baskets lined with salmon skin, and keep for the winter, and trade with other peoples.

[pause]

{img:fish-drying-scaffolds-columbia-villages-baskets}
The Columbia is a great river of trade, like the Missouri. The peoples of the river trade with the peoples of the plains, and the peoples of the coast, and the goods of the whole Northwest pass along it.

[long-pause]

At the end of October, they come to the Great Falls of the Columbia, which are called Celilo Falls.

[pause]

Here, the whole river pours over a series of rocky ledges, in a great roar of white water. This is the greatest fishing place in all of North America. For thousands of years, the peoples of the river have come here, every year, to fish for salmon, standing on wooden platforms over the rushing water, with long dip nets.

[pause]

The men carry the canoes and the baggage around the falls, and then go on.

[long-pause]

Below the falls, the river goes through a narrow channel, between walls of black rock, where the whole great river is squeezed into a gap about forty-five yards wide, and rushes through, boiling and swirling. The local people call it the Long Narrows, and later the French traders will call it the Dalles.

[pause]

The people watching from the rocks expect the strangers to drown. But the captains decide to take the canoes through, rather than carry them around. And the men, who are now skilled boatmen, take the canoes through the swirling water, one by one, and come out safely on the other side, to the amazement of the watchers.

[pause]

{img:canoes-narrow-channel-dark-basalt-walls-swirling}
Then they go on, down through the Columbia River Gorge, where the river cuts through the Cascade Mountains.

[long-pause]

The gorge is wonderfully beautiful.

[pause]

On both sides, high cliffs rise up, covered with dark green forests of fir and cedar, with waterfalls falling from the tops of the cliffs, hundreds of feet, in long white ribbons. The country changes completely. Instead of the dry, brown, treeless hills, now there are great forests, and moss, and ferns, and mist, and rain.

[pause]

{img:columbia-gorge-cliffs-waterfalls-green-forest-mist}
They see a great snowy mountain to the south, which they later learn is called Mount Hood, which Captain Vancouver's men had seen and named from the ocean, years before. And they know that they are getting close to the sea.

[long-pause]

They pass the last rapids, the Cascades of the Columbia, and come out into the lower river, which is wide and calm, and flows between low, forested hills.

[pause]

And then, they notice something: the river is rising and falling, twice a day. It is the tide. The sea is near.

[pause]

The people here, the Chinook and the Clatsop and others, have things that come from the sea: copper kettles, and blue cloth, and sailors' jackets and hats, and even a few words of English, which they have learned from the sailors of the trading ships that come to the mouth of the river to buy sea otter furs. One woman has the name of a sailor, J. Bowman, tattooed on her arm.

[long-pause]

On the seventh of November, 1805, the fog lifts from the river, in the morning.

[pause]

And Clark looks out, and writes in his notebook, in his wonderful spelling: Ocian in view! O! the joy.

[pause]

{img:fog-lifting-wide-estuary-canoes-clark-standing-joy}
In fact, what he sees is not yet the ocean itself, but the great wide estuary of the Columbia, where the river widens out into a bay, twenty miles long, before it meets the sea. But it does not matter. They have come four thousand miles, from Saint Louis, in a year and a half. And they are almost there.

[long-pause]

{img:stormy-estuary-rocky-shore-canoes-pulled-up-rain}
But the last few miles are the hardest of all.

[pause]

For more than two weeks, in November 1805, the expedition is trapped on the north shore of the estuary, by terrible storms. The rain pours down, day after day. The wind blows the waves so high that the canoes cannot go out. Huge logs, whole trees, are tossed by the waves against the rocks where the men are camped.

[long-pause]

The men are wet, all the time, day and night. Their clothes are rotting on their bodies. Their leather tents and blankets fall to pieces. They have little to eat, except dried fish, and a little meat.

[pause]

Clark writes that it is the most disagreeable time he has ever known. He calls the place Dismal Nitch.

[pause]

But at last, the storm eases, and they move to a better camp, on a sandy beach, near a point of land, where they can see the open ocean.

[long-pause]

On the eighteenth of November, Clark and some of the men walk out to the end of the point, Cape Disappointment, and look out over the Pacific Ocean.

[pause]

They see the great waves rolling in, white and grey, as far as the eye can see, and breaking on the rocks with a roar. Clark carves his name on a tree, with the date, and the words, By Land from the U. States in 1804 and 1805.

[pause]

{img:cape-disappointment-headland-ocean-waves-men-looking}
They have done it. They have crossed the continent, from the Atlantic side to the Pacific. They are the first Americans ever to do so by land.

[long-pause]

Now they must decide where to spend the winter.

[pause]

The captains do something unusual. They ask everyone in the party to vote. Every man, and also York, Clark's enslaved servant, and Sacagawea, a Native woman, are asked for their opinion. At a time when, in the United States, neither enslaved people nor women nor Native people can vote at all, this is a remarkable thing.

[pause]

{img:camp-vote-everyone-gathered-beach-grey-sky}
Most of the party vote to cross to the south side of the estuary, where the people say there are more elk to hunt. Sacagawea says she wants to go wherever there are plenty of wapato roots, a kind of wild potato. And so they cross the river.

[long-pause]

On the south side, near the present town of Astoria, in Oregon, in a thick forest of tall spruce and fir, on a little river, they build their winter fort.

[pause]

They call it Fort Clatsop, after the Clatsop people, who live nearby. It is a small fort of logs, fifty feet square, with two rows of little cabins, facing each other across a small parade ground, with a gate, and a wall of logs. They move in on Christmas Eve, 1805.

[pause]

On Christmas Day, the men fire their guns, and sing, and give each other small gifts. Lewis gives Clark a shirt, and drawers, and socks. Sacagawea gives Clark two dozen white weasel tails. But the Christmas dinner is poor: spoiled elk meat, and a few roots, and some bad fish. Clark writes that they would have spent the day with more merriment if they had anything either to raise their spirits or even gratify their appetites.

[long-pause]

The winter at Fort Clatsop is long, and wet, and dull.

[pause]

It rains almost every day. In four months, there are only twelve days without rain, and only six days when the sun shines. The men's clothes and bedding are always damp. Fleas live in everything. Many of the men catch colds, and suffer from rheumatism.

[pause]

{img:fort-interior-cabin-men-sewing-moccasins-firelight}
The hunters go out every day to hunt elk in the dripping forest, and the men cut up the elk, and smoke the meat, and make clothes and moccasins from the skins. During the winter, they make three hundred and thirty-eight pairs of moccasins, for the long journey home.

[long-pause]

{img:salt-makers-kettles-fire-beach-ocean-waves}
Not far from Fort Clatsop, a few of the men set up a camp on the beach, by the ocean, to make salt.

[pause]

They build a stone oven, and set five big kettles on it, and fill them with sea water, and boil it, day and night, until only the salt is left. They make about three or four quarts of salt a day. By the end of the winter, they have made about twenty gallons of salt, enough to season their food on the journey home.

[long-pause]

The salt makers' camp is in what is now the town of Seaside, in Oregon. You can still see the place today, with a rebuilt oven of stones, near the sea.

[pause]

One day, the people living nearby tell the captains that a great whale has washed up on the beach, a few miles to the south.

[pause]

Clark goes to see it, with some of the men, and with Sacagawea, who insists on coming. She has come so far, she says, to see the great waters, and now that there is a monstrous fish to be seen too, she thinks it very hard that she should not be allowed to see either. So Clark takes her along.

[long-pause]

When they reach the whale, the local people have already taken most of the meat and the blubber. Only the skeleton is left, a hundred and five feet long.

[pause]

Clark buys about three hundred pounds of blubber, and some oil, from the people, and brings it back to the fort, where the men cook it. Clark writes that it tastes rather like pork fat, or beaver tail, and that he is thankful to Providence for sending the whale to them, instead of swallowing them, as it did Jonah.

[pause]

{img:sacagawea-standing-beach-looking-at-pacific-ocean}
So Sacagawea, the young woman from the Rocky Mountains, sees the Pacific Ocean, and a whale, as she wished.

[long-pause]

The captains spend the winter writing.

[pause]

Lewis writes long descriptions of the plants and the animals of the coast: the sea otter, the condor, the great trees, the salal berry, the different kinds of salmon and trout. Many of these are new to science. He writes about the Clatsop and Chinook people: their houses, built of great cedar planks; their canoes, carved from huge cedar trunks, beautifully shaped, with high, carved bows; their hats, woven from grass and cedar bark, so tightly that they keep out the rain.

[pause]

{img:chinook-plank-house-cedar-canoes-beach-misty}
Clark works on his maps, and draws a great map of the whole of the West, from the Mississippi to the Pacific, from all the notes he has made on the journey, and all the information he has learned from the Native people. It is the first good map of the American West. It will be used for many years.

[long-pause]

The Clatsop people visit the fort often, to trade.

[pause]

Their chief, Coboway, is friendly, and brings gifts. The Clatsop bring fish, and roots, and berries, and elk, and dogs, to trade. But they are hard bargainers, as Lewis writes, because they have traded with the sailors for years, and know the value of things. The expedition has very little left to trade with. Their supply of beads and presents is almost gone.

[pause]

The men are eager to go home. They count the days.

[long-pause]

The captains hope that a trading ship will come into the mouth of the Columbia while they are there, so that they can send some of the men home by sea, with the journals, or get new supplies.

[pause]

But no ship comes that winter. And so, at the end of March, when the weather begins to improve a little, they decide to set off for home, overland, the way they came.

[pause]

{img:fort-clatsop-gate-morning-departure-canoes-loaded}
On the twenty-third of March, 1806, they give Fort Clatsop, and its furniture, to Coboway, the Clatsop chief, and load the canoes, and set off up the Columbia, toward the east, and home.

[long-pause]

{img:canoes-upstream-columbia-spring-green-hills}
The journey home is faster than the journey out, because now they know the way.

[pause]

They go up the Columbia, against the current, in the spring of 1806, past the Cascades, and the gorge, and the Long Narrows, and the falls. At the falls, they trade the canoes for horses, and go on overland, along the river.

[long-pause]

In May, they come back to the Nez Perce country.

[pause]

The Nez Perce welcome them back, as old friends, and give them back their horses, which they have kept safe all winter. Almost all the horses are there.

[pause]

But the snow is still deep on the Bitterroot Mountains, and the Nez Perce tell them that they cannot cross until the summer. So they must wait. They camp on the Clearwater River, near the Nez Perce villages, for almost a month, at a place they call Camp Chopunnish. It is a happy time.

[long-pause]

The men trade with the Nez Perce for food, with anything they have left: buttons cut from their coats, and old files, and kettles. Clark trades his medical skills, and treats the sick Nez Perce people, with eyewash and ointment, and they pay him with horses and food. He becomes famous as a doctor.

[pause]

The men run races, and play games, with the young Nez Perce men, and shoot at targets, and the Nez Perce win as often as the men do. Little Pomp, now more than a year old, is beginning to walk, and is the favourite of the whole camp.

[pause]

{img:foot-races-meadow-young-men-cheering-summer-camp}
In June, they cross the mountains, with Nez Perce guides, over the snow, which is now packed hard enough to walk on. This time, it takes only six days. And at the beginning of July, they are back at Travelers' Rest.

[long-pause]

There, the captains decide to divide the expedition, to explore more of the country.

[pause]

Lewis, with a small party, goes northeast, over the mountains by a shorter way, to the Great Falls of the Missouri, and then north, to explore the Marias River. Clark, with the rest of the party, including Sacagawea and Pomp, goes south and east, back to Camp Fortunate, to pick up the canoes, and then over to the Yellowstone River, and down it, to the Missouri. They plan to meet where the Yellowstone flows into the Missouri.

[pause]

It is a bold plan, in dangerous country. But both parties come through.

[long-pause]

On Clark's journey, Sacagawea is a great help.

[pause]

She knows the country of her childhood, and guides Clark to a pass through the mountains, which is now called Bozeman Pass, the shortest way to the Yellowstone. Clark writes that she has been of great service to him, as a pilot through this country.

[pause]

{img:sacagawea-pointing-mountain-pass-clark-riders}
On the Yellowstone River, Clark's party makes canoes, and goes down the river. And on the twenty-fifth of July, 1806, they come to a great rock, standing alone on the bank of the river, a tall pillar of yellow sandstone, rising out of the flat land.

[long-pause]

Clark climbs up to the top, and looks out over the beautiful valley of the Yellowstone, with great herds of buffalo and elk grazing.

[pause]

And on the side of the rock, he carves his name and the date: W. Clark, July 25, 1806. He names the rock Pompy's Tower, after little Pomp, Sacagawea's son.

[pause]

His signature is still there today, on the rock, now called Pompeys Pillar. It is the only physical mark of the expedition that can still be seen along the whole trail.

[long-pause]

Lewis's journey is harder.

[pause]

On the Marias River, Lewis and three men meet a party of young Blackfeet men, and camp with them for the night. In the morning, there is a quarrel, over guns and horses, and there is fighting, and two of the young Blackfeet are lost. It is the only time, in the whole journey, that the expedition fights with any Native people. Lewis and his men ride away, very fast, for more than a hundred miles, to the Missouri.

[pause]

{img:riders-crossing-wide-plains-dusk-missouri-ahead}
A few weeks later, Lewis is accidentally shot in the hip by one of his own men, Pierre Cruzatte, the one-eyed fiddler, who mistakes him for an elk, in the willows. It is painful, but not serious, and Lewis recovers on the way home, lying in a canoe.

[long-pause]

On the twelfth of August, 1806, the two parties meet again, on the Missouri, below the mouth of the Yellowstone. Everyone is safe.

[pause]

And now, they go home, down the Missouri, with the current, very fast, sometimes seventy or eighty miles a day.

[pause]

At the Mandan villages, they say goodbye to Sacagawea and Charbonneau and little Pomp, who stay there. Clark offers to take Pomp to Saint Louis, and raise him and educate him as his own son, when he is old enough. Sacagawea and Charbonneau agree to think about it.

[long-pause]

On the twenty-third of September, 1806, at noon, the Corps of Discovery comes down the Missouri, and into the Mississippi, and arrives at Saint Louis.

[pause]

The whole town comes down to the river to greet them, cheering. Everyone had thought they were lost. They have been away for two years, four months and ten days. They have travelled about eight thousand miles.

[pause]

{img:st-louis-riverfront-crowd-cheering-canoes-arriving}
The men fire their guns in salute, and step ashore. The great journey is over.

[long-pause]

{img:jefferson-reading-letters-monticello-study-candle}
When the news reaches Washington, President Jefferson is overjoyed.

[pause]

He writes to Lewis that he received the news of his return with unspeakable joy. The whole country celebrates. There are dinners and balls and toasts in honour of the explorers. Congress gives every man of the expedition double pay, and land: three hundred and twenty acres each, and the captains sixteen hundred acres each.

[long-pause]

The journey of Lewis and Clark has opened the West to the young United States.

[pause]

They have mapped the Missouri, and crossed the Rocky Mountains, and found the way to the Pacific. They have met with nearly fifty Native nations, most of them in peace. They have described hundreds of plants and animals, new to science. And they have brought back maps, and journals, and specimens, which will be studied for many years.

[pause]

But they have not found the easy water route across the continent, that Jefferson hoped for. There is none.

[long-pause]

Meriwether Lewis is made governor of the great Louisiana Territory, and goes to live in Saint Louis.

[pause]

But he is not happy there. He is better at exploring than at governing. He quarrels with other officials, and falls into debt, and his dark moods grow worse. He never finishes writing the book about the expedition that everyone is waiting for.

[pause]

{img:lonely-road-tennessee-forest-autumn-evening-inn}
In the autumn of 1809, on a journey to Washington, at a lonely inn on a forest road in Tennessee, called the Natchez Trace, Meriwether Lewis passes away, at the age of only thirty-five. Most historians believe that he took his own life, in a time of great sadness. He is buried near the inn, and today there is a stone monument there, a broken column, the symbol of a life cut short.

[long-pause]

William Clark lives a long and busy life.

[pause]

He marries Julia Hancock, whom he has known since she was a girl, and after whom he named a river, the Judith, in Montana. He becomes the chief official in charge of relations with the Native nations of the West, and later governor of the Missouri Territory. He lives in Saint Louis for the rest of his life, and every traveller to the West comes to see him, and to look at his maps, and at his museum of Native objects.

[pause]

Clark finishes the work on the journals, with the help of a young writer, and the history of the expedition is published at last in 1814. He dies in 1838, at the age of sixty-eight, in Saint Louis.

[long-pause]

Clark keeps his promise to Sacagawea.

[pause]

In 1809, Sacagawea and Charbonneau bring little Pomp, Jean Baptiste, now four years old, to Saint Louis, and leave him with Clark, who pays for his education at a school there.

[pause]

{img:young-boy-school-saint-louis-books-1810s-window}
Jean Baptiste grows up to have an extraordinary life. At eighteen, he meets a German prince, Paul of Württemberg, who is travelling in America, and goes with him to Europe, and lives for six years in Germany, and learns to speak German, French and Spanish. Then he comes back to America, and becomes a mountain man, and a guide, and a fur trader, in the West where he was born. He passes away in 1866, in Oregon, at the age of sixty-one.

[long-pause]

Sacagawea's own story after the expedition is less certain.

[pause]

Most historians believe that she passed away in December 1812, at a trading post on the upper Missouri, called Fort Manuel, at the age of about twenty-five, after the birth of a daughter, named Lizette. A clerk at the fort wrote in his journal that the wife of Charbonneau, a Snake squaw, had died of a putrid fever, and that she was a good and the best woman in the fort.

[pause]

But some people, especially among the Shoshone, believe that she lived much longer, and went home to her people, and lived to be a very old woman, at the Wind River Reservation, in Wyoming, where there is a grave with her name. We will never know for certain.

[long-pause]

York's story is a sad one.

[pause]

After the journey, York asks Clark for his freedom, as a reward for everything he has done on the expedition. He has been one of the most valuable men of the whole party: a hunter, a boatman, a nurse to the sick. He has voted with the others at the Pacific. But Clark refuses.

[pause]

{img:york-standing-riverside-looking-west-thoughtful}
For years, York remains enslaved, and is separated from his wife, who lives in Kentucky. At last, about ten years after the expedition, Clark gives him his freedom, and York starts a business, hauling goods with a wagon and horses, in Kentucky and Tennessee. He is believed to have passed away some time before 1832. Today, there are statues of York in Louisville, Kentucky, and in Portland, Oregon, honouring him as a member of the Corps of Discovery.

[long-pause]

{img:old-soldier-farmhouse-porch-telling-stories-children}
The other men of the Corps of Discovery go back to ordinary lives.

[pause]

Some of them return to the West, as fur trappers and traders. John Colter, one of the best hunters, leaves the expedition on the way home, at the Mandan villages, and goes back up the Missouri with two trappers. He becomes the first white man to see the hot springs and geysers of the country that will one day be Yellowstone National Park. When he describes them, nobody believes him, and they call the place Colter's Hell.

[long-pause]

George Drouillard, the great hunter, goes back to the upper Missouri as a trapper, and is lost there, in the Blackfeet country, in 1810.

[pause]

Patrick Gass, the tough little carpenter, publishes his journal in 1807, the first book about the expedition. He fights in the War of 1812, and marries at the age of sixty, and has seven children. He lives to be ninety-eight years old, and is the last surviving member of the Corps of Discovery, when he passes away in 1870, in West Virginia.

[pause]

{img:elderly-man-white-beard-reading-old-journal-fireside}
John Ordway, the careful sergeant, buys land in Missouri, and becomes a farmer. Several of the men settle near Saint Louis. Some of them we know almost nothing about, after the journey ends.

[long-pause]

And Seaman, Lewis's big black Newfoundland dog, who travelled all the way to the Pacific and back, is last mentioned in Lewis's journal in July 1806, being plagued by mosquitoes. Nobody knows for certain what became of him.

[pause]

There is a story that, years later, his collar was seen in a museum in Virginia, with words saying that he had followed his master to the Pacific, and stayed with him to the end. But the collar has never been found.

[pause]

There are statues of Seaman today, beside the statues of Lewis and Clark, in many places along the trail.

[long-pause]

For the Native nations of the West, the coming of Lewis and Clark is the beginning of great changes.

[pause]

After them come the fur traders, and the mountain men, and the missionaries, and the settlers, and the soldiers, and the railroads. Within a lifetime, the West is changed forever. The great herds of buffalo are almost gone. The peoples of the plains and the mountains lose most of their lands, and are moved onto reservations.

[pause]

{img:plains-at-dusk-tipis-distant-quiet-reflective}
The Nez Perce, who welcomed the starving explorers out of the mountains, remain friendly with the Americans for seventy years. But in 1877, when the government tries to force them off their homeland, some of them, led by Chief Joseph, try to escape to Canada, and are stopped only forty miles from the border. Chief Joseph says, in words that are still remembered: From where the sun now stands, I will fight no more forever.

[long-pause]

The Mandan villages, where the explorers spent their first winter, are struck by smallpox in 1837, and most of the Mandan people are lost.

[pause]

Sheheke, the Mandan chief, Big White, travels with Lewis and Clark to Washington in 1806, to meet President Jefferson, and sees the cities of the East, and is the guest of the President. He goes home in 1809, after a long and difficult journey, and passes away a few years later, in a quarrel among the villages.

[pause]

{img:earth-lodge-village-site-grassy-hollows-river-bluff}
Today, the Mandan, the Hidatsa and the Arikara live together as the Three Affiliated Tribes, on the Fort Berthold Reservation, in North Dakota, not far from where their villages once stood.

[long-pause]

The Shoshone, the Nez Perce, the Salish, the Clatsop, the Chinook, the Lakota, and all the other nations that the expedition met are still there too.

[pause]

They have their own memories of the strangers who came through their country, so long ago, and their own stories about them. Some of these stories are told at the places along the trail, where the Native nations themselves now tell the history of their peoples, in their own words.

[long-pause]

{img:missouri-river-today-bluffs-quiet-evening}
Today, more than two hundred years later, you can still follow the path of Lewis and Clark across the American West.

[pause]

It is called the Lewis and Clark National Historic Trail, and it runs for almost five thousand miles, from Pittsburgh, in Pennsylvania, where Lewis built his keelboat, down the Ohio River, and up the Missouri, and over the mountains, and down the Columbia, to the Pacific Ocean.

[long-pause]

Much of the country has changed completely.

[pause]

The Missouri River is now held back by great dams, which have made long lakes, where the river once flowed, and covered many of the places where the expedition camped. The great herds of buffalo and elk are gone from the plains, which are now farms and ranches, with fields of wheat and corn. There are cities along the rivers: Saint Louis, Kansas City, Omaha, Sioux City, Bismarck, Great Falls, Portland.

[pause]

But some places are still very much as Lewis and Clark saw them.

[long-pause]

The White Cliffs of the Missouri, in Montana, are still there, with their strange towers and arches of white stone, and the river flowing quietly between them. There are no roads, no towns, no houses along this part of the river. People come in canoes and kayaks, and camp on the banks where the expedition camped, and read Lewis's words about the scenes of visionary enchantment, and see the same scenes.

[pause]

{img:canoe-paddlers-white-cliffs-missouri-summer-calm}
The Gates of the Mountains, the great canyon where the river runs between high walls of rock, is still there, near the city of Helena, and boats take visitors through it.

[long-pause]

At Great Falls, in Montana, the falls are still there, although now there are dams on most of them, for electricity. But in the spring, when the river is high, the water still pours over them, with a great roar. There is a fine museum, the Lewis and Clark Interpretive Center, on a bluff above the river, where you can see how the men dragged the canoes across the prairie.

[pause]

At the Three Forks of the Missouri, there is a state park, where you can stand at the place where the three rivers meet, and where Sacagawea was taken, as a girl, and where she came back, as a young mother.

[long-pause]

High in the mountains, on the border of Montana and Idaho, at Lemhi Pass, there is still a little spring, coming out of the ground, which is the farthest source of the Missouri River. People go there, and stand with one foot on each side of the little stream, like Hugh McNeal. And they walk to the top of the ridge, and look west, and see what Lewis saw: range after range of mountains, all the way to the horizon.

[pause]

{img:lemhi-pass-dirt-road-ridge-mountains-summer}
The Lolo Trail, over the Bitterroot Mountains, is still a wild and lonely place, with thick forests, and steep ridges, and snow for much of the year. There is a narrow dirt road now, along part of the old trail, which you can drive in summer, slowly, for a hundred miles, without seeing a town.

[long-pause]

On the Weippe Prairie, in Idaho, where the starving explorers came out of the mountains, and met the Nez Perce, the camas still flowers in the spring, blue as a lake.

[pause]

The Nez Perce National Historical Park tells the history of the Nez Perce people, in their own words. And on the Clearwater River, there is a little park at Canoe Camp, where the men burned out their canoes, with a dugout canoe to show how it was done.

[pause]

The Columbia River is held back by dams too, and Celilo Falls, the great fishing place, where people had fished for more than ten thousand years, disappeared in 1957, under the waters of a lake, behind a dam. But the Columbia River Gorge is still there, with its cliffs, and its waterfalls, and its forests, and is one of the most beautiful places in America.

[long-pause]

Near the mouth of the Columbia, near Astoria, in Oregon, there is a copy of Fort Clatsop, built of logs, among the tall spruce trees, on the same little river, where the expedition spent that long, wet winter. In the summer, people in buckskin clothes show visitors how the men lived, and how they made candles and moccasins and smoked elk meat.

[pause]

And at Seaside, by the ocean, the stone oven of the salt makers has been rebuilt, on the place where they boiled sea water all that winter.

[long-pause]

At Cape Disappointment, on the north side of the river, there is a lighthouse now, on the cliffs where Clark and the men stood and first looked out over the Pacific. The great waves still roll in, white and grey, and break on the rocks.

[pause]

And on the Yellowstone River, in Montana, Pompeys Pillar still stands, the tall rock of yellow sandstone, rising out of the flat valley. There is a wooden staircase now, up the side of the rock, and a glass case over Clark's signature, to protect it. You can still read it: W. Clark, July 25, 1806.

[pause]

{img:pompeys-pillar-boardwalk-signature-glass-case}
It is the only mark left by the expedition, along the whole trail, that you can still see with your own eyes.

[long-pause]

The journals of Lewis and Clark are still read today, by historians and scientists, and by ordinary people, all over the world.

[pause]

They are kept in libraries, in Philadelphia, and in other places. There are more than a million words in them, written by the captains and the sergeants and the privates, by candlelight and firelight, in tents and in canoes, in the rain and the snow and the heat, over two and a half years. They describe the land and the rivers and the animals and the plants and the peoples of the West, as it was, before everything changed.

[pause]

In them, you can still read Clark's joyful words, as the fog lifted: Ocian in view! O! the joy.

[long-pause]

So the story of the Corps of Discovery comes to an end for tonight.

[pause]

A small company of soldiers and hunters, a Black man who was not free, a young Shoshone mother and her baby, and a big black dog, who went up a great river, and over the mountains, and down to the sea, and came home again, and were greeted as if they had come back from the dead.

[pause]
