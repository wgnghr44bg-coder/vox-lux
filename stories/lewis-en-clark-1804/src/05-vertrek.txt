On the afternoon of Monday, the fourteenth of May, 1804, in a gentle rain, the Corps of Discovery sets out.

Captain Clark is in command, because Captain Lewis is still in Saint Louis, finishing his business. The men push the keelboat and the two pirogues out into the Mississippi, and cross the wide brown river, and turn into the mouth of the Missouri. A few people from the nearby farms stand on the bank to watch them go, and wave.

--

Clark writes in his journal that evening: I set out at four o'clock, in the presence of many of the neighbouring inhabitants, and proceeded on under a gentle breeze, up the Missouri.

They go only a few miles, that first afternoon, and camp on an island. It is a short beginning to a very long journey.

A few days later, they reach the little French village of Saint Charles, on the north bank of the river, where the people welcome them with a ball, and the men dance with the French girls. And there, on the twentieth of May, Captain Lewis rides in from Saint Louis, and joins them. The next day, they set out again, up the river, all together.

--

Travelling up the Missouri is very hard work.

The river flows fast, against them, and its channel twists and turns, and is full of sandbars and snags: whole trees, which have fallen into the river, and lie hidden under the muddy water, with their branches pointing upstream, waiting to rip the bottom out of a boat. The banks are always falling in, as the river cuts away at them, with great crashes, sometimes just beside the boats.

{img:men-towing-keelboat-rope-riverbank-mud}
When the wind is behind them, they can raise the sail. But most of the time, they must row, or push the boats along with long poles, walking along the narrow gangways on each side of the keelboat, pushing the poles into the river bed, and walking back toward the stern. And when the current is too strong, the men must get out, and walk along the bank, or wade in the water, pulling the boats with long ropes, like horses on a towpath.

--

On a good day, they travel fifteen or twenty miles. On a bad day, only a few.

The men work very hard, from dawn until dusk, in the heat and the rain, the mud and the mosquitoes. Their hands are blistered from the oars and the ropes. Their feet are cut by the rocks and the sharp sticks in the mud. Many of them suffer from boils, and upset stomachs, and sunstroke.

But they are young and strong and cheerful, and they sing as they row, the old songs of the French boatmen, and the songs of Kentucky and Virginia.

--

The country along the lower Missouri is beautiful.

There are woods of cottonwood and sycamore and walnut and oak along the banks, and open meadows and prairies between them, covered with tall grass and wild flowers. There are steep bluffs of limestone, and caves, and clear creeks running into the river. There are wild grapes and raspberries and plums and mulberries, which the men pick and eat.

{img:limestone-bluffs-prairie-flowers-missouri-river-june}
There are animals everywhere: deer, and turkeys, and geese, and ducks, and beaver, and bears, and, further up the river, elk. The hunters go out every day, along the banks, and bring back meat for the men's dinner.

--

In June, they pass the last little settlements of white people along the river: a few farms, and a little village called La Charette, where an old frontiersman named Daniel Boone, the famous hunter and explorer of Kentucky, now lives, in his old age.

After that, there are no more settlements. They are in the wilderness.

They meet a few fur traders, coming down the river in canoes, loaded with furs and buffalo grease, who tell them about the country upstream, and the Native nations who live there. But otherwise, they are alone.

--

In the evenings, they camp on the bank of the river, or on an island, or on a sandbar, where there are fewer mosquitoes.

The men pull the boats up onto the bank, and set up the tents, and gather wood, and light fires, and cook their supper: venison or turkey or beef, with corn and flour, boiled or roasted over the fire. They clean their rifles, and mend their clothes and their moccasins, and the captains write their journals by the light of the fire.

{img:evening-camp-sandbar-tents-fires-boats-pulled-up}
Then Cruzatte takes out his fiddle, and plays, and some of the men dance, on the sand, in the firelight, and the others clap and sing. And then they lie down, wrapped in their blankets, under the stars, with the river rushing past in the darkness, and the sentries watching, and the frogs and the whip-poor-wills calling from the woods.
