On the fourth of July, 1804, the Corps of Discovery celebrates Independence Day, on the banks of the Missouri River, in what is now Kansas.

It is the first Independence Day ever celebrated west of the Mississippi. In the morning, they fire the swivel gun on the keelboat, as a salute. In the evening, they fire it again, and each man is given an extra ration of whisky.

--

They name a creek Fourth of July Creek, and another Independence Creek.

And Clark writes, that evening, about the beautiful prairie that they passed that day, one of the most beautiful he has ever seen, open and high, with a little creek winding through it, lined with trees, and the grass and flowers waving in the wind.

The men are happy, and well. They have been on the river for seven weeks, and are now about five hundred miles from Saint Louis.

--

As they go further up the river, the country begins to change.

The woods along the banks grow thinner, and the prairies grow wider. The hills are lower and smoother, covered with tall grass. The sky seems to grow bigger. And they begin to see new animals: elk, in great herds, and antelope, and, in the distance, the first buffalo.

{img:wide-open-prairie-big-sky-elk-herd-distance-river}
At the beginning of August, they meet the first of the Native nations, the Oto and the Missouria, at a place on the river bank that they call Council Bluff.

--

The meeting is friendly.

The captains put on their best uniforms, with their cocked hats and their swords, and set up a sail as an awning, for shade, and the men parade with their rifles. The chiefs come, with their people, and sit in a circle, and smoke the pipe, and listen while Lewis makes a long speech, telling them that they now have a new great father, the President in Washington, who wants to be their friend, and to trade with them, and wants them to live in peace with their neighbours.

Then they give the chiefs presents: medals with the face of Jefferson, and flags, and clothes, and beads and paint and tobacco and kettles. The chiefs make speeches too, and ask for more presents, and for guns, and for traders to come. It is the first of many such meetings, all the way up the river.

--

In August, there is a sad time.

One of the sergeants, Charles Floyd, a young man of twenty-two, from Kentucky, well liked by everyone, falls ill. The captains do everything they can for him. But he grows weaker. And on the twentieth of August, he passes away, quietly, near the present city of Sioux City, in Iowa.

{img:hilltop-cedar-post-grave-overlooking-river-sunset}
He is the only member of the Corps of Discovery to die during the whole journey. Today, doctors believe that he had appendicitis, which nobody, at that time, anywhere in the world, knew how to cure.

The men bury him on the top of a high bluff, overlooking the river, with military honours, and Lewis reads a prayer, and they put up a cedar post on his grave, with his name carved on it. They name the bluff Floyd's Bluff, and a little river nearby Floyd's River.

--

A few days later, they choose a new sergeant, by vote of the men. It is the first election held west of the Mississippi.

The men choose Patrick Gass, the tough little carpenter. And they go on up the river.

{img:men-voting-hands-raised-camp-riverbank-morning}
At the end of August, Lewis and Clark and some of the men walk out onto the prairie, to visit a strange, round hill, which the local people call Spirit Mound, and believe is the home of little spirits. From the top, they see a wonderful sight: the plains all around, for miles and miles, covered with great herds of buffalo, grazing.

--

From now on, they see buffalo almost every day.

Sometimes there are so many that they cover the whole plain, as far as the eye can see, a dark, moving mass of animals, grazing slowly, or moving together, with a sound like distant thunder. The men can hardly believe it. They estimate that they can see three thousand, five thousand, ten thousand at a time.

The buffalo meat is delicious, the men say, better than beef. The hunters shoot them for food, and the men eat the tongues, and the humps, and the marrow from the bones, which they think the greatest treat of all.

--

In September, they pass through the country of the Lakota, the great Sioux nation, who are the most powerful people on the Missouri.

There is a tense meeting, near the mouth of the Bad River, where some of the young Lakota warriors seize the rope of one of the boats, and for a few moments, it seems that there might be fighting. But the chief, Black Buffalo, steps in, and calms everyone down, and the danger passes. The captains and the Lakota smoke the pipe, and share a feast, and watch the Lakota dance, and then the boats go on up the river.

{img:river-boats-continuing-upstream-autumn-plains-calm}
As autumn comes, the leaves of the cottonwoods along the river turn yellow, and the nights grow cold. Great flocks of geese fly south over the boats, honking. And the captains know that they must find a place to spend the winter, before the river freezes.
