When the news reaches Washington, President Jefferson is overjoyed.

He writes to Lewis that he received the news of his return with unspeakable joy. The whole country celebrates. There are dinners and balls and toasts in honour of the explorers. Congress gives every man of the expedition double pay, and land: three hundred and twenty acres each, and the captains sixteen hundred acres each.

--

The journey of Lewis and Clark has opened the West to the young United States.

They have mapped the Missouri, and crossed the Rocky Mountains, and found the way to the Pacific. They have met with nearly fifty Native nations, most of them in peace. They have described hundreds of plants and animals, new to science. And they have brought back maps, and journals, and specimens, which will be studied for many years.

But they have not found the easy water route across the continent, that Jefferson hoped for. There is none.

--

Meriwether Lewis is made governor of the great Louisiana Territory, and goes to live in Saint Louis.

But he is not happy there. He is better at exploring than at governing. He quarrels with other officials, and falls into debt, and his dark moods grow worse. He never finishes writing the book about the expedition that everyone is waiting for.

{img:lonely-road-tennessee-forest-autumn-evening-inn}
In the autumn of 1809, on a journey to Washington, at a lonely inn on a forest road in Tennessee, called the Natchez Trace, Meriwether Lewis passes away, at the age of only thirty-five. Most historians believe that he took his own life, in a time of great sadness. He is buried near the inn, and today there is a stone monument there, a broken column, the symbol of a life cut short.

--

William Clark lives a long and busy life.

He marries Julia Hancock, whom he has known since she was a girl, and after whom he named a river, the Judith, in Montana. He becomes the chief official in charge of relations with the Native nations of the West, and later governor of the Missouri Territory. He lives in Saint Louis for the rest of his life, and every traveller to the West comes to see him, and to look at his maps, and at his museum of Native objects.

Clark finishes the work on the journals, with the help of a young writer, and the history of the expedition is published at last in 1814. He dies in 1838, at the age of sixty-eight, in Saint Louis.

--

Clark keeps his promise to Sacagawea.

In 1809, Sacagawea and Charbonneau bring little Pomp, Jean Baptiste, now four years old, to Saint Louis, and leave him with Clark, who pays for his education at a school there.

{img:young-boy-school-saint-louis-books-1810s-window}
Jean Baptiste grows up to have an extraordinary life. At eighteen, he meets a German prince, Paul of Württemberg, who is travelling in America, and goes with him to Europe, and lives for six years in Germany, and learns to speak German, French and Spanish. Then he comes back to America, and becomes a mountain man, and a guide, and a fur trader, in the West where he was born. He passes away in 1866, in Oregon, at the age of sixty-one.

--

Sacagawea's own story after the expedition is less certain.

Most historians believe that she passed away in December 1812, at a trading post on the upper Missouri, called Fort Manuel, at the age of about twenty-five, after the birth of a daughter, named Lizette. A clerk at the fort wrote in his journal that the wife of Charbonneau, a Snake squaw, had died of a putrid fever, and that she was a good and the best woman in the fort.

But some people, especially among the Shoshone, believe that she lived much longer, and went home to her people, and lived to be a very old woman, at the Wind River Reservation, in Wyoming, where there is a grave with her name. We will never know for certain.

--

York's story is a sad one.

After the journey, York asks Clark for his freedom, as a reward for everything he has done on the expedition. He has been one of the most valuable men of the whole party: a hunter, a boatman, a nurse to the sick. He has voted with the others at the Pacific. But Clark refuses.

{img:york-standing-riverside-looking-west-thoughtful}
For years, York remains enslaved, and is separated from his wife, who lives in Kentucky. At last, about ten years after the expedition, Clark gives him his freedom, and York starts a business, hauling goods with a wagon and horses, in Kentucky and Tennessee. He is believed to have passed away some time before 1832. Today, there are statues of York in Louisville, Kentucky, and in Portland, Oregon, honouring him as a member of the Corps of Discovery.
