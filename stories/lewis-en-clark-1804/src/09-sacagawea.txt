Her name is Sacagawea.

She is a young Shoshone woman, about sixteen years old, small and quiet, with dark eyes and long black hair. She is living in one of the Hidatsa villages, near the Mandan, with her husband, a French Canadian fur trader named Toussaint Charbonneau, who is much older than she is.

--

She was not born here.

She was born far away to the west, in the Rocky Mountains, among the Shoshone people, who live in the high valleys and the mountains, and hunt buffalo on the plains, and fish for salmon in the rivers. When she was about twelve years old, a raiding party of Hidatsa warriors attacked her people's camp, at a place called the Three Forks of the Missouri, and carried her away, with some other girls, hundreds of miles east, to the Hidatsa villages.

There, she grew up, and learned the Hidatsa language, and was later sold, or won in a game, to Charbonneau, who took her as his wife.

--

The captains hire Charbonneau as an interpreter, because he speaks French and Hidatsa.

But they want Sacagawea to come too. Because she speaks Shoshone. And they know that, to cross the Rocky Mountains, they will need horses. And the people who live in the mountains, and have horses, are the Shoshone, Sacagawea's people.

{img:charbonneau-sacagawea-captains-fort-mandan-meeting}
So, when the expedition sets out again in the spring, Sacagawea will come with them, as an interpreter, and as a guide, and as a link with her people.

--

In February 1805, at Fort Mandan, Sacagawea gives birth to a baby boy.

It is a long and difficult birth. A French trader tells Lewis that a little of the crushed rattle of a rattlesnake, mixed with water, will help. Lewis happens to have a rattlesnake rattle, and gives it to her. A few minutes later, the baby is born. Lewis writes that he does not know if it was the rattle that helped, but he is glad.

The baby is named Jean Baptiste Charbonneau. But Clark, who becomes very fond of him, calls him Pomp, or Pompey, which, in the Shoshone language, means first born.

--

So, when the expedition leaves Fort Mandan in the spring, there will be a young mother, and a newborn baby, among the soldiers and the hunters.

Some people might think that a woman with a baby would slow the expedition down. But it is just the opposite. Sacagawea will prove to be strong, and calm, and brave, and very useful.

She knows the plants of the plains and the mountains, and finds wild foods for the men to eat: roots, and berries, and wild artichokes, dug from the burrows of mice, and wild licorice, and currants. She carries her baby on her back, in a cradleboard, all the way to the Pacific and back.

--

And the presence of a woman and a baby will tell the Native nations they meet that this is not a war party.

As Clark will write later, a woman with a party of men is a token of peace. No war party, in that country, ever travelled with a woman and a baby.

{img:canoes-river-woman-baby-men-paddling-calm-water}
Sacagawea will become the most famous woman in the history of the American West. There will be more statues of her, in the United States, than of almost any other woman. Her face will be on a golden dollar coin. Rivers and lakes and mountains will be named after her.

But in the spring of 1805, she is simply a young mother, of sixteen or seventeen, with a baby on her back, setting out on a long journey toward the mountains where she was born.

--

On the seventh of April, 1805, the ice on the river has broken up, and the Corps of Discovery is ready to go on.

The big keelboat goes back down the river to Saint Louis, with a few men, carrying the reports and the maps and the specimens and the letters for President Jefferson, and the live prairie dog and the magpies.

And the rest of the expedition, thirty-three people in all, including Sacagawea, and her baby, and her husband, and York, and Seaman the dog, set off up the river, in the two pirogues and six small canoes, carved from cottonwood trunks, toward the west, into country that no white man has ever seen.

--

That evening, Lewis writes in his journal.

He writes that their little fleet, although not quite so respectable as that of Columbus or Captain Cook, was still viewed by them with as much pleasure as those famous adventurers ever beheld theirs. He writes that they are about to penetrate a country, at least two thousand miles in width, on which the foot of civilized man had never trodden.

{img:lewis-writing-journal-evening-campfire-hopeful}
And he writes that he believes this moment to be among the most happy of his life.
