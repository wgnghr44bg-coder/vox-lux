The winter of 1803 and 1804 is a long one, at Camp Dubois.

The camp stands in a clearing in the woods, on the east bank of the Mississippi, opposite the mouth of the Missouri. The men have built log huts to live in, with chimneys of sticks and mud, and fireplaces, and bunks. There is a storehouse, and a smithy, and a little hut for the captains.

--

There are about forty men in the camp, that winter.

Some of them are soldiers, volunteers from the army forts along the Ohio and the Mississippi. Some are young frontiersmen from Kentucky, chosen because they are good hunters and woodsmen: the Field brothers, Joseph and Reubin; George Shannon, who is only eighteen, the youngest of them all; John Colter, a tall, quiet hunter. There is George Drouillard, a skilled hunter and interpreter, half French and half Shawnee, who speaks several Native languages, and the sign language of the plains.

There are three sergeants: Charles Floyd, John Ordway, and Nathaniel Pryor. There is Patrick Gass, a carpenter, short and tough. There is Pierre Cruzatte, a French boatman, who has only one eye, and is short-sighted in the other, but who knows the Missouri River well, and plays the fiddle beautifully.

--

The men are young, mostly in their twenties, and full of energy.

All through the winter, the captains train them, and drill them, and teach them to work together. They go hunting in the woods for deer and turkey. They practise shooting, and hold shooting matches, against the local farmers, and usually win. They build and repair the boats, and pack and repack the supplies.

{img:men-shooting-match-target-clearing-winter-woods}
But they are also wild and restless, and sometimes they get into trouble. They fight, and drink whisky, and slip away to the nearby farms and taverns without permission. The captains have to be strict. Slowly, through the winter, the men learn to become a team.

--

Captain Clark spends most of the winter at the camp, training the men, and preparing the boats.

He studies the keelboat, and decides how to load it, so that it will be balanced in the water. He has lockers built along its sides, with lids that can be raised to make a kind of wall, to protect the men from arrows or bullets. He makes maps, from the reports of traders who have been up the Missouri.

Captain Lewis spends much of the winter in Saint Louis, across the river, talking to the fur traders and the merchants, and buying more supplies, and collecting information about the river, and the Native nations along it.

--

Every day, Clark writes down the weather in his journal: cold, snow, rain, ice in the river, a fine day.

In January, the river freezes, and great sheets of ice float down the Mississippi, grinding and crashing against each other. In February, there is snow. In March, the ice begins to break up, and the river rises, and floods. The birds come back: the geese and the ducks and the swans, flying north in great flocks, honking, over the camp.

{img:geese-flying-north-over-river-ice-breaking-march}
And in April, the trees begin to leaf, and the woods fill with flowers, and the men grow impatient to go.

--

They pack the boats.

They pack seven tons of supplies: barrels of flour and pork and corn and salt and lard and biscuits; bags of coffee and sugar; casks of whisky; kegs of gunpowder, sealed with wax in lead canisters, which can be melted down to make bullets when the powder is used; rifles and muskets and tools; tents and blankets; medicines; scientific instruments; and twenty-one bales of presents for the Native nations.

They plan to hunt for most of their food along the way. But they must carry enough to keep them alive if the hunting is poor.

--

On the evening of the thirteenth of May, 1804, everything is ready.

The men sit around their fires, on the last night at Camp Dubois, talking quietly. Cruzatte plays his fiddle. Some of the men dance. Some write letters to their families, to be left behind and posted. Some just sit and look at the fire, and at the dark river, and think about what lies ahead.

{img:last-night-camp-fiddle-campfire-men-dancing-spring}
Tomorrow, they will set out up the Missouri River, into country that no American has ever seen. They do not know how long they will be gone, or if they will ever come back.
