In August 1805, the expedition is in trouble.

They are going up the Jefferson River, toward the mountains. But the river is getting smaller and shallower and faster every day, with rapids and shoals, and the men must drag the canoes up it, wading in the cold water, slipping on the stones, pulling on ropes, hour after hour. The men are exhausted. Their feet are cut and bruised. And soon, they know, the river will be too small for the canoes at all.

--

They need horses, to carry their baggage over the mountains. And they have not seen a single Shoshone.

They have seen signs of them: old campfires, and tracks, and smoke in the distance. But the Shoshone are hiding. They have been attacked so often by raiding parties from the plains, the Blackfeet and the Hidatsa, who have guns, that they are very cautious, and keep away from strangers.

One day, Sacagawea points to a great rock, standing out in the valley, shaped like the head of a swimming beaver. She tells them that she knows this place. It is near the summer camp of her people, on a river that flows to the west, over the mountains. They are close.

--

So Lewis goes ahead, with three men, on foot, to find the Shoshone.

He walks for days, up the valley, along a little stream, which grows smaller and smaller. And on the twelfth of August, 1805, he comes to the place where the stream begins, a little spring, coming out of the ground, at the foot of a hill.

{img:tiny-spring-mountain-meadow-source-stream-lewis-kneeling}
One of the men, Hugh McNeal, stands with one foot on each side of the little stream, and thanks God that he has lived to bestride the mighty and heretofore deemed endless Missouri. They have reached the very source of the great river, that they have followed for three thousand miles.

--

Then Lewis climbs to the top of the ridge, to the Continental Divide, the backbone of the continent.

He hopes to see, on the other side, a great plain, with a river running down it to the Pacific. That is what everyone, even Jefferson, has believed: that there is only a short distance, perhaps half a day's walk, between the waters of the Missouri and the waters of the Columbia.

But instead, he sees, stretching away to the west, as far as the eye can see, range after range of high mountains, their tops covered with snow.

There is no easy way to the Pacific. The dream of an all-water route across the continent, that Jefferson has hoped for, is gone. Lewis goes down the other side of the ridge, and drinks from a little stream flowing west, the first water of the Columbia.

--

The next day, Lewis meets the Shoshone.

First, he comes upon three women, who are frightened, and sit down on the ground, with their heads bowed, expecting to be killed. Lewis gently raises them up, and paints their cheeks with red paint, which is a sign of peace among the Shoshone, and gives them presents of beads and mirrors.

{img:shoshone-women-meeting-lewis-peace-paint-gifts}
Then sixty Shoshone warriors come galloping up on horseback, ready to fight. But the women show them the presents, and the chief comes forward, and embraces Lewis, and presses his cheek against Lewis's cheek, saying ah-hi-e, ah-hi-e, which means, I am much pleased, I am much rejoiced. All the warriors do the same, until Lewis is covered with grease and paint, and is heartily tired of the national hug, as he writes.

--

The chief's name is Cameahwait.

He is a tall, serious man, the leader of a small band of Shoshone, who are very poor. They have many horses, but few guns, and they are often hungry. They live in the mountains in summer, for fear of their enemies, eating berries and roots and fish, and go down to the plains only once a year, to hunt buffalo, in great danger.

Lewis persuades Cameahwait and some of his people to come with him, back over the pass, to meet Clark and the boats, and the woman of their own people.

--

On the seventeenth of August, they meet.

Sacagawea is walking ahead with Clark, along the river, when she sees the Shoshone coming. She begins to dance, and to suck her fingers, which is a sign that these are her own people. A young woman runs out from among the Shoshone, and embraces her. They were girls together, and were both captured by the Hidatsa, five years ago. But this girl escaped, and found her way home.

{img:sacagawea-embracing-childhood-friend-riverbank-joy}
Then the captains sit down in a tent, with Cameahwait, to talk, and they call Sacagawea in to interpret.

--

She sits down, and begins to translate.

And then she looks up at Cameahwait, and recognizes him. He is her brother.

She jumps up, and runs to him, and throws her blanket over him, and weeps. Cameahwait is moved too. Then she sits down again, and tries to go on translating, but she keeps breaking into tears. Most of her family, he tells her, have passed away since she was taken. Only two brothers, and a little son of her older sister, are left.

--

Of all the chances of the long journey, this is the strangest and the most wonderful.

That, after five years, and thousands of miles, the young woman taken from her people as a child should come home with these strangers, and find that the chief of the very first band of Shoshone they meet is her own brother.
