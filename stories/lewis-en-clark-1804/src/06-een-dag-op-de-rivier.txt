Let us spend an ordinary day with the Corps of Discovery, on the Missouri River, in the summer of 1804.

The day begins at dawn, or even before. A sergeant goes from blanket to blanket, waking the men. They roll up their blankets, and pack their things, and eat a quick breakfast of cold meat from the night before, or hominy, boiled corn, and a little coffee, if there is any.

--

Then they load the boats, and push off from the bank, as the sun rises over the river, and the mist lifts from the water.

The captains take turns. One day, Clark stays in the keelboat, steering it, and directing the men, and making his maps of every bend in the river, with a compass, measuring the distances and the directions. And Lewis walks along the bank, with Seaman, his dog, collecting plants, and watching the birds and the animals, and writing his notes.

The next day, they change places.

--

The hunters go out early, on foot, or sometimes on horseback, along the riverbank, ahead of the boats.

Drouillard is the best hunter of all. He can track any animal, and almost never misses. He and the other hunters bring back deer, and elk, and turkeys, and later, buffalo, and leave the meat hanging in a tree by the river, where the boats can pick it up as they pass.

{img:hunter-deer-carcass-hanging-tree-riverbank-boat-approaching}
The men need a great deal of meat. Each man eats about nine pounds of meat a day, when they can get it, because the work is so hard. That is about one deer, or a quarter of a buffalo, for every four men, every day.

--

At midday, they stop for a little while, and rest in the shade, and eat.

Then they go on, through the long, hot afternoon. The sun beats down on the river, and the men row and pole and pull, and sweat, and swat at the mosquitoes and the gnats and the ticks, which are terrible in the summer. They tie handkerchiefs over their faces, and smear grease on their skin.

Clark writes in his journal that the mosquitoes are so thick that he cannot keep them out of his eyes, nose, ears and throat.

--

Along the way, the captains name things.

Every creek and river and island and hill that they pass, that does not already have a name that they know, they give a name to. They name rivers after the men of the expedition, and after their friends and their families, and after the things that happen there. There is a Floyd's River, and a Shannon's Creek, and a Fourth of July Creek, and a Pryor's Creek, and an Ordway's Creek.

{img:clark-writing-names-map-boat-stern-river-bend}
Some of the names are still used today. Most of them have been forgotten.

--

The captains also write down everything new that they see.

They describe more than a hundred and seventy plants, and more than a hundred animals, that no scientist has ever described before. They see a coyote, which they call a prairie wolf, and a jack rabbit, and a badger, and a pronghorn antelope, and a mule deer, with its great ears. They see magpies, and burrowing owls, and prairie dogs.

{img:prairie-dog-town-burrows-mounds-grassland-sun}
They send specimens back to President Jefferson: skins and bones and horns, plants pressed between sheets of paper, seeds, minerals, and even, in the spring of 1805, a live prairie dog and four live magpies, in cages, which travel all the way down the river and across the country to Washington. Only the prairie dog and one magpie survive the journey. Jefferson is delighted with them.

--

In the late afternoon, the captains look for a good place to camp for the night.

The men pull the boats into the bank, and unload what they need for the night. They put up the tents, for the captains and the sergeants. The other men sleep in the open, or under little shelters of branches, or under the sail, stretched over poles.

They cook supper, a great meal of meat, roasted or boiled, with corn or flour. They mend their clothes, and their moccasins, which wear out in a few days, and must be replaced, cut and sewn from deer skin. They clean their rifles, and check their powder.

--

And as night falls, the captains sit by the fire, and write their journals.

Lewis writes about the plants and animals he has seen that day, carefully, in his long, flowing hand. Clark writes about the river, and the distance they have travelled, and the weather, and the events of the day, in his rough, cheerful spelling. Sergeant Ordway and Sergeant Gass and Private Whitehouse write their journals too, in their own way.

{img:captains-writing-journals-campfire-night-candle-stars}
Then the captains go to their tent, and the men lie down to sleep, and the fire burns low, and the river rushes past, and the stars wheel slowly over the wide, dark land.
