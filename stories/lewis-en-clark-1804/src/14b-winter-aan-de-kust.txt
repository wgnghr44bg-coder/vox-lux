Not far from Fort Clatsop, a few of the men set up a camp on the beach, by the ocean, to make salt.

They build a stone oven, and set five big kettles on it, and fill them with sea water, and boil it, day and night, until only the salt is left. They make about three or four quarts of salt a day. By the end of the winter, they have made about twenty gallons of salt, enough to season their food on the journey home.

--

The salt makers' camp is in what is now the town of Seaside, in Oregon. You can still see the place today, with a rebuilt oven of stones, near the sea.

One day, the people living nearby tell the captains that a great whale has washed up on the beach, a few miles to the south.

Clark goes to see it, with some of the men, and with Sacagawea, who insists on coming. She has come so far, she says, to see the great waters, and now that there is a monstrous fish to be seen too, she thinks it very hard that she should not be allowed to see either. So Clark takes her along.

--

When they reach the whale, the local people have already taken most of the meat and the blubber. Only the skeleton is left, a hundred and five feet long.

Clark buys about three hundred pounds of blubber, and some oil, from the people, and brings it back to the fort, where the men cook it. Clark writes that it tastes rather like pork fat, or beaver tail, and that he is thankful to Providence for sending the whale to them, instead of swallowing them, as it did Jonah.

{img:sacagawea-standing-beach-looking-at-pacific-ocean}
So Sacagawea, the young woman from the Rocky Mountains, sees the Pacific Ocean, and a whale, as she wished.

--

The captains spend the winter writing.

Lewis writes long descriptions of the plants and the animals of the coast: the sea otter, the condor, the great trees, the salal berry, the different kinds of salmon and trout. Many of these are new to science. He writes about the Clatsop and Chinook people: their houses, built of great cedar planks; their canoes, carved from huge cedar trunks, beautifully shaped, with high, carved bows; their hats, woven from grass and cedar bark, so tightly that they keep out the rain.

{img:chinook-plank-house-cedar-canoes-beach-misty}
Clark works on his maps, and draws a great map of the whole of the West, from the Mississippi to the Pacific, from all the notes he has made on the journey, and all the information he has learned from the Native people. It is the first good map of the American West. It will be used for many years.

--

The Clatsop people visit the fort often, to trade.

Their chief, Coboway, is friendly, and brings gifts. The Clatsop bring fish, and roots, and berries, and elk, and dogs, to trade. But they are hard bargainers, as Lewis writes, because they have traded with the sailors for years, and know the value of things. The expedition has very little left to trade with. Their supply of beads and presents is almost gone.

The men are eager to go home. They count the days.

--

The captains hope that a trading ship will come into the mouth of the Columbia while they are there, so that they can send some of the men home by sea, with the journals, or get new supplies.

But no ship comes that winter. And so, at the end of March, when the weather begins to improve a little, they decide to set off for home, overland, the way they came.

{img:fort-clatsop-gate-morning-departure-canoes-loaded}
On the twenty-third of March, 1806, they give Fort Clatsop, and its furniture, to Coboway, the Clatsop chief, and load the canoes, and set off up the Columbia, toward the east, and home.
