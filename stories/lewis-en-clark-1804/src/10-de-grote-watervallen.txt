In the spring and early summer of 1805, the expedition travels up the Missouri, through some of the most beautiful country they have ever seen.

They pass the mouth of the Yellowstone River, a great river coming in from the southwest, out of the mountains. They pass through badlands, of strange, eroded hills, coloured red and yellow and grey. And then, in what is now Montana, they pass through a place that Lewis describes as scenes of visionary enchantment: the White Cliffs of the Missouri.

--

Here, the river flows between tall cliffs of white sandstone, carved by the wind and the rain into strange and wonderful shapes: towers and arches and pillars and statues and ruined castles, gleaming in the sun.

Lewis writes that it is as if the cliffs had been built by the hand of some great architect, a thousand years ago. He walks along the cliffs for hours, gazing at them.

They see more and more animals: great herds of buffalo and elk and antelope, and bighorn sheep on the cliffs, and beaver everywhere. And they meet grizzly bears for the first time: huge, powerful brown bears, which are very hard to frighten, and give the men some very anxious moments. Several times, men are chased by bears, and escape only by jumping into the river.

--

At the beginning of June, they come to a fork in the river, where two great rivers meet, and they do not know which one is the Missouri.

The men think it is the northern river, which is muddy, like the Missouri they know. The captains think it is the southern river, which is clear and fast. They spend a week exploring both. In the end, the men say that they will follow the captains wherever they go, even though they still think the captains are wrong.

{img:two-rivers-fork-captains-standing-bluff-looking}
Lewis names the northern river the Marias, after his cousin, Maria Wood. And they go up the southern river, which is indeed the Missouri.

--

A few days later, Lewis, walking ahead of the boats with a few men, hears a strange sound in the distance: a roaring, like thunder. And he sees, far away, a spray rising into the air, like a column of smoke.

He walks toward it, for several miles, and comes to the edge of a cliff, and looks down. And there it is: the Great Falls of the Missouri.

The whole river pours over a wall of rock, eighty feet high, in a great sheet of white water and foam and spray, with a roar that shakes the ground. Rainbows dance in the spray. Lewis sits down on a rock, and gazes at it, and writes that it is the grandest sight he has ever seen.

--

But the falls are also a great problem.

The boats cannot go up them. And when Lewis explores further, he finds that there is not just one waterfall, but five, one after another, along about ten miles of the river. The boats, and all the supplies, must be carried around them, overland, across the plains, eighteen miles.

{img:men-hauling-canoe-on-wooden-wheels-across-prairie}
It takes them a month. The men build rough wagons, with wheels cut from the trunk of a cottonwood tree, and drag the canoes and the supplies across the rough, rocky prairie, which is full of prickly pear cactus, which pierces their moccasins and their feet. They are attacked by hailstorms, and thunderstorms, and grizzly bears, and mosquitoes. It is the hardest work of the whole journey.

--

But at last, in the middle of July, the boats are back in the river, above the falls, and they go on.

Soon, the mountains appear ahead of them, blue and distant, with snow on their peaks, even in summer. The river becomes narrower and faster and clearer, and winds between steep, rocky walls, through a great canyon, which Lewis calls the Gates of the Rocky Mountains.

And at the end of July, they come to the Three Forks of the Missouri, where three rivers come together to form the great river. They name them the Jefferson, the Madison, and the Gallatin, after the President and two members of his government.

--

And Sacagawea recognizes the place.

This, she tells them, is where she was captured, as a little girl, five years ago, by the Hidatsa raiders. Her people's camp was here. She shows them the place where it happened.

{img:three-forks-rivers-meeting-valley-mountains-quiet}
Lewis writes that she shows no sign of grief or joy, but is calm. They go on, up the Jefferson River, toward the mountains, and toward her people.
