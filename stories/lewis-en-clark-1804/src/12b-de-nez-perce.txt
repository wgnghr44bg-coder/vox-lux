The Nez Perce are a great people, living in the valleys of the Clearwater River and the Snake River, in what is now Idaho.

They call themselves the Nimiipuu, which means the real people. The name Nez Perce, which means pierced nose, was given to them by French traders, although very few of them ever pierced their noses.

--

They live in long houses, made of poles, covered with mats of woven reeds, in villages along the rivers.

In the spring and summer, they fish for salmon, which swim up the rivers in their millions, from the sea, and they dry the salmon, and store it for the winter. They dig camas roots on the prairies, and gather berries in the mountains, and every year, they cross the mountains to hunt buffalo on the plains.

They are also famous for their horses. They breed them carefully, and have great herds of fine, fast, strong horses, many of them spotted, which will later be called Appaloosas, after the Palouse River, in their country.

--

When the men of the expedition first eat the Nez Perce food, the dried salmon and the camas bread, after so many days of hunger, they eat too much, and are all ill for several days, with bad stomachs.

But slowly, they recover, and grow stronger.

{img:men-resting-recovering-shade-pine-trees-river-camp}
Lewis is so ill that he can hardly ride his horse. Clark gives the men medicine from his medicine chest, and they rest, by the Clearwater River, under the pines.

--

The captains meet the Nez Perce chiefs.

One of them is called Twisted Hair, an old man, who is friendly and helpful. He draws a map of the rivers for Clark, on a white elk skin, showing the Clearwater flowing into the Snake, and the Snake flowing into the great river, the Columbia, and the Columbia flowing to the sea, with great falls on it.

Twisted Hair agrees to look after the expedition's horses for the winter, until they come back in the spring. The captains brand the horses, and cut their forelocks, so that they can recognize them, and leave them with the Nez Perce.

--

Now they need canoes, to go down the rivers to the sea.

So they go to a place on the Clearwater River, near where it meets another river, where there are tall ponderosa pine trees, and the men begin to make canoes, from the trunks of the pines.

{img:men-burning-hollowing-pine-log-canoes-riverbank}
They are too weak to cut out the inside of the logs with axes, so they do it the Nez Perce way: they burn out the inside, with small fires, slowly, and then scrape out the charred wood. They make five canoes, in about ten days. They call the place Canoe Camp.

--

On the seventh of October, 1805, they put the canoes into the Clearwater River, and set off downstream, toward the sea.

Two Nez Perce chiefs, Twisted Hair and another named Tetoharsky, go with them for a while, as guides, and to tell the peoples further down the river that these strangers are friends.

And Old Toby, the Shoshone guide, who led them over the mountains, leaves them now, without saying goodbye, and goes home, across the mountains. He does not even wait to be paid. The captains are sorry, because they owe him a great deal.

--

For the first time in more than a year, the men are going downstream, with the current, instead of against it.

After all the months of rowing and poling and pulling, up the long Missouri, and dragging the canoes up the shallow Jefferson, and climbing the mountains, now the river carries them along, swiftly, toward the west. The men are happy.

{img:canoes-swift-current-river-canyon-golden-hills}
But the rivers ahead have many rapids, and the canoes are heavy and hard to steer. Several times, canoes hit rocks, and fill with water, and the men must swim, and dry their things on the bank. But they always go on.
