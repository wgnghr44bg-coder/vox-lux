In July 1518, a woman began to dance in a street in Strasbourg, and she could not stop. In the middle of July 1518, a woman named Frau Troffea stepped into a narrow street in Strasbourg and began to dance. There was no music and no celebration. She danced on and on, through the afternoon and into the evening, until she collapsed with exhaustion. After a short rest, she got up and began again. Historians know about her from chronicles written in the city and from the writings of the physician Paracelsus, who later studied the event. Neighbours gathered to watch. Some were amused, some were worried, and some thought she must be possessed. Even so, she continued to dance for several days. Her feet became bruised and bloody, but she could not stop. This was the beginning of one of the strangest events in European history. Within weeks, dozens of other people in Strasbourg had joined her. The event became known as the dancing plague of 1518.

Others feared that the strange illness might spread to them. The city's leaders were alarmed. They did not know what was happening or how to stop it. As July turned into August, the number of dancers kept growing. Strasbourg, one of the richest cities of the Holy Roman Empire, was facing a mystery no one could explain.

The city council of Strasbourg met many times to decide what to do about the dancers. Strasbourg was governed by a city council made up of nobles and representatives of the guilds, the organisations of craftsmen and merchants. Historians note that the council was responsible for keeping order, protecting public health and dealing with emergencies. When the dancing spread, the council met to discuss the crisis. Some members believed that the dancing was a natural illness. Others believed that it was a punishment from God or the work of a saint. The council made several decisions over the following weeks, some of which seem strange today. They tried different remedies, changing their approach as the crisis continued. Their records give a vivid picture of a city trying to deal with an unexplained emergency.

The crisis finally came to an end in September, after the dancers were taken on a pilgrimage to a holy shrine in the hills. The story of how the city coped is as remarkable as the dancing itself.

Families who had suffered during the dancing tried to recover. The dancers themselves returned to their homes and work, though some may have been weakened for a long time. The extraordinary summer of 1518 was over.

{img:66-medieval-churchyard-dancers-christmas-snow}

Stories of dancing illnesses had been told in Europe for centuries before 1518. The dancing plague of 1518 was not the first of its kind. Historians note that stories of uncontrollable dancing had been told in Europe since the Middle Ages. One of the oldest is a legend from the village of Kölbigk, in Saxony, around the year 1021. According to the story, a group of young people danced in a churchyard on Christmas Eve, disturbing the priest's mass. The priest cursed them, and they were forced to dance for a whole year without stopping. Even so, this story is a legend, told as a warning against sinful behaviour. It may not describe a real event. Yet it shows that the idea of a dancing curse was old and widespread. People in the Middle Ages believed that dancing at the wrong time or place could anger God or the saints. These beliefs were still alive in 1518. They helped shape how the people of Strasbourg understood the dancing that broke out in their city.

[long-pause]

{img:67-children-dancing-road-erfurt-1237}

In 1237, a large group of children was said to have danced from the town of Erfurt to the town of Arnstadt. According to a chronicle, in 1237 a large group of children left the town of Erfurt, in Germany, and danced and jumped along the road to the town of Arnstadt, about twenty kilometres away. Historians note that the children were exhausted when they arrived, and their parents had to fetch them home. Some of them, according to the chronicle, suffered from shaking for the rest of their lives. Even so, historians are not sure what really happened. The story may have been exaggerated over time. It is sometimes compared with the legend of the Pied Piper of Hamelin, in which the children of a town follow a mysterious piper and disappear. That legend is linked to an event in 1284. Historians note that many medieval stories describe groups of people, especially children, being led away by music or dancing. These stories reflect the fears and beliefs of the time about the power of music, dancing and strange forces.

[long-pause]

{img:68-dancers-rhineland-1374-procession-town}

In 1374, a great wave of dancing spread through towns along the Rhine and in the Low Countries. The largest outbreak of dancing mania before 1518 happened in 1374. Historians note that it began in the city of Aachen, in Germany, in the summer of that year. Groups of dancers travelled from town to town, dancing in streets, churches and squares. The dancing spread along the Rhine to Cologne, Metz and Trier, and into the Low Countries, including Liège, Utrecht and other towns. Even so, the outbreak was different from the one in 1518. The dancers of 1374 travelled in groups and seemed to be part of a movement. Some said that they saw visions of Christ or the saints. Some cried out that they were being tormented by demons. Chronicles describe dancers who swelled up after dancing and asked people to bind them tightly with cloth. Priests tried to cure them with exorcisms and prayers. The outbreak lasted for several months and then faded away. Smaller outbreaks continued in the following decades.

{img:69-black-death-memory-town-graveyard}

The dancing manias of the fourteenth century followed the terrible years of the Black Death. Historians who study the dancing manias note that they often happened after times of great suffering. The outbreak of 1374 came about a generation after the Black Death, which had killed perhaps a third of the population of Europe between 1347 and 1351. In the years that followed, plague returned several times. There were also famines, floods and wars. Even so, the connection between suffering and dancing is not simple. Many disasters were not followed by dancing manias. Historians believe that dancing outbreaks happened when great suffering combined with particular beliefs. In the Rhineland and nearby regions, people believed that saints could curse them with dancing. When they were desperate, frightened and exhausted, some of them may have fallen into a trance-like state shaped by these beliefs. The same pattern can be seen in 1518. After years of famine and disease, the people of Strasbourg were suffering greatly, and they believed in the curse of Saint Vitus.

{img:70-italian-village-musicians-tarantella}

In southern Italy, people believed that dancing to special music could cure the bite of a spider. In southern Italy, especially in the region of Apulia, a different kind of dancing illness was known. Historians note that it was called tarantism. People believed that they had been bitten by a tarantula spider, and that its poison caused illness, sadness and strange behaviour. The cure was to dance to special music, played by musicians on violins, tambourines and other instruments. The dancing could last for hours or days, until the poison was thought to have been driven out. Even so, scientists today know that the bite of the local spiders was not usually dangerous. Historians believe that tarantism was a cultural tradition, shaped by local beliefs and customs. It may have allowed people, especially women, to express emotions and find relief from hardship. Tarantism continued in some villages until the twentieth century. It gave its name to a lively folk dance, the tarantella, which is still danced in Italy today.

{img:71-medieval-chronicle-book-illustrated-dancers}

The Strasbourg dancing plague of 1518 is one of the last and best recorded dancing manias. After 1518, dancing manias became rarer in Europe. Historians note that there were a few smaller outbreaks in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, but none on the same scale. The Strasbourg dancing plague is one of the last major outbreaks and one of the best recorded. Its council records, chronicles and the writings of Paracelsus give historians a detailed picture of what happened. Even so, historians still debate why dancing manias stopped. Some believe that changes in religion played a part. The Protestant Reformation rejected the worship of saints, which may have weakened belief in the curse of Saint Vitus. Others point to changes in medicine and in daily life. Over time, people stopped believing that saints could make them dance, and the dancing stopped. Historians note that this supports the idea that dancing manias were shaped by beliefs. When the beliefs faded, the dancing faded too. The memory, however, survived in chronicles and stories.

[long-pause]

{img:72-rye-field-dark-grains-ergot-fungus}

One theory suggests that the dancers were poisoned by a fungus that grows on rye bread. One of the best known explanations for the dancing plague is ergot poisoning. Historians note that ergot is a fungus that grows on rye and other grains, especially in damp weather. It forms dark, hard growths in place of the grains. If ergot is ground into flour and eaten in bread, it can cause a serious illness called ergotism. In the Middle Ages, ergotism was known as Saint Anthony's fire. It caused burning pain, hallucinations, convulsions and, in severe cases, gangrene, which made fingers and toes blacken and fall off. Even so, many historians doubt that ergot caused the dancing plague. Ergot poisoning causes spasms and convulsions, but it also cuts off blood flow to the limbs. People with ergotism could not have danced for days on end. The movements of the dancers, according to witnesses, were not like the spasms of ergotism. Ergot may explain some medieval outbreaks, but probably not the dancing of 1518.

{img:73-historian-desk-books-notes-library}

The historian John Waller has argued that the dancers fell into a trance caused by stress and belief. In 2008, the historian John Waller published a book about the dancing plague of 1518, called A Time to Dance, a Time to Die. Historians note that it is the most detailed study of the event. Waller argued that the dancing was a form of mass psychogenic illness. This means an illness caused by the mind rather than by an infection or poison, which spreads among people who share the same fears and beliefs. According to Waller, the people of Strasbourg were under extreme stress from famine, disease and poverty. They believed that Saint Vitus could curse them with dancing. Even so, he did not suggest that the dancers were pretending. He believed that they fell into a genuine trance, which they could not control. Their belief in the saint's curse shaped the form of their trance, making them dance. When they saw others dancing, they feared that they too had been cursed, and the trance spread from person to person.

[long-pause]

{img:74-trance-dancer-eyes-closed-crowd}

A trance is a state in which a person seems unaware of their surroundings and loses control of their actions. A trance is a state of mind in which a person seems to lose awareness of their surroundings. Historians and scientists note that people in a trance may move, speak or act without full control, and they may not remember what happened afterwards. Trances can be caused by many things, including music, dancing, prayer, stress, exhaustion and strong beliefs. In many cultures around the world, trances are part of religious rituals and celebrations. Even so, trances are not always pleasant or voluntary. Some people fall into trances because of extreme stress or fear. They may feel that they are possessed by a spirit or controlled by an outside force. According to this theory, the dancers of 1518 fell into such a state. They believed that Saint Vitus had cursed them, and their bodies responded by dancing. Their trance continued until they were taken to the saint's shrine, where they believed the curse could be lifted.

[long-pause]

{img:75-crowd-watching-anxious-faces-square}

Mass psychogenic illness has been observed many times in history, in schools, factories and towns. Mass psychogenic illness, sometimes called mass hysteria, is a phenomenon in which a group of people develop similar symptoms without a physical cause. Historians and scientists note that it has been observed many times in history and in modern times. It often happens in close communities, such as schools, factories, convents and villages. Symptoms can include fainting, shaking, laughing, headaches and breathing problems. Even so, the symptoms are real. The people affected are not pretending or lying. Their minds and bodies are responding to stress and fear in ways they cannot control. The illness usually spreads among people who know each other and share the same worries. One famous example happened in 1962 in Tanganyika, now Tanzania, where an outbreak of uncontrollable laughter spread among schoolchildren and to nearby villages. Historians believe that the dancing plague of 1518 may be an example of this phenomenon, shaped by the beliefs and suffering of its time.

[long-pause]

{img:76-poor-woman-thoughtful-window-light}

Historians believe that the dancers' suffering, fear and faith all played a part in what happened to them. Historians who have studied the dancing plague agree that it cannot be explained by a single cause. Most believe that it was the result of several factors working together. The people of Strasbourg had suffered years of famine, disease and poverty. They were exhausted, hungry and frightened. They lived in a world in which saints were believed to have great power, and in which Saint Vitus was thought to curse people with dancing. Even so, not everyone in Strasbourg danced. Only a few hundred people out of twenty thousand were affected. Historians believe that those who danced may have been under particular stress or may have been especially devout. Many were poor, and many were women. Frau Troffea may have been suffering in ways that the sources do not describe. When she began to dance, others who shared her fears and hardships may have been drawn into the same state. Their suffering, fear and faith combined in an extraordinary way.

[long-pause]

{img:77-candle-flickering-quiet-room-mystery}

Even today, some questions about the dancing plague cannot be answered with certainty. Despite careful research, some questions about the dancing plague of 1518 remain unanswered. Historians do not know exactly how many people danced or how many died. They do not know the names of most of the dancers or what happened to them afterwards. They cannot be certain about the cause of the dancing, though most agree that it was linked to stress and belief. Even so, the event is well documented compared with many others of its time. The council records prove that it happened and that the authorities took it seriously. The chronicles and the writings of Paracelsus add detail and colour. Together, they tell a remarkable story about how people in the past understood illness, faith and suffering. Historians note that the dancing plague is a reminder that the human mind and body are closely connected. Under certain conditions, fear and belief can have powerful effects. The dancers of 1518 were not foolish. They were people overwhelmed by their circumstances.

{img:78-scholar-paracelsus-writing-notes-candle}

Paracelsus believed the dancing was an illness of the mind, and his ideas were surprisingly modern. When Paracelsus wrote about the dancing plague in the 1520s, he suggested that it was an illness of the mind and the imagination. Historians note that he divided dancing illnesses into several types. Some, he thought, were caused by imagination, others by the senses, and others by natural causes in the body. He criticised the idea that saints caused the dancing. Instead, he believed that the dancers' own thoughts and emotions had led them into the dance. Even so, his explanations also reflected the beliefs of his time. He suggested remedies such as fasting, isolation and cold water. He also had harsh views about women. Yet historians note that his idea that the mind could cause illness was surprisingly modern. It anticipated, in a simple form, some of the explanations given by historians and scientists today. Paracelsus was a controversial figure in his own time, but he is now remembered as an important pioneer in the history of medicine.

{img:79-reformation-preacher-strasbourg-1523}

A few years after the dancing plague, Strasbourg became a centre of the Protestant Reformation. In the years after the dancing plague, Strasbourg changed greatly. Historians note that the ideas of Martin Luther and other reformers spread quickly to the city in the early 1520s. Preachers such as Matthäus Zell, Wolfgang Capito and Martin Bucer taught that people should rely on the Bible and on faith alone, rather than on saints, relics and pilgrimages. Even so, the change was gradual. At first, Catholics and Protestants lived side by side, and there were many debates. By 1529, the city council had abolished the Catholic mass, and Strasbourg became officially Protestant. The cathedral was used for Protestant services. The worship of saints, including Saint Vitus, was rejected. Historians note that this change may have helped prevent future dancing manias in Strasbourg, since people no longer believed in the saint's curse. The city became famous as a place where reformers from many countries could find refuge and share ideas.

In 1525, the peasants of Alsace rose up against their lords in the great Peasants' War. The hardships that troubled Strasbourg in 1518 did not end with the dancing. Historians note that in 1525, a great uprising broke out across southern and central Germany and Alsace. It was called the German Peasants' War. Peasants demanded lower rents and taxes, the right to choose their own priests and an end to serfdom, a system that tied them to their lords' land. Some were inspired by the ideas of the Reformation. Even so, the uprising was crushed. In Alsace, the Duke of Lorraine led an army against the peasants. In May 1525, thousands of them were killed near Saverne, the same town where the dancers had been taken in 1518. Historians estimate that about one hundred thousand peasants died in the war across Germany. Strasbourg itself did not join the uprising, though some of its citizens sympathised with the peasants. The war showed how deep the suffering and anger of ordinary people had become.

In 1681, Strasbourg became part of France, when it was taken by King Louis the Fourteenth. For more than a century after the dancing plague, Strasbourg remained a free city of the Holy Roman Empire. Historians note that it survived the religious wars and the Thirty Years' War, which devastated much of Germany between 1618 and 1648. Even so, its independence did not last forever. After the Franco-Prussian War of 1870 to 1871, it became part of Germany. After the First World War, it returned to France. The cathedral of Strasbourg still stands, just as it did when the dancers filled the streets below.
