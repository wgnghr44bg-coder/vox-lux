TOPIC: Saint Vitus and his curse
===
IMG: saint-vitus-painted-altar-panel-church | a painted wooden altar panel in a small Gothic church showing a young saint in rich robes holding a palm branch, gold leaf glowing in candlelight, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a vaulted ceiling, warm candlelight
HEAD: Saint Vitus was an early Christian martyr who, people believed, could send and cure a dancing illness.
BODY: Saint Vitus was a young Christian who, according to legend, lived in Sicily around the year 300. Historians note that very little is known about his real life. According to the stories told about him, he was the son of a pagan nobleman and converted to Christianity as a boy. During the persecution of Christians under the Roman emperor Diocletian, he was arrested and put to death. Even so, his fame grew after his death. His relics, the remains of his body, were taken to several places in Europe, including the abbey of Corvey in Germany and later the cathedral of Prague. He became one of the most popular saints of the Middle Ages. People prayed to him for protection against many illnesses. In German-speaking lands, he was especially linked to dancing. People believed that he could curse sinners with a dancing illness, and that he could cure those who prayed to him with true faith.
===
IMG: medieval-saints-day-festival-dancing | villagers in medieval clothes dancing in a circle around a decorated pole on a feast day outside a small stone church, musicians playing pipes and drums, summer meadow, warm afternoon light
HEAD: Saint Vitus's feast day fell in June, in the middle of summer, and was celebrated with dancing.
BODY: The feast of Saint Vitus was celebrated on the fifteenth of June. Historians note that in many parts of Germany and central Europe, it was a time of summer festivals, with music, bonfires and dancing. People danced in front of statues of the saint, hoping to receive his blessing and good health for the coming year. Even so, the dancing had a darker side. People believed that if they offended the saint, he might punish them with an uncontrollable dancing illness. This belief was widespread in the Rhineland and Alsace. The illness was called Saint Vitus's dance. Historians note that the name was later used by doctors for a medical condition that causes jerky movements, now called Sydenham's chorea. However, the dancing illness of 1518 was different from this medical condition. It was shaped by beliefs about the saint. People who feared the saint's anger may have been more likely to fall into the dancing state.

IMG: woman-praying-saint-statue-candles | a woman in clothes of 1518 kneeling before a painted wooden statue of a young saint in a side chapel, lighting a candle among many others, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a vaulted ceiling, warm candlelight
HEAD: People in Strasbourg believed that Saint Vitus had cursed the dancers, and only he could free them.
BODY: By August 1518, many people in Strasbourg were convinced that the dancing was the work of Saint Vitus. Historians note that some chronicles record that the dancers themselves called on the saint for help. Some said that they saw visions or felt as if they were being punished. Even so, belief in Saint Vitus did not only cause fear. It also offered hope. If the saint had sent the illness, he could also take it away. People prayed to him in churches across the city. They lit candles before his images and made promises to him. Some made offerings of money or wax, hoping to win his favour. The city council shared these beliefs. Historians note that its members decided to send the dancers to a shrine dedicated to Saint Vitus, where they could ask the saint directly for healing. This decision marked a turning point in the crisis. It brought together the city's faith, its leaders and its suffering people.

IMG: saint-vitus-relics-cathedral-prague | a golden reliquary decorated with jewels on an altar in a dim Gothic cathedral chapel, candles burning around it and pilgrims kneeling in prayer, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a vaulted ceiling, warm candlelight
HEAD: The relics of Saint Vitus were treasured in great churches across Europe, including in Prague.
BODY: In the Middle Ages, people believed that the relics of saints had special powers. Historians note that relics included bones, hair, clothing and objects that had belonged to saints. They were kept in precious containers called reliquaries, often decorated with gold and jewels. Pilgrims travelled long distances to pray before them, hoping for healing or forgiveness. Even so, the relics of Saint Vitus were divided among many places. Some were kept at the abbey of Corvey in Saxony, where they had been brought in the ninth century. In 1355, the emperor Charles the Fourth brought some to Prague, where the great Cathedral of Saint Vitus was being built. Smaller shrines and chapels dedicated to the saint existed across the German lands, including in Alsace. The nearest to Strasbourg was at Saverne, a town at the foot of the Vosges mountains, about forty kilometres to the northwest. It was there that the dancers would be taken.

IMG: shoes-red-crosses-holy-oil-altar | a pair of small red leather shoes marked with crosses lying on a stone altar beside a small flask of holy oil and burning candles in a dim chapel, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a vaulted ceiling, soft candlelight
HEAD: The dancers were given red shoes marked with crosses and holy oil, as part of the healing ritual.
BODY: According to the records of the Strasbourg council and the chronicles, the dancers who were taken to Saverne were given small crosses and red shoes. Historians note that crosses were marked on the shoes, both on the top and the soles, with holy oil. The red shoes were connected with the dancing. In some legends, red shoes were associated with sinful dancing and could force their wearer to dance. By marking them with crosses and holy oil, the priests turned them into objects of healing. Even so, historians do not know exactly how the ritual was performed. The sources are brief. They mention that priests said mass, prayed over the dancers and led them around the altar. The dancers were sprinkled with holy water and asked to pray to the saint. The rituals were meant to show the saint that the dancers repented their sins, so that he would lift his curse and free them from the dance.

HEAD: Priests prayed over the dancers and asked Saint Vitus to forgive them and end their suffering.
BODY: The priests at the shrine of Saint Vitus played an important role in the healing of the dancers. Historians note that they led the religious services and performed the rituals. They prayed over each dancer, asking the saint to forgive their sins and release them from the curse. Even so, the priests were not only performing rituals. They were also offering comfort and hope to people who had suffered greatly. The dancers had been exhausted, frightened and confused for weeks. At the shrine, they were treated with care and respect. They were in a quiet place, away from the crowds and noise of the city. They were surrounded by people who believed that they could be healed. Historians believe that this calm and hopeful atmosphere may have been one of the reasons the pilgrimage worked. For people who believed in the saint's power, the ritual itself may have helped to end their dancing state.

HEAD: The shrine of Saint Vitus near Saverne was a small chapel in a cave on a wooded hillside.
BODY: The shrine of Saint Vitus near Saverne was located in a grotto, a small cave, on a hill above the town. Historians note that a chapel had been built there, and pilgrims had visited it for many years. It was a quiet and peaceful place, surrounded by forests. From the hill, there were views across the plain of Alsace. Even so, the shrine was not grand. It was a simple chapel with an altar and an image of the saint. Pilgrims climbed the path up the hill to reach it, praying as they went. Many came seeking healing from illnesses, especially those linked to Saint Vitus. Some left offerings, such as candles, wax figures or small objects. Historians note that the chapel's peaceful setting may have been important for the dancers. After weeks of noise and chaos in the city, the calm of the forest and the quiet of the chapel offered rest. The site can still be visited today.

HEAD: In August 1518, the dancers were put into wagons and taken out of the city towards Saverne.
BODY: In August 1518, the city council organised the journey of the dancers to the shrine of Saint Vitus near Saverne. Historians note that the dancers were placed in wagons and taken out of the city in groups. They were accompanied by guards, priests and helpers. Their families watched them leave, hoping that they would return healed. Even so, the journey was not easy. The dancers were weak and exhausted, and some may have continued to move restlessly in the wagons. The road to Saverne was about forty kilometres long, crossing the plain of Alsace. It took about a day to travel. Historians note that sending the dancers away from the city may also have helped to calm the situation in Strasbourg. With the dancers gone, the crowds dispersed, and the atmosphere of fear began to ease. The council hoped that the saint would heal the dancers, and that the city would return to normal.

HEAD: The road to Saverne crossed the plain of Alsace, through fields, villages and vineyards.
BODY: The road from Strasbourg to Saverne ran across the wide plain of Alsace. Historians note that it passed through fields of wheat and barley, orchards and small villages with timber-framed houses and church towers. In the distance, the Vosges mountains rose blue against the sky. In August, the harvest was underway. Peasants worked in the fields, cutting grain with sickles and gathering it into sheaves. Even so, the harvest of 1518 was not a happy one for many. After years of bad weather, many families were still struggling. The peasants watched the wagons of dancers pass by. Some may have known about the dancing plague in Strasbourg. Others may have been surprised by the strange procession. The journey across the plain was a slow one, with stops for rest and water. For the dancers, it was a journey of hope. At the end of the road, they believed, the saint was waiting to heal them.

HEAD: Saverne was a small town at the foot of the Vosges mountains, where the bishops of Strasbourg had a castle.
BODY: Saverne, called Zabern in German, is a small town at the foot of the Vosges mountains, on the road from Strasbourg to Lorraine. Historians note that in the sixteenth century, it was the residence of the bishops of Strasbourg. They had moved there after the citizens of Strasbourg gained their independence. The bishops had a castle in the town. Even so, Saverne was not a large or wealthy place. It had a few thousand inhabitants, who lived from farming, trade and crafts. It was surrounded by forests and hills. Pilgrims came to the town to visit the shrine of Saint Vitus on the hill nearby. In 1518, the arrival of the dancers from Strasbourg must have been a remarkable event for the people of Saverne. They had heard about the dancing plague and may have watched with curiosity and sympathy as the wagons arrived and the dancers were led up the hill to the chapel.

Even so, the walk through the quiet forest may have been calming after the chaos of the city. The trees gave shade from the summer sun, and birds sang in the branches. At the top of the hill, the chapel waited. The priests prepared the altar and lit candles. The dancers were brought inside, one group at a time. Historians imagine the scene: exhausted people, their feet bruised and bandaged, standing or kneeling before the altar in the dim light of the chapel. The priests began to pray. For the dancers, this was the moment they had been waiting for. They hoped that the saint would hear their prayers and set them free.

HEAD: After the pilgrimage, many dancers stopped dancing, and by September the plague was over.
BODY: After the rituals at the shrine of Saint Vitus, many of the dancers stopped dancing. Historians note that the sources report that the pilgrimage was successful, and that the dancers returned to Strasbourg healed. By early September 1518, the dancing plague had come to an end. Even so, historians cannot be certain that every dancer was cured, or that the pilgrimage alone ended the crisis. Some dancers may have recovered on their own as the summer heat faded. Others may have died. Yet the sources suggest that the pilgrimage played an important role. For people who believed that the saint had cursed them, the ritual offered a way to be released. Historians believe that the dancers' faith in the saint may have helped them to recover. The crowds that had gathered to watch the dancers dispersed, and the streets became quiet again. The ban on public music and dancing remained in place for some time. Even so, the memory of the dancing plague did not fade quickly. People talked about it for years. Some remained afraid that it might return. The council continued to watch for signs of new dancers. Historians note that there were a few later cases of dancing in Strasbourg, but nothing on the scale of 1518. Life went on. The harvest was gathered, and the city prepared for winter. Families who had suffered during the dancing tried to recover. The dancers themselves returned to their homes and work, though some may have been weakened for a long time. The extraordinary summer of 1518 was over.

TOPIC: Dancing manias before 1518
===
IMG: medieval-churchyard-dancers-christmas-snow | a small group of villagers in early medieval clothes dancing in a circle in a snowy churchyard on Christmas Eve, a small stone church with lit windows behind, cold blue night light
HEAD: Stories of dancing illnesses had been told in Europe for centuries before 1518.
BODY: The dancing plague of 1518 was not the first of its kind. Historians note that stories of uncontrollable dancing had been told in Europe since the Middle Ages. One of the oldest is a legend from the village of Kölbigk, in Saxony, around the year 1021. According to the story, a group of young people danced in a churchyard on Christmas Eve, disturbing the priest's mass. The priest cursed them, and they were forced to dance for a whole year without stopping. Even so, this story is a legend, told as a warning against sinful behaviour. It may not describe a real event. Yet it shows that the idea of a dancing curse was old and widespread. People in the Middle Ages believed that dancing at the wrong time or place could anger God or the saints. These beliefs were still alive in 1518. They helped shape how the people of Strasbourg understood the dancing that broke out in their city.
===
IMG: children-dancing-road-erfurt-1237 | a long line of children in medieval clothes dancing and skipping along a country road between two towns in 1237, worried parents following behind, green fields and a distant town, soft morning light
HEAD: In 1237, a large group of children was said to have danced from the town of Erfurt to the town of Arnstadt.
BODY: According to a chronicle, in 1237 a large group of children left the town of Erfurt, in Germany, and danced and jumped along the road to the town of Arnstadt, about twenty kilometres away. Historians note that the children were exhausted when they arrived, and their parents had to fetch them home. Some of them, according to the chronicle, suffered from shaking for the rest of their lives. Even so, historians are not sure what really happened. The story may have been exaggerated over time. It is sometimes compared with the legend of the Pied Piper of Hamelin, in which the children of a town follow a mysterious piper and disappear. That legend is linked to an event in 1284. Historians note that many medieval stories describe groups of people, especially children, being led away by music or dancing. These stories reflect the fears and beliefs of the time about the power of music, dancing and strange forces.
===
IMG: dancers-rhineland-1374-procession-town | a crowd of men and women in fourteenth century clothes dancing wildly through the streets of a German Rhineland town in 1374, townspeople watching from doorways, a church tower behind, summer light
HEAD: In 1374, a great wave of dancing spread through towns along the Rhine and in the Low Countries.
BODY: The largest outbreak of dancing mania before 1518 happened in 1374. Historians note that it began in the city of Aachen, in Germany, in the summer of that year. Groups of dancers travelled from town to town, dancing in streets, churches and squares. The dancing spread along the Rhine to Cologne, Metz and Trier, and into the Low Countries, including Liège, Utrecht and other towns. Even so, the outbreak was different from the one in 1518. The dancers of 1374 travelled in groups and seemed to be part of a movement. Some said that they saw visions of Christ or the saints. Some cried out that they were being tormented by demons. Chronicles describe dancers who swelled up after dancing and asked people to bind them tightly with cloth. Priests tried to cure them with exorcisms and prayers. The outbreak lasted for several months and then faded away. Smaller outbreaks continued in the following decades.

IMG: black-death-memory-town-graveyard | a quiet medieval graveyard beside a stone church with many simple wooden crosses, an old man and a woman praying among the graves, grey overcast light
HEAD: The dancing manias of the fourteenth century followed the terrible years of the Black Death.
BODY: Historians who study the dancing manias note that they often happened after times of great suffering. The outbreak of 1374 came about a generation after the Black Death, which had killed perhaps a third of the population of Europe between 1347 and 1351. In the years that followed, plague returned several times. There were also famines, floods and wars. Even so, the connection between suffering and dancing is not simple. Many disasters were not followed by dancing manias. Historians believe that dancing outbreaks happened when great suffering combined with particular beliefs. In the Rhineland and nearby regions, people believed that saints could curse them with dancing. When they were desperate, frightened and exhausted, some of them may have fallen into a trance-like state shaped by these beliefs. The same pattern can be seen in 1518. After years of famine and disease, the people of Strasbourg were suffering greatly, and they believed in the curse of Saint Vitus.

IMG: italian-village-musicians-tarantella | villagers in southern Italy dancing to the music of a tambourine and a violin in a sunny courtyard of a whitewashed farmhouse, a woman dancing in the middle, warm summer light
HEAD: In southern Italy, people believed that dancing to special music could cure the bite of a spider.
BODY: In southern Italy, especially in the region of Apulia, a different kind of dancing illness was known. Historians note that it was called tarantism. People believed that they had been bitten by a tarantula spider, and that its poison caused illness, sadness and strange behaviour. The cure was to dance to special music, played by musicians on violins, tambourines and other instruments. The dancing could last for hours or days, until the poison was thought to have been driven out. Even so, scientists today know that the bite of the local spiders was not usually dangerous. Historians believe that tarantism was a cultural tradition, shaped by local beliefs and customs. It may have allowed people, especially women, to express emotions and find relief from hardship. Tarantism continued in some villages until the twentieth century. It gave its name to a lively folk dance, the tarantella, which is still danced in Italy today.

HEAD: The Strasbourg dancing plague of 1518 is one of the last and best recorded dancing manias.
BODY: After 1518, dancing manias became rarer in Europe. Historians note that there were a few smaller outbreaks in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, but none on the same scale. The Strasbourg dancing plague is one of the last major outbreaks and one of the best recorded. Its council records, chronicles and the writings of Paracelsus give historians a detailed picture of what happened. Even so, historians still debate why dancing manias stopped. Some believe that changes in religion played a part. The Protestant Reformation rejected the worship of saints, which may have weakened belief in the curse of Saint Vitus. Others point to changes in medicine and in daily life. Over time, people stopped believing that saints could make them dance, and the dancing stopped. Historians note that this supports the idea that dancing manias were shaped by beliefs. When the beliefs faded, the dancing faded too. The memory, however, survived in chronicles and stories.

TOPIC: What caused the dancing
===
IMG: rye-field-dark-grains-ergot-fungus | a field of rye in Alsace ready for harvest with some ears showing small dark purple fungal growths, peasants with sickles in the distance, warm late summer light
HEAD: One theory suggests that the dancers were poisoned by a fungus that grows on rye bread.
BODY: One of the best known explanations for the dancing plague is ergot poisoning. Historians note that ergot is a fungus that grows on rye and other grains, especially in damp weather. It forms dark, hard growths in place of the grains. If ergot is ground into flour and eaten in bread, it can cause a serious illness called ergotism. In the Middle Ages, ergotism was known as Saint Anthony's fire. It caused burning pain, hallucinations, convulsions and, in severe cases, gangrene, which made fingers and toes blacken and fall off. Even so, many historians doubt that ergot caused the dancing plague. Ergot poisoning causes spasms and convulsions, but it also cuts off blood flow to the limbs. People with ergotism could not have danced for days on end. The movements of the dancers, according to witnesses, were not like the spasms of ergotism. Ergot may explain some medieval outbreaks, but probably not the dancing of 1518.
===
IMG: historian-desk-books-notes-library | a quiet library reading room with old leather-bound books, notes and a reading lamp on a wooden table, tall windows with soft daylight, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling
HEAD: The historian John Waller has argued that the dancers fell into a trance caused by stress and belief.
BODY: In 2008, the historian John Waller published a book about the dancing plague of 1518, called A Time to Dance, a Time to Die. Historians note that it is the most detailed study of the event. Waller argued that the dancing was a form of mass psychogenic illness. This means an illness caused by the mind rather than by an infection or poison, which spreads among people who share the same fears and beliefs. According to Waller, the people of Strasbourg were under extreme stress from famine, disease and poverty. They believed that Saint Vitus could curse them with dancing. Even so, he did not suggest that the dancers were pretending. He believed that they fell into a genuine trance, which they could not control. Their belief in the saint's curse shaped the form of their trance, making them dance. When they saw others dancing, they feared that they too had been cursed, and the trance spread from person to person.

IMG: trance-dancer-eyes-closed-crowd | a young woman in clothes of 1518 dancing with her eyes closed and arms raised as if in a trance in a crowded square in Strasbourg, onlookers watching in wonder and fear, warm hazy light
HEAD: A trance is a state in which a person seems unaware of their surroundings and loses control of their actions.
BODY: A trance is a state of mind in which a person seems to lose awareness of their surroundings. Historians and scientists note that people in a trance may move, speak or act without full control, and they may not remember what happened afterwards. Trances can be caused by many things, including music, dancing, prayer, stress, exhaustion and strong beliefs. In many cultures around the world, trances are part of religious rituals and celebrations. Even so, trances are not always pleasant or voluntary. Some people fall into trances because of extreme stress or fear. They may feel that they are possessed by a spirit or controlled by an outside force. According to this theory, the dancers of 1518 fell into such a state. They believed that Saint Vitus had cursed them, and their bodies responded by dancing. Their trance continued until they were taken to the saint's shrine, where they believed the curse could be lifted.
===
IMG: crowd-watching-anxious-faces-square | a crowd of anxious townspeople in clothes of 1518 watching something out of view in a market square in Strasbourg, some covering their mouths, a mother holding her child close, overcast light
HEAD: Mass psychogenic illness has been observed many times in history, in schools, factories and towns.
BODY: Mass psychogenic illness, sometimes called mass hysteria, is a phenomenon in which a group of people develop similar symptoms without a physical cause. Historians and scientists note that it has been observed many times in history and in modern times. It often happens in close communities, such as schools, factories, convents and villages. Symptoms can include fainting, shaking, laughing, headaches and breathing problems. Even so, the symptoms are real. The people affected are not pretending or lying. Their minds and bodies are responding to stress and fear in ways they cannot control. The illness usually spreads among people who know each other and share the same worries. One famous example happened in 1962 in Tanganyika, now Tanzania, where an outbreak of uncontrollable laughter spread among schoolchildren and to nearby villages. Historians believe that the dancing plague of 1518 may be an example of this phenomenon, shaped by the beliefs and suffering of its time.

HEAD: Historians believe that the dancers' suffering, fear and faith all played a part in what happened to them.
BODY: Historians who have studied the dancing plague agree that it cannot be explained by a single cause. Most believe that it was the result of several factors working together. The people of Strasbourg had suffered years of famine, disease and poverty. They were exhausted, hungry and frightened. They lived in a world in which saints were believed to have great power, and in which Saint Vitus was thought to curse people with dancing. Even so, not everyone in Strasbourg danced. Only a few hundred people out of twenty thousand were affected. Historians believe that those who danced may have been under particular stress or may have been especially devout. Many were poor, and many were women. Frau Troffea may have been suffering in ways that the sources do not describe. When she began to dance, others who shared her fears and hardships may have been drawn into the same state. Their suffering, fear and faith combined in an extraordinary way.

HEAD: Even today, some questions about the dancing plague cannot be answered with certainty.
BODY: Despite careful research, some questions about the dancing plague of 1518 remain unanswered. Historians do not know exactly how many people danced or how many died. They do not know the names of most of the dancers or what happened to them afterwards. They cannot be certain about the cause of the dancing, though most agree that it was linked to stress and belief. Even so, the event is well documented compared with many others of its time. The council records prove that it happened and that the authorities took it seriously. The chronicles and the writings of Paracelsus add detail and colour. Together, they tell a remarkable story about how people in the past understood illness, faith and suffering. Historians note that the dancing plague is a reminder that the human mind and body are closely connected. Under certain conditions, fear and belief can have powerful effects. The dancers of 1518 were not foolish. They were people overwhelmed by their circumstances.

IMG: scholar-paracelsus-writing-notes-candle | a physician in a dark robe writing notes by candlelight in a cluttered study full of books, glass flasks and dried herbs around 1526, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm candlelight
HEAD: Paracelsus believed the dancing was an illness of the mind, and his ideas were surprisingly modern.
BODY: When Paracelsus wrote about the dancing plague in the 1520s, he suggested that it was an illness of the mind and the imagination. Historians note that he divided dancing illnesses into several types. Some, he thought, were caused by imagination, others by the senses, and others by natural causes in the body. He criticised the idea that saints caused the dancing. Instead, he believed that the dancers' own thoughts and emotions had led them into the dance. Even so, his explanations also reflected the beliefs of his time. He suggested remedies such as fasting, isolation and cold water. He also had harsh views about women. Yet historians note that his idea that the mind could cause illness was surprisingly modern. It anticipated, in a simple form, some of the explanations given by historians and scientists today. Paracelsus was a controversial figure in his own time, but he is now remembered as an important pioneer in the history of medicine.

TOPIC: Strasbourg after the dancing
===
IMG: reformation-preacher-strasbourg-1523 | a Protestant preacher in a plain black gown and cap preaching from a pulpit to a crowded congregation in a Gothic church in Strasbourg in 1523, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a vaulted ceiling, soft window light
HEAD: A few years after the dancing plague, Strasbourg became a centre of the Protestant Reformation.
BODY: In the years after the dancing plague, Strasbourg changed greatly. Historians note that the ideas of Martin Luther and other reformers spread quickly to the city in the early 1520s. Preachers such as Matthäus Zell, Wolfgang Capito and Martin Bucer taught that people should rely on the Bible and on faith alone, rather than on saints, relics and pilgrimages. Even so, the change was gradual. At first, Catholics and Protestants lived side by side, and there were many debates. By 1529, the city council had abolished the Catholic mass, and Strasbourg became officially Protestant. The cathedral was used for Protestant services. The worship of saints, including Saint Vitus, was rejected. Historians note that this change may have helped prevent future dancing manias in Strasbourg, since people no longer believed in the saint's curse. The city became famous as a place where reformers from many countries could find refuge and share ideas.

HEAD: In 1525, the peasants of Alsace rose up against their lords in the great Peasants' War.
BODY: The hardships that troubled Strasbourg in 1518 did not end with the dancing. Historians note that in 1525, a great uprising broke out across southern and central Germany and Alsace. It was called the German Peasants' War. Peasants demanded lower rents and taxes, the right to choose their own priests and an end to serfdom, a system that tied them to their lords' land. Some were inspired by the ideas of the Reformation. Even so, the uprising was crushed. In Alsace, the Duke of Lorraine led an army against the peasants. In May 1525, thousands of them were killed near Saverne, the same town where the dancers had been taken in 1518. Historians estimate that about one hundred thousand peasants died in the war across Germany. Strasbourg itself did not join the uprising, though some of its citizens sympathised with the peasants. The war showed how deep the suffering and anger of ordinary people had become.

HEAD: In 1681, Strasbourg became part of France, when it was taken by King Louis the Fourteenth.
BODY: For more than a century after the dancing plague, Strasbourg remained a free city of the Holy Roman Empire. Historians note that it survived the religious wars and the Thirty Years' War, which devastated much of Germany between 1618 and 1648. Even so, its independence did not last forever. In 1681, the French king Louis the Fourteenth took control of Strasbourg. The city surrendered to a French army without a fight and became part of France. The cathedral was returned to the Catholic Church, though many citizens remained Protestant. Over the following centuries, Strasbourg changed hands several times. After the Franco-Prussian War of 1870 to 1871, it became part of Germany. After the First World War, it returned to France. It was occupied by Germany again during the Second World War and returned to France in 1944. Today, its history is reflected in its mix of French and German culture.

HEAD: Many of the old streets and houses of Strasbourg from the time of the dancing plague can still be seen today.
BODY: Today, Strasbourg is the capital of the region of Alsace and one of the largest cities in eastern France. Historians note that its old centre, the Grande Île, surrounded by the branches of the river Ill, has been listed as a World Heritage Site since 1988. Visitors can walk through narrow streets lined with timber-framed houses, many built in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. The Petite France quarter, where millers, tanners and fishermen once worked, is especially beautiful, with its canals, locks and flower-covered balconies. Even so, the city is not just a museum. It is a lively modern city, home to universities and international institutions, including the European Parliament. The cathedral still rises above the old town, its pink sandstone glowing in the sunlight. Its bells still ring. Walking through the old streets, visitors can imagine the summer of 1518, when the sound of dancing feet echoed between the same houses.

HEAD: The cathedral of Strasbourg still stands, just as it did when the dancers filled the streets below.
BODY: More than five hundred years after the dancing plague, the cathedral of Strasbourg still dominates the city. Historians note that it has survived wars, fires, revolutions and bombings. During the French Revolution, its statues were damaged, and its spire was nearly pulled down. A clever citizen saved it by placing a giant red cap of liberty on its top, so that it would be seen as a symbol of the revolution. Even so, the cathedral remains much as it was in 1518. Its stained glass windows, its pulpit and its great spire are still there. It also contains a famous astronomical clock, rebuilt in the nineteenth century, which shows the movements of the sun, moon and planets. Every day at half past twelve, its mechanical figures move in a small procession. Visitors come from around the world to see it. The cathedral is a living link to the people of the past, including those who danced in its shadow.

HEAD: The council records of 1518 are still kept in the archives of Strasbourg.
BODY: The records of the Strasbourg city council from 1518 have survived for more than five hundred years. Historians note that they are kept today in the city's archives, along with many other documents from the history of Strasbourg. Researchers come to study them, reading the old handwriting and piecing together the story of the dancing plague. Even so, many documents from the period have been lost over the centuries, through fires, wars and neglect. The ones that survive are precious. They show the decisions of the council, the payments made to musicians and helpers, and the arrangements for the pilgrimage to Saverne. Historians note that the records make the dancing plague one of the best documented events of its kind. Without them, the story might have been dismissed as a legend. Instead, it is recognised as a real event, which tells us much about life, belief and suffering in early sixteenth century Europe.

HEAD: The story of the dancing plague has inspired artists, writers and musicians for five centuries.
BODY: The dancing plague of 1518 has fascinated people for centuries. Historians note that it has inspired paintings, poems, novels, plays, music and films. The artist Pieter Brueghel the Younger painted a famous picture of pilgrims dancing on their way to a shrine, based on a drawing by his father, Pieter Bruegel the Elder, which showed epileptics or dancers being led to the church of Saint John at Molenbeek. Even so, these images were not made in Strasbourg and do not show the events of 1518 directly. Yet they reflect the same beliefs and experiences. In recent years, writers and artists have returned to the story. Exhibitions, documentaries and books have explored it. In 2018, the five hundredth anniversary of the dancing plague was marked by events in Strasbourg. The story continues to capture the imagination, because it is both strange and deeply human. It shows how people can be overwhelmed by forces they do not understand.

HEAD: Five hundred years later, the summer of 1518 is remembered as one of the strangest seasons in European history.
BODY: The summer of 1518 lasted only a few months, but it left a lasting mark on history. Historians note that the dancing plague of Strasbourg is now one of the most famous events of its kind. It is studied by historians, doctors, psychologists and anthropologists, each looking for different lessons. Some see it as an example of how stress and belief can affect the body. Others see it as a window into the religious and medical ideas of the time. Even so, at its heart, the story is about people. Frau Troffea and the hundreds of others who danced were real men and women, suffering in a difficult time. Their families tried to help them, and their city tried to find a cure. In the end, it was faith, rest and care that seemed to bring them peace. On quiet summer evenings in Strasbourg today, the streets are calm, and the only dancing is the swallows in the sky.
