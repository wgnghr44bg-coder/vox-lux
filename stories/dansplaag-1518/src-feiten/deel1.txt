TOPIC: The dancing plague of Strasbourg begins
===
IMG: woman-dancing-alone-medieval-street-1518 | a woman in a simple long dress and white headscarf dancing alone in a narrow street of timber-framed houses in Strasbourg in July 1518, neighbours watching from doorways and windows, warm summer light
HEAD: In July 1518, a woman began to dance in a street in Strasbourg, and she could not stop.
BODY: In the middle of July 1518, a woman named Frau Troffea stepped into a narrow street in Strasbourg and began to dance. There was no music and no celebration. She danced on and on, through the afternoon and into the evening, until she collapsed with exhaustion. After a short rest, she got up and began again. Historians know about her from chronicles written in the city and from the writings of the physician Paracelsus, who later studied the event. Neighbours gathered to watch. Some were amused, some were worried, and some thought she must be possessed. Even so, she continued to dance for several days. Her feet became bruised and bloody, but she could not stop. This was the beginning of one of the strangest events in European history. Within weeks, dozens of other people in Strasbourg had joined her. The event became known as the dancing plague of 1518.
===
IMG: crowd-watching-dancers-square-strasbourg | a crowd of townspeople in clothes of 1518 standing around a small group of people dancing wildly in a cobbled square in Strasbourg, the cathedral spire rising behind, warm summer afternoon light
HEAD: Within a week, more than thirty people were dancing in the streets of the city.
BODY: Frau Troffea did not dance alone for long. According to the chronicles, within about a week, more than thirty people had begun to dance in the same way. Men and women, young and old, were seized by the urge to dance. They danced in the streets, in squares and in their homes, day and night. Historians note that the dancers did not seem to enjoy it. Witnesses described them as being in a trance, unable to stop, crying out for help and pain. Even so, the dancing continued. Crowds gathered to watch. Some people prayed for the dancers. Others feared that the strange illness might spread to them. The city's leaders were alarmed. They did not know what was happening or how to stop it. As July turned into August, the number of dancers kept growing. Strasbourg, one of the richest cities of the Holy Roman Empire, was facing a mystery no one could explain.
===
IMG: chronicler-writing-book-monastery-desk | a chronicler in a dark robe and cap writing in a large book with a quill at a wooden desk by a small leaded window in a room in Strasbourg around 1520, books and candles around him, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft daylight
HEAD: Chronicles, council records and a doctor's writings tell the story of the dancing plague.
BODY: The story of the dancing plague is known from several kinds of sources. Historians note that the most important are the records of Strasbourg's city council, which describe the decisions made during the crisis. Other information comes from chronicles written by citizens of Strasbourg, some at the time and some many years later. The physician Paracelsus, who visited Strasbourg in the 1520s, wrote about the event and described Frau Troffea. Some chronicles were written in verse, others in plain prose. Even so, the sources do not always agree. They give different numbers of dancers, different dates and different explanations. Some later chronicles add dramatic details that may not be true. The historian John Waller studied these sources carefully in his book about the event, published in 2008. He showed that the dancing plague really did happen, and that it was taken very seriously by the people and the authorities of Strasbourg.
===
IMG: exhausted-dancers-resting-street-evening | a few exhausted people in clothes of 1518 sitting and lying on the cobbles of a narrow street at evening, worn shoes and bare feet, concerned neighbours bringing water, warm fading light
HEAD: The dancers danced until they collapsed, rested briefly and then rose to dance again.
BODY: Witnesses described the dancers as moving in a way that looked uncontrolled. Historians note that they jumped, twirled and swayed, sometimes with their arms flailing. They seemed unaware of the people around them. When they became exhausted, they collapsed to the ground and lay still for a while. Then they got up and began to dance again. Their feet swelled and bled, and their bodies were covered in sweat. Even so, they could not stop. Some cried out that they were in pain or that they had visions. Families tried to care for them, bringing water and food and trying to hold them still. It was summer, and the weather was hot. The dancers suffered from heat and thirst. According to some later chronicles, some dancers died from exhaustion, heart failure or strokes. Historians are not sure how many died, since the records from the time do not give clear numbers.
===
IMG: city-council-meeting-hall-strasbourg-1518 | members of a city council in dark robes and fur-trimmed coats sitting around a long table in a wood-panelled council hall in Strasbourg in 1518, papers and an hourglass on the table, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft window light
HEAD: The city council of Strasbourg met many times to decide what to do about the dancers.
BODY: Strasbourg was governed by a city council made up of nobles and representatives of the guilds, the organisations of craftsmen and merchants. Historians note that the council was responsible for keeping order, protecting public health and dealing with emergencies. When the dancing spread, the council met to discuss the crisis. Its records show that the members were deeply worried. They consulted physicians, priests and other experts. Even so, they did not know what to do at first. They had never faced anything like it. Some members believed that the dancing was a natural illness. Others believed that it was a punishment from God or the work of a saint. The council made several decisions over the following weeks, some of which seem strange today. They tried different remedies, changing their approach as the crisis continued. Their records give a vivid picture of a city trying to deal with an unexplained emergency.
===
IMG: sunset-strasbourg-rooftops-cathedral-1518 | the steep rooftops and timber-framed houses of Strasbourg at sunset in 1518, the tall Gothic spire of the cathedral rising above them, swifts in the golden sky
HEAD: The dancing plague lasted for about two months, through the hot summer of 1518.
BODY: The dancing in Strasbourg began in mid July 1518 and continued until early September. Historians note that it lasted about two months. During this time, the number of dancers rose and fell. Some chronicles say that as many as four hundred people were affected, though historians are not sure that this number is accurate. Even so, it was clear that the dancing was not just a few people's strange behaviour. It had become a crisis affecting the whole city. Every day, people heard stories of new dancers. Every evening, the bells of the cathedral rang for prayers, and many citizens prayed for the dancers and for the city. As the summer went on, the authorities searched for a cure. The crisis finally came to an end in September, after the dancers were taken on a pilgrimage to a holy shrine in the hills. The story of how the city coped is as remarkable as the dancing itself.
===
IMG: paracelsus-physician-portrait-study-1520s | a Swiss physician with a round face in a dark doctor's robe and cap holding a book in a study filled with glass bottles, herbs and old books around 1525, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft window light
HEAD: The famous physician Paracelsus later wrote that Frau Troffea had started dancing to annoy her husband.
BODY: Paracelsus was a Swiss physician and thinker, born in 1493. His real name was Theophrastus von Hohenheim. He travelled widely across Europe, studying medicine and chemistry, and he often challenged the ideas of older doctors. Historians note that he visited Strasbourg in the 1520s, a few years after the dancing plague, and he wrote about it. He called the illness chorea lasciva, which can be translated as the dance of lust or the wild dance. He described Frau Troffea as the first dancer. According to Paracelsus, she began dancing to annoy her husband, who did not like dancing, and then could not stop. Even so, historians do not take this story too seriously. Paracelsus was writing years later and liked to tell colourful stories. His explanation reflected the attitudes of his time towards women. Yet his writings are important, because they show that the dancing plague was remembered and discussed by educated people.

TOPIC: A free city on the Rhine
===
IMG: strasbourg-city-walls-rhine-river-1500 | the walled city of Strasbourg around 1500 seen from the countryside, stone walls and towers, red roofs and the tall cathedral spire, the river Ill and boats in the foreground, soft morning light
HEAD: In 1518, Strasbourg was a rich and proud free city of the Holy Roman Empire.
BODY: Strasbourg lies near the river Rhine, in the region of Alsace, in what is now eastern France. In 1518, it was part of the Holy Roman Empire, a large group of lands in central Europe ruled by an emperor. Historians note that Strasbourg was a free imperial city. This meant that it did not belong to a local lord or bishop. It was governed by its own citizens and owed loyalty only to the emperor. The city had about twenty thousand inhabitants, making it one of the larger cities in the empire. Even so, it was not only its size that made it important. Strasbourg was a centre of trade, crafts, learning and printing. Merchants from many countries came to its markets. Its location on the Rhine made it a natural meeting place between the German lands and France. The city was proud of its freedom and wealth, which it had defended for centuries.
===
IMG: river-ill-boats-mills-tanneries-strasbourg | the river Ill flowing through Strasbourg in 1518 with wooden boats, water mills and timber-framed tanneries with open lofts for drying hides, workers at the riverbank, soft daylight
HEAD: The river Ill wound through Strasbourg, turning mills and carrying boats loaded with goods.
BODY: Strasbourg was built on an island, surrounded by the branches of the river Ill, which flows into the Rhine nearby. Historians note that the river was essential to the life of the city. It supplied water for drinking, washing and crafts. It turned the wheels of water mills, which ground grain into flour. Boats carried goods along the river to and from the Rhine. Even so, the river also brought problems. It could flood in spring, and its water was often dirty. The quarter where the river divided into several channels was home to millers, tanners and fishermen. Tanners worked hides into leather, using water from the river. Their buildings had open lofts, where hides were hung to dry. Today, this area is known as Petite France and is one of the most beautiful parts of the city. Its timber-framed houses, many built in the sixteenth century, still stand along the water.
===
IMG: guild-hall-craftsmen-meeting-1518 | craftsmen in leather aprons and wool caps meeting in the hall of a guild with a painted coat of arms on the wall, tankards on a long table, in Strasbourg around 1518, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm light
HEAD: Strasbourg's craftsmen and merchants were organised into powerful guilds.
BODY: In Strasbourg, as in many European cities, craftsmen and merchants belonged to organisations called guilds. Each guild represented a particular trade, such as bakers, butchers, weavers, furriers, goldsmiths or boatmen. Historians note that there were about twenty guilds in Strasbourg in 1518. They set rules for their trades, controlled prices and quality, trained apprentices and helped members who were sick or poor. Each guild had its own hall, where members met, ate and celebrated. Even so, the guilds were also political organisations. They elected representatives to the city council and had a strong voice in the government of the city. Guild halls were large buildings, often decorated with coats of arms and paintings. During the dancing plague, some guild halls played a surprising role. The city council arranged for dancers to be taken into them, where they could dance under supervision, sheltered from the summer sun.
===
IMG: printing-press-workshop-strasbourg-1518 | printers working a large wooden printing press in a workshop in Strasbourg around 1518, freshly printed sheets hanging to dry, cases of metal type and an apprentice inking the type, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm light
HEAD: Strasbourg was one of the great centres of printing in Europe.
BODY: Johannes Gutenberg, the inventor of printing with movable type in Europe, lived and worked in Strasbourg in the 1430s and 1440s. It was there that he probably began his first experiments with printing. Historians note that after he returned to Mainz and printed his famous Bible in the 1450s, the new technology spread quickly. By 1518, Strasbourg was one of the most important centres of printing in Europe, with many printing workshops. They printed books, pamphlets, calendars, images and news sheets. Even so, printing was not only about books. It helped spread news and ideas rapidly across Europe. News of strange events, such as floods, comets and illnesses, was printed and sold in the streets. Historians believe that news of the dancing plague may also have spread this way. Printing was changing the way people learned about the world. In the years that followed, it would also help spread the ideas of the Protestant Reformation.
===
IMG: sebastian-brant-scholar-portrait-study | an elderly scholar and city secretary with a long face in a dark fur-lined robe and black cap reading at a desk with books and documents in Strasbourg around 1518, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft window light
HEAD: The writer Sebastian Brant, author of The Ship of Fools, was a city official in Strasbourg in 1518.
BODY: Sebastian Brant was born in Strasbourg in 1457. He studied law and became a professor in Basel. In 1494, he published a book called The Ship of Fools, a long poem that made fun of human foolishness and sins. Historians note that it was one of the most popular books of its time, translated into many languages. In 1500, Brant returned to Strasbourg and became the city secretary, an important official who kept the city's records and advised the council. Even so, he was not only a writer. He was a careful administrator who took part in the council's work. During the dancing plague of 1518, Brant was still the city secretary. Historians believe that he recorded some of the council's decisions about the dancers. His records are among the most reliable sources for the event. Brant died in 1521, three years after the dancing plague, having served his city faithfully for twenty years.
===
IMG: merchants-market-square-strasbourg-goods | merchants and townspeople in clothes of 1518 trading cloth, wine, spices and grain at stalls in a busy market square in Strasbourg, timber-framed houses with painted fronts, warm morning light
HEAD: Merchants from across Europe came to Strasbourg to trade wine, cloth, grain and spices.
BODY: Strasbourg's wealth came largely from trade. The city stood on important routes between northern and southern Europe, and between the German lands and France. Historians note that merchants traded in many goods, including wine from the vineyards of Alsace, cloth, grain, timber, metals and spices from distant lands. Markets were held regularly in the city's squares, and great fairs attracted merchants from far away. Even so, trade depended on peace and good harvests. Wars, bad weather and disease could disrupt it. The wine of Alsace was especially famous. Barrels of wine were shipped down the Rhine to the Netherlands and beyond. The city's merchants grew rich, building fine houses with painted fronts and carved wooden decorations. Many of these houses still stand today. In 1518, however, the city's prosperity did not protect everyone. Many ordinary people lived in poverty, struggling to buy bread at high prices.
===
IMG: holy-roman-emperor-maximilian-portrait | an elderly emperor with a long nose in a rich fur-lined robe and a golden chain of office, holding a sceptre, standing in a castle hall around 1518, warm light
HEAD: In 1518, the Holy Roman Empire was ruled by the ageing Emperor Maximilian the First.
BODY: In 1518, the Holy Roman Emperor was Maximilian the First, of the Habsburg family. He had ruled since 1493 and was nearing the end of his life. Historians note that Maximilian was known as the last knight, because he loved tournaments and the ideals of chivalry. He also worked hard to increase the power of his family through clever marriages. His grandson Charles would later become one of the most powerful rulers in European history. Even so, the empire was not a single country with a strong central government. It was made up of hundreds of territories, including kingdoms, duchies, bishoprics and free cities like Strasbourg. Each had its own rulers and laws. The emperor had limited power over them. Maximilian died in January 1519, a few months after the dancing plague. His death led to a contest for the imperial crown, which his grandson Charles won in the same year.

TOPIC: The great cathedral
===
IMG: strasbourg-cathedral-facade-pink-stone | the tall Gothic facade of Strasbourg cathedral built of pink sandstone with a great rose window and a single towering spire, townspeople of 1518 in the square below, soft golden light
HEAD: Strasbourg's cathedral, built of pink sandstone, was one of the tallest buildings in the world.
BODY: The cathedral of Notre-Dame in Strasbourg is one of the most famous Gothic churches in Europe. Historians note that it was built over several centuries, from 1176 to 1439. It is made of pink sandstone from the Vosges mountains, which gives it a warm reddish colour. Its single spire, completed in 1439, rises to about one hundred and forty-two metres. From 1647 to 1874, it was the tallest building in the world. Even so, the cathedral was meant to have two spires. The second was never built, giving the building its unusual shape. In 1518, the cathedral was the heart of religious life in Strasbourg. People attended mass there, prayed for help and celebrated festivals. During the dancing plague, its bells rang for prayers for the dancers. Its tall spire could be seen from far away, rising above the city and the surrounding plain, a symbol of faith and pride.
===
IMG: cathedral-interior-stained-glass-light | the soaring interior of a Gothic cathedral with tall pillars and brilliant stained glass windows casting coloured light on the stone floor, townspeople of 1518 praying, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a vaulted ceiling
HEAD: Inside the cathedral, stained glass windows filled the great space with coloured light.
BODY: Inside Strasbourg cathedral, tall columns rise towards a high vaulted ceiling. Historians note that the cathedral contains some of the finest stained glass in Europe, made between the twelfth and fourteenth centuries. The windows show scenes from the Bible, the lives of saints and emperors. When the sun shines through them, the stone floor is filled with patches of red, blue, gold and green light. Even so, the cathedral was not a quiet museum in 1518. It was busy with people coming and going, priests saying mass at many altars, pilgrims visiting relics and merchants meeting near the doors. Bells rang throughout the day, marking the hours of prayer. For the people of Strasbourg, the cathedral was a place of comfort and hope. During the dancing plague, families of the dancers came to pray there. They lit candles and asked the saints for help, hoping that their loved ones would be healed.
===
IMG: stonemasons-carving-cathedral-lodge | stonemasons in leather aprons carving pink sandstone blocks with chisels and mallets in a workshop beside a great Gothic cathedral, wooden scaffolding above, warm daylight
HEAD: The cathedral's builders and stonemasons formed one of the most famous workshops in Europe.
BODY: Building a great cathedral required many skilled craftsmen. Stonemasons cut and carved the stone, carpenters built scaffolding and roofs, and glaziers made the stained glass windows. Historians note that the stonemasons of Strasbourg cathedral were organised into a workshop called the lodge, or Bauhütte. It was one of the most respected in the Holy Roman Empire. Its master builders were famous, and they shared their knowledge with masons in other cities. Even so, the work was hard and sometimes dangerous. Masons worked high on the scaffolding, cutting and lifting heavy stones. They used simple tools: chisels, mallets, saws, ropes and wooden cranes worked by men walking inside large wheels. In 1518, the cathedral was finished, but masons still worked on repairs and decorations. The workshop continued for centuries. Today, a foundation still employs stonemasons to care for the cathedral, carving new stones to replace those worn by weather.
===
IMG: cathedral-preacher-pulpit-crowd-1500 | a preacher in a black robe preaching passionately from a carved stone pulpit in a Gothic cathedral to a large crowd of townspeople of 1518, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a vaulted ceiling, soft light
HEAD: A famous preacher had once warned the people of Strasbourg about sin and God's anger.
BODY: From 1478 to 1510, a famous preacher named Johann Geiler von Kaysersberg preached in Strasbourg cathedral. A special stone pulpit was built for him, which can still be seen today. Historians note that Geiler was a powerful speaker who attracted large crowds. He criticised the sins of the rich, the clergy and ordinary people. He warned that God would punish those who did not repent. Even so, he also cared for the poor and the sick, and he called for reforms in the church. His sermons were printed and widely read. Geiler died in 1510, eight years before the dancing plague. Yet his words still echoed in the minds of many people in Strasbourg. Historians believe that his warnings about God's anger and the power of saints helped shape how the people reacted to the dancing plague. Many believed that the dancing was a sign of divine displeasure, a punishment for their sins.
===
IMG: cathedral-bells-tower-ringers-ropes | bell ringers pulling thick ropes in the stone bell chamber of a Gothic cathedral tower, great bronze bells above them, light coming through narrow openings, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling
HEAD: The bells of the cathedral rang throughout the day, marking the hours of prayer and work.
BODY: In a sixteenth century city, the bells of churches were an important part of daily life. They marked the hours, called people to prayer and announced important events. Historians note that Strasbourg cathedral had several great bells, which could be heard throughout the city and beyond its walls. They rang at dawn, at noon and in the evening. They also rang for festivals, deaths and emergencies, such as fires or floods. Even so, bells were not only practical. Many people believed that the sound of church bells could protect them from storms, evil spirits and disease. During the dancing plague, the bells rang for special prayers and processions. Some people believed that the sound would help heal the dancers. The great bells of the cathedral rang out over the timber-framed houses, the market squares and the river, calling the people of Strasbourg to pray for their troubled neighbours.
===
IMG: view-from-cathedral-tower-alsace-plain | a wide view from high on a Gothic cathedral tower over the steep red roofs of Strasbourg in 1518, the river and the green Alsace plain beyond with the blue Vosges mountains in the distance, soft afternoon light
HEAD: From the top of the cathedral, the people of Strasbourg could see across the plain to the Vosges mountains.
BODY: The tower of Strasbourg cathedral had a platform, which could be reached by climbing more than three hundred steps. Historians note that watchmen were stationed there to look out for fires in the city and for approaching enemies. From the platform, the view stretched across the whole of Strasbourg and far beyond. To the east lay the river Rhine and, beyond it, the hills of the Black Forest. To the west lay the plain of Alsace, with its villages, vineyards and fields. On the horizon rose the blue Vosges mountains. Even so, the view also showed the region's troubles. In 1518, the fields of Alsace had suffered from bad weather and poor harvests. Many villages were struggling. At the foot of the Vosges lay the town of Saverne, where there was a shrine to Saint Vitus. It was there that the dancers of Strasbourg would finally be taken, in search of a cure for their strange illness.
===
