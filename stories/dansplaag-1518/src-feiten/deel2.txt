TOPIC: Daily life in Strasbourg, 1518
===
IMG: timber-framed-houses-narrow-street-morning | a narrow cobbled street in Strasbourg in 1518 lined with tall timber-framed houses with overhanging upper floors, townspeople opening shutters and a cart passing, soft morning light
HEAD: Most people in Strasbourg lived in tall, narrow timber-framed houses along crowded streets.
BODY: The houses of Strasbourg in 1518 were mostly built with a wooden frame, filled with clay, straw and plaster. Historians note that the upper floors often stuck out over the street, giving more space inside but making the streets darker. Families usually lived above their shops or workshops. The ground floor was used for work or trade, while the family lived and slept upstairs. Even so, the houses were crowded. Several generations of a family often lived together, along with servants and apprentices. Fire was a constant danger, since the houses were made of wood and heated with open fires. The city had strict rules about fire safety and kept watchmen to look out for flames at night. The streets were narrow and winding, paved with stones in some places and muddy in others. Pigs and chickens sometimes wandered through them. The city was lively, noisy and full of smells.
===
IMG: bakers-oven-bread-loaves-shop-1518 | a baker in a white apron taking round loaves of bread from a brick oven in a small bakery in Strasbourg in 1518, a woman buying bread at the window, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm firelight
HEAD: Bread was the most important food for the people of Strasbourg, and its price mattered greatly.
BODY: For most people in Strasbourg in 1518, bread was the main food. Historians note that ordinary people ate bread with almost every meal, along with porridge, soup, vegetables, cheese and occasionally meat or fish. Bread was made from wheat, rye or a mixture of grains. The price of bread depended on the harvest. In good years, bread was cheap, and families could afford enough to eat. In bad years, the price of grain rose sharply, and many families went hungry. Even so, the city took steps to help. The council controlled the price and weight of bread, to protect people from dishonest bakers. In times of famine, it bought grain and sold it cheaply to the poor. Bakers were members of a guild and had to follow strict rules. They baked bread early each morning, and the smell of fresh loaves filled the streets. For many families, a loaf of bread was the measure of a good or bad day.
===
IMG: women-washing-clothes-river-ill | women in long dresses and white headscarves washing clothes on wooden boards at the edge of the river Ill in Strasbourg in 1518, baskets of linen beside them, timber-framed houses across the water, soft morning light
HEAD: Women did much of the hard daily work, from washing clothes in the river to helping in family workshops.
BODY: In sixteenth century Strasbourg, women's lives were full of hard work. Historians note that most women cared for the home and family, cooking, cleaning, spinning and sewing. They washed clothes in the river or in wooden tubs, beating them on boards to get them clean. Many women also worked in family businesses, selling goods in shops and markets, or helping their husbands in workshops. Some women ran businesses of their own, especially widows. Even so, women had fewer rights than men. They could not hold public office or join most guilds as full members. Their lives were often shaped by the decisions of their fathers and husbands. Historians note that many of the dancers in 1518 seem to have been women, though men also danced. Some historians believe that the hardships and pressures of women's lives at that time may help to explain why so many were affected by the dancing plague.
===
IMG: children-playing-street-hoop-1518 | children in simple clothes of 1518 playing with a wooden hoop and stick and spinning tops in a cobbled street in Strasbourg, a dog running beside them, timber-framed houses, warm afternoon light
HEAD: Children in Strasbourg played in the streets, but many also began work at a young age.
BODY: Children in sixteenth century Strasbourg played games much like children today. Historians note that they played with balls, hoops, spinning tops and dolls, and they ran, chased and played hide and seek in the streets. On holidays, they joined in festivals and processions. Even so, childhood was often short. Many children began working from the age of seven or eight. Boys might become apprentices to craftsmen, learning a trade over several years. Girls usually helped at home or went into service in other households. Some children went to school, where they learned to read, write and do sums, and studied religion and Latin. Strasbourg had several schools, including one at the cathedral. Life was also uncertain. Many children died young from disease or accidents. Families often had many children, knowing that not all would survive. During the hard years before 1518, children suffered especially from hunger and illness.
===
IMG: tavern-evening-wine-music-strasbourg | townspeople of 1518 drinking wine from pottery cups in a lively tavern with wooden beams, a musician playing a fiddle in the corner, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm candlelight
HEAD: In the evenings, people gathered in taverns to drink the wine of Alsace and listen to music.
BODY: Taverns were important gathering places in Strasbourg. Historians note that people went there to drink, eat, talk, play games and listen to music. The wine of Alsace was cheap and plentiful, and it was the most common drink in the region. Beer was also brewed. Musicians played fiddles, pipes, drums and lutes, and people sometimes danced. Even so, the city authorities kept a close watch on the taverns. They set rules about opening hours and behaviour and fined those who caused trouble. Some preachers warned that taverns encouraged sin, drunkenness and wasted money. Dancing was popular at weddings, festivals and celebrations. People danced in circles and lines, holding hands, to the sound of music. In 1518, however, the strange dancing in the streets was nothing like these cheerful dances. It was joyless, uncontrolled and frightening. Soon, the authorities would ban public music and dancing altogether.
===
IMG: city-watchmen-night-gate-lantern | two city watchmen in leather jerkins and helmets with halberds and a lantern standing at a stone city gate at night in Strasbourg in 1518, the heavy wooden gate closed, quiet street behind
HEAD: At night, the gates of Strasbourg were closed, and watchmen walked the streets with lanterns.
BODY: Every evening, the gates of Strasbourg were closed, and no one could enter or leave the city until morning. Historians note that the city was protected by strong walls and towers, guarded by watchmen. Inside the city, other watchmen walked the streets at night. They carried lanterns and weapons and called out the hours. They looked out for fires, thieves and disturbances. Even so, life at night was quiet for most people. There were no streetlights, and people stayed at home after dark. They lit candles or oil lamps and went to bed early. In the summer of 1518, the nights may not have been quiet. Some dancers continued through the night, their feet tapping on the cobbles. Their families and neighbours stayed awake, watching over them. The watchmen reported the dancing to the city's leaders. Night after night, the strange sound of feet on the stones echoed through the dark streets.
===
IMG: vineyards-alsace-villages-harvest | workers harvesting grapes in sunny vineyards on gentle hills in Alsace around 1518, a small village with a church tower and timber-framed houses below, baskets and carts, warm golden light
HEAD: Around Strasbourg, the vineyards and fields of Alsace supplied the city with wine and food.
BODY: Strasbourg depended on the countryside around it for food. Historians note that the plain of Alsace was fertile, with fields of wheat, rye and barley, vegetable gardens, orchards and meadows for animals. On the hills at the foot of the Vosges mountains grew the famous vineyards of Alsace. Peasants worked the land, living in small villages with timber-framed houses and churches. They brought their produce to the markets of Strasbourg. Even so, life in the countryside was hard. Peasants paid heavy rents and taxes to landowners, the church and the authorities. They depended on the weather. A hailstorm or a cold spring could destroy the harvest. In the years before 1518, the peasants of Alsace suffered greatly. Bad harvests and rising prices caused hunger and anger. Some peasants joined secret groups that plotted against their landlords. Seven years later, in 1525, many of them rose up in the great Peasants' War.

TOPIC: Hard years of hunger and sickness
===
IMG: hailstorm-ruined-wheat-fields-peasants | peasants in rough clothes standing sadly in a field of flattened, ruined wheat after a hailstorm in Alsace around 1517, dark storm clouds moving away, grey light
HEAD: In the years before 1518, storms and bad weather ruined the harvests of Alsace.
BODY: The years before the dancing plague were very difficult for the people of Alsace. Historians note that a series of bad harvests struck the region. Cold winters, wet summers and hailstorms damaged crops and vineyards. In 1517, the harvest was especially poor. Grain became scarce, and its price rose sharply. Bread became too expensive for many families. Even so, the crisis was not limited to Alsace. Bad weather affected large parts of Europe in these years. The city of Strasbourg tried to help by buying grain and distributing it to the poor. Yet many people still went hungry. Beggars filled the streets, and the poor crowded into the city looking for food and work. Historians believe that the hunger and suffering of these years played an important part in the dancing plague. People who were weak, hungry and frightened may have been more likely to fall into the strange state that affected the dancers.
===
IMG: poor-people-queue-bread-distribution | poor townspeople and beggars in ragged clothes queuing for bread handed out from a doorway by monks and city officials in Strasbourg around 1517, grey morning light
HEAD: Hungry people from the countryside crowded into Strasbourg, hoping for food and charity.
BODY: When the harvests failed, many poor people from the countryside came to Strasbourg. Historians note that they hoped to find food, work or charity in the city. Monasteries, churches and hospitals gave out bread and soup to the hungry. The city council also organised help for the poor. Even so, there was not enough for everyone. The city became crowded with beggars, and the authorities worried about disease and disorder. They sometimes expelled beggars who were not citizens, sending them back to the countryside. The poor lived in terrible conditions, sleeping in the streets or in crowded shelters. Many were weak from hunger and easily fell ill. Historians note that the suffering of the poor was visible everywhere in Strasbourg in 1517 and 1518. It created an atmosphere of fear and despair. For many people, it seemed as if God had turned against them. The dancing plague came at a time of deep distress.
===
IMG: sick-people-hospital-beds-nuns-1518 | sick people lying in rows of wooden beds in a long hospital hall in Strasbourg around 1518, nuns in white caring for them, a crucifix on the wall and light from tall windows, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling
HEAD: Diseases such as smallpox, leprosy and a new illness called the French disease added to the suffering.
BODY: In addition to hunger, the people of Strasbourg faced many diseases. Historians note that smallpox, leprosy and other illnesses were common. Plague returned from time to time, killing many. A new disease had also appeared in Europe in the 1490s. It was called the French disease or the pox, and today it is known as syphilis. It spread quickly and caused terrible suffering. Even so, the city did what it could. It had a large hospital, run by the city, where the sick and poor were cared for. It also had a house for lepers outside the walls and a special hospital for people with the French disease. Doctors, barber-surgeons and nuns cared for the sick. Yet medicine could do little to cure most diseases. People prayed to saints for protection and healing. The constant presence of disease made people anxious about their health. A strange new illness, such as the dancing plague, was especially frightening.
===
IMG: preacher-warning-crowd-church-steps | a priest in a dark robe preaching with raised hands from the steps of a church to a worried crowd of townspeople in clothes of 1518, the cathedral spire behind, grey overcast light
HEAD: Many people believed that hunger and sickness were punishments sent by God.
BODY: In the early sixteenth century, most people in Europe believed that God controlled everything that happened in the world. Historians note that disasters such as famine, plague and war were often seen as punishments for sin. Preachers warned their listeners to repent and change their ways, or face God's anger. People also believed in the power of saints. Each saint was thought to protect against particular dangers and to be able to send illnesses as punishments. Even so, religion also gave comfort. People prayed, went on pilgrimages and made offerings, hoping to win God's mercy. In Strasbourg in 1518, many people feared that their city was being punished. The hunger, disease and the strange dancing seemed to be signs of divine anger. Historians believe that these fears shaped how people understood the dancing plague. Many believed that it was a curse sent by a particular saint, Saint Vitus.
===
IMG: martin-luther-theses-church-door-1517 | a monk in a dark habit nailing a sheet of paper to the great wooden door of a church in a German town in 1517, a few townspeople watching, soft autumn light
HEAD: In 1517, a German monk named Martin Luther began to challenge the teachings of the Church.
BODY: In October 1517, a German monk named Martin Luther wrote a list of ninety-five arguments, called theses, criticising the sale of indulgences by the Catholic Church. Indulgences were documents that promised to reduce the punishment for sins in exchange for money. Historians note that Luther's ideas spread quickly, helped by the printing press. They led to the Protestant Reformation, which divided the church in Western Europe. Even so, in the summer of 1518, the Reformation had only just begun. Most people in Strasbourg were still devoted Catholics. They prayed to saints, went on pilgrimages and trusted the church to help them. Within a few years, however, Strasbourg would become one of the most important centres of the Reformation. Preachers such as Martin Bucer would lead the city to adopt Protestant teachings. The dancing plague of 1518 happened at a moment just before this great change, when old beliefs were still strong.
===
IMG: anxious-family-praying-candle-home | a poor family in clothes of 1518 kneeling in prayer by candlelight in a small bare room of a timber-framed house, a simple wooden cross on the wall, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm candlelight
HEAD: For many families in Strasbourg, 1518 was a year of fear, worry and prayer.
BODY: Historians who have studied the dancing plague emphasise how difficult life had become for the people of Strasbourg in 1518. Many families had lost relatives to hunger and disease. Prices were high, work was scarce, and debts were growing. Some families had been forced to sell their belongings or beg for food. Even so, people tried to carry on. They worked, cared for their children, went to church and helped their neighbours. Yet anxiety and fear were everywhere. People worried about the next harvest, about illness and about God's anger. Historians believe that this atmosphere of fear and distress created the conditions for the dancing plague. People who were under great stress and believed in the power of saints to curse them may have been especially vulnerable. When Frau Troffea began to dance, others who were suffering may have feared the same fate. And for some of them, that fear seems to have come true.

TOPIC: The dancers multiply
===
IMG: dancers-street-many-people-wild-movement | a group of men and women in clothes of 1518 dancing in a frenzied, uncontrolled way in a cobbled street in Strasbourg, their eyes half closed, onlookers stepping back, hot summer light
HEAD: By the end of July 1518, dozens of people were dancing in the streets of Strasbourg.
BODY: In the days after Frau Troffea began to dance, more people joined her. Historians note that by the end of July, there were dozens of dancers in Strasbourg. They were men and women, rich and poor, young and old, though many seem to have been poor and many were women. Some danced in the streets, others in their homes. They danced for hours and days without rest. Even so, their dancing was not like normal dancing. They moved jerkily and wildly, as if possessed. They cried out, screamed and wept. Some said that they saw visions or felt as if they were burning. Their families were frightened and did not know how to help them. Neighbours watched in fear and confusion. The city's leaders were alarmed. Every day, they heard reports of new dancers. Historians believe that the number of dancers grew quickly because people saw others dancing and feared that they too would be affected.
===
IMG: dancer-family-holding-man-still-home | a worried family in clothes of 1518 trying to hold still a young man who is dancing helplessly in a small room of a timber-framed house, a woman crying, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm light
HEAD: Families tried to hold the dancers still, but they could not stop them.
BODY: When a member of the family began to dance, relatives tried everything they could to help. Historians note that some tried to hold the dancers still, tying them up or locking them in rooms. Others prayed over them or sprinkled them with holy water. Some took them to priests or doctors. Even so, nothing seemed to work. The dancers struggled to break free and continue dancing. They seemed to be in a trance, unaware of their surroundings or their pain. Their families were exhausted and heartbroken. They had to care for the dancers, feeding them and tending their wounds, while also continuing their own work. The dancing affected whole households. Some families feared that their own members would be next. Historians note that the sources describe the dancers' families with sympathy. They show how a strange illness could bring suffering to a whole community, not only to those who were directly affected.
===
IMG: sun-hot-summer-street-dancers-collapsing | dancers in clothes of 1518 collapsing from exhaustion in a hot sunlit square in Strasbourg, some lying on the stones while others continue dancing, townspeople bringing water in jugs, harsh bright summer light
HEAD: In the summer heat, the dancers suffered from exhaustion and thirst, and some collapsed.
BODY: The summer of 1518 was hot, and the dancing took place in the heat of July and August. Historians note that the dancers suffered terribly. They sweated heavily, lost water and became dehydrated. Their muscles ached, their feet were bruised and bleeding, and their hearts were strained. Many collapsed from exhaustion. Even so, after resting for a while, most rose and began to dance again. Some later chronicles claim that many dancers died, perhaps as many as fifteen a day at the peak of the crisis. Historians are cautious about this number. The council records from 1518 do not mention deaths on this scale. Yet it is likely that some dancers, especially the weak and the elderly, did die from exhaustion, heart attacks or strokes. The dancing was not a harmless curiosity. It was a real and dangerous illness, causing great suffering to those affected and fear among those who watched.
===
IMG: townspeople-whispering-fear-market | groups of townspeople in clothes of 1518 whispering anxiously together in a market square in Strasbourg, some looking towards a distant dancer, a woman clutching a child, overcast light
HEAD: As the dancing spread, rumours and fear spread through the city as well.
BODY: As the number of dancers grew, the people of Strasbourg became more and more frightened. Historians note that rumours spread quickly through the city. Some said that the dancers were possessed by demons. Others said that they had been cursed by a saint. Some feared that the illness was contagious, like the plague, and that anyone could catch it. Even so, most people tried to help rather than run away. Neighbours brought food and water, priests prayed with the dancers, and doctors tried to understand their illness. Yet the atmosphere in the city was tense. People watched each other for signs of the dancing. They avoided places where the dancers gathered. Some prayed constantly, hoping to be protected. Historians believe that this fear may have made the dancing worse. Some people who were worried that they would begin to dance may have done so, simply because they expected it. The fear itself seems to have spread the illness.
===
IMG: horse-market-square-crowd-strasbourg | a large open square used as a horse market in Strasbourg in 1518, horses and traders at one side, a crowd gathered around a group of dancers in the middle, timber-framed houses around, hot summer light
HEAD: Many of the dancers gathered in the city's horse market and the grain market.
BODY: As the dancing spread, the dancers began to gather in certain places in the city. Historians note that the council records mention the horse market and the grain market, two large open spaces where crowds could gather. There, the dancers danced in groups, watched by crowds of onlookers. Even so, the gatherings created new problems. Crowds attracted thieves, beggars and troublemakers. Some people came to mock the dancers. Others came out of curiosity or pity. The authorities worried about disorder and about the spread of the illness. They also worried about the dancers' safety, since they could be trampled or injured. The council began to think about how to control the dancing. Its members wanted to keep the dancers safe and to stop the illness from spreading further. Their first plan, as historians note, was a surprising one. They decided that the dancers should be allowed to dance even more, in organised places.
===
IMG: procession-priests-banners-street-dancers | a religious procession of priests in white robes carrying a cross, banners and candles through a street in Strasbourg in 1518, townspeople kneeling and a few dancers watching, soft light
HEAD: Priests led processions and prayers through the streets, asking God to end the dancing.
BODY: As the dancing continued, the church tried to help. Historians note that priests led processions through the streets of Strasbourg, carrying crosses, banners, relics and candles. People followed them, singing hymns and praying for the dancers. Special masses were said in the cathedral and other churches. Even so, the prayers did not seem to stop the dancing. The number of dancers continued to grow. Some priests tried to drive out demons from the dancers through exorcism, a ritual of prayers and commands. Others believed that the dancing was a punishment from a saint, and that only that saint could cure it. The church and the city council worked together to find a solution. Their efforts show how seriously they took the dancing plague. It was not seen as a joke or as foolish behaviour. It was seen as a real crisis, which required both medical and spiritual remedies.
===
IMG: night-dancers-torchlight-street | a few dancers in clothes of 1518 still dancing in a dark narrow street at night, lit by torches held by worried neighbours, long shadows on the timber-framed walls
HEAD: Some dancers continued even through the night, by the light of torches and lanterns.
BODY: For some dancers, the dancing did not stop at night. Historians note that witnesses described dancers continuing through the dark hours, when the city was supposed to be quiet. Their families and neighbours held torches and lanterns to light the streets and keep them safe. Even so, many people found these night scenes especially frightening. In the darkness, the dancers' shadows leapt on the walls of the houses. The sound of their feet and their cries echoed through the empty streets. People inside their homes heard them and lay awake, worried and afraid. The night watchmen reported the dancing to the authorities. Historians note that the dancers may have been unable to sleep, or that they simply could not stop their movements. Exhaustion eventually forced them to rest, but only for short periods. As July turned into August, the dancing continued day and night, and the city's leaders became desperate to find a cure.

TOPIC: The doctors and the council
===
IMG: physicians-meeting-books-skull-study | several physicians in long dark robes and caps discussing a case around a table with medical books, a skull and glass bottles in a study in Strasbourg in 1518, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft window light
HEAD: The council asked the city's physicians what was causing the dancing, and they blamed hot blood.
BODY: When the dancing spread, the city council asked the local physicians for their advice. Historians note that the physicians examined the dancers and discussed the illness. They ruled out supernatural causes, such as demons or curses. Instead, they said that it was a natural illness caused by hot blood. According to the medical ideas of the time, the human body contained four fluids, called humours: blood, phlegm, yellow bile and black bile. Health depended on keeping them in balance. Even so, the physicians' advice was unusual. They did not recommend bloodletting, the common treatment for too much blood. Instead, they suggested that the dancers should be allowed to dance until the fever left them. The physicians believed that the dancing would burn off the excess heat. This idea seems strange today, but it made sense within the medical theories of the time. The council accepted their advice and began to plan accordingly.
===
IMG: four-humours-medical-book-illustration | an old open medical book showing a hand-coloured illustration of four figures representing the four humours, on a wooden desk beside a candle and glass bottles of herbs, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm candlelight
HEAD: Medicine in 1518 was based on the ancient idea of the four humours of the body.
BODY: In the sixteenth century, European medicine was based on ideas that went back to ancient Greece. Historians note that the Greek physician Hippocrates and the Roman physician Galen had taught that the body contained four humours. Each humour was connected with an element, a season and a type of personality. Blood was hot and wet, like air and spring. Yellow bile was hot and dry, like fire and summer. Black bile was cold and dry, like earth and autumn. Phlegm was cold and wet, like water and winter. Even so, these ideas were not foolish. They were based on careful observation and reasoning, and they guided doctors for over a thousand years. Illness was thought to be caused by an imbalance of humours. Doctors tried to restore balance with diet, rest, herbs, bloodletting and other treatments. In 1518, the physicians of Strasbourg believed that the summer heat had overheated the dancers' blood.
===
IMG: barber-surgeon-shop-tools-1518 | a barber-surgeon in an apron at work in a small shop in Strasbourg in 1518 with basins, razors, jars of ointment and a patient seated in a chair, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft window light
HEAD: Most ordinary people were cared for by barber-surgeons, healers and herbalists, not by university doctors.
BODY: In 1518, there were several kinds of medical practitioners in Strasbourg. Historians note that university-trained physicians were the most respected, but they were few and expensive. Most ordinary people were cared for by barber-surgeons, who cut hair, pulled teeth, set bones, treated wounds and let blood. Others went to apothecaries, who made and sold medicines, or to herbalists, midwives and folk healers. Even so, each group had its own skills. Barber-surgeons were especially skilled at practical treatments. They belonged to a guild and trained as apprentices. One of the most famous surgeons of Strasbourg, Hieronymus Brunschwig, who had died a few years earlier, wrote a book on surgery and another on distilling medicines. During the dancing plague, barber-surgeons may have treated the dancers' injuries, such as bleeding feet and bruises. Apothecaries may have provided medicines to calm or strengthen them. The care of the dancers involved many people across the city.
===
IMG: council-decree-proclamation-town-crier | a town crier in a colourful tabard reading a proclamation from a scroll to a crowd of townspeople in a square in Strasbourg in 1518, a guard beside him, warm daylight
HEAD: The council announced its decisions to the people of the city through town criers.
BODY: In 1518, most people in Strasbourg could not read. Historians note that the city council announced its decisions through town criers, who read proclamations aloud in squares and markets. They rang a bell or blew a horn to attract attention and then read the council's orders. Even so, important decisions were also written down and posted on church doors and public buildings. During the dancing plague, the council issued several proclamations. Some told people how to care for the dancers. Others set rules about music, dancing and public gatherings. People gathered around the town criers to hear the latest news. They discussed the council's decisions in the streets, taverns and markets. Historians note that the council's records preserve the texts of some of these decisions. They show how the authorities tried to manage the crisis step by step. The council was trying to protect the city, but its decisions sometimes changed as the situation developed.
===
IMG: council-members-debating-papers-1518 | council members in fur-trimmed robes debating anxiously around a table covered with papers and a city seal in a panelled hall in Strasbourg in 1518, one standing and gesturing, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft window light
HEAD: Some council members believed the dancing was a sickness, while others saw it as a punishment from heaven.
BODY: The members of Strasbourg's council did not all agree about the cause of the dancing. Historians note that some accepted the physicians' explanation that it was a natural illness. Others believed that it was a punishment from God or a curse from a saint. Some may have suspected that the dancers were pretending, or that they were drunk. Even so, they agreed that something had to be done. The council was responsible for the health and safety of the city. It could not ignore a crisis that was affecting hundreds of people. Its members met often and discussed different remedies. They consulted physicians, priests and other experts. They tried to balance medical advice with religious beliefs. Historians note that their decisions show a mix of practical and spiritual approaches. They tried medicine, music, prayer and pilgrimage. Some of their decisions seem strange today, but they were trying their best in a situation that no one fully understood.
===
IMG: sebastian-brant-writing-council-record | an elderly city secretary in a black robe writing a record in a large leather-bound book at a desk in a council chamber in Strasbourg in 1518, other officials in the background, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft window light
HEAD: The council's records give an unusual and detailed picture of how a city faced an unknown illness.
BODY: The council records of Strasbourg are one of the most important sources for the dancing plague. Historians note that they were written by the city's officials at the time, and they are more reliable than chronicles written years later. They record the council's decisions, such as hiring musicians, opening guild halls, banning music and sending dancers to the shrine of Saint Vitus. Even so, the records are brief and practical. They do not describe the dancers' feelings or experiences. They do not explain the council's reasons in detail. Historians have had to piece together the story from these records and from other sources. The records are kept today in the archives of Strasbourg. They show that the dancing plague was a real event, taken seriously by the authorities. They also show how a sixteenth century city government worked, responding to a crisis with the knowledge and beliefs of its time.

TOPIC: Music to cure the dance
===
IMG: guild-hall-dancers-musicians-inside | people in clothes of 1518 dancing wildly inside a large wooden guild hall while musicians play fiddles, pipes and drums on a raised platform, onlookers along the walls, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm light
HEAD: Following the doctors' advice, the council opened guild halls where the dancers could dance under supervision.
BODY: Based on the physicians' advice, the council decided that the dancers should be allowed to dance until they were cured. Historians note that it opened two guild halls for the dancers. There, they could dance under supervision, sheltered from the sun and away from the crowds in the streets. Even so, the council went further. It also cleared space in the horse market and the grain market for dancing. The idea was to help the dancers burn off their fever as quickly as possible. Historians note that this was not as strange as it sounds. In the medical theories of the time, sweating and exercise were thought to help drive out illness. Yet the plan had an unexpected result. Instead of curing the dancers, it seems to have encouraged more people to dance. The public spectacle may have helped spread the illness further.
===
IMG: musicians-fiddles-pipes-drums-platform | a group of musicians in clothes of 1518 playing fiddles, bagpipes, pipes and drums on a wooden platform in a market square in Strasbourg, dancers moving below, warm afternoon light
HEAD: The council hired musicians to play for the dancers, believing that music would help them dance the illness away.
BODY: To help the dancers, the council hired musicians to play for them. Historians note that the records mention fiddlers, pipers and drummers, who were paid to play day and night. The music was meant to help the dancers keep moving and to make the dancing more orderly. Even so, some historians believe that the music made the dancing worse. It attracted crowds and created a festive atmosphere. People who were already anxious and suggestible may have been drawn into the dancing by the sound of the music. The music also made the dancing more visible and dramatic. Historians note that musicians were a common sight in sixteenth century towns. They played at weddings, festivals and in taverns. Yet playing for the dancers of 1518 was a strange job. The musicians played for hours on end, watching the dancers suffer, unable to stop them. Some may have wondered whether their music was helping or harming.

HEAD: The council even built a wooden stage in the market, so that the dancers could be seen and helped.
BODY: In addition to opening the guild halls, the council ordered a wooden stage to be built in the horse market. Historians note that the stage allowed the dancers to dance in an organised space, where they could be watched and cared for. Carpenters built it quickly, using planks and beams. Even so, the stage also turned the dancing into a public spectacle. Crowds gathered to watch, and the dancers became the centre of attention. The council hired strong men to hold the dancers up when they became too tired to stand, keeping them moving. This was meant to help them sweat out their illness. Historians believe that this decision may have made things worse. The public display of dancing may have encouraged more people to join, especially those who feared that they too might be affected. Within a few weeks, the council realised that its approach was not working and changed its plans.

HEAD: Strong men were hired to hold up the dancers and keep them moving when they grew tired.
BODY: According to the council records, strong men were paid to support the dancers. Historians note that their job was to hold the dancers up when they became too exhausted to stand, keeping them moving to the music. The idea was that the dancers needed to continue dancing until their fever was gone. Even so, the work was exhausting for the helpers as well. They had to hold up people who were sweating, struggling and sometimes crying out in pain. They worked in shifts, through the heat of the day and the cool of the night. Historians imagine that some of the helpers felt sympathy for the dancers. Others may have been frightened that they would be affected too. The scene must have been extraordinary: musicians playing, dancers whirling, strong men holding them up and crowds watching. It was one of the strangest attempts at a cure in the history of medicine.

HEAD: When the music did not help, the council banned public music and dancing in the city.
BODY: By early August 1518, the council realised that its plan was not working. The number of dancers was still growing, and the public dancing seemed to be making things worse. Historians note that the council changed its approach completely. It banned public music and dancing in Strasbourg. The guild halls were closed to the dancers, and the stage was removed. Even so, the ban was not total. Music was still allowed at weddings and religious celebrations, but only with string instruments, such as fiddles and lutes. Drums and tambourines, which were thought to excite people, were forbidden. Historians note that the council also ordered that people found dancing in public should be fined. The ban was a sign that the council's thinking had changed. Its members now believed that the dancing was not a natural illness that could be cured by more dancing. Instead, they began to see it as a punishment from heaven.

HEAD: The council now turned to religion, believing that only Saint Vitus could end the dancing.
BODY: When the medical approach failed, the council turned to religion for help. Historians note that its members and many people in Strasbourg believed that the dancing was a curse sent by Saint Vitus, a saint who was thought to have the power to make people dance. According to popular belief, Saint Vitus could punish sinners by sending them a dancing illness. He could also cure it, if people prayed to him and asked for forgiveness. Even so, the council did not abandon practical measures. It continued to care for the dancers and to keep order in the city. But it now focused on spiritual remedies. It ordered masses to be said and prayers to be offered. It also banned gambling, swearing and other sinful activities, hoping to win back God's favour. Finally, it decided to send the dancers on a pilgrimage to a shrine of Saint Vitus in the hills near the town of Saverne.
