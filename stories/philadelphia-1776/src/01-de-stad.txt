Tonight we are going two hundred and fifty years back, to a busy brick city on the banks of a wide river, in the summer of 1776.

--

It is early morning, and a light mist is lying on the Delaware River.

Out on the water, the tall wooden ships stand at anchor, their sails furled, their masts like a little forest against the pale sky. Small boats are already moving between them, rowed by men in shirtsleeves, carrying barrels and sacks to the wharves. Gulls drift and call above the water, and the tide slaps gently against the wooden piers.

If you enjoy quiet journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly invited to subscribe. And now, let us begin.

--

Beyond the wharves, the city of Philadelphia climbs gently up from the river.

From the water, it is a fine sight: a long line of red brick warehouses and houses along the shore, and behind them, row after row of brick buildings with white window frames and steep roofs, and here and there, a church steeple rising above the rooftops. The tallest of all is the white wooden spire of Christ Church, with its little weathervane glinting in the first light of the sun.

{img:christ-church-steeple-brick-rooftops-morning}
Further back, on the edge of the city, there is another tower, belonging to a large and handsome brick building with a clock on its side. That building is called the State House. It is where the government of Pennsylvania meets. And in the summer of 1776, it is also where a group of men from thirteen colonies are meeting, to decide the future of a continent.

--

Philadelphia is the largest city in British America.

About thirty thousand people live here, perhaps a few more. It is bigger than New York, bigger than Boston, and one of the largest cities in the whole British Empire, after London itself. And it is still growing, every year.

It was founded less than a hundred years ago, in 1682, by an Englishman named William Penn. Penn was a Quaker, a member of a peaceful Christian society that believes in simplicity and honesty and plain speech, and that does not believe in war. He wanted his city to be a place where people of every faith could live together in peace. He called it Philadelphia, which means brotherly love, in Greek.

--

{img:william-penn-grid-plan-old-map-desk}
Penn planned the city carefully, before a single house was built.

He laid it out between two rivers, the Delaware on the east and the Schuylkill on the west, in a neat grid of straight streets crossing each other at right angles, like the lines on a sheet of paper. The streets running east and west were named after trees that grew in the woods of Pennsylvania: Chestnut, Walnut, Spruce, Pine, Mulberry, Sassafras, Vine. The streets running north and south were simply numbered: Front Street by the river, then Second Street, Third Street, Fourth, and so on, out toward the west.

He planned five green squares, one in the middle and one in each quarter of the city, where people could walk and rest under the trees.

--

In 1776, the city has not yet reached the Schuylkill.

Most of the houses are crowded together near the Delaware, along the first eight or nine streets from the river. Beyond that, there are fields and orchards, gardens and brickyards, a few big country houses, and then the woods and farms of Pennsylvania, rolling away to the west.

{img:brick-row-houses-street-trees-1776}
The streets near the river are paved with round stones, and have brick pavements on either side, with wooden posts along the edge to keep the carts and carriages away from the people walking. There are street lamps, too, with whale oil burning in glass globes, which are lit every evening by the lamplighters. Visitors from other cities are always impressed by them.

--

The houses are mostly built of red brick, two or three storeys high, standing side by side in long rows, with doors opening straight onto the pavement.

Many of them have white marble steps at the front door, which the servants or the daughters of the house scrub every morning until they shine. Some have a little wooden bench beside the door, where the family can sit on summer evenings and watch the street.

At the back of each house, there is a narrow garden or yard, with a well or a pump, a little kitchen building, a woodshed, a privy, and perhaps a vegetable bed and a fruit tree.

--

{img:marble-steps-girl-scrubbing-morning}
On this early summer morning, the city is waking up.

The bakers have been up for hours, and the smell of fresh bread is drifting along the streets. The milkmaids are going from door to door with their pails. Carts are rumbling over the cobblestones toward the market, piled high with vegetables and fruit from the farms across the river in New Jersey.

Somewhere, a church bell is ringing. A dog barks. A shopkeeper takes down the wooden shutters from his window, and a boy sweeps the pavement in front of the door.

--

It is the beginning of a summer that the people of Philadelphia will remember for the rest of their lives.

For more than a year now, there has been fighting between the American colonies and the soldiers of the King of Britain. It began in April 1775, at the little towns of Lexington and Concord, in Massachusetts, far to the north. Since then, the colonies have raised an army of their own, under a tall, quiet Virginian named George Washington.

{img:state-house-tower-summer-trees-1776}
But most people in Philadelphia, as in the other colonies, still think of themselves as British. They still pray for the King in church on Sundays. And many of them still hope that, somehow, the quarrel can be mended.

In a few weeks, in the brick State House at the end of Chestnut Street, all of that will change.
