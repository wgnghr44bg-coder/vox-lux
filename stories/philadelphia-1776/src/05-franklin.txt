In the summer of 1776, Benjamin Franklin is seventy years old.

He is a stout, comfortable-looking old man, with long, thin grey hair falling to his shoulders, a broad, kindly face, and small spectacles perched on his nose. He walks slowly, with a stick, because his legs are troubled by gout. He dresses plainly, in a simple brown suit, without lace or ornaments.

--

He has lived an extraordinary life.

After he became a successful printer, he did more for Philadelphia than almost anyone else in its history. He helped to found the first lending library in America, so that ordinary people could borrow books. He started the city's first volunteer fire company, so that neighbours would help each other put out fires. He helped to found a hospital, and an academy which later became a university, and a society for scientists and thinkers.

He arranged for the streets to be paved and swept and lit at night. He reorganized the post office, so that letters travelled faster between the colonies. He invented a new kind of stove, which heated a room better and used less wood, and he refused to take out a patent on it, because he wanted everyone to be able to use it freely.

--

And he became one of the greatest scientists of his age.

He was fascinated by electricity, which in those days was a mysterious force that nobody understood. He did many experiments, in his house and his garden. And in 1752, it is said, he flew a kite, made of a silk handkerchief, into a thunderstorm, with a metal key tied to the string, to show that lightning was a kind of electricity.

{img:kite-silk-thunderclouds-field-distant}
From this, he invented the lightning rod, a pointed metal rod fixed to the top of a building, with a wire running down to the ground, so that lightning would pass harmlessly into the earth. Soon, there were lightning rods on houses and churches all over America and Europe, and Franklin was famous everywhere.

--

He has spent much of the last twenty years in London, representing the American colonies to the British government.

He loved London, and had many friends there. For many years, he tried very hard to keep the colonies and Britain together, and to find a peaceful way to settle their quarrels. But in the end, he failed. The British government would not listen, and some of its ministers treated him with great rudeness.

{img:sailing-ship-atlantic-old-man-deck}
In the spring of 1775, he sailed home to Philadelphia, sad and disappointed. On the voyage, which took six weeks, he spent his time measuring the temperature of the sea every day, to study the great warm current that flows across the Atlantic, which he called the Gulf Stream.

When he arrived home, he learned that the fighting at Lexington and Concord had already begun.

--

Franklin lives in a large brick house, which he built for himself and his wife, in a quiet courtyard set back from Market Street, between Third and Fourth Streets.

His wife, Deborah, the girl who laughed at him when he first walked up Market Street as a young runaway, and who later married him, died two years ago, while he was in London. He misses her very much. His daughter, Sally, lives with him now, with her husband and their children, and the house is full of grandchildren.

{img:franklin-courtyard-house-grandchildren-garden}
In the garden, there is a mulberry tree, and in the shade under its branches, Franklin likes to sit on summer afternoons with his friends, drinking tea, talking about science and politics, and telling stories.

--

He is a wonderful storyteller.

He has a dry, gentle sense of humour, and he likes to make his points with little jokes and fables. Many of his sayings, from the almanac he used to publish, are known to everyone in the colonies. Early to bed and early to rise makes a man healthy, wealthy and wise. A penny saved is a penny earned. Well done is better than well said.

People in the city love him. When he walks slowly down the street with his stick, people take off their hats and greet him, and children run up to him, and he stops to talk to everyone.

--

Since he came home, Franklin has been very busy.

He was chosen as one of the delegates from Pennsylvania to the Continental Congress, the meeting of representatives from all thirteen colonies, which meets in the State House. He sits on many of its committees. He has helped to organize the defence of the city, and the printing of paper money, and the post office of the new country.

He is the oldest man in the Congress, and the most famous. The younger delegates look up to him, and listen when he speaks, which is not very often. He prefers to listen, and to say a few wise words at the right moment.

--

Once, Franklin was proud to be British.

He loved the King, and the English countryside, and his friends in London. But now he has made up his mind. The colonies must be free and independent. There is no other way.

{img:franklin-walking-street-stick-evening}
In the evenings, after the long days in the Congress, he walks home slowly through the warm streets, past the market sheds and the shops, nodding to the people he meets, and goes into his courtyard, and sits under the mulberry tree with his grandchildren, while the swallows wheel overhead, and the bells of Christ Church ring out across the rooftops.
