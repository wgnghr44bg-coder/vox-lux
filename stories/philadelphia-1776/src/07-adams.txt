John Adams is forty years old in the summer of 1776.

He is short and rather stout, with a round face, a high forehead, bright, sharp eyes, and thin hair which he powders and ties back. He is a lawyer from the little town of Braintree, near Boston, and he is one of the cleverest, hardest-working, and most outspoken men in the whole Congress.

--

He is also, by his own admission, rather difficult.

He is proud and touchy, quick to take offence, and quick to say exactly what he thinks, which does not always make him popular. He worries a great deal about what other people think of him. He writes in his diary that he talks too much, and that he must learn to be quieter. But he never does.

In the Congress, he is a tireless worker. He sits on dozens of committees, and works from early morning until late at night. And for many months now, he has been arguing, louder and longer than anyone, that the colonies must declare their independence from Britain.

--

Far away in Massachusetts, on the family farm in Braintree, lives his wife, Abigail.

She is thirty-one years old, a small, slim woman with dark hair and dark, intelligent eyes. She never went to school, because girls in her time rarely did. But she taught herself, from the books in her father's library, and she reads everything: history and poetry, philosophy and politics, newspapers and sermons.

{img:abigail-adams-farmhouse-window-writing}
She is as clever as her husband, and just as outspoken. And while John is away in Philadelphia, she runs the farm and the household alone, and looks after their four children: Abigail, whom they call Nabby, John Quincy, Charles and Thomas.

--

John and Abigail write to each other constantly.

Their letters take a week or two to travel between Philadelphia and Braintree, carried by riders and stagecoaches along the roads through New Jersey and New York and Connecticut. But they write often, long letters, full of news and love and ideas, and they keep every one.

{img:letters-bundle-ribbon-wooden-box}
Over their lives, they will write more than a thousand letters to each other. And because they kept them, we can still read them today, and hear their voices, as if they were in the room.

--

In their letters, they talk about everything.

John writes about the Congress, the debates and the arguments, the other delegates, the heat and the flies, the food at the City Tavern, his loneliness and his worries. He asks about the children, and the farm, and the weather in Braintree, and the price of things. He gives advice about the planting and the animals.

Abigail writes about the children, and the household, and the farm. She writes about an outbreak of smallpox in Boston, and how she has taken the children into the city to be inoculated against it, which was brave and new and dangerous in those days. She writes about the war, which has come very close to her home. From a hill near the farm, she has watched the smoke of the battles around Boston.

--

In one of her letters, in the spring of 1776, Abigail writes something that has been remembered ever since.

{img:woman-quill-letter-candlelight-farmhouse}
She asks her husband, in the new laws that the Congress will make for an independent country, to remember the ladies, and to be more generous and favourable to them than their ancestors were. Do not put such unlimited power into the hands of the husbands, she writes. Remember, all men would be tyrants if they could.

John writes back, half joking, half serious. But Abigail's words are not forgotten.

--

John misses his family terribly.

He writes to Abigail that he longs for home, for the farm, for the children, for her. He writes that he would rather be a farmer in Braintree than a great man in Philadelphia. He dreams of the day when the war will be over, and he can go home for good.

But he does not go home. He believes that what is happening in Philadelphia is too important. He believes that he and the other delegates are making a new country, and that it is the most important thing he will ever do.

--

In the evenings, in his rented room in a boarding house, John sits at the table by the window, with a candle and a quill pen, and writes.

{img:quiet-city-street-evening-lamps-1776}
Outside, the streets of Philadelphia are growing dark. The lamplighters are going by with their ladders. The watchman is calling out the hour and the weather. The bells of Christ Church ring for nine o'clock. A carriage rattles past on the cobbles.

John writes to Abigail about the day, and about his hopes, and about the great decision that the Congress must soon make. Then he signs his name, and folds the letter, and seals it with a drop of wax, ready to be sent north in the morning.

--

Among the other delegates, John Adams has made a friend.

He is a tall, quiet, red-haired young man from Virginia, seven years younger than Adams, who hardly ever speaks in the Congress, but who writes beautifully. Adams likes him very much, and admires him.

His name is Thomas Jefferson.
