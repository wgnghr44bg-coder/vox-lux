On the evening of the ninth of July, in New York, General Washington orders his soldiers to gather on the common, in their regiments, to hear the Declaration read aloud.

They stand in long lines in the evening light, thousands of young men in their mismatched uniforms and hunting shirts, with their muskets on their shoulders, while officers read the words to them. When the reading is over, the soldiers cheer.

--

That same evening, a crowd of soldiers and townspeople goes down to the Bowling Green, a little park at the southern tip of Manhattan.

In the middle of the park, on a tall marble pedestal, there stands a great gilded statue of King George the Third, on horseback, dressed as a Roman emperor. The crowd throws ropes over it, and pulls it down. Later, the lead from the statue will be melted down and made into musket balls for the American army.

{img:gilded-equestrian-statue-park-evening-crowd}
In the days and weeks that follow, the Declaration is read aloud in towns and villages all over the new country: from the steps of courthouses and churches, in market squares and on village greens, to crowds of farmers and craftsmen and their families. Bells are rung, cannons fired, toasts drunk. In some places, people hold mock funerals for the King.

--

In Philadelphia, on the nineteenth of July, the Congress orders that the Declaration be written out beautifully by hand, on a single large sheet of parchment, so that it can be signed by all the delegates.

The work is given to a clerk named Timothy Matlack, who has a fine, clear handwriting. He writes it out carefully, over several days, with a quill pen, in a large, elegant script, on a sheet of parchment about two and a half feet wide.

At the top, in great flowing letters, he writes: The unanimous Declaration of the thirteen united States of America.

--

On the second of August, 1776, the delegates gather in the Assembly Room to sign it.

The parchment is laid out on a table at the front of the room. One by one, the delegates come forward, dip the quill into the silver inkstand, and sign their names, grouped by their colonies, from north to south.

John Hancock signs first, in the middle, in large, bold, flowing letters, much bigger than anyone else's. There is a story that he said he wrote it large so that King George could read it without his spectacles. Nobody knows if he really said it. But ever since, in America, to put your John Hancock on a paper means to sign your name.

{img:silver-inkstand-quill-parchment-signatures}
Then the others sign, below him and around him: John Adams and Samuel Adams, Benjamin Franklin, Thomas Jefferson, Caesar Rodney, Roger Sherman, and the rest. Some of the delegates are not there that day, and sign later. In the end, fifty-six men put their names to the Declaration.

--

They know that it is a dangerous thing to do.

In the eyes of the British government, what they are doing is treason, and the punishment for treason is death. If the war is lost, every man who signed this paper may be arrested and hanged.

{img:delegates-signing-solemn-candlelit-room}
There is a story that as they signed, Benjamin Franklin said, with a dry smile: We must all hang together, or most assuredly we shall all hang separately. Like many stories about Franklin, nobody knows for sure if he said it. But it sounds very much like him.

--

The war that follows is long and hard.

It lasts for more than seven years. In the autumn of 1776, the American army is driven out of New York, and retreats across New Jersey, cold and hungry and beaten. On Christmas night, General Washington leads his men across the icy Delaware River, in small boats, in a snowstorm, and surprises the enemy at the town of Trenton, and wins a small victory that gives everyone new hope.

In September 1777, the British army captures Philadelphia itself. The Congress flees to the countryside. For nine months, British officers live in the fine houses of the city, and hold balls and plays, while Washington's army spends a bitter winter in log huts at a place called Valley Forge, about twenty miles away.

--

During those months, Elizabeth Drinker's husband Henry is sent away from Philadelphia, with other Quakers, because the Americans suspect that they are loyal to the King. Elizabeth stays at home with the children, and a British officer is lodged in her house.

She writes about it all in her diary, every day: the soldiers in the streets, the high prices, the cold, her worry for her husband. In the spring, she travels with some other Quaker women to Valley Forge, to ask General Washington to let their husbands come home. Washington receives them politely, and Martha Washington gives them dinner. Soon afterwards, the men are released.

--

{img:quaker-women-carriage-winter-road-camp}
In the summer of 1778, the British leave Philadelphia, and the Congress comes back.

In 1778, France joins the war on the side of the Americans, thanks in large part to Benjamin Franklin, who has gone to Paris, where he is adored by everyone, and wears a fur cap, and is visited by philosophers and scientists and beautiful ladies.

And in October 1781, at a little town called Yorktown, in Virginia, the American and French armies surround the main British army, and it surrenders.

--

The war is over. The United States of America is free.

In 1787, delegates from the states meet again in the same Assembly Room in the State House, to write a constitution for the new country. Benjamin Franklin, now eighty-one, is among them. George Washington sits in the president's chair at the front, the same chair that John Hancock once sat in.

{img:constitution-convention-room-elderly-franklin}
On the back of that chair, there is a carving of half a sun, sinking or rising on the horizon. On the last day, as the delegates are signing, Franklin points to it. He says that during all the long months of the meeting, he had often looked at that sun, and wondered whether it was rising or setting.

But now, at length, he says, I have the happiness to know that it is a rising, and not a setting sun.
