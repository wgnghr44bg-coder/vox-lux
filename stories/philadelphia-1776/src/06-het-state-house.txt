At the western end of Chestnut Street, between Fifth and Sixth, stands the State House.

It is the grandest building in Philadelphia, a long, handsome building of red brick, two storeys high, with tall windows with white frames, and on the roof, a little white wooden cupola. At the back, there is a tall brick tower, with a wooden steeple on top, and inside the steeple hangs a great bronze bell.

--

The bell was made for the State House about twenty-five years ago.

Around its rim, there are words from the Bible: Proclaim Liberty throughout all the Land unto all the Inhabitants thereof. When it first arrived from England, it cracked the very first time it was rung, and two local craftsmen melted it down and recast it, twice, until it rang properly.

Its voice is not especially beautiful. But it rings to call the members of the government to their meetings, and to announce important news, and the people of Philadelphia know its sound well.

Much later, it will become known all over the world as the Liberty Bell.

--

Behind the State House, there is a large garden, surrounded by a high brick wall, with gravel paths and young elm trees and benches. It is called the State House Yard. People like to walk there on summer evenings.

Inside the building, there is a wide central hall, with a staircase leading up to the second floor. To the right of the hall is the courtroom of the Supreme Court of Pennsylvania. And to the left, behind a pair of tall doors, is the Assembly Room.

--

{img:assembly-room-green-tables-windsor-chairs}
The Assembly Room is a large, light room, about forty feet square, with tall windows on two sides, and walls painted a pale grey.

The members sit at small tables covered with green cloth, arranged in rows facing the front of the room, each table shared by the delegates of one colony. They sit on wooden Windsor chairs. On the tables are inkstands with quill pens, and candlesticks for the evening, and stacks of papers and letters.

At the front of the room, on a low platform, there is a tall-backed chair and a desk, where the president of the Congress sits.

--

In the summer of 1776, the president is John Hancock, of Massachusetts.

He is thirty-nine years old, a tall, handsome, elegant man, one of the richest merchants in Boston, who likes fine clothes and fine carriages, and who dresses in velvet and lace and silk stockings. He is vain, some people say, and likes to be admired. But he is also generous and brave, and he has given a great deal of his fortune to the American cause.

{img:elegant-president-high-chair-desk-room}
Beside him sits the secretary of the Congress, Charles Thomson, a thin, serious man with a long face, who writes down everything that is decided, in a careful hand, in the great leather-bound journal of the Congress.

--

There are about fifty delegates in the Congress, from all thirteen colonies, from New Hampshire in the north to Georgia in the south.

They are lawyers and merchants, planters and doctors, farmers and ministers. Some are rich, and some are not. Some are young, in their twenties and thirties. Some, like Franklin, are old. They come from very different places, with different ways of speaking and dressing and thinking. Many of them had never met each other before they came to Philadelphia.

--

The Congress meets every day, except on Sundays, from about ten in the morning until late in the afternoon.

The doors are kept closed. The debates are secret. Even the windows are often kept shut, so that nobody outside can hear what is being said, although in the summer heat, this makes the room terribly hot and stuffy.

{img:delegates-debating-hot-room-fans}
Worse still, there is a stable next door, and the flies from the stable come in, big black horseflies, and bite the delegates through their silk stockings. The men sit at their tables, sweating in their wool coats and their wigs, swatting at the flies with their handkerchiefs, and arguing, and arguing.

--

In the evenings, when the Congress rises, the delegates go out into the city.

Many of them go to the City Tavern, a new and handsome tavern on Second Street, near the river. It is the finest tavern in America, with a large dining room, a coffee room, and private rooms upstairs where gentlemen can dine and talk in private.

There, in the candlelight, over dinners of turtle soup and roast duck and Madeira wine, the delegates go on talking: about the war, about the news from the other colonies, about what the Congress should do next. Some of the most important decisions of the Revolution are made, not in the Assembly Room, but over the dinner tables of the City Tavern.

--

The delegates live in boarding houses and rented rooms around the city.

They are far from their families, and many of them are lonely. They write long letters home, every few days, to their wives and children, which are carried north and south by riders on horseback, and take days or weeks to arrive.

{img:delegate-writing-letter-rented-room-candle}
And the most famous of all those letters are the ones written by a short, stout, quick-tempered lawyer from Massachusetts, to his wife, who is far away on their farm near Boston.

His name is John Adams.
