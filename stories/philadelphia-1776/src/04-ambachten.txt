Philadelphia is a city of craftsmen, and the craftsmen of Philadelphia are among the finest in the world.

All over the city, in small workshops at the back of their houses, or in rooms on the ground floor that open onto the street, they make almost everything that people need: furniture and clocks, silver and pewter, shoes and saddles, hats and wigs, barrels and carriages, books and newspapers.

--

The cabinetmakers of Philadelphia are especially famous.

In their workshops, which smell of sawdust and glue and wood shavings, they make beautiful chairs and tables, desks and bookcases, chests of drawers and high chests on tall legs, out of dark, glowing mahogany brought on ships from the West Indies, and walnut and cherry from the forests of Pennsylvania.

The master craftsman draws the design, and chooses the wood. The apprentices saw the boards and plane them smooth. Then the carvers set to work, with their sharp little chisels, carving shells and leaves and flowers, and feet shaped like the claws of birds holding a ball. A single high chest might take months to make.

Today, pieces of furniture made in Philadelphia in these years are treasured in museums all over the world.

--

There are silversmiths, too, who hammer out teapots and spoons and candlesticks from sheets of silver, at their little anvils, by the light of a window. There are clockmakers, who make tall clocks in wooden cases, with brass faces and painted moons. There are pewterers, who cast plates and mugs and porringers in moulds.

{img:silversmith-hammering-teapot-anvil}
There are tailors and dressmakers, shoemakers and hatters, saddlers and harness makers, coopers making barrels, wheelwrights making wheels, and blacksmiths in their dark, glowing forges, shoeing horses and making nails and hinges and tools.

--

On Arch Street, in a small brick house, there lives a young woman named Betsy Ross.

She is twenty-four years old in 1776, with a pleasant, round face and dark hair. She is an upholsterer, which means that she makes and repairs curtains, cushions, bed hangings, and the coverings of chairs and sofas. She learned the trade as a young girl, as an apprentice in the workshop of an upholsterer named John Webster.

{img:young-upholsterer-sewing-fabric-window}
Earlier this year, in January, her young husband, John, died, after an accident while he was guarding military supplies on the waterfront. Betsy is a widow now, carrying on the upholstery business alone, in her little shop.

Many years later, her grandson will tell a story that, in the spring of 1776, George Washington himself came to her shop, with two other gentlemen, and asked her to sew the first American flag, with stars and stripes, and that she showed them how to cut a five-pointed star with a single snip of the scissors. Nobody knows for sure if the story is true. But it has been told and loved for a very long time.

--

Down by the river, there are the shipyards.

In the yards along the Delaware, the shipwrights are building ships: merchant ships for trade, and now, because of the war, small warships for the new American navy. The great wooden ribs of the ships stand on the slipways like the skeletons of whales, and the men climb over them with their hammers and adzes and saws.

{img:shipyard-wooden-ribs-river-workers}
The sound of the shipyards carries across the water all day long: the ringing of hammers, the rasp of saws, the shouts of the men, and the creak of the ropes as the timbers are hauled into place.

--

And there are the printers.

Philadelphia has more printing shops than any other city in America, and several newspapers, some in English and one in German. In the printing shops, the compositors stand at their tall wooden cases, picking out the little metal letters one by one, and setting them in lines. Then the pressmen ink the type with leather balls, lay a sheet of damp paper on top, and pull the heavy wooden lever of the press, and lift out the printed page.

It is slow work, and it takes two men a whole day to print a few hundred copies of a newspaper. But the newspapers carry the news all over the colonies, and the printers of Philadelphia are some of the most important men in the city.

--

Most craftsmen learn their trade as apprentices.

A boy of twelve or fourteen is sent by his parents to live with a master craftsman, for seven years or so, to learn the trade. He lives in the master's house, eats at his table, and works in his shop from dawn to dusk, six days a week. He sweeps the floor, runs errands, fetches and carries, and slowly, year by year, learns the skills of the trade.

When his apprenticeship is over, he becomes a journeyman, and works for wages. And one day, if he is skilful and lucky and careful with his money, he may open a shop of his own, and take apprentices of his own.

--

The most famous printer in Philadelphia began exactly that way.

{img:old-printing-shop-sign-street-evening}
He came to the city as a runaway apprentice of seventeen, more than fifty years ago, with only a few coins in his pocket, and walked up Market Street eating a bread roll, while a girl standing in a doorway laughed at him. He found work in a printing shop, and later opened his own, and published a newspaper and a famous almanac, and became rich and respected.

Later still, he became one of the most famous men in the world.

His name is Benjamin Franklin.
