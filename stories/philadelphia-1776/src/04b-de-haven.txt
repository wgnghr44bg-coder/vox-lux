Along the edge of the Delaware River, for more than a mile, the wharves of Philadelphia stretch out into the water.

They are long wooden piers, built on great timber posts driven into the mud of the river bed, with warehouses of brick and wood standing at their landward ends. Between them, the ships lie moored, side by side, their tall masts and spars and rigging making a tangled web of lines against the sky.

--

In ordinary times, the harbour of Philadelphia is one of the busiest in the world.

Ships come in from London and Bristol and Liverpool, from Lisbon and Madeira, from the islands of the West Indies, and from the other colonies along the coast. They bring cloth and tea, china and glass, books and furniture, wine and rum, sugar and molasses, and many other things that the people of Pennsylvania cannot make for themselves.

And they carry away the riches of Pennsylvania: flour and bread, barrels of salted pork and beef, timber and iron, furs and flaxseed. The farms of Pennsylvania are so rich that people call it the breadbasket of the colonies, and much of the flour that feeds the West Indies comes from the mills near Philadelphia.

--

The wharves are a noisy, busy, crowded place.

Sailors swarm up and down the rigging of the ships, shouting to each other. Porters carry sacks and barrels on their backs along the wooden planks. Carts and drays rumble along Water Street, behind the warehouses, piled high with goods. Merchants in their fine coats walk up and down with their clerks, checking their cargoes and their accounts.

{img:merchant-clerk-ledger-wharf-barrels}
The air smells of tar and rope and fish, of the river and the mud, of coffee and spices and tobacco from the warehouses, and of the bread baking in the bakehouses on Front Street.

--

But in the summer of 1776, the harbour is much quieter than usual.

Because of the war, trade with Britain has stopped. British warships patrol the mouth of the Delaware, far down the river, near the sea, and stop any ship that tries to go in or out. Many of the merchants have no business, and their ships lie idle at the wharves, with their sails taken down and their crews gone ashore.

Some of the merchants have turned to a new kind of business. They fit out their fastest ships with cannons, and send them to sea as privateers, to hunt for British merchant ships and capture them. Others build small warships for the new American navy, or bring in gunpowder and weapons secretly from France and the Dutch islands in the Caribbean.

--

All along the river, too, there are smaller boats.

There are ferries, broad, flat boats rowed or poled across the river, carrying people and horses and wagons to and from New Jersey, on the other side. There are fishing boats, which go out every morning to catch shad and herring and sturgeon in the river. There are little sailing boats called shallops, which carry firewood and hay and farm produce up and down the river from the towns along its banks.

{img:ferry-boat-river-horses-passengers}
On a sunny afternoon, the river is dotted with sails, white and brown, moving slowly up and down the wide, shining water.

--

The men who work on the river and the wharves are a tough, hardworking crowd.

There are sailors from every country, English and Irish and Scottish, Dutch and German, Portuguese and Spanish, and many free Black sailors, who have found a kind of freedom and respect at sea that is hard to find on land. There are shipwrights and ropemakers, sailmakers and coopers, dockworkers and porters.

{img:ropewalk-long-shed-workers-twisting}
Near the river, there are long, narrow buildings called ropewalks, sometimes a quarter of a mile long, where men walk slowly backwards, twisting strands of hemp into rope for the ships' rigging. There are sail lofts, big airy rooms high under the roofs of the warehouses, where the sailmakers sit cross-legged on the floor, stitching the great sheets of canvas with their needles and palms.

--

In the evenings, the sailors gather in the taverns along Water Street, near the wharves.

They drink rum and beer, and play cards and dice, and sing the old songs of the sea, and tell stories of storms and shipwrecks and faraway ports. Sometimes they argue about the war, and about independence. Many of them have already joined the new navy, or the privateers, and are eager for action.

And when the night grows late, they stumble back along the dark wharves to their ships, or to their lodgings, while the river laps softly against the wooden piers, and the riding lights of the ships glimmer on the water.

--

At the very end of the wharves, at the southern edge of the city, the Delaware widens, and flows on, past green marshes and islands and the little forts that the Americans are building to defend the river, toward the bay and the open sea, about a hundred miles away.

{img:river-sunset-marshes-distant-sails}
Every evening, as the sun goes down behind the city, the river turns to gold, and then to silver, and the last boats come home, and the gulls settle on the posts of the wharves, and the city lights begin to shine along the shore.
