Summer in Philadelphia is hot.

By the middle of the day, in July, the sun beats down on the brick houses and the cobbled streets, and the heat rises from the stones in shimmering waves. The air is heavy and damp, because of the two rivers, and there is often very little wind. People move slowly, keeping to the shady side of the street.

--

The people of the city have their own ways of dealing with the heat.

They get up very early, at dawn or before, to do the hardest work in the cool of the morning. They close the wooden shutters of their windows during the day, to keep out the sun, and open them again in the evening, to let in the cooler air. They wear light clothes, of linen and cotton, as light as they can while still being respectable.

In the middle of the afternoon, after dinner, many people rest for an hour or two in the darkest, coolest room of the house, while the city grows quiet around them, and only the buzzing of the flies and the distant cry of a street seller break the silence.

--

The rich families have country houses outside the city, on the banks of the Schuylkill, among green lawns and shady trees, where the air is fresher.

In the summer, they leave the city and go to their country houses for weeks at a time. They sit on their verandas in the evenings, looking down at the river, and walk in their gardens, and visit their neighbours in their carriages along the country lanes.

{img:country-house-schuylkill-lawn-shade-trees}
For the people who stay in the city, there are other pleasures. There are pleasure gardens, where people can stroll in the evening among flower beds and fountains, and drink lemonade and eat ice cream, which has recently become very fashionable, and listen to music.

--

For the children, summer is a wonderful time.

The boys go swimming in the rivers, jumping off the wharves into the Delaware, or splashing in the shallow, clear water of the Schuylkill, further out of town. They go fishing, with homemade rods and lines, for perch and catfish. They climb trees in the orchards at the edge of the city, and eat cherries and apples until they are sick.

The girls help their mothers in the house and the garden, but they have their own games too: skipping and hoops, dolls and hopscotch, and on summer evenings, catching fireflies in their hands in the gardens, and putting them in glass jars, to watch them glow.

--

Summer is also a time of sickness.

In the heat, the water from the wells and the pumps sometimes goes bad, and the food spoils quickly. There are fevers, and stomach illnesses, especially among the children. There are mosquitoes in the marshes by the rivers, which carry fevers too, though nobody knows it yet.

{img:mother-cool-cloth-child-bed-summer}
The doctors of Philadelphia, who are among the best in America, go from house to house, with their bags of medicines. The mothers sit by their children's beds, fanning them, and bathing their foreheads with cool water, and praying.

--

In the evening, the city comes alive again.

As the sun goes down, a cool breeze sometimes comes up from the river. People open their windows and doors, and come out of their houses, and sit on the benches by their front doors, or on the white marble steps. The children play in the streets. The neighbours talk across the street to each other. Young couples walk arm in arm along the pavements, or out into the fields at the edge of the city.

{img:families-on-steps-summer-evening-street}
In the State House Yard, people stroll along the gravel paths under the trees, in the long golden light. In the gardens behind the houses, the roses and the honeysuckle smell sweet in the warm air.

--

The street sellers are out, too, in the evening.

There are women selling hot corn, roasted on the cob, from big baskets. There are men selling oysters, opening them with a quick twist of a knife, and pepper pot soup, a spicy stew sold steaming hot from a kettle on a little cart. There are boys selling newspapers, and calling out the latest news from the army and the Congress.

Each seller has a special cry, a little song or call that everyone in the city knows: Pepper pot, smoking hot! Oysters, fine oysters! And the cries drift through the warm streets until long after dark.

--

At ten o'clock, the watchman comes round, with his lantern and his staff, calling out the hour and the weather.

People go inside, and close their doors. The candles go out, one by one, in the windows. The streets grow quiet, except for the footsteps of the watchman, and the occasional rattle of a carriage going home late, and the barking of a dog.

{img:watchman-lantern-night-brick-street-stars}
And over the city, the summer stars come out, faint at first, and then brighter, above the steeple of Christ Church and the tower of the State House, and the river flows on, dark and quiet, toward the sea.
