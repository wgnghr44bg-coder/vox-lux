On Monday, the first of July, 1776, the Congress meets in the State House to debate independence.

It is a hot, heavy morning. The delegates arrive in twos and threes, walking up Chestnut Street in their coats and hats, and go in through the tall doors into the Assembly Room, and take their places at the green tables. The doors are closed. The windows are shut. And the debate begins.

--

It goes on all day.

John Dickinson of Pennsylvania, a thoughtful, cautious lawyer, who has done a great deal for the American cause, stands up and speaks against independence. He says that it is too soon. He says that the colonies should first make an alliance with France, and agree on a government among themselves, before they cut themselves off from Britain. He speaks long and seriously, and many delegates are moved.

Then John Adams stands up to answer him.

--

Adams speaks for a long time, without notes, with great passion.

Outside, during his speech, a summer storm breaks over the city. The sky turns dark, and the thunder rolls, and the rain pours down on the roof, and lightning flashes at the windows. Inside, the candles are lit, and Adams goes on speaking, through the thunder, about liberty and independence and the future of America.

{img:thunderstorm-rain-windows-candles-room}
Afterwards, Jefferson will call him the colossus of independence. Adams himself writes that evening that he has said nothing new, and that he only repeated what had been said many times before.

--

At the end of the day, there is a trial vote.

Nine colonies are in favour of independence. But Pennsylvania and South Carolina vote against. The two delegates from Delaware who are present are divided, one for and one against, so Delaware's vote cannot be counted. And the New York delegates say that they have no instructions, and cannot vote at all.

The Congress agrees to hold the real vote the next morning, to give everyone time to think.

--

That night, a messenger rides south out of Philadelphia, as fast as he can.

He is carrying a letter to Caesar Rodney, the third delegate from Delaware, who is at home, eighty miles away, because of trouble in his colony. The letter tells him that his vote is needed, and that he must come to Philadelphia at once.

{img:rider-night-storm-road-lantern}
Caesar Rodney is forty-seven years old, a thin, tall man, and he is not well. He has suffered for years from a painful illness of the face, and he usually wears a green silk scarf to hide it. But when he reads the letter, he does not hesitate.

He mounts his horse and rides north, all through the night, through thunder and pouring rain, along the muddy roads, stopping only to change horses.

--

On the morning of Tuesday, the second of July, it is still raining.

In the State House, the delegates gather again. And just as the session is beginning, Caesar Rodney arrives, still in his riding boots and spurs, splashed with mud from head to foot, and walks into the Assembly Room.

{img:muddy-rider-entering-assembly-room-boots}
He has ridden about eighty miles through the night. He is exhausted. But he takes his place at the Delaware table, and when the vote is called, he votes for independence. Now Delaware is in favour.

--

During the night, other things have changed too.

The delegates from South Carolina have decided to vote in favour, so that the vote will be united. And two of the Pennsylvania delegates who were against it, John Dickinson and his friend Robert Morris, have decided not to come to the State House that morning. They stay away, quietly, so that the other Pennsylvania delegates, who are in favour, can carry the vote.

And so, when the vote is taken, twelve colonies vote for independence. New York's delegates, still without instructions from home, do not vote. A week later, New York will agree too.

--

Outside the State House, the people of Philadelphia do not yet know what has happened.

The doors of the Assembly Room are closed, and the delegates have promised to keep their debates secret. In the streets, the rain is falling steadily. The market people are packing up their stalls under the dripping sheds. The shopkeepers stand in their doorways, watching the water run along the gutters. A few soldiers hurry past with their collars turned up.

Only a few people, who know the delegates well, have guessed what the Congress is doing that day. They stand under the trees of the State House Yard, sheltering from the rain, and watch the tall windows of the Assembly Room, and wait for news.

--

It is done.

On the second of July, 1776, without any ceremony, in a hot, closed room, with the rain falling outside, the delegates of the Continental Congress vote that these united colonies are, and of right ought to be, free and independent states.

There are no bells. There are no cannons. The delegates simply go on with the business of the day.

--

The next day, John Adams writes to Abigail, full of excitement.

{img:adams-writing-excited-letter-candle-evening}
The second day of July, 1776, he writes, will be the most memorable epoch in the history of America. I am apt to believe that it will be celebrated by succeeding generations as the great anniversary festival. It ought to be solemnized with pomp and parade, with shows, games, sports, guns, bells, bonfires and illuminations, from one end of this continent to the other, from this time forward forevermore.

He is right about almost everything, except the date. Americans will celebrate, with parades and games and fireworks, from one end of the continent to the other. But they will do it on the fourth of July.
