June turns into a warm, green summer in Philadelphia.

The trees along the streets are in full leaf. The gardens behind the houses are full of roses and lilies and honeysuckle. Cherries and strawberries are piled high on the market stalls. The evenings are long and golden, and the swallows dart and twitter over the rooftops until it is almost dark.

--

But the city does not feel peaceful.

There are soldiers everywhere. Companies of militia, the citizen soldiers of Pennsylvania, drill in the fields at the edge of the city and in the State House Yard, marching up and down with their muskets, in their hunting shirts and their homemade uniforms, while the fifes and drums play and the children watch.

New regiments march through the streets on their way north, to join General Washington's army in New York. People come out to watch them pass, and wave, and give them bread and water.

--

The news from the war is worrying.

In the spring, the American army drove the British out of Boston, after a long siege. That was a great victory, and everyone celebrated. But now, everyone knows, the British are coming back, with a much bigger force. A great fleet of warships and transport ships is crossing the Atlantic, carrying thousands of soldiers. Most people think they will land at New York.

{img:fleet-of-sails-on-horizon-ocean}
General Washington has taken his army to New York, to try to defend the city. And everyone in Philadelphia waits for news, every day, from the riders who come galloping in along the New York road.

--

In the Congress, the delegates wait too, for the vote on independence that is coming at the beginning of July.

In the meantime, they work on many other things: raising money for the army, buying gunpowder and cannons and uniforms, writing letters to the colonies, making plans for an alliance with France. The delegates work long hours, in the heat, in the closed room, with the flies buzzing at the windows.

In the evenings, they meet in the taverns and in each other's lodgings, and talk and argue, and count the votes.

--

The colonies do not all agree.

Some of them, like Massachusetts and Virginia, are strongly in favour of independence. Their delegates have been told by their colonies to vote for it. But others are not so sure. Pennsylvania is divided, and so is Delaware. New York's delegates have not been given any instructions at all, and they will not vote either way until they hear from home. South Carolina is unsure.

{img:men-arguing-tavern-table-pipes}
The delegates who want independence know that it is very important for the vote to be unanimous, or as close to unanimous as possible. If some colonies vote against it, the British will be able to say that the Americans are divided, and the other countries of Europe will not take the new nation seriously.

So they work hard, through those last weeks of June, talking and persuading and writing letters home.

--

In the city, too, everyone is talking about independence.

In the taverns and the coffee houses, in the market and on the doorsteps, people argue about it. Some are eager for it, and talk about liberty and rights. Some are afraid of it, because it means a long war against the most powerful country in the world. Some are loyal to the King, and want no part of it, and keep their opinions quietly to themselves, behind closed doors.

{img:neighbours-talking-doorstep-summer-evening}
Elizabeth Drinker writes in her diary about the soldiers, and the worried faces of her neighbours, and the feeling that something is about to happen.

--

On the twenty-ninth of June, news arrives that the British fleet has been seen off the coast near New York.

Over the next few days, more and more ships arrive, until there are more than a hundred of them, anchored in the harbour off Staten Island, a forest of masts and sails. Soon there will be more than four hundred ships, and more than thirty thousand soldiers, the largest force that Britain has ever sent across the sea.

The news reaches Philadelphia within a day or two, and the city holds its breath.

--

The last evening of June is warm and still.

The sun goes down over the fields to the west of the city, and the sky turns pink and gold behind the State House tower. The lamplighters come out. The watchman begins his rounds. In the boarding houses and the taverns, the delegates are sitting late, talking quietly, about the vote that will begin in the morning.

{img:sunset-behind-state-house-tower-june}
In his rooms at the Graff house, Jefferson sits by the window in the warm darkness, looking out at the fields. In his lodgings, John Adams writes a letter to Abigail. In his courtyard, Franklin sits under the mulberry tree, with his grandchildren asleep in the house behind him.

And far to the south, in Delaware, a man named Caesar Rodney is at his farm, not knowing that in two days' time, he will be asked to make the most important ride of his life.
