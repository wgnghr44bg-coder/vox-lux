Benjamin Franklin came home from France in 1785, an old man of seventy-nine, and the people of Philadelphia met him at the wharf with cannons and bells and cheering crowds.

He spent his last years in his house in the courtyard off Market Street, with his daughter and his grandchildren around him. He added a library and a dining room to the house, and invented a long pole with a little grabbing hand at the end, to reach books from the high shelves. He sat under his mulberry tree in the summer, and talked with his friends, and played games with the children.

--

In his very last years, he became the president of a society in Philadelphia that worked to end slavery, and he signed a petition to the new Congress, asking it to abolish slavery in the whole country.

He died in April 1790, at the age of eighty-four. About twenty thousand people came to his funeral, the largest crowd that Philadelphia had ever seen. He is buried beside his wife, Deborah, in the little burial ground of Christ Church, on Arch Street.

To this day, visitors throw pennies onto his grave, for good luck, remembering his saying that a penny saved is a penny earned.

--

Betsy Ross married again, twice, and went on running her upholstery business for many years, making flags for the American navy and the government, as well as curtains and cushions and chair covers.

She had seven daughters, and lived to the age of eighty-four. She spent her last years living with one of her daughters, telling her grandchildren stories about the old days in Philadelphia.

{img:elderly-woman-telling-stories-grandchildren-fireside}
Her little house on Arch Street still stands, and is visited by many people every year.

--

Elizabeth Drinker kept her diary until a few weeks before her death, in 1807.

In it, she wrote about everything she saw in her long life in Philadelphia: the war, the British army in the city, the yellow fever epidemic of 1793, when thousands of people died and everyone who could left the city, the new government, the new century. And always, the weather, and her family, and the small events of every day.

{img:old-diary-pages-quill-window-light}
Her diary, many volumes of it, is now kept in a library in Philadelphia, and historians still read it, to learn what life was like in those years.

--

Caesar Rodney, who rode through the storm to vote for independence, served as the president of Delaware during the war, and worked hard to supply the army. He died in 1784, before he could see his country's constitution.

Today, he rides on the back of the Delaware state quarter, on his galloping horse, and there is a statue of him in the main square of Wilmington, in Delaware.

--

{img:president-washington-carriage-philadelphia-street}
Thomas Paine, whose little pamphlet Common Sense had done so much to make people think about independence, went with the army as a soldier and a writer.

In the dark winter of 1776, when the army was retreating across New Jersey, beaten and cold and hungry, he wrote another pamphlet, by the light of a campfire, it is said, on a drumhead. These are the times that try men's souls, it began. General Washington ordered it to be read aloud to his soldiers before they crossed the icy Delaware on Christmas night.

Later, Paine travelled to England and to France, and wrote many more books, and lived a restless, stormy life. He died in New York in 1809, poor and almost forgotten. But his words are still read today.

--

For ten years, from 1790 to 1800, Philadelphia was the capital of the United States.

President George Washington lived in a fine house on Market Street, just a block from where Franklin had lived, and from the Graff house where Jefferson had written the Declaration. The Congress met in a new building right next to the State House. Philadelphia was the busiest and most important city in the country.

Then, in 1800, the government moved to a new city built on the banks of the Potomac River, which was named Washington.

--

John Adams became the first Vice President of the United States, under Washington, and then, in 1797, the second President.

He was the first President to live in the new White House, in Washington. On his second night there, he wrote to Abigail a little prayer, which is now carved over the fireplace in the State Dining Room: I pray Heaven to bestow the best of blessings on this house, and on all that shall hereafter inhabit it. May none but honest and wise men ever rule under this roof.

When his term was over, he went home to the farm in Braintree, with Abigail, and lived there quietly for the rest of his life, reading and writing and farming.

--

Thomas Jefferson became the third President, in 1801.

During his presidency, he bought the vast Louisiana territory from France, which doubled the size of the country, and sent Lewis and Clark on their famous journey across the continent to the Pacific Ocean.

{img:monticello-hilltop-house-dome-sunset}
When his presidency was over, he went home to Monticello, his beloved house on its hilltop in Virginia, and spent his last years there, among his books and his gardens. He founded the University of Virginia, and designed its buildings himself.

--

For many years, Adams and Jefferson were not friends.

They had quarrelled bitterly over politics, when they were both in government, and had stopped writing to each other. Adams was hurt and angry. Jefferson was cold and distant. Each believed the other had betrayed him.

But in 1812, when they were both old men, a friend of theirs, a doctor named Benjamin Rush, who had also signed the Declaration, persuaded them to write to each other again.
