The State House still stands today, at the end of Chestnut Street, in the middle of Philadelphia.

Today, it is called Independence Hall. Its red brick walls, its white window frames, and its tall tower with the white wooden steeple are just as they were in 1776. Around it, there is a large green park, called Independence National Historical Park, with lawns and trees and brick paths.

--

Every day, visitors come here from all over the world, and wait in line to go inside.

A park ranger leads them through the wide central hall, and into the Assembly Room. The room has been carefully restored, to look just as it did in the summer of 1776. The green cloths are on the tables. The quill pens and the silver inkstands are on the desks. The Windsor chairs stand in their rows.

And at the front of the room, on its low platform, stands the tall chair with the carved half sun on its back, the very same chair that George Washington sat in, and that Franklin looked at and wondered whether the sun was rising or setting.

The visitors stand quietly, and look, and the ranger tells them the story of what happened in this room. And then, very often, there is a silence, as people try to imagine it.

--

Across the street from Independence Hall, in a modern building of glass, hangs the Liberty Bell.

It hangs low, on a wooden yoke, so that visitors can see it up close, with the long crack running up its side, and the words around its rim. Through the glass wall behind it, they can see the tower of Independence Hall, where it used to hang.

{img:liberty-bell-cracked-glass-hall-tower-view}
The bell has not been rung for a very long time. But it has become a symbol of freedom for people all over the world. People who fought to end slavery took their name from it. People who fought for women's right to vote made a copy of it. Every year, people come from many countries, and stand in front of it, and touch the glass.

--

Not far away, in a quiet courtyard off Market Street, is the place where Franklin's house stood.

The house itself was pulled down long ago. But in its place, there is a steel frame, the outline of the house, standing in the courtyard, so that visitors can see where it was. Under the ground, there is a little museum about Franklin's life. And in the courtyard, there is a printing shop, where a printer in old-fashioned clothes still prints pages on an old wooden press, as Franklin once did.

{img:franklin-court-ghost-house-steel-frame}
At the corner of Seventh and Market, there is a copy of the Graff house, where Jefferson wrote the Declaration, with his two rooms on the second floor restored, with a copy of his portable desk.

--

The City Tavern on Second Street has been rebuilt too, exactly as it was, with its long dining rooms and its tall windows, so that visitors can see where the delegates once sat over their dinners by candlelight.

{img:candlelit-tavern-dining-room-colonial-waiters}
Elfreth's Alley is still there, with its little brick houses, and people still live in them. Christ Church still stands on Second Street, with its white steeple, and Franklin still lies in its little burial ground on Arch Street, with the pennies on his grave. Betsy Ross's little house still stands on Arch Street.

--

This year, on the fourth of July, 2026, the United States celebrated the two hundred and fiftieth anniversary of the Declaration of Independence.

In Philadelphia, there were parades and concerts and fireworks, and enormous crowds gathered around Independence Hall, just as the people of the city once gathered in the State House Yard to hear the Declaration read aloud for the first time. Bells rang out across the city, and across the whole country, from one end of the continent to the other.

John Adams would have been pleased. It was exactly the kind of celebration he had imagined, in his letter to Abigail, two hundred and fifty years before.

--

In the evening, when the visitors have gone, the park around Independence Hall grows quiet.

The light turns golden on the red brick walls, and then soft and grey. The lamps come on along the paths. Squirrels run across the lawns, and the birds settle in the trees. The great white steeple glows against the darkening sky.

{img:independence-hall-dusk-lamps-quiet-park}
A few blocks away, the Delaware River flows on, wide and dark, past the old wharves, toward the sea, just as it did when the tall ships lay at anchor there in the summer of 1776.

--

So we leave Philadelphia for tonight, the old brick city by the river, with its quiet courtyards and its lamplit streets, and the tall white steeple of the old State House standing against the stars.
