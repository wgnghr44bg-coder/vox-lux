On the second and third of July, the Congress turns to Jefferson's declaration.

The delegates sit at their green tables, with copies of the draft in front of them, and go through it, line by line, sentence by sentence. They read it aloud, and talk about it, and change it.

--

For Jefferson, it is a hard few days.

He sits quietly at the Virginia table, saying almost nothing, while the delegates argue about his words, and cross out some of them, and change others. He feels, he will write later, as if his work were being mangled. He does not defend it. He simply listens, and suffers.

Franklin, sitting near him, sees that he is unhappy, and leans over to comfort him. He tells him a little story, about a young hatter who wanted to make a sign for his shop, and asked his friends what it should say, and each friend suggested taking out a word, until in the end, there was nothing left on the sign but a picture of a hat. Jefferson smiles a little.

--

The Congress makes many changes.

They take out about a quarter of what Jefferson wrote. Some of the changes make the Declaration shorter and sharper. Others take out things that some delegates do not want to say.

{img:quill-crossing-out-manuscript-closeup}
One long passage, in which Jefferson blamed the King for the slave trade, is removed altogether, because the delegates from South Carolina and Georgia will not accept it, and some delegates from the North, whose merchants have also profited from the trade, are uncomfortable with it too.

But the beginning, the famous words about equality and rights, the Congress leaves almost exactly as Jefferson wrote them.

--

Thursday, the fourth of July, 1776, begins as a pleasant summer day in Philadelphia.

Jefferson, who loves to measure things, writes down the temperature in his notebook at six o'clock in the morning: sixty-eight degrees. A little later, he goes out shopping. He visits the shop of a man named John Sparhawk, and buys a new thermometer, for three pounds and fifteen shillings. He also buys seven pairs of gloves for his wife, Martha.

At one o'clock in the afternoon, according to his new thermometer, it is seventy-six degrees. A warm day, but not too hot, with a gentle breeze.

--

In the Assembly Room, the delegates finish their work on the Declaration.

Some time late in the morning, or in the early afternoon, the Congress agrees to the final words. Then the Declaration of Independence is formally adopted, in the name of all the colonies, and John Hancock, as president of the Congress, and Charles Thomson, as secretary, sign the official copy.

{img:president-signing-document-secretary-watching}
There is no great ceremony. Most of the delegates do not sign anything that day. They go on with the other business of the Congress, and in the evening, they go home, or to the City Tavern, or to their lodgings, to have dinner.

--

That evening, a copy of the Declaration is carried a few streets away, to the printing shop of a young Irishman named John Dunlap, on Market Street.

Dunlap is twenty-nine, a printer and newspaper publisher, who came to Philadelphia from Ireland as a boy to work for his uncle, a printer too. He is asked to print copies of the Declaration, as quickly as possible, to be sent out to all the colonies, and to the army.

{img:print-shop-night-candles-pressmen-working}
So all night long, in his shop, Dunlap and his men work by the light of candles. The compositors set the words in type, letter by letter, at their wooden cases. The pressmen ink the type and pull the lever of the press, again and again, and lift out sheet after sheet, and hang them up to dry on strings across the room.

They print perhaps two hundred copies, large single sheets, with the words In Congress, July 4, 1776, A Declaration By the Representatives of the United States of America, in General Congress Assembled, printed in large letters across the top.

--

Outside the printing shop, the city is quiet.

The lamps are burning along the streets. The watchman goes by, calling the hour: twelve o'clock, and a fine night. The houses are dark, and most of the people of Philadelphia are asleep, not yet knowing what has happened in the State House that day.

In his rooms at the Graff house, Jefferson is perhaps still awake, sitting by the window in the warm night, thinking about the words he wrote and the words that were taken away. In the boarding house, John Adams is perhaps writing another letter. In his courtyard, under the mulberry tree, Franklin is asleep.

--

On the morning of the fifth of July, the printed copies of the Declaration are folded and packed into saddlebags.

Riders set out at a gallop from Philadelphia, on every road: north to New York and Boston, south to Baltimore and Williamsburg and Charleston, west to the frontier towns. They carry the Declaration to the governments of all the colonies, and to General Washington and his army, and to the newspapers.

{img:riders-galloping-out-of-city-morning}
Over the next days and weeks, the news will spread from town to town and village to village, all across the thirteen colonies, now the thirteen United States of America.

Only twenty-six copies of John Dunlap's printing survive today. They are some of the most treasured pieces of paper in the world.
