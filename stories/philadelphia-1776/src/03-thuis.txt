In a comfortable brick house on Front Street, near the river, a Quaker woman named Elizabeth Drinker sits at a table by the window, writing in her diary.

She is about forty years old, a calm, observant woman with a plain white cap over her hair, and a simple grey dress, without ribbons or lace. She has been keeping her diary since she was a young girl, and she will go on keeping it almost every day for the rest of her long life, for nearly fifty years.

--

Elizabeth is married to Henry Drinker, a merchant, who owns ships and trades with Britain and the West Indies. They have several children, and a large household, with servants and a cook and a maid.

Her diary is not about great events. It is about the ordinary things of everyday life. Who came to visit, and who went out. What the weather was like. Which of the children was ill, and what medicine they were given. What was bought at the market, and what it cost.

{img:quaker-family-plain-dress-front-room}
Because of her diary, we know a great deal about what life was like inside a Philadelphia house in those years.

--

A Quaker household is quiet and orderly.

The furniture is well made, of polished walnut and mahogany, but plain, without carvings or decoration. There are no paintings on the walls, except perhaps a simple portrait. The family dresses simply, in good cloth of sober colours. They do not dance, or go to the theatre, or play cards. They speak to each other using the old words thee and thou, as Quakers have done since the time of their founder.

On Sundays, which they call First Day, they go to the Meeting House, a large, plain brick building with no altar and no steeple, and sit together in silence on long wooden benches, until someone feels moved to stand up and speak.

--

Quakers were the first people to settle Philadelphia, and they are still among the richest and most respected families in the city.

{img:quaker-meeting-house-silent-benches}
But they are no longer the majority. Over the years, people of many other faiths have come: Anglicans and Presbyterians, Lutherans and German Reformed, Baptists and Methodists, Catholics and Jews. Each has its own church or meeting house or synagogue. On a Sunday morning, the bells of many different churches ring out across the city together.

--

Not every house in Philadelphia is as comfortable as the Drinkers'.

In the narrow alleys and lanes behind the main streets, there are smaller houses, two rooms on top of each other, where the craftsmen and the labourers live with their families. One of the oldest of these lanes, close to the river, is called Elfreth's Alley. It is a narrow, quiet street of little brick houses, built side by side for the blacksmiths and carpenters and shipwrights who worked nearby.

The rooms are small and low, and in winter they are cold. In summer, they are hot. The families cook over an open fire, and fetch water from a pump at the end of the lane. The children play in the alley, and the mothers sit on the steps in the evening and talk.

--

There are also many poorer people in the city.

There are sailors and labourers, who work on the wharves and in the shipyards. There are indentured servants, young men and women who have come from Europe and must work for a master for several years to pay for their passage across the ocean. There are free Black families, many of them working as servants, sailors, or craftsmen. And there are enslaved people, too, several hundred of them, owned by wealthier families, even here, in the city of brotherly love.

Some of the Quakers, among them a few of the city's most respected people, have begun to say that slavery is wrong, and that it should end. In a few years, Pennsylvania will become the first state to begin to abolish it.

--

{img:mother-children-kitchen-hearth-1776}
In every household, rich or poor, the day is long and busy.

The women and girls cook and clean, sew and mend, wash and iron, look after the children, and tend the garden and the hens. The fire must be kept burning all day in the kitchen, for cooking and for hot water, and the wood must be carried in, and the ashes carried out. Water must be fetched from the pump, bucket after bucket.

Washing day is the hardest of all. The clothes are boiled in a great copper kettle, scrubbed on a board, rinsed, wrung out by hand, and hung up to dry in the yard. Ironing takes another whole day.

--

In the evenings, families sit together by the light of candles.

Candles are precious, and are not wasted. The family gathers around one or two of them, in the parlour, and the mother sews, and the father reads aloud from the newspaper or the Bible, and the children do their lessons, or play quietly on the floor.

{img:candlelit-parlour-family-reading-aloud}
In summer, when it is light until late, they sit outside on the bench by the front door, and talk with the neighbours, and watch the people walking by, and the swallows darting over the rooftops.

--

In her diary, in these months of 1776, Elizabeth Drinker writes about the war.

She writes about the soldiers drilling in the streets, and the rumours of British ships in the river, and the worries of her husband, whose business with Britain has been stopped by the quarrel.

The Quakers, who believe that all war is wrong, will not take sides. This will bring them great trouble in the years ahead. But for now, Elizabeth writes quietly, every evening, by the light of her candle, about the weather, and the children, and the visitors, and the price of butter at the market, and about the strange times in which they are living.
