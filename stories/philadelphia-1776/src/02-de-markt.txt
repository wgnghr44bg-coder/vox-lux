The heart of Philadelphia is its market.

It stands in the middle of High Street, the widest street in the city, which everyone has begun to call Market Street. Down the centre of the street, starting near the river and running west for several blocks, there is a long row of open sheds: brick pillars holding up a low roof, with stalls and tables underneath, and the street running on either side.

--

On market days, which are Wednesdays and Saturdays, the market is crowded from before dawn.

The farmers come in from the countryside in their wagons, some of them from many miles away, and set up their stalls under the sheds. Others come across the Delaware on the ferries from New Jersey, with baskets of vegetables and crates of chickens and tubs of butter on their laps.

By sunrise, the whole long row of sheds is full. There are farmers' wives in white caps and aprons, selling eggs and butter and cheese. There are butchers with fresh beef and pork and mutton hanging on hooks. There are fishermen with shad and herring and rockfish from the river, and oysters in barrels, and crabs.

--

There are fruits and vegetables of every kind.

In the summer, there are peas and beans, cucumbers and lettuce, radishes and onions, early potatoes, cabbages and turnips. There are strawberries and cherries, raspberries and currants, and later in the summer, peaches and melons and apples. There are herbs in bunches, parsley and sage and thyme, and flowers for the table.

Visitors from Europe are amazed by the market. They say it is one of the finest in the world, and that they have never seen so much good food, so cheap, in one place.

--

{img:servant-girl-basket-market-bargaining}
The people who come to buy are just as varied.

There are the wives of merchants, in silk dresses and lace caps, with a servant walking behind them carrying the basket. There are the wives of craftsmen and labourers, in plain linen and wool, counting their pennies carefully. There are Quaker women in grey and brown, with simple white caps and no ornaments at all. There are sailors from the ships in the harbour, buying fresh food for the next voyage.

There are free Black families, too, and enslaved Black servants sent by their masters to do the shopping, and German farmers from the countryside, speaking their own language, and Irish and Scottish immigrants who have just arrived on the ships.

--

The market has rules.

A clerk of the market walks up and down the sheds, making sure that the weights and measures are honest, that the butter is not too light, and that the meat is fresh. Nobody is allowed to buy food before the market bell rings in the morning, so that the rich cannot buy everything first and sell it again at a higher price.

{img:market-clerk-scales-checking-butter}
When the bell rings, the buying begins, and the market fills with the noise of bargaining and laughing, the clucking of chickens, the cries of the sellers calling out their wares, and the rattle of carts on the cobbles.

--

All along the streets near the market, there are shops.

There are grocers selling tea and coffee, sugar and chocolate, spices and dried fruit, brought on the ships from the West Indies and from Europe and from as far away as China. There are bakers, with their round loaves in the windows, and confectioners selling little cakes and sugared almonds. There are taverns and coffee houses, where men sit at wooden tables and drink and talk and read the newspapers.

{img:coffee-house-men-reading-newspapers-1776}
There are bookshops and printing shops, too, more of them in Philadelphia than in any other city in America. People here love to read. They read newspapers and almanacs, sermons and pamphlets, histories and novels and poems, and they argue about them in the taverns and the coffee houses late into the night.

--

In the early months of 1776, there has been one pamphlet that everyone in the city has been reading.

It is called Common Sense, and it was written by an Englishman named Thomas Paine, who came to Philadelphia only a little over a year ago, with almost nothing, and found work as a writer.

In plain, simple language, Paine argues that it is foolish for a whole continent to be ruled by a small island across the ocean, and by a king whom the colonists have never seen. It is time, he says, for America to be independent.

Tens of thousands of copies have been sold, all over the colonies. People read it aloud to each other in taverns and on doorsteps. Many people who had never thought about independence before are now beginning to think about it very seriously.

--

At midday, when the market is over, the sheds are swept clean, and the farmers load up their empty wagons and drive home.

The city grows a little quieter in the heat of the afternoon. People go home for their dinner, which is the main meal of the day, eaten at about two or three o'clock. There might be roast beef or a boiled ham, a fish from the river, fresh vegetables from the market, bread and butter, and a pudding or a pie, with cider or beer to drink, or a glass of Madeira wine for those who can afford it.

{img:family-dinner-table-afternoon-pewter}
After dinner, the shops open again, and the work goes on until evening, when the lamplighters come out with their ladders, and the whale oil lamps begin to glow, one by one, along the brick streets.
