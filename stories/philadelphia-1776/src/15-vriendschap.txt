On the first of January, 1812, John Adams sat down in his study at his farm in Quincy, Massachusetts, and wrote a short, friendly letter to Thomas Jefferson.

He was seventy-six years old. Jefferson, at Monticello, was sixty-eight. They had not written to each other for more than eleven years.

--

Jefferson wrote back at once, warmly.

He wrote about the old days, about the Congress in Philadelphia, about the struggles they had shared. A letter from you, he wrote, carries me back to the times when, beset with difficulties and dangers, we were fellow labourers in the same cause, struggling for what is most valuable to man, his right of self-government.

And so began one of the most famous correspondences in history.

--

For the next fourteen years, until the end of their lives, the two old men wrote to each other, again and again.

They wrote about everything. About books they had read, and the books they were reading. About history and religion, philosophy and science. About the ancient Greeks and Romans. About their health, their grandchildren, their gardens and their farms. About growing old.

{img:letters-crossing-old-postal-carriage-road}
They disagreed about many things, and said so. But they disagreed now as old friends do, gently, with good humour and respect. And they remembered together the days of their youth, in the hot Assembly Room in Philadelphia, when they had worked side by side to make a new nation.

--

Adams, who was often lonely, loved the letters.

In 1818, his beloved Abigail died, after more than fifty years of marriage. He was heartbroken. Jefferson wrote to him with great tenderness, about the loss of his own wife, many years before, and about how time slowly softens grief, though it never takes it away.

Adams wrote back that the letters from Jefferson were among the greatest comforts of his old age.

--

Jefferson, at Monticello, rose with the sun every morning, and wrote letters for hours at his desk, often more than a thousand letters a year.

He rode his horse around his farms every afternoon, even in his old age, and walked in his gardens, where he grew more than three hundred kinds of vegetables and fruit, and kept careful notes of when each one was planted and when it was picked. In the evenings, he read, by the light of candles, in Greek and Latin, the old books he had loved as a young man.

At Quincy, Adams rose early too, and walked in his fields, and read and read, often until late at night, so many books that his eyes grew tired, and his grandchildren had to read aloud to him.

--

As the years went by, the two old men became, more and more, a kind of living memory of the American Revolution.

Most of the other men who had signed the Declaration had died. People came to visit them, to hear their stories, and to see the men who had been there. Painters came to paint their portraits. Young men came to ask their advice.

{img:visitors-old-statesman-porch-respect}
In 1826, as the fiftieth anniversary of the Declaration of Independence approached, both men were invited to great celebrations in Washington and in other cities. But both of them were too old and too weak to travel.

--

On the fourth of July, 1826, exactly fifty years after the day the Declaration was adopted in Philadelphia, the whole country celebrated.

There were parades and speeches, bells and cannons and fireworks, in every city and town, from one end of the country to the other, just as John Adams had once hoped.

{img:village-celebration-flags-bells-1826}
At Monticello, in Virginia, Thomas Jefferson had been very ill for several days. On the night of the third of July, he woke briefly, and asked: Is it the Fourth? His doctor told him that it soon would be. Jefferson fell asleep again, peacefully. He died a little after noon on the fourth of July, aged eighty-three.

--

That same afternoon, five hundred miles away, at his farm in Quincy, John Adams, now ninety years old, was also lying very ill in his bed.

He did not know that his friend had died a few hours before. Late in the afternoon, he woke for a moment, and whispered a few last words. People who were there remembered him saying: Thomas Jefferson survives.

A little while later, as the sun was going down, with the sound of the celebrations drifting faintly from the town, John Adams died too.

--

When the news spread across the country, people were amazed.

The two men who had done more than anyone else to bring about the Declaration of Independence, the one who had argued for it and the one who had written it, had died on the same day, the fiftieth anniversary of the day it was adopted, only a few hours apart.

Many people felt that it could not have been an accident. They said it was a sign from heaven, that the work of those two men had been blessed.

--

{img:two-old-portraits-side-by-side-candlelight}
And in the city where it had all begun, in Philadelphia, the bells rang once more, slowly this time, for the two old friends.

Some time in those years, the great bell in the State House tower cracked again, and could no longer be rung properly. It was repaired, and rung once more, in 1846, for Washington's birthday, and then the crack grew longer, and the bell fell silent, for good.

But by then, its message had gone out to the whole world.

Proclaim liberty throughout all the land, unto all the inhabitants thereof.
