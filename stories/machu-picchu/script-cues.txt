Tonight we travel more than five hundred years back in time, high into the mountains of South America. We go to the land of the Inca, around the year 1460. On a narrow ridge between two green peaks, something remarkable is being built.

[long-pause]

It is early morning, and the air is cool and damp. Clouds fill the deep valleys below the ridge like a soft white sea. The sharp green mountains rise out of the clouds like islands. Far below, a river is rushing over the rocks, but you cannot see it. You can only hear its low and steady roar.

[pause]

On the ridge itself stand walls of grey stone. There are houses and temples and long stone stairways. Steps of farmland climb up the slopes like a giant green staircase. Some of the buildings are finished, with thick roofs of golden grass. Others are still only half built, with stones waiting beside them. Thin smoke rises from a few small cooking fires. And here and there, people are already beginning their day's work.

[pause]

Later, this place will be called Machu Picchu. In the language of the Inca, that means old peak. It is one of the most beautiful places on earth. And for a long time, it was also one of the most mysterious.

[pause]

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

{img:inca-stonemasons-shaping-granite-blocks-terraces-mountain-morning}
For a long time, people asked many questions about this place. Who built Machu Picchu, so high up on a mountain? How did they move the heavy stones without wheels or iron tools? Why did they choose this narrow ridge above the clouds? And why was it forgotten for hundreds of years afterwards?

[pause]

Some people made up wild stories to answer those questions. But today, archaeologists know a great deal about the real builders. They have studied the walls stone by stone. They have read the old Spanish writings about the Inca. They have even found the graves of people who lived here. Slowly, the real story has come back to light.

[pause]

It is a story of ordinary people as much as kings. Farmers and herders, weavers and stone cutters, cooks and runners. People who carried baskets of soil up steep paths and sang songs in the evening. Tonight we will spend our time with them.

[pause]

{img:inca-empire-andes-mountains-roads-villages-terraces-vast-landscape}
We will travel through the Inca Empire, the biggest empire the Americas have ever known. We will walk along the great stone roads and visit the golden city of Cusco. We will cross a bridge made of woven grass and meet the runners who carry news over the mountains. We will meet the emperor who ordered Machu Picchu to be built. And we will follow a young stonemason called Amaru as he helps to build it.

[pause]

Much of our evening will be quiet, as daily life usually is. We will cook potatoes and watch llamas graze and listen to grandmothers telling stories. Only now and then will something more exciting happen. A heavy stone to move, perhaps, or a swaying bridge to cross.

[long-pause]

{img:llamas-grazing-high-andes-snow-peaks-shepherd-girl-morning}
So let us begin high in the mountains, in the clear morning air. The sky is a deep blue and the highest peaks are white with snow. On a grassy slope, a small herd of llamas is grazing slowly. They have little coloured ribbons tied in their ears. A shepherd girl sits on a rock nearby, wrapped in a warm woven shawl. She is playing a small flute made of bone. The soft notes float out across the valley, and the llamas lift their heads to listen.

[long-pause]

{img:inca-emperor-sapa-inca-litter-carried-procession-gold-feathers}
The Inca are a people of the Andes, the long mountain range of South America. In the year 1460, they rule the biggest empire the Americas have ever seen. But it was not always like that.

[pause]

For a long time, the Inca were a small people living around the city of Cusco. There were many peoples in the Andes in those days. Each had its own valleys and lords and gods. The Inca were only one of them, and not even the strongest.

[long-pause]

Then, a little before our story, everything changed. A powerful rival people marched toward Cusco with a large army. The old Inca ruler did not think he could win. He left the city with his chosen heir and went to a safe place in the hills.

[pause]

But one of his younger sons refused to leave. He gathered the people who stayed behind and prepared to defend the city. Old stories say even the stones of the fields rose up to help him. Against all hope, Cusco held, and the rival army was driven away.

[pause]

That brave son became the new ruler of the Inca. He took the name Pachacuti, which means he who turns the world around. And he truly did turn it around. Under Pachacuti and his sons, the Inca joined one people after another to their kingdom. Some joined through gifts and marriages and promises. Others were brought in by force.

[pause]

In less than a hundred years, the empire stretched for more than four thousand kilometres. It ran along the mountains from what is now Colombia in the north to Chile in the south. Perhaps ten million people lived under Inca rule.

[pause]

{img:inca-empire-four-quarters-map-like-andes-coast-jungle-cusco-centre}
The Inca called their empire Tawantinsuyu, which means the land of the four parts. The empire was divided into four great regions, one in each direction. At the centre, where the four parts met, lay Cusco. People called it the navel of the world.

[pause]

The ruler of it all is called the Sapa Inca, which means the only Inca. People believe he is the son of the Sun itself. When he travels, he is carried on a golden seat by strong men. He wears the finest cloth, with gold and bright feathers from the forest. Nobody may look him straight in the eye. People bow low as he passes.

[long-pause]

But the emperor is far away from most people's lives. Most people in the empire are farmers living in small villages. Their houses stand in the valleys and on the green mountainsides. They grow potatoes up high and maize lower down. They keep llamas and alpacas for wool and for carrying loads.

[pause]

{img:inca-family-farmers-mountain-terraces-potatoes-maize-highlands}
A farming family rises early and works hard in the fields all day. The father turns the soil with a long wooden foot plough. The mother spins wool as she walks to the fields. The children chase birds away from the young plants and look after the animals. In the evening, everyone gathers around the fire to eat and talk.

[pause]

Most of these families have never seen the emperor. But they know his roads, which run past their villages. They know his storehouses full of food on the hillsides. And they know that each year, some of them must go and work for him for a while. That is simply how life is in the land of the four parts.

[pause]

The Inca were also very orderly people. They counted every family carefully. They grouped people in tens and hundreds and thousands, each group with its own leader. Every leader answered to a higher one, all the way up to Cusco. It was a little like a huge ladder reaching from every village to the emperor's palace.

[long-pause]

{img:andes-mountains-snow-peaks-deep-valleys-cloud-forest-river-gorge}
The Andes are the longest mountain chain in the whole world. They run down the western side of South America like a great stone spine. Some of the peaks rise more than six thousand metres high. Their tops are covered in snow and ice all year round.

[pause]

It is a land of big differences that lie very close together. High up, there are cold windy grasslands where only tough grass grows. A little lower, there are warm green valleys with rivers and fields of maize. And on the eastern side, the mountains drop steeply into a warm, wet forest. That forest is full of tall trees, bright birds and drifting mist.

[long-pause]

You can sometimes walk from the snow to the warm forest in a single day. In the morning you wear a thick woollen cloak against the frost. By the afternoon you are walking among orchids and butterflies in a sticky heat. People in the Andes are used to this. They simply add or take off layers as they go.

[pause]

Living up here is not easy, though. The air high in the mountains is thin, which means there is less of it to breathe. Visitors from the lowlands often feel tired and dizzy when they arrive. But the people of the Andes have lived here for thousands of years. Their bodies have slowly adapted to the thin air. They have strong hearts and deep chests. They can work hard at heights where others can barely walk.

[pause]

The weather can also be harsh. The nights are often freezing, while the sun burns your skin at midday. Storms and hail can arrive without much warning. Now and then, the ground shakes in an earthquake. In the rainy season, landslides can wash away paths and fields.

[pause]

{img:condor-soaring-over-andes-valley-wings-wide-clouds-below}
Yet people have learned to live well in this land. They grow different crops at different heights on the same mountain. Potatoes and quinoa grow up high where it is cold. Maize and beans grow in the warmer valleys below. Fruit and coca leaves come from the edges of the forest. Families and villages trade with each other up and down the slopes. That way everyone has a little of everything.

[pause]

High above the valleys flies the condor, one of the largest birds in the world. Its wings stretch more than three metres from tip to tip. It glides on the wind for hours without flapping, turning slow circles over the peaks. For the people of the Andes, the condor is a sacred bird. They say it carries messages between the world of people and the world above.

[long-pause]

{img:apus-mountain-spirits-snow-peak-offering-stones-andean-pass}
The mountains themselves are sacred too. The great snowy peaks are called apus. People believe each apu is a powerful spirit that watches over the valleys below. The apus send the water that flows down from their melting snow. Without that water, nothing would grow. So the apus must be honoured and thanked.

[pause]

When travellers cross a high mountain pass, they stop for a moment. They pick up a small stone or a coca leaf and add it to a pile at the top. It is a little gift for the mountain, and a thank you for a safe journey. Over hundreds of years, some of these piles have grown into small hills of stone.

[pause]

An old woman crossing a pass with her grandson might pause beside such a pile. She shows him how to hold the leaf and blow gently across it toward the peak. Then she says a few quiet words to the mountain. The boy copies her carefully. One day, he will show his own grandchildren the same thing.

[long-pause]

{img:cusco-inca-capital-stone-walls-plaza-mountains-around-afternoon}
At the heart of the empire lies the city of Cusco. It sits in a high green valley, more than three thousand metres above the sea. Mountains rise on every side of it. Two small rivers flow through the city between neat stone walls. And around them, the Inca have built a city of stone.

[long-pause]

The centre of Cusco is a huge open square. The Inca call it the place of joy and rest. On feast days, thousands of people gather there to dance and sing and eat. The ground of the square is covered with fine sand. That sand was carried all the way up from the coast of the sea. It reminds everyone that the empire reaches from the mountains to the ocean.

[pause]

Around the square stand the palaces of the emperors. Each new emperor builds a palace of his own. When he passes away, his palace does not go to the next ruler. His family keeps living there, and they keep caring for him. His body is carefully preserved and dressed in fine clothes. Servants bring him food and drink, as if he were still alive. On feast days, he is carried out to sit with the living. In Cusco, the old rulers never really leave.

[pause]

{img:cusco-street-inca-stone-wall-trapezoid-doorway-people-llamas}
The walls of Cusco are famous throughout the empire. They are built of large stones that fit together without any mortar between them. Mortar is the paste that builders use to stick stones or bricks together. Here, the stones fit so tightly that a knife blade cannot slide between them. Some stones have twelve corners or more, and still they fit their neighbours perfectly. Doors and windows are shaped like trapezoids. That means they are wider at the bottom than at the top. This shape helps the walls stand firm during earthquakes.

[long-pause]

The streets of Cusco are narrow and straight, with little stone channels running down the middle. Clean water flows through them from the hills. Llamas walk past with loads of firewood on their backs. Women sit in doorways spinning wool and talking. Children run between the walls, chasing each other and laughing. The smell of roasting maize drifts from a courtyard.

[pause]

People in Cusco wear their best clothes when they go out. Each person's hat or headband shows where their family comes from. A man from the high grasslands wears a different hat from a man of the coast. So a busy street in Cusco is full of many different hats.

[pause]

{img:sacsayhuaman-zigzag-giant-stone-walls-hill-above-cusco-clouds}
Some people say the city is shaped like a puma, a large mountain cat. The city itself is the body of the cat. Where the two rivers meet is its tail. And on the hill above the city stands its head, a great stone building called Sacsayhuamán. Its walls zigzag across the hillside like the teeth of the puma. Some of its stones are taller than two people. A few of them weigh more than a hundred tons.

[long-pause]

In the year 1460, Cusco is growing quickly. Pachacuti is rebuilding much of it in fine stone. Workers come from every corner of the empire to help. There are stone cutters and builders and carriers. The best stonemasons learn their skills here, in the yards of Cusco.

[pause]

A young man called Amaru grew up in a village near the city. As a little boy, he played among the great stones in the building yards. He watched the masons shaping blocks with their round hammerstones. He listened to the old men arguing about which stone would fit where. He did not know it then, but one day he would be sent to build something new. Far away down the valley, on a ridge in the clouds.

[long-pause]

{img:coricancha-sun-temple-gold-covered-walls-courtyard-priests-sunlight}
In Cusco stands the most important temple of the whole empire. It is called the Coricancha, which means the golden enclosure. It is the temple of the Sun, the great god of the Inca. Its walls are built of the most perfect stone in all of Cusco. And they were once covered with gold.

[long-pause]

The old Spanish writers describe it with amazement. They say sheets of gold covered the walls of the main rooms. There were hundreds of these sheets, each of them heavy. In the room of the Sun, a great golden image of the Sun hung on the wall. When the morning light came through the doorway, the gold shone so brightly that people had to look away.

[pause]

There were rooms for other gods too, each with its own colour and metal. The room of the Moon was covered with silver. There were rooms for the stars, for the rainbow and for thunder. Priests looked after each room and kept its lamps and offerings in order.

[pause]

The priests of the Sun live in houses near the temple. They rise before dawn to greet the Sun with songs. They burn offerings of maize and coca leaves and fine cloth. They keep the calendar of feasts for the whole year. People come to them with questions about planting and harvests and the will of the gods.

[pause]

{img:inca-golden-garden-life-size-maize-llamas-gold-silver-courtyard}
Beside the temple there was a garden unlike any other in the world. It was a garden made of gold and silver. There were stalks of maize made of gold, with silver leaves and golden cobs. There were golden llamas with their shepherds beside them. There were golden butterflies, birds and flowers. All of it shone and sparkled in the mountain sunlight.

[long-pause]

Why did the Inca use so much gold? For them, gold was not money at all. The Inca did not use money. They traded goods and paid for things with work. Gold was something holy. They called gold the sweat of the Sun, and silver the tears of the Moon. These metals belonged to the gods and to the emperor. They were used to honour the gods, not to buy bread or cloth.

[pause]

A young priest in training might spend his first years simply sweeping the temple courtyard. Each morning he watches the older priests at their work. He learns the long prayers by heart, one line at a time. He learns to read the shadows of the Sun on the walls. Slowly, over many years, he is trusted with more important tasks.

[pause]

{img:priests-coricancha-watching-sunrise-solstice-window-cusco-mountains}
The priests watch the Sun very carefully through the year. On the hills around Cusco, they have set up stone pillars. When the Sun rises or sets beside a certain pillar, they know what time of year it is. Then they tell the farmers it is time to plant, or time to harvest.

[long-pause]

Today, almost all the gold of the Coricancha is gone. Later, a church was built on top of the old temple. Earthquakes have shaken Cusco many times since then. Parts of the church have cracked and fallen down. But the Inca walls underneath have never moved. They are still standing smooth and perfect, five hundred years after they were built.

[long-pause]

{img:pachacuti-inca-emperor-portrait-royal-headband-red-fringe-gold}
The man who ordered Machu Picchu to be built is the emperor Pachacuti. We have already met him as a young prince. He was the one who stayed to defend Cusco when everyone else had fled. After that great day, he became the ruler of the Inca. And he changed almost everything about his people's world.

[long-pause]

Pachacuti added many new lands to his kingdom. He rebuilt Cusco in fine stone. He set up the great roads and the storehouses along them. He made rules about work and food and travel. Much of what we now think of as Inca really began with him.

[pause]

The emperor wears a special sign of his position. It is a woven band around his head with a red fringe hanging over his forehead. Only the Sapa Inca may wear it. It is his crown. When people see the red fringe coming, they bow down low to the ground.

[pause]

Pachacuti is a great leader in war. But he is also a builder and a thinker and a man who loves beauty. Old stories say he wrote poems and prayers to the Sun. He watched the stars at night from the hills around Cusco. And he loved the mountains and valleys of his land more than anything.

[pause]

There is an old story about him from his youth. Before the great battle for Cusco, he went to pray beside a spring. As he looked into the water, a shining crystal fell in. Inside the crystal, he saw a strange figure with rays like the sun around its head. It told him he would become a great ruler. Pachacuti kept that crystal all his life, the story says. Whenever he looked into it, he could see what was coming.

[pause]

{img:inca-royal-estate-planning-pachacuti-architects-model-clay-ridge}
Like other Inca rulers, Pachacuti owns several royal estates. These are country homes away from the noise of the busy capital. Each estate has houses, temples, gardens and fields. There are servants to look after it all. The emperor can go there to rest, to hunt or to hold great feasts. When he passes away, his estate will belong to his family forever.

[long-pause]

Pachacuti wants one estate to be more special than all the others. To the north of Cusco, along the sacred river, there is a high mountain ridge. It is surrounded by green peaks and drifting clouds. It stands above a roaring river and close to the warm forest with its birds and flowers. He has seen it, and he wants to build there.

[pause]

The planners make a model of the ridge out of clay. They sit around it with the emperor and talk for a long time. Where will the temples go? Where will the emperor's own house stand? Where will the fields and the fountains be? They press little stones into the clay to mark each building.

[pause]

{img:pachacuti-standing-on-ridge-looking-at-peaks-clouds-advisers}
Then the emperor travels to the ridge to see it for himself. He stands on the narrow saddle of land and looks around slowly. Clouds drift past below his feet. The peaks rise on every side. His advisers stand quietly behind him, a little worried.

[pause]

They can see the problems clearly. The ridge is narrow and very steep. There is no flat ground anywhere. The rock is full of cracks. It rains a great deal here. How could anyone build a royal estate on such a place?

[long-pause]

But the emperor has made up his mind. And when the Sapa Inca decides something, it will be done. The planners bow and return to Cusco to think. They have been given the hardest building task of their lives. And they have no idea yet how they will manage it.

[long-pause]

{img:mysterious-machu-picchu-ruins-mist-overgrown-question-mood}
Who really built Machu Picchu? For a long time, that simple question had many strange answers.

[pause]

When the outside world first learned about Machu Picchu, about a hundred years ago, people were amazed. How could anyone build such a perfect place so high on a mountain? The builders had no iron tools and no wheels. They had no horses or oxen to pull heavy loads. Some people thought the city must be much older than the Inca. Others thought it was built by some lost people nobody had heard of. A few even told stories about giants or visitors from the stars.

[long-pause]

But the archaeologists kept looking, carefully and patiently. Archaeologists are people who study the past by digging up old things and looking at them closely. Slowly, they found the real answer to the question. Machu Picchu was built by the Inca. And it was built by people, thousands of them, working with their own hands.

[pause]

How can they be so sure? The walls are built in exactly the same style as Inca walls in Cusco. The pots and tools found there are Inca pots and tools. Old Spanish papers mention an estate of Pachacuti in exactly this area. And scientists have measured the age of bones and seeds found on the ridge. They show that people lived at Machu Picchu from about 1420 to about 1530. That is just the time of the Inca Empire.

[pause]

{img:archaeologist-studying-inca-wall-joints-notebook-machu-picchu-light}
The graves at Machu Picchu tell another part of the story. More than a hundred people were buried on the slopes around the city. Scientists studied their bones and especially their teeth. Your teeth keep a quiet record of where you grew up as a child. They remember the water you drank and the food you ate. And the teeth of these people held a surprise.

[long-pause]

The people buried at Machu Picchu came from all over the empire. Some grew up on the hot dry coast. Some came from the cold high grasslands. Others came from the shores of a great lake far to the south, or from the warm forest. They were not all from one village or one valley. They were brought here from far away to live and work on the emperor's estate.

[pause]

They were weavers and farmers, builders and cooks, herders and servants. They spoke different languages at home and wore different clothes. And yet they lived together here, on one small ridge in the clouds.

[pause]

{img:diverse-andean-people-arriving-mountain-path-bundles-different-clothes}
Imagine a group of them arriving for the first time. They come up the steep path with bundles on their backs. A young woman from the lake country looks around in wonder at the peaks. A man from the coast shivers in the cool mountain air. A boy from the forest stares at the snow on the distant mountains. He has never seen snow before in his life.

[pause]

So the answer to our question is this. The emperor Pachacuti ordered Machu Picchu. His master builders planned it. And thousands of ordinary people from all over the Andes built it, one stone at a time. Tonight, let us go and meet some of them.

[long-pause]

{img:narrow-ridge-between-peaks-before-construction-forest-urubamba-river-below}
The ridge where Machu Picchu will stand is narrow and very steep. On one side, the mountain falls almost straight down to the river far below. On the other side, the slope is just as steep. The ridge sits between two peaks like a saddle on the back of a horse.

[pause]

To build on a place like this, the workers must first make it safe. And much of that work will never be seen by anyone.

[long-pause]

Deep under the ridge, the rock is broken by great cracks in the earth. Two of these cracks cross each other right beneath the place where the city will stand. In a way, that is dangerous. But it also helps the builders. The cracks have broken the hard granite into many loose pieces. Those pieces are easy to pick up and shape. It is as if the mountain has prepared a quarry for them.

[pause]

But the cracks also bring water, and a great deal of it. This is a very rainy place. Almost two metres of rain fall here every year. If all that water soaked into the ground, the whole ridge could slide away. Walls would crack and buildings would tumble down the slope. So before anything else, the builders must solve the problem of water.

[pause]

{img:inca-workers-digging-deep-foundations-crushed-stone-layers-ridge}
They dig deep into the ridge. At the bottom, they lay thick layers of broken stone and gravel. Above that, they lay smaller stones. On top of those comes soil. Rainwater can now run down through these layers quickly. It flows away into hidden channels and out of the city.

[pause]

Some experts say that more than half of all the work at Machu Picchu lies under the ground. Nobody sees it, and nobody admires it. But without it, nothing on the ridge would still be standing today.

[long-pause]

The work is slow and steady. Men dig with wooden tools and bronze bars. Others carry the broken rock away in baskets on their backs. Each basket is tipped out, and the carrier walks back for another. Hour after hour, day after day, the deep layers grow.

[pause]

In the evenings, the workers rest by their fires and eat potatoes and maize. They talk about home and their families. One old man always tells the same joke about a llama that would not climb a hill. Everyone has heard it a dozen times. And everyone still laughs.

[pause]

{img:terraces-being-built-mountainside-workers-carrying-soil-baskets}
Next, the builders must make flat ground where there is none. So they build stone walls across the slope and fill the space behind them with stones and soil. Each wall holds up a flat strip of land. These strips are called terraces. On the steepest slopes, they rise one above another like a great staircase. Some terraces will hold buildings. Others will be used for growing food.

[long-pause]

It takes years just to prepare the ridge. Year after year, the workers dig and carry and lay stone. The rains come and go. The clouds drift past. And slowly, the wild steep ridge turns into something new. It becomes a place where people can safely build. It is ready, at last, for the stonemasons.

[long-pause]

{img:machu-picchu-quarry-granite-boulders-workers-splitting-stone}
At Machu Picchu, the builders do not need to bring stone from far away. Most of it is already there on the ridge.

[pause]

In the middle of the ridge lies a great tumbled heap of broken granite. There are huge boulders and smaller rocks, all jumbled together. This is the quarry. Some blocks only have to be moved a few dozen metres to reach the place where they are needed.

[long-pause]

Granite is a very hard stone, grey and speckled with tiny crystals. It does not crumble easily, which makes it good for building. But it is also very hard to cut. The Inca have no iron tools. Their tools are made of bronze, which is a mixture of copper and tin. And above all, they use other stones.

[pause]

To split the granite, the workers look for natural cracks in the rock. They hammer wedges into these cracks. Sometimes they carve a line of small holes and drive wedges into those. Slowly, with patience, the rock splits along the line. Some blocks in the quarry still show these little holes today. They were started and never finished.

[pause]

{img:unfinished-block-quarry-wedge-holes-line-grass-growing-mist}
Not every stone is used in the end. Some blocks crack in the wrong place. Some have a hidden flaw deep inside. Some turn out to be simply too big to move. These stones are left lying in the quarry. Today visitors can still see them, half shaped and waiting. It looks as if the builders put down their tools yesterday and went home for supper.

[long-pause]

To shape the stone, the masons use hammerstones. These are round, very hard stones about the size of a fist or a melon. A mason holds one in both hands and pounds the granite, again and again. Each blow knocks off a tiny chip or a puff of dust. Very slowly, the block begins to take shape.

[pause]

The hammerstones come from the river far below the ridge. They are smooth river stones of an even harder rock. Boys carry them up the long steep path in baskets. Many of these hammerstones have been found at Machu Picchu. There are big ones for rough work and small ones for fine work. Some are worn smooth from years of use.

[pause]

{img:hammerstones-river-rocks-pile-different-sizes-mason-workshop}
The quarry is a noisy place from morning until evening. Tap, tap, tap, the hammerstones go. The sound echoes back from the mountains around the ridge. Dust rises in the slanting morning sunlight. The masons work side by side, sweating and talking a little. Now and then someone starts a working song, and the others join in.

[long-pause]

At midday, a woman brings up a large pot of soup and a basket of roasted maize. The masons put down their hammerstones gladly. They sit on the warm rocks and eat slowly, looking out at the clouds. A hummingbird darts past, green and shining. Then it is back to work again. They are building something for the emperor himself. And every one of them wants it to be perfect.

[long-pause]

{img:young-inca-stonemason-pounding-block-hammerstone-older-master-watching}
Let us meet the young stonemason. His name is Amaru, which means snake. The snake is a sacred animal in the Andes, a sign of wisdom and of the world below.

[pause]

Amaru is seventeen years old. He comes from a village near Cusco. His father was a stonemason, and so was his grandfather. When Amaru was small, he played in the stone yards of the capital. He learned to tell good stone from bad before he could even lift a hammerstone. At fourteen, he began to learn the craft properly.

[long-pause]

Now he has been sent to Machu Picchu, which is a great honour. Only good masons are chosen to work on the emperor's estate. He walked for several days down the sacred valley with a group of other workers. When he first saw the ridge in the clouds, he stopped and stared for a long time. He had never seen anything so beautiful, or so steep.

[pause]

His master is an old mason called Kusi. Kusi has built walls in Cusco for the emperor himself. His hands are rough and covered with small scars. His back is a little bent from years of work. But his eyes are still sharp. He can look at a stone and see exactly where it should go.

[pause]

{img:mason-fitting-stone-trial-lifting-checking-gaps-adjusting-wall}
The finest Inca walls are made of stones that fit together perfectly. How do the masons manage it? Slowly, and with great patience. First the mason shapes a stone roughly. Then the workers lift it onto the wall. The mason looks carefully at where it touches and where there are gaps. They lift it off again, and he pounds away a little more. Then they try again, over and over, until it fits.

[long-pause]

Some stones are tried dozens of times before they are right. It can take days, or even weeks, for one large stone. The edges of each stone are slightly rounded and sunk in. That makes the joints look like thin lines of shadow. Each stone seems to swell out gently, like a soft pillow.

[pause]

{img:perfect-inca-wall-pillow-stones-tight-joints-shadow-lines-close}
There is a clever reason for all this care. The Andes often shake with earthquakes. Walls held together with mortar crack and fall down. But Inca walls without mortar can move a little. During an earthquake, the stones jump and shiver. Then they settle back into their places. That is why so many Inca walls still stand after five hundred years.

[pause]

Not every wall at Machu Picchu is this perfect, though. Most walls are simpler, with rougher stones set in clay. Those are much quicker to build. The most perfect stonework is kept for the most important places. The temples, and the house of the emperor.

[long-pause]

Amaru works on the simpler walls at first. He is careful and does not want to make mistakes. One afternoon, Kusi watches him trying to fit a stone. Amaru tries three times, but there is still a gap. He feels hot and frustrated and wants to give up.

[pause]

Kusi walks over slowly and puts his rough hand on the stone. Listen to the stone, he says quietly. It will tell you where it wants to go. Amaru looks at him, and then at the stone again. He runs his fingers along its edges. And slowly, he begins to see what his master means.

[pause]

{img:old-master-hand-on-stone-teaching-young-mason-evening-light}
That evening, as the sun goes down behind the peaks, Amaru fits a stone on the very first try. He looks up hopefully. Kusi nods at him, just once, without a word. But for Amaru, it is the best moment of his life so far.

[long-pause]

{img:mita-workers-arriving-construction-site-groups-village-leaders-ropes}
Amaru is a skilled mason. But most people building Machu Picchu are not masons at all. They are farmers who have come to do their turn of work for the empire.

[pause]

In the Inca Empire, there is no money. People do not pay taxes with coins. They pay with their work. Every family must give some of its time to the state each year. This system is called mita, which means a turn. Everyone takes their turn, and then goes home again.

[long-pause]

So every year, groups of men come from villages all over the empire. Each village sends its share of workers. The village leader walks with them and keeps an eye on them. They stay for a few weeks or a few months. They carry stone and dig soil. They build walls and roads and terraces. They cut wood in the forest below. Then they return home, and another group comes to take their place.

[pause]

It is hard work in a hard place. But the state takes care of them while they are here. They are given food and maize beer. They sleep in simple houses with roofs of grass. They are given new clothes. And when their turn is finished, there is often a feast before they leave.

[pause]

{img:workers-carrying-stone-on-backs-tumpline-steep-stairs-terrace}
How do they move heavy stones without wheels? They use ropes, wooden rollers, ramps of earth and long wooden poles. Teams of men pull together on the ropes while others lever from behind. Smaller stones are carried on the back. A strap goes around the forehead to help hold the weight. Some of the biggest stones at Machu Picchu weigh several tons. Moving one takes many people and a great deal of patience.

[long-pause]

The workers come from many different peoples. At home they speak different languages. In the Inca Empire, each people wears its own kind of hat. So the building site at Machu Picchu is full of different hats. There are round woollen caps and tall felt hats. There are headbands with feathers and cloths tied in special ways.

[pause]

But they all learn the language of the Inca, called Quechua. It is the language of the whole empire. Millions of people in the Andes still speak it today.

[pause]

One of the workers is an older man from the shores of the great lake in the south. He has done his turn of work many times in his life. He has helped build roads, storehouses and bridges. He has seen half of the empire. In the evenings, the young men gather around him to hear his stories. He tells them about the hot coast with its strange fish. He tells them about the warm forest full of parrots and monkeys. He tells them about the cold high plain where the wind never stops blowing. The young men listen with wide eyes. Most of them have never been more than a day's walk from home.

[pause]

{img:workers-resting-evening-sharing-food-different-hats-fire-mountains}
Around the fires, the workers share potatoes and maize and cups of maize beer. They sing songs from their own villages, one after another. Somebody has brought a little drum, and someone else a flute. Above them, the snowy peaks glow pale in the moonlight. Many of them miss their families. But they are proud, too. They are building something for the emperor. Something that will last.

[long-pause]

{img:machu-picchu-agricultural-terraces-green-steps-mountainside-crops}
On the steep sides of Machu Picchu there are terraces. They are long, narrow steps of flat land, each held up by a stone wall. Together they climb the mountain like a giant green staircase.

[pause]

The Inca are masters at building terraces. They build them all over the Andes, on slopes so steep that nothing could grow there otherwise. With terraces, they turn whole mountainsides into farmland.

[long-pause]

A terrace is much more than a flat step of earth. It is built carefully in layers. At the bottom lie big stones. On top of them come smaller stones, then gravel, then sand. And on the very top lies rich soil. Some of that soil was carried up from the valley far below, basket by basket, on people's backs.

[pause]

The layers let rainwater drain away slowly. The soil stays damp, but never soaked. The stone walls soak up the warmth of the sun during the day. At night, they give that warmth back to the plants. On cold nights, that small extra warmth can save a whole crop from frost.

[pause]

{img:terrace-cross-section-layers-stones-gravel-sand-soil-diagram-like}
On the terraces of Machu Picchu, people grow maize, potatoes and other plants. There is enough to feed some of the people who live on the ridge. But not all of them. More food has to be carried up from the farms in the valley.

[pause]

The terrace gardeners work slowly and carefully. In the morning, a woman walks along one terrace pulling weeds. A little boy follows her, carrying a basket. She shows him which plants to pull out and which to leave. He learns quickly, and soon he is helping her properly.

[long-pause]

The terraces also protect the mountain itself. Without them, heavy rain would wash the soil off the slopes. With them, the rain is slowed down and held in place. The terraces work like a strong net holding the whole ridge together.

[pause]

One night during the building, there is a terrible storm. Rain pours down for hours and water rushes down the mountain. The workers lie awake in their houses, listening to it roar. Will the new terraces hold? In the morning, they hurry outside to look. One small wall near the edge has slipped. Stones and soil lie scattered on the slope below. But all the other terraces have held firm. The builders rebuild the fallen wall, deeper and stronger this time. It never falls again.

[pause]

{img:moray-circular-terraces-bowl-rings-green-grass-andes-later}
Not far from Machu Picchu, in another valley, the Inca built very strange terraces. They form great circles that go down into the ground like a bowl. Each ring is a little warmer or cooler than the one above it. Some experts think the Inca used these rings to test which crops grow best at each temperature. It was a kind of outdoor laboratory.

[long-pause]

The terraces of Machu Picchu have lasted for more than five hundred years. They have survived heavy rains, earthquakes and centuries without anyone to care for them. And still they stand, green steps on a green mountain, holding the ridge in place.

[long-pause]

{img:inca-stone-fountain-channel-water-flowing-machu-picchu-steps}
A city on a mountain needs fresh water. And Machu Picchu has a spring of its own.

[pause]

High on the slope above the ridge, clear cold water comes out of the rock. The Inca engineers found it while they were planning the city. Then they built a stone channel to bring the water down to where people would live.

[long-pause]

The channel is about seven hundred and fifty metres long. It is cut into stone and lined with stone. It runs gently downhill all the way. If it were too steep, the water would rush and splash out. If it were too flat, the water would stop and go stale. The slope is just right. The engineers measured it with great care, using only strings, sticks and their own eyes.

[pause]

When the water reaches the city, it flows into a row of stone fountains. There are sixteen of them, one after another, down a long stairway. At each fountain, the water pours from a little stone spout into a basin. People fill their jars there. Then the water flows on to the next fountain below.

[pause]

{img:woman-filling-clay-jar-stone-fountain-spout-morning-light-terraces}
The first fountain stands next to the house of the emperor. So the emperor always gets the cleanest water first. Then the water flows past the temples, and then past the houses of the workers. At the bottom, it flows away down the mountain.

[pause]

Every morning, women and girls come to the fountains with their clay jars. They wait their turn, chatting about the weather and their families. They fill the jars and lift them carefully onto their backs. Then they walk home along the narrow stone paths, careful not to spill a drop.

[long-pause]

Remember that it rains a great deal at Machu Picchu. So the builders needed to bring water in, but also to get water out. They built more than a hundred drains. These are small channels in the walls, the floors and the stairs. Rainwater runs into them and away. It sinks down into the layers of gravel under the city and flows out down the mountain.

[pause]

{img:rain-falling-machu-picchu-water-channels-drains-flowing-misty}
The drains work so well that, after five hundred years, Machu Picchu still does not flood. When heavy rain falls, it all flows quietly away. Engineers today admire this hidden system very much. Some say it is the most impressive thing about the whole place.

[long-pause]

On a rainy afternoon, the whole ridge is filled with the soft sound of running water. It trickles in the channels and gurgles in the drains. It drips from the grass roofs and splashes in the fountains. People sit inside their houses with their weaving or their tools. They listen to the rain and wait for it to pass.

[pause]

Amaru likes to sit by the fountains in the evening when his work is done. He listens to the water splashing and trickling from basin to basin. After a long day of pounding stone, it sounds very peaceful. Sometimes he dips his tired hands into the cold water. And he thinks of the river near his home village. He thinks of his mother, who will be waiting for news of him.

[long-pause]

{img:andean-potato-varieties-colours-shapes-baskets-market-highland}
The most important food in the Andes is the potato. The potato comes from these very mountains. People here were growing it for thousands of years before anyone else in the world had seen one.

[pause]

The Inca do not grow just one kind of potato. They grow hundreds of different kinds. There are small ones and big ones, round ones and long ones, smooth ones and knobbly ones. There are yellow, white, purple, red and even almost black potatoes. Some taste sweet and some taste a little bitter. Some grow in the warm valleys, and some grow high up where nothing else will.

[long-pause]

Farmers plant potatoes in small mounds of earth. They use a foot plough, which is a long wooden pole with a pointed end and a little step for the foot. The farmer pushes it into the ground with his foot and turns the earth over. Behind him come the women, dropping seed potatoes into the holes. They often work in long rows, singing together as they go.

[pause]

In a farming village, the whole family helps at planting time. Grandparents, parents and children all go out to the fields together. The smallest children carry little baskets of seed potatoes. When everyone is tired, they sit at the edge of the field to rest and share some boiled maize.

[pause]

{img:families-stomping-potatoes-chuno-frost-morning-high-plateau}
The Inca have a very clever way to keep potatoes for a long time. It is called chuño. High in the mountains during the dry season, the nights are freezing and the days are sunny. So people spread potatoes out on the ground. At night, the potatoes freeze. During the day, they thaw. Then the family walks on them with bare feet to squeeze out the water.

[pause]

They do this for several days, freezing and thawing and stomping. In the end, the potatoes become dry and light, almost like small stones. They can be kept for years without going bad. To eat them, you soak them in water and cook them in a stew. In a bad year with a poor harvest, chuño can save a whole village from hunger.

[long-pause]

The Inca also grow maize, but maize does not like the cold. It grows in the warm valleys lower down. Maize is a special food. It is used to make maize beer and is given out at feasts. The emperor's own fields grow a great deal of it.

[pause]

There is also quinoa, a small round seed that grows high in the mountains. It is very good for you. And there are beans, squash, peppers and many other roots and grains. Many of them are still eaten in the Andes today.

[pause]

{img:inca-storehouses-qollqa-hillside-row-stone-buildings-food-stored}
All over the empire, along the roads, there are storehouses. They are rows of small stone buildings on the hillsides, where cool winds keep the food fresh. They are full of chuño, maize and dried meat. Some hold cloth and tools. If a harvest fails, the storehouses can feed the people. If travellers pass by, they can be fed too. The Inca like to plan ahead. Nobody in the empire should go hungry. At least, that is the idea.

[long-pause]

At harvest time, the potato fields are full of people again. They dig carefully with sticks, so the potatoes are not cut. The children shout each time they find a big one. In the evening, the family sits around the fire, roasting the first new potatoes in the hot ashes. They are crisp outside and soft inside. And everybody agrees that nothing tastes quite as good.

[long-pause]

{img:llama-caravan-loads-mountain-path-herders-ribbons-ears-andes}
In the high Andes live animals you will find nowhere else on earth. They are called llamas and alpacas.

[pause]

They are cousins of the camel, but smaller and without humps. They have long necks, soft dark eyes and very long eyelashes. And they are perfect for life in the mountains. They can breathe the thin air easily. They can eat tough mountain grass. And they can walk on narrow rocky paths without slipping.

[long-pause]

Llamas are the bigger of the two. They are used for carrying loads. A llama can carry about thirty kilos on its back, but not more. If you give it too much, it simply lies down and refuses to move. It might even spit at you. Llamas have strong opinions about their work.

[pause]

Long lines of llamas carry goods along the roads of the empire. They carry food, cloth and pots from one valley to another. Sometimes a caravan has hundreds of llamas walking in a row. Their herders walk beside them, singing and whistling to keep them moving. The llamas wear coloured tassels and ribbons in their ears. Each family has its own colours, so they can tell whose llama is whose.

[pause]

{img:alpacas-grazing-high-meadow-soft-wool-colours-white-brown-black}
Alpacas are smaller than llamas and are kept for their wool. Alpaca wool is soft and very warm. It comes in many natural colours, from white and cream to brown, grey and black. The women spin it into thread and weave it into warm clothes and blankets. On cold mountain nights, a good alpaca blanket is a treasure.

[pause]

Once a year, the alpacas are sheared. The herders gently hold each animal still and cut away its thick coat. The animals do not much like it. They grumble and hum and wriggle. Afterwards they look very thin and a little embarrassed. But their wool soon grows back.

[long-pause]

There is also a wild cousin called the vicuña. It is small and shy and lives very high in the mountains. Its wool is the finest in the world, softer than almost anything. Only the emperor may wear cloth made from vicuña wool. Every few years, people gather in large groups and gently round up the wild vicuñas. They cut a little of their wool. Then they let them all run free again.

[pause]

{img:vicuna-wild-herd-high-puna-grass-snow-peak-gentle-light}
For the people of the high Andes, llamas and alpacas are everything. They give wool, meat and dung for the fire. They carry loads. And they are almost part of the family. Children grow up with them and give them names. At festivals, the animals are decorated with flowers and blessed.

[long-pause]

At Machu Picchu, a line of llamas comes up the path every day, carrying food and supplies. Amaru likes to watch them arrive. One white llama with a red tassel in its ear always walks last. And it always stops at the top of the stairs to look at the view, as if it is enjoying it. The workers call it the Inspector. Every day someone says that the Inspector has arrived, and everybody laughs.

[long-pause]

{img:inca-road-qhapaq-nan-stone-steps-mountain-pass-travellers-llamas}
The Inca Empire is held together by its roads. The Inca built a network of roads more than thirty thousand kilometres long. That is almost long enough to go around the whole earth. Two great roads run from north to south, one through the mountains and one along the coast. Many smaller roads link them together.

[long-pause]

The roads are amazing to see. In the mountains, they climb steep slopes with thousands of stone steps. They wind along the edges of cliffs. They cross high passes more than four thousand metres above the sea. In wet places, they are raised on stone walls so they stay dry. In the desert, they are marked by stones and low walls. Some parts are paved with flat stones, while others are hard-packed earth.

[pause]

Along the roads, about every day's walk, there are resting places called tambos. Each tambo has rooms where travellers can sleep. It has storehouses with food and firewood. There are pens for llamas. Officials and messengers can stop there, eat a warm meal and get a good night's rest. Then they go on their way in the morning.

[pause]

{img:tambo-way-station-road-stone-buildings-storehouses-llamas-evening}
The families who look after a tambo are always busy. They cook for travellers and look after the animals. They keep the fires burning and the rooms clean. They hear news from every corner of the empire as people pass through. In the evening, they sit with the travellers around the fire and listen to their stories.

[pause]

Ordinary people may not travel on the roads just for fun. You need a good reason, such as work for the state or a pilgrimage. Guards at the bridges and passes check everyone who comes by. The roads belong to the empire.

[long-pause]

Amaru walked part of the royal road to get to Machu Picchu. He remembers the endless steps going up and up, until his legs shook. Then down and down, until his knees ached. He remembers a cold night at a high tambo, wrapped in his blanket, with frost on the ground in the morning. And he remembers the moment the road turned a corner. Suddenly the deep green valley lay spread out below him, full of drifting clouds. He had never been so tired, or so amazed.

[pause]

{img:sun-gate-inti-punku-view-down-machu-picchu-dawn-light-mist}
Several roads lead to Machu Picchu. One comes from Cusco along the mountains. It passes through other Inca places on the way. It climbs over high passes and goes down through the misty cloud forest. At the very end, it reaches a stone gate high on the mountain above the city. It is called the Sun Gate. From there, a traveller sees Machu Picchu for the first time, far below on its ridge.

[long-pause]

Today, this road is called the Inca Trail, and people from all over the world walk it. It takes about four days. They climb and climb and grow very tired. On the last morning, they get up in the dark and walk to the Sun Gate as the sun rises. They look down at Machu Picchu in the first golden light. Many of them say it is the most beautiful thing they have ever seen.

[long-pause]

{img:chasqui-runner-conch-shell-trumpet-mountain-road-running-fast}
How do you send a message across an empire four thousand kilometres long? There are no horses, no paper letters and no wheels. The Inca have a clever answer. They use runners, called chasquis.

[long-pause]

Along the roads, every few kilometres, there are small huts. In each hut, two or more young men are always waiting. They are chasquis. They have been chosen for their speed and their strong lungs. Most of them have trained since they were boys. And they are always ready to run.

[pause]

When a message comes, a runner sprints along the road toward the next hut. As he gets close, he blows a trumpet made from a large sea shell. The next runner hears the sound and comes out. He starts to run beside the first one. As they run together, the first runner tells him the message out loud. He also hands him a cord with knots, called a quipu. Then the second runner speeds off alone. The first runner stops, out of breath. His part of the journey is done.

[pause]

{img:two-chasqui-runners-passing-message-quipu-running-together-road}
Like a relay race, the message travels on from runner to runner and hut to hut. Each runner only runs a short distance, but as fast as he can. They run by day and by night, in sun and rain and snow. In this way, a message can travel up to two hundred and fifty kilometres in one day. A message from Cusco can reach the far north of the empire in about a week. A walker would need months.

[pause]

The runners do not only carry messages. Sometimes they carry small things too. Old stories say that the emperor in Cusco could eat fresh fish from the sea, two days after it was caught. The runners brought it up from the coast and over the mountains, passing it from hand to hand.

[long-pause]

Being a chasqui is an honour, but it is also hard. The runners must be ready at any hour, even in the middle of the night. If they are too slow, they will be in trouble. The message must always get through.

[pause]

{img:chasqui-hut-roadside-runner-waiting-blanket-night-stars-mountains}
Between messages, life in a chasqui hut is quiet. The runners sit wrapped in their blankets and chew coca leaves to stay awake. They watch the road and the stars. They talk softly about their villages and their families. Now and then one of them dozes off, while the other keeps listening for the trumpet.

[long-pause]

At Machu Picchu, Amaru sometimes hears a conch trumpet echoing faintly across the mountains. Somewhere on the road, a runner is coming with news from Cusco. Everyone stops their work for a moment and looks up. Is the emperor coming to visit? Has something important happened? They wait and wonder, until the news reaches them.

[long-pause]

{img:inca-rope-bridge-grass-woven-deep-gorge-river-below-crossing}
The Andes are full of deep river gorges, with steep cliffs and a roaring river at the bottom. How do the Inca roads cross them? With bridges woven from grass.

[long-pause]

The Inca make their bridges from a tough mountain grass. First, people gather huge amounts of it from the hillsides. They twist it into thin cords. Then they twist those thin cords into thicker ropes. Finally, they twist the ropes into great cables as thick as a person's body. Each cable is strong enough to hold many people at once.

[pause]

The big cables are stretched across the gorge and tied to stone towers on each side. Some cables form the floor of the bridge. Others form handrails, higher up. Smaller ropes join the floor to the handrails, like the sides of a long basket. Then sticks and grass mats are laid along the floor so people can walk across.

[pause]

{img:villagers-twisting-grass-cords-ropes-communal-work-riverbank-andes}
Grass does not last forever. Rain and sun slowly make it weak. So every year, each bridge must be rebuilt. The villages nearby are responsible for their own bridge. Every family brings grass. They sit together on the riverbank, twisting cords and talking and laughing. Children run between the piles of grass. Old women sing as they work. It is hard work, but it is also a kind of festival.

[pause]

When all the ropes are ready, the strongest men pull the new cables across the gorge. The old bridge is taken down and the new one is woven in its place. It takes a few days. And then there is a feast, with music and maize beer.

[long-pause]

The bridges sway and bounce when people walk on them. Far below, the river rushes and foams over the rocks. Many travellers are afraid to cross. Even the Spanish, when they came later, were frightened. Some of them crawled across on their hands and knees. But the bridges are strong. Guards live beside each one. They look after the bridge and make sure it is safe.

[pause]

Amaru crossed a grass bridge on his way to Machu Picchu. He remembers it very well. The swaying under his feet. The roar of the river below. Not looking down. Holding the rope so tightly that his hands hurt. And then the solid ground on the other side. He was so relieved that he laughed out loud. The other workers laughed too, because every one of them had been afraid.

[pause]

{img:new-rope-bridge-crossing-celebration-villagers-cheering-sunlight}
The most wonderful thing is that one of these bridges still exists. It crosses a river gorge south of Cusco. Every year, the people of four nearby villages rebuild it in the old way. They use grass, ropes and their bare hands, just as their ancestors did for more than five hundred years. They work for three or four days. They sing and say prayers to the mountain spirits. When the new bridge is finished, they walk across it and cheer.

[long-pause]

There is another famous Inca bridge near Machu Picchu, but it is not made of grass. It is a narrow path cut into the side of a sheer cliff, high above the valley. In the middle there is a gap about six metres wide. Over that gap, the Inca laid a few wooden planks. If enemies came, the guards could pull the planks away. Then nobody could pass.

[pause]

{img:inca-drawbridge-cliff-path-wooden-planks-gap-sheer-drop-cloud}
Today it is called the Inca Bridge. Visitors can walk along the path and look at it from a safe distance. But nobody is allowed to cross it anymore. Clouds drift past below the planks, and the drop is very deep indeed.

[long-pause]

{img:quipu-knotted-strings-colours-hanging-cord-inca-record-keeper}
The Inca rule a huge empire, and they need to count everything. They count people, llamas, potatoes, cloth and workers. But they do not have writing in the way we do. So how do they keep their records? They use knots.

[long-pause]

The Inca use something called a quipu. It is one long main cord with many smaller strings hanging down from it. Sometimes there are hundreds of strings. They are made in different colours. And they have knots tied in them, of different kinds and in different places.

[pause]

The knots stand for numbers. A knot near the bottom of a string means ones. Higher up, the knots mean tens, then hundreds, then thousands. The Inca count in tens, just as we do. And they even have a way of showing zero. They simply leave an empty space where a knot would be.

[pause]

{img:quipucamayoc-record-keeper-reading-knots-fingers-official-listening}
The colours have meanings too. Perhaps yellow stands for maize and brown for potatoes. Nobody today is completely sure. A quipu can show how many people live in a village. It can show how much food is in a storehouse, or how many llamas a family owns. It can show how much work each village must do for the empire.

[long-pause]

The people who make and read the quipus are special officials. They are called keepers of the knots. They train for many years. They can read a quipu by running their fingers along the strings, almost like reading with their hands.

[pause]

Every village has its own keeper of the knots. He records everything that matters. He sends his quipus to a more important keeper, and so on, all the way up to Cusco. In this way, the emperor's officials know what is happening in every corner of the empire.

[pause]

Amaru's uncle is a keeper of the knots in his home village. When Amaru was small, his uncle let him hold an old quipu. It felt soft and heavy, like a bundle of hair. Amaru ran his fingers along the strings, just as his uncle did. He felt bumps and smooth places, but he could not read a thing. His uncle laughed and said that one day, if he was patient, he would learn. But Amaru chose stone instead of string.

[pause]

{img:inca-quipu-in-museum-case-faded-colours-many-strings-soft-light}
Some experts think quipus could hold more than numbers. Perhaps names, or stories, or history. The Spanish wrote that the keepers could tell long stories from their quipus. But nobody today knows how to read that part. The secret was lost long ago.

[long-pause]

About a thousand quipus have survived until today. They lie in museums around the world, their colours a little faded. Scientists are still trying to understand them. They use computers to compare the knots and the colours. Little by little, they are learning more. Perhaps one day the secret of the knots will be solved. And then the Inca will speak to us again in their own words.

[long-pause]

{img:andean-village-stone-houses-thatch-courtyard-dawn-mountains-smoke}
Let us visit a small village in the valley below Machu Picchu. About thirty families live there. Their houses are made of stone and mud, with roofs of thick grass. They stand around small courtyards on a slope above the river. Terraces climb the hill behind the village. Above them, the mountain disappears into the clouds.

[long-pause]

The family we visit has a father, a mother, three children and a grandmother. The father is a farmer. Last year he did his turn of work at Machu Picchu, carrying stone for two months. The mother farms, weaves and cooks. The children help wherever they can. The grandmother tells stories and keeps a close eye on the guinea pigs.

[pause]

Yes, guinea pigs. In the Andes, people keep guinea pigs inside the house. They run around the kitchen floor, squeaking and nibbling. They eat potato peels and bits of grass. Their small warm bodies help keep the house warm at night. On special days, a guinea pig is cooked as a treat.

[pause]

{img:guinea-pigs-running-kitchen-floor-stone-house-hearth-pots-woman}
The day starts before sunrise, when it is still cold and dark. The mother lights the fire in the little clay stove. She warms up the soup from yesterday, made of potatoes, quinoa and a few herbs. Everyone eats it hot from bowls of clay. Then the father and the eldest son go out to the fields. They carry the foot plough and a little bag of coca leaves.

[pause]

Coca leaves are important in the Andes. People chew them slowly while they work. The leaves give a little energy and help against the cold, hunger and thin air. They are also sacred. People offer them to the mountains and to the earth.

[long-pause]

The girls help their mother at home. They fetch water from the stream and sweep the courtyard. As they walk, they spin wool on little spinning sticks. In the Andes, women are almost always spinning. Walking to the fields, talking with friends or watching the llamas, their hands never stop.

[pause]

{img:andean-girls-spinning-wool-while-walking-path-llamas-mountains}
In the afternoon, the mother weaves at her loom in the courtyard. One end of the loom is tied to a post. The other end is tied around her waist. She leans back to pull the threads tight. She weaves patterns of zigzags and diamonds in red, yellow and black. Her mother taught her these patterns, and her grandmother taught her mother. Now she is teaching her eldest daughter.

[pause]

The little boy is in charge of the guinea pigs and the dog. He gives the guinea pigs fresh grass and chases the dog out of the kitchen. He also guards the drying maize on the roof from the birds. He takes his job very seriously, though he sometimes falls asleep in the sun.

[long-pause]

In the evening, the family eats together around the fire. There are boiled potatoes, toasted maize and a little dried llama meat with chilli sauce. The grandmother tells a story about the fox and the condor. The fox wanted to fly to a feast in the sky, and begged the condor to carry him. But that is a long story, and the children fall asleep before the end.

[pause]

Outside, the night is dark and cold, and the sky is full of stars. High above, on the mountain they cannot see from here, the fires of Machu Picchu are burning.

[long-pause]

{img:women-making-chicha-maize-beer-big-clay-jars-courtyard-steam}
At every feast in the Andes, there is chicha. Chicha is a drink made from maize. It is a kind of beer, thick and yellowish and a little sour. It is not very strong. People drink it every day, and at feasts they drink a great deal of it.

[long-pause]

Chicha is usually made by women. They boil maize in big clay pots over the fire. Then they pour it into huge jars and leave it to ferment for a few days. Some of the jars are taller than a child. Slowly the drink bubbles and changes, until it is ready.

[pause]

Chicha is drunk from cups of wood or clay, and often in pairs. The host fills two cups, one for himself and one for his guest. They drink together, facing each other. That is how friendships are made and kept. Some of these paired cups are beautifully painted with little pictures of people and animals.

[pause]

{img:paired-wooden-cups-kero-painted-toasting-two-men-feast-andes}
The Inca state uses chicha too. When workers come to do their turn of work, they are given food and chicha. At the end, there is a feast for them. The more food and drink the state gives, the happier the workers are. So whole houses of women spend their days making chicha for the empire.

[pause]

Before anyone drinks, a little chicha is poured onto the ground. It is for Pachamama, the Earth Mother. She must always drink first. Many people in the Andes still do this today.

[long-pause]

What else do people eat? Potatoes, always, boiled or roasted or dried. Maize is roasted on the cob or ground into flour. Quinoa is cooked like porridge. There are beans, squash and peppers. Sometimes there is llama meat, often dried in long strips. On feast days, there is guinea pig. There is fish from the lakes and rivers. And there is fruit from the warm valleys, like sweet pale avocados.

[pause]

{img:pachamanca-earth-oven-family-uncovering-steaming-food-hot-stones}
One favourite dish is cooked inside the ground. The family digs a pit and fills it with hot stones from the fire. They lay potatoes, meat and maize on the stones. Then they cover everything with grass and earth. And they wait. The children sit around the little mound, impatient. A few hours later, the father digs it up again. The food comes out soft and smoky and delicious. It is called pachamanca, which means earth pot. People in the Andes still cook it today, especially on feast days.

[long-pause]

At Machu Picchu, the workers eat simple food most days. Potatoes, maize and chicha. Food comes up the mountain every day on the backs of llamas and people. The kitchen fires burn from morning until night. And when the emperor visits, there are great feasts, with roasted meat and fruit and the finest chicha. The workers get a share too, and the whole ridge smells of good cooking.

[long-pause]

{img:aqllakuna-chosen-women-weaving-fine-cloth-courtyard-looms-cusco}
For the Inca, cloth is more precious than gold. That may sound strange, but it is true. Gold belongs to the gods. Cloth is what people give to each other. It is given at weddings, births and funerals. The emperor gives fine cloth as his highest reward. Soldiers are paid in cloth. The gods are dressed in cloth. A rich family is a family with many fine cloths.

[long-pause]

There are two main kinds of cloth. The everyday kind is woven by families at home on simple looms. It is used for clothes, blankets and bags. The finest kind is called cumbi. It is made from the softest alpaca and vicuña wool. It is woven so tightly that it feels smooth and shiny, almost like silk. Some cumbi has more than a hundred threads in a single centimetre.

[pause]

Cumbi is woven with patterns of tiny squares in many colours. Each little square means something. A family, perhaps, or a people, or a message. Nobody today can read them all.

[pause]

{img:cumbi-tunic-tiny-squares-patterns-many-colours-museum-light}
Cumbi is made by special weavers. Some of them are men. But many are women called the chosen women. They are girls chosen from villages across the empire because of their skill. They live together in special houses in the cities. They weave cloth and make chicha for the emperor and the gods. Some stay there all their lives. Others are later married to nobles and officers.

[long-pause]

At Machu Picchu there were weavers too. They were women from many lands, living together on the mountain. Their spindle whorls have been found there. These are little round weights of stone or clay with a hole in the middle. They help the spindle turn smoothly while the wool is spun.

[pause]

{img:spindle-whorls-clay-stone-small-round-found-machu-picchu-hands}
One of the weavers at Machu Picchu is called Kantu. She comes from the shores of the great lake far to the south. She was chosen when she was twelve years old. She has woven for the emperor for six years now. Her fingers are quick and light on the threads.

[pause]

At the moment she is weaving a tunic for the emperor himself. It has already taken her four months. Every morning she sits down at her loom as the sun comes up over the peaks. She works slowly and carefully, square by square. Sometimes she sings softly to herself, an old song from the lake.

[long-pause]

She is nervous, though she does not say so. The emperor will come to Machu Picchu soon. What if he does not like the tunic? So she checks every square again and again. Every colour, every line, every tiny knot. It has to be perfect. In the evenings, she folds it carefully into a cloth and puts it away safely. Then she lies awake for a while, thinking about tomorrow's rows.

[long-pause]

{img:inti-sun-god-golden-disc-face-rays-temple-wall-inca-symbol}
The Inca have many gods, and the greatest of them is the Sun. They call him Inti. He gives light and warmth and makes the crops grow. Without him, there would be nothing at all. And the emperor is his son. That is why the emperor is so important. He is the link between the people and the Sun.

[long-pause]

There is also the Moon, Mama Killa, who is the wife of the Sun. She watches over women and over the calendar of months. There is Illapa, the god of thunder, lightning and rain. He carries a sling. When he swings it round, there is thunder. When he lets go, there is lightning. And there is Viracocha, the creator, who made everything at the beginning of time. Then, the old stories say, he walked away across the sea.

[pause]

But the goddess people speak to most often is Pachamama, the Earth Mother. She is the earth itself. She gives the potatoes, the maize and the grass for the llamas. She must be thanked and cared for. People give her coca leaves, chicha and little gifts. They bury small offerings in the fields. In August, before planting, they give her special gifts. People say she is hungry then and must be fed before she can give.

[pause]

{img:andean-farmers-offering-coca-leaves-earth-pachamama-field-kneeling}
A farming family might make their offering together on a quiet morning. They kneel at the edge of their field. The father digs a small hole with his hands. The mother places coca leaves, a little maize and a few sweets inside. The grandmother pours a little chicha over them. Then they cover the hole again and say a few words of thanks. The children watch closely, so they will know how to do it when they are grown.

[long-pause]

There are also the apus, the spirits of the mountains. And there are the huacas. A huaca is any holy place or holy thing. It might be a spring, a cave, a large rock or a strange old tree. All over the Andes, there are thousands of huacas. People visit them, leave little offerings and ask for help.

[pause]

At Machu Picchu, there are many holy places. On top of a small hill stands a great carved rock called the Intihuatana. The name means the place where the sun is tied. People think it was used to watch the sun and mark the seasons. There is a curved temple with windows that catch the sunrise on the shortest and longest days. Beneath it is a cave, carved inside with great care. And there is a rock shaped just like the mountain behind it, as if it were the mountain's little twin.

[pause]

{img:intihuatana-carved-stone-hilltop-machu-picchu-shadow-sun-peaks}
The Inca do not separate holy things from everyday things. For them, the whole land is alive with spirits. Every stone, river and mountain is someone. When Amaru shapes a stone, he says a quiet word to it first. When a farmer plants a potato, he gives a few coca leaves to the earth.

[long-pause]

And every morning, when the sun rises over the peaks, people at Machu Picchu turn toward it. They lift their hands a little and greet it softly. Then they go about their work for the day, while the light spreads slowly down the mountainsides.

[long-pause]

{img:inti-raymi-festival-cusco-plaza-crowds-emperor-raised-cup-sunrise}
The greatest festival of the Inca year is Inti Raymi, the festival of the Sun. It takes place in June. In the Andes, south of the middle of the world, June is the middle of winter. The days are short and the sun is low in the sky. On the shortest day, the sun seems to stop moving north. People fear that it might go away for good. So there must be a great festival to call it back.

[long-pause]

For three days before the festival, nobody lights a fire. People eat very little and wait quietly. Then, on the morning of the shortest day, all of Cusco gathers in the great square before dawn. Nobles, priests and ordinary people stand together in their finest clothes. The emperor himself is there. Everyone faces east, toward the mountains, and waits for the sun.

[pause]

The sky slowly grows pale, then pink, then gold. Nobody speaks. Then, over the mountains, the first ray of sunlight appears. A great shout rises from the crowd. The emperor lifts a golden cup of chicha toward the sun. He greets it and thanks it, and pours some chicha out as a gift.

[pause]

{img:priest-lighting-new-fire-mirror-sunlight-cotton-cusco-ceremony}
Then a priest takes a curved mirror of polished metal. He holds it up to catch the first sunlight. He points the bright spot of light onto a little pile of dry cotton. Everyone watches closely. The cotton begins to smoke. Then a small flame appears. It is a new fire, lit by the sun itself. From this fire, all the fires in the city are lit again. The sun has come back, and the new year can begin.

[long-pause]

After that come days of feasting and celebration. There is music and dancing in the square. Drums beat and flutes play and people sing together. There is plenty of food and chicha for everyone. Gifts of cloth and llamas are offered to the Sun. The preserved bodies of the old emperors are carried out in their fine clothes. They sit in the square with the living, as honoured guests.

[pause]

Families from the countryside travel to Cusco for the festival. A farmer might bring his children for the very first time. They stare at the great walls and the crowds of people in their bright clothes. They try to see the emperor between all the heads. They eat roasted maize and listen to the music late into the night. And they talk about it for the rest of the year.

[pause]

{img:inti-raymi-today-sacsayhuaman-reenactment-costumes-crowd-later}
Inti Raymi is still celebrated today. Every year on the twenty-fourth of June, a great show takes place at the old stone fortress above Cusco. Thousands of people come to watch. Actors play the emperor, the priests and the nobles. They speak in Quechua, the language of the Inca. And together they greet the sun, just as people did more than five hundred years ago.

[long-pause]

{img:machu-picchu-completed-emperor-arriving-litter-procession-terraces}
After many years of hard work, Machu Picchu is finally ready. And one day, a runner arrives with important news. The emperor is coming.

[pause]

Everyone hurries to prepare. The paths are swept clean and the fountains are scrubbed. Fresh flowers are placed in the temples. The cooks begin preparing a great feast. The weavers finish their finest cloth. Kantu completes the emperor's tunic just in time, after a week with very little sleep.

[long-pause]

The emperor comes along the road from Cusco on his golden litter. Strong men carry it carefully on their shoulders. Nobles and soldiers walk alongside him. Servants carry his belongings, and musicians play flutes and drums. People along the way throw flowers onto the path. At the Sun Gate high above the city, the procession stops. The emperor looks down at his finished estate for the first time.

[pause]

We do not know what he said. But we can imagine what he saw. Grey stone walls on a green ridge. Terraces stepping down the mountainside. Temples glowing in the afternoon sun. Peaks all around, wrapped in soft white cloud. And the river far below. It was everything he had dreamed of, years before, standing on this same ridge with his worried advisers.

[pause]

{img:royal-residence-machu-picchu-emperor-garden-private-fountain-view}
The emperor has his own house at Machu Picchu. It stands beside the first fountain, with its own garden and courtyard. It has its own bathroom with a stone drain. The stonework here is the finest in the whole city. From his windows, the emperor can watch the clouds drift between the mountains.

[long-pause]

While the emperor stays, Machu Picchu is full of life. There are feasts and ceremonies at the temples. There are prayers to the Sun at the carved rock on the hill. In the evenings there is music and dancing. Priests read signs in the stars and in coca leaves. Nobles go hunting in the forest below. And the emperor rests, far from the busy capital.

[pause]

Amaru sees the emperor only once, from a distance. He is working on a wall near a temple when the procession passes. Everyone kneels and bows to the ground. Amaru does not dare to look up. But he feels the procession stop near his wall. There is a moment of silence. Then it moves on.

[pause]

{img:young-mason-kneeling-bowing-procession-passing-wall-temple-afternoon}
Later, Kusi tells him what happened. The emperor stopped and looked at your wall, he says. And he nodded. Amaru cannot sleep at all that night. He lies looking at the stars through the doorway, smiling to himself.

[pause]

Kantu's tunic is presented to the emperor at the great feast. He looks at it for a long time. He runs his fingers slowly over the tiny squares. Then he gives an order. Kantu is to receive a fine cloth of her own, and a cup of chicha from the emperor's own jar. She can hardly believe it. Her friends hug her and laugh.

[long-pause]

When the emperor leaves, Machu Picchu becomes quiet again. A few hundred people live there all year. There are servants, weavers, farmers and priests. They look after the estate and farm the terraces. They care for the temples. And they wait for the next visit.

[pause]

{img:machu-picchu-quiet-evening-residents-terraces-smoke-peaks-golden-light}
In the evening, golden light falls on the peaks. Smoke rises from the kitchen fires. Llamas graze quietly on the terraces. Children play on the stone stairs while their mothers call them in for supper. Far below, the river roars on, as it always has. Machu Picchu is alive. And it seems as if it will stay that way forever.

[long-pause]

{img:spanish-ships-arriving-pacific-coast-peru-1532-distant-sails}
People lived at Machu Picchu for less than a hundred years. Then everything changed.

[pause]

In the year 1532, strangers arrived on the coast of the Inca Empire. They came from Spain, across the wide ocean. They had horses, which nobody in the Andes had ever seen before. They had armour and weapons made of steel. Their leader was called Francisco Pizarro. There were fewer than two hundred of them.

[long-pause]

They came at a very bad time for the Inca. A few years earlier, a terrible sickness had swept through the empire. It had come from Europe, travelling ahead of the Spanish themselves. Nobody in the Americas had ever had this sickness before. Their bodies had no defence against it. A great many people were lost, among them the emperor and his heir.

[pause]

After that, two of the emperor's sons quarrelled over who should rule. The empire was split in two. Just as one of the brothers won, the Spanish arrived. They captured him. They asked for a room filled with gold and silver in return for his freedom. The Inca filled the room. But the Spanish did not let him go.

[pause]

{img:inca-gold-objects-melted-down-room-full-sorrow-gentle-later}
We will leave those hard years there, gently, in the past. They brought great sorrow to the people of the Andes. The great Inca Empire fell. The Spanish took Cusco. The golden garden of the Sun temple was melted down into bars of metal. Much was lost that can never be found again.

[long-pause]

Some Inca kept resisting for many years. They moved into the warm forests and mountains north of Cusco. For forty years they kept a small free Inca kingdom there. It was not far from Machu Picchu. But in the end, that kingdom was taken too.

[pause]

And Machu Picchu? The Spanish never found it. It does not appear in any of their papers about the places they visited. It seems they simply did not know it was there, hidden on its ridge in the clouds, far from the main roads.

[pause]

{img:machu-picchu-empty-quiet-mist-last-people-leaving-path-dusk}
But the people of Machu Picchu left all the same. The emperor's family was gone, and nobody came to visit any more. Nobody sent food up the mountain. The estate had lost its reason to exist. So slowly, the servants, weavers and farmers went away. Some returned to their home villages. Others went to find new lives.

[long-pause]

Imagine the last family walking down the path for the final time. They carry their bundles on their backs. The mother turns to look back at the empty houses and the quiet fountains. Then she follows the others down into the mist. The fires went out one by one. The fountains kept flowing, but there was nobody left to drink from them.

[long-pause]

{img:machu-picchu-overgrown-jungle-vines-trees-walls-centuries-mist}
For almost four hundred years, Machu Picchu slept.

[pause]

The forest slowly came back. Seeds blew in on the wind, and birds dropped seeds on the terraces. Grass grew between the stones, and then bushes, and then trees. Their roots crept into cracks in the walls. Vines climbed over the temples and moss grew on the stairs. Year after year, the green slowly covered the grey.

[long-pause]

The grass roofs rotted away and the wooden beams fell down. But the stone walls stood firm. The perfect walls of the masons did not move. The drains kept carrying the rain away. The terraces kept holding the mountain in place. Even without people, the city held together, as if it were waiting.

[pause]

Animals lived there now. Deer walked along the terraces in the early morning. Pumas hunted quietly on the slopes. Bears came in season to eat the fruit. Hummingbirds drank from flowers growing out of the old walls. Butterflies floated over the empty squares. And the clouds came and went every morning and evening, as they always had.

[pause]

{img:hummingbird-flower-growing-from-inca-wall-moss-quiet-forest}
But Machu Picchu was never completely forgotten. The farmers in the valley below knew it was there. Some of them even farmed the old terraces. A few families built small houses among the ruins. They grew maize and potatoes on the steps the Inca had built. They knew the old stones were special. But to them, it was simply a place on their own mountain.

[long-pause]

One of those families lived high on the ridge for many years. The father went down to the river each week to fish. The mother kept chickens and grew vegetables on a terrace. The children played hide and seek among the old walls. They knew every corner, every staircase, every carved stone. To them, it was not a mystery. It was home.

[pause]

{img:farmer-family-hut-among-inca-ruins-maize-terraces-1900-quiet}
Outside the valley, almost nobody knew about it. Maps from those centuries do not show it. Travellers passed along the river far below without ever looking up. A few people may have climbed up and seen the ruins. Someone scratched a name on a wall there in the eighteen hundreds. But nobody told the wider world.

[long-pause]

So the ridge stayed quiet, green and grey in the clouds. The fountains still trickled softly. The walls still stood. And the city of Pachacuti waited high on its mountain, for someone to come and tell its story.

[long-pause]

{img:hiram-bingham-1911-expedition-mules-urubamba-valley-hats-camera}
In the year 1911, an American explorer came to the valley. His name was Hiram Bingham. He taught at a university in America. He was searching for the lost last city of the Inca, the place where they had held out after the Spanish came.

[pause]

He travelled down the sacred valley with a small team and some mules. At a camp beside the river, he met a local farmer called Melchor Arteaga. Bingham asked him if he knew of any ruins nearby. Arteaga said that he did. There were ruins high up on the mountain, on the ridge. He called the place Machu Picchu.

[long-pause]

The next morning it was raining. Bingham's companions did not want to climb. It was too steep and too wet, they said. So Bingham went with Arteaga and a soldier. They crossed the river on a wobbly bridge made of logs. Bingham crawled across on his hands and knees. Then they climbed up and up, through thick wet forest, holding on to roots and branches. It took them hours.

[pause]

Near the top, they found a small hut where two farming families lived. They grew crops on the old terraces. They gave the tired visitors cool water and boiled potatoes. The families had lived up there for a few years already. They liked it on the ridge. The old terraces were good for growing maize and potatoes. The soil was rich, and there was clean water from the old Inca channels. Nobody came to bother them, and there were no taxes to pay. In the mornings they lit a small fire and warmed their hands. In the evenings they watched the clouds fill the valley below.

[pause]

They told Bingham that there were old walls everywhere around their fields. Sometimes they found a stone tool in the earth, or a piece of an old pot. They simply set it aside and went on with their work. To them, the old city was just part of the mountain, like the rocks and the trees.

[pause]

Then a small boy, about ten years old, offered to show Bingham the ruins. His name was Pablito.

[pause]

{img:local-boy-guiding-explorer-ruins-machu-picchu-1911-overgrown-walls}
Pablito led Bingham along the terraces and into the trees. And there, among the vines and the moss, Bingham saw the walls. Perfect Inca walls, half hidden in the forest. Temples and stairways and houses. A whole city, wrapped in green. He wrote later that it took his breath away.

[long-pause]

Bingham was not really the first person to see Machu Picchu. The local families had always known it was there. But he was the one who told the rest of the world. He came back with teams of workers to clear the forest and to dig. He took hundreds of photographs. In 1913, a famous magazine filled a whole issue with his pictures and his story. Suddenly, everyone wanted to know about the lost city in the clouds.

[pause]

{img:workers-clearing-forest-machu-picchu-1912-walls-revealed-photographs}
Clearing the forest was slow and careful work. Local men cut away the trees and vines with long knives. Bit by bit, the walls came back into the light. Bingham's team photographed each part as it appeared. Some of the workers had grown up on that very mountain. Now they saw the old city clearly for the first time.

[pause]

The workers lived in simple camps on the ridge during those months. They cooked soup over small fires and slept under rough shelters. At night it was cold, and the rain often drummed on the roofs. In the mornings they climbed back to work with their knives and baskets. Some of them found old bones and pots, and these were carefully packed in straw. Others carried earth away in baskets, one load at a time, just like the Inca builders long ago.

[pause]

Bingham believed he had found the last city of the Inca. But he was wrong about that. Today we know that Machu Picchu was Pachacuti's royal estate. Bingham also took many objects from Machu Picchu back to America. For about a hundred years they stayed there. Then, after many requests from Peru, they were finally sent home. Today they are kept in a museum in Cusco, back where they belong.

[long-pause]

And Pablito, the boy who showed the way? His name is still remembered. When people tell the story of Machu Picchu today, many remember to mention the small boy on the mountain who led a stranger through the trees.

[long-pause]

{img:machu-picchu-today-classic-view-huayna-picchu-terraces-visitors-sun}
Today, Machu Picchu is one of the most famous places in the world. It is a World Heritage Site, protected for everyone. People around the world have voted it one of the new wonders of the world. Every year, more than a million visitors come to see it.

[pause]

They come by train up the narrow valley, beside the rushing river. Then they take a bus up a winding road to the ridge. Or they walk the Inca Trail for four days and arrive through the Sun Gate at dawn.

[long-pause]

They walk on the stairs that the Inca built. They touch the perfect walls. They see the fountains, which still flow after five hundred years. They watch the clouds rise out of the valley in the morning. And they stand looking at the view for a long time. Many of them say it is even more beautiful than in the pictures.

[pause]

So many visitors come that Machu Picchu must be protected carefully. Only a certain number of people may enter each day. Visitors walk along set paths, and guards make sure nobody climbs on the walls. The stones that have stood for five hundred years must stand for five hundred more.

[pause]

{img:quechua-family-today-traditional-clothes-llamas-andes-village-market}
And the people of the Andes are still here. Millions of them still speak Quechua, the language of the Inca. They still grow potatoes in a hundred colours. They still make chuño in the frost. They still keep llamas with ribbons in their ears. They still weave on looms tied around their waists. They still pour a little chicha for Pachamama. And every year, four villages still rebuild a bridge of grass over a deep river gorge.

[long-pause]

In the small town at the bottom of the mountain, life goes on gently. Shops sell warm woollen hats and soft blankets in bright colours. Small restaurants serve soup with quinoa and potatoes. In the mornings, children walk to school beside the rushing river. Trains whistle as they come and go along the valley. And above it all, the old city waits quietly in the clouds, as it has always done.

[pause]

Some of the guides who lead visitors today are Quechua people from nearby villages. They tell the old stories in their own words. They point out the stones that fit together without a gap. They show where the sun lights up a window on the longest day. And sometimes they pause and say a quiet thank you to the mountains, just as their grandparents taught them.

[pause]

So we leave Machu Picchu tonight, in the time of the emperor Pachacuti, as the evening falls.

[pause]

The sun goes down behind the peaks. The light turns from gold to pink to a deep, soft blue. Clouds rise slowly out of the valley, white and quiet. They fill the gorges and creep up the terraces. The fountains trickle in the dusk.

[pause]

In the workers' houses, small fires are glowing. Amaru sits outside with old Kusi, his tired hands resting on his knees. They do not talk much. They do not need to. In the weavers' house, Kantu folds her new cloth carefully and lays it in a basket. On the terraces, the llamas lie down in the grass. The white one with the red tassel is the last to settle.

[pause]

{img:machu-picchu-night-stars-milky-way-peaks-silhouette-clouds-below}
