Tonight we go more than five hundred years back. We go high into the mountains of South America, to the land of the Inca. The year is around 1460. And on a narrow ridge between two mountain peaks, something remarkable is being built.

[long-pause]

It is early morning. Clouds fill the deep valleys below like a white sea. The sharp green peaks rise out of them like islands. Far below, a river roars, but you cannot see it. Only hear it. And on the ridge, between the peaks, stand walls of grey stone. Houses and temples and stairways and steps of land, climbing up the mountainside. Some are finished. Some are still being built. Smoke rises from little fires. And people are already at work.

[pause]

This place will later be called Machu Picchu. It means old peak, in the language of the Inca. It is one of the most beautiful places on earth. And for a long time, it was one of the most mysterious.

[pause]

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

{img:inca-stonemasons-shaping-granite-blocks-terraces-mountain-morning}
Who built Machu Picchu? How did they get the stones up a steep mountain? Why here, of all places, on a ridge above the clouds? And why was it forgotten for hundreds of years?

[pause]

For a long time, people did not know. Some made up wild stories. But today, archaeologists know a great deal. They have studied the walls and the stones. They have read the old Spanish writings about the Inca. They have found the graves of people who lived there. And slowly, the answers have come.

[pause]

{img:inca-empire-andes-mountains-roads-villages-terraces-vast-landscape}
Tonight we will travel through the Inca Empire. The biggest empire in the Americas. We will walk the great stone roads. We will cross a bridge made of grass. We will meet the runners who carry messages over the mountains. We will visit the golden city of Cusco. We will meet the great emperor who ordered Machu Picchu. And we will follow a young stonemason as he helps to build it.

[pause]

There will be worries along the way. Can the stones be moved? Will the bridge hold? Will the emperor be pleased?

[long-pause]

{img:llamas-grazing-high-andes-snow-peaks-shepherd-girl-morning}
So let us start high in the Andes, in the cold clear morning air. The sky is very blue. The mountains are white with snow at the top. Llamas graze on the slopes, with little ribbons in their ears. And somewhere a shepherd girl is playing a flute made of bone. The notes float out across the valley.

[long-pause]

{img:inca-emperor-sapa-inca-litter-carried-procession-gold-feathers}
The Inca are a people of the Andes mountains in South America. In the year 1460, they rule the biggest empire the Americas have ever seen.

[pause]

It was not always so. For a long time, the Inca were a small people living around the city of Cusco, high in the mountains. There were many peoples in the Andes. Each had its own lands and lords and gods. The Inca were just one of them.

[long-pause]

Then, a little before our story, everything changed. A rival people attacked Cusco. The old Inca ruler and his chosen son fled. The city seemed lost. But a younger son stayed. He gathered the people and defended the city. And against all hope, he won.

[pause]

That son became the new ruler. He took the name Pachacuti. It means he who turns the world around. And he did. Under Pachacuti and his sons, the Inca conquered one people after another. In less than a hundred years, their empire stretched for more than four thousand kilometres. Along the mountains, from what is now Colombia in the north to Chile and Argentina in the south. Perhaps ten million people lived under Inca rule.

[pause]

{img:inca-empire-four-quarters-map-like-andes-coast-jungle-cusco-centre}
The Inca called their empire Tawantinsuyu. It means the land of the four parts. It was divided into four great regions, one in each direction. And at the centre, where the four parts met, was Cusco. The navel of the world.

[long-pause]

The ruler is called the Sapa Inca. The only Inca. People believe he is the son of the Sun. He is carried on a golden litter, a seat on poles carried by men. He wears fine cloth and gold and bright feathers. Nobody may look him in the eye. And everything in the empire belongs, in the end, to him.

[pause]

{img:inca-family-farmers-mountain-terraces-potatoes-maize-highlands}
But most people in the empire are farmers. They live in small villages in the valleys and on the mountainsides. They grow potatoes and maize. They keep llamas and alpacas. They weave and they work for the state. They have never seen the emperor. But they know his roads and his storehouses. And they know that when the call comes, they must go and work for him.

[long-pause]

The Inca are very organised. They count every family. They group people in tens and hundreds and thousands. Each group has a leader. Each leader answers to a higher leader. All the way up to the emperor in Cusco. It is a little like a huge ladder. Nothing happens in the empire that the emperor does not hear about. At least, that is how it is meant to work.

[long-pause]

{img:andes-mountains-snow-peaks-deep-valleys-cloud-forest-river-gorge}
The Andes are the longest mountain chain in the world. They run all the way down the west side of South America. Some of their peaks are more than six thousand metres high. Covered in snow and ice all year.

[pause]

It is a land of huge differences. Very close together. High up, there are cold windy grasslands where only tough grass grows. Lower down, there are warm valleys with rivers and fields. And on the eastern side, the mountains fall steeply into a hot wet forest full of trees and birds and mist. All of this can be just a day's walk apart.

[long-pause]

Living here is hard. The air high up is thin. There is less of it to breathe. Visitors from the lowlands get tired and dizzy and short of breath. But the people of the Andes are used to it. Their bodies have adapted over thousands of years. They have bigger chests and stronger hearts. They can work hard at heights where others can barely walk.

[pause]

The weather can be harsh. Freezing nights and burning sun by day. Sudden storms and hail. Earthquakes shake the ground. Landslides wipe out paths. Rivers flood in the rainy season. Nothing here is easy.

[pause]

{img:condor-soaring-over-andes-valley-wings-wide-clouds-below}
But the people of the Andes have learned to live with all of it. And to use it. They grow different crops at different heights. Potatoes high up. Maize in the warm valleys. Coca and fruit in the forest edges. Families and villages trade with each other up and down the mountain. Everyone has a little of everything.

[long-pause]

Above the mountains flies the condor. It is one of the biggest birds in the world. Its wings stretch more than three metres from tip to tip. It glides for hours on the wind without flapping. For the people of the Andes, the condor is sacred. It is the messenger between the world of people and the world above.

[pause]

{img:apus-mountain-spirits-snow-peak-offering-stones-andean-pass}
And the mountains themselves are sacred. The great snowy peaks are called apus. People believe they are powerful spirits. They watch over the valleys. They send the water that flows down from their snow. They must be honoured and thanked. When people cross a high mountain pass, they leave a little stone or a leaf on a pile, as a gift to the apu. The piles grow over the years into small hills of stone.

[long-pause]

{img:cusco-inca-capital-stone-walls-plaza-mountains-around-afternoon}
At the heart of the empire is the city of Cusco.

[pause]

Cusco lies in a high valley, more than three thousand metres above the sea. Mountains rise all around it. Two small rivers flow through it. They have been channelled between stone walls so they do not flood. And over them, the Inca have built a city of stone.

[long-pause]

The heart of Cusco is a great square. It is called the Haukaypata, the place of joy and rest. It is huge. On feast days, thousands of people gather there. The floor of the square is covered with sand brought all the way from the sea. The sand of the ocean, in the middle of the mountains. A sign that the empire reaches from the mountains to the coast.

[pause]

Around the square stand the palaces of the emperors. Each emperor builds his own palace. When he passes away, his palace stays. His family lives on in it. And his body, carefully preserved, still lives there too. Servants care for it. They dress it in fine clothes and offer it food and drink. The old rulers are still part of the city.

[pause]

{img:cusco-street-inca-stone-wall-trapezoid-doorway-people-llamas}
The walls of Cusco are famous. They are built of huge stones fitted together without any mortar, the paste that holds bricks together. The stones fit so tightly that you cannot slide a knife between them. Some have twelve corners or more. And still they fit their neighbours perfectly. Doorways and windows are shaped like trapezoids. Wider at the bottom, narrower at the top. That makes them stronger in earthquakes.

[long-pause]

Some people say Cusco is shaped like a puma, a great mountain cat. The city is its body. A river forms its tail. And on the hill above the city stands its head. A huge fortress called Sacsayhuamán. Its walls zigzag across the hill like the teeth of the puma. Some of its stones are taller than two people and weigh more than a hundred tons.

[pause]

{img:sacsayhuaman-zigzag-giant-stone-walls-hill-above-cusco-clouds}
In 1460, Cusco is growing. Pachacuti is rebuilding it in stone. Workers come from all over the empire. Stone cutters, builders, carriers. The best stonemasons learn their skills here. And some of them will soon be sent on to a new project. Far away, down the valley, on a ridge in the clouds.

[long-pause]

{img:coricancha-sun-temple-gold-covered-walls-courtyard-priests-sunlight}
In Cusco stands the most important temple of the empire. It is called the Coricancha. That means the golden enclosure.

[pause]

It is the temple of the Sun, the great god of the Inca. Its walls are built of the most perfect stone in all of Cusco. Smooth and grey and fitted like glass. And they were once covered with gold.

[long-pause]

The old Spanish writers describe it with amazement. Sheets of gold covered the walls of the main rooms. Seven hundred sheets, they say, each one heavy. In the room of the Sun, a great golden image of the Sun hung on the wall. When the morning light came through the door, it shone so brightly people could hardly look at it.

[pause]

There were rooms for other gods too. For the Moon, covered in silver. For the stars and the rainbow and thunder. Each room had its own colour and its own metal and its own priests.

[pause]

{img:inca-golden-garden-life-size-maize-llamas-gold-silver-courtyard}
And there was a garden. A garden made of gold. Life-size plants of maize made of gold and silver. With golden leaves and silver cobs. Golden llamas with their shepherds. Golden butterflies and birds. Golden flowers. All shining in the sun. It must have been the most amazing garden in the world.

[long-pause]

Why so much gold? For the Inca, gold is not money. They do not use money at all. Gold is the sweat of the Sun. Silver is the tears of the Moon. They are holy metals. They belong to the gods and to the emperor. They are used to honour the gods. Not to buy things.

[pause]

The priests of the Sun live near the temple. They lead the great ceremonies. They watch the movements of the sun through the year. They know when to plant and when to harvest. And they keep the calendar of feasts.

[pause]

{img:priests-coricancha-watching-sunrise-solstice-window-cusco-mountains}
Today almost all the gold is gone. But the walls are still there. A church was later built on top of the old temple. Earthquakes have shaken the church. Parts of it have fallen down. But the Inca walls underneath have never moved. They are still standing, smooth and perfect, five hundred years later.

[long-pause]

{img:pachacuti-inca-emperor-portrait-royal-headband-red-fringe-gold}
The man who ordered Machu Picchu is the emperor Pachacuti. The one who turns the world around.

[pause]

We met him before. As a young prince, he saved Cusco when everyone else had fled. Then he became emperor. And he changed everything. He conquered new lands. He rebuilt Cusco in stone. He reorganised the whole empire. He set up the roads and the storehouses and the rules for work. Much of what we think of as Inca began with him.

[long-pause]

The Sapa Inca wears a special sign of his rank. A woven band around his head, with a red fringe hanging down over his forehead. It is called the mascaypacha. Only the emperor may wear it. It is like a crown. When people see the red fringe, they bow down low.

[pause]

Pachacuti is a great warrior. But he is also a builder and a thinker. Old stories say he wrote poems and prayers. He loved the Sun god and built temples to him. He watched the stars. And he loved the mountains and the valleys of his land.

[pause]

{img:inca-royal-estate-planning-pachacuti-architects-model-clay-ridge}
Like other emperors, Pachacuti owns royal estates. These are like country palaces. Places to rest and hunt and hold feasts, away from the busy capital. Each estate has houses and temples and fields and servants. And when an emperor passes away, his estate belongs to his family forever.

[long-pause]

Pachacuti wants a very special estate. In a valley to the north of Cusco, along the sacred river, there is a mountain ridge. It is beautiful beyond words. Surrounded by peaks and clouds. Above a roaring river. Close to the warm forest and its birds and flowers. And he wants to build there. A place for the emperor and his family. A place close to the gods of the mountains and the sun.

[pause]

{img:pachacuti-standing-on-ridge-looking-at-peaks-clouds-advisers}
His advisers look at the ridge. They are worried. It is narrow and steep. There is no flat land. The rock is cracked. It rains a lot. How could anyone build there? But the emperor has decided. And when the Sapa Inca decides, it will be done. So the planners go home and begin to think. How? It will be the hardest building job of their lives.

[long-pause]

There is an old story about Pachacuti from his youth. Before the great battle for Cusco, he went to pray at a spring. As he looked into the water, he saw a shining crystal fall in. And in the crystal, he saw a strange figure. It had rays like the sun coming from its head. And it spoke to him. You will be a great ruler, it said. You will conquer many lands. Pachacuti kept the crystal all his life, the story says. And whenever he looked into it, he saw what was to come.

[long-pause]

{img:mysterious-machu-picchu-ruins-mist-overgrown-question-mood}
Who built Machu Picchu? For a long time, that question had many strange answers.

[pause]

When the outside world learned about Machu Picchu, about a hundred years ago, people were amazed. How could anyone build such a place, so high, so perfect, without iron tools or wheels or big animals to pull loads? Some people thought it must be much older than the Inca. Some thought it was built by a lost people. Some even made up wild stories about giants or visitors from the stars.

[long-pause]

But the archaeologists kept looking. And slowly, they found the real answer. It was built by the Inca. And it was built by people. Thousands of them.

[pause]

How do they know? The style of the walls is pure Inca. The same as in Cusco and other Inca places. The pots and tools found there are Inca. Old Spanish papers mention an estate of Pachacuti in exactly this area. And scientists have measured the age of bones and plants found there. They show that people lived at Machu Picchu from around 1420 to around 1530. The time of the Inca Empire.

[pause]

{img:archaeologist-studying-inca-wall-joints-notebook-machu-picchu-light}
The graves at Machu Picchu tell another part of the story. More than a hundred people were buried there. Scientists studied their bones and teeth. Your teeth hold a record of where you grew up. Of the water you drank and the food you ate as a child. And the teeth showed something surprising.

[long-pause]

The people buried at Machu Picchu came from all over the empire. From the coast. From the high grasslands. From the lake lands in the south. From the forest. They were not all from one place. They were brought here from far away to live and work. Servants of the emperor. Weavers and farmers and builders and cooks. People from many peoples, living together on a mountain ridge.

[pause]

{img:diverse-andean-people-arriving-mountain-path-bundles-different-clothes}
So the answer to who built Machu Picchu is this. The emperor Pachacuti ordered it. His master builders planned it. And thousands of ordinary people from all over the Andes built it. Stone by stone. With their hands. Let us go and meet them.

[long-pause]

{img:narrow-ridge-between-peaks-before-construction-forest-urubamba-river-below}
The ridge where Machu Picchu will be built is narrow and steep. On one side, the mountain falls away almost straight down to the river, hundreds of metres below. On the other side, too. It is like a saddle between two peaks.

[pause]

To build on a place like this, you first have to make it safe. And that means work you will never see.

[long-pause]

The ridge sits on top of cracks in the earth. Deep below, two great breaks in the rock cross each other. That sounds dangerous. And in a way it is. But it also helped the builders. The cracks have broken the granite rock into pieces. Many pieces are already loose. Easy to pick up and shape. A ready-made quarry.

[pause]

But the cracks also mean water. Lots of it. This is a place of heavy rain. Almost two metres of rain fall every year. If all that water soaked into the ground, the whole mountain could slide away. Buildings would crack. Walls would fall. So before anything else, the builders had to solve the water.

[pause]

{img:inca-workers-digging-deep-foundations-crushed-stone-layers-ridge}
They dug deep. Under the city, they laid thick layers of crushed stone and gravel. Then smaller stones. Then soil. Water can run down through these layers quickly. It drains away into channels and out of the city. Some experts say that more than half of the work at Machu Picchu is under the ground. Hidden. Nobody sees it. But without it, nothing would still be standing.

[long-pause]

Then the builders had to make flat land. There was none. So they built walls and filled them in. Step after step, up the mountainside. Each step is a flat strip of land held up by a stone wall. These are called terraces. On the steep sides, they go up like a giant staircase. Some are for buildings. Some are for growing food.

[pause]

{img:terraces-being-built-mountainside-workers-carrying-soil-baskets}
It took years just to prepare the ridge. Year after year, workers dug and carried and laid stone. And slowly, the steep wild ridge became something new. A safe place to build. Ready for the stonemasons.

[long-pause]

{img:machu-picchu-quarry-granite-boulders-workers-splitting-stone}
At Machu Picchu, the stone does not have to travel far. It is already there.

[pause]

In the middle of the ridge, there is a big pile of broken granite. Huge boulders and smaller rocks, tumbled together. This is the quarry. The builders take their stone from here. Some blocks only have to be moved a few dozen metres.

[long-pause]

Granite is a very hard stone. It is grey and speckled. It does not crumble easily. That makes it good for building. But it also makes it very hard to cut. The Inca have no iron tools. Their tools are made of bronze, a mix of copper and tin. And of stone.

[pause]

So how do they split the granite? They look for natural cracks. They hammer wedges into the cracks. Sometimes they make small holes in a line and drive in wedges there. With patience, the rock splits along the line. Some blocks at the quarry still show these holes today. They were started and never finished.

[pause]

{img:unfinished-block-quarry-wedge-holes-line-grass-growing-mist}
To shape the stone, the masons use hammerstones. These are round, very hard stones, about the size of a fist or a big melon. The mason holds one in his hands and pounds the granite. Again and again. Each blow knocks off a tiny chip. Slowly, very slowly, the block takes shape.

[long-pause]

The hammerstones come from the river far below. They are smooth river stones of an even harder rock. Workers carry them up the mountain in baskets. Many have been found at Machu Picchu. Big ones for rough work. Small ones for fine work. Some are worn smooth from years of use. Each one tells of thousands of blows.

[pause]

{img:hammerstones-river-rocks-pile-different-sizes-mason-workshop}
The quarry is a noisy place. Tap, tap, tap. Thud, thud. All day long. The sound echoes off the mountains. Dust rises in the morning sun. And the masons work side by side, sweating, talking a little, singing sometimes. They are building something for the emperor. And they want it to be perfect.

[long-pause]

Not every stone is used. Some blocks crack in the wrong place. Some have a hidden flaw. Some are simply too big to move. These are left in the quarry. Today, visitors can still see them lying there. Half-shaped blocks, waiting for masons who never came back. As if the builders just put down their hammerstones yesterday and went home for supper.

[long-pause]

{img:young-inca-stonemason-pounding-block-hammerstone-older-master-watching}
Let us meet a young stonemason. His name is Amaru. It means snake, a sacred animal in the Andes.

[pause]

Amaru is seventeen. He comes from a village near Cusco. His father was a stonemason too. And his grandfather. When Amaru was small, he played in the stone yards of Cusco. He watched the masons shape the great blocks. He learned to tell good stone from bad. And when he was fourteen, he started to learn the craft himself.

[long-pause]

Now he has been sent to Machu Picchu. It is a great honour. Only good masons are chosen. He walked for several days down the sacred valley with a group of other workers. He crossed rivers and climbed steep paths. And when he first saw the ridge in the clouds, he stopped and stared. He had never seen anything so beautiful. Or so steep.

[pause]

His master is an old mason called Kusi. Kusi has built walls in Cusco for the emperor himself. His hands are rough and scarred. His back is a little bent. But his eyes are sharp. He can look at a stone and see exactly where it will fit.

[pause]

{img:mason-fitting-stone-trial-lifting-checking-gaps-adjusting-wall}
The best Inca walls are made of stones that fit perfectly together. No mortar. Each stone is shaped to fit its neighbours exactly. How do they do it? Slowly. The mason shapes a stone roughly. Then the workers lift it onto the wall. The mason looks at where it touches and where there are gaps. Then they lift it off again. He pounds a little more. They try again. Again and again. Until it fits perfectly.

[long-pause]

Some stones are tried dozens of times. It takes days. Sometimes weeks for one big stone. And the edges of each stone are slightly rounded and sunk in. That makes the joints look like lines of shadow. Each stone seems to swell out like a soft pillow.

[pause]

There is a clever reason for all this care. Earthquakes. The Andes shake often. Mortar walls crack and fall. But Inca walls without mortar can move. In an earthquake, the stones jump and bounce a little. Then they settle back into place. That is why Inca walls are still standing after five hundred years of earthquakes. And newer walls on top of them have fallen.

[pause]

{img:perfect-inca-wall-pillow-stones-tight-joints-shadow-lines-close}
Not every wall at Machu Picchu is this perfect. Most are simpler. Rougher stones set in clay. Those are quicker to build. The most perfect stonework is kept for the most important places. The temples. The emperor's house.

[long-pause]

Amaru works on the simpler walls at first. He is careful. He does not want to make mistakes. One day, Kusi watches him fit a stone. Amaru tries it three times. It still does not fit. He is frustrated. He wants to give up. Kusi comes over. He puts his rough hand on the stone. Listen to the stone, he says. It tells you where it wants to go. Amaru looks at him. Then he looks at the stone again. And slowly, he begins to see.

[pause]

{img:old-master-hand-on-stone-teaching-young-mason-evening-light}
That evening, Amaru fits a stone on the first try. He looks up. Kusi nods at him. Just once. But for Amaru, it is the best moment of his life so far.

[long-pause]

{img:mita-workers-arriving-construction-site-groups-village-leaders-ropes}
Amaru is a skilled mason. But most of the people building Machu Picchu are not masons. They are farmers who have come to do their turn of work.

[pause]

In the Inca Empire, there is no money. People do not pay taxes in coins. They pay in work. Every family must give some of its time to the state. This system is called mita. It means a turn. Everyone takes a turn.

[long-pause]

So every year, groups of men come from villages all over the empire. Each village sends its share. The village leader brings them. They work for a few weeks or months. They carry stone and dig earth. They build terraces and roads. They cut and move wood. Then they go home. And another group comes to take their place.

[pause]

It is hard work. Carrying heavy stones up steep slopes. In the rain and the cold and the sun. But the state looks after them. They are given food and drink. They sleep in simple houses. They get new clothes. And when they finish, there is often a feast.

[pause]

{img:workers-carrying-stone-on-backs-tumpline-steep-stairs-terrace}
How do they move the big stones without wheels? They use ropes and wooden rollers and ramps. Teams of men pull together. They lever the stones with long poles. They drag them slowly up earth ramps. Some of the biggest stones at Machu Picchu weigh several tons. Moving one takes many people and a lot of patience. And some days, a rope snaps or a stone slips. Then everyone jumps back. Usually, they are lucky.

[long-pause]

The workers come from many peoples. They speak different languages at home. They wear different hats and different clothes. In the Inca Empire, each people wears its own kind of hat. So you can tell at a glance where someone comes from. At Machu Picchu, the building site is full of different hats.

[pause]

But they all learn the language of the Inca. It is called Quechua. It is the language of the empire. People still speak it in the Andes today. Millions of them.

[pause]

{img:workers-resting-evening-sharing-food-different-hats-fire-mountains}
In the evenings, the workers sit around their fires. They share potatoes and maize. They drink chicha, a kind of maize beer. They tell stories from their own villages. They sing songs from home. And they look up at the mountains, white with snow in the moonlight. Many of them are homesick. But they are also proud. They are building something for the emperor. Something that will last.

[long-pause]

One of the workers is an older man from a village by the great lake in the south. He has done his turn of work many times. Roads, storehouses, terraces. He has seen half the empire. He tells the young men stories in the evening about the places he has been. The hot coast with its strange fish. The warm forest with its parrots. The cold high plain where the wind never stops. The young men listen with wide eyes. They have never been more than a day's walk from home. Until now.

[long-pause]

{img:machu-picchu-agricultural-terraces-green-steps-mountainside-crops}
On the steep sides of Machu Picchu, there are terraces. Long narrow steps of flat land, held up by stone walls. They climb up the mountain like a giant green staircase.

[pause]

The Inca are masters of terraces. They build them all over the Andes. On mountainsides so steep that nothing could grow there otherwise. With terraces, they turn whole mountains into farmland.

[long-pause]

A terrace is more than just a flat step. It is carefully built in layers. At the bottom are big stones. Then smaller stones. Then gravel. Then sand. And on top, rich soil. Some of the soil was carried up from the valley below. Basket by basket. On people's backs.

[pause]

The layers let water drain away slowly. Not too fast, not too slow. The soil stays damp but not soaked. And the stone walls soak up the heat of the sun during the day. At night, they give the warmth back. That keeps the plants a little warmer on cold nights. It can make the difference between a good harvest and a frozen one.

[pause]

{img:terrace-cross-section-layers-stones-gravel-sand-soil-diagram-like}
On the terraces of Machu Picchu, the people grow maize and potatoes and other plants. Enough to feed some of the people who live there. But not all. More food has to come from the valley below.

[long-pause]

The terraces also protect the mountain. Without them, the heavy rains would wash the soil down the slopes. The mountainside could slide. With them, the rain is slowed down and held. The terraces are like a strong net holding the whole ridge together.

[pause]

Near Machu Picchu, in another place, the Inca built strange round terraces. They go down into the ground like a bowl. Each ring is a little warmer or cooler than the next. Some experts think the Inca used them to test which crops grow best at which temperature. Like an outdoor laboratory.

[pause]

{img:moray-circular-terraces-bowl-rings-green-grass-andes-later}
The terraces of Machu Picchu have lasted for more than five hundred years. Heavy rains, earthquakes, and centuries of nobody caring for them. And still they stand. Green steps on a green mountain. Holding the ridge in place.

[long-pause]

One night during the building, there is a terrible storm. Rain pours down for hours. Water rushes down the mountain. The workers lie awake in their houses, listening. Will the new terraces hold? In the morning, they hurry out to look. One small wall near the edge has slipped. Stones and soil lie on the slope below. But all the others stand firm. The drains have done their work. The builders sigh with relief. They rebuild the fallen wall, deeper this time. And it never falls again.

[long-pause]

{img:inca-stone-fountain-channel-water-flowing-machu-picchu-steps}
A city on a mountain needs water. And Machu Picchu has its own spring.

[pause]

High up on the slope above the ridge, there is a spring. Clean cold water comes out of the rock. The Inca engineers found it. And they built a stone channel to bring the water down to the city.

[long-pause]

The channel is about seven hundred and fifty metres long. It is cut into stone and lined with stone. It runs gently downhill. Not too steep, or the water would rush and splash. Not too flat, or it would stop. The slope is just right. The engineers measured it carefully, without any modern tools.

[pause]

When the water reaches the city, it flows into a series of stone fountains. Sixteen of them, one after the other, down a long stairway. At each one, the water pours out of a little stone spout into a basin. People can fill their jars there. Then the water flows on to the next fountain below.

[pause]

{img:woman-filling-clay-jar-stone-fountain-spout-morning-light-terraces}
The first fountain is next to the emperor's house. So the emperor gets the cleanest water first. Then it flows past the temples. Then past the houses of the workers. At the bottom, it flows away.

[long-pause]

And remember, at Machu Picchu it rains a lot. So besides bringing water in, the builders had to get water out. They built more than a hundred drains. Little channels in the walls and the floors and the stairs. Rain water runs into them and away. Down into the layers of gravel under the city. Out of the city and down the mountain.

[pause]

The drains work so well that, after five hundred years, there are no floods inside Machu Picchu. Rain falls hard, and it all flows away. Engineers today admire it. Some say the hidden water system is the most impressive thing about the whole place.

[pause]

{img:rain-falling-machu-picchu-water-channels-drains-flowing-misty}
Amaru likes to sit by the fountains in the evening. He listens to the water. Splash, trickle, splash. It sounds peaceful after a day of pounding stone. Sometimes he dips his tired hands in the cold water. And he thinks of the river at home. And of his mother, who will be waiting for news.

[long-pause]

{img:andean-potato-varieties-colours-shapes-baskets-market-highland}
The most important food in the Andes is the potato.

[pause]

The potato comes from these mountains. People here grew it for thousands of years before anyone else in the world had ever seen one. And the Inca do not grow just one kind. They grow hundreds. Small and big. Round and long and curly. Yellow and white and purple and red and black. Some are sweet. Some are bitter. Some grow in the warm valleys. Some grow high up where nothing else will.

[long-pause]

Farmers plant potatoes in small mounds of earth. They use a foot plough called a chakitaqlla. It is a long wooden pole with a pointed end and a step for your foot. The farmer pushes it into the ground with his foot and turns the earth over. The women follow behind. They drop seed potatoes into the holes. Farmers often work in groups, singing as they go.

[pause]

The Inca have a very clever way of keeping potatoes. It is called chuño. In the cold dry season, high up, the nights are freezing and the days are sunny. So the people spread potatoes on the ground. At night, they freeze. In the day, they thaw. Then people walk on them with bare feet. They squeeze out the water.

[pause]

{img:families-stomping-potatoes-chuno-frost-morning-high-plateau}
They do this for several days. Freeze, thaw, stomp. In the end, the potatoes are dry and light, like little stones. They can be kept for years without going bad. When you want to eat them, you soak them in water and cook them. Chuño is like a store cupboard that never empties. In a bad year, it can save lives.

[long-pause]

The Inca also grow maize. Maize does not like the cold high places. It grows in the warm valleys. It is precious. It is used for chicha, the maize beer. It is given at feasts. The emperor's fields grow lots of maize.

[pause]

And there is quinoa, a small round seed that grows high up. It is full of goodness. And there are beans and squash and chili peppers. And many other roots and grains that are still eaten in the Andes today.

[pause]

{img:inca-storehouses-qollqa-hillside-row-stone-buildings-food-stored}
All over the empire, along the roads, there are storehouses. Rows of small stone buildings on the hillsides. They are full of food. Chuño and maize and dried meat. Cloth and tools. If a harvest fails, the storehouses can feed the people. If an army marches past, it can be fed. The Inca plan ahead. Nobody in the empire should go hungry. At least, that is the idea.

[long-pause]

{img:llama-caravan-loads-mountain-path-herders-ribbons-ears-andes}
In the high Andes, there are animals you will not find anywhere else. Llamas and alpacas.

[pause]

They are cousins of the camel, but smaller and without humps. They have long necks and soft eyes and long eyelashes. And they are perfect for the mountains. They can breathe the thin air. They can eat tough mountain grass. They can walk on steep rocky paths without slipping.

[long-pause]

Llamas are the bigger ones. They are used to carry loads. A llama can carry about thirty kilos on its back. Not more. If you load it too heavily, it simply lies down and refuses to move. It might even spit at you. Llamas have strong opinions.

[pause]

Long lines of llamas carry goods along the roads. Food and cloth and pots. Sometimes hundreds of them in one caravan. Their herders walk beside them. They decorate the llamas with coloured tassels and ribbons in their ears. Each family has its own colours. That way they can tell whose llama is whose.

[pause]

{img:alpacas-grazing-high-meadow-soft-wool-colours-white-brown-black}
Alpacas are smaller. They are kept for their wool. Alpaca wool is very soft and warm. It comes in many natural colours. White and cream and brown and grey and black. It is spun into thread and woven into cloth. Warm clothes for the cold mountain nights.

[long-pause]

There is also a wild cousin called the vicuña. It is small and shy and lives high up in the mountains. Its wool is the finest in the world. Softer than anything. Only the emperor may wear cloth made from vicuña wool. Once every few years, the people round up the wild vicuñas. They gently cut their wool. And then they let them go free again.

[pause]

{img:vicuna-wild-herd-high-puna-grass-snow-peak-gentle-light}
Llamas and alpacas are everything to the people of the high Andes. They give wool and meat and dung for fire. They carry loads. And they are part of the family. Children grow up with them. They give them names. They talk to them. And at festivals, the llamas are decorated with flowers and given special blessings.

[long-pause]

At Machu Picchu, llamas come up the path every day with food and supplies. Amaru likes to watch them come. One white llama in particular has a red tassel in its ear. It always walks last. And it always stops at the top of the stairs to look at the view, as if it is enjoying it. The workers call it the Inspector. And every day, someone says, the Inspector has arrived. And everyone laughs.

[long-pause]

{img:inca-road-qhapaq-nan-stone-steps-mountain-pass-travellers-llamas}
The Inca Empire is held together by roads.

[pause]

They built a network of roads more than thirty thousand kilometres long. That is almost enough to go around the whole earth. Two great roads run north to south. One through the mountains. One along the coast. And many smaller roads link them together. The Inca call the road system the Qhapaq Ñan. The royal road.

[long-pause]

The roads are amazing. In the mountains, they climb up steep slopes with stone steps. Thousands of steps. They wind along cliff edges. They cross high passes, more than four thousand metres up. In the wet places, they are raised up on stone walls so they stay dry. In the desert, they are marked with stones and lined with walls to keep the sand off. In some places, they are paved with flat stones. In others, they are simply hard earth.

[pause]

Along the roads, every day's walk or so, there are resting places. They are called tambos. Each one has rooms to sleep in. Store houses full of food and firewood. Pens for the llamas. Officials and soldiers and messengers can stop there. They get food and a bed. Then they go on.

[pause]

{img:tambo-way-station-road-stone-buildings-storehouses-llamas-evening}
Ordinary people may not travel on the roads just for fun. You need a reason. Work for the state. A message. A pilgrimage. Guards at the bridges and passes check who is passing. The roads are for the empire.

[long-pause]

A road leads to Machu Picchu too. In fact, several. One comes from Cusco along the mountains. It passes through other Inca places on the way. It climbs high passes and goes down through cloud forest. At the end, it reaches a stone gate high on the mountain above Machu Picchu. It is called the Sun Gate. From there, the traveller sees Machu Picchu for the first time, far below, on its ridge.

[pause]

{img:sun-gate-inti-punku-view-down-machu-picchu-dawn-light-mist}
Today, this road is called the Inca Trail. People from all over the world walk it. It takes about four days. They climb and climb. They are tired and sore. And on the last morning, they get up in the dark. They walk to the Sun Gate as the sun rises. And they look down at Machu Picchu in the first light. Many of them say it is the most beautiful thing they have ever seen.

[long-pause]

Amaru walked part of the royal road to get here. He remembers the endless steps. Up, up, up, until his legs shook. Then down, down, down, until his knees ached. He remembers the cold night at a high resting place, wrapped in his blanket, with frost on the ground in the morning. And he remembers the moment the road turned a corner, and he saw the valley spread out below, green and deep and full of clouds. He had never been so tired. Or so amazed.

[long-pause]

{img:chasqui-runner-conch-shell-trumpet-mountain-road-running-fast}
How do you send a message across an empire four thousand kilometres long? Without horses, without writing on paper, without wheels?

[pause]

The Inca use runners. They are called chasquis.

[long-pause]

Along the roads, every few kilometres, there are small huts. In each hut, two or more young men wait. They are chasquis. They are chosen for their speed and their strong lungs. They have trained since they were boys. And they are always ready.

[pause]

When a message comes, a runner sprints along the road toward the next hut. As he gets close, he blows a trumpet made of a big sea shell. Tooot! The next runner hears it and comes out. He starts running beside the first one. The first runner tells him the message, out loud, as they run together. He also hands him a quipu, a string with knots. And then the second runner speeds off alone. The first runner stops, panting. His part is done.

[pause]

{img:two-chasqui-runners-passing-message-quipu-running-together-road}
Like a relay race, the message goes on. From runner to runner, hut to hut. Each runner only runs a short distance. But he runs it as fast as he can. Day and night. In rain and snow and sun. And so the message travels incredibly fast. Up to two hundred and fifty kilometres in one day. A message from Cusco can reach the far north in about a week. For a walker, it would take months.

[long-pause]

The runners do not only carry messages. They carry small things too. Old stories say the emperor in Cusco could eat fresh fish from the sea. Two days after it was caught. The runners brought it up from the coast, over the mountains, in relays.

[pause]

Being a chasqui is an honour. But it is also hard. The runners must be ready at any moment. Even in the middle of the night. If they are too slow, they will be in trouble. The message must always get through.

[pause]

{img:chasqui-hut-roadside-runner-waiting-blanket-night-stars-mountains}
At Machu Picchu, Amaru sometimes hears the conch trumpet echo across the mountains. Faint and far away. Somewhere on the road, a runner is coming. With news from Cusco. Is the emperor coming to see his estate? Has something happened? Everyone looks up. And waits to hear.

[long-pause]

{img:inca-rope-bridge-grass-woven-deep-gorge-river-below-crossing}
The Andes are full of deep river gorges. Steep cliffs with a roaring river at the bottom. How do the Inca roads cross them?

[pause]

With bridges made of grass.

[long-pause]

The Inca weave bridges from a tough mountain grass. First, people collect huge amounts of the grass. They twist it into thin cords. Then they twist the thin cords into thicker ropes. Then they twist the thicker ropes into great cables, as thick as a person's body. Strong enough to hold many people.

[pause]

The big cables are stretched across the gorge. They are tied to stone towers on each side. Some cables form the floor. Others form handrails, higher up. Then smaller ropes link the floor and the handrails. Sticks and grass mats are laid on the floor so people can walk.

[pause]

{img:villagers-twisting-grass-cords-ropes-communal-work-riverbank-andes}
The bridges sway and bounce when you walk on them. Far below, the river rushes and foams over the rocks. Many people are scared to cross. Even the Spanish, when they came, were frightened. Some of them crawled across on their hands and knees. But the bridges are strong. Guards live at each bridge. They look after it. They check who crosses. And they make sure it is safe.

[long-pause]

Grass does not last forever. Rain and sun make it weak. So every year, the bridges must be rebuilt. The villages near each bridge are responsible for it. Every year, all the families come. They bring grass. They twist ropes together. They take down the old bridge and build a new one. It takes a few days. And then they have a feast.

[pause]

{img:new-rope-bridge-crossing-celebration-villagers-cheering-sunlight}
The most amazing thing is this. One of these bridges still exists. It is called the Q'eswachaka. It crosses a river gorge south of Cusco. And every year, the people of four villages nearby still rebuild it in the old way. With grass and ropes and their bare hands. Just as their ancestors did for more than five hundred years. They make it in three or four days. They sing and pray to the mountain spirits. And when it is finished, they walk across it, and they cheer.

[long-pause]

There is one more famous Inca bridge near Machu Picchu. It is not made of grass. It is a narrow path cut into a sheer cliff wall, high above the valley. In the middle there is a gap. Six metres wide. And over the gap, the Inca laid a few wooden planks. If an enemy came, the guards could pull the planks away. And nobody could pass. Today it is called the Inca Bridge. Visitors can look at it, but nobody is allowed to cross. It is far too dangerous.

[pause]

{img:inca-drawbridge-cliff-path-wooden-planks-gap-sheer-drop-cloud}
Amaru crossed a grass bridge on his way to Machu Picchu. He remembers it well. The swaying. The noise of the river. Not looking down. Holding the rope so tight his hands hurt. And then the solid ground on the other side. He was so relieved he laughed out loud. The other workers laughed too. Every one of them had been afraid.

[long-pause]

{img:quipu-knotted-strings-colours-hanging-cord-inca-record-keeper}
The Inca rule a huge empire. They need to count everything. People and llamas. Maize and potatoes. Cloth and workers. But they do not have writing like we do. So how do they keep records?

[pause]

With knots.

[long-pause]

The Inca use something called a quipu. It is a long main cord. Many smaller strings hang down from it. Sometimes hundreds. The strings are of different colours. And they have knots tied in them. Different kinds of knots in different places.

[pause]

The knots stand for numbers. A knot near the bottom means ones. Higher up, tens. Higher still, hundreds. Then thousands. The Inca count in tens, like we do. And they even have a way to show zero. You just leave a space with no knot.

[pause]

{img:quipucamayoc-record-keeper-reading-knots-fingers-official-listening}
The colours mean different things. Maybe yellow for maize. Brown for potatoes. Red for soldiers. Nobody is quite sure now. A quipu can show how many people live in a village. How much food is in a storehouse. How many llamas a family has. How much work each village must do.

[long-pause]

The people who make and read the quipus are special officials. They are called quipucamayoc. That means keepers of the knots. They are trained for years. They can read a quipu by running their fingers along the strings. Like reading with their hands.

[pause]

Every village has a keeper of the knots. He records everything. He sends his quipus up to the next level. And they go up and up, all the way to Cusco. There, the emperor's officials know exactly what is happening in every corner of the empire.

[pause]

{img:inca-quipu-in-museum-case-faded-colours-many-strings-soft-light}
Some experts think quipus could hold more than numbers. Maybe names. Maybe stories. Maybe history. The Spanish wrote that the knot keepers could tell long stories from their quipus. But nobody today knows how to read that part. The secret was lost.

[long-pause]

About a thousand quipus have survived. They are in museums around the world. Scientists are still trying to understand them. They use computers to study the knots and colours. Little by little, they are learning more. But the full secret of the knots has not been solved. Perhaps one day it will be. And then the Inca will speak to us in their own words.

[long-pause]

Amaru's uncle is a keeper of the knots in his home village. When Amaru was small, his uncle let him hold an old quipu. It was soft and heavy, like a bundle of hair. Amaru ran his fingers along the strings, the way his uncle did. Bump, bump, smooth, bump. He could not read a thing. His uncle laughed. One day, if you are patient, he said. But Amaru chose stone instead of strings. Sometimes he wonders what his uncle's knots would say about him now. Amaru, one mason, at Machu Picchu. Fitting stones for the emperor.

[long-pause]

{img:andean-village-stone-houses-thatch-courtyard-dawn-mountains-smoke}
Let us visit a village in the valley below Machu Picchu.

[pause]

It is a small village. Maybe thirty families. The houses are built of stone and mud, with roofs of thick grass. They stand around small courtyards. The village sits on a slope above the river. Terraces climb the hill behind it. Above them, the mountain disappears into the clouds.

[long-pause]

The family we meet has a father, a mother, three children and a grandmother. The father farms. He also does his turn of work for the state. Last year, he carried stone at Machu Picchu for two months. The mother farms and weaves and cooks. The children help. The grandmother tells stories and keeps an eye on the guinea pigs.

[pause]

Yes, guinea pigs. In the Andes, people keep guinea pigs in the house. They run around the kitchen floor and squeak. They eat potato peels and grass. They keep the house warm. And on special days, they are eaten. It is a treat.

[pause]

{img:guinea-pigs-running-kitchen-floor-stone-house-hearth-pots-woman}
The day starts before sunrise. It is cold. The mother lights the fire in the little clay stove. She warms soup from yesterday. Potatoes and quinoa and a few herbs. Everyone eats it hot, in bowls of clay. Then the father and the eldest son go to the fields. They carry the foot plough and a little bag of coca leaves.

[long-pause]

Coca leaves are important in the Andes. People chew them slowly. The leaves give a little energy and help with the cold and the hunger and the thin air. They are also sacred. People offer them to the mountains and the earth.

[pause]

The girls help their mother. They fetch water from the stream. They spin wool on spinning sticks as they walk. In the Andes, women are always spinning. Walking to the fields, talking with friends, watching the llamas. Their hands never stop.

[pause]

{img:andean-girls-spinning-wool-while-walking-path-llamas-mountains}
In the afternoon, the mother weaves on her loom. One end is tied to a post. The other end is tied around her waist. She leans back to make the threads tight. She weaves in red and yellow and black. Patterns of zigzags and diamonds. Her mother taught her. And her mother before that.

[long-pause]

In the evening, the family eats together around the fire. Potatoes. Maize. A little dried llama meat. Chili sauce. The grandmother tells a story about the fox and the condor. The children laugh. Outside, it is dark and cold. Stars fill the sky. And high above, on the mountain they cannot see, the fires of Machu Picchu are burning.

[long-pause]

{img:women-making-chicha-maize-beer-big-clay-jars-courtyard-steam}
At every feast in the Andes, there is chicha.

[pause]

Chicha is a drink made from maize. It is a kind of beer. Thick and yellowish and a little sour. Not very strong. People drink it every day. And at feasts, they drink a lot of it.

[long-pause]

Chicha is usually made by women. They boil maize in big clay pots. They let it ferment for a few days in huge jars. Some jars are taller than a child. The chicha bubbles and changes. Then it is ready. It is drunk from cups of wood or clay. Often in pairs. The host fills two cups. One for himself and one for his guest. They drink together. That is how friendships are made and kept.

[pause]

The Inca state uses chicha too. When workers come to do their turn of work, they are given food and chicha. At the end, there is a feast. The more food and chicha the state gives, the happier the workers are. Huge amounts of chicha are made for the emperor's feasts. Whole houses of women do nothing but make chicha for the state.

[pause]

{img:paired-wooden-cups-kero-painted-toasting-two-men-feast-andes}
Before anyone drinks, a little chicha is poured on the ground. That is for Pachamama, the Earth Mother. She must drink first. People in the Andes still do this today.

[long-pause]

What else do the people eat? Potatoes, always. Boiled or roasted or dried. Maize, roasted on the cob or ground into flour. Quinoa, cooked like porridge. Beans and squash and peppers. Llama meat, sometimes, often dried into strips. Guinea pig on feast days. Fish from the lakes and rivers. And fruit from the warm valleys.

[pause]

There is one dish cooked in the ground. People dig a pit and fill it with hot stones. They put potatoes and meat and maize on the stones. They cover it all with grass and earth. And they wait. A few hours later, they dig it up. The food is soft and smoky and delicious. It is called pachamanca. It means earth pot. People in the Andes still cook it today, especially on feast days.

[pause]

{img:pachamanca-earth-oven-family-uncovering-steaming-food-hot-stones}
At Machu Picchu, the workers eat simply. Potatoes and maize and chicha, mostly. Food comes up the mountain every day on the backs of llamas and people. The kitchen fires burn all day. And when the emperor visits, there are great feasts. With roasted meat and fruit and the finest chicha. And the workers get a share too.

[long-pause]

{img:aqllakuna-chosen-women-weaving-fine-cloth-courtyard-looms-cusco}
For the Inca, cloth is more precious than gold.

[pause]

That sounds strange. But it is true. Gold belongs to the gods. But cloth is what people give each other. It is given at weddings and births and funerals. The emperor gives fine cloth as his highest reward. Soldiers are paid in cloth. Gods are dressed in cloth. Cloth is even burned as an offering. A rich family is a family with lots of fine cloth.

[long-pause]

There are two kinds of cloth. The everyday cloth is called awasqa. It is woven by families at home, on simple looms. It is used for clothes and blankets and bags.

[pause]

The finest cloth is called cumbi. It is made of the softest alpaca and vicuña wool. It has very tight weaving. So tight that it is smooth and shiny like silk. Some cumbi cloth has more than a hundred threads in a single centimetre. It is woven with patterns of tiny squares in many colours. Each little square means something. A family. A people. A message. Nobody can read them all today.

[pause]

{img:cumbi-tunic-tiny-squares-patterns-many-colours-museum-light}
Cumbi is made by special weavers. Some are men. But many are women called the chosen women. These are girls chosen from villages all over the empire for their skill and beauty. They live together in special houses in the cities. They weave and they make chicha for the emperor and the gods. Some stay there all their lives. Others are given in marriage to nobles and officers.

[long-pause]

At Machu Picchu too, there were weavers. Women from many lands, living together on the mountain. Their spindle whorls have been found there. Little round weights of stone or clay, with a hole in the middle. They helped the spindle turn evenly as the women spun the wool.

[pause]

{img:spindle-whorls-clay-stone-small-round-found-machu-picchu-hands}
One of the women at Machu Picchu is called Kantu. She comes from the shores of a great lake far to the south. She was chosen when she was twelve. She has woven for the emperor for six years. Her fingers are quick and light. She is weaving a tunic for the emperor himself. It has taken her four months so far. She is nervous. He will come to Machu Picchu soon. What if he does not like it? She checks every square again and again. Every colour. Every line. It must be perfect.

[long-pause]

{img:inti-sun-god-golden-disc-face-rays-temple-wall-inca-symbol}
The Inca have many gods. And the greatest is the Sun.

[pause]

The Sun is called Inti. He gives light and warmth. He makes the crops grow. Without him, there would be nothing. And the emperor is his son. That is why the emperor is so important. He is the link between the people and the Sun.

[long-pause]

There is also the Moon, Mama Killa. She is the wife of the Sun. She watches over women and the calendar. There is Illapa, the god of thunder and lightning and rain. He carries a sling. When he swings it, there is thunder. When he throws it, there is lightning. And there is Viracocha, the creator. The one who made everything at the very beginning, and then walked away across the sea.

[pause]

But the god people pray to most in their daily lives is Pachamama. The Earth Mother. She is the earth itself. She gives the potatoes and the maize and the grass for the llamas. She must be thanked and cared for. People give her coca leaves and chicha and little gifts. They bury small offerings in the fields. In August, before planting, they give her special gifts. Because she is hungry then, and must be fed before she can give.

[pause]

{img:andean-farmers-offering-coca-leaves-earth-pachamama-field-kneeling}
Then there are the apus, the spirits of the mountains. And the huacas. A huaca is any holy place or thing. A spring. A cave. A big rock. A strange tree. All over the Andes, there are thousands of huacas. People visit them and leave offerings and ask for help.

[long-pause]

At Machu Picchu, there are many holy places. There is a great carved rock on top of a small hill. It is called the Intihuatana. That means the place where the sun is tied. People think it was used to watch the sun and mark the seasons. There is a temple shaped like a curve, with windows that catch the sunrise on the shortest and the longest days. There is a cave under it, carved with great care. And there is a rock shaped like the mountain behind it, as if it is the mountain's little twin.

[pause]

{img:intihuatana-carved-stone-hilltop-machu-picchu-shadow-sun-peaks}
The Inca did not separate the holy from the everyday. The whole land is alive with spirits. Every stone and river and mountain is someone. When Amaru shapes a stone, he says a little prayer to it first. When the farmer plants a potato, he gives a few coca leaves to the earth. And when the sun rises over the peaks in the morning, everyone at Machu Picchu turns toward it. And greets it.

[long-pause]

{img:inti-raymi-festival-cusco-plaza-crowds-emperor-raised-cup-sunrise}
The greatest festival of the Inca year is Inti Raymi. The festival of the Sun.

[pause]

It takes place in June. In the Andes, south of the equator, June is the middle of winter. The days are short. The sun is low in the sky. On the shortest day, the sun seems to stop moving north. People fear that it might go away for good. So there must be a great festival to call it back.

[long-pause]

For three days before, nobody lights a fire. Nobody eats much. Everyone waits. Then, on the morning of the shortest day, the whole of Cusco gathers in the great square before dawn. The nobles and the priests and the people. Everyone wears their finest clothes. The emperor himself is there. Everyone faces east. And they wait for the sun.

[pause]

The sky grows pale. Then pink. Then gold. Everyone is silent. And then, over the mountains, the first ray of the sun appears. A great shout goes up from the crowd. The emperor raises a golden cup of chicha toward the sun. He greets it. He thanks it. And he pours some chicha out as an offering.

[pause]

{img:priest-lighting-new-fire-mirror-sunlight-cotton-cusco-ceremony}
Then a priest takes a curved mirror of polished metal. He holds it up to catch the first sunlight. He focuses the light on a little pile of dry cotton. The cotton begins to smoke. And then it catches fire. It is a new fire, lit by the sun itself. From it, all the fires in the city are lit again. The sun has come back. The new year can begin.

[long-pause]

Then there are days of feasting. Music and dancing in the square. Drums and flutes and singing. Huge amounts of food and chicha. Offerings of llamas and cloth to the Sun. The bodies of the old emperors are carried out in their fine clothes. They sit in the square with the living, as honoured guests.

[pause]

{img:inti-raymi-today-sacsayhuaman-reenactment-costumes-crowd-later}
Inti Raymi is still celebrated today. Every year on the twenty-fourth of June, a great show takes place in Cusco. At the old fortress above the city. Thousands of people come to watch. Actors play the emperor and the priests and the nobles. They speak in Quechua, the language of the Inca. And they greet the sun, just as people did five hundred years ago.

[long-pause]

{img:machu-picchu-completed-emperor-arriving-litter-procession-terraces}
After many years of work, Machu Picchu is ready. And one day, a runner arrives with news. The emperor is coming.

[pause]

Everyone hurries to get ready. The paths are swept. The fountains are cleaned. Fresh flowers are placed at the temples. The cooks prepare a great feast. The weavers finish their finest cloth. Kantu finishes the emperor's tunic just in time. She has hardly slept for a week.

[long-pause]

The emperor comes along the road from Cusco. He sits on his golden litter, carried on the shoulders of strong men. Nobles and soldiers walk with him. Servants carry his things. Musicians play flutes and drums. People along the way throw flowers. And at the Sun Gate high above, the procession stops. The emperor looks down at Machu Picchu for the first time since it was finished.

[pause]

Nobody knows what he says. But we can imagine. The grey stone walls on the green ridge. The terraces stepping down the mountain. The temples glowing in the afternoon sun. The peaks all around, wrapped in cloud. The river far below. It is everything he dreamed of.

[pause]

{img:royal-residence-machu-picchu-emperor-garden-private-fountain-view}
The emperor has his own house at Machu Picchu. It is next to the first fountain. It has its own garden and courtyard. Its own bathroom with a drain. And the best stonework in the whole place. From his windows, he can see the mountains and the clouds.

[long-pause]

While the emperor is there, Machu Picchu is full of life. There are feasts and ceremonies at the temples. There are prayers to the Sun at the carved rock. There is music and dancing in the evenings. Priests read the signs in the stars and in the leaves of coca. Nobles go hunting in the forest below. And the emperor rests, far from the busy capital.

[pause]

Amaru sees the emperor only once. From a distance. He is working on a wall near the temple. The procession passes. Everyone kneels and bows to the ground. Amaru does not dare to look up. But he feels the procession stop near his wall. A moment of silence. Then it moves on. Later, his master Kusi tells him. The emperor stopped and looked at your wall. And he nodded. Amaru cannot sleep that night.

[pause]

{img:young-mason-kneeling-bowing-procession-passing-wall-temple-afternoon}
Kantu's tunic is presented to the emperor at the feast. He looks at it for a long time. He runs his fingers over the tiny squares. Then he gives an order. Kantu is to receive a gift. A fine cloth of her own. And a cup of chicha from the emperor's own jar. She can hardly believe it.

[long-pause]

When the emperor leaves, Machu Picchu becomes quiet again. A few hundred people live there all year. Servants and weavers and farmers and priests. They look after the estate. They farm the terraces. They care for the temples. They wait for the next visit.

[pause]

{img:machu-picchu-quiet-evening-residents-terraces-smoke-peaks-golden-light}
In the evening, the light turns gold on the peaks. Smoke rises from the kitchen fires. Llamas graze on the terraces. Children play on the stone stairs. And far below, the river roars on, as it always has. Machu Picchu is alive. And it seems as if it will stay that way forever.

[long-pause]

{img:spanish-ships-arriving-pacific-coast-peru-1532-distant-sails}
Machu Picchu was lived in for less than a hundred years. Then everything changed.

[pause]

In 1532, strangers arrived on the coast of the Inca Empire. They came from Spain, across the ocean. They had horses, which nobody in the Andes had ever seen. They had armour and weapons of steel. Their leader was called Francisco Pizarro. There were fewer than two hundred of them.

[long-pause]

They came at a bad time for the Inca. A terrible sickness had swept through the empire a few years before. It had come from Europe, ahead of the Spanish themselves. Nobody in the Americas had ever had it before. Their bodies had no defence. Many people died. Among them was the emperor and his heir.

[pause]

After that, two of the emperor's sons fought over who should rule. The empire was split. Just as one of the brothers won, the Spanish arrived. They captured him. They demanded a room full of gold and silver. The Inca filled it. But the Spanish did not let him go.

[pause]

{img:inca-gold-objects-melted-down-room-full-sorrow-gentle-later}
We will leave those hard years there, gently, in the past. They were full of sorrow. The great Inca Empire fell. The Spanish took Cusco. The golden garden of the Sun temple was melted down into bars. Much was lost that can never be found again.

[long-pause]

Some Inca kept fighting. They retreated into the warm forests and mountains north of Cusco. For forty years, they kept a small free Inca kingdom there. Not far from Machu Picchu. But in the end, it too was taken.

[pause]

And Machu Picchu? The Spanish never found it. It is not in any of their papers about places they visited. It seems they did not know it was there. Hidden on its ridge in the clouds. Far from the main roads.

[pause]

{img:machu-picchu-empty-quiet-mist-last-people-leaving-path-dusk}
But the people of Machu Picchu left anyway. The emperor's family was gone. Nobody came to visit anymore. Nobody sent food up the mountain. The estate had no more reason to exist. So, slowly, the servants and weavers and farmers went away. Back to their villages. Or to new lives. The fires went out. The fountains kept flowing. But there was nobody to drink from them.

[long-pause]

{img:machu-picchu-overgrown-jungle-vines-trees-walls-centuries-mist}
For almost four hundred years, Machu Picchu slept.

[pause]

The forest came back. Seeds blew in on the wind. Birds dropped seeds on the terraces. Grass grew between the stones. Then bushes. Then trees. Their roots crept into the cracks of the walls. Vines climbed over the temples. Moss grew on the stairs. Year after year, the green covered the grey.

[long-pause]

The thatched roofs rotted away. The wooden beams fell. But the stone walls stood. The perfect walls of the masons did not move. The drains kept carrying the rain away. The terraces kept holding the mountain in place. Even without people, the city stayed together. Waiting.

[pause]

Animals lived there now. Deer walked the terraces. Pumas hunted on the slopes. Bears came for the fruit. Hummingbirds drank from the flowers growing out of the walls. Butterflies floated over the empty plazas. And the clouds came and went, every morning and every evening, as they always had.

[pause]

{img:hummingbird-flower-growing-from-inca-wall-moss-quiet-forest}
But Machu Picchu was not completely forgotten. The local farmers in the valley knew it was there. Some of them even farmed the old terraces. A few families built small houses among the ruins. They grew maize and potatoes on the steps the Inca had made. They knew the old stones were special. But to them, it was just a place on their mountain.

[long-pause]

Outside the valley, almost nobody knew. Maps from those centuries do not show it. Travellers passed down the river far below without looking up. A few people may have climbed up and seen the ruins. A name was scratched on a wall in the 1800s. But nobody told the wider world.

[pause]

{img:farmer-family-hut-among-inca-ruins-maize-terraces-1900-quiet}
So the ridge stayed quiet. Green and grey in the clouds. The fountains still trickled. The walls still stood. And the city of Pachacuti waited, high on its mountain, for someone to come and tell its story.

[long-pause]

{img:hiram-bingham-1911-expedition-mules-urubamba-valley-hats-camera}
In 1911, an American explorer came to the valley. His name was Hiram Bingham. He was a teacher at a university in America. And he was looking for the lost last city of the Inca. The place where they had fought on after the Spanish came.

[pause]

He travelled down the sacred valley with a small team and mules. At a camp by the river, he met a local farmer called Melchor Arteaga. Bingham asked him about ruins. Arteaga said yes. There were ruins. Up on the mountain. On the ridge. He called it Machu Picchu.

[long-pause]

The next morning, it was raining. Bingham's companions did not want to climb. Too steep. Too wet. So Bingham went with Arteaga and a soldier. They crossed the river on a wobbly bridge of logs. Bingham crawled across on his hands and knees. Then they climbed. Up and up. Through thick wet forest. Holding on to roots and branches. It took hours.

[pause]

Near the top, they found a little hut. Two farming families lived there. They grew crops on the old terraces. They gave the visitors cool water and boiled potatoes. And then a small boy, about ten years old, offered to show Bingham the ruins. His name was Pablito.

[pause]

{img:local-boy-guiding-explorer-ruins-machu-picchu-1911-overgrown-walls}
Pablito led Bingham along the terraces and into the forest. And there, among the trees and vines, Bingham saw the walls. Perfect Inca walls. Temples. Stairs. Houses. A whole city, half hidden in the green. He wrote later that it took his breath away.

[long-pause]

Bingham was not the first person to see Machu Picchu. The local families had always known about it. But he was the one who told the world. He came back with teams to clear the forest and dig. He took hundreds of photographs. In 1913, a famous magazine filled a whole issue with his story and his pictures. And suddenly, everyone wanted to know about the lost city in the clouds.

[pause]

{img:workers-clearing-forest-machu-picchu-1912-walls-revealed-photographs}
Bingham thought he had found the last Inca city. He was wrong about that. We now know it was Pachacuti's estate. Bingham also took many objects from Machu Picchu back to America. For a hundred years, they stayed there. Then, after many requests from Peru, they were finally sent home. Today they are in a museum in Cusco. Back where they belong.

[long-pause]

{img:machu-picchu-today-classic-view-huayna-picchu-terraces-visitors-sun}
Today Machu Picchu is one of the most famous places in the world.

[pause]

It is a World Heritage Site. In 2007, people around the world voted it one of the new seven wonders of the world. Every year, more than a million visitors come. They come by train up the valley. Then they take a bus up the winding road to the ridge. Or they walk the Inca Trail for four days and arrive through the Sun Gate at dawn.

[long-pause]

They walk on the stairs the Inca built. They touch the perfect walls. They drink from the fountains, which still flow. They watch the clouds rise out of the valley in the morning. And they stand looking at the view for a long time. Many say it is even more beautiful than in the pictures.

[pause]

So many visitors come that Machu Picchu must be protected. The number of people each day is limited. Visitors must walk on set paths. Guards make sure nobody climbs on the walls. Because the stones that have stood for five hundred years must stand for five hundred more.

[pause]

{img:quechua-family-today-traditional-clothes-llamas-andes-village-market}
And the people of the Andes are still here. Millions of them still speak Quechua, the language of the Inca. They still grow potatoes of a hundred colours. They still make chuño in the frost. They still keep llamas with ribbons in their ears. They still weave on looms tied around their waists. They still pour a little chicha for Pachamama. And every year, four villages still rebuild a grass bridge over a river gorge.

[long-pause]

So we leave Machu Picchu tonight, in the time of the emperor Pachacuti, as the evening falls.

[pause]

The sun goes down behind the peaks. The light turns from gold to pink to deep blue. Clouds rise slowly out of the valley, soft and white. They fill the gorges and creep up the terraces. The fountains trickle in the dusk. In the workers' houses, little fires glow. Amaru sits outside with Kusi, his tired hands resting on his knees. In the weavers' house, Kantu folds her new cloth carefully. On the terraces, the llamas lie down in the grass. The white one with the red tassel is the last to settle. And above the ridge, the stars come out one by one. Over the snowy peaks. Over the sleeping city. Over the whole quiet empire of the mountains.
