The Inca rule a huge empire. They need to count everything. People and llamas. Maize and potatoes. Cloth and workers. But they do not have writing like we do. So how do they keep records?

With knots.

--

The Inca use something called a quipu. It is a long main cord. Many smaller strings hang down from it. Sometimes hundreds. The strings are of different colours. And they have knots tied in them. Different kinds of knots in different places.

The knots stand for numbers. A knot near the bottom means ones. Higher up, tens. Higher still, hundreds. Then thousands. The Inca count in tens, like we do. And they even have a way to show zero. You just leave a space with no knot.

{img:quipucamayoc-record-keeper-reading-knots-fingers-official-listening}
The colours mean different things. Maybe yellow for maize. Brown for potatoes. Red for soldiers. Nobody is quite sure now. A quipu can show how many people live in a village. How much food is in a storehouse. How many llamas a family has. How much work each village must do.

--

The people who make and read the quipus are special officials. They are called quipucamayoc. That means keepers of the knots. They are trained for years. They can read a quipu by running their fingers along the strings. Like reading with their hands.

Every village has a keeper of the knots. He records everything. He sends his quipus up to the next level. And they go up and up, all the way to Cusco. There, the emperor's officials know exactly what is happening in every corner of the empire.

{img:inca-quipu-in-museum-case-faded-colours-many-strings-soft-light}
Some experts think quipus could hold more than numbers. Maybe names. Maybe stories. Maybe history. The Spanish wrote that the knot keepers could tell long stories from their quipus. But nobody today knows how to read that part. The secret was lost.

--

About a thousand quipus have survived. They are in museums around the world. Scientists are still trying to understand them. They use computers to study the knots and colours. Little by little, they are learning more. But the full secret of the knots has not been solved. Perhaps one day it will be. And then the Inca will speak to us in their own words.

--

Amaru's uncle is a keeper of the knots in his home village. When Amaru was small, his uncle let him hold an old quipu. It was soft and heavy, like a bundle of hair. Amaru ran his fingers along the strings, the way his uncle did. Bump, bump, smooth, bump. He could not read a thing. His uncle laughed. One day, if you are patient, he said. But Amaru chose stone instead of strings. Sometimes he wonders what his uncle's knots would say about him now. Amaru, one mason, at Machu Picchu. Fitting stones for the emperor.
