Today, Machu Picchu is one of the most famous places in the world. It is a World Heritage Site, protected for everyone. People around the world have voted it one of the new wonders of the world. Every year, more than a million visitors come to see it.

They come by train up the narrow valley, beside the rushing river. Then they take a bus up a winding road to the ridge. Or they walk the Inca Trail for four days and arrive through the Sun Gate at dawn.

--

They walk on the stairs that the Inca built. They touch the perfect walls. They see the fountains, which still flow after five hundred years. They watch the clouds rise out of the valley in the morning. And they stand looking at the view for a long time. Many of them say it is even more beautiful than in the pictures.

So many visitors come that Machu Picchu must be protected carefully. Only a certain number of people may enter each day. Visitors walk along set paths, and guards make sure nobody climbs on the walls. The stones that have stood for five hundred years must stand for five hundred more.

{img:quechua-family-today-traditional-clothes-llamas-andes-village-market}
And the people of the Andes are still here. Millions of them still speak Quechua, the language of the Inca. They still grow potatoes in a hundred colours. They still make chuño in the frost. They still keep llamas with ribbons in their ears. They still weave on looms tied around their waists. They still pour a little chicha for Pachamama. And every year, four villages still rebuild a bridge of grass over a deep river gorge.

--

In the small town at the bottom of the mountain, life goes on gently. Shops sell warm woollen hats and soft blankets in bright colours. Small restaurants serve soup with quinoa and potatoes. In the mornings, children walk to school beside the rushing river. Trains whistle as they come and go along the valley. And above it all, the old city waits quietly in the clouds, as it has always done.

Some of the guides who lead visitors today are Quechua people from nearby villages. They tell the old stories in their own words. They point out the stones that fit together without a gap. They show where the sun lights up a window on the longest day. And sometimes they pause and say a quiet thank you to the mountains, just as their grandparents taught them.

So we leave Machu Picchu tonight, in the time of the emperor Pachacuti, as the evening falls.

The sun goes down behind the peaks. The light turns from gold to pink to a deep, soft blue. Clouds rise slowly out of the valley, white and quiet. They fill the gorges and creep up the terraces. The fountains trickle in the dusk.

In the workers' houses, small fires are glowing. Amaru sits outside with old Kusi, his tired hands resting on his knees. They do not talk much. They do not need to. In the weavers' house, Kantu folds her new cloth carefully and lays it in a basket. On the terraces, the llamas lie down in the grass. The white one with the red tassel is the last to settle.

{img:machu-picchu-night-stars-milky-way-peaks-silhouette-clouds-below}
Above the ridge, the stars come out one by one. They shine over the snowy peaks and the sleeping city. They shine over the whole quiet empire of the mountains, from the cold high grasslands to the warm forest.
