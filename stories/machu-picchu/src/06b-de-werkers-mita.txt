Amaru is a skilled mason. But most of the people building Machu Picchu are not masons. They are farmers who have come to do their turn of work.

In the Inca Empire, there is no money. People do not pay taxes in coins. They pay in work. Every family must give some of its time to the state. This system is called mita. It means a turn. Everyone takes a turn.

--

So every year, groups of men come from villages all over the empire. Each village sends its share. The village leader brings them. They work for a few weeks or months. They carry stone and dig earth. They build terraces and roads. They cut and move wood. Then they go home. And another group comes to take their place.

It is hard work. Carrying heavy stones up steep slopes. In the rain and the cold and the sun. But the state looks after them. They are given food and drink. They sleep in simple houses. They get new clothes. And when they finish, there is often a feast.

{img:workers-carrying-stone-on-backs-tumpline-steep-stairs-terrace}
How do they move the big stones without wheels? They use ropes and wooden rollers and ramps. Teams of men pull together. They lever the stones with long poles. They drag them slowly up earth ramps. Some of the biggest stones at Machu Picchu weigh several tons. Moving one takes many people and a lot of patience. And some days, a rope snaps or a stone slips. Then everyone jumps back. Usually, they are lucky.

--

The workers come from many peoples. They speak different languages at home. They wear different hats and different clothes. In the Inca Empire, each people wears its own kind of hat. So you can tell at a glance where someone comes from. At Machu Picchu, the building site is full of different hats.

But they all learn the language of the Inca. It is called Quechua. It is the language of the empire. People still speak it in the Andes today. Millions of them.

{img:workers-resting-evening-sharing-food-different-hats-fire-mountains}
In the evenings, the workers sit around their fires. They share potatoes and maize. They drink chicha, a kind of maize beer. They tell stories from their own villages. They sing songs from home. And they look up at the mountains, white with snow in the moonlight. Many of them are homesick. But they are also proud. They are building something for the emperor. Something that will last.

--

One of the workers is an older man from a village by the great lake in the south. He has done his turn of work many times. Roads, storehouses, terraces. He has seen half the empire. He tells the young men stories in the evening about the places he has been. The hot coast with its strange fish. The warm forest with its parrots. The cold high plain where the wind never stops. The young men listen with wide eyes. They have never been more than a day's walk from home. Until now.
