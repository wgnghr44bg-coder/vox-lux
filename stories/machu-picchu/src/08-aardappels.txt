The most important food in the Andes is the potato. The potato comes from these very mountains. People here were growing it for thousands of years before anyone else in the world had seen one.

The Inca do not grow just one kind of potato. They grow hundreds of different kinds. There are small ones and big ones, round ones and long ones, smooth ones and knobbly ones. There are yellow, white, purple, red and even almost black potatoes. Some taste sweet and some taste a little bitter. Some grow in the warm valleys, and some grow high up where nothing else will.

--

Farmers plant potatoes in small mounds of earth. They use a foot plough, which is a long wooden pole with a pointed end and a little step for the foot. The farmer pushes it into the ground with his foot and turns the earth over. Behind him come the women, dropping seed potatoes into the holes. They often work in long rows, singing together as they go.

In a farming village, the whole family helps at planting time. Grandparents, parents and children all go out to the fields together. The smallest children carry little baskets of seed potatoes. When everyone is tired, they sit at the edge of the field to rest and share some boiled maize.

{img:families-stomping-potatoes-chuno-frost-morning-high-plateau}
The Inca have a very clever way to keep potatoes for a long time. It is called chuño. High in the mountains during the dry season, the nights are freezing and the days are sunny. So people spread potatoes out on the ground. At night, the potatoes freeze. During the day, they thaw. Then the family walks on them with bare feet to squeeze out the water.

They do this for several days, freezing and thawing and stomping. In the end, the potatoes become dry and light, almost like small stones. They can be kept for years without going bad. To eat them, you soak them in water and cook them in a stew. In a bad year with a poor harvest, chuño can save a whole village from hunger.

--

The Inca also grow maize, but maize does not like the cold. It grows in the warm valleys lower down. Maize is a special food. It is used to make maize beer and is given out at feasts. The emperor's own fields grow a great deal of it.

There is also quinoa, a small round seed that grows high in the mountains. It is very good for you. And there are beans, squash, peppers and many other roots and grains. Many of them are still eaten in the Andes today.

{img:inca-storehouses-qollqa-hillside-row-stone-buildings-food-stored}
All over the empire, along the roads, there are storehouses. They are rows of small stone buildings on the hillsides, where cool winds keep the food fresh. They are full of chuño, maize and dried meat. Some hold cloth and tools. If a harvest fails, the storehouses can feed the people. If travellers pass by, they can be fed too. The Inca like to plan ahead. Nobody in the empire should go hungry. At least, that is the idea.

--

At harvest time, the potato fields are full of people again. They dig carefully with sticks, so the potatoes are not cut. The children shout each time they find a big one. In the evening, the family sits around the fire, roasting the first new potatoes in the hot ashes. They are crisp outside and soft inside. And everybody agrees that nothing tastes quite as good.
