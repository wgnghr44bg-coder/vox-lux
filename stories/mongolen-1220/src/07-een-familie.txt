Now let us meet a family who lives on the steppe in 1220.

They live in the green valley beside the small river. There are three gers standing close together on the grass. One ger is for the grandmother, one is for the family, and one is for storage and guests. All around them, their animals graze peacefully. There are about two hundred sheep and goats, and twenty cows with long horns. There are thirty horses of every colour, and four big camels for moving camp.

--

The father of the family is called Batu, but he is not at home. He rode west with the army more than a year ago. He is one of the khan's many riders. He sends news home whenever he can, through the messengers of the khan. The last news came three months ago, on a cold spring day. He was well, the messenger said, and he was very far away. He was in a land of great rivers and cities, with walls and gardens. Nobody at home can really imagine such a place.

The mother is called Sarnai, which means rose. She runs everything while Batu is away. She looks after the herds and the children and the whole camp. Mongol women have always carried a lot of responsibility. When the men are away, the women are in charge of everything. And they are very good at it.

{img:sarnai-mother-checking-herd-hillside-horse-whip-wind-hair}
Every morning, Sarnai rides up the hill to look over the herds. The wind blows her hair across her face, and she pushes it back. She counts the animals with a quick look and checks the far corners of the valley. Then she rides down again, already thinking about the work of the day.

There are four children in the family. The eldest is a girl of fourteen, called Khulan. She is a wonderful rider, quiet and brave. Next comes a boy of eleven, called Temur, who wants to be a rider like his father. Then there is a girl of seven, called Altan, which means gold. And last is a little boy of three, called Bayar, which means joy. He is nearly always laughing about something.

--

Batu's mother lives with the family as well. She is old now, about seventy years of age. Her face is brown and lined, like an old map of the steppe. She cannot ride the way she did when she was young. But she knows everything about animals and weather and healing herbs. She knows all the old stories and all the old songs. And she watches over the little ones while the others are working.

{img:grandmother-teaching-girl-herbs-steppe-flowers-basket-summer}
In summer, she walks slowly over the hills with Altan and a small basket. She shows the girl which flowers are good for tea and which are good for a sore stomach. This one is for coughs, she says, and this one helps you sleep. Altan listens carefully and puts each flower into the basket.

There is also a dog in the family, a big shaggy black dog. He has a brown spot just above each of his eyes. People say those spots are like a second pair of eyes. That way the dog can watch the camp even while he is asleep. He guards the gers at night and barks at every stranger who comes near.

--

Sarnai often thinks about her husband, far away in the west. She does not talk about it very much. But every time a rider appears on the horizon, she stops what she is doing. She shades her eyes with her hand and looks for a long moment. Is it a messenger, she wonders, and is there any news? Usually it is just a neighbour riding by. Then she goes calmly back to her work. But she keeps looking up, now and then, all day long.
