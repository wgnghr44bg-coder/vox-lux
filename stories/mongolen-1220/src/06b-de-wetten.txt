Genghis Khan made laws for his people to live by. These laws were called the Yassa.

Nobody has ever found a complete copy of the Yassa. Perhaps it was never all written down in one single place. But people who lived at the time wrote about many of its rules. Those writings tell us how Genghis Khan hoped his people would live together.

--

Some of the rules were about keeping the peace between neighbours. Do not take another person's animals, said the Yassa. Do not tell lies, and do not fight among yourselves. In the old days, these things had happened all the time on the steppe. Now they were firmly forbidden by the khan. Judges sat in special gers to listen to quarrels and decide what was fair. A scribe sat beside them and wrote down every decision on a scroll. People took the rules very seriously, and most of them were glad of them.

Some rules were about respecting the land and the water. Do not wash in running water, said the Yassa, and do not make a river dirty. Rivers were sacred to the Mongols. And clean water meant life for both people and animals on the dry steppe.

{img:mongol-traveller-alone-safe-steppe-golden-plate-paiza-peaceful}
Some rules were about faith and belief. Genghis Khan allowed everyone to follow their own religion freely. There were Christians and Buddhists, Muslims and Daoists, and followers of the old sky religion. Priests and monks of all faiths did not have to pay any taxes. That was quite unusual in those days. Many rulers in other lands made all their people follow one single religion. Genghis Khan did not do that.

--

There were also rules about helping travellers on the road. If a traveller came to your ger, you had to give him food and a place to sleep. This was true even if he was a complete stranger. The steppe is a big and lonely place, especially in winter. A traveller who was turned away might not find another ger for days.

So when a stranger appears at the door, the family welcomes him in. They give him the warm seat near the stove and a bowl of salty milk tea. They give him a bit of dried cheese and some boiled mutton. They do not ask too many questions at first. Only later, when he has eaten, do they ask where he has come from. And he tells them news from the places he has seen along the way.

A story was told about the Mongol lands under Genghis Khan. People said a young woman carrying a golden plate could walk across the whole empire alone. And nobody would bother her or take the gold away. It was probably not quite true, of course. But it shows what people believed in those days. Under the great khan, they said, the roads were safe.

{img:quiet-steppe-camp-evening-neighbours-sharing-food-peace}
For the family in our story, the laws mean one big and simple thing. They mean peace at home, day and night. They do not have to worry that someone will take their horses in the night. They can let their animals graze far away on the hills. They can sleep soundly without someone sitting up to guard the camp. In the evenings, neighbours ride over to share food and stories by the fire. After so many years of trouble on the steppe, that peace is a very great gift.
