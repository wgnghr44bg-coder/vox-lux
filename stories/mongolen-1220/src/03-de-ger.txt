The Mongols live in round tents, which they call gers. People from other lands often call them yurts.

From the outside, a ger looks quite simple. It is a round white tent with a gently sloping roof and a small wooden door. But it is a very clever kind of home. It stays warm in winter and cool in summer. It stands strong and steady in the wind. And it can be taken down in about an hour, packed onto a few camels or a cart, and put up again somewhere new.

--

So how is a ger made? The walls are made of a wooden lattice, like a folding garden fence. Thin wooden poles are crossed over each other and tied together with strips of leather. The lattice can be folded up flat for travel, or opened out into a curve. Several sections are joined together to make the round wall. A painted wooden door frame is set into the gap. The door always faces south, away from the cold north wind.

Then come the roof poles, long and thin and often painted orange or red. They reach from the top of the wall up to a wooden ring in the middle. This ring is called the crown of the ger, and it is held up by two posts. The crown is open to the sky, so smoke can go out and light can come in. At night, you can lie on your back and see the stars through it.

{img:ger-wooden-frame-lattice-wall-roof-poles-crown-ring-being-built}
After that, the whole frame is covered with thick sheets of felt. There is one layer in summer, and two or three layers in the cold of winter. Over the felt goes a white cloth, which keeps out the rain and the dust. Long ropes made of horse hair are wound around the outside to hold it all down. And then the ger is ready to live in.

When a family puts up a ger, everyone helps, even the small children. One person holds the door frame, and two others open out the lattice walls. The father or the oldest son lifts the crown and holds it high. The roof poles are slotted in one by one, while someone counts them out loud. The children carry the smaller pieces and hand the ropes around. And when the last rope is tied, everyone steps back and looks at their home with a smile.

--

Inside, everything has its proper place. In the middle stands the stove, or a fire on a flat stone hearth. The smoke rises straight up and out through the crown. The north side, opposite the door, is the place of honour. That is where the oldest and most respected people sit, and where a small altar stands. The west side is for the men, and saddles and bows and tools hang there. The east side is for the women, and pots and buckets and food are kept there.

{img:ger-interior-hearth-centre-felt-rugs-chests-family-sitting-lamplight}
The floor is covered with soft felt rugs, some of them stitched with patterns. Painted wooden chests stand around the walls, full of clothes and treasures. The beds are low wooden frames, piled with felt and warm furs. It is cosy and snug and warm inside. The whole family lives together in one round room. They eat and sleep and work and laugh, all in the same small space. In winter, the newborn lambs sometimes come inside as well, to keep warm by the stove.

In the evening, a small lamp of mutton fat gives a soft yellow light. Its flame flickers when someone opens the door, and the shadows dance on the walls. The kettle sits on the stove, always ready for another bowl of tea.

--

There are a few rules that everyone follows in a ger. You must not step on the threshold when you come in, because that would bring bad luck. You must walk around the inside in the direction the sun moves across the sky. You must not lean on the two posts that hold up the crown. They hold up the sky of the home, people say. Children learn these little rules before they can even talk.
