Temüjin slowly grew from a hungry boy into a great leader. Over many years, step by step, he brought the tribes of the steppe together.

He did this in a new way that people had not seen before. In the old days, people were loyal first to their own family and clan. Rich families ruled, and poor families simply followed them. But Temüjin did things differently from the chiefs before him. He chose people for what they could do, and not for who their father was. A brave herder from a poor family could become a leader of thousands. A loyal friend could become a great lord, with many herds of his own. People liked this very much, and many came to join him.

--

He was also known for keeping his promises, even when it was hard. And he was known for rewarding loyalty generously. If you were loyal to Temüjin, he would never forget it. If you broke your word to him, he would never forget that either.

In the year 1206, all the peoples of the felt tents gathered by the river Onon. It was the same river where Temüjin had been born. It was a great meeting, which the Mongols called a kurultai. Chiefs came riding in from every part of the steppe. Thousands of gers were set up in rows on the green grass. Thousands of horses grazed on the hills nearby. Cooking fires smoked everywhere, and the air smelled of mutton and tea. And there, the chiefs named Temüjin their great ruler. He took a new name for himself, which was Genghis Khan.

{img:white-standard-nine-horse-tails-pole-wind-steppe-sacred-banner}
What does the name Genghis really mean? Nobody is quite sure, even today. It might mean ocean, or strong, or ruler of everything. Khan simply means ruler, or king. So perhaps his name meant something like the ruler of all the lands between the seas.

--

At the kurultai, a great white banner was raised high above the crowd. It was a tall pole with nine white horse tails tied at the very top. The tails flew and rippled in the wind of the steppe. This was the banner of peace. There was also a black banner, which was used in times of war. The Mongols believed that the spirit of their ruler lived inside his banner.

From that day on, the Mongols were one people, one nation of the felt tents. Old quarrels between the clans were forgotten, or simply not allowed anymore. The raiding of each other's herds came to an end. A man could now ride across the steppe in safety, from one camp to the next. That had never really been true before.

{img:genghis-khan-older-grey-beard-seated-tent-calm-eyes-advisers}
After that, the armies rode out, first to the south and later to the far west. That story is long and hard, and we will leave it there, far away. Tonight we stay at home, on the quiet steppe. In 1220, Genghis Khan is about fifty-eight years old. His beard has turned grey, and his eyes are calm and watchful. He is far away in the west, in a big tent among his advisers. And at home, ordinary life goes on as it always has.

At home, the herders still get up before dawn to milk the mares. Children still chase the lambs and ride out to bring in the horses. The grandmothers still spin wool in the sun beside the doors. The empire is very big, but the day of a family is still small and simple.

--

Genghis Khan himself never learned to read or write a single word. But he understood very well how important writing was. He ordered that his laws and decisions should be written down carefully. He kept scribes with him in his camp, wherever he went. He sent written orders on long scrolls across the whole empire. And he made sure that his sons learned the new letters. The boy who once dug roots beside the river now ruled from a tent full of writers.
