In summer, the Mongols drink something very special. It is called airag, and it is made from the milk of horses.

Mares, the female horses, give milk after their foals are born in spring. In summer, the families milk their mares several times a day, every two hours or so. It is not easy work, and it takes patience. A mare will only let her milk flow if her foal is close beside her. So the foals are tied in a long line near the gers, on a rope pegged to the ground. Their mothers stay close by, grazing and keeping watch. The woman leads a foal to its mother and lets it drink for a moment. Then she quickly milks the mare into a small wooden bucket. Afterwards the foal is allowed to drink whatever is left.

--

The milk is poured into a big leather bag that hangs beside the door of the ger. Inside the bag there is always a little old airag from the day before. That helps the fresh milk to slowly change. And then comes the most important part of all, which is the stirring. Someone must stir the milk with a long wooden paddle, again and again. It must be stirred thousands of times every day. Everyone who walks past the bag gives it a few stirs without thinking. Guests stir it, and children stir it, and the grandmother stirs it. It almost never stops.

After a day or two, the milk has turned into airag. It is a little sour and a little fizzy on the tongue. It is not very strong at all. It tastes a bit like a mix of yogurt and lemonade. The Mongols drink it from big wooden bowls all summer long. Some people drink several litres of it in a single day. It is full of goodness, people say, and it keeps you healthy through the whole year.

{img:leather-bag-airag-hanging-ger-boy-stirring-wooden-paddle}
Airag is offered to every guest who comes into the ger in summer. When you visit a family, the first thing you are given is a bowl of airag. It would be very rude to say no to it. You take the bowl with your right hand, and you hold your right elbow with your left hand. You drink a little, slowly. Then you hand the bowl back to your host with a smile.

Little Bayar, who is only three, likes to help with the stirring. He holds the paddle with both hands and pushes it up and down. Most of the time, the paddle does more work than he does. But everyone tells him he is a very good stirrer, and he beams with pride.

--

The Mongols also make many good things from the milk of cows and sheep and goats and camels. They make thick yogurt and yellow butter. They boil milk slowly until a thick skin of cream forms on top. They make hard dried cheese, cut into small pieces and laid out to dry on the roof of the ger. This dried cheese can be kept for many months. Children chew it like sweets, and it is very hard indeed. You can chew a single piece for nearly an hour.

{img:dried-curds-cheese-pieces-drying-on-ger-roof-sun-flies}
The Mongols call all of these the white foods, which means milk and everything made from it. White foods are thought of as pure and good and kind. In summer, people live mostly on white foods, and meat is kept for the cold months. The first airag of each summer is a reason for a small celebration. The family pours a few drops toward the sky and the earth, and toward the four directions. It is a thank you to the spirits for the milk and the grass. And then, at last, everyone drinks.
