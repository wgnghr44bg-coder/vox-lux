Mongolia is a land of huge open spaces, and it is not all flat grassland.

In the north there are mountains, covered with dark forests of pine and larch. Clear rivers flow through deep valleys, and the water is very cold, even in summer. Bears and deer and wolves live quietly in those forests. In the middle of the land lie the rolling green steppes, which are the heart of Mongol life. And in the south there is the Gobi, a great desert of stones and gravel and a few sand dunes. Camels live there, and the air is very dry.

--

Mongolia lies very high, much of it more than a thousand metres above the sea. It is also very far from any sea, so the weather can be quite wild. In summer, the days are often warm and sunny and long. But the nights are cool, even in the middle of summer. In winter, it can be forty degrees below zero for weeks at a time. Then the rivers freeze solid, and snow covers everything. The wind blows across the open land, sharp and very cold.

But there is one thing that Mongolia has in plenty, and that is sky. People call it the land of the blue sky, and it is easy to see why. The sky is huge and clear and deep blue, almost every day of the year. At night the stars are so many and so bright that the sky looks white with them.

{img:night-sky-mongolia-milky-way-gers-glowing-steppe-silent}
For the Mongols, the sky is sacred. They call it the Eternal Blue Sky, or in their own language, Tengri. It is their greatest god, and it looks down on everything below. It gives power to rulers, and it sends the rain and the wind. When a Mongol makes a serious promise, he says, may the Eternal Blue Sky be my witness.

--

The land is sacred too, in its own quiet way. Mountains and rivers and springs all have their own spirits. People must take care never to harm them. You must not wash yourself in a river or throw dirt into it. That would upset the spirits who live in the water. You must not dig up the earth without a very good reason. You must treat the land gently, because without the grass and the water, nothing could live.

{img:river-valley-mongolia-horses-drinking-larch-trees-mountains-evening}
In 1220, the land at home is mostly peaceful. The armies are far away in the west, many weeks of riding away. The family we will meet lives in a green valley in the middle of the steppe. A small river runs through it, with clear water over round grey stones. Low hills rise gently on both sides, covered with grass and small flowers. This is their summer place, where they stay for the warm months. Soon they will move again. But for now the grass is good, and the horses are fat and content.

In the evenings, the horses walk down to the river to drink. They stand in the shallow water, swishing their tails, and the water runs past their legs. The low sun turns the river into a ribbon of gold. A few larch trees stand on the far slope, dark against the sky.

--

The grandmother of our family can read the land like a book. She looks at the clouds in the west and says, rain by evening. She sniffs the wind and says, snow will come in three days. She looks at the grass and knows how long the animals can stay. She is almost always right about these things. The children think she can talk to the sky itself. And maybe, in a way, she can.
