On the steppe, children learn to ride almost before they can walk.

When they are two or three years old, they are put on a horse for the first time. It is always a gentle old horse that walks slowly and never startles. The little ones are tied into the saddle, so that they cannot fall off. They hold on to the thick mane and bounce along, laughing. And they love it more than anything. By four or five they can ride on their own, and by six they can gallop.

--

Children have their own work from a young age, just like the grown-ups. At five or six, they watch over the lambs and the young goats. At seven or eight, they can herd the sheep all by themselves. They fetch water from the river in leather buckets. They gather dried dung for the fire in big baskets on their backs. They help with the milking, morning and evening. Girls and boys do much the same work as each other. And both girls and boys learn to ride and herd and shoot a bow.

But there is always plenty of time for play as well. A favourite game uses the little ankle bones of sheep. Each small bone has four different sides. One side looks like a horse, and one like a sheep. One side looks like a goat, and one like a camel. The children throw the bones and see how they land. They flick them across the floor of the ger, trying to hit each other's bones. They line them up and race them, like little herds. There are dozens of different games with ankle bones. Mongolian children still play them today.

{img:children-playing-ankle-bones-shagai-ger-floor-felt-rug-laughing}
Outside, they wrestle on the soft grass, just like the grown-up wrestlers. They race their horses along the river and back again. They play tag around the gers and hide behind the carts. They chase the young animals, who run away bleating. They make little bows and arrows from willow sticks and string. And in the long winter evenings, they sit close to the old people and listen to stories.

--

The children learn from everyone around them. They learn from their parents and their grandparents, and from older brothers and sisters. They learn from the neighbours, and from every visitor who comes by. They learn which plants are good to eat and which ones make you ill. They learn how to read the weather from the clouds and the wind. They learn how to tell when a sheep is not feeling well. They learn how to follow tracks in the grass and in the snow. And they learn how to find their way home across the empty steppe, by the hills and the sun and the stars.

{img:grandfather-showing-boy-tracks-in-snow-steppe-winter-pointing}
Temur, the boy of eleven, is learning to use a bow. His father made it for him before he rode away to the west. It is small but very strong, made of wood and horn. Every evening, Temur practises behind the ger until it is too dark to see. At first he missed the target nearly every time. Now he hits it most of the time, and sometimes right in the middle. When his father comes home, Temur wants to show him what he can do. He imagines that moment over and over again. His father watching him, and then nodding slowly. Good, his father will say. That is very good, my son.
