What do the Mongols believe in?

The greatest god is the Eternal Blue Sky, called Tengri, high above everything. Below the sky is the Earth, called Etügen, who is like a mother to all things. Between the sky and the earth live people and animals. And all around them, the Mongols believe, are spirits. There are spirits of mountains and rivers and springs. There are spirits of the ancestors, who watch over their families. And there is the spirit of the fire in every hearth.

--

Every ger has its own fire, and that fire is sacred. You must never throw dirt or rubbish into it. You must never step over the fire, or poke it with a knife. You must not splash water on it carelessly. The fire spirit protects the family who lives around it. When a new ger is set up, the very first thing done is to light the fire. And then the fire is given a little gift of milk or butter.

Some people are believed to be able to speak with the spirits. They are called shamans, and they can be men or women. A shaman wears a special coat hung with ribbons and little bells and small round mirrors. He beats a big flat drum with a stick, slowly and steadily. He sings and sways in the firelight while the family watches. People believe that his spirit can travel to the world of the spirits. There he can ask them for help with many things. He can ask for healing, for good weather, or for finding lost animals.

{img:ovoo-stone-cairn-hilltop-blue-silk-scarves-wind-offerings-riders}
On hilltops and mountain passes, there are piles of stones called ovoos. When travellers pass an ovoo, they always stop for a moment. They walk around it three times, in the same direction the sun moves. They pick up a stone and add it to the pile. They leave a small gift there as well. It might be a splash of milk, a coin, or a blue silk scarf tied to a stick. Then they say a quiet prayer for a safe journey, and they ride on.

The children love ovoos and always want to stop at them. Altan likes to choose a special stone, smooth and round, from the river bank. She carries it in her sash for days until they pass an ovoo. Then she places it on the pile very carefully and walks around three times.

--

Genghis Khan himself believed deeply in the Eternal Blue Sky. Before he made great decisions, he climbed a sacred mountain alone. He took off his hat and his belt, as a sign that he was humble. He knelt down on the earth and prayed, sometimes for several days. Then he came down from the mountain and did what he had decided.

But he also respected the faiths of other people. He let everyone believe whatever they wanted to believe. Christian priests and Buddhist monks came to the Mongol camps. So did Muslim scholars and Daoist masters from China. They all prayed in their own ways, side by side. And the khan listened to all of them with real interest.

{img:different-faiths-priests-monk-imam-gathered-khan-tent-respectful}
Later, his grandson held a famous meeting about faith. He invited a Christian, a Muslim and a Buddhist to his tent. Each one explained why he thought his own faith was the best. They talked and talked, for a whole long day. At the end, the khan gave them all plenty of airag to drink. Then everyone went home, tired but content. Nobody had won, but everyone had been heard.
