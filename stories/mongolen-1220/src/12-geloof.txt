What do the Mongols believe?

The great god is the Eternal Blue Sky. Tengri. It is above everything. And below is the Earth, Etügen. The mother. Between them live people, and all around are spirits. Spirits of mountains and rivers and springs. Spirits of ancestors. Spirits of the fire in the hearth.

--

Every ger has its fire. And the fire is sacred. You must never put dirt into it. You must never step over it. You must never poke it with a knife or put out water on it carelessly. The fire spirit protects the family. When a new ger is set up, the first thing done is to light the fire. And it is given a little milk or butter or fat, as a gift.

Some people can speak with the spirits. They are called shamans. Men and women. A shaman wears a special coat with ribbons and bells and little mirrors. He beats a big flat drum. He sings and dances. And people believe his spirit can travel to the world of the spirits. He can ask them for help. For healing. For good weather. For finding lost animals.

{img:ovoo-stone-cairn-hilltop-blue-silk-scarves-wind-offerings-riders}
On hilltops and mountain passes, there are piles of stones called ovoos. When people pass an ovoo, they stop. They walk around it three times in the direction the sun moves. They add a stone. They leave a little gift. Some milk, a coin, a blue silk scarf tied to a stick. And they pray for a safe journey.

--

Genghis Khan himself believed in the Eternal Blue Sky. Before great decisions, he went up a sacred mountain alone. He took off his hat and his belt as a sign of humility. He knelt. And he prayed for days. Then he came down and acted.

But he also respected other faiths. He let people believe what they wanted. Christian priests, Buddhist monks, Muslim scholars, Daoist masters. They all came to the Mongol camps. They all prayed in their own ways. And the khan listened to them all with interest.

{img:different-faiths-priests-monk-imam-gathered-khan-tent-respectful}
Later, his grandson held a famous debate. He invited a Christian, a Muslim and a Buddhist to argue which faith was best. They talked and argued for a whole day. At the end, the Mongols gave them all a lot of airag to drink. And everyone went home. Nobody won. But everyone had been heard.
