Let us spend one summer day with this family, from morning until night.

Sarnai wakes up before dawn, while the children are still asleep under their sheepskins. It is cool and dark inside the ger. She kneels by the stove and lights the fire with a little dry grass. The fuel is dried animal dung, because there is no wood on the open steppe. Dried dung burns well and gives a steady, gentle heat. She sets a pot of water on the fire to boil. Then she adds tea leaves and fresh milk and a pinch of salt. Mongol tea is salty and milky, and it warms you right through.

--

When the tea is ready, the first thing she does is something special. She carries a little of the first tea outside, into the grey morning light. With a small wooden spoon, she flicks a few drops toward the sky. Then a few drops toward the earth, and toward each of the four directions. It is a small gift to the spirits for a good and peaceful day. Only after that does the family drink their tea.

Then the work of the day begins for everyone. Khulan goes out to the line of foals to milk the mares. Temur jumps on a horse and rides off to bring the herd back from the hill. The grandmother sits by the door in the first sun, spinning wool and watching little Bayar. Altan helps her mother make yogurt in a big wooden tub.

{img:boy-temur-riding-bareback-bringing-horses-down-hill-dawn-mist}
Temur rides bareback, without a saddle, his legs tight around the horse's sides. The mist is still lying in the valley as he climbs the hill. He finds the herd grazing near the top, their backs wet with dew. He whistles and calls, and the horses lift their heads and start to move. Then he brings them all down the hill at a trot, their manes bouncing in the morning light.

--

Later in the morning, the sheep and goats are taken out to graze. Altan goes with them, together with the big black dog. Even at seven years old, she can herd a flock of two hundred animals. She sits on a small, patient horse that knows the work as well as she does. She keeps the flock together and away from the soft ground by the river. And she sings to them as she rides, old herding songs that tell the sheep where to go.

At midday, the family gathers in the ger to eat. There are bowls of thick yogurt and pieces of dried cheese. There is a little flat bread, fried in mutton fat until it is golden. And there is airag, of course, plenty of cool airag. After the meal, some of them rest in the shade beside the ger. The sun is hot now, and the flies are buzzing lazily in the grass.

{img:little-girl-on-small-horse-herding-sheep-dog-steppe-midday}
In the afternoon, Sarnai rides out to check on the cows in the next valley. She takes Bayar on the saddle in front of her, holding him with one arm. He laughs and points at everything the whole way there. The grandmother sits in the shade and sews a pair of felt boots. Khulan stirs the airag bag and churns butter in a tall wooden churn. Temur practises with his bow behind the ger, shooting at a target made of old felt.

--

As the sun goes down, the animals come home from the hills. The sheep and goats are put into a pen made of felt and wooden fences. The foals are tied once more in their long line. The dog lies down across the doorway with a big sigh. The family eats a simple supper of soup with noodles and a little mutton. Then they all sit outside the ger and watch the sky turn red and purple.

{img:family-sitting-outside-ger-sunset-sky-purple-sheep-pen-dog}
The grandmother tells a story while the light fades. It is about a clever horse who saved his master from a great flood. The children have heard it many times before. But they listen anyway, leaning against her and against each other. As the first stars appear, Sarnai looks toward the west, where the sun has gone. That is where her husband is, so very far away. And she says a quiet prayer to the Eternal Blue Sky.
