How do you rule an empire that stretches across half of Asia? And how do you get a message from one end of it to the other?

Genghis Khan had a very clever answer to that question. It was called the Yam.

--

The Yam was a long chain of stations stretching across the whole empire. Every thirty or forty kilometres along the main routes, there was a station. Each station was a small group of gers, with a few families living there. They always kept fresh horses ready, as well as food and drink and beds. They were ready by day and by night, in summer and in winter.

When an official messenger arrives, he jumps down from his tired, sweating horse. He eats a little and drinks a bowl of tea. Then he takes a fresh horse from the station and rides on as fast as he can. Sometimes he does not stop to sleep at all. He ties himself into the saddle and dozes as he rides along. When a message is very urgent, it can travel two or three hundred kilometres in a single day. Nothing else in the world at that time is nearly as fast.

{img:messenger-riding-night-bells-on-belt-moonlight-steppe-alone}
The messengers wear little bells on their belts as they ride. As they come close to a station, the keepers hear the bells ringing across the grass. They get a fresh horse saddled and ready at once. So the messenger loses no time at all when he arrives. He simply jumps from one horse to the other and is gone again.

--

Each official traveller carries a special tablet with him. It is called a paiza, and it is a flat plate of metal with writing on it. A gold paiza is for the most important travellers, and a silver one for others. Simple ones of wood are for the least important. The writing on it means, this person travels for the khan, so give him what he needs. With a paiza, you can get horses and food at every station along the road. Without one, you get nothing at all.

{img:golden-paiza-tablet-mongol-script-hole-cord-hand-close}
The families who run the stations do this as their duty to the khan. They do it instead of going to war or paying other kinds of tax. They look after the horses and keep them fed and healthy. They keep the gers clean and warm for the travellers who come. They cook soup and boil tea at all hours of the day and night. And they see every kind of person pass by their door. There are messengers and officials, traders and monks, and visitors from faraway kings. They come from China and Persia and the lands far beyond.

--

For Sarnai's family, the Yam is very important indeed. That is because it carries news from the west, news of the armies. And sometimes it brings a few words from the men who are far away. These are not written letters, usually, because most of the men cannot write. But they can send a few words with a messenger who is riding east. Your husband is well, the messenger might say. Your son is well. He says to tell the family he is thinking of them. Sometimes, those few short words are the most precious thing in the whole world.

The station near Sarnai's valley is run by an old couple and their grown-up sons. Sometimes Temur rides over there to help with the horses. The old woman gives him a bowl of tea and a piece of cheese for his trouble. He loves to watch the messengers come in, dusty and tired, with their bells jingling. They jump off one horse and onto another in a single moment. And then they are gone again, over the next hill. One day, Temur thinks to himself, I will ride just like that.
