Mongol women are strong and free, more than in many other lands at that time.

They ride horses just as well as the men do. They herd the animals and run the camp. They make the gers and the felt and nearly all of the clothes. They make all the milk foods, from airag to the hard dried cheese. They handle trade and keep track of what the family owns. And when the men are away, which is often, the women are in charge of everything.

--

Visitors from other lands were quite surprised by this. Travellers from Europe and from China wrote about it in their books. The Mongol women drive the carts, they wrote, and they load the camels. They ride like the men do, and they give orders. And their husbands listen carefully to what they say.

Some women had a great deal of power and respect. Hoelun, the mother of Genghis Khan, kept her children alive when everyone else had left. Later she gave wise advice to her son, and he listened to her. Börte, his wife, was his closest and most trusted adviser. His daughters ruled over whole lands of their own. Later in the empire, some women even ruled for a time on behalf of their young sons. One famous Mongol princess was a champion wrestler. She said she would only marry a man who could beat her in a match. For a long time, nobody could.

Women's work on the steppe is hard and never quite finished. A woman is up before everyone else, and she goes to bed after everyone else. There is milking and cooking and sewing, felting and herding and moving camp. There are the children to care for, from babies to almost grown. But women are deeply respected for all that they do. The Mongols have a little saying about it. The man is the head of the ger, they say, but the woman is its neck. And the neck is what turns the head.

--

Mongol women love beautiful jewellery. They wear headdresses decorated with silver and red coral and blue turquoise. They wear earrings and rings and bracelets that jingle softly when they move. On special days, married women of high rank wear a very tall hat. It is called a boqta, and it looks like a tall thin tube. Some of them are almost a metre high. They are decorated with feathers and pearls and shining silk. When a woman wearing one rides into a camp, she must bend low at every door.

{img:mongol-noblewoman-tall-boqta-headdress-feathers-pearls-silk-ger}
Sarnai has worn a tall hat like that only twice in her life. Once at her own wedding, and once at a great gathering many years ago. She keeps it carefully wrapped in cloth, inside a painted chest. Sometimes Khulan asks if she may see it. Then Sarnai takes it out, slowly and gently, and holds it up in the light. Khulan touches the soft feathers with her fingertips. One day you will wear one too, says her mother. Khulan smiles shyly and helps to wrap it up again, very carefully.

On ordinary days, Sarnai wears a simple headscarf and a plain blue deel. Her only jewellery is a pair of small silver earrings, which Batu gave her long ago. She touches them sometimes when she thinks of him. The earrings are small, but they mean a great deal to her.

--

When the khan's armies were away, the great camp at home was run by women. They decided where the herds should graze and when to move. They settled quarrels between families and listened to everyone. They welcomed visitors and sent supplies to the armies far away. One traveller wrote that the camp moved as smoothly as a city on the march. There were carts in long rows, and great gers on wheels pulled by twenty oxen. And at the very front of it all, he wrote, rode a woman, deciding where to stop.
