Tonight we travel about eight hundred years back in time, to the wide grasslands of Mongolia. Mongolia lies in the very heart of Asia, far away from any sea. The year is 1220. And the Mongols now rule the biggest land empire the world has ever seen.

[long-pause]

It is early morning on the steppe, and the air is fresh and cool. The steppe is a huge sea of grass that rolls on and on to the edge of the sky. There are no trees here, and no fences, and no roads at all. There is only grass and gentle hills and a very big sky. A light wind moves over the grass, like slow waves moving over water.

[pause]

On a gentle slope beside a small stream, there are a few round white tents. A thin line of grey smoke rises from the top of each one. Mist still lies in the low places, soft and white, like wool. Somewhere a horse snorts and stamps its feet in the cold morning air.

[pause]

A woman steps out of one of the tents, carrying a wooden bucket. She walks across the wet grass to the horses tied in a long line. She kneels down beside a mare and begins to milk her, slowly and calmly. A boy runs past with a big shaggy dog at his heels. Sheep are bleating softly on the hillside. And far away, an eagle circles in the pale blue sky. It is quiet here, very quiet, and very big.

[pause]

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

{img:mongol-family-outside-ger-morning-horses-sheep-blue-sky-wide}
The Mongols are a people of the steppe, and they live together with their animals. They move with the seasons, from one good pasture to the next. And wherever they go, they carry their homes along with them. Less than twenty years before our story, they were only a handful of quarrelling tribes. Then one man brought them all together. His name was Temüjin, but the world would come to know him as Genghis Khan.

[pause]

Most stories about Genghis Khan are stories about war, about armies and battles and cities. Those stories are true, and that time was very hard for many people. But tonight we will not follow the armies on their long road to the west. We will stay at home, on the quiet steppe, with the families who stayed behind. We will stay with the women and the children and the old people, and with the herders and their horses.

[pause]

{img:old-mongol-grandmother-sitting-ger-door-spinning-wool-sun}
We will learn how a round felt tent is made, and how it is put up in an hour. We will taste milk that has been turned into a fizzy summer drink. We will ride with children who learn to ride before they can properly walk. We will meet the riders who carry messages across half of a continent. We will watch the great summer games beside the river. And we will follow an old monk from China on his long journey to meet the great khan.

[long-pause]

{img:eagle-circling-over-steppe-grassland-hills-endless-sky-morning}
There will be a few worries along the way, as there always are. Many of the men are far away, and the winter is never very far off. Everyone wonders when the khan and his riders will finally come home. But for now, the sun is rising slowly over the grass. The horses are snorting softly in the cool morning air. And a new day is beginning, gently, on the steppe.

[long-pause]

{img:mongol-clans-gathering-steppe-tents-banners-horses-many-people}
So who are the Mongols, and how do they live?

[pause]

For thousands of years, people have lived on the steppe as herders. They keep sheep and goats, cattle and horses, and big woolly camels. They live in round felt tents that can be taken down and put up again. Several times a year, they move to find fresh grass for their animals. The steppe is a good place for animals to graze. But it is too dry and too cold for growing crops. So instead of farming, the people simply follow the grass.

[long-pause]

In the time before Genghis Khan, the steppe was divided into many tribes and clans. The Mongols were only one of them, and not even the biggest. There were also the Tatars and the Kereyits, the Merkits and the Naimans, and many more. They spoke similar languages, and they lived in very similar ways. But they did not get along with each other very well. They took each other's horses and argued about old wrongs. Nobody on the steppe ever felt quite safe in those days.

[pause]

Then Genghis Khan brought them all together, one people at a time. Some joined him through friendship and promises, and others joined him because they had to. In the year 1206, all the peoples of the felt tents gathered on the steppe. There they named him their great ruler. And from then on, they were all called Mongols.

[pause]

{img:mongol-herder-man-deel-robe-hat-holding-horse-hillside}
The Mongols are a tough and patient people, because the steppe is not an easy home. The winters are very cold, colder than almost anywhere else people live. The summers can be hot and dry, and storms can come quite suddenly. Wolves sometimes come down from the hills to look at the herds. To live well here, you must be strong and clever and ready for anything.

[pause]

On a normal day, a herder rises early and rides out to look at his animals. He counts them with his eyes, without needing to say the numbers out loud. He knows each horse by its colour and the way it walks. He knows which sheep likes to wander off, and which goat is always hungry. In the evening he rides home slowly, with the low sun warm on his back.

[long-pause]

The Mongols wear long thick robes, which are called deels. A deel is like a long coat that wraps across the front and ties at the side. A wide sash of cloth is wound around the waist, and things are tucked into it. There might be a small knife there, or a pouch of tea, or a little wooden bowl. In winter the deel is lined with warm fur, and at night it can be used as a blanket. People wear felt boots with pointed toes, and fur hats with flaps to cover the ears. Men and women dress in almost exactly the same way.

[pause]

{img:mongol-woman-deel-sash-headdress-silver-coral-beads-proud}
Their language is called Mongolian, and in 1220 it has only just begun to be written. Genghis Khan himself ordered that it should be written down. A scholar from a conquered people taught the Mongols to write with the letters he knew. These letters run from top to bottom, in long straight columns. They look like a row of little hooks and loops, hanging down from a line. Mongolians still use this beautiful old writing today, alongside newer letters.

[long-pause]

{img:mongolia-landscape-steppe-mountains-forest-gobi-desert-varied}
Mongolia is a land of huge open spaces, and it is not all flat grassland.

[pause]

In the north there are mountains, covered with dark forests of pine and larch. Clear rivers flow through deep valleys, and the water is very cold, even in summer. Bears and deer and wolves live quietly in those forests. In the middle of the land lie the rolling green steppes, which are the heart of Mongol life. And in the south there is the Gobi, a great desert of stones and gravel and a few sand dunes. Camels live there, and the air is very dry.

[long-pause]

Mongolia lies very high, much of it more than a thousand metres above the sea. It is also very far from any sea, so the weather can be quite wild. In summer, the days are often warm and sunny and long. But the nights are cool, even in the middle of summer. In winter, it can be forty degrees below zero for weeks at a time. Then the rivers freeze solid, and snow covers everything. The wind blows across the open land, sharp and very cold.

[pause]

But there is one thing that Mongolia has in plenty, and that is sky. People call it the land of the blue sky, and it is easy to see why. The sky is huge and clear and deep blue, almost every day of the year. At night the stars are so many and so bright that the sky looks white with them.

[pause]

{img:night-sky-mongolia-milky-way-gers-glowing-steppe-silent}
For the Mongols, the sky is sacred. They call it the Eternal Blue Sky, or in their own language, Tengri. It is their greatest god, and it looks down on everything below. It gives power to rulers, and it sends the rain and the wind. When a Mongol makes a serious promise, he says, may the Eternal Blue Sky be my witness.

[long-pause]

The land is sacred too, in its own quiet way. Mountains and rivers and springs all have their own spirits. People must take care never to harm them. You must not wash yourself in a river or throw dirt into it. That would upset the spirits who live in the water. You must not dig up the earth without a very good reason. You must treat the land gently, because without the grass and the water, nothing could live.

[pause]

{img:river-valley-mongolia-horses-drinking-larch-trees-mountains-evening}
In 1220, the land at home is mostly peaceful. The armies are far away in the west, many weeks of riding away. The family we will meet lives in a green valley in the middle of the steppe. A small river runs through it, with clear water over round grey stones. Low hills rise gently on both sides, covered with grass and small flowers. This is their summer place, where they stay for the warm months. Soon they will move again. But for now the grass is good, and the horses are fat and content.

[pause]

In the evenings, the horses walk down to the river to drink. They stand in the shallow water, swishing their tails, and the water runs past their legs. The low sun turns the river into a ribbon of gold. A few larch trees stand on the far slope, dark against the sky.

[long-pause]

The grandmother of our family can read the land like a book. She looks at the clouds in the west and says, rain by evening. She sniffs the wind and says, snow will come in three days. She looks at the grass and knows how long the animals can stay. She is almost always right about these things. The children think she can talk to the sky itself. And maybe, in a way, she can.

[long-pause]

{img:mongol-ger-exterior-white-felt-wooden-door-smoke-hole-morning}
The Mongols live in round tents, which they call gers. People from other lands often call them yurts.

[pause]

From the outside, a ger looks quite simple. It is a round white tent with a gently sloping roof and a small wooden door. But it is a very clever kind of home. It stays warm in winter and cool in summer. It stands strong and steady in the wind. And it can be taken down in about an hour, packed onto a few camels or a cart, and put up again somewhere new.

[long-pause]

So how is a ger made? The walls are made of a wooden lattice, like a folding garden fence. Thin wooden poles are crossed over each other and tied together with strips of leather. The lattice can be folded up flat for travel, or opened out into a curve. Several sections are joined together to make the round wall. A painted wooden door frame is set into the gap. The door always faces south, away from the cold north wind.

[pause]

Then come the roof poles, long and thin and often painted orange or red. They reach from the top of the wall up to a wooden ring in the middle. This ring is called the crown of the ger, and it is held up by two posts. The crown is open to the sky, so smoke can go out and light can come in. At night, you can lie on your back and see the stars through it.

[pause]

{img:ger-wooden-frame-lattice-wall-roof-poles-crown-ring-being-built}
After that, the whole frame is covered with thick sheets of felt. There is one layer in summer, and two or three layers in the cold of winter. Over the felt goes a white cloth, which keeps out the rain and the dust. Long ropes made of horse hair are wound around the outside to hold it all down. And then the ger is ready to live in.

[pause]

When a family puts up a ger, everyone helps, even the small children. One person holds the door frame, and two others open out the lattice walls. The father or the oldest son lifts the crown and holds it high. The roof poles are slotted in one by one, while someone counts them out loud. The children carry the smaller pieces and hand the ropes around. And when the last rope is tied, everyone steps back and looks at their home with a smile.

[long-pause]

Inside, everything has its proper place. In the middle stands the stove, or a fire on a flat stone hearth. The smoke rises straight up and out through the crown. The north side, opposite the door, is the place of honour. That is where the oldest and most respected people sit, and where a small altar stands. The west side is for the men, and saddles and bows and tools hang there. The east side is for the women, and pots and buckets and food are kept there.

[pause]

{img:ger-interior-hearth-centre-felt-rugs-chests-family-sitting-lamplight}
The floor is covered with soft felt rugs, some of them stitched with patterns. Painted wooden chests stand around the walls, full of clothes and treasures. The beds are low wooden frames, piled with felt and warm furs. It is cosy and snug and warm inside. The whole family lives together in one round room. They eat and sleep and work and laugh, all in the same small space. In winter, the newborn lambs sometimes come inside as well, to keep warm by the stove.

[pause]

In the evening, a small lamp of mutton fat gives a soft yellow light. Its flame flickers when someone opens the door, and the shadows dance on the walls. The kettle sits on the stove, always ready for another bowl of tea.

[long-pause]

There are a few rules that everyone follows in a ger. You must not step on the threshold when you come in, because that would bring bad luck. You must walk around the inside in the direction the sun moves across the sky. You must not lean on the two posts that hold up the crown. They hold up the sky of the home, people say. Children learn these little rules before they can even talk.

[long-pause]

{img:felt-making-steppe-women-wool-spread-hide-rolling-horse-pulling}
The walls of the ger are made of felt. And making felt is one of the great events of the summer.

[pause]

Felt is a thick and soft kind of cloth, but it is not woven on a loom. It is made by pressing wool together until the tiny fibres tangle up and hold tight. The finished felt is warm and strong, and it keeps out both the wind and the rain. The Mongols use it for nearly everything in their lives. They use it for tents and rugs, for boots and hats, and for soft pads under their saddles.

[long-pause]

Felt making happens in late summer, after the sheep have been sheared of their wool. Many families from the valley come together to help each other. First the women beat the wool with long thin sticks to make it fluffy and loose. The wool flies up in little clouds, and the children sneeze and laugh. Then an old piece of felt is laid out flat on the grass, as a base. A thick, even layer of new wool is spread carefully on top of it. And the women sprinkle the wool with water, a handful at a time.

[pause]

Then they roll it all up tightly around a long wooden pole. They wrap the roll in a wet animal hide and tie it firmly with ropes. And then comes the part that everyone loves best. A rider ties the ends of the ropes to his horse. The horse pulls the heavy roll across the grass, back and forth, back and forth. This goes on for hours, slowly and steadily, under the summer sun. The rolling and the pressing and the water make the wool tangle together.

[pause]

{img:rider-dragging-felt-roll-across-grass-children-running-alongside}
The children love this part of the day more than anything. They run alongside the roll and shout and cheer for the horse. The rider calls out to them to stay back, but he is smiling. The women sing old songs about felt and wool and good luck. Every now and then, everyone stops and unrolls the felt to look at it. Is it ready yet? Not yet, says the oldest woman, so they roll it up again.

[pause]

In the shade of a ger, the old men sit and watch, sipping tea from wooden bowls. They remember felt days from when they were young, and they tell each other stories. The dogs lie in the grass, with their tongues hanging out in the heat.

[long-pause]

At last, late in the afternoon, the felt is thick and firm enough. They unroll it one final time and lay it flat on the grass. Then the women kneel down in a row along its edge. They press and rub the felt with their forearms and their elbows. They do this for a long time, chatting and laughing as they work. Slowly the felt becomes smooth and strong and even. Then it is hung up over a rope to dry in the wind.

[pause]

{img:women-kneeling-pressing-new-felt-elbows-sunlight-sheep-behind}
When the work is done, there is always a feast. The family that gets the new felt gives food and drink to everyone who helped. There is boiled mutton, and fresh yogurt, and big bowls of airag. Someone says blessings over the new felt. May it be as white as milk, they say. May it be as strong as stone. May it keep this family warm through many long winters. And then everyone eats and drinks and sings until the sun goes down behind the hills.

[long-pause]

{img:mongol-horses-small-sturdy-herd-running-steppe-manes-dust}
For the Mongols, the horse is almost everything.

[pause]

Mongol horses are small, much smaller than the big horses of Europe. They have short legs, thick necks and rather large heads. In winter their coats grow long and shaggy, like a warm woolly blanket. They do not look very grand at first sight. But they are amazingly tough and strong. They can run for hours without getting tired, and they can live on very little. In winter, when snow covers the grass, they dig through it with their hooves to find food. Very few other horses in the world could live like that.

[long-pause]

Every family on the steppe owns many horses. An ordinary family might have a few dozen, and a rich family might have hundreds. The horses live outside all year round, in big loose herds. They are never kept in stables or behind walls. They are half wild and free, and they wander the hills as they please.

[pause]

The Mongols ride everywhere they go, every single day. They ride to visit their neighbours and to check on the herds. They ride out to hunt, and they ride to the summer games. A Mongol without a horse is like a bird without wings, people say. They even joke that a Mongol would rather walk on his hands than on his feet. And that is not entirely a joke.

[pause]

{img:mongol-rider-galloping-standing-stirrups-deel-flying-hills}
They sit on wooden saddles with high fronts and high backs. Their stirrups are short, so their knees are bent and close to the horse. They can stand up in the stirrups even at a full gallop. And they can turn around and shoot an arrow backwards while riding fast. Their bows are small and very strong. They are made of thin wood and horn and animal sinew, all glued together.

[long-pause]

When a Mongol rider must travel far, he takes several horses with him. He rides on one, and the others follow along behind without a rider. When the first horse gets tired, he simply jumps onto a fresh one. Sometimes he does this without even stopping. In this way he can cover a hundred kilometres or more in a single day. That was faster than almost any traveller in the world at that time.

[pause]

{img:mongol-boy-catching-horse-urga-pole-lasso-herd-running}
To catch a horse from the running herd, the herders use a special tool. It is a long thin wooden pole with a loop of rope at the end. It is called an urga. The rider gallops right into the moving herd, calm and steady. He reaches out with the long pole and drops the loop over the chosen horse's head. Then he holds on tight while the horse slows down. It takes great skill and strength to do this well. Boys and girls practise it from a young age, often on gentle old horses first.

[long-pause]

The Mongols love their horses very deeply. They give them names, and they sing songs about them in the evenings. A famous racehorse is remembered and talked about for generations. Every morning, a herder rubs the nose of his favourite horse and talks to it softly. When a beloved horse grows old and passes away, its skull is sometimes placed on a sacred pile of stones on a hill. A blue silk scarf is tied around it with care. In this way its spirit can rest high up, close to the Eternal Blue Sky.

[pause]

{img:herd-of-horses-sunset-steppe-silhouettes-manes-golden-grass}
In the evening, the horses graze on the hillside not far from the gers. Their shapes are dark against the orange and golden sky. Now and then, one of them lifts its head and neighs into the cool air. Another horse answers from further away. And the sound carries softly across the quiet, darkening steppe.

[long-pause]

{img:mongol-woman-milking-mare-foal-tied-line-summer-morning}
In summer, the Mongols drink something very special. It is called airag, and it is made from the milk of horses.

[pause]

Mares, the female horses, give milk after their foals are born in spring. In summer, the families milk their mares several times a day, every two hours or so. It is not easy work, and it takes patience. A mare will only let her milk flow if her foal is close beside her. So the foals are tied in a long line near the gers, on a rope pegged to the ground. Their mothers stay close by, grazing and keeping watch. The woman leads a foal to its mother and lets it drink for a moment. Then she quickly milks the mare into a small wooden bucket. Afterwards the foal is allowed to drink whatever is left.

[long-pause]

The milk is poured into a big leather bag that hangs beside the door of the ger. Inside the bag there is always a little old airag from the day before. That helps the fresh milk to slowly change. And then comes the most important part of all, which is the stirring. Someone must stir the milk with a long wooden paddle, again and again. It must be stirred thousands of times every day. Everyone who walks past the bag gives it a few stirs without thinking. Guests stir it, and children stir it, and the grandmother stirs it. It almost never stops.

[pause]

After a day or two, the milk has turned into airag. It is a little sour and a little fizzy on the tongue. It is not very strong at all. It tastes a bit like a mix of yogurt and lemonade. The Mongols drink it from big wooden bowls all summer long. Some people drink several litres of it in a single day. It is full of goodness, people say, and it keeps you healthy through the whole year.

[pause]

{img:leather-bag-airag-hanging-ger-boy-stirring-wooden-paddle}
Airag is offered to every guest who comes into the ger in summer. When you visit a family, the first thing you are given is a bowl of airag. It would be very rude to say no to it. You take the bowl with your right hand, and you hold your right elbow with your left hand. You drink a little, slowly. Then you hand the bowl back to your host with a smile.

[pause]

Little Bayar, who is only three, likes to help with the stirring. He holds the paddle with both hands and pushes it up and down. Most of the time, the paddle does more work than he does. But everyone tells him he is a very good stirrer, and he beams with pride.

[long-pause]

The Mongols also make many good things from the milk of cows and sheep and goats and camels. They make thick yogurt and yellow butter. They boil milk slowly until a thick skin of cream forms on top. They make hard dried cheese, cut into small pieces and laid out to dry on the roof of the ger. This dried cheese can be kept for many months. Children chew it like sweets, and it is very hard indeed. You can chew a single piece for nearly an hour.

[pause]

{img:dried-curds-cheese-pieces-drying-on-ger-roof-sun-flies}
The Mongols call all of these the white foods, which means milk and everything made from it. White foods are thought of as pure and good and kind. In summer, people live mostly on white foods, and meat is kept for the cold months. The first airag of each summer is a reason for a small celebration. The family pours a few drops toward the sky and the earth, and toward the four directions. It is a thank you to the spirits for the milk and the grass. And then, at last, everyone drinks.

[long-pause]

{img:young-temujin-boy-steppe-riverbank-mother-hoelun-brothers-hard-times}
Genghis Khan was not always a great ruler. He began life as an ordinary boy called Temüjin. And his childhood was a very hard one.

[pause]

He was born around the year 1162, beside the river Onon in the north of Mongolia. His father was the chief of a small clan, not a rich or famous one. An old story says that baby Temüjin was born holding something red in his tiny fist. People who heard about it said it was a sign. This child would grow up to be a great man, they said.

[long-pause]

When Temüjin was about nine years old, his father took him on a long ride. They went to visit another clan, to find a future wife for the boy. That was the custom on the steppe in those days. There Temüjin met a girl called Börte, who was a year older than him. The two families agreed that the children would marry when they were grown. Temüjin stayed with Börte's family for a while, to get to know them. And his father rode home alone across the steppe.

[pause]

On the way home, his father stopped at a camp of Tatars, who were old rivals of his clan. They gave him food to eat, as travellers were given. But the food made him very ill. He reached home and passed away not long afterwards. Temüjin was called back from Börte's family. He was still only a boy, and now he had no father.

[pause]

{img:hoelun-gathering-roots-berries-river-children-poverty-steppe}
Then something sad happened. The people of the clan packed up and left. They did not want to follow a widow and her small children. They took most of the herds with them and moved away. Temüjin, his mother Hoelun and his brothers and sister were left almost alone. They had very little left in the world.

[long-pause]

Those years were very hungry ones for the little family. They lived on roots and wild berries and the small wild onions of the steppe. The boys fished in the river and caught marmots and birds. Their mother Hoelun kept them all alive through those years. She was strong and brave, and she never once gave up. She dug roots with a sharpened stick and mended their clothes by the fire. And she taught her children to always stick together.

[pause]

{img:mother-teaching-sons-bundle-of-arrows-cannot-break-fire-night}
One evening by the fire, she gave each of her sons a single arrow. Break it, she said, and each boy snapped his arrow easily. Then she tied five arrows together in a bundle. Now break this, she said, and none of them could do it. One arrow breaks easily, she told them. But a bundle of arrows cannot be broken. Stay together, and nobody can break you.

[pause]

Life stayed difficult for Temüjin for many years. Once he was caught by a rival clan. They put a heavy wooden collar around his neck so he could not run away. But one night, during a feast, he quietly slipped away into the dark. He hid in the river with only his face above the water. A kind man saw him there, in the moonlight. Instead of calling the others, he decided to help him. He hid Temüjin in a cart piled high with wool. The searchers came and looked around, and then they went away. Temüjin was free again.

[long-pause]

He never forgot those years, not for the rest of his life. He remembered the hunger and the worry, and the people who had left. And he remembered the few people who had been kind to him. When he became powerful, he rewarded every one of them. And he made sure that nobody loyal to him would ever be left alone on the steppe like that.

[long-pause]

{img:borte-young-woman-ger-door-headdress-strong-calm-face-steppe}
When Temüjin was about sixteen, he rode back to the family of Börte. He had never forgotten the girl he had been promised to. And she had never forgotten him either.

[pause]

They were married on the steppe, with a small and simple feast. Börte's family gave the young couple a wonderful gift. It was a coat of black sable fur, soft and shining and very precious. Temüjin owned very little else in the world. But he took that coat to a powerful chief, who had once been his father's friend. He gave the coat to the chief as a gift of respect. And the chief agreed to help him and stand by him. It was the very beginning of Temüjin's rise.

[pause]

Börte brought her own things to the new home. She brought a painted chest, some good felt, and a few copper pots. She set up the ger just the way she liked it. She knew how to milk and make cheese and sew boots, and she was quick and careful. The young couple were poor, but they were happy together.

[long-pause]

But not long after the wedding, trouble came to the camp. Riders from another tribe came at dawn, and everyone had to flee. Temüjin escaped into the mountains, but Börte was taken away by the riders. Temüjin was heartbroken, and he prayed on the sacred mountain for help. Then he gathered his friends and allies around him. Months later, they rode out together to bring her back home.

[pause]

The story says it was night when they reached the other camp. People were running here and there in the dark, and there was great confusion. Temüjin rode through it all, calling out her name again and again. Börte, Börte, he called. And in the moonlight, Börte heard his voice and knew it at once. She ran toward him and caught hold of the reins of his horse. He looked down and saw that it was her. The old book says they held each other, and they were together again, as before.

[pause]

{img:night-reunion-moonlight-rider-woman-holding-reins-steppe-camp}
Börte became Temüjin's first wife and his most trusted adviser in everything. She was wise and calm and very clever. When he was unsure what to do, he listened to her advice. Once she quietly warned him that a close friend could no longer be trusted. He listened to her, and later he saw that she was right.

[long-pause]

Together they had four sons and several daughters. Their sons became great rulers after their father was gone. Their daughters married the chiefs of other peoples, and they were given real power of their own. Some of them ruled whole lands while their husbands were away. Genghis Khan trusted his daughters very much, and he often said so.

[pause]

{img:borte-managing-camp-giving-orders-women-officials-gers-busy}
In the year 1220, Börte is about sixty years old. Her husband is far away in the west, with the armies. He has been gone for more than a year now. She stays at home in Mongolia, at the great camp of the family. She runs everything at home, calmly and carefully. She looks after the family, the herds and all the many camps. She makes decisions and settles small quarrels between people. She welcomes visitors with tea and kind words. Messengers come and go from her ger every day. And every day, like so many women on the steppe, she waits for news from the west.

[long-pause]

{img:kurultai-1206-great-gathering-steppe-banners-white-standard-crowd}
Temüjin slowly grew from a hungry boy into a great leader. Over many years, step by step, he brought the tribes of the steppe together.

[pause]

He did this in a new way that people had not seen before. In the old days, people were loyal first to their own family and clan. Rich families ruled, and poor families simply followed them. But Temüjin did things differently from the chiefs before him. He chose people for what they could do, and not for who their father was. A brave herder from a poor family could become a leader of thousands. A loyal friend could become a great lord, with many herds of his own. People liked this very much, and many came to join him.

[long-pause]

He was also known for keeping his promises, even when it was hard. And he was known for rewarding loyalty generously. If you were loyal to Temüjin, he would never forget it. If you broke your word to him, he would never forget that either.

[pause]

In the year 1206, all the peoples of the felt tents gathered by the river Onon. It was the same river where Temüjin had been born. It was a great meeting, which the Mongols called a kurultai. Chiefs came riding in from every part of the steppe. Thousands of gers were set up in rows on the green grass. Thousands of horses grazed on the hills nearby. Cooking fires smoked everywhere, and the air smelled of mutton and tea. And there, the chiefs named Temüjin their great ruler. He took a new name for himself, which was Genghis Khan.

[pause]

{img:white-standard-nine-horse-tails-pole-wind-steppe-sacred-banner}
What does the name Genghis really mean? Nobody is quite sure, even today. It might mean ocean, or strong, or ruler of everything. Khan simply means ruler, or king. So perhaps his name meant something like the ruler of all the lands between the seas.

[long-pause]

At the kurultai, a great white banner was raised high above the crowd. It was a tall pole with nine white horse tails tied at the very top. The tails flew and rippled in the wind of the steppe. This was the banner of peace. There was also a black banner, which was used in times of war. The Mongols believed that the spirit of their ruler lived inside his banner.

[pause]

From that day on, the Mongols were one people, one nation of the felt tents. Old quarrels between the clans were forgotten, or simply not allowed anymore. The raiding of each other's herds came to an end. A man could now ride across the steppe in safety, from one camp to the next. That had never really been true before.

[pause]

{img:genghis-khan-older-grey-beard-seated-tent-calm-eyes-advisers}
After that, the armies rode out, first to the south and later to the far west. That story is long and hard, and we will leave it there, far away. Tonight we stay at home, on the quiet steppe. In 1220, Genghis Khan is about fifty-eight years old. His beard has turned grey, and his eyes are calm and watchful. He is far away in the west, in a big tent among his advisers. And at home, ordinary life goes on as it always has.

[pause]

At home, the herders still get up before dawn to milk the mares. Children still chase the lambs and ride out to bring in the horses. The grandmothers still spin wool in the sun beside the doors. The empire is very big, but the day of a family is still small and simple.

[long-pause]

Genghis Khan himself never learned to read or write a single word. But he understood very well how important writing was. He ordered that his laws and decisions should be written down carefully. He kept scribes with him in his camp, wherever he went. He sent written orders on long scrolls across the whole empire. And he made sure that his sons learned the new letters. The boy who once dug roots beside the river now ruled from a tent full of writers.

[long-pause]

{img:mongol-judges-court-ger-hearing-case-scribe-writing-scroll}
Genghis Khan made laws for his people to live by. These laws were called the Yassa.

[pause]

Nobody has ever found a complete copy of the Yassa. Perhaps it was never all written down in one single place. But people who lived at the time wrote about many of its rules. Those writings tell us how Genghis Khan hoped his people would live together.

[long-pause]

Some of the rules were about keeping the peace between neighbours. Do not take another person's animals, said the Yassa. Do not tell lies, and do not fight among yourselves. In the old days, these things had happened all the time on the steppe. Now they were firmly forbidden by the khan. Judges sat in special gers to listen to quarrels and decide what was fair. A scribe sat beside them and wrote down every decision on a scroll. People took the rules very seriously, and most of them were glad of them.

[pause]

Some rules were about respecting the land and the water. Do not wash in running water, said the Yassa, and do not make a river dirty. Rivers were sacred to the Mongols. And clean water meant life for both people and animals on the dry steppe.

[pause]

{img:mongol-traveller-alone-safe-steppe-golden-plate-paiza-peaceful}
Some rules were about faith and belief. Genghis Khan allowed everyone to follow their own religion freely. There were Christians and Buddhists, Muslims and Daoists, and followers of the old sky religion. Priests and monks of all faiths did not have to pay any taxes. That was quite unusual in those days. Many rulers in other lands made all their people follow one single religion. Genghis Khan did not do that.

[long-pause]

There were also rules about helping travellers on the road. If a traveller came to your ger, you had to give him food and a place to sleep. This was true even if he was a complete stranger. The steppe is a big and lonely place, especially in winter. A traveller who was turned away might not find another ger for days.

[pause]

So when a stranger appears at the door, the family welcomes him in. They give him the warm seat near the stove and a bowl of salty milk tea. They give him a bit of dried cheese and some boiled mutton. They do not ask too many questions at first. Only later, when he has eaten, do they ask where he has come from. And he tells them news from the places he has seen along the way.

[pause]

A story was told about the Mongol lands under Genghis Khan. People said a young woman carrying a golden plate could walk across the whole empire alone. And nobody would bother her or take the gold away. It was probably not quite true, of course. But it shows what people believed in those days. Under the great khan, they said, the roads were safe.

[pause]

{img:quiet-steppe-camp-evening-neighbours-sharing-food-peace}
For the family in our story, the laws mean one big and simple thing. They mean peace at home, day and night. They do not have to worry that someone will take their horses in the night. They can let their animals graze far away on the hills. They can sleep soundly without someone sitting up to guard the camp. In the evenings, neighbours ride over to share food and stories by the fire. After so many years of trouble on the steppe, that peace is a very great gift.

[long-pause]

{img:mongol-family-group-outside-ger-grandmother-mother-children-dogs}
Now let us meet a family who lives on the steppe in 1220.

[pause]

They live in the green valley beside the small river. There are three gers standing close together on the grass. One ger is for the grandmother, one is for the family, and one is for storage and guests. All around them, their animals graze peacefully. There are about two hundred sheep and goats, and twenty cows with long horns. There are thirty horses of every colour, and four big camels for moving camp.

[long-pause]

The father of the family is called Batu, but he is not at home. He rode west with the army more than a year ago. He is one of the khan's many riders. He sends news home whenever he can, through the messengers of the khan. The last news came three months ago, on a cold spring day. He was well, the messenger said, and he was very far away. He was in a land of great rivers and cities, with walls and gardens. Nobody at home can really imagine such a place.

[pause]

The mother is called Sarnai, which means rose. She runs everything while Batu is away. She looks after the herds and the children and the whole camp. Mongol women have always carried a lot of responsibility. When the men are away, the women are in charge of everything. And they are very good at it.

[pause]

{img:sarnai-mother-checking-herd-hillside-horse-whip-wind-hair}
Every morning, Sarnai rides up the hill to look over the herds. The wind blows her hair across her face, and she pushes it back. She counts the animals with a quick look and checks the far corners of the valley. Then she rides down again, already thinking about the work of the day.

[pause]

There are four children in the family. The eldest is a girl of fourteen, called Khulan. She is a wonderful rider, quiet and brave. Next comes a boy of eleven, called Temur, who wants to be a rider like his father. Then there is a girl of seven, called Altan, which means gold. And last is a little boy of three, called Bayar, which means joy. He is nearly always laughing about something.

[long-pause]

Batu's mother lives with the family as well. She is old now, about seventy years of age. Her face is brown and lined, like an old map of the steppe. She cannot ride the way she did when she was young. But she knows everything about animals and weather and healing herbs. She knows all the old stories and all the old songs. And she watches over the little ones while the others are working.

[pause]

{img:grandmother-teaching-girl-herbs-steppe-flowers-basket-summer}
In summer, she walks slowly over the hills with Altan and a small basket. She shows the girl which flowers are good for tea and which are good for a sore stomach. This one is for coughs, she says, and this one helps you sleep. Altan listens carefully and puts each flower into the basket.

[pause]

There is also a dog in the family, a big shaggy black dog. He has a brown spot just above each of his eyes. People say those spots are like a second pair of eyes. That way the dog can watch the camp even while he is asleep. He guards the gers at night and barks at every stranger who comes near.

[long-pause]

Sarnai often thinks about her husband, far away in the west. She does not talk about it very much. But every time a rider appears on the horizon, she stops what she is doing. She shades her eyes with her hand and looks for a long moment. Is it a messenger, she wonders, and is there any news? Usually it is just a neighbour riding by. Then she goes calmly back to her work. But she keeps looking up, now and then, all day long.

[long-pause]

{img:dawn-inside-ger-mother-lighting-fire-dung-tea-kettle-children-sleeping}
Let us spend one summer day with this family, from morning until night.

[pause]

Sarnai wakes up before dawn, while the children are still asleep under their sheepskins. It is cool and dark inside the ger. She kneels by the stove and lights the fire with a little dry grass. The fuel is dried animal dung, because there is no wood on the open steppe. Dried dung burns well and gives a steady, gentle heat. She sets a pot of water on the fire to boil. Then she adds tea leaves and fresh milk and a pinch of salt. Mongol tea is salty and milky, and it warms you right through.

[long-pause]

When the tea is ready, the first thing she does is something special. She carries a little of the first tea outside, into the grey morning light. With a small wooden spoon, she flicks a few drops toward the sky. Then a few drops toward the earth, and toward each of the four directions. It is a small gift to the spirits for a good and peaceful day. Only after that does the family drink their tea.

[pause]

Then the work of the day begins for everyone. Khulan goes out to the line of foals to milk the mares. Temur jumps on a horse and rides off to bring the herd back from the hill. The grandmother sits by the door in the first sun, spinning wool and watching little Bayar. Altan helps her mother make yogurt in a big wooden tub.

[pause]

{img:boy-temur-riding-bareback-bringing-horses-down-hill-dawn-mist}
Temur rides bareback, without a saddle, his legs tight around the horse's sides. The mist is still lying in the valley as he climbs the hill. He finds the herd grazing near the top, their backs wet with dew. He whistles and calls, and the horses lift their heads and start to move. Then he brings them all down the hill at a trot, their manes bouncing in the morning light.

[long-pause]

Later in the morning, the sheep and goats are taken out to graze. Altan goes with them, together with the big black dog. Even at seven years old, she can herd a flock of two hundred animals. She sits on a small, patient horse that knows the work as well as she does. She keeps the flock together and away from the soft ground by the river. And she sings to them as she rides, old herding songs that tell the sheep where to go.

[pause]

At midday, the family gathers in the ger to eat. There are bowls of thick yogurt and pieces of dried cheese. There is a little flat bread, fried in mutton fat until it is golden. And there is airag, of course, plenty of cool airag. After the meal, some of them rest in the shade beside the ger. The sun is hot now, and the flies are buzzing lazily in the grass.

[pause]

{img:little-girl-on-small-horse-herding-sheep-dog-steppe-midday}
In the afternoon, Sarnai rides out to check on the cows in the next valley. She takes Bayar on the saddle in front of her, holding him with one arm. He laughs and points at everything the whole way there. The grandmother sits in the shade and sews a pair of felt boots. Khulan stirs the airag bag and churns butter in a tall wooden churn. Temur practises with his bow behind the ger, shooting at a target made of old felt.

[long-pause]

As the sun goes down, the animals come home from the hills. The sheep and goats are put into a pen made of felt and wooden fences. The foals are tied once more in their long line. The dog lies down across the doorway with a big sigh. The family eats a simple supper of soup with noodles and a little mutton. Then they all sit outside the ger and watch the sky turn red and purple.

[pause]

{img:family-sitting-outside-ger-sunset-sky-purple-sheep-pen-dog}
The grandmother tells a story while the light fades. It is about a clever horse who saved his master from a great flood. The children have heard it many times before. But they listen anyway, leaning against her and against each other. As the first stars appear, Sarnai looks toward the west, where the sun has gone. That is where her husband is, so very far away. And she says a quiet prayer to the Eternal Blue Sky.

[long-pause]

{img:mongol-family-migrating-camels-loaded-ger-parts-carts-steppe-autumn}
The Mongols do not live in one place all year. They move, usually about four times a year, once for each season.

[pause]

Every family has its own special places for each season. There is a spring place, a summer place, an autumn place and a winter place. Families have used the same places for many generations. Batu's father used them, and his father before him, and so on, far back in time. Each place is chosen carefully for the season it belongs to.

[long-pause]

The summer place lies beside a river or a lake, in a wide open valley. There is plenty of grass and water there, and the wind keeps the flies away. The spring place is somewhere sheltered and calm, where the lambs and foals can be born safely. The autumn place has rich, tall grass, so the animals can grow fat before the cold. And the winter place lies in a sheltered valley, tucked behind a hill and out of the wind. It is close to good grass that the snow does not bury too deeply.

[pause]

When it is time to move, the whole family works together. The gers are taken down, and the felt is rolled up into big bundles. The lattice walls are folded flat, and the roof poles are tied together in neat bunches. The chests and pots and beds are packed and tied with rope. It only takes an hour or two, because everyone knows exactly what to do. Then everything is loaded onto the camels and the creaking ox carts.

[pause]

{img:ox-cart-big-wooden-wheels-loaded-ger-felt-chests-children-on-top}
Then the long line slowly moves off across the steppe. The camels walk in front with their tall loads swaying. The ox carts follow behind, creaking on their big wooden wheels. The herds of sheep and goats and cows walk ahead, grazing as they go. The horses run alongside, and the riders keep them all together. The dog trots happily at the side, sniffing at everything. And the youngest children ride on top of the carts, sitting on the rolled-up felt, like little kings and queens.

[long-pause]

The journey may take a single day, or it may take several days. They travel slowly, because the animals must eat as they walk. In the evening, they stop in a sheltered spot beside some water. They do not put up the whole ger for just one night. They make a simple shelter of felt and poles instead. They light a small fire and cook a pot of soup. And then they sleep under the open sky, with the animals resting all around.

[pause]

{img:migration-evening-camp-fire-simple-shelter-animals-resting-stars}
Those nights on the road are special for the children. They lie wrapped in their warm deels and look up at the stars. The fire crackles softly, and the camels make low grumbling sounds in the dark. The grandmother points to the stars and names them, one by one.

[pause]

When they reach the new place, everything is put up again, just as it was before. The door faces south, as it always does. Everything inside goes back to its proper place. The chests stand by the walls, and the altar stands in the north. By the evening, it feels as if they have always lived there. The fire is burning, and the tea is boiling, and the animals are grazing nearby. Home has moved to a new valley, but it is still home.

[long-pause]

This autumn, Sarnai must lead the move herself, because Batu is not here. She must decide when it is the right time to go. If they go too early, the summer grass is wasted. If they go too late, the first snow could catch them on the way. So she watches the sky carefully every morning, and she smells the wind. And one morning, when the grass has turned golden and the geese are flying south, she speaks to the family. Tomorrow, she says, we go.

[long-pause]

{img:mongol-toddler-sitting-on-horse-saddle-mother-holding-laughing}
On the steppe, children learn to ride almost before they can walk.

[pause]

When they are two or three years old, they are put on a horse for the first time. It is always a gentle old horse that walks slowly and never startles. The little ones are tied into the saddle, so that they cannot fall off. They hold on to the thick mane and bounce along, laughing. And they love it more than anything. By four or five they can ride on their own, and by six they can gallop.

[long-pause]

Children have their own work from a young age, just like the grown-ups. At five or six, they watch over the lambs and the young goats. At seven or eight, they can herd the sheep all by themselves. They fetch water from the river in leather buckets. They gather dried dung for the fire in big baskets on their backs. They help with the milking, morning and evening. Girls and boys do much the same work as each other. And both girls and boys learn to ride and herd and shoot a bow.

[pause]

But there is always plenty of time for play as well. A favourite game uses the little ankle bones of sheep. Each small bone has four different sides. One side looks like a horse, and one like a sheep. One side looks like a goat, and one like a camel. The children throw the bones and see how they land. They flick them across the floor of the ger, trying to hit each other's bones. They line them up and race them, like little herds. There are dozens of different games with ankle bones. Mongolian children still play them today.

[pause]

{img:children-playing-ankle-bones-shagai-ger-floor-felt-rug-laughing}
Outside, they wrestle on the soft grass, just like the grown-up wrestlers. They race their horses along the river and back again. They play tag around the gers and hide behind the carts. They chase the young animals, who run away bleating. They make little bows and arrows from willow sticks and string. And in the long winter evenings, they sit close to the old people and listen to stories.

[long-pause]

The children learn from everyone around them. They learn from their parents and their grandparents, and from older brothers and sisters. They learn from the neighbours, and from every visitor who comes by. They learn which plants are good to eat and which ones make you ill. They learn how to read the weather from the clouds and the wind. They learn how to tell when a sheep is not feeling well. They learn how to follow tracks in the grass and in the snow. And they learn how to find their way home across the empty steppe, by the hills and the sun and the stars.

[pause]

{img:grandfather-showing-boy-tracks-in-snow-steppe-winter-pointing}
Temur, the boy of eleven, is learning to use a bow. His father made it for him before he rode away to the west. It is small but very strong, made of wood and horn. Every evening, Temur practises behind the ger until it is too dark to see. At first he missed the target nearly every time. Now he hits it most of the time, and sometimes right in the middle. When his father comes home, Temur wants to show him what he can do. He imagines that moment over and over again. His father watching him, and then nodding slowly. Good, his father will say. That is very good, my son.

[long-pause]

{img:mongol-women-gathering-camp-council-gers-confident-talking}
Mongol women are strong and free, more than in many other lands at that time.

[pause]

They ride horses just as well as the men do. They herd the animals and run the camp. They make the gers and the felt and nearly all of the clothes. They make all the milk foods, from airag to the hard dried cheese. They handle trade and keep track of what the family owns. And when the men are away, which is often, the women are in charge of everything.

[long-pause]

Visitors from other lands were quite surprised by this. Travellers from Europe and from China wrote about it in their books. The Mongol women drive the carts, they wrote, and they load the camels. They ride like the men do, and they give orders. And their husbands listen carefully to what they say.

[pause]

Some women had a great deal of power and respect. Hoelun, the mother of Genghis Khan, kept her children alive when everyone else had left. Later she gave wise advice to her son, and he listened to her. Börte, his wife, was his closest and most trusted adviser. His daughters ruled over whole lands of their own. Later in the empire, some women even ruled for a time on behalf of their young sons. One famous Mongol princess was a champion wrestler. She said she would only marry a man who could beat her in a match. For a long time, nobody could.

[pause]

{img:mongol-woman-riding-galloping-steppe-headdress-confident-free}
Women's work on the steppe is hard and never quite finished. A woman is up before everyone else, and she goes to bed after everyone else. There is milking and cooking and sewing, felting and herding and moving camp. There are the children to care for, from babies to almost grown. But women are deeply respected for all that they do. The Mongols have a little saying about it. The man is the head of the ger, they say, but the woman is its neck. And the neck is what turns the head.

[long-pause]

Mongol women love beautiful jewellery. They wear headdresses decorated with silver and red coral and blue turquoise. They wear earrings and rings and bracelets that jingle softly when they move. On special days, married women of high rank wear a very tall hat. It is called a boqta, and it looks like a tall thin tube. Some of them are almost a metre high. They are decorated with feathers and pearls and shining silk. When a woman wearing one rides into a camp, she must bend low at every door.

[pause]

{img:mongol-noblewoman-tall-boqta-headdress-feathers-pearls-silk-ger}
Sarnai has worn a tall hat like that only twice in her life. Once at her own wedding, and once at a great gathering many years ago. She keeps it carefully wrapped in cloth, inside a painted chest. Sometimes Khulan asks if she may see it. Then Sarnai takes it out, slowly and gently, and holds it up in the light. Khulan touches the soft feathers with her fingertips. One day you will wear one too, says her mother. Khulan smiles shyly and helps to wrap it up again, very carefully.

[pause]

On ordinary days, Sarnai wears a simple headscarf and a plain blue deel. Her only jewellery is a pair of small silver earrings, which Batu gave her long ago. She touches them sometimes when she thinks of him. The earrings are small, but they mean a great deal to her.

[long-pause]

When the khan's armies were away, the great camp at home was run by women. They decided where the herds should graze and when to move. They settled quarrels between families and listened to everyone. They welcomed visitors and sent supplies to the armies far away. One traveller wrote that the camp moved as smoothly as a city on the march. There were carts in long rows, and great gers on wheels pulled by twenty oxen. And at the very front of it all, he wrote, rode a woman, deciding where to stop.

[long-pause]

{img:yam-relay-station-steppe-gers-fresh-horses-rider-arriving-dust}
How do you rule an empire that stretches across half of Asia? And how do you get a message from one end of it to the other?

[pause]

Genghis Khan had a very clever answer to that question. It was called the Yam.

[long-pause]

The Yam was a long chain of stations stretching across the whole empire. Every thirty or forty kilometres along the main routes, there was a station. Each station was a small group of gers, with a few families living there. They always kept fresh horses ready, as well as food and drink and beds. They were ready by day and by night, in summer and in winter.

[pause]

When an official messenger arrives, he jumps down from his tired, sweating horse. He eats a little and drinks a bowl of tea. Then he takes a fresh horse from the station and rides on as fast as he can. Sometimes he does not stop to sleep at all. He ties himself into the saddle and dozes as he rides along. When a message is very urgent, it can travel two or three hundred kilometres in a single day. Nothing else in the world at that time is nearly as fast.

[pause]

{img:messenger-riding-night-bells-on-belt-moonlight-steppe-alone}
The messengers wear little bells on their belts as they ride. As they come close to a station, the keepers hear the bells ringing across the grass. They get a fresh horse saddled and ready at once. So the messenger loses no time at all when he arrives. He simply jumps from one horse to the other and is gone again.

[long-pause]

Each official traveller carries a special tablet with him. It is called a paiza, and it is a flat plate of metal with writing on it. A gold paiza is for the most important travellers, and a silver one for others. Simple ones of wood are for the least important. The writing on it means, this person travels for the khan, so give him what he needs. With a paiza, you can get horses and food at every station along the road. Without one, you get nothing at all.

[pause]

{img:golden-paiza-tablet-mongol-script-hole-cord-hand-close}
The families who run the stations do this as their duty to the khan. They do it instead of going to war or paying other kinds of tax. They look after the horses and keep them fed and healthy. They keep the gers clean and warm for the travellers who come. They cook soup and boil tea at all hours of the day and night. And they see every kind of person pass by their door. There are messengers and officials, traders and monks, and visitors from faraway kings. They come from China and Persia and the lands far beyond.

[long-pause]

For Sarnai's family, the Yam is very important indeed. That is because it carries news from the west, news of the armies. And sometimes it brings a few words from the men who are far away. These are not written letters, usually, because most of the men cannot write. But they can send a few words with a messenger who is riding east. Your husband is well, the messenger might say. Your son is well. He says to tell the family he is thinking of them. Sometimes, those few short words are the most precious thing in the whole world.

[pause]

The station near Sarnai's valley is run by an old couple and their grown-up sons. Sometimes Temur rides over there to help with the horses. The old woman gives him a bowl of tea and a piece of cheese for his trouble. He loves to watch the messengers come in, dusty and tired, with their bells jingling. They jump off one horse and onto another in a single moment. And then they are gone again, over the next hill. One day, Temur thinks to himself, I will ride just like that.

[long-pause]

{img:silk-road-caravan-camels-merchants-arriving-mongol-camp-goods}
The Mongols are herders, and not farmers or craftsmen. They have animals and milk, wool and felt and leather. But there are many things they cannot make for themselves. They need iron for knives and pots, and they love silk and tea. They need grain and needles and thread. So, for all of these things, they trade with others.

[long-pause]

For a long time, the steppe was a risky place for traders to cross. A caravan could be robbed on the way, and the traders could lose everything. But under Genghis Khan, the roads became much safer. And the khan liked traders and wanted them to come. He gave them his protection and let them travel freely through his lands. Sometimes he even lent them money to help them buy their goods.

[pause]

So the caravans came, slowly, from every direction. They came in long lines of camels, each one loaded high with goods. From China in the east came silk and tea and fine porcelain bowls. From Persia and the lands in the west came cotton and glass, silver and spices. From the forests in the north came soft and valuable furs. And all of it passed through the Mongol lands, along the old roads. Much later, people would call those roads the Silk Road.

[pause]

{img:merchant-unrolling-silk-inside-ger-family-admiring-colours}
When a caravan comes near the camp, it is a very big event for everyone. The children run out to stare at the tall camels and their bells. The traders unpack their goods on felt rugs in front of the gers. There are bolts of silk in red and blue and gold, shining in the sun. There are iron pots and sharp knives, and bags of salt and grain. There are needles and thread, and tiny mirrors, and strings of coloured beads. The families gather around to look and touch and talk about prices.

[long-pause]

The Mongols pay the traders with animals and wool and felt and furs. A good horse can buy a great many things. Sometimes they pay with small pieces of silver instead. The traders count everything carefully and weigh the silver on little scales. Everyone talks about prices, and it is noisy and cheerful and fun.

[pause]

{img:caravan-leaving-steppe-sunset-camels-line-dust-trader-waving}
Sarnai buys a little bag of tea and a new iron pot for cooking. And she buys a piece of red silk for Khulan, who is almost grown up now. She will need a fine new deel for the summer gathering. Khulan holds the red silk against her cheek and closes her eyes. It is so soft and smooth and cool. She has never touched anything so soft in her life. She thanks her mother again and again, and she cannot stop smiling.

[pause]

That evening, Khulan sits by the lamp and holds the silk in her lap. Her grandmother shows her how to cut it and how to sew a neat edge. They work slowly, talking quietly, while the others sleep.

[long-pause]

The traders also bring news from the places they have passed through. They have seen cities and markets and palaces, far away in the west. They tell stories about the lands where the armies are. They say the khan has taken great cities, and that the armies are moving further still. Sarnai listens carefully to everything they say. Is there any news of the men from this valley, she asks? The traders shake their heads kindly. The armies are huge, they say, and they do not know every rider. But they have heard that the men are well. It is something, at least. Sarnai thanks them and gives them a bowl of airag for the road.

[long-pause]

{img:karakorum-early-camp-orkhon-valley-gers-tents-wooden-buildings-1220}
In 1220, Genghis Khan chose a place to be the heart of his empire. It was a valley in the middle of Mongolia, beside the river Orkhon. The place was called Karakorum.

[pause]

At first, Karakorum was really just a great camp on the grass. There were many gers and tents, set up in long rows. There were storehouses and workshops, where smiths and carpenters worked. It was a place to keep supplies and treasure safe. It was a place where people could meet and talk. But in time, it would grow into something much bigger.

[long-pause]

The Orkhon valley is a very good place to live. It is green and wide, with a clear river and plenty of good grass. Mountains rise to the west and keep off the worst of the wind. And it lies near the middle of all the Mongol lands. Long before the Mongols, other peoples of the steppe had their capitals here too. It is an old and important place, where rulers have often gathered.

[pause]

After Genghis Khan, his son built a real city at Karakorum. It had walls and a palace, streets and markets and temples. People from all over the empire came to live there. There were Mongol nobles and Chinese craftsmen, Persian traders and visitors from Europe. There were Muslim and Christian and Buddhist priests, each with their own place to pray. All of them lived together in one city, in the middle of the steppe.

[pause]

{img:karakorum-silver-fountain-tree-palace-hall-guests-later-wonder}
A visitor from Europe described one wonder of Karakorum a few decades later. A great silver tree stood just in front of the palace. It had been made by a skilled craftsman from Paris who had come to Mongolia. At the top of the tree stood a silver angel holding a trumpet. Four silver lions sat around its roots. And from four silver pipes came four different drinks for the guests. There was mare's milk and wine, a sweet honey drink and rice beer. When the khan wanted his guests to drink, the angel lifted its trumpet. And then the drinks began to flow.

[long-pause]

But in 1220, all of that still lies in the future. For now, Karakorum is just the place where the great camp gathers. Messengers come and go there, and goods are stored in the warehouses. The family of the khan sometimes stays there for a while. Smoke rises from hundreds of gers, and horses graze by the river. Children play between the carts, and dogs bark at the strangers.

[pause]

{img:orkhon-valley-today-green-river-horses-stone-turtle-later}
Much later, Karakorum was left empty and almost forgotten. Today, very little of the old city is left. A big stone turtle lies quietly in the grass. Long ago, it held up a tall stone tablet on its back. There are buried foundations, and a scattering of old stones. And next to them stands a beautiful Buddhist monastery, built later from some of the old stones. Its white walls are topped with many small white towers. Horses graze all around it, just as they always have.

[long-pause]

In 1220, Sarnai has never been to Karakorum. It is many days' ride away from her valley. But Batu told her about it before he rode off to the west. There are hundreds of gers there, standing in long rows, he said. There are carts piled high with goods, and horses as far as the eye can see. There are people there from every land under the sky. Sarnai tried hard to imagine it, sitting by the stove. One day, she thinks, when he comes home, maybe we will go there together. And she smiles quietly at the thought.

[long-pause]

{img:mongol-shaman-drum-feathers-ceremony-fire-night-ger-family}
What do the Mongols believe in?

[pause]

The greatest god is the Eternal Blue Sky, called Tengri, high above everything. Below the sky is the Earth, called Etügen, who is like a mother to all things. Between the sky and the earth live people and animals. And all around them, the Mongols believe, are spirits. There are spirits of mountains and rivers and springs. There are spirits of the ancestors, who watch over their families. And there is the spirit of the fire in every hearth.

[long-pause]

Every ger has its own fire, and that fire is sacred. You must never throw dirt or rubbish into it. You must never step over the fire, or poke it with a knife. You must not splash water on it carelessly. The fire spirit protects the family who lives around it. When a new ger is set up, the very first thing done is to light the fire. And then the fire is given a little gift of milk or butter.

[pause]

Some people are believed to be able to speak with the spirits. They are called shamans, and they can be men or women. A shaman wears a special coat hung with ribbons and little bells and small round mirrors. He beats a big flat drum with a stick, slowly and steadily. He sings and sways in the firelight while the family watches. People believe that his spirit can travel to the world of the spirits. There he can ask them for help with many things. He can ask for healing, for good weather, or for finding lost animals.

[pause]

{img:ovoo-stone-cairn-hilltop-blue-silk-scarves-wind-offerings-riders}
On hilltops and mountain passes, there are piles of stones called ovoos. When travellers pass an ovoo, they always stop for a moment. They walk around it three times, in the same direction the sun moves. They pick up a stone and add it to the pile. They leave a small gift there as well. It might be a splash of milk, a coin, or a blue silk scarf tied to a stick. Then they say a quiet prayer for a safe journey, and they ride on.

[pause]

The children love ovoos and always want to stop at them. Altan likes to choose a special stone, smooth and round, from the river bank. She carries it in her sash for days until they pass an ovoo. Then she places it on the pile very carefully and walks around three times.

[long-pause]

Genghis Khan himself believed deeply in the Eternal Blue Sky. Before he made great decisions, he climbed a sacred mountain alone. He took off his hat and his belt, as a sign that he was humble. He knelt down on the earth and prayed, sometimes for several days. Then he came down from the mountain and did what he had decided.

[pause]

But he also respected the faiths of other people. He let everyone believe whatever they wanted to believe. Christian priests and Buddhist monks came to the Mongol camps. So did Muslim scholars and Daoist masters from China. They all prayed in their own ways, side by side. And the khan listened to all of them with real interest.

[pause]

{img:different-faiths-priests-monk-imam-gathered-khan-tent-respectful}
Later, his grandson held a famous meeting about faith. He invited a Christian, a Muslim and a Buddhist to his tent. Each one explained why he thought his own faith was the best. They talked and talked, for a whole long day. At the end, the khan gave them all plenty of airag to drink. Then everyone went home, tired but content. Nobody had won, but everyone had been heard.

[long-pause]

{img:burkhan-khaldun-sacred-mountain-forest-slopes-river-onon-mist}
In the north of Mongolia, close to where Genghis Khan was born, there is a mountain. It is called Burkhan Khaldun. It is the most sacred mountain of all the Mongols.

[pause]

It is not a very high mountain, compared with others. Its slopes are covered with dark forests of pine and larch. Its top is bare and rocky, with patches of snow even in early summer. Two rivers begin on its slopes, the Onon and the Kherlen. These were the rivers of Genghis Khan's childhood. Bears and deer and wolves live quietly in its forests. In the mornings, mist often hangs among the trees, soft and grey.

[long-pause]

When Temüjin was young, the mountain once gave him shelter. On the day that Börte was taken, he fled up its slopes into the forest. Riders searched the mountain for him for three long days. But the trees and the rocks hid him well. When the riders finally gave up and left, Temüjin came down again. He was safe and unharmed.

[pause]

He was so thankful that he made a solemn promise. He hung his belt around his neck and took off his hat. He knelt down facing the morning sun. He sprinkled a little milk into the air as a gift. And then he prayed out loud. He said that the mountain had protected his life, though it was as small as a louse. He promised to honour the mountain every single morning. And he promised that his children and their children would honour it too, forever.

[pause]

{img:young-temujin-praying-kneeling-mountain-forest-belt-around-neck}
And they did honour it, for hundreds of years. The mountain became a holy place for the Mongols. Ordinary people were not allowed to go there at all. Only the khans and their families could climb its slopes. Later there were long years when nobody was allowed to visit. But the people of the steppe never forgot their sacred mountain.

[long-pause]

Many people believe that Genghis Khan was buried somewhere on or near this mountain. But nobody knows exactly where the place is. The secret was kept very carefully, and it is still kept today. That is another story, and we will come to it a little later.

[pause]

The herders who live in the valleys near the mountain treat it with great respect. They do not shout or make loud noises when they pass beneath it. They do not cut its trees or hunt its animals without good reason. When they ride by, they often bow their heads a little. The grandmother in our story says the mountain is like an old, wise guardian. It watches over the steppe, she says, and it remembers everything.

[pause]

{img:sacred-mountain-silent-forest-snow-patches-mist-evening-light}
Today, Burkhan Khaldun is carefully protected, as a World Heritage Site. Mongolians still honour the mountain as their ancestors did. Every few years, there is a great ceremony there. People climb slowly up to the ovoo at the very top. They walk around it three times and leave blue scarves and quiet prayers. The wind carries the scarves fluttering out over the forests. And the mountain stays as quiet and green as it was eight hundred years ago.

[long-pause]

{img:naadam-summer-games-steppe-crowds-gers-banners-wrestlers-horses}
In midsummer, the people of the steppe gather together for the great games. Later these games will be called Naadam, which simply means games. There are three of them: wrestling, archery and horse racing. People call them the three games of men. But women and children take part in them too.

[long-pause]

People come from far away to be there, riding for days across the grass. They put up their gers on a wide meadow beside a river. There are hundreds of gers and thousands of horses, as far as the eye can see. Everyone wears their very best clothes for the games. There are bright deels in blue and red and green, and fine hats with little tassels. It is a time to meet old friends and to make new ones. People trade and talk and plan weddings for the autumn. And, of course, they come to watch the games.

[pause]

Between the gers, women cook big pots of mutton and dumplings. The smell drifts across the whole meadow. Old men sit together in the shade and argue happily about which wrestler will win. Little children run everywhere, with sticky hands and dusty boots.

[pause]

The wrestling is the game that people love most of all. The wrestlers wear small vests that cover their arms and backs, but leave the chest open. They wear tight short trousers and tall leather boots. Before each match, every wrestler does a special dance. He spreads his arms wide and moves them slowly, like a great eagle flying. Then the two wrestlers take hold of each other. They push and pull and twist, slowly and carefully. The first one to touch the ground with anything but his feet has lost.

[pause]

{img:mongol-wrestlers-eagle-dance-arms-spread-crowd-watching-grass}
There are no weight classes in Mongol wrestling. A small wrestler can face a very big one. There is no time limit either, so a match can last a long time. And the last wrestler left standing at the end becomes the champion. People talk about him for years afterwards.

[long-pause]

After the wrestling comes the archery. Men and women shoot at small targets on the ground, far away. When someone hits a target, the judges sing a little song. They raise their arms high and call out the score. And the whole crowd cheers loudly.

[pause]

{img:mongol-archers-men-women-shooting-targets-judges-singing-arms}
And then comes the horse race, which is the most exciting part of all. The riders are children, both boys and girls. Some of them are only six or seven years old. The horses are young and fast, and they have been trained all summer. The race is not run on a track, like races in other lands. It goes across the open steppe, for twenty or thirty kilometres. It goes over hills and through valleys, far out of sight.

[long-pause]

Khulan is riding in the race this year for the very first time. She is older than most of the other riders. But she is a wonderful rider, light and calm in the saddle. Her horse is a young grey called Cloud. She has trained him every evening all summer long. At the start, dozens of children wait on their horses in a long row. Together they sing a song to call up the spirits of the horses. Then they are off, galloping into the distance. Soon they disappear over a far hill, and the meadow is quiet.

[pause]

{img:children-jockeys-galloping-steppe-dust-horses-race-finish-crowd}
Everyone waits at the finish, looking toward the hills. The minutes pass slowly, and nobody talks very much. Then a small cloud of dust appears on the horizon. Riders are coming, and they are coming fast. People shout and point and stand up on the carts to see. Sarnai stands on her toes, shading her eyes with her hand. Is Khulan there among them? The first rider comes over the last hill. It is a small boy on a brown horse. Then two more riders come, close together. Then a grey horse appears, with a girl leaning low over its neck. It is Khulan, and she comes in fourth, out of more than forty riders. Sarnai shouts with joy. When Khulan rides up, flushed and dusty and grinning, her mother hugs her so hard she nearly falls off.

[long-pause]

{img:eagle-hunter-golden-eagle-arm-horseback-mountains-snow-hood}
In the west of the Mongol lands, high up in the mountains, some people hunt with eagles.

[pause]

These are not small birds, but great golden eagles. They are huge and powerful, with wings more than two metres wide. Their claws are as strong as iron, and their eyes are very sharp. They can see a fox moving on a hillside from kilometres away.

[long-pause]

An eagle hunter begins by finding a young eagle. It is usually a female, because the females are bigger and stronger. He takes her from the nest when she is just old enough to leave it. Or sometimes he catches a young bird with a soft net. Then he begins to train her, slowly and with great patience. It takes many months of quiet work. He keeps her close to him, day and night. He talks to her softly and feeds her from his own hand. She learns to sit on his arm and to fly to him when he calls. She learns to wear a little leather hood over her eyes. With the hood on, she stays calm and still.

[pause]

The hunter wears a thick leather glove on his arm, because the claws are sharp. The eagle is very heavy, as heavy as a small child. So when he rides, he rests his arm on a wooden stand fixed to the saddle. Riding with an eagle on your arm is not at all easy. The horse must be very calm and steady as well.

[pause]

In the evenings, the hunter sits by the stove with his eagle on a wooden perch nearby. He mends his glove and oils the leather hood. Sometimes he hums a song to the eagle, and she turns her head to listen. His children sit and watch the great bird with wide eyes.

[pause]

{img:eagle-taking-off-from-arm-wings-wide-valley-below-winter-light}
The hunting happens in winter, when snow lies on the ground. Then the animals are easy to see against the white. The hunter rides slowly up a hill with his eagle on his arm. From the top, he looks down into the valley below. When he spots a fox moving across the snow, he takes off the hood. The eagle sees it too, at once. She spreads her great wings and launches herself into the cold air. And she glides down over the valley.

[long-pause]

The soft fox fur is used for warm hats and coats. It is very valuable in the long cold winters. A good eagle can help her hunter catch many foxes in one season.

[pause]

The hunter and his eagle become very close, like two old partners. After about ten years together, the hunter takes his eagle up into the mountains one last time. He leaves her a good meal on a rock. And then he lets her go free. Now she can find a mate and raise chicks of her own. He watches her fly away until she is only a speck in the sky. And quietly, he says goodbye.

[pause]

{img:old-hunter-releasing-eagle-mountains-sunset-farewell-wings}
In the far west of Mongolia today, there are still eagle hunters. Most of them belong to the Kazakh people who live there. Fathers teach their sons how to train and care for the eagles. And nowadays, sometimes they teach their daughters too. Every autumn there is an eagle festival in the mountains. Hunters come with their eagles to show what they can do. People come from all over the world to watch them. And the great birds soar over the mountains, just as they did eight hundred years ago.

[long-pause]

{img:old-daoist-monk-changchun-white-beard-robe-mountain-temple-china}
In the year 1220, a messenger from Genghis Khan arrives at a mountain temple in northern China. He brings an invitation for an old monk who lives there.

[pause]

The monk's name is Changchun, which means eternal spring. He is a Daoist master. Daoists believe in living in harmony with nature, simply and calmly. They hope to live a long and healthy life through quiet living and a peaceful heart. Changchun is about seventy years old, with a long white beard. He is famous across China for his wisdom and his kindness.

[long-pause]

Genghis Khan has heard about this wise old monk. The khan is getting older now, and he thinks about such things. He has heard that Daoist masters know secrets about living a long life. Perhaps, he thinks, they even have a medicine to live forever. So he wants to meet this monk and ask him. He sends an official with a golden paiza and a polite letter. Please come to me, the letter says. I am far away in the west, but I will make your journey safe.

[pause]

Changchun thinks about it for a long time. He is old, and the journey is thousands of kilometres long. It goes over mountains and deserts and across the whole wide steppe. It leads to a place he has never seen. Many younger men would not want to go. But in the end, he decides that he will go. He chooses about eighteen of his students to travel with him. And in the spring, they set out together.

[pause]

{img:monk-and-students-setting-out-carts-horses-leaving-temple-spring}
One of his students writes everything down, every single day of the journey. He writes about what they see and who they meet. He writes about the weather and the food and the land. His book still exists today, eight hundred years later. It is one of the best descriptions we have of the Mongol lands at that time.

[long-pause]

First they travel north, out of China, through hills and small towns. Then they cross the wide steppe of Mongolia. The student writes about the endless grass and the round white gers. The people here are friendly, he writes. They bring us airag and meat, and they ask nothing in return. In one place, Mongol women come out to greet them warmly. In another, they cross a land of strange rocks and wide skies. They watch the sun rise over endless grass, and the old monk sits quietly to look at it.

[pause]

{img:monk-caravan-crossing-mongolian-steppe-gers-welcoming-families}
They pass through the great camp of the khan's family on their way. The ladies of the court welcome the old monk kindly. They give him felt and furs and food for the long journey ahead. They ask him to bless their camp, and he does so gladly. And then he travels on, west, toward the mountains and toward the khan.

[long-pause]

Perhaps they pass through the valley where Sarnai's family lives. Perhaps Khulan and Temur see a strange little group riding slowly by. At the front rides an old man with a long white beard and grey robes. He sits very straight on his horse, calm and still. Young men in robes ride behind him, and Mongol guards ride in front. The children stand by the gers and stare. Sarnai brings out a bowl of airag and offers it to the old man. He takes it with both hands, drinks a little, and thanks her. Then he smiles at the children and rides on, over the hill.

[long-pause]

{img:altai-mountains-snow-pass-caravan-climbing-cold-wind-monk}
Changchun's journey to the khan is very long and often very hard.

[pause]

After the steppe, the travellers reach a range of great mountains called the Altai. They are high and cold, and their tops are covered with snow. The road climbs up through steep and narrow passes. The carts cannot climb up by themselves on such steep ground. So the soldiers tie ropes to them and pull them up by hand. Then they let them down slowly on the other side. In some places, they must first build a rough road out of stones. Crossing the mountains takes them many days.

[long-pause]

After the mountains comes a desert, a great sea of sand and stones. There is no water there and no grass at all. It is burning hot by day and freezing cold by night. So the travellers rest in the daytime and move on at night, under the stars. The student writes that they heard strange sounds in the desert. They sounded like voices calling softly in the dark. The guides told them not to worry and to keep walking. Travellers long ago believed the desert was full of spirits. Today we know that the wind and the moving sand can make such sounds.

[pause]

They are very glad when they reach the green oasis towns on the other side. There are fruit trees and cool water and shady gardens. The people there greet them kindly and give them melons and sweet grapes. After the long dry desert, it feels like a paradise. The old monk sits in the shade of a tree and rests for many days.

[pause]

{img:oasis-town-gardens-melons-grapes-travellers-resting-shade}
The student writes happily about the oasis towns. The melons here are huge and sweet, he writes, and the grapes hang in heavy bunches. Water runs in little channels between the gardens, and children splash in it. In the evenings, people sit on carpets under the trees and drink tea together. The monk talks with the old men of the town about life and nature.

[pause]

The journey goes on for more than a whole year. They cross wide rivers and still more mountains. They pass through great old cities with markets and gardens. They meet people of many lands who speak many different languages. The old monk grows very tired, but he keeps on going.

[long-pause]

At last, in the spring of 1222, they reach the camp of the khan. He is staying in the high mountains of what is now Afghanistan. Genghis Khan welcomes the old monk with great respect and kindness. You have come from so very far, he says. You must be very tired. Then he asks the question he has wanted to ask for so long. Do you have a medicine for a long life? Is there a medicine to live forever?

[pause]

{img:genghis-khan-meeting-old-monk-tent-respect-mountains-1222}
Changchun answers him honestly and calmly. There are ways to protect and care for life, he says. But there is no medicine that lets anyone live forever. The khan is quiet for a long moment. Then he thanks the old monk for telling him the truth. Over the next months, they talk together many times. The monk teaches him about living simply and eating well. He talks about caring for ordinary people and being gentle where you can. He talks about calming the heart and the mind. And the great khan sits and listens.

[long-pause]

After that, the old monk travels all the way home to China again. The khan gives him gifts and promises to protect his temples. Changchun lives a few more years in peace, among his students and his gardens. And his student's book about the long journey has survived for eight hundred years. So we can still travel along with them today. We can follow them across the steppe and the mountains and the desert, to the tent of the great khan.

[long-pause]

{img:mongolian-winter-camp-snow-sheltered-valley-gers-smoke-cold-blue}
Winter comes to the steppe, and winter in Mongolia is long and very cold.

[pause]

By late autumn, the family has moved to their winter place. It is a sheltered valley tucked behind a hill, out of the north wind. The gers are covered with extra layers of thick felt. The animals have pens built of stone and wood, with roofs of branches. These keep off the worst of the wind and snow. Stacks of dried dung are piled high beside the gers, for fuel. Dried meat and hard cheese hang in the storage ger, enough for many weeks.

[long-pause]

Then the real cold arrives, quietly, one clear night. It can be forty degrees below zero. The air is so cold that it stings your nose when you breathe. Your breath freezes into white frost on your scarf. The river freezes solid from bank to bank. The snow squeaks and crunches under your felt boots. The sky is very clear and very blue during the day. And at night, the stars are so bright and close that you feel you could touch them.

[pause]

Inside the ger, it is warm and snug. The stove burns all day and all night, and someone always keeps it fed. The family sits close together on the soft felt rugs. The children play their games with the little ankle bones. The grandmother tells long stories, one after another. Sarnai sews new deels lined with sheepskin. Khulan makes felt boots, and Temur mends the leather harness. In a corner, a few newborn lambs sleep wrapped in felt, born too early in the cold.

[pause]

{img:inside-ger-winter-stove-glowing-family-close-lambs-wrapped-corner}
In winter, the family eats more meat, to stay strong and warm. Sarnai makes a big pot of soup with mutton and noodles. She also makes little dumplings, filled with meat and onion, and steams them on the stove. The children help to fold the dumplings, with flour on their noses. Everyone eats together in the warm glow, and the windows of the crown are dark and frosty.

[pause]

The greatest worry of the winter is called the dzud. A dzud is a very hard winter for the animals. Sometimes too much snow falls, and it covers the grass so deeply that the animals cannot dig through. Or the snow melts a little and then freezes again into hard ice. Then the animals cannot reach the grass beneath it. In a bad dzud, a family can lose many of its animals.

[long-pause]

This winter, the snow comes early, and then a little rain falls on it. The next night it freezes hard. A thin crust of ice now covers the steppe. Sarnai looks at it in the morning, and her face becomes very serious. She knows what it could mean for the herds. The whole family works from dawn until dark. They break the ice with sticks and axes so the animals can reach the grass. They feed them the hay they saved in the autumn. They bring the weakest lambs into the ger to keep them warm. Everyone is very tired. And they all wonder if the herds will be all right.

[pause]

{img:family-breaking-ice-snow-sticks-sheep-grass-underneath-cold-dawn}
Then, after a week, the wind changes and begins to blow from the south. Warmer air comes over the hills. The ice softens and cracks, and the animals can graze again. A few of the animals have been lost in the cold. But most of the herd has come through safely. Sarnai sits down on the floor of the ger and cries, just for a moment. The grandmother puts a warm hand on her shoulder. We are still here, she says softly, and so are they.

[long-pause]

And one evening in the deep of winter, a rider comes slowly down the valley. There are bells on his belt, ringing in the cold air. It is a messenger from the Yam. He stops outside the ger and calls out a greeting. He brings words from the far west. The khan is coming home, he says, and the armies will return next year. Batu is well, and he sends his love to all of you. For a moment, Sarnai cannot speak at all. The children shout and dance around the stove. Even the grandmother laughs out loud. And that night, nobody minds the cold at all.

[long-pause]

{img:riders-returning-home-steppe-spring-families-running-to-meet}
The armies did come home in the end. In 1225, Genghis Khan returned to Mongolia. And with him came many of the riders, Batu among them.

[pause]

Imagine that day in early spring, when the snow is melting on the hills. Sarnai is outside the ger, hanging felt to dry in the sun. She sees a group of riders on the horizon, small and dark against the sky. She knows the shape of one of them long before she can see his face. She drops the felt and stands very still. The children see her face and start to run. Batu jumps down from his horse and holds out his arms. Khulan and Temur and Altan reach him first, all at once.

[pause]

Little Bayar does not recognise his father at first. He was so small when his father left. He hides shyly behind his mother's deel and peeks out. Batu kneels down in the wet grass and holds out his hand. He waits patiently, smiling. And slowly, step by step, Bayar comes to him.

[pause]

That evening, the whole valley comes to the family's ger. There is boiled mutton and fresh airag and music. Temur shows his father how well he can shoot with his little bow. Batu watches carefully, and then he nods slowly. Good, he says, that is very good, my son. And Temur's face shines brighter than the fire.

[long-pause]

Genghis Khan lived for only a few more years after that. In 1227, far away in the south, he passed away. He was about sixty-five years old. His sons and grandsons carried on what he had started. Under them, the Mongol Empire grew bigger still. In the end, it stretched from Korea in the east to Hungary in the west. It reached from Siberia in the north all the way to Persia in the south. It was the largest land empire in all of history.

[pause]

Those wars brought great sorrow to many lands, and we remember that gently. But the empire also brought something new into the world. For about a hundred years, people and goods could travel safely across the whole of Asia. They could go from China all the way to Europe and back. This time is sometimes called the Mongol Peace.

[pause]

{img:marco-polo-caravan-arriving-mongol-court-china-later-gifts}
Travellers came from very far away during that time. The most famous of them was a young man from Venice called Marco Polo. He travelled all the way to China with his father and his uncle. There he visited the court of Kublai Khan, the grandson of Genghis Khan. He stayed in China for many years. When he finally went home, he told the story of his travels, and it was written down in a book. People in Europe could hardly believe what they read. Paper money, black stones that burn, and cities with a million people!

[long-pause]

Many inventions and ideas travelled along those safe roads too. There was paper and printing, and the compass for finding your way. There was medicine and maps, and knowledge about the stars. Foods and spices and stories passed from one land to the next. East and west met in a way that they never had before.

[pause]

{img:mongol-yuan-dynasty-later-palace-china-grand-hall-kublai-court}
Later, the great empire broke up into several parts. Each part was ruled by a different branch of the khan's family. Over time, those parts slowly faded away. But in Mongolia itself, the people went on living much as they always had. They lived in their gers, with their horses and their herds. They moved with the seasons, from pasture to pasture. And they lived, as always, under the Eternal Blue Sky.

[long-pause]

{img:funeral-procession-steppe-night-riders-quiet-secret-mountains}
Where is Genghis Khan buried?

[pause]

The answer is that nobody knows. It is one of the great quiet mysteries of history.

[long-pause]

When he passed away in 1227, his body was carried slowly back to Mongolia. The old stories say it was a long and secret journey. The riders travelled quietly and made sure that nobody saw them pass. They wanted the place where he rested to stay hidden forever. That way, nobody could ever disturb his long sleep.

[pause]

The stories say he was buried somewhere near the sacred mountain, Burkhan Khaldun. That was where he had hidden as a young man, and where he had prayed. Some stories say a river was turned aside to flow over the place. Others say that a great herd of horses was ridden over the ground to hide every trace. Then grass and trees grew over it, year after year. And after a while, nobody could tell where the place had been.

[pause]

{img:forest-hills-near-sacred-mountain-quiet-trees-hidden-place-mist}
For many hundreds of years afterwards, the whole area around the mountain was closed. It was called the Great Taboo, which means a forbidden place. Guards kept everyone away from the forests and the hills. Only the khan's family and their special guards were allowed to enter. Even long after the empire was gone, people stayed away from that place. They did it out of respect for the great khan.

[pause]

The herders in the nearby valleys told quiet stories about the forbidden forest. Some said you could hear horses there at night, though no horses could be seen. Others said the trees there were older and taller than anywhere else. The grandmothers told the children to leave the forest in peace. And the children always did.

[long-pause]

In our own time, explorers have gone searching for the grave. They have used satellites and aeroplanes and special cameras. They have walked for weeks through the hills and forests. Some of them have found old graves and stone circles from long ago. But nobody has found the grave of Genghis Khan.

[pause]

And many Mongolians do not want it to be found at all. Let him rest, they say. He wanted his grave to stay secret, and that wish should be respected. And perhaps they are right about that.

[pause]

{img:mausoleum-shrine-genghis-khan-white-tents-later-pilgrims-incense}
Far away, in the north of China, there is a shrine for Genghis Khan. It is a large building with three domes, shaped like round gers. People come there from far and near to remember him. Families come, and old people, and small children holding their parents' hands. They light candles and burn sweet incense. They offer bowls of milk and bow their heads. It is not his grave, they know that. But it is a quiet place to remember him.

[long-pause]

On the steppe, the wind blows softly over the long grass. The forests on the sacred mountain are quiet and dark. Somewhere beneath the trees, perhaps, the great khan sleeps. Nobody knows exactly where. And perhaps that is just as it should be.

[long-pause]

{img:modern-mongolia-ger-solar-panel-motorbike-horses-steppe-today}
Today, Mongolia is a country of about three and a half million people. It lies between Russia in the north and China in the south. Its capital is Ulaanbaatar, a busy city with tall buildings, traffic and markets.

[pause]

But outside the city, the steppe is still there, as it always was. It is wide and green and empty, under the same huge blue sky.

[long-pause]

About one in four Mongolians still live as herders, just like their ancestors. They live in gers and keep sheep and goats, horses and camels and yaks. They still move with the seasons, from pasture to pasture. They make airag in the summer and felt in the autumn. They still ride horses every day. Some families also have motorbikes now, and solar panels on the roof of the ger. A family might watch television in the evening, inside their round felt home. But the life itself is still the life of the steppe.

[pause]

Children still learn to ride before they can properly walk. They still play their games with the little ankle bones. They still herd the sheep when they are seven years old. And they still race their horses across the steppe at the summer games.

[pause]

{img:naadam-today-ulaanbaatar-stadium-wrestlers-crowd-flags-summer}
Naadam is now the national festival of Mongolia, held every July. There are games in towns and villages all over the country. The biggest games are held in the capital, in a great stadium. Wrestlers in their small vests still do the slow eagle dance. Archers shoot at small targets, and the judges still sing out the scores. And children still race horses across the open steppe. For a few days, the whole country stops to watch.

[long-pause]

Genghis Khan is a national hero in Mongolia today. His face is printed on the money, and the airport is named after him. Not far from the capital, there is a giant silver statue of him riding a horse. It is forty metres tall. You can climb up inside it and stand on the horse's head. From up there, you can see the steppe stretching away for kilometres in every direction.

[pause]

So we leave the steppe tonight, in the year 1220, as the evening falls.

[long-pause]

The sun is setting slowly over the grassland. The sky turns gold, then pink, and then a deep, soft blue. The sheep and goats are safe in their pens for the night. The foals are tied once more in their long line. The horses graze on the hillside, their shapes dark against the sky. A thin line of smoke rises from the crown of the ger.

[pause]

Inside, the fire glows warm and orange. Sarnai sits by the stove, mending a small felt boot. Khulan stirs the airag bag slowly, a few last times. Temur rubs a little fat into his bow, to keep the wood supple. Altan and Bayar are fast asleep under a soft sheepskin. The grandmother hums an old song, so quietly that you can hardly hear it. And the big black dog lies across the doorway, with his eyes half closed.

[pause]

Outside, the stars come out one by one, and then more and more. Soon the whole sky is white with them. They shine over the sleeping camp and over the endless grass. They shine over the whole quiet steppe, under the Eternal Blue Sky.

[pause]
