{img:mongolian-steppe-1220-dawn-white-gers-horses-grassland-mist}
Tonight we go about eight hundred years back. We go to the wide grasslands of Mongolia, in the heart of Asia. The year is 1220. And the Mongols rule the biggest land empire the world has ever seen.

[long-pause]

It is early morning on the steppe. The steppe is a huge sea of grass. It rolls on and on, to the edge of the sky. There are no trees here. No fences. No roads. Only grass and hills and sky. A light wind moves over the grass like waves on water. And on a gentle slope by a stream, there are a few round white tents. Smoke rises from the top of each one.

[pause]

A woman steps out of one tent with a wooden bucket. She walks to the horses tied in a line. She begins to milk a mare. A boy runs past with a dog at his heels. Sheep bleat on the hillside. Far away, an eagle circles in the pale blue sky. It is quiet. Very quiet. And very big.

[pause]

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

{img:mongol-family-outside-ger-morning-horses-sheep-blue-sky-wide}
The Mongols are a people of the steppe. They live with their animals. They move with the seasons, from pasture to pasture. They carry their homes with them. And less than twenty years before our story, they were just a handful of quarrelling tribes. Then one man joined them all together. His name was Temüjin. The world would know him as Genghis Khan.

[pause]

Most stories about Genghis Khan are about war. About armies and battles and conquered cities. That is true, and it was terrible for many people. But tonight we will not follow the armies. We will stay at home, on the steppe. With the families who stayed behind. The women and children and old people. The herders and the horses.

[pause]

{img:old-mongol-grandmother-sitting-ger-door-spinning-wool-sun}
We will learn how a round felt tent is made. We will taste milk that has been turned into a drink. We will ride with children who learn to ride before they can walk. We will meet the riders who carry messages across half a continent. We will watch the great summer games. And we will follow an old monk from China on his long journey to meet the great khan.

[long-pause]

{img:eagle-circling-over-steppe-grassland-hills-endless-sky-morning}
There will be worries. The men are away. The winter is coming. And everyone wonders when the khan and his riders will come home. But for now, the sun is rising over the grass. The horses are snorting in the cool air. And a new day begins on the steppe.

[long-pause]

{img:mongol-clans-gathering-steppe-tents-banners-horses-many-people}
Who are the Mongols?

[pause]

For thousands of years, people have lived on the steppe as herders. They keep sheep and goats and cattle and horses and camels. They live in felt tents that can be taken down and put up again. They move several times a year to find fresh grass for their animals. The steppe is good for animals. But it is too dry and too cold for farming. So people follow the grass.

[long-pause]

In the time before Genghis Khan, the steppe was divided into many tribes and clans. The Mongols were one of them. There were also the Tatars and the Kereyits, the Merkits and the Naimans, and many more. They spoke similar languages. They lived in similar ways. But they did not get along. They raided each other's herds. They stole each other's horses. They took revenge for old wrongs. Nobody was ever quite safe.

[pause]

Then Genghis Khan joined them all. Some by friendship and promises. Some by force. In 1206, all the peoples of the felt tents gathered on the steppe. And they named him their great ruler. From then on, they were all called Mongols.

[pause]

{img:mongol-herder-man-deel-robe-hat-holding-horse-hillside}
The Mongols are tough people. The steppe has no mercy. Winters are very cold, colder than almost anywhere people live. Summers can be hot and dry. Storms come suddenly. Wolves hunt the herds. To live here, you must be strong and clever and ready for anything.

[long-pause]

They wear long thick robes called deels. The deel is like a coat that wraps across the front and ties at the side. It has a wide sash around the waist. In winter, it is lined with fur. At night it can be used as a blanket. They wear felt boots with pointed toes and fur hats with flaps for the ears. Men and women dress in almost the same way.

[pause]

{img:mongol-woman-deel-sash-headdress-silver-coral-beads-proud}
Their language is Mongolian. In 1220 it has only just begun to be written down. Genghis Khan ordered it. A scholar from a conquered people taught the Mongols how to write their language with his letters. The letters run from top to bottom in columns. They look like a row of little hooks and loops hanging from a line. Mongolians still use this writing today, alongside newer letters.

[long-pause]

{img:mongolia-landscape-steppe-mountains-forest-gobi-desert-varied}
Mongolia is a land of huge open spaces. And it is not all flat grassland.

[pause]

In the north, there are mountains covered with dark forests of pine and larch. Rivers flow through deep valleys. Bears and deer and wolves live in the forests. In the middle, there are the rolling green steppes, the heart of Mongol life. And in the south, there is the Gobi. A great desert of stones and gravel and some sand dunes. Camels live there. And it is very dry.

[long-pause]

Mongolia is very high. Much of it is more than a thousand metres above the sea. And it is very far from any sea. So the weather is wild. In summer, the days can be warm and sunny. But the nights are cool, even then. In winter, it can be forty degrees below zero. Rivers freeze solid. Snow covers everything. The wind cuts like a knife.

[pause]

But there is one thing Mongolia has in plenty. Sky. People call it the land of the blue sky. The sky is huge and clear and deep blue, almost every day of the year. At night, the stars are so many and so bright that the sky seems white with them.

[pause]

{img:night-sky-mongolia-milky-way-gers-glowing-steppe-silent}
For the Mongols, the sky is sacred. They call it the Eternal Blue Sky. Tengri. It is their greatest god. It looks down on everything. It gives power to rulers. It sends the rain and the wind. When a Mongol makes a promise, he says, may the Eternal Blue Sky be my witness.

[long-pause]

The land is sacred too. Mountains and rivers and springs have spirits. People must not harm them. You must not wash in a river or throw dirt into it. That would anger the water spirits. You must not dig up the earth without a good reason. You must treat the land with care. Because without the grass and the water, nothing can live.

[pause]

{img:river-valley-mongolia-horses-drinking-larch-trees-mountains-evening}
In 1220, the land is mostly peaceful at home. The armies are far away in the west. The family we will meet lives in a green valley in the middle of the steppe. A small river runs through it. Hills rise on both sides. It is their summer place. Soon they will move. But for now, the grass is good. And the horses are fat.

[long-pause]

The grandmother of our family can read the land like a book. She looks at the clouds and says, rain by evening. She smells the wind and says, snow is coming in three days. She looks at the grass and knows how long the animals can stay. She is almost always right. The children think she can talk to the sky. Maybe she can.

[long-pause]

{img:mongol-ger-exterior-white-felt-wooden-door-smoke-hole-morning}
The Mongols live in round tents called gers. Outsiders often call them yurts.

[pause]

A ger looks simple from outside. A round white tent with a gently sloping roof and a small wooden door. But it is very clever. It is warm in winter and cool in summer. It stands strong in the wind. And it can be taken down in an hour, packed onto a few camels or a cart, and put up again somewhere else.

[long-pause]

How is it made? The walls are a wooden lattice. Thin wooden poles crossed like a fence, tied together with leather. The lattice can be folded up flat, or opened out into a circle. Several sections are joined to make the round wall. A wooden door frame is set into it. The door always faces south, away from the cold north wind.

[pause]

Then come the roof poles. Long thin poles that go from the top of the wall up to a wooden ring in the middle. The ring is the crown of the ger. It is held up by two posts. It is open to the sky. Smoke goes out through it. Light comes in. And at night, you can lie on your back and see the stars through it.

[pause]

{img:ger-wooden-frame-lattice-wall-roof-poles-crown-ring-being-built}
Then the whole frame is covered with thick sheets of felt. One layer in summer. Two or three layers in winter. Over the felt goes a white cloth to keep out the rain. Ropes of horse hair tie it all down. And the ger is ready.

[long-pause]

Inside, everything has its place. In the middle is the stove, or a fire on a hearth. The smoke rises up and out of the crown. The north side, opposite the door, is the place of honour. That is where the oldest and most respected people sit. And where a small altar stands. The west side is for the men. Saddles and bows and tools hang there. The east side is for the women. Pots and buckets and food are kept there.

[pause]

{img:ger-interior-hearth-centre-felt-rugs-chests-family-sitting-lamplight}
The floor is covered with felt rugs. Painted wooden chests stand around the walls. Beds are low wooden frames with felt and furs. It is cosy and warm. The whole family lives in one round room. They eat and sleep and work and laugh together. In winter, the newborn lambs sometimes come inside too, to keep warm.

[long-pause]

There are rules in a ger. You must not step on the threshold when you come in. That would bring bad luck. You must walk around the inside in the direction the sun moves. Left to right. You must not lean on the posts that hold up the crown. They hold up the sky of the home. Children learn these rules before they can talk.

[long-pause]

{img:felt-making-steppe-women-wool-spread-hide-rolling-horse-pulling}
The walls of the ger are made of felt. And making felt is one of the great events of the summer.

[pause]

Felt is a thick soft cloth. But it is not woven. It is made by pressing wool together until the fibres tangle up and hold tight. It is warm and strong and keeps out the wind and the rain. The Mongols use it for everything. Tents and rugs and boots and hats and saddle pads.

[long-pause]

Felt making happens in late summer, after the sheep have been sheared. Many families come together for it. The women beat the wool with sticks to make it fluffy and loose. Then they lay out an old piece of felt on the grass, as a base. They spread a thick even layer of new wool on top of it. They sprinkle it with water.

[pause]

Then they roll it all up tightly, around a long pole, and wrap it in a wet animal hide. They tie it with ropes. And then the fun part. A rider ties the ends of the rope to his horse. And the horse pulls the roll across the grass. Back and forth. Back and forth. For hours. The rolling and the pressing and the water make the wool tangle together.

[pause]

{img:rider-dragging-felt-roll-across-grass-children-running-alongside}
The children love this. They run beside the roll and shout. The rider calls out. The women sing songs about felt and wool and good luck. Every now and then, they stop. They unroll it and look. Is it ready? Not yet. Roll it again.

[long-pause]

At last, the felt is thick and firm. They unroll it and lay it flat. Then the women kneel along it. They press and rub it with their arms and elbows. For a long time. Until it is smooth and strong. Then it is hung up to dry.

[pause]

{img:women-kneeling-pressing-new-felt-elbows-sunlight-sheep-behind}
When it is done, there is a feast. The family that gets the new felt gives food and drink to everyone who helped. They say blessings over the new felt. May it be as white as milk. May it be as strong as stone. May it keep the family warm for many winters. And everyone eats and drinks and sings until the sun goes down.

[long-pause]

{img:mongol-horses-small-sturdy-herd-running-steppe-manes-dust}
For the Mongols, the horse is everything.

[pause]

Mongol horses are small. Much smaller than the big horses of Europe. They have short legs, thick necks and big heads. In winter their coats grow long and shaggy. They do not look grand. But they are amazingly tough. They can run for hours without tiring. They can live on almost nothing. In winter, when snow covers the grass, they dig through it with their hooves to find something to eat. No other horse could live like that.

[long-pause]

Every family has many horses. A family might have dozens. A rich family might have hundreds. They live outside all year in big herds. They are not kept in stables. They are half wild, and free.

[pause]

The Mongols ride everywhere. They ride to visit neighbours. They ride to check the herds. They ride to hunt. They ride to war. A Mongol without a horse is like a bird without wings. People say a Mongol would rather walk on his hands than walk on his feet. And it is not much of a joke.

[pause]

{img:mongol-rider-galloping-standing-stirrups-deel-flying-hills}
They sit on wooden saddles with high fronts and backs. Their stirrups are short. They can stand up in the stirrups at a full gallop. And they can turn and shoot an arrow backwards while riding fast. Their bows are small and very strong. Made of wood and horn and sinew glued together.

[long-pause]

When a Mongol travels far, he takes several horses. He rides one. The others follow, without a rider. When the first one gets tired, he jumps onto a fresh one. Without even stopping. That way he can travel a hundred kilometres or more in a day. Faster than any traveller in the world at that time.

[pause]

{img:mongol-boy-catching-horse-urga-pole-lasso-herd-running}
To catch a horse from the herd, the herders use a special tool. A long thin pole with a loop of rope at the end. It is called an urga. The rider gallops into the running herd. He reaches out with the pole and drops the loop over the chosen horse's head. Then he holds on tight. It takes great skill and strength. Boys practise it from a young age.

[long-pause]

The Mongols love their horses. They name them. They sing songs about them. A famous racehorse is remembered for generations. When a beloved horse dies, its skull is sometimes placed on a sacred pile of stones on a hill. With a blue silk scarf tied around it. So its spirit can rest high up, close to the Eternal Blue Sky.

[pause]

{img:herd-of-horses-sunset-steppe-silhouettes-manes-golden-grass}
In the evening, the horses graze on the hillside near the gers. Their shapes are dark against the orange sky. Now and then one of them lifts its head and neighs. Another answers. And the sound carries far across the quiet steppe.

[long-pause]

{img:mongol-woman-milking-mare-foal-tied-line-summer-morning}
In summer, the Mongols drink something special. It is called airag. It is made from the milk of horses.

[pause]

Mares, the female horses, give milk after their foals are born. In summer, the families milk their mares several times a day. It is not easy. A mare will only let her milk flow if her foal is close by. So the foals are tied in a line near the gers. The mother stays near. The woman brings the foal to its mother for a moment. Then she quickly milks the mare into a wooden bucket. Then the foal can drink the rest.

[long-pause]

The milk goes into a big leather bag that hangs by the door of the ger. Inside the bag, there is a little old airag from before. That helps the new milk to change. And then comes the important part. Stirring. Someone must stir the milk with a wooden paddle. Again and again. Thousands of times a day. Everyone who walks past the bag gives it a few stirs. Guests stir it. Children stir it. It never stops.

[pause]

After a day or two, the milk becomes airag. It is a little sour and a little fizzy. It is not very strong. It tastes like a mix of yogurt and lemonade. Mongols drink it in big bowls all summer long. Some drink many litres a day. It is full of goodness. People say it keeps you healthy all year.

[pause]

{img:leather-bag-airag-hanging-ger-boy-stirring-wooden-paddle}
Airag is offered to every guest. When you visit a ger in summer, the first thing you get is a bowl of airag. It would be very rude to refuse. You take the bowl with your right hand. You drink a little. Then you hand it back.

[long-pause]

The Mongols also make many things from the milk of cows and sheep and goats and camels. Yogurt and butter. Cream that forms a thick skin on top of boiled milk. Hard dried cheese, cut into pieces and dried on the roof of the ger. It can be kept for months. Children chew it like sweets. It is very hard. You can chew one piece for an hour.

[pause]

{img:dried-curds-cheese-pieces-drying-on-ger-roof-sun-flies}
The Mongols call these the white foods. Milk and everything made from it. They are pure and good. In summer, people live mostly on white foods. Meat is for winter. And the first airag of the summer is a reason for a little celebration. The family pours a few drops toward the sky and the earth and the four directions. A thank you to the spirits. And then everyone drinks.

[long-pause]

{img:young-temujin-boy-steppe-riverbank-mother-hoelun-brothers-hard-times}
Genghis Khan was not always a great ruler. He began life as a boy called Temüjin. And his childhood was very hard.

[pause]

He was born around the year 1162, by the river Onon, in the north of Mongolia. His father was a chief of a small clan. The story says baby Temüjin was born holding a clot of blood in his little fist. People said it was a sign. He would be a great warrior.

[long-pause]

When Temüjin was about nine, his father took him to another clan to find him a future wife. That was the custom. There he met a girl called Börte. She was a year older than him. The two families agreed that they would marry when they were older. Temüjin stayed with her family for a while. And his father rode home.

[pause]

On the way, his father stopped at a camp of Tatars, old enemies. They gave him food. But the food was poisoned. He reached home and passed away soon after. Temüjin was called back. He was still a boy. And now he had no father.

[pause]

{img:hoelun-gathering-roots-berries-river-children-poverty-steppe}
Then the worst thing happened. The people of the clan left. They did not want to follow a widow and her small children. They took the herds and moved away. Temüjin, his mother Hoelun and his brothers and sisters were left alone on the steppe. With almost nothing.

[long-pause]

They nearly starved. They lived on roots and berries and wild onions. The boys fished in the river and caught small animals. Their mother Hoelun kept them alive. She was strong and brave and never gave up. She dug roots with a stick. She sewed their clothes. She taught them to stick together. Like arrows, she told them. One arrow breaks easily. But a bundle of arrows cannot be broken.

[pause]

{img:mother-teaching-sons-bundle-of-arrows-cannot-break-fire-night}
Life stayed dangerous. Once, Temüjin was captured by enemies. They put a heavy wooden board around his neck, like a collar, so he could not escape. But one night, during a feast, he slipped away. He hid in a river with only his face above the water. A kind man saw him. And instead of calling the others, he helped him. He hid him in a cart full of wool. The searchers came. They poked at the wool. And then they went away. Temüjin was free.

[long-pause]

He never forgot those years. The hunger. The fear. The people who left him. And the few who helped. When he became great, he rewarded those who had been kind to him. And he made sure that nobody would ever be left alone on the steppe like that again. At least, nobody who was loyal to him.

[long-pause]

{img:borte-young-woman-ger-door-headdress-strong-calm-face-steppe}
When Temüjin was about sixteen, he rode back to Börte's family. He had not forgotten the girl he was promised to. And she had not forgotten him.

[pause]

They married. Her family gave a wonderful gift. A coat of black sable fur. It was very precious. Temüjin had very little. But with that coat, he went to visit a powerful chief who had been his father's friend. He gave him the coat as a gift. And the chief agreed to help him. It was the beginning of Temüjin's rise.

[long-pause]

But soon after the wedding, danger came. Riders from another tribe attacked the camp at dawn. Everyone fled. Temüjin escaped into the mountains. But Börte was caught. She was carried away. Temüjin was heartbroken. He prayed on the sacred mountain. And then he gathered his friends and allies. Months later, they rode out to bring her back.

[pause]

The story says it was night when they reached the enemy camp. People fled in the dark. Temüjin rode through the chaos, calling her name. Börte! Börte! And in the moonlight, Börte heard his voice. She ran toward him. She grabbed the reins of his horse. And he knew her. They held each other. The old book says, they were together again, like before.

[pause]

{img:night-reunion-moonlight-rider-woman-holding-reins-steppe-camp}
Börte became Temüjin's first wife and his most trusted adviser. She was wise and calm and clever. When he was unsure, he listened to her. Once, she warned him that a close friend could no longer be trusted. He listened. She was right.

[long-pause]

They had four sons and several daughters. Their sons became great rulers after their father. Their daughters married the chiefs of other peoples. And they were given great power. Some ruled whole lands while their husbands were at war. Genghis Khan trusted his daughters very much.

[pause]

{img:borte-managing-camp-giving-orders-women-officials-gers-busy}
In 1220, Börte is about sixty years old. Her husband is far away in the west, with the armies. He has been gone for over a year. She stays in Mongolia, at the great camp. She runs things at home. She looks after the family and the herds and the camps. She makes decisions. She welcomes visitors. Messengers come and go. And every day she waits for news from the west.

[long-pause]

{img:kurultai-1206-great-gathering-steppe-banners-white-standard-crowd}
Temüjin grew from a hungry boy into a great leader. Slowly, over many years, he brought the tribes of the steppe together.

[pause]

He did it in a new way. In the old days, people were loyal to their family and their clan. Rich families ruled. Poor families followed. But Temüjin did things differently. He chose people for what they could do, not for who their father was. A brave poor herder could become a general. A loyal friend could become a great lord. People liked that. Many joined him.

[long-pause]

He was also known for keeping his promises. And for rewarding loyalty. If you were loyal to Temüjin, he would never forget it. If you betrayed him, he would never forgive it.

[pause]

In the year 1206, all the peoples of the felt tents gathered by the river Onon. Where Temüjin was born. It was a great meeting called a kurultai. Chiefs came from everywhere. Thousands of gers were set up on the grass. Thousands of horses grazed nearby. And there, the chiefs named Temüjin their great ruler. He took a new title. Genghis Khan.

[pause]

{img:white-standard-nine-horse-tails-pole-wind-steppe-sacred-banner}
What does Genghis mean? Nobody is quite sure. Maybe ocean. Or strong. Or universal. Khan means ruler. So perhaps, the ruler of everything between the seas.

[long-pause]

At the kurultai, a great white banner was raised. It was a tall pole with nine white horse tails tied at the top. They flew in the wind. It was the banner of peace. There was also a black banner, for war. The Mongols believed the spirit of the ruler lived in his banner.

[pause]

From then on, the Mongols were one people. One nation of the felt tents. Old feuds were forgotten, or forbidden. The raiding stopped. A man could ride across the steppe in safety. That had never been true before.

[pause]

{img:genghis-khan-older-grey-beard-seated-tent-calm-eyes-advisers}
Then the armies rode out. To the south, to China. And later, to the west. That story is long and hard. We will leave it there, far away. Tonight we stay at home on the steppe. In 1220, Genghis Khan is about fifty-eight years old. His beard is grey. He is far away in the west. And at home, life goes on.

[long-pause]

Genghis Khan himself never learned to read or write. But he understood how important it was. He ordered that the laws and decisions be written down. He had scribes in his camp. He sent written orders across the empire. And he made sure his sons learned the new letters. The boy who once dug roots by the river now ruled from a tent full of writers.

[long-pause]

{img:mongol-judges-court-ger-hearing-case-scribe-writing-scroll}
Genghis Khan made laws for his people. They were called the Yassa.

[pause]

Nobody has found a complete copy of the Yassa. It may never have been written down all in one place. But people at the time wrote about many of its rules. And they tell us how Genghis Khan wanted his people to live.

[long-pause]

Some rules were about keeping the peace. Do not steal another person's animals. Do not kidnap women. Do not lie. Do not fight among yourselves. In the old days, these things happened all the time on the steppe. Now they were forbidden. And the punishments were very strict. People took the rules seriously.

[pause]

Some rules were about respecting the land and the water. Do not wash in running water. Do not pollute rivers. Rivers were sacred. And clean water meant life for people and animals.

[pause]

{img:mongol-traveller-alone-safe-steppe-golden-plate-paiza-peaceful}
Some rules were about religion. Genghis Khan allowed everyone to follow their own faith. Christians and Buddhists and Muslims and Daoists and followers of the old sky religion. Priests of all faiths did not have to pay taxes. That was unusual in those days. Many rulers forced people to follow one religion. Genghis Khan did not.

[long-pause]

There were rules about helping travellers. If a traveller came to your ger, you had to give him food and shelter. Even a stranger. The steppe is a hard place. Turning someone away could mean they would not survive.

[pause]

A story was told about the Mongol lands under Genghis Khan. People said a young woman carrying a golden plate could walk from one end of the empire to the other. Alone. And nobody would harm her or take the gold. It was probably not quite true. But it shows what people believed. Under the great khan, the roads were safe.

[pause]

{img:quiet-steppe-camp-evening-neighbours-sharing-food-peace}
For the family in our story, the laws mean one big thing. Peace at home. They do not have to fear that their neighbours will steal their horses in the night. They can let their animals graze far away. They can sleep without a guard. After generations of raiding and fear, that is a great gift.

[long-pause]

{img:mongol-family-group-outside-ger-grandmother-mother-children-dogs}
Let us meet a family on the steppe in 1220.

[pause]

They live in the green valley by the river. There are three gers close together. One for the grandparents. One for the family. One for storage and guests. Around them graze their animals. About two hundred sheep and goats. Twenty cows. Thirty horses. And four camels for moving.

[long-pause]

The father is called Batu. He is not at home. He rode west with the army more than a year ago. He is one of the khan's riders. He sends news when he can, through the messengers. The last news came three months ago. He was well. He was far away, in a land of great rivers and cities. Nobody at home can really imagine it.

[pause]

The mother is called Sarnai. That means rose. She runs everything while Batu is away. The herds. The family. The camp. Mongol women have always had a lot of responsibility. When the men are away, the women are in charge. And they are very good at it.

[pause]

{img:sarnai-mother-checking-herd-hillside-horse-whip-wind-hair}
There are four children. The eldest is a girl of fourteen, called Khulan. She is a wonderful rider. Next is a boy of eleven, called Temur. He wants to be a warrior like his father. Then a girl of seven, called Altan, which means gold. And a little boy of three, called Bayar. That means joy. He is always laughing.

[long-pause]

Batu's mother lives with them too. She is old now, about seventy. Her face is brown and lined like an old map. She cannot ride as she used to. But she knows everything about animals and weather and herbs. She knows all the old stories. And she watches over the little ones.

[pause]

{img:grandmother-teaching-girl-herbs-steppe-flowers-basket-summer}
There is also a dog. A big shaggy black dog with a brown spot over each eye. People say the spots are like a second pair of eyes. So the dog can see danger even when it is asleep. It guards the camp at night. And it barks at every wolf and every stranger.

[long-pause]

Sarnai worries about her husband. She does not say it. But every time a rider appears on the horizon, she stops what she is doing. She shades her eyes and looks. Is it a messenger? Is there news? Usually it is just a neighbour. And she goes back to work. But she keeps looking.

[long-pause]

{img:dawn-inside-ger-mother-lighting-fire-dung-tea-kettle-children-sleeping}
Let us spend a summer day with this family.

[pause]

Sarnai wakes before dawn. It is still cool. She lights the fire in the middle of the ger. The fuel is dried animal dung. There is no wood on the open steppe. Dried dung burns well and gives a steady heat. She puts a pot of water on the fire. She adds tea leaves and milk and a little salt. Mongol tea is salty and milky. It warms you up.

[long-pause]

The first thing she does when the tea is ready is something special. She takes a little of the first tea outside. She flicks some drops with a wooden spoon toward the sky. Toward the earth. And toward the four directions. A gift to the spirits for a good day. Only then does the family drink.

[pause]

Then the work begins. Khulan goes out to milk the mares. Temur rides off to bring the horses back from the hill where they grazed at night. The grandmother sits by the door, spinning wool and keeping an eye on little Bayar. Altan helps her mother make yogurt.

[pause]

{img:boy-temur-riding-bareback-bringing-horses-down-hill-dawn-mist}
In the morning, the sheep and goats are taken out to graze. Altan goes with them, with the dog. Even at seven, she can herd a flock of two hundred. She sits on a little horse. She keeps the flock together and away from the river. She sings to them. Old herding songs that tell the sheep where to go.

[long-pause]

At midday, the family eats in the ger. Bowls of yogurt. Dried cheese. Bread made from a little flour, fried in mutton fat. And airag, of course. Lots of airag. Then some people rest in the shade of the ger. The sun is hot now. The flies buzz.

[pause]

{img:little-girl-on-small-horse-herding-sheep-dog-steppe-midday}
In the afternoon, Sarnai rides out to check on the cows in the next valley. She takes Bayar on the saddle in front of her. He laughs the whole way. The grandmother makes felt boots. Khulan stirs the airag and churns butter. Temur practises with his bow behind the ger, shooting at a target of felt.

[long-pause]

As the sun goes down, the animals come home. The sheep and goats are put in a pen of felt and wooden fences. The foals are tied in their line. The dog lies down by the door. The family eats a simple supper. Then they sit outside and watch the sky turn red and purple.

[pause]

{img:family-sitting-outside-ger-sunset-sky-purple-sheep-pen-dog}
The grandmother tells a story. About a clever horse who saved his master from a flood. The children have heard it many times. But they listen anyway. And as the first stars appear, Sarnai looks to the west, where the sun has gone. Where her husband is. And she says a quiet prayer to the Eternal Blue Sky.

[long-pause]

{img:mongol-family-migrating-camels-loaded-ger-parts-carts-steppe-autumn}
The Mongols do not live in one place. They move. Usually four times a year. Once each season.

[pause]

Each family has its own places. A spring place, a summer place, an autumn place and a winter place. They have used these places for generations. The father's father used them. And his father before him. Each place is chosen for the season.

[long-pause]

The summer place is by a river or a lake, in an open valley. There is plenty of grass and water. The wind keeps the flies away. The spring place is somewhere sheltered, where the lambs and foals can be born safely. The autumn place has rich grass, so the animals can get fat before winter. And the winter place is in a sheltered valley, behind a hill. Out of the wind. Close to good grass that the snow does not bury too deeply.

[pause]

When it is time to move, the whole family works. The gers are taken down. The felt is rolled up. The lattice walls are folded flat. The roof poles are tied in bundles. The chests and pots and beds are packed. It takes only an hour or two. Then everything is loaded onto camels and ox carts.

[pause]

{img:ox-cart-big-wooden-wheels-loaded-ger-felt-chests-children-on-top}
Then the long line moves off. The camels with their loads. The carts creaking. The herds of sheep and goats and cows walking ahead. The horses running alongside. The riders herding them all. The dog trotting at the side. And the children riding on top of the carts, on the rolled-up felt, like little kings.

[long-pause]

The journey may take a day or several days. They go slowly. The animals must eat as they go. In the evening, they stop. They do not put up the whole ger. They just make a simple shelter. They light a fire. They cook. And they sleep under the stars.

[pause]

{img:migration-evening-camp-fire-simple-shelter-animals-resting-stars}
When they reach the new place, everything is put up again. The ger is set up in the same way it was before. The door faces south. Everything inside goes back to its place. By the evening, it is as if they have always lived there. The fire is burning. The tea is boiling. The animals are grazing. Home has moved. But it is still home.

[long-pause]

This autumn, Sarnai must lead the move herself. Batu is not here. She must decide when to go. Too early, and the summer grass is wasted. Too late, and the first snow could catch them on the way. She watches the sky every morning. She smells the wind. And one morning, when the grass has turned golden and the geese are flying south, she says to the family. Tomorrow we go.

[long-pause]

{img:mongol-toddler-sitting-on-horse-saddle-mother-holding-laughing}
On the steppe, children learn to ride almost before they can walk.

[pause]

At two or three, they are put on a horse for the first time. A gentle old horse that walks slowly. They are tied into the saddle so they will not fall. They hold the mane and bounce along. And they love it. By four or five, they can ride alone. By six, they can gallop.

[long-pause]

Children have work from a young age. At five or six, they watch the lambs and the goat kids. At seven or eight, they herd the sheep alone. They fetch water and gather dung for the fire. They help milk the animals. Girls and boys do much the same work. Both learn to ride and herd and shoot a bow.

[pause]

But they play too. They play a game with ankle bones of sheep. Each little bone has four different sides. One side looks like a horse. One like a sheep. One like a goat. One like a camel. The children throw them and see how they land. They flick them across the floor of the ger. They race them. There are dozens of games with ankle bones. Mongolian children still play them today.

[pause]

{img:children-playing-ankle-bones-shagai-ger-floor-felt-rug-laughing}
They wrestle on the grass. They race their horses. They play tag around the gers. They chase the young animals. They make little bows and arrows. And they sit with the old people and listen to stories in the long winter evenings.

[long-pause]

The children learn from everyone. From their parents and grandparents. From older brothers and sisters. From the neighbours. They learn which plants are good and which are poisonous. How to read the weather. How to tell if a sheep is sick. How to follow tracks in the grass. How to find their way home across the empty steppe, by the hills and the sun and the stars.

[pause]

{img:grandfather-showing-boy-tracks-in-snow-steppe-winter-pointing}
Temur, the boy of eleven, is learning to use a bow. His father made it for him before he left. It is small but strong. Every evening he practises behind the ger. At first he missed the target every time. Now he hits it most of the time. When his father comes home, he wants to show him. He imagines it all the time. His father watching. His father nodding. Good. That is good, son.

[long-pause]

{img:mongol-women-gathering-camp-council-gers-confident-talking}
Mongol women are strong and free. More than in many other lands at that time.

[pause]

They ride horses as well as men. They herd the animals. They run the camp. They make the gers and the felt and the clothes. They make all the milk foods. They handle money and trade. And when the men are away, which is often, the women are in charge of everything.

[long-pause]

Visitors from other lands were surprised by this. Travellers from Europe and China wrote about it. The Mongol women drive the carts, they wrote. They load the camels. They ride like men. They give orders. And their husbands listen to them.

[pause]

Some women had great power. Hoelun, the mother of Genghis Khan, kept her children alive when everyone left them. Later, she gave advice to her son. Börte, his wife, was his closest adviser. His daughters ruled lands. Later in the empire, some women ruled for a time as regents. One famous Mongol princess even fought as a wrestler. She said she would only marry a man who could beat her. Nobody could.

[pause]

{img:mongol-woman-riding-galloping-steppe-headdress-confident-free}
Women's work is hard. Up before everyone else. To bed after everyone else. Milking, cooking, sewing, felting, herding, moving camp. Bearing and raising the children. But they are respected for it. The Mongols have a saying. The man is the head of the ger. But the woman is its neck. And the neck turns the head.

[long-pause]

Mongol women love jewellery. They wear headdresses with silver and coral and turquoise. They wear earrings and rings and bracelets. On special days, married women wear a very tall hat. It is called a boqta. It is like a tall thin cone or a tube, sometimes a metre high. It is decorated with feathers and pearls and silk. Riding into a ger, they must bend down so it does not hit the door frame.

[pause]

{img:mongol-noblewoman-tall-boqta-headdress-feathers-pearls-silk-ger}
Sarnai has worn hers only twice. At her wedding. And at the great gathering when the khan came to their valley years ago. She keeps it in a painted chest. Sometimes Khulan asks to see it. Sarnai takes it out carefully. Khulan touches the feathers. One day you will wear one, says her mother. Khulan smiles. And puts it back very gently.

[long-pause]

When the khan's armies were away, the great camp at home was run by women. They decided where to graze the herds. They settled quarrels. They welcomed visitors and sent supplies to the armies. One traveller wrote that the camp moved as smoothly as a city. With carts in long rows and gers on wheels pulled by twenty oxen. And at the front of it all, a woman, deciding where to stop.

[long-pause]

{img:yam-relay-station-steppe-gers-fresh-horses-rider-arriving-dust}
How do you rule an empire that stretches across half of Asia? How do you get a message from one end to the other?

[pause]

Genghis Khan had an answer. The Yam.

[long-pause]

The Yam is a chain of stations across the empire. Every thirty or forty kilometres, along the main routes, there is a station. It is a group of gers with a few families living there. They keep fresh horses ready. And food and drink and beds. Day and night.

[pause]

When an official messenger arrives, he jumps off his tired horse. He eats a little, drinks a little. He takes a fresh horse. And he rides on. Fast. Sometimes he does not stop to sleep. He ties himself into the saddle and dozes as he rides. In an emergency, a message can travel two or three hundred kilometres in one day. Nothing in the world at that time is faster.

[pause]

{img:messenger-riding-night-bells-on-belt-moonlight-steppe-alone}
The messengers wear bells on their belts. As they come close to a station, the station keepers hear the bells. They get a fresh horse ready at once. So the messenger loses no time at all.

[long-pause]

Each official traveller carries a special tablet. It is called a paiza. It is a flat plate of metal with writing on it. Gold for the most important. Silver for others. Wood for the least. It means, this person travels for the khan. Give him what he needs. With a paiza, you can get horses and food at every station. Without one, you get nothing.

[pause]

{img:golden-paiza-tablet-mongol-script-hole-cord-hand-close}
The families who run the stations do this as their duty to the khan. Instead of going to war, or paying other taxes. They look after the horses. They keep the gers clean and warm. They cook for travellers. And they see every kind of person pass by. Messengers and officials and traders. Monks and ambassadors. From China and Persia and the lands beyond.

[long-pause]

For Sarnai's family, the Yam is very important. Because it carries news from the west. News of the armies. And sometimes, letters from the men. Not written letters, usually. The men cannot write. But they can send words through a messenger. Your husband is well. Your son is well. Tell the family I am thinking of them. And sometimes, those few words are the most precious thing in the world.

[pause]

The station near Sarnai's valley is run by an old couple and their sons. Sometimes Temur rides over to help with the horses. He loves to watch the messengers come in. Dusty and tired, with bells jingling. They jump off one horse and onto another in a moment. Then they are gone. One day, Temur thinks, I will ride like that.

[long-pause]

{img:silk-road-caravan-camels-merchants-arriving-mongol-camp-goods}
The Mongols are herders, not farmers or craftsmen. They have animals and milk and wool and felt. But many other things they cannot make themselves. Iron and silk. Grain and tea. Pots and needles. So they trade.

[long-pause]

For a long time, the steppe was dangerous for traders. Raiders could rob a caravan at any time. But under Genghis Khan, the roads are safer. And the khan likes traders. He gives them protection. He lets them travel freely. He even lends them money sometimes.

[pause]

So caravans come. Long lines of camels, loaded with goods. From China in the east come silk and tea and porcelain and paper. From Persia and the lands in the west come cotton and glass and silver and spices. From the forests in the north come furs. And all of it passes through the Mongol lands. Along the old road that will later be called the Silk Road.

[pause]

{img:merchant-unrolling-silk-inside-ger-family-admiring-colours}
When a caravan comes near the camp, it is a big event. The children run out to look at the camels. The traders unpack their goods in front of the gers. Bolts of silk in red and blue and gold. Iron pots and knives. Bags of salt and grain. Needles and thread. Little mirrors and beads. The families come to look and touch and bargain.

[long-pause]

The Mongols pay with animals and wool and felt and furs. A good horse can buy a lot. Sometimes they pay with silver. The traders count carefully. Everyone argues about prices. It is noisy and fun.

[pause]

{img:caravan-leaving-steppe-sunset-camels-line-dust-trader-waving}
Sarnai buys a little bag of tea and a new iron pot. And a piece of red silk for Khulan. She is almost grown up now. She will need a fine deel for the summer gathering. Khulan holds the silk against her face. It is so soft. She has never touched anything so soft. She thanks her mother again and again.

[long-pause]

The traders also bring news. They have seen cities and markets and palaces. They tell stories of the far west, where the armies are. They say the khan has taken great cities. They say the armies are moving further. Sarnai listens carefully. Any news of the men? The traders shrug. The armies are huge. They do not know every rider. But they have heard the men are well and the khan is winning. It is something. She thanks them and gives them a bowl of airag for the road.

[long-pause]

{img:karakorum-early-camp-orkhon-valley-gers-tents-wooden-buildings-1220}
In 1220, Genghis Khan chose a place for the heart of his empire. A valley in the middle of Mongolia, by the river Orkhon. The place was called Karakorum.

[pause]

At first, it was just a great camp. A huge gathering of gers and tents. Store houses and workshops. A place to keep supplies and treasure. A place to meet. But it would grow.

[long-pause]

The Orkhon valley is a good place. It is green and wide, with a river and good grass. Mountains protect it to the west. And it is near the middle of the Mongol lands. Long before the Mongols, other peoples of the steppe had their capitals here too. It is an old place of power.

[pause]

After Genghis Khan, his son built a real city at Karakorum. With walls and a palace. Streets and markets and temples. People from all over the empire lived there. Mongol nobles. Chinese craftsmen. Persian traders. European visitors. Muslim and Christian and Buddhist priests. All in one place, in the middle of the steppe.

[pause]

{img:karakorum-silver-fountain-tree-palace-hall-guests-later-wonder}
A visitor from Europe described one wonder of Karakorum a few decades later. A great silver tree stood in front of the palace. It was made by a craftsman from Paris, who had been brought to Mongolia. At the top of the tree stood an angel holding a trumpet. Four silver lions sat at its foot. And from four silver pipes came four different drinks. Mare's milk and wine and honey drink and rice beer. When the khan wanted drinks, the angel blew its trumpet. And the drinks began to flow.

[long-pause]

But in 1220, all this is still in the future. Now, Karakorum is just the place where the camp is gathered. Where messengers come and go. Where goods are stored. Where the family of the khan sometimes stays.

[pause]

{img:orkhon-valley-today-green-river-horses-stone-turtle-later}
Later, Karakorum was abandoned and almost forgotten. Today very little is left. A big stone turtle lies in the grass. It once held up a stone tablet. Buried foundations. A few stones. And next to it, a beautiful Buddhist monastery built later, partly from the stones of the old city. Its white walls are topped with many small white towers. Horses graze around it, just as they always have.

[long-pause]

In 1220, Sarnai has never been to Karakorum. It is many days' ride away. But Batu told her about it before he left. Hundreds of gers in rows, he said. Carts piled with treasure. Horses as far as the eye can see. People from every land. She tried to imagine it. One day, she thinks, when he comes home, maybe we will go together. And she smiles at the thought.

[long-pause]

{img:mongol-shaman-drum-feathers-ceremony-fire-night-ger-family}
What do the Mongols believe?

[pause]

The great god is the Eternal Blue Sky. Tengri. It is above everything. And below is the Earth, Etügen. The mother. Between them live people, and all around are spirits. Spirits of mountains and rivers and springs. Spirits of ancestors. Spirits of the fire in the hearth.

[long-pause]

Every ger has its fire. And the fire is sacred. You must never put dirt into it. You must never step over it. You must never poke it with a knife or put out water on it carelessly. The fire spirit protects the family. When a new ger is set up, the first thing done is to light the fire. And it is given a little milk or butter or fat, as a gift.

[pause]

Some people can speak with the spirits. They are called shamans. Men and women. A shaman wears a special coat with ribbons and bells and little mirrors. He beats a big flat drum. He sings and dances. And people believe his spirit can travel to the world of the spirits. He can ask them for help. For healing. For good weather. For finding lost animals.

[pause]

{img:ovoo-stone-cairn-hilltop-blue-silk-scarves-wind-offerings-riders}
On hilltops and mountain passes, there are piles of stones called ovoos. When people pass an ovoo, they stop. They walk around it three times in the direction the sun moves. They add a stone. They leave a little gift. Some milk, a coin, a blue silk scarf tied to a stick. And they pray for a safe journey.

[long-pause]

Genghis Khan himself believed in the Eternal Blue Sky. Before great decisions, he went up a sacred mountain alone. He took off his hat and his belt as a sign of humility. He knelt. And he prayed for days. Then he came down and acted.

[pause]

But he also respected other faiths. He let people believe what they wanted. Christian priests, Buddhist monks, Muslim scholars, Daoist masters. They all came to the Mongol camps. They all prayed in their own ways. And the khan listened to them all with interest.

[pause]

{img:different-faiths-priests-monk-imam-gathered-khan-tent-respectful}
Later, his grandson held a famous debate. He invited a Christian, a Muslim and a Buddhist to argue which faith was best. They talked and argued for a whole day. At the end, the Mongols gave them all a lot of airag to drink. And everyone went home. Nobody won. But everyone had been heard.

[long-pause]

{img:burkhan-khaldun-sacred-mountain-forest-slopes-river-onon-mist}
In the north of Mongolia, near where Genghis Khan was born, there is a mountain. It is called Burkhan Khaldun. It is the most sacred mountain of the Mongols.

[pause]

It is not very high. It is covered with dark pine and larch forests. Its top is bare and rocky. Rivers begin on its slopes. The Onon and the Kherlen. The rivers of Genghis Khan's childhood. Bears and deer and wolves live in its forests.

[long-pause]

When Temüjin was young and in danger, he hid on this mountain. The night his wife Börte was taken, he fled up its slopes into the forest. His enemies searched for him for three days. But the mountain hid him. When they finally left, Temüjin came out. He was alive.

[pause]

He was so grateful that he made a promise. He hung his belt around his neck and took off his hat. He knelt toward the sun. He sprinkled milk into the air. And he prayed. He said that the mountain had protected his life, as small as a louse. He promised that every morning he would honour it. And that his children and their children would honour it too. Forever.

[pause]

{img:young-temujin-praying-kneeling-mountain-forest-belt-around-neck}
And they did. For centuries, the mountain was a holy place. Ordinary people were not allowed to go there. Only the khans and their families. Later, during some hard times, people were forbidden to visit. But they never forgot.

[long-pause]

Many people believe Genghis Khan was buried somewhere on or near this mountain. Nobody knows exactly where. The secret was kept very carefully. That is another story, for later.

[pause]

{img:sacred-mountain-silent-forest-snow-patches-mist-evening-light}
Today, Burkhan Khaldun is protected. It is a World Heritage Site. Mongolians still honour it. Every few years, the president of Mongolia leads a ceremony there. People climb up to the ovoo at the top. They walk around it. They leave blue scarves and prayers. The wind carries them away over the forests. And the mountain stays as quiet and green as it was eight hundred years ago.

[long-pause]

{img:naadam-summer-games-steppe-crowds-gers-banners-wrestlers-horses}
In midsummer, the people of the steppe gather for games. Later this will be called Naadam. It means games. And there are three of them. Wrestling, archery and horse racing. The three manly games, people call them. But women and children take part too.

[long-pause]

People come from far away. They ride for days. They put up their gers on the grass by a river. Hundreds of gers. Thousands of horses. Everyone wears their best clothes. Bright deels and fine hats. It is a time to meet old friends and make new ones. To trade and talk and arrange marriages. And to watch the games.

[pause]

The wrestling is the most loved. The wrestlers wear little vests that cover their arms and back, but leave the chest open. And tight short trousers. And boots. Before a match, each wrestler does a special dance. He spreads his arms and moves them slowly like a great eagle, or a falcon. Then the two wrestlers grab each other. They push and pull and twist. The one who first touches the ground with anything but his feet loses.

[pause]

{img:mongol-wrestlers-eagle-dance-arms-spread-crowd-watching-grass}
There are no weight classes. A small wrestler can face a huge one. There is no time limit. A match can last for a long time. And the last wrestler standing is the champion. People talk about him for years.

[long-pause]

The archery is next. Men and women shoot at targets on the ground, far away. When someone hits, the judges sing a little song. They raise their arms and call out the score. Everyone cheers.

[pause]

{img:mongol-archers-men-women-shooting-targets-judges-singing-arms}
And then the horse race. This is the most exciting. The riders are children. Boys and girls, some only six or seven years old. The horses are young and fast. The race is not on a track. It is across the open steppe. Twenty or thirty kilometres. Over hills and through valleys.

[long-pause]

Khulan is riding this year. She is older than most. But she is a wonderful rider. Her horse is a grey called Cloud. She has trained him all summer. At the start, dozens of children wait on their horses. They sing a song together to call the horses' spirits. Then they are off. Galloping into the distance until they disappear over a hill.

[pause]

{img:children-jockeys-galloping-steppe-dust-horses-race-finish-crowd}
Everyone waits at the finish. Long minutes pass. Then a cloud of dust appears on the horizon. Riders. Coming fast. People shout and point. Sarnai stands on tiptoe. Is Khulan there? The first rider comes over the last hill. It is a small boy on a brown horse. Then two more. Then a grey horse. A grey horse with a girl on its back, leaning low over its neck. It is Khulan. She comes in fourth. Out of more than forty. Sarnai shouts with joy. And when Khulan rides up to her, flushed and dusty and grinning, her mother hugs her so hard she almost falls off.

[long-pause]

{img:eagle-hunter-golden-eagle-arm-horseback-mountains-snow-hood}
In the west of the Mongol lands, in the high mountains, some people hunt with eagles.

[pause]

Not small birds. Golden eagles. Huge powerful birds with wings more than two metres wide. Their claws are as strong as iron. They can see a fox moving on a hillside from kilometres away.

[long-pause]

An eagle hunter catches a young eagle. Usually a female, because they are bigger and stronger. He takes her from the nest when she is just old enough. Or he catches her with a net. Then he trains her. It takes months of patience. He keeps her with him day and night. He talks to her. He feeds her by hand. She learns to sit on his arm. To fly to him when he calls. To wear a little leather hood over her eyes. With the hood on, she stays calm.

[pause]

The hunter wears a thick leather glove on his arm. The eagle is heavy. So when he rides, he rests his arm on a wooden stand fixed to the saddle. Riding with an eagle on your arm is not easy. The horse must be calm too.

[pause]

{img:eagle-taking-off-from-arm-wings-wide-valley-below-winter-light}
The hunting happens in winter. When there is snow on the ground. Animals are easy to see against the white. The hunter rides up a hill with his eagle. He looks down into the valley. When he sees a fox, he takes off the hood. The eagle sees it too. She spreads her wings and launches herself into the air. And she dives.

[long-pause]

The fox fur is used for warm hats and coats. Very valuable in the cold winters. A good eagle can catch many foxes in a season.

[pause]

The hunter and his eagle become very close. Like partners. After about ten years, the hunter takes his eagle up into the mountains. He leaves her a last meal. And he lets her go free. So she can find a mate and have chicks of her own. He watches her fly away. And he says goodbye.

[pause]

{img:old-hunter-releasing-eagle-mountains-sunset-farewell-wings}
In the far west of Mongolia today, there are still eagle hunters. Mostly among the Kazakh people who live there. Fathers teach their sons. And nowadays, sometimes their daughters too. There is an eagle festival every autumn. Hunters come with their eagles and show their skills. People come from all over the world to watch. And the great birds soar over the mountains, just as they did eight hundred years ago.

[long-pause]

{img:old-daoist-monk-changchun-white-beard-robe-mountain-temple-china}
In the year 1220, a messenger from Genghis Khan arrives at a mountain temple in the north of China. He brings an invitation for an old monk.

[pause]

The monk's name is Changchun. That means eternal spring. He is a Daoist master. Daoists believe in living in harmony with nature. They seek a long and healthy life through calm and simple living. Changchun is about seventy years old. He is famous for his wisdom.

[long-pause]

Genghis Khan has heard about him. The khan is getting older. He has heard that Daoist masters know secrets of long life. Maybe even a medicine to live forever. So he wants to meet this monk. He sends an official with a golden tablet and a polite letter. Come to me, the letter says. I am far away in the west. But I will make your journey safe.

[pause]

Changchun hesitates. He is old. The journey is thousands of kilometres. Over mountains and deserts and the whole steppe. To a place he has never seen. Many younger men would be afraid. But he decides to go. He chooses about eighteen of his students to come with him. And in the spring, they set out.

[pause]

{img:monk-and-students-setting-out-carts-horses-leaving-temple-spring}
One of his students writes everything down. Every day of the journey. What they see. Who they meet. What the weather is like. His book still exists. It is one of the best descriptions of the Mongol lands at the time of Genghis Khan.

[long-pause]

First they travel north, out of China. Then they cross the steppe of Mongolia. The student writes about the wide grass and the gers. The people are friendly, he says. They bring us airag and meat. In one place, they meet Mongol women who greet them warmly. In another, they cross a land of strange rocks. They see the sun rise over endless grass.

[pause]

{img:monk-caravan-crossing-mongolian-steppe-gers-welcoming-families}
They pass through the great camp of the khan's family. The ladies of the court welcome them. They give the old monk felt and furs and food for the journey. And then he goes on. West. Toward the mountains. Toward the khan.

[long-pause]

Perhaps they pass through the valley where Sarnai's family lives. Perhaps Khulan and Temur see a strange group riding by. An old man with a long white beard and grey robes, sitting very straight on his horse. Young men in robes riding behind him. Mongol guards in front. The children stare. The old monk smiles at them. And then he rides on, over the hill.

[long-pause]

{img:altai-mountains-snow-pass-caravan-climbing-cold-wind-monk}
Changchun's journey is long and hard.

[pause]

After the steppe, they reach great mountains. The Altai. High and cold and covered with snow. The road goes up through steep passes. The carts cannot go up. So soldiers tie ropes to them and pull them up by hand. Then let them down slowly on the other side. In some places, they have to build a road first. It takes days.

[long-pause]

Then comes the desert. A great sea of sand and stones. No water. No grass. Burning by day and freezing by night. They travel at night to avoid the heat. The student writes that they heard strange sounds in the desert. Like voices calling. The guides told them, those are spirits. Ignore them. Keep going. Ancient travellers believed the desert was full of spirits that tried to lead people astray. Today we know the wind and the moving sand can make strange sounds.

[pause]

They are very glad when they reach the green oasis towns on the other side. Fruit trees and water and gardens. People who greet them and give them melons and grapes. After the desert, it feels like paradise.

[pause]

{img:oasis-town-gardens-melons-grapes-travellers-resting-shade}
The journey goes on for more than a year. They cross rivers and more mountains. They pass through great cities, some of them damaged by war. They meet people of many lands and languages. The old monk is tired. But he keeps going.

[long-pause]

At last, in the spring of 1222, they reach the khan. He is camped in the high mountains of what is now Afghanistan. Genghis Khan welcomes the old monk with great respect. You came from so far, he says. You must be tired. Then he asks his question. Do you have a medicine for long life? A medicine to live forever?

[pause]

{img:genghis-khan-meeting-old-monk-tent-respect-mountains-1222}
Changchun answers honestly. There are ways to protect life, he says. But there is no medicine to live forever. The khan is quiet for a moment. Then he thanks the monk for his honesty. Over the next months, they talk many times. The monk teaches him about living simply. About caring for the people. About not killing without need. About calming the heart. The khan listens.

[long-pause]

Then the old monk returns home to China. The khan gives him gifts and protects his temples. Changchun lives a few more years in peace. And his student's book about the long journey has survived for eight hundred years. So we can still travel with them today. Across the steppe and the mountains and the desert. To the tent of the great khan.

[long-pause]

{img:mongolian-winter-camp-snow-sheltered-valley-gers-smoke-cold-blue}
Winter comes to the steppe. And winter in Mongolia is very hard.

[pause]

By late autumn, the family has moved to their winter place. A sheltered valley behind a hill. The gers are covered with extra layers of felt. The animals have pens made of stone and wood, with roofs of branches, to keep off the worst of the wind. Stacks of dried dung are piled high for fuel. Dried meat and cheese hang in the store ger.

[long-pause]

Then the cold comes. Real cold. Forty degrees below zero. The air is so cold it hurts to breathe. Your breath freezes on your scarf. The river freezes solid. The snow squeaks under your boots. The sky is very clear and very blue. And at night, the stars are so bright and close you feel you could touch them.

[pause]

Inside the ger, it is warm. The stove burns all day and all night. The family sits close together on the felt. The children play with ankle bones. The grandmother tells stories. Sarnai sews. Khulan makes felt boots. Temur fixes harness. And the newborn lambs and goats, born too early in the cold, sleep in a corner wrapped in felt.

[pause]

{img:inside-ger-winter-stove-glowing-family-close-lambs-wrapped-corner}
The greatest danger is called the dzud. It is a terrible winter disaster. Sometimes there is too much snow. It covers the grass so deeply that the animals cannot dig through it. Or the snow melts and then freezes into hard ice. The animals cannot break through. They cannot eat. In a bad dzud, families can lose most of their herds. And that means losing everything.

[long-pause]

This winter, the snow comes early. Then it rains a little. Then it freezes. A crust of ice covers the steppe. Sarnai looks at it in the morning and her face goes grey. She knows what it could mean. The family works day and night. They break the ice with sticks and axes so the animals can reach the grass. They feed them the hay they have saved. They bring the weakest ones into the ger. Everyone is exhausted. Will the herds survive?

[pause]

{img:family-breaking-ice-snow-sticks-sheep-grass-underneath-cold-dawn}
Then, after a week, the wind changes. It blows from the south. Warm air comes. The ice cracks and softens. The animals can graze again. Some of the herd has been lost. But most of them have made it. Sarnai sits down on the floor of the ger and cries. Just for a moment. The grandmother puts her hand on her shoulder. We are still here, she says. And so are they.

[long-pause]

And one evening in the deep winter, a rider comes down the valley. Bells on his belt. A messenger from the Yam. He stops at the ger. He brings words from the west. The khan is coming home. The armies will return next year. Batu is well. He sends his love. Sarnai cannot speak. The children shout and dance around the ger. Even the grandmother laughs. And that night, nobody minds the cold at all.

[long-pause]

{img:riders-returning-home-steppe-spring-families-running-to-meet}
The armies did come home. In 1225, Genghis Khan returned to Mongolia. And with him came many of the riders. Batu among them.

[pause]

Imagine the day. Sarnai sees a group of riders on the horizon. She knows the shape of one of them before she can see his face. The children run. Batu jumps off his horse. Little Bayar does not recognise him at first. He hides behind his mother. Batu kneels down and holds out his hand. And slowly, Bayar comes to him.

[long-pause]

Genghis Khan lived for only a few more years. In 1227, far away in the south, he passed away. He was about sixty-five. His sons and grandsons continued what he had started. Under them, the Mongol Empire grew even bigger. It stretched from Korea in the east to Hungary in the west. From Siberia in the north to Persia in the south. It was the largest land empire in history.

[pause]

The war and conquest caused great suffering in many lands. We remember that with sorrow. But the empire also brought something new. For about a hundred years, people and goods and ideas could travel safely across the whole of Asia. From China to Europe. This time is sometimes called the Mongol Peace.

[pause]

{img:marco-polo-caravan-arriving-mongol-court-china-later-gifts}
Travellers came from far away. The most famous was a young man from Venice called Marco Polo. He travelled all the way to China, to the court of Genghis Khan's grandson, Kublai Khan. He stayed for many years. When he went home, he wrote a book about it. People in Europe could hardly believe his stories. Paper money! Stones that burn! Cities of a million people!

[long-pause]

Inventions travelled along the roads too. Paper and printing. The compass. Gunpowder. Medicine and maps and the stars. Foods and spices and stories. East and west met as never before.

[pause]

{img:mongol-yuan-dynasty-later-palace-china-grand-hall-kublai-court}
Later, the empire broke up into several parts. Each part was ruled by a different branch of the family. Over time, they faded. But in Mongolia itself, the people went on living as they always had. With their gers and their horses and their herds. Moving with the seasons. Under the Eternal Blue Sky.

[long-pause]

{img:funeral-procession-steppe-night-riders-quiet-secret-mountains}
Where is Genghis Khan buried?

[pause]

Nobody knows. It is one of the greatest mysteries of history.

[long-pause]

When he passed away in 1227, his body was carried back to Mongolia. Old stories say it was a long secret procession. Some say the riders made sure nobody saw them pass. They wanted the place to stay hidden forever. So that nobody could ever disturb him.

[pause]

The stories say he was buried somewhere near the sacred mountain. Burkhan Khaldun. Where he hid as a young man. Where he prayed. Some say a river was turned to flow over the grave. Some say a thousand horses were ridden over the place to hide every trace. And then trees grew over it. And after a few years, nobody could tell where it was.

[pause]

{img:forest-hills-near-sacred-mountain-quiet-trees-hidden-place-mist}
For many centuries afterwards, the whole area around the mountain was forbidden. It was called the Great Taboo. Guards kept everyone away. Only the khan's family and special guards could enter. Even after the empire was gone, people kept away from it. Out of respect.

[long-pause]

In our own time, explorers have searched for the grave. They have used satellites and planes and special cameras. They have walked the hills and forests. Some have found old graves and stone circles. But not the grave of Genghis Khan.

[pause]

And many Mongolians do not want it to be found. They say, let him rest. He wanted his grave to be secret. That wish should be respected. And maybe they are right.

[pause]

{img:mausoleum-shrine-genghis-khan-white-tents-later-pilgrims-incense}
Far away, in the north of China, there is a shrine for Genghis Khan. It is a big building with three domes shaped like gers. People come to honour him there. Families come. Old people. Children. They light candles and burn incense. They offer milk and bow. It is not his grave. But it is a place to remember him.

[long-pause]

On the steppe, the wind blows over the grass. The forests on the sacred mountain are quiet. Somewhere under the trees, perhaps, the great khan sleeps. And nobody knows where. And perhaps that is just as it should be.

[long-pause]

{img:modern-mongolia-ger-solar-panel-motorbike-horses-steppe-today}
Today, Mongolia is a country of about three and a half million people. It lies between Russia and China. Its capital is Ulaanbaatar, a busy city with tall buildings and traffic and markets.

[pause]

But outside the city, the steppe is still there. Wide and green and empty, under the huge blue sky.

[long-pause]

About one in four Mongolians still live as herders. Like their ancestors. They live in gers. They keep sheep and goats and horses and camels and yaks. They move with the seasons. They make airag in the summer and felt in the autumn. They ride horses. Now some also have motorbikes and solar panels and satellite dishes. A family might have a television in their ger. But the life is still the life of the steppe.

[pause]

Children still learn to ride before they can walk. They still play with ankle bones. They still herd sheep at seven. And they still race horses across the steppe at the summer games.

[pause]

{img:naadam-today-ulaanbaatar-stadium-wrestlers-crowd-flags-summer}
Naadam is now the national festival of Mongolia. Every July, there are games all over the country. The biggest is in the capital, in a great stadium. Wrestlers in their little vests do the eagle dance. Archers shoot at targets. And children race horses across the open steppe. The whole country stops to watch.

[long-pause]

Genghis Khan is a national hero in Mongolia. His face is on the money. The airport is named after him. Near the capital, there is a giant silver statue of him on a horse. It is forty metres tall. You can climb up inside it and stand on the horse's head. From there, you can see the steppe for kilometres around.

[pause]

So we leave the steppe tonight, in the year 1220, as the evening falls.

[long-pause]

The sun is setting over the grassland. The sky turns gold, then pink, then deep blue. The sheep and goats are in their pens. The foals are tied in their line. The horses graze on the hillside, their shapes dark against the sky. Smoke rises from the crown of the ger. Inside, the fire glows. Sarnai sits by the stove, mending a boot. Khulan stirs the airag. Temur oils his little bow. Altan and Bayar are asleep under a sheepskin. The grandmother hums an old song. The big black dog lies across the doorway. And outside, the stars come out one by one. More and more. Until the whole sky is white with them. Over the sleeping camp. Over the endless grass. Under the Eternal Blue Sky.
