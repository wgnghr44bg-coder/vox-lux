In 1941, the island of Oahu was a quiet corner of the Pacific. It had green mountains, sugar fields, a relaxed town called Honolulu, and a great naval harbour full of grey ships. Thousands of young sailors lived there, alongside families from Hawaii, Japan, China, the Philippines and the mainland. In this story, we will look at everyday life on the island in the months before the seventh of December, at the attack itself, told gently, and at what came after.

In 1941, Hawaii was not yet a state, and most visitors reached it after five days at sea. Hawaii in 1941 was a territory of the United States, not a state. It lay more than two thousand miles from California, in the middle of the Pacific Ocean. Most people travelled there by passenger ship, a voyage of four or five days. A small number arrived by flying boat. Pan American Airways operated large aircraft called Clippers, which landed in the harbour after a flight of nearly twenty hours. About four hundred thousand people lived in the islands, most of them on Oahu. The island rose steeply from the sea, with long green ridges along its centre and coral reefs along the shore. Rain fell often on the mountains, and rainbows were common over the valleys. Census figures from 1940 show how small and distant the territory still was. Even so, it was growing quickly. For many visitors from the mainland, Hawaii seemed remote and almost foreign, though it was part of their own country.

[long-pause]

{img:02-aloha-tower-boat-day-crowds-leis}

The arrival of a passenger ship in Honolulu was a public celebration known as Boat Day. When a large passenger liner reached Honolulu, much of the town came down to the harbour. Ships of the Matson line, such as the Lurline and the Matsonia, arrived in the morning after passing Diamond Head. Local boys swam out to meet them and dived for coins thrown from the decks. At the pier stood the Aloha Tower, a white clock tower opened in 1926. A band played Hawaiian music, and hula dancers performed for the arriving passengers. As visitors came down the gangway, they received leis, garlands of fresh flowers such as plumeria, ginger and orchids. The word aloha was used as a greeting and a farewell. Photographs and newsreels from the 1930s show large crowds on these occasions. The custom was promoted by the shipping lines and the tourist industry. Even so, it also reflected genuine local hospitality. Boat Day became one of the best-remembered rituals of travel to Hawaii before the war.

[long-pause]

{img:03-palm-trees-hibiscus-garden-trade-wind}

Oahu's climate was mild all year, with frequent short showers that fell even in sunshine. The weather on Oahu was warm but rarely hot, and it was never cold. Trade winds blew from the northeast on most days, bringing cool air from the ocean. They carried clouds over the mountains, where much of the rain fell. Short showers passed over the lower ground as well, often while the sun was still shining. Weather records from Honolulu show only small differences between summer and winter temperatures. Gardens were full of flowering plants, many of them introduced from other tropical regions. Hibiscus, bougainvillea, plumeria and flame trees were common in the town. Visitors from mainland cities often wrote about the softness of the light and the scent of flowers after rain. Even so, the climate varied considerably across the island. The windward side was much wetter than the leeward side, and the mountain peaks received heavy rainfall. For residents, the mild climate shaped daily life, from clothing to houses with open verandas.

[long-pause]

{img:04-plantation-workers-pineapple-fields-mountains}

By 1941, more than a third of Hawaii's population was of Japanese ancestry. Hawaii's population came from many places. Native Hawaiians were descended from Polynesian voyagers who had reached the islands more than a thousand years earlier, navigating by the stars and the ocean swells. In the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, plantation owners recruited workers from across Asia and Europe. Large numbers came from Japan, as well as from China, Korea, the Philippines and Portugal. By 1940, census figures showed that about thirty-seven percent of the territory's people were of Japanese ancestry. Americans from the mainland worked as teachers, merchants, officials and members of the armed forces. Children of different backgrounds attended school together. A local language known as pidgin developed, combining words from Hawaiian, English, Japanese, Portuguese and other languages. Even so, the society was not equal. Plantation owners and mainland Americans held most of the economic and political power. Yet daily life in the islands was unusually mixed for an American community of that time.

[long-pause]

{img:05-taro-terraces-windward-valley-cliffs}

Traditional Hawaiian society divided each island into land units called ahupua'a, running from the mountains to the sea. In the traditional Hawaiian system, an island was divided into long wedges of land. Each one, called an ahupua'a, usually stretched from the mountain ridge down to the reef. It contained everything a community needed. Upland forests provided timber, plants and feathers. Valleys held irrigated terraces where taro was grown. Taro is a plant with a starchy root, and it was the staple food of old Hawaii. The coast and reef provided fish and shellfish. People in each ahupua'a shared resources and were expected to care for the land and water. Historians and Hawaiian scholars describe this as a careful system of resource management. By 1941, most land was organised in very different ways, and plantations covered much of it. Yet in some windward valleys, families still cultivated taro in flooded terraces in the traditional manner. Efforts to restore taro farming and the ahupua'a approach continue in Hawaii today.

[long-pause]

{img:06-sugar-cane-fields-wind-plantation-houses}

In 1941, sugar and pineapple plantations covered much of Oahu's farmland. Plantation agriculture dominated Hawaii's economy before the Second World War. Sugar cane grew on the flat central plains of Oahu, taller than a person and swaying in the wind. Pineapples grew in long rows on the red upland soils. Hawaii was then one of the world's leading producers of canned pineapple. Workers went into the fields early in the morning, wearing wide hats, long sleeves and gloves to protect themselves from the sun and the sharp leaves. Many lived in plantation camps, groups of small wooden houses provided by the companies. At lunchtime, workers often shared food from their tin lunch boxes, a custom that helped create Hawaii's mixed local cuisine. Historians of the plantations describe long hours, low pay and strict supervision. Workers organised strikes at various times to demand better conditions. Even so, the camps also developed strong community life, with shared festivals, sports and schools. Many island families trace their history to these plantations.

[long-pause]

{img:07-pearl-harbor-lochs-from-hills-morning}

The Hawaiian name for Pearl Harbor was Wai Momi, meaning pearl waters. Pearl Harbor lies on the southern coast of Oahu, a few miles west of Honolulu. It is a large natural harbour with several branching inlets, called lochs, and a flat island in the middle. The Hawaiian name, Wai Momi, means pearl waters. Pearl oysters once lived in its shallow lagoons, and Hawaiians built stone-walled fishponds along its shores. For centuries, the area supported fishing and farming communities. In the late nineteenth century, the United States gained rights to use the harbour, and in the early twentieth century the navy began dredging the entrance channel and building a base. By 1941, the pearl beds had long disappeared, partly because of silt and development. The old fishponds were mostly gone too. Archaeologists have recorded remains of some of them. Even so, the name survived in English as Pearl Harbor. It preserved a trace of the harbour's earlier life as a place of fish, pearls and quiet waters.

[long-pause]

{img:08-honolulu-street-1941-shops-palms}

Iolani Palace, the former home of Hawaii's monarchs, stood in the centre of Honolulu. Honolulu in 1941 had about two hundred thousand residents. Its downtown streets were lined with low stone and wooden buildings, many with shaded verandas. At the centre stood Iolani Palace, completed in 1882 for King Kalakaua. It was a handsome building with columns and wide verandas, surrounded by large banyan trees. The last Hawaiian monarch, Queen Liliuokalani, lived there until the overthrow of the kingdom in 1893. In 1941, the palace was used as the capitol building of the territorial government. Officials worked in rooms that had once been used by royalty. The palace was restored in later decades and opened as a museum in 1978. It is often described as the only official royal residence in the United States. Historians see it as a symbol of Hawaii's history as an independent kingdom. Even in 1941, it was a reminder that the islands had a long political history before they became an American territory.

[long-pause]

{img:09-honolulu-sidewalk-aloha-shirts-cotton-dresses}

Brightly printed aloha shirts became popular in Honolulu during the 1930s. In the 1930s, shirts printed with flowers, palm trees and island scenes became fashionable in Honolulu. They were known as aloha shirts. Local tailors and shops, some of them run by Chinese and Japanese families, produced them using colourful fabrics. Advertisements from the period show that by the late 1930s they were popular with both residents and tourists. Many men also wore light-coloured suits suited to the warm climate. Women often wore cotton dresses in bright colours, and some wore a flower behind one ear. Local tradition held that a flower behind the left ear signalled that a woman was attached, while one behind the right ear meant she was not. Street vendors sold newspapers and shaved ice with sweet syrup. Visitors often described the pace of life as relaxed. Even so, Honolulu was a working city with offices, shops and a busy port. The aloha shirt later became a lasting symbol of Hawaiian style.

[long-pause]

{img:10-fish-market-chinatown-morning-1941}

Honolulu's Chinatown fish market sold catch landed only hours earlier, and the town's food mixed many cuisines. Every morning, the fish markets in Honolulu's Chinatown were busy with buyers. Tuna, mahi-mahi and snapper were laid out on ice, and sellers called out prices in several languages. Nearby shops sold dried fish, noodles, rice, ginger and tropical fruit such as mangoes, papayas and lychees. Food in Honolulu reflected the many communities living there. Hawaiian dishes included poi, made from pounded taro, and laulau, pork and fish wrapped in leaves and steamed. Japanese families brought miso soup and noodles. Chinese restaurants served dumplings, and Portuguese bakers made sweet bread. Korean, Filipino and American dishes were also common. Food historians describe this mixture as the origin of modern Hawaiian local food, including the plate lunch. Even so, many families kept their own traditions at home. The markets of Chinatown were a meeting place for all these influences. Some of the same fish markets and shops still operate in the district today.

[long-pause]

{img:11-waikiki-beach-1941-surfers-diamond-head}

Duke Kahanamoku, a Hawaiian Olympic champion, helped spread surfing around the world. Waikiki Beach curved for about two miles east of downtown Honolulu, with Diamond Head at its far end. Waves rolled in from the reef long and gentle, ideal for swimming and surfing. Hawaiian beachboys taught visitors to surf on long wooden boards and took them out in outrigger canoes. The most famous Hawaiian surfer was Duke Kahanamoku. He won Olympic gold medals in swimming in 1912 and 1920. During his travels, he gave surfing demonstrations in Australia and California, which helped introduce the sport in those places. Surfing had deep roots in Hawaiian culture, where it was once practised by chiefs and commoners alike. It had declined during the nineteenth century and was revived in the early twentieth. Historians credit Kahanamoku with much of that revival's international reach. He later served as sheriff of Honolulu. A statue of him now stands at Waikiki. In 1941, he was already a celebrated figure in the islands.

[long-pause]

{img:12-pink-hotel-palms-beach-terrace-evening}

The Royal Hawaiian Hotel at Waikiki was known as the Pink Palace of the Pacific. The Royal Hawaiian Hotel opened in 1927 on the beach at Waikiki. It was built in a Spanish and Moorish style, with towers and arches, and painted a distinctive pink. People called it the Pink Palace of the Pacific. Wealthy guests arrived on the Matson liners and often stayed for weeks. Film stars, business leaders and their families were among them. Some brought large trunks of clothing and even their own cars. In the evenings, guests dined on terraces facing the ocean while bands played. The hotel was owned by the same company as the shipping line, which promoted travel to the islands. Even so, the beach itself was used by local residents as well as hotel guests. During the war, the hotel was used by the navy as a rest centre for submarine crews. It reopened to the public afterwards and still operates today. It remains one of the best-known landmarks at Waikiki.

[long-pause]

{img:13-wooden-house-porch-valley-rain-evening}

Most families in Honolulu lived in small wooden houses with corrugated metal roofs and deep porches. Ordinary residents of Honolulu lived very differently from hotel guests. Many families occupied small wooden houses with corrugated metal roofs and wide porches, built in neighbourhoods that climbed the valleys behind the town. Yards were often planted with bananas, papayas and flowers. Rain fell frequently in the valleys, especially in the evenings, carried by the trade winds. Families spent much of their time on their porches, which were cooler than the interiors. Radios were common by 1941 and brought news and music into many homes. Children often walked to school barefoot or in simple sandals. After school, they swam, fished and played outdoors. At weekends, families picnicked at beaches around the island. Oral history projects in Hawaii have recorded many memories of this period from people who grew up in these neighbourhoods. Even so, housing conditions varied widely. Some areas were crowded, and many families had modest incomes. Yet the outdoor life of the islands shaped childhood for almost everyone.

[long-pause]

{img:14-steel-guitar-player-porch-evening}

The Hawaiian steel guitar is said to have been developed by a schoolboy named Joseph Kekuku in the 1880s. Much traditional Hawaiian music features a gliding, sliding sound. It comes from the steel guitar, which is played flat across the lap. Instead of pressing the strings with the fingers, the player slides a smooth metal bar along them. According to the most widely told account, a Hawaiian schoolboy named Joseph Kekuku discovered the technique in the 1880s. He is said to have run a metal object along his guitar strings and liked the sound. He practised for years and later toured the United States and Europe, performing for large audiences. Music historians note that other players may have developed similar techniques around the same time. Even so, Kekuku is widely credited as its pioneer. In the early twentieth century, Hawaiian music became extremely popular on the American mainland. The steel guitar later influenced country music and other styles. By 1941, its sound was closely associated with Hawaii and was heard regularly on island radio.

[long-pause]

{img:15-hawaiian-musicians-ukulele-evening-lanai}

On some nights, a Honolulu radio station broadcast music until dawn to guide aircraft flying from California. Radio played an important role in island life by 1941. Honolulu had two main stations, KGU and KGMB. They broadcast news, music and entertainment throughout the day. Programmes of Hawaiian music, with steel guitars, ukuleles and singing, were popular in the evenings. A radio show called Hawaii Calls, broadcast from Waikiki from 1935, carried Hawaiian music to listeners on the mainland. Radio also served a practical purpose. When aircraft were flying to Hawaii from California, a station sometimes stayed on the air through the night. Pilots used its signal to help guide them to the island. Historians mention that this was one reason a radio station was broadcasting music early on the morning of the seventh of December. In the evenings, many residents also went to the cinema or to dances. Some attended a lu'au, a feast where a whole pig was cooked in an underground oven of heated stones.

[long-pause]

{img:16-battleship-row-ford-island-morning-calm}

The United States Pacific Fleet moved its main base from California to Pearl Harbor in 1940. Until 1940, the main body of the Pacific Fleet was based on the coast of California. In the spring of that year, the fleet went to Hawaii for exercises. President Franklin Roosevelt then decided to keep it there. The aim was to show Japan that the United States was paying close attention to events in Asia. Several senior officers disagreed. The commander at the time, Admiral James Richardson, argued that Pearl Harbor lacked adequate facilities and was too far from supply bases. He was replaced in early 1941. The fleet remained. Fuel, supplies and many workers had to be brought across the ocean by ship. Construction expanded rapidly, with new barracks, housing and workshops. Historians see the decision as part of a wider effort to deter Japan. Even so, the move placed a large part of the American fleet in one harbour, far from the mainland. By late 1941, Pearl Harbor was one of the busiest naval bases in the world.

[long-pause]

{img:17-destroyers-moored-nests-harbour-hills}

At Pearl Harbor, destroyers were often moored side by side in groups called nests. In late 1941, the harbour held many types of warship. Battleships were the largest, each more than six hundred feet long. There were also cruisers, destroyers, submarines, minelayers, supply ships, repair ships and tugboats. A white hospital ship, the Solace, was anchored in the harbour as well. Destroyers were commonly moored together in groups of several ships, tied side by side to a buoy or tender. Sailors called these groups nests. To reach shore from an outer ship, crew members crossed the decks of the others. Submarines were based at their own facility on the eastern side of the harbour. Aircraft carriers usually berthed on the western side of Ford Island, but none were in port on the seventh of December. Navy records list more than ninety vessels present that weekend. Even so, the busy harbour looked calm from the surrounding hills. Photographs taken in 1941 show long grey ships lying still on the water.

[long-pause]

{img:18-ford-island-houses-lawns-palms-seaplanes}

Ford Island, in the middle of Pearl Harbor, held a naval air station and homes for officers' families. Ford Island is a low, flat island about a mile and a half long, in the centre of Pearl Harbor. In 1941, it was the site of a naval air station, with runways, hangars and a control tower. Seaplanes were parked on ramps along the water. The island also had housing for officers and their families, with gardens, lawns and a swimming pool at the officers' club. Children lived there and played in its quiet streets. Along the southeastern shore was a line of large concrete mooring quays. Battleships tied up there, one behind another, and sometimes two side by side. This line became known as Battleship Row. Accounts written later by people who grew up on Ford Island describe it as a pleasant, almost suburban place. Even so, it was also an active military installation. Today, the island remains part of the naval base. It contains memorials and museums, including the battleship Missouri and an aviation museum.

[long-pause]

{img:19-battleship-arizona-moored-1941-calm}

The battleships at Pearl Harbor were named after American states, and the Arizona was launched in 1915. American battleships were traditionally named after states. On the morning of the seventh of December, Battleship Row held the California, the Maryland, the Oklahoma, the Tennessee, the West Virginia, the Arizona and the Nevada. The Pennsylvania, the fleet's flagship, was in dry dock across the channel. The Arizona had been built at the New York Navy Yard and launched in 1915. She was six hundred and eight feet long and carried twelve fourteen-inch guns in four turrets. Her crew numbered about fifteen hundred men. She had been modernised in the early 1930s, with new engines and a tripod mast. Sailors often spoke of their ships with strong loyalty, much as people speak of their home towns. Letters and memoirs from crew members reflect this pride. Even so, the Arizona was one of the older battleships in the fleet. Today, her sunken hull lies in the harbour beneath the white memorial that bears her name.

[long-pause]

{img:20-navy-yard-workers-cranes-dry-dock}

The Pearl Harbor Navy Yard operated like a small town, with workshops, dry docks and its own services. Across the channel from Ford Island lay the Navy Yard. It contained workshops, warehouses, offices, power plants and dry docks, where ships could be lifted out of the water for repair. Large cranes stood along the quays. The yard also had a hospital, a fire station, a post office, a cinema and recreation buildings. Thousands of people worked there in 1941, many of them civilians from Hawaii. They included welders, machinists, carpenters, electricians and clerks. Employment records show that the workforce grew rapidly in the months before the war. Work went on through the day to the sound of hammers and riveting. At the end of the shift, workers left by bus and ferry for homes around Honolulu and the surrounding districts. After the attack, the yard's workers played a major role in repairing and salvaging damaged ships. Historians often highlight their contribution. The yard remains an active naval shipyard today.

[long-pause]

{img:21-army-barracks-green-valley-mountains}

Schofield Barracks, in central Oahu, was the largest army post in the United States in 1941. Pearl Harbor was only one of many military installations on Oahu. Hickam Field, an army air base, lay just to the southeast of the harbour. Wheeler Field, in the centre of the island, was home to army fighter aircraft. Next to it was Schofield Barracks, then the largest army post in the United States. It housed thousands of soldiers in long rows of barracks with lawns and shade trees. On the windward side, at Kaneohe Bay, the navy operated a station for patrol flying boats. In total, more than forty thousand soldiers, sailors and airmen were stationed on Oahu in 1941. At weekends, many of them came into Honolulu. Letters written home by servicemen often described Hawaii as a pleasant posting. Even so, military life involved regular training, inspections and long hours. Schofield Barracks is still an active army post today. It was also the setting for the novel From Here to Eternity, published in 1951.

[long-pause]

{img:22-boy-bicycle-palm-road-battleships-behind}

Children growing up on Ford Island learned to recognise the battleships by the shapes of their masts. For children living on Ford Island, the naval base was an ordinary part of daily life. They rode bicycles along roads lined with palm trees, swam in the officers' club pool and fished from the sea walls. Many learned to identify each battleship by its masts and silhouette. Every morning, they heard ships' bands playing as flags were raised, and in the evening they heard bugles across the water. To reach Honolulu, they took a small ferry across the channel and then a bus. Several people who were children on Ford Island in 1941 later wrote memoirs or gave interviews to oral history projects. They often described those years as a happy time. Some returned decades later and pointed out where each ship had been moored. Even so, their memories also include the events of the seventh of December. Their accounts offer a rare view of the base as a family community, not only as a military installation.

[long-pause]

{img:23-admiral-office-window-harbour-view}

In 1941, Admiral Husband Kimmel commanded the Pacific Fleet, and General Walter Short commanded the army in Hawaii. Admiral Husband Kimmel took command of the United States Pacific Fleet in February 1941. He was fifty-eight years old, from Kentucky, and had spent his entire career in the navy. Colleagues described him as serious, hard-working and demanding. His headquarters overlooked the harbour. General Walter Short commanded the army's Hawaiian Department, which was responsible for defending the islands and the base. The two men were on friendly terms and sometimes played golf together on Sunday mornings. Like most American officials, they expected that if war with Japan came, it would begin far to the west, perhaps in the Philippines or Southeast Asia. After the attack, both men were relieved of their commands. Several official inquiries examined their decisions. Historians continue to debate how much responsibility they bore, given the information available to them. In 1999, the United States Senate passed a resolution suggesting that they had been treated unfairly, though no official change was made.

[long-pause]

{img:24-harbour-sunset-ships-lights-reflections}

In the evenings, Pearl Harbor was usually calm, with the lights of the ships reflected in the water. At sunset, the sun went down behind the Waianae Mountains to the west of Pearl Harbor. The sky turned orange and pink, and the calm water reflected the changing colours. As darkness fell, lights came on aboard the ships and along the shore. Sailors who were off duty often sat on deck in the cool evening air. Music from radios drifted across the water. Small boats carried officers and men to and from the landings. Frogs and insects called from the shore. Many sailors later described these evenings as among the most peaceful moments of their time in Hawaii. Letters home from the autumn of 1941 often mention the beauty of the harbour at night. Even so, the fleet maintained watches and duty crews at all times. Training continued during the week, and tension with Japan was growing. Yet in the evening light, the harbour looked quiet and settled, much as it had for months.

[long-pause]

{img:25-reveille-sailors-waking-bunks-ship}

A battleship's day began at about half past five in the morning with reveille. Life aboard an American battleship in 1941 followed a strict daily routine. Shortly after half past five, a bugle sounded reveille over the ship's loudspeakers. A traditional call followed, ordering the men to get out of their bunks and stow them away. Hundreds of sailors rose at once in long, crowded sleeping compartments below decks. The compartments were warm and stuffy, even at night, and filled with the hum of machinery. Men slept in canvas bunks stacked three or four high, with little space between them. Each sailor had a small locker for uniforms, shaving gear and letters from home. Navy regulations of the period set out the daily schedule in detail, from reveille to taps. Memoirs written by former sailors often describe the noise and closeness of these spaces. Even so, many remembered the routine with some affection. The fixed schedule gave shape to life at sea and in port. For young recruits, it was often their first experience of such discipline.

[long-pause]

{img:26-mess-deck-long-tables-breakfast-sailors}

A battleship's cooks started work around three in the morning to feed more than a thousand men. Feeding a battleship's crew was a large operation. Cooks and bakers began work in the galley in the early hours, baking bread in large ovens and frying bacon in enormous pans. After washing and shaving, the men put on working uniforms of blue denim trousers and light blue shirts. They ate at long tables on the mess decks, which folded away when not in use. Breakfast was plentiful, often including eggs, bacon, sausages, potatoes, pancakes, toast and strong coffee. Navy menus from the period show that sailors received three substantial meals each day. Many recruits had grown up during the Depression, when food at home was often scarce. In letters and interviews, some later said they had never eaten so well. Even so, the work of the cooks was relentless. A ship with more than a thousand men aboard had to be fed three times a day, every day, at sea or in harbour.

[long-pause]

{img:27-sailors-scrubbing-deck-holystones-sun}

Sailors scrubbed the wooden decks every morning with blocks of sandstone called holystones. After breakfast, the working day began. Battleships had wooden planks laid over their steel decks, and these were cleaned every morning. Sailors scrubbed them with salt water and blocks of sandstone known as holystones. One explanation for the name is that men used them while kneeling, as if in prayer. The scrubbing continued until the planks were pale and smooth. Brass fittings were polished, and paintwork was scraped and repainted to protect the steel from salt air. Drills took up much of the day as well. Crews practised loading guns with dummy shells, fighting fires and abandoning ship. They repeated these exercises until the actions became automatic. Navy training manuals of the period emphasised constant practice. Many former sailors remembered the work as dull and repetitive. Even so, it served a clear purpose. A well-drilled crew could act quickly and correctly under pressure, without having to stop and think.

[long-pause]

{img:28-ship-barber-tailor-small-workshop}

A battleship carried its own barbers, tailors, bakers, doctors and dentist. Each man aboard a battleship had a specific role. There were gunners, signalmen, radiomen and electricians. Deep in the hull, firemen and machinists tended the boilers and engines in great heat and noise. The crew also included cooks, bakers, barbers, tailors and laundrymen, who washed white uniforms in large machines. Storekeepers managed supplies of food, clothing and equipment. A doctor, a dentist and a chaplain served the crew. Ships often had a small store selling sweets, soap and cigarettes, as well as a library and a print shop that produced a ship's newspaper. Historians often describe a battleship of this era as a floating town. It was designed to be largely self-sufficient during long periods at sea. Crew lists show how varied the skills on board were. For many young men from small farms or towns, it was the most complex community they had ever lived in. Each job helped keep the whole ship running.

[long-pause]

{img:29-ship-band-practising-deck-instruments}

Each battleship had its own band, and the Arizona's band had trained together at the Navy School of Music. Battleships carried bands that played at ceremonies, concerts and dances. The band of the Arizona, officially Navy Band Unit 22, had twenty-one members. Most were in their early twenties. They had trained together at the Navy School of Music in Washington, D.C., and were assigned to the Arizona as a unit in 1941. The band played each morning when the flag was raised. It also performed at official events and entertained the crew. In the evenings, members often played swing and jazz, the popular dance music of the time. They practised on the ship's stern, with the harbour around them. Records and family accounts describe a close-knit and talented group. All twenty-one members of the band died on the seventh of December. Historians note that it was the only American military band to lose all its members in a single event. Today, their names are remembered at the Arizona Memorial and in navy music traditions.

[long-pause]

{img:30-church-service-deck-sailors-folding-chairs}

On Sundays, chaplains held church services in the open air on the decks of the battleships. Sunday aboard a battleship was quieter than other days. Apart from essential duties, there was no routine work. In the morning, the ship's chaplain often led a service on deck. A small portable altar was set up, and sailors sat on benches and folding chairs. Hymns were sung in the open air, sometimes accompanied by the band. After the service came a large Sunday meal. For the rest of the day, men were free to rest, read, write letters or go ashore if they had liberty. Many simply sat on deck in the shade and talked. Photographs from Pearl Harbor in 1941 show services held in this way. Navy chaplains served sailors of many denominations and also offered personal support. Memoirs mention the calm of these Sunday mornings. Even so, ships always kept a duty crew on board. On the morning of the seventh of December, preparations for such services were underway on several ships.

[long-pause]

{img:31-young-sailor-writing-letter-bunk-lamp}

Many brothers served together aboard the Arizona, and there was also a father serving with his son. Most sailors in the Pacific Fleet were very young. Many had enlisted at seventeen or eighteen, straight from high school. They came from farms in the Midwest, mining towns in Pennsylvania and West Virginia, and cities across the country. During the Depression, the navy offered steady pay, food and training. Families often encouraged brothers to serve on the same ship, so they could look after each other. Records show that thirty-seven pairs or trios of brothers were serving aboard the Arizona in December 1941. There was also a father and son, Thomas Free and his son William. Thomas was a machinist's mate, and William was a seaman. Both died on the seventh of December. After the war, the navy discouraged brothers from serving together on the same ship. That policy is often linked to the loss of the five Sullivan brothers in 1942. The Arizona's family connections remain one of the most moving parts of its history.

[long-pause]

{img:32-sailors-relaxing-deck-shade-afternoon}

In the afternoons, off-duty sailors read, played cards, and sometimes fished over the side of their ships. When the day's work was finished, sailors had time to themselves. Many wrote letters, read magazines or listened to the radio. Others played cards or checkers, or polished their shoes. Some rested in the shade of the big gun turrets. Fishing from the side of the ship with a simple line was a common pastime in harbour. Games of catch with a baseball took place on open stretches of deck. Some ships also had unofficial pets, such as cats that slept near warm machinery, despite rules against animals. Photographs and personal snapshots from 1941 show many of these relaxed moments. Former sailors often described the afternoons in Pearl Harbor as easy and pleasant. Even so, the routine could become monotonous. Many young men felt homesick, especially as the holidays approached. The balance of hard work in the morning and quieter afternoons was typical of life aboard a warship in port during peacetime.

[long-pause]

{img:33-movie-screen-deck-sailors-stars}

In the evenings, sailors watched Hollywood films projected on screens set up on deck. Films were a popular form of entertainment aboard ships in port. In the evenings, a large white screen was set up on deck, and men sat in rows to watch the latest releases from Hollywood. The navy distributed films to ships through a regular exchange system. Showings took place in the open air, with the harbour and the stars around the audience. Sailors' memoirs mention screens rippling in the wind and loud reactions from the crowd. At ten o'clock, the bugle sounded taps, the signal for lights out. Men returned to their bunks, and the ship grew quiet except for the hum of machinery and the steps of the watch. The officer of the deck remained on duty through the night. Even so, life in harbour was not entirely relaxed. Duty sections rotated, and some men always had to remain alert. Yet for most sailors, the evening film was a familiar and welcome end to the day.

[long-pause]

{img:34-texas-farm-family-1930s-porch}

Doris Miller, known as Dorie, grew up on a farm near Waco, Texas, the third of four sons. Doris Miller was born on the twelfth of October, 1919, near Waco, Texas. He was the third of four sons of Conery and Henrietta Miller, a Black farming family. They grew cotton and vegetables and raised livestock on a small farm. Miller worked in the fields with his brothers from a young age. At school, he played football and was known for his size and strength. Family members and neighbours later described him as kind and helpful at home, and as a regular churchgoer. By adulthood, he was more than six feet tall and powerfully built. Historians have relied on family interviews and local records to reconstruct his early life, since few documents from his childhood survive. Even so, the outline is clear. He came from a modest rural background, typical of many young men who joined the military during these years. His later fame made his family and hometown proud.

[long-pause]

{img:35-young-man-recruiting-office-1939}

When Dorie Miller enlisted in 1939, Black sailors could only serve in the navy's messman branch. In September 1939, Miller enlisted in the United States Navy at a recruiting office in Dallas. He was nineteen. The navy offered steady wages, training and a chance to travel, at a time when jobs in Texas were scarce. However, the navy was racially segregated. Black sailors were not permitted to serve as gunners, radiomen, machinists or officers. They could join only the messman branch, which meant cooking, serving meals to officers, cleaning cabins and handling laundry. This policy had been in place since the end of the First World War. Civil rights organisations criticised it strongly, and the Black press reported on it often. Even so, many young Black men enlisted, seeking work and a chance to serve. Historians note that the restrictions began to change during the war, partly because of public pressure. In 1942, the navy began accepting Black recruits for other roles. Full integration came later, after a presidential order in 1948.

[long-pause]

{img:36-mess-attendant-white-jacket-wardroom}

As a mess attendant, Miller served the officers of the battleship West Virginia. After training at the Naval Training Station in Norfolk, Virginia, Miller was assigned in early 1940 to the battleship West Virginia. He worked as a mess attendant in the officers' wardroom and quarters. His duties included serving meals, setting tables, making beds and collecting laundry. He wore a white jacket while on duty. Accounts from officers who served with him describe him as reliable and good-natured. His closest friends aboard were other mess attendants, young Black men from Southern and Eastern states. They worked, ate and slept together in a separate compartment. Historians note that this segregation shaped every part of daily life for Black sailors. Even so, many formed strong friendships and supported one another. Navy records show that Miller was promoted to Mess Attendant Second Class during his service. His routine was quiet and largely unnoticed until the morning of the seventh of December, 1941.

[long-pause]

{img:37-shipboard-boxing-ring-sailors-watching}

Dorie Miller was the heavyweight boxing champion of the battleship West Virginia. Boxing was popular in the navy in the 1930s and 1940s. Ships organised their own teams and held matches on deck, with large crowds of sailors watching from all around. Miller became the heavyweight boxing champion of the West Virginia. Contemporary accounts describe him as strong, quick and difficult to beat. His success made him well known throughout the ship. Outside the ring, shipmates described him as friendly and easy-going. He enjoyed cards, music and conversation. He sent part of his pay home to his parents each month, as many young sailors did. Biographers have drawn on interviews with shipmates and family members to build this picture. Even so, relatively few written records describe his daily life in detail. His boxing title is one of the clearest documented facts about his time aboard. It shows that he was respected by many of his shipmates well before the events that made him famous.

[long-pause]

{img:38-battleship-wake-open-ocean-dolphins}

Serving on the West Virginia gave Dorie Miller his first experience of the open ocean. In 1940, the West Virginia took part in fleet exercises in the Pacific and visited several ports. For Miller, it was the first time he had been at sea. The open Pacific was vast, with flying fish, dolphins and nights full of stars far from any city lights. Many sailors from inland states described their first ocean voyages with amazement in letters home. Biographers report that Miller wrote to his family about the size of the sea. In Pearl Harbor, he often went ashore with friends, visiting beaches, cinemas and restaurants in Honolulu. Historians point out that Black sailors also faced segregation and prejudice in some places ashore. Even so, many found Hawaii's mixed population more welcoming than the segregated South. For young men from rural Texas, Louisiana or Georgia, the islands were a very different world. Life in the fleet broadened their experience in ways that few of them could have imagined at home.

[long-pause]

{img:39-sailor-rail-night-harbour-lights-thinking}

Like many young sailors, Dorie Miller hoped one day to return home and farm. Family members later said that Miller planned to complete his service and return to Texas. He hoped to help his parents and perhaps one day own land of his own. He saved part of his pay toward that future. Such plans were common among sailors from farming families. Letters from servicemen in 1941 often mention land, homes and families as their hopes after their service. In the evenings, after the officers' meals were cleared away, mess attendants sometimes had time to go on deck. From there, the lights of the other ships and the shore were visible across the dark harbour. Historians stress that most men in the fleet expected their service to remain peaceful. War seemed possible, but distant. Even so, events were moving quickly elsewhere. Miller's plans, like those of many others, were changed by the attack. He continued to serve in the navy after the seventh of December, until his death in 1943.

[long-pause]

{img:40-liberty-launch-sailors-going-ashore}

When sailors had time off at Pearl Harbor, they went on liberty, travelling ashore in small launches. Time off for sailors was known as liberty. At weekends, small motor launches carried crowds of men from the battleships to landings around the harbour. Sailors wore clean white uniforms for liberty, and officers inspected them before they left the ship. A man with a stained uniform or a dull button could be sent back to change. From the landings, sailors took buses or taxis into Honolulu, about eight miles away, along roads through sugar cane fields. Some had saved their pay for weeks. Others had very little money. Navy regulations limited how long men could stay ashore and when they had to return. Most liberty ended at midnight. Historians note that the large number of servicemen in Hawaii in 1941 strongly affected Honolulu's economy. Shops, restaurants and entertainment businesses depended on their spending. Even so, relations between servicemen and local residents were sometimes tense. Liberty was nevertheless the highlight of many sailors' weeks.

[long-pause]

{img:41-hotel-street-1941-neon-sailors-evening}

Hotel Street in downtown Honolulu was a favourite destination for sailors and soldiers on liberty. Hotel Street ran through the older part of downtown Honolulu. In 1941, it was lined with cafés, cheap restaurants, shooting galleries, souvenir shops, photograph studios and tattoo parlours. It was crowded with servicemen day and night. Photograph studios offered novelty pictures, such as posing beside a hula dancer or sitting in a painted aeroplane cut-out. These photographs were popular as souvenirs to send home. Tattoo artists worked on anchors, ships and names. Restaurants served hamburgers, chili and pie. The Black Cat Café, across the street from the YMCA, was a well-known meeting place. Historians of wartime Honolulu describe Hotel Street as noisy and lively. It also had a rougher side, with bars and other businesses that worried military authorities. Even so, for many young men, it offered simple entertainment at prices they could afford. The street changed greatly after the war, though some old buildings from the period still stand.

[long-pause]

{img:42-sailors-bicycles-quiet-beach-palms}

Some sailors spent their liberty cycling around Oahu or visiting its quieter beaches. Not every sailor spent liberty in downtown Honolulu. Many went to Waikiki to swim, sunbathe and try surfing. Some rented bicycles and rode out across the island, past pineapple fields and plantation villages, to quiet beaches on the far side. The YMCA in Honolulu offered a swimming pool, a reading room, an inexpensive cafeteria and simple rooms for overnight stays. Other sailors visited churches, the library or the Honolulu Zoo. A popular excursion was the trip to the Nuuanu Pali, a mountain pass with dramatic cliffs and wide views over the windward coast. The wind there was famously strong. Letters home from sailors often describe these outings with enthusiasm. Historians use such letters to show the variety of ways servicemen experienced the islands. Even so, transport and money limited where many men could go. Still, for those who explored, Oahu offered landscapes very different from anything they had known on the mainland.

[long-pause]

{img:43-sailor-writing-postcard-park-bench}

In December 1941, sailors sent Hawaiian souvenirs home as Christmas presents. Letter writing was a major part of military life in 1941. Sailors wrote to parents, siblings, wives and girlfriends, describing the islands, their ships and their friends. Many asked for news from home about family, pets and local sports teams. As December began, letters increasingly mentioned Christmas. Sailors bought presents in Honolulu shops, such as grass skirts, ukuleles and printed silk scarves, and mailed them to the mainland. Parcels from home arrived in return, often containing fruitcake, cookies and knitted socks. Mail had to travel by ship, so delays of several weeks were normal. Museums and archives now hold many letters written by servicemen at Pearl Harbor in late 1941. Some were mailed only days before the attack. Families later treasured them. Historians value them as records of everyday thoughts and plans. Even so, most of these letters are simple and cheerful. They show young men preparing for an ordinary Christmas far from home.

[long-pause]

{img:44-navy-family-small-house-garden-evening}

Some married sailors and officers lived in Hawaii with their families. Most young sailors were unmarried, but many older enlisted men and officers had families with them in Hawaii. They lived in rented houses and apartments in Honolulu and nearby towns, or in navy housing on Ford Island and at the Navy Yard. When they were off duty, they returned home in the evening. At weekends, families went to beaches, parks and on drives around the island. Interviews with people who were children in Hawaii at the time often describe a relaxed and pleasant family life. Housing was sometimes scarce and expensive, however, because the military population had grown so quickly. Navy records show a large rise in the number of dependents in Hawaii during 1940 and 1941. After the attack, many families were evacuated to the mainland in the following months. Historians note how suddenly their lives changed. Before December, many families considered their posting to Hawaii one of the most enjoyable of their military careers.

[long-pause]

{img:45-big-band-dance-hall-sailors-couples}

In late 1941, bands from the fleet's ships competed in a contest called the Battle of Music. Dances were held every weekend in Honolulu's clubs, hotels and dance halls. Big bands played swing music, and couples danced the jitterbug and the foxtrot. In the autumn of 1941, the navy organised a series of contests between ships' bands, known as the Battle of Music. Competitions took place at Bloch Arena, a large new recreation hall at the Navy Yard. Each band played a set, and the audience judged the performances. Sailors cheered loudly for their own ships. Semi-final rounds were held through the autumn, and the final was planned for the twentieth of December. Bands from several battleships took part, including the band of the Arizona. Accounts from surviving musicians describe the strong but friendly rivalry. The final was never held. After the attack, the trophy was later named in honour of the Arizona's band, all of whose members had died. The contest remains a poignant detail of the last weeks of peace.

[long-pause]

{img:46-family-radio-news-living-room-1941}

By late 1941, war had been underway in Europe for more than two years and in China for four. In the autumn of 1941, much of the world was at war. In Europe, fighting had begun in September 1939. Germany had conquered much of the continent and in June 1941 had invaded the Soviet Union. Britain continued to resist, under attack from the air and at sea. In Asia, Japan had been at war with China since 1937, occupying much of its coast and many major cities. In 1940 and 1941, Japanese forces also moved into French Indochina, the region of present-day Vietnam, Laos and Cambodia. The United States was not yet at war. Public opinion polls of the period show that most Americans wanted to stay out of the fighting. Many remembered the losses of the First World War. Even so, the United States was supplying Britain and other countries with weapons and materials. In Hawaii, families followed the news on the radio. The fighting still seemed far away from the islands.

[long-pause]

{img:47-harbour-oil-tanks-hills-afternoon}

In 1941, Japan depended heavily on imported oil, much of it from the United States. Japan had few natural resources of its own. Its industry and armed forces relied on imported oil, steel and rubber. Much of its oil had come from the United States. In July 1941, after Japanese forces moved into southern French Indochina, the American government froze Japanese assets. This effectively cut off oil supplies. Britain and the Dutch government in exile took similar measures. Japanese leaders calculated that their oil reserves would last only a limited time. They faced a choice between withdrawing from China and Indochina, as the United States demanded, or seizing oil-rich territories in Southeast Asia, such as the Dutch East Indies. Diplomats from both countries continued negotiations in Washington through the autumn. Historians have studied these talks in great detail. Neither side was willing to accept the other's main demands. Even so, some officials on both sides hoped until the last weeks that war could be avoided.

[long-pause]

{img:48-admiral-study-calligraphy-quiet-evening}

Admiral Isoroku Yamamoto, who planned the attack, had studied in the United States and opposed war with it. Admiral Isoroku Yamamoto commanded Japan's Combined Fleet in 1941. He was born in 1884, making him fifty-seven at the time. As a young officer, he studied at Harvard University from 1919 to 1921. Later, he served as naval attaché at the Japanese embassy in Washington. He travelled widely in the United States and was well aware of its industrial strength. He was known for his skill at card games and the board game shogi. Yamamoto argued against war with the United States, warning that Japan could not win a long conflict. Historians often quote his reported remark that he could run wild for six months, but had no confidence after that. Once war became the policy of the government, however, he planned for it. He believed a surprise attack on the Pacific Fleet at Pearl Harbor offered the best chance of success. His plan was approved by the Japanese Naval General Staff in the autumn of 1941.

[long-pause]

{img:49-northern-bay-snow-ships-anchored-fog}

The Japanese strike force assembled secretly in a remote bay in the Kuril Islands. In November 1941, a Japanese naval force gathered in Hitokappu Bay, also known as Tankan Bay, on the island of Etorofu in the Kuril Islands. The location was remote, cold and often covered in fog. The force included six aircraft carriers, carrying more than four hundred aircraft, along with battleships, cruisers, destroyers, submarines and tankers. On the twenty-sixth of November, the fleet departed and sailed east across the North Pacific. It followed a northern route, far from normal shipping lanes, where few other vessels were likely to see it. The ships maintained strict radio silence and showed no lights at night. Refuelling at sea in rough weather was difficult but successful. Historians describe the voyage as a remarkable feat of planning and seamanship. American intelligence did not detect the fleet's departure. Even so, Japanese commanders were prepared to turn back if the force was discovered or if negotiations in Washington succeeded.

[long-pause]

{img:50-young-pilots-ship-quarters-letters-lamp}

Many of the Japanese airmen in the attack were young men, close in age to the American sailors at Pearl Harbor. During the voyage east, Japanese pilots and aircrew studied maps and models of Pearl Harbor. They reviewed their targets and checked their aircraft. Many also wrote letters to their families, knowing they might not return. Many of the airmen were in their twenties, close in age to the American sailors in the harbour. Diaries and letters from Japanese servicemen of the period mention families, home towns and seasonal images such as cherry blossoms. Some wrote poems, as was common in Japanese military culture. Historians who study these personal documents emphasise the human side of both forces. Most of the young men involved had not chosen the war. They served within systems and decisions made by their governments. Even so, they carried out their orders. Fifty-five Japanese airmen died during the attack. Their letters, like those of American servicemen, are now held in archives and museums and are read by visitors on both sides of the Pacific.

[long-pause]

{img:51-christmas-trees-honolulu-store-palm}

In early December 1941, Honolulu prepared for Christmas, unaware that a Japanese fleet was approaching. American commanders in Hawaii knew that war with Japan was possible. On the twenty-seventh of November, Washington sent a formal war warning to Pacific commanders. However, most officials expected any Japanese attack to fall on the Philippines, Malaya or the Dutch East Indies. Few believed that Japan would strike as far east as Hawaii. In the first week of December, life on Oahu continued normally. Ships came and went, sailors went on liberty and bands practised for the Battle of Music. Shops in Honolulu displayed Christmas decorations. Fresh Christmas trees arrived by ship from the mainland and were sold outside stores. The fleet's aircraft carriers were away from Pearl Harbor that week, delivering planes to Wake and Midway islands. Historians consider their absence one of the most significant facts of the attack. Even so, no one in Hawaii knew it would matter. To residents, it was simply an ordinary early December.

[long-pause]

{img:52-captain-inspection-sailors-rows-deck}

On Saturday, the sixth of December 1941, the battleships held their usual weekly inspections. The sixth of December was a fine, clear day on Oahu, with light trade winds. At Pearl Harbor, Saturday morning brought the regular weekly inspection aboard the battleships. Crews stood in rows on deck in their best white uniforms. Commanding officers walked along the lines, checking uniforms, shoes and haircuts. They then inspected living spaces, sometimes running a white-gloved finger along shelves to look for dust. Navy regulations required such inspections to maintain discipline and cleanliness. After inspection, many men were granted liberty for the rest of the weekend. Ship logs and personal accounts describe the day as calm and routine. Many sailors spent the afternoon ashore. Even so, a portion of every crew remained aboard on duty. Historians often use this Saturday to illustrate how normal life appeared at Pearl Harbor less than a day before the attack. Nothing in the routine suggested that anything unusual was about to happen.

[long-pause]

{img:53-department-store-christmas-tinsel-palms}

In Honolulu, department stores were decorated for Christmas, and Santa Claus received children in the warm sunshine. By early December, Honolulu's shopping streets were busy with Christmas shoppers. Department stores displayed tinsel, paper bells and other decorations, and played carols about snow and sleigh bells. A Santa Claus in a heavy red suit received children in the stores, despite the warm weather. Families bought toys and books and wrapped presents for the holiday. Many had ordered gifts from the mainland months in advance, hoping they would arrive by ship in time. Newspaper advertisements from the first week of December 1941 show sales, gift suggestions and holiday events. Historians use these advertisements to illustrate everyday life on the eve of the war. The contrast between the cold-weather imagery of Christmas and the tropical setting was often noted by visitors. Even so, island families had developed their own customs, mixing mainland traditions with local food and flowers. For most residents, the holiday season of 1941 began in an entirely ordinary way.

[long-pause]

{img:54-football-stadium-crowd-sunny-afternoon}

On the afternoon of the sixth of December, about twenty-four thousand people watched a football game in Honolulu. On Saturday afternoon, the University of Hawaii played Willamette University, from Salem, Oregon, in a game called the Shrine Bowl. It took place at Honolulu Stadium, and about twenty-four thousand spectators attended. The Willamette team had travelled to Hawaii by ship for the occasion. The University of Hawaii won the game. Newspapers the next morning reported on it. The visiting players planned to stay in Hawaii for several more days, and some were due to play another game the following week. After the attack, those plans were cancelled. Several Willamette players and fans helped with defence work and volunteered in the days that followed, before returning home weeks later. Historians and the universities themselves have recorded the story in later years. The game shows how many civilians from the mainland were present in Honolulu that weekend. To the crowd in the stadium, it was simply a sunny afternoon of college football.

[long-pause]

{img:55-waikiki-saturday-families-picnic-sand}

On the last Saturday before the attack, many families spent the afternoon at Waikiki Beach. Waikiki Beach was crowded on the afternoon of the sixth of December. Families had picnics on the sand, and children swam in the calm water inside the reef. Beachboys taught visitors to surf and took them out in outrigger canoes. Off-duty servicemen joined the crowds. As the afternoon went on, the shadows of the palm trees grew longer across the beach. Families packed up and went home as the sun set behind the mountains. Photographs taken that weekend, some now held in museums, show ordinary scenes of leisure on the beach. Many people who were children at the time later recalled it in interviews. They often described it as their last memory of peacetime Hawaii. Historians emphasise that such memories help explain how sudden the change was. Within a day, beaches would be closed and lined with barbed wire. Yet on that Saturday, Waikiki looked as it had for years, relaxed and sunlit.

[long-pause]

{img:56-band-on-stage-arena-sailors-dancing}

On the evening of the sixth of December, a round of the Battle of Music was held at Bloch Arena. On Saturday evening, sailors crowded into Bloch Arena at the Navy Yard for another round of the Battle of Music. Several ships' bands competed, and the audience danced and cheered. The band of the Pennsylvania won that evening's competition, according to accounts from the period. The Arizona's band had already qualified in an earlier round. When the contest ended, those present reportedly sang God Bless America together, a popular song at the time. Many of the musicians returned to their ships for the night. The final competition was scheduled for the twentieth of December. It never took place. Survivors later spoke about the evening in oral history interviews. They remembered the music, the dancing and the friendly rivalry between ships. Even so, for many of those present, it was the last evening of their lives. Historians often mention the event because it captures the atmosphere at Pearl Harbor only hours before the attack.

[long-pause]

{img:57-moonlit-harbour-battleships-silhouettes}

On the night of the sixth of December, the harbour was calm under a bright moon. The night of the sixth of December was clear over Oahu. The moon, a few days past full, lit the harbour. The battleships lay in their usual places along Battleship Row, their outlines visible against the water. Most of the crews had returned from liberty by midnight. Watchstanders remained on duty while the rest of the men slept. The Navy Yard was quiet, apart from the normal sounds of a large base at night. Hundreds of miles to the north, the Japanese carrier force was steaming south toward its launch position. Historians reconstructing the events note that the attack force was then about two hundred and thirty miles from Oahu by early morning. In Honolulu, most residents were asleep. Some nightclubs and dance halls stayed open late. Survivors who remembered that night often described it as peaceful and beautiful. The harbour showed no sign of what was about to happen.

[long-pause]

{img:58-sunday-dawn-oahu-mountains-clouds-pink}

Sunday, the seventh of December 1941, dawned calm and clear over Oahu. Sunrise on the seventh of December came a little before seven o'clock. The weather was mild, with scattered clouds over the mountains and light winds. Most military personnel were not on duty, since it was Sunday. Many slept late after Saturday night. On the ships, the duty sections were preparing for the morning routine. In Honolulu and the surrounding towns, families prepared breakfast and got ready for church. Radio stations played music. Weather records and many personal accounts describe an unusually pleasant morning. Survivors later remembered details such as the softness of the light and the smell of breakfast. Historians reconstructing the morning use logs, reports and testimony to establish the times of events. By then, the first wave of Japanese aircraft had already taken off from the carriers to the north, at about six o'clock. Even so, nobody on Oahu knew it. For a short time longer, the morning remained entirely ordinary.

[long-pause]

{img:59-small-destroyer-patrol-calm-sea-dawn}

Early on the seventh of December, the destroyer Ward fired on a small submarine near the harbour entrance. The USS Ward was an old destroyer patrolling the entrance to Pearl Harbor. At about a quarter to seven on the morning of the seventh of December, its crew spotted a small submarine trying to follow another ship into the harbour. The Ward opened fire and dropped depth charges. The submarine sank. It was one of five Japanese midget submarines sent to take part in the attack. These were the first American shots of the Pacific War. The Ward's commander reported the action to headquarters. However, the message took time to reach senior officers, and earlier false alarms had made some officials cautious. No general alert followed. In 2002, researchers from the University of Hawaii found the wreck of the midget submarine on the seabed outside the harbour, with a shell hole in its tower. The discovery confirmed the Ward's account. Historians still discuss how this early warning might have changed events if it had been acted on.

[long-pause]

{img:60-radar-station-hilltop-north-shore-dawn}

At the Opana radar station, two young soldiers stayed on duty after their shift to practise with the equipment. The army operated several new mobile radar stations on Oahu in 1941. One was at Opana, on a hill near the northern tip of the island. On the morning of the seventh of December, it was staffed by two privates, Joseph Lockard and George Elliott. Their scheduled watch ended at seven o'clock. Because the truck to take them to breakfast was late, they kept the radar running to give Elliott more practice. At about two minutes past seven, they saw an unusually large signal on the screen. It indicated many aircraft about a hundred and thirty miles to the north. Radar was still very new, and operators were inexperienced. Lockard and Elliott checked their equipment and then telephoned the information centre at Fort Shafter. Historians regard their observation as one of the most important missed warnings of the day. The original Opana radar set is now preserved in a museum.

[long-pause]

{img:61-young-soldiers-radar-screen-hut}

The officer who received the radar report believed the aircraft were American bombers expected from California. The call from Opana reached the information centre at Fort Shafter, where a young officer, Lieutenant Kermit Tyler, was on duty. He was a fighter pilot with little training in the new radar system. A flight of American B-17 bombers was expected that morning from California. Tyler assumed the signal was those aircraft and told the radar operators not to worry about it. The radar operators continued tracking the signal until it was lost near the island. Later investigations concluded that Tyler had acted on the information available to him. The information centre was not yet fully operational, and procedures were still being developed. Historians note that even a warning of thirty or forty minutes might have allowed some preparation. Even so, many defences would still have needed time to be readied. The B-17s did arrive that morning, unarmed and low on fuel, in the middle of the attack. Several were damaged while landing.

[long-pause]

{img:62-plantation-village-church-bell-sunday}

Across Oahu, Sunday morning began with church bells and families preparing for the day. In towns and plantation villages across Oahu, the seventh of December started like any Sunday. Church bells rang for morning services. Families ironed clothes, prepared breakfast and listened to the radio. Children played outside. In Honolulu, newspapers were delivered and shops were mostly closed. Many military families on the bases were still asleep or having a late breakfast. Oral history projects in Hawaii have collected hundreds of memories of that morning. People often recall precise details, such as what they were eating or doing when they first heard unusual noises. Historians value these accounts because they show how the attack affected civilians as well as servicemen. Most residents of Oahu were not directly in danger, but many saw or heard the attack. Some civilians were killed, mostly by anti-aircraft shells that fell on Honolulu. Before eight o'clock, however, the island was quiet. Daily life seemed settled, and the holidays were approaching.

[long-pause]

{img:63-sailors-on-deck-soft-morning-light-ships}

On the morning of the seventh of December, Dorie Miller was collecting laundry aboard the West Virginia. Aboard the battleship West Virginia, mess attendant Doris Miller rose at about six o'clock, as usual. According to later accounts, he was collecting laundry from officers' quarters when the attack began. Across the harbour, other sailors were at breakfast, preparing for church services or resting on deck. Duty sections were carrying out the normal Sunday routine. The West Virginia was moored outboard of the Tennessee on Battleship Row. Navy records and survivor testimony allow historians to place many individual sailors at particular locations that morning. Miller's own later statements and the citation for his medal describe his actions after the attack began. Even so, details of his exact movements vary between accounts. Before eight o'clock, his morning was like that of hundreds of other mess attendants. The routine tasks of a large ship were underway. Within minutes, the situation aboard every battleship would change completely.

[long-pause]

{img:64-flag-detail-stern-morning-band-ready}

At about a quarter to eight, colour guards on the ships prepared to raise the flags at eight o'clock. Raising the colours, the national flag, was a daily naval ceremony performed at eight o'clock in the morning. On the seventh of December, colour guards and bands assembled on the sterns of many ships shortly before eight. The bands were ready to play the national anthem as the flags rose. On the Nevada, the band and colour guard were in place at the stern. Similar preparations were taking place across the harbour. Navy regulations prescribed the ceremony in detail, and it was carried out every day, in port or at sea. Survivor accounts often mention that they were waiting for the eight o'clock ceremony when the first aircraft appeared. The first bombs fell at about five minutes to eight. Historians use the ceremony as a fixed reference point for the timeline of the attack. It also shows how closely ordinary routine and sudden war met that morning, within a few minutes of each other.

[long-pause]

{img:65-distant-planes-over-green-hills-morning}

When the first aircraft appeared, many people on Oahu thought they were watching a Sunday training exercise. The first wave of Japanese aircraft reached Oahu shortly before eight o'clock. Military exercises with aircraft were common, so many people who saw planes overhead at first assumed they were American. Survivors frequently said they did not realise what was happening until they saw explosions or the red circle markings on the aircraft. At about five minutes to eight, the attack began on airfields and on the ships in the harbour. Aircraft at Hickam Field, Wheeler Field and other bases were attacked so that they could not take off. Within minutes, the harbour was under attack. A message sent from Ford Island, announcing an air raid and stating that it was not a drill, was broadcast soon afterwards. Historians have reconstructed the timeline from logs, photographs and testimony. Even so, accounts of the first moments differ. Confusion was common. Many people struggled to believe that an attack was really taking place on such a peaceful morning.

[long-pause]

{img:66-navy-band-stern-flag-morning-sun}

On the Nevada, the band continued playing the national anthem as the attack began. Aboard the battleship Nevada, the band was on the stern to play the national anthem for the eight o'clock flag ceremony. As the band began to play, Japanese aircraft flew over the ship. According to accounts from crew members, the band continued playing until the anthem was finished before taking cover. The flag was raised as planned. Survivor testimony and later histories of the ship record this event. It has been retold many times as an example of discipline and composure. Historians note that some details, such as exact timing, vary between accounts. Even so, the core of the story is widely accepted. The moment reflected the strength of naval routine and training. Members of the band then took up emergency duties, as bandsmen were trained to do in an emergency. Several were assigned to help with ammunition and damage control during the rest of the attack. The episode remains one of the best-known moments of that morning.

[long-pause]

{img:67-battleship-leaving-channel-flag-flying}

The Nevada was the only battleship to get underway during the attack. The Nevada was moored at the northern end of Battleship Row. Its crew managed to raise steam and get the ship moving during the attack, a process that normally took much longer. With many senior officers ashore, junior officers and enlisted men took charge. The battleship moved down the channel toward the harbour entrance. It came under heavy attack during the second wave. To avoid sinking in the narrow channel and blocking the harbour, the Nevada was deliberately run aground near Hospital Point. Later, it was moved to the other side of the channel. Navy records and survivor accounts describe the effort in detail. Historians regard the Nevada's movement as one of the most notable actions of the day. The ship was repaired and returned to service. In 1944, it supported the landings in Normandy. The Nevada's crew received several awards for their actions on the seventh of December.

[long-pause]

{img:68-quiet-sky-over-harbour-late-morning-haze}

The attack lasted less than two hours and was over by about ten o'clock in the morning. The Japanese attack came in two waves. The first began shortly before eight o'clock, and the second arrived about an hour later. By about ten o'clock, the last aircraft had left, returning to the carriers to the north. The Japanese commanders decided not to launch a third attack. In less than two hours, eight American battleships had been damaged, and several were sunk or capsized. Many other ships and more than three hundred aircraft were destroyed or damaged. A total of two thousand four hundred and three Americans were killed, including sixty-eight civilians. More than one thousand one hundred of the dead were aboard the Arizona. Historians note that important targets, such as fuel storage tanks and repair facilities, were largely untouched. The American aircraft carriers were also at sea. These factors affected the course of the war that followed. On Oahu, the morning ended in smoke and silence, as people began rescue and recovery work.

[long-pause]

{img:69-big-young-sailor-carrying-shipmate-deck}

Dorie Miller, who had no training on anti-aircraft guns, helped carry wounded men and then fired a machine gun. When the West Virginia was hit by torpedoes and bombs, Miller went to help. According to his medal citation and later accounts, he helped carry wounded sailors to safety, including the ship's captain, Mervyn Bennion, who was mortally wounded. Miller was then ordered to help with a machine gun. As a mess attendant, he had never been trained to operate one. He fired the gun at attacking aircraft until he was ordered to leave the ship. The West Virginia sank in shallow water. Miller's actions were first reported without his name. The Black press, especially the Pittsburgh Courier, campaigned to identify him and to see him recognised. In 1942, he was awarded the Navy Cross. Historians note that his story became important to the civil rights movement. It showed both courage and the injustice of the restrictions placed on Black sailors. Even so, Miller himself spoke modestly about what he had done that morning.

[long-pause]

{img:70-sailor-hangar-ramp-kaneohe-bay-morning}

At Kaneohe Bay, Chief Petty Officer John Finn fired a machine gun for more than two hours despite many wounds. The naval air station at Kaneohe Bay, on the windward side of Oahu, was attacked shortly before the main assault on Pearl Harbor. John Finn, a chief aviation ordnanceman, was at home with his wife when the attack began. He drove to the station and set up a machine gun on an exposed position near the seaplane ramps. He fired at attacking aircraft for more than two hours. During this time, he was wounded more than twenty times. After receiving medical treatment, he returned to help rearm aircraft. In 1942, Finn was awarded the Medal of Honor. He was the first American in the Second World War to receive it for actions on the seventh of December. Finn lived to the age of a hundred, dying in 2010. He was the last living Medal of Honor recipient from the attack. In interviews, he insisted that he had simply done his job, as many others had done that day.

[long-pause]

{img:71-navy-nurses-hospital-ward-white-uniforms}

Navy nurse Ruth Erickson later recalled that the wounded men were quiet and brave, and asked first about their friends. On the morning of the seventh of December, Ruth Erickson was a young navy nurse at the Naval Hospital at Pearl Harbor. She was having breakfast when the attack began. Within minutes, wounded men began arriving at the hospital. Nurses, doctors and corpsmen worked without rest throughout the day and night. Many patients had severe burns. Erickson later gave a detailed oral history interview to the Navy. She described the calm and courage of the injured sailors, many of whom asked about their shipmates before themselves. The hospital was quickly overwhelmed, and additional space was used for treatment. Navy nurses from other facilities and civilian volunteers helped. Historians use accounts like Erickson's to describe the medical response to the attack. Even so, the nurses rarely spoke about their own exhaustion. Their work continued for many weeks. Erickson's testimony is now part of the Navy's historical archive and is often quoted in histories of the day.

[long-pause]

{img:72-volunteers-line-blood-bank-honolulu}

After the attack, so many people volunteered to give blood that collection supplies ran short. In the hours after the attack, hospitals across Oahu needed large amounts of blood. Earlier in 1941, doctors in Honolulu had organised a blood bank, which proved vital. Radio announcements asked for donors, and long lines formed outside hospitals and donation centres. People of every background came forward, including many Japanese American residents. According to accounts from the period, there were more volunteers than containers to collect their blood. Additional bottles had to be found and sterilised. Historians of the medical response note how quickly civilians organised to help. Doctors from Honolulu also went to military hospitals to assist. Local newspapers later praised the response. Even so, conditions remained difficult for days, as hospitals cared for many seriously injured patients. The blood donation effort showed the community's support for the wounded. It also demonstrated that people of Japanese ancestry in Hawaii were part of the island's response, a point that became important in the months that followed.

[long-pause]

{img:73-workers-small-boat-ship-hull-tools}

Rescuers cut through the hull of the capsized Oklahoma and freed thirty-two trapped sailors. The battleship Oklahoma was struck by several torpedoes and capsized within minutes. Many crew members were trapped inside the overturned hull. Rescuers on the surface heard tapping from inside. Civilian workers from the Navy Yard, along with sailors, began cutting through the steel bottom of the ship. The work was dangerous and slow. Cutting torches could ignite fuel or consume the air in the compartments. Workers used drills and chisels where possible. Over the following day and night, they rescued thirty-two men. Julio DeCastro, a civilian yard worker, led one of the main rescue teams. His team was later recognised for its efforts. Survivors described seeing daylight through the hole in the hull as one of the most powerful moments of their lives. Historians note that four hundred and twenty-nine men from the Oklahoma died. Even so, the rescues saved lives that would otherwise have been lost.

[long-pause]

{img:74-harbour-small-boats-afternoon-calm-light}

During and after the attack, many small boats, including private craft, helped rescue men from the water. When ships were hit and burning oil spread across the harbour, many sailors were thrown or jumped into the water. Small boats from ships and shore stations went to pick them up. Launches, motor whaleboats and tugboats carried survivors to safety and took the wounded to hospitals. Some privately owned boats and fishing craft also joined the rescue efforts. Survivors later described being pulled from the water by people they never saw again. Navy reports from the following days record many acts of rescue by boat crews. Historians point out that much of this work was done by people with no formal orders, acting on their own initiative. Even so, the burning oil made rescue very dangerous. Many rescuers were later decorated. Others were never formally recognised. Their efforts saved many lives during the attack and in the hours that followed, as smoke continued to cover parts of the harbour.

[long-pause]

{img:75-blackout-house-candle-family-night}

On the night of the seventh of December, a blackout was imposed across Oahu. On the afternoon of the seventh of December, the governor of Hawaii declared martial law, and the army took control of the territory. That night, a complete blackout was ordered across Oahu. All lights had to be turned off or covered. Cars drove without headlights, and windows were blacked out. Many people feared another attack or an invasion. Rumours spread quickly, and nervous sentries fired at shadows. Some American aircraft returning that night were fired on by mistake. With the town and bases dark, the night sky was unusually clear. Many residents later remembered the brightness of the stars. Oral history accounts describe families sitting together in darkened rooms, listening to the radio for news. Historians note that martial law remained in place in Hawaii until October 1944. Blackouts and curfews became part of daily life for many months. The first night set the pattern for this long period of restrictions.

[long-pause]

{img:76-workers-factory-gathered-around-radio}

On the eighth of December, President Roosevelt addressed Congress, and millions listened on the radio. On Monday, the eighth of December, President Franklin Roosevelt spoke to a joint session of Congress. He described the seventh of December as a date which will live in infamy. His speech lasted only a few minutes. It was broadcast nationwide, and historians estimate that it reached the largest radio audience in American history up to that time. Factories, offices and schools paused so that people could listen. Within an hour, Congress declared war on Japan, with only one dissenting vote. Three days later, Germany and Italy declared war on the United States. The country was now fully involved in the Second World War. Historians often describe the attack as a turning point in American public opinion. Before it, many Americans opposed entering the war. Afterwards, enlistment offices were crowded with volunteers. Even so, many families also prepared for long separations. The speech remains one of the most famous in American history.

[long-pause]

{img:77-honolulu-christmas-1941-quiet-blackout}

Christmas 1941 in Hawaii was celebrated quietly, under blackout and martial law. Christmas came less than three weeks after the attack. In Hawaii, the blackout continued, and Christmas lights could not be used outdoors. Families decorated their trees indoors, behind blackout curtains. Many military families had lost relatives or were preparing to leave for the mainland. Parcels and letters from the mainland were delayed by wartime shipping restrictions. Churches held services, though evening gatherings were limited by the curfew. Newspapers carried messages encouraging residents to keep up their spirits. Oral history interviews describe a subdued holiday, with small gatherings and simple meals. Historians note that many residents also helped wounded servicemen and families affected by the attack. Volunteers organised gifts for patients in hospitals. Even so, the mood was serious. Nobody knew how long the war would last. For many families in Hawaii, Christmas 1941 was remembered as a time of uncertainty, but also of quiet kindness within the community.

[long-pause]

{img:78-young-soldiers-hawaii-families-farewell-pier}

Thousands of Japanese Americans from Hawaii later served in the army, many in units with the motto Go for Broke. After the attack, Japanese Americans faced suspicion and discrimination. On the mainland, more than one hundred and ten thousand people of Japanese ancestry were forcibly removed and held in camps. In Hawaii, where they made up more than a third of the population, mass removal was not carried out, though some community leaders were detained. Many young Japanese Americans in Hawaii sought to prove their loyalty. Soldiers of Japanese ancestry already in the Hawaii National Guard formed the 100th Infantry Battalion. In 1943, the army formed the 442nd Regimental Combat Team, and thousands of volunteers from Hawaii joined. Its motto was Go for Broke, a phrase from island gambling slang meaning to risk everything. The 100th and the 442nd fought in Italy and France. Historians describe them as among the most highly decorated American units of their size in the war. Their record later helped change attitudes toward Japanese Americans across the country.

[long-pause]

{img:79-white-house-lawn-christmas-tree-evening}

On Christmas Eve 1941, Winston Churchill joined President Roosevelt at the lighting of the national Christmas tree. British Prime Minister Winston Churchill travelled to Washington in December 1941 to meet President Roosevelt and plan the joint war effort. He stayed at the White House over Christmas. On Christmas Eve, the two leaders appeared together at the lighting of the national Christmas tree on the White House grounds. A large crowd attended. Both men spoke to the crowd and to radio listeners. Churchill addressed the children of America, encouraging them to enjoy their Christmas despite the war. Recordings and transcripts of the speeches survive. Historians often cite the occasion as a symbol of the new alliance between Britain and the United States. After that evening, the tree remained unlit for the rest of the war, because of wartime blackout rules. The joint meeting, known as the Arcadia Conference, produced important decisions about strategy. Churchill also addressed the United States Congress on the twenty-sixth of December.

[long-pause]

{img:80-admiral-nimitz-arriving-flying-boat-harbour}

Admiral Chester Nimitz arrived at Pearl Harbor on Christmas morning 1941 to take command of the Pacific Fleet. After the attack, Admiral Kimmel was relieved of command. President Roosevelt chose Admiral Chester Nimitz, from Texas, to replace him. Nimitz travelled across the country and then flew to Hawaii in a navy flying boat. He arrived at Pearl Harbor on the morning of the twenty-fifth of December, 1941, in the rain. Officers who met him later said he spoke little and took in the damage carefully. He formally took command on the thirty-first of December, in a ceremony aboard a submarine. Nimitz decided to keep most of Kimmel's staff, which helped morale. Historians credit him with steady and effective leadership throughout the war. Under his command, the Pacific Fleet won major victories, including the Battle of Midway in June 1942. On the second of September 1945, Nimitz signed the Japanese surrender document aboard the battleship Missouri. The Missouri is now moored at Pearl Harbor, near the Arizona Memorial.

[long-pause]

{img:81-salvage-divers-harbour-calm-morning}

Navy divers worked for months in dark, oily water to raise and repair the sunken ships. After the attack, the navy began a large salvage operation at Pearl Harbor. Divers worked in heavy canvas suits and brass helmets, supplied with air from the surface. The water in the harbour was dark and covered with oil, so divers often worked by touch. They sealed holes, removed ammunition and recovered bodies. Engineers pumped water out of the damaged ships so that they could be refloated and repaired. The work continued for more than a year. Navy records describe thousands of hours of diving. Several divers lost their lives in accidents during the operation. Historians describe the salvage effort as one of the largest of its kind ever undertaken. Captain Homer Wallin, who led much of the work, later wrote a detailed account of it. The Oklahoma was righted in 1943 but was never returned to service. The Arizona was judged beyond repair and left in place, where it remains today.

[long-pause]

{img:82-grey-battleships-at-sea-sunset-pacific}

Six of the eight battleships damaged at Pearl Harbor were repaired and returned to service. Of the eight battleships damaged in the attack, only the Arizona and the Oklahoma were permanently lost. The other six, the California, the Maryland, the Nevada, the Pennsylvania, the Tennessee and the West Virginia, were repaired. Some needed only minor work and returned to service within weeks. Others, such as the California and the West Virginia, had sunk in shallow water and required major reconstruction. They returned to the fleet in 1944, extensively modernised. Several of these ships took part in later battles. In October 1944, at the Battle of Surigao Strait in the Philippines, five Pearl Harbor survivors were part of the battle line that defeated a Japanese force. Historians often mention this as a striking turn in the story. The ships that had been sunk or damaged at their moorings later fought at sea. The skill of the repair workers at Pearl Harbor and on the mainland made this possible.

[long-pause]

{img:83-medal-ceremony-carrier-deck-1942-sun}

Dorie Miller became the first Black American to receive the Navy Cross. On the twenty-seventh of May, 1942, Admiral Chester Nimitz presented Doris Miller with the Navy Cross aboard the aircraft carrier Enterprise at Pearl Harbor. It was then the navy's third-highest award for bravery. Miller was the first Black American to receive it. Nimitz said that the award was the first of its kind to a member of his race in this conflict and would not be the last. Miller later took part in a war bond tour in the United States. He was then assigned to the escort carrier Liscome Bay. On the twenty-fourth of November, 1943, the ship was sunk by a Japanese submarine near the Gilbert Islands, and Miller was among those lost. In 2020, the navy announced that a future aircraft carrier would be named the USS Doris Miller. Historians note that it will be the first aircraft carrier named after a Black American and the first named after an enlisted sailor for actions in the Second World War.

[long-pause]

{img:84-elderly-woman-telling-story-living-room}

Ruth Erickson, who nursed the wounded at Pearl Harbor, later became director of the Navy Nurse Corps. After the attack, Ruth Erickson continued her career in the Navy Nurse Corps. She served at hospitals in the United States and overseas during and after the war, including during the Korean War. Over the years, she rose through the ranks. From 1962 to 1966, she served as director of the Navy Nurse Corps, the highest position in the corps. In later life, she gave oral history interviews describing her experiences on the seventh of December and in her long career. These recordings are now held by the Navy's history programmes and are widely used by historians and museums. She described the nurses' work with care and modesty. Historians note that the role of women in the medical response to the attack was for many years less widely known. Accounts like hers have helped change that. Erickson died in 2008. Her life spanned the period from the attack to the modern navy, in which women serve in nearly every role.

[long-pause]

{img:85-japanese-farmer-rice-field-evening-1950s}

Mitsuo Fuchida, who led the Japanese air attack, later became a Christian evangelist and visited the United States many times. Commander Mitsuo Fuchida led the first wave of the attack on Pearl Harbor. He survived the war, during which he took part in several major battles. After the surrender, he returned to his home village and worked as a farmer for a time. In 1950, he converted to Christianity, influenced in part by the story of an American former prisoner of war who had become a missionary. Fuchida then travelled widely as an evangelist. He visited the United States many times, speaking about reconciliation and forgiveness. He also met some American veterans of the war. He wrote and contributed to several books about his experiences, though historians have found that some of his accounts contain inaccuracies. Even so, his later life became a well-known story of change. Fuchida died in 1976. His journey from wartime commander to speaker on peace is often mentioned in discussions of how former enemies came to terms with the past.

[long-pause]

{img:86-arizona-memorial-white-calm-water-morning}

The white memorial over the Arizona dips in the middle and rises at both ends, a design chosen deliberately. The USS Arizona Memorial was designed by the architect Alfred Preis and dedicated in 1962. It spans the sunken hull of the battleship without touching it. The structure is white and about one hundred and eighty-four feet long. Its shape sags in the middle and rises at both ends. Preis explained that the design represented initial defeat and ultimate victory. Inside, a shrine room displays the names of the men who died aboard the Arizona. Visitors reach the memorial by boat from the visitor centre on shore. More than a million people visit each year. Since 1982, some Arizona survivors have chosen to have their ashes placed inside the ship, to rest with their shipmates. Historians and architects consider the memorial one of the most significant in the United States. It is managed by the National Park Service. The ship beneath it is still officially a war grave and remains part of the navy.

[long-pause]

{img:87-oil-drops-rainbow-rings-calm-water}

Small amounts of oil still rise from the sunken Arizona every day. The Arizona carried large quantities of fuel oil when it sank in 1941. Small amounts of that oil still escape from the wreck and rise to the surface. Visitors to the memorial can often see drops of oil appearing on the water and spreading into thin, rainbow-coloured films. Some people call them the black tears of the Arizona. The National Park Service and scientists monitor the wreck and the oil leakage. Studies have estimated that a large amount of oil remains inside the hull. Researchers are examining how the steel structure is changing over time, in order to plan for the future. Removing the oil would be extremely difficult without disturbing the ship, which is a war grave. Historians and visitors often describe the rising oil as one of the most moving sights at the memorial. It gives a sense that the ship is still connected to the events of 1941. The drops continue to appear more than eighty years later.

[long-pause]

{img:88-diamond-head-waikiki-evening-surfers}

Every year on the seventh of December, a moment of silence is observed at Pearl Harbor at five minutes to eight. Each year on the seventh of December, a remembrance ceremony is held at Pearl Harbor. It is organised by the National Park Service and the navy. At five minutes to eight, the time the first bombs fell in 1941, a moment of silence is observed. Ships in the harbour may sound their horns, and military aircraft sometimes fly overhead in a missing-man formation. For many years, survivors of the attack attended and were honoured at the ceremony. As their numbers have declined, their families and younger generations have taken their place. The Pearl Harbor National Memorial includes the Arizona Memorial, the Utah Memorial, the Oklahoma Memorial and other sites. Historians note that the attack is remembered not only in the United States but also in Japan, where it is studied as part of the history of the war. Today, Oahu is once again a place of beaches, surfers and quiet evenings. The ceremony keeps the memory of 1941 alive.
