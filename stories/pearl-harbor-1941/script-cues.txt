In 1941, Hawaii wasn't yet a state, and it took most people five days to get there.

[pause]

The island of Oahu rises steeply out of the Pacific. Picture yourself leaning on the rail of a big white ship, with nothing but blue ocean all around you. Long green ridges run down its middle, with their tops hidden in soft white cloud. Rain falls gently on the high slopes, and rainbows come and go over the valleys. Along the shore, waves break white on the coral reefs. Inside the reefs, the water is pale green and very clear. In 1941, Hawaii wasn't yet a state of the United States. It was a territory, a faraway part of the country. It lay more than two thousand miles from California. Most people came by ship, on a voyage of four or five days across the open ocean. A lucky few came by flying boat. These big planes were called Clippers. They landed in the harbour after a flight of nearly twenty hours. About four hundred thousand people lived on the islands. Most of them lived on Oahu. You'd count the days, watching the sea change colour. For many visitors, it was the most beautiful place they had ever seen.

{img:02-aloha-tower-boat-day-crowds-leis}

When a passenger ship arrived in Honolulu, the whole town came down to watch.

[pause]

The arrival of a passenger ship was a great event. You'd hear a band playing on the pier long before the ship was tied up. The big white liners had names like the Lurline and the Matsonia. They came into the harbour in the morning, past the old crater called Diamond Head. Boys swam out to meet them and dived for coins in the clear water. At the pier stood a tall white clock tower, called the Aloha Tower. A crowd waited beside it. A band played Hawaiian music, with guitars and ukuleles and soft singing. There were hula dancers in grass skirts. As the passengers came down the gangway, people placed leis around their necks. These were garlands of fresh flowers, plumeria and ginger and orchids. They smelled sweet and felt cool. People said aloha, which means hello, goodbye and love, all at once. The islanders called this Boat Day. Can you imagine a whole town turning out just to say hello? You'd feel a flower garland slipped over your head as you came ashore. On Boat Day, Honolulu did exactly that.

[long-pause]

{img:03-palm-trees-hibiscus-garden-trade-wind}

On Oahu, it rained while the sun was shining, and nobody minded at all.

[pause]

The air in Hawaii was soft and warm all year round. You'd feel a soft, warm shower on your arms, and look up to see blue sky. It was never very hot and never cold. Almost every day, the trade winds blew gently from the northeast. They came cool and fresh off the ocean. They rustled the palm trees and carried the clouds over the mountains. Short showers came and went, often while the sun was still shining. Afterwards, the air smelled of wet earth and flowers. And there were flowers everywhere. Hibiscus bloomed red and yellow and pink in every garden. Bougainvillea climbed over the walls. Plumeria trees dropped white and yellow blossoms on the paths. Great flame trees glowed scarlet against the green hills. Visitors from cold cities often wrote home about the light. They said it was softer and brighter than anything they knew. Have you ever felt a warm rain on your face, with the sun on your back? Wouldn't you love a shower like that on a hot afternoon? On Oahu, that happened almost every afternoon.

[long-pause]

{img:04-plantation-workers-pineapple-fields-mountains}

More than a third of the people in the islands had families that came from Japan.

[pause]

The people of the islands came from many places. You'd hear many languages on a single walk through town. There were Native Hawaiians, whose ancestors had sailed here in great canoes more than a thousand years earlier. They had found their way across the ocean by the stars. Then, in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, workers came from far away to work on the plantations. They came from Japan, in great numbers. By 1941, more than a third of everyone in the islands had Japanese parents or grandparents. Others came from China, Korea, the Philippines and Portugal. And there were Americans from the mainland, too. There were teachers and shopkeepers, soldiers and sailors and their families. They all lived side by side. Their children went to school together. They ate each other's food. They even spoke a mixture of each other's languages, which the islanders called pidgin. A child might say a word in Hawaiian, a word in Japanese and a word in English, all in one sentence.

[long-pause]

{img:05-taro-terraces-windward-valley-cliffs}

The old Hawaiians divided their island into slices, running from the mountains down to the sea.

[pause]

In the old Hawaiian way of life, the island was divided into long slices of land. You could walk from the cool green mountains all the way down to the sea without leaving one slice. Each slice ran from the top of the mountains all the way down to the sea. Each one was called an ahupua'a. It held everything a community needed. On the high slopes were forests, for wood and for birds' feathers. In the valleys were flooded terraces where taro grew, watered by cool streams. Taro is a plant with a starchy root, a little like a potato. And at the bottom were the shore and the reef, for fishing. The people of each slice shared what they had. They looked after the land and the water, so there would always be enough. In 1941, in some quiet valleys on the windward side, the old terraces were still green and full of water. Families still tended them in the old way. They stood knee-deep in the mud under the cliffs, while light rain drifted down the mountains.

[long-pause]

{img:06-sugar-cane-fields-wind-plantation-houses}

In the plantation camps, the smell of cooking rice drifted across the yards each evening.

[pause]

Much of the island was covered with plantations. You'd hear children laughing between the little wooden houses. On the flat land in the middle grew great fields of sugar cane. The cane was taller than a man, and it swayed in the wind like a green sea. On the red earth of the uplands grew pineapples, in long straight rows. They stretched all the way to the foot of the mountains. The workers went out to the fields early in the morning. They wore wide straw hats, long sleeves and gloves. These kept off the sun and the sharp leaves. At lunchtime, they opened their tin lunch boxes and shared what was inside. There was a little rice, a little fish, a pickle and a piece of Portuguese sausage. So everybody tasted everybody's food. In the evening, they walked home to small wooden houses in the plantation camps. Their families were waiting. Children played in the dusty lanes, and the smell of cooking rice drifted across the yards.

[long-pause]

{img:07-pearl-harbor-lochs-from-hills-morning}

The Hawaiians called the harbour Wai Momi, which means pearl waters.

[pause]

On the southern side of Oahu, a few miles west of Honolulu, there was a great natural harbour. You'd see the water shine like the inside of a shell. You'd see why, if you stood at the water's edge at sunrise. It was shaped a little like a hand with spread fingers. A wide calm inlet of the sea had several long arms of water reaching inland. Low green hills and sugar fields surrounded it. In the middle lay a flat little island. The old Hawaiians called this place Wai Momi. It means pearl waters. Long ago, oysters with pearls inside them were found in its shallow lagoons. Fish were raised there too, in stone-walled ponds along the shore. For many centuries, it was a quiet place of fishermen and farmers. Then, in the twentieth century, the American navy built a great base there. By 1941, the shallow pearl beds were long gone. But the old name lived on, in English. Everyone called it Pearl Harbor. It's strange to think of it. A name so gentle, for a place that would soon be so famous.

[long-pause]

{img:08-honolulu-street-1941-shops-palms}

The ship is tied up, and the music on the pier fades away. Let's take a slow walk into town together. Can you smell the flowers on the warm air?

[pause]

The old palace of the Hawaiian kings stood in the middle of town, among giant banyan trees.

[pause]

Honolulu in 1941 was a pleasant town of about two hundred thousand people. You'd stand in the shade of the banyan trees and look up at its tall windows. Its downtown streets were lined with low buildings of stone and wood. Many had deep shady verandas and balconies. There were banks and offices, department stores, cinemas and restaurants. And in the middle of it all stood the old palace of the Hawaiian kings and queens. It was called Iolani Palace. It was a handsome building with tall columns and wide verandas. Great banyan trees grew around it, with roots hanging down from their branches like curtains. The last Hawaiian queen had lived there, fifty years earlier. Now the government of the territory had its offices inside. Clerks walked up the same grand staircase that royalty had once used. Children climbed the banyan trees outside. The palace is still there today. It's the only royal palace in the whole of the United States. You'd feel how quiet it was, even in the middle of town. Visitors can walk through its quiet rooms and see the royal thrones.

[long-pause]

{img:09-honolulu-sidewalk-aloha-shirts-cotton-dresses}

In Honolulu, men wore shirts printed with flowers, and called them aloha shirts.

[pause]

The streets of Honolulu were busy, but never in a hurry. You'd probably want one yourself. People strolled along the pavements in light, loose clothes. Some men wore white or pale suits. Others wore short-sleeved shirts printed with flowers, palm trees or surfboards. The islanders called them aloha shirts. They had only become popular a few years earlier. Now every tourist wanted one to take home. The women wore cotton dresses in bright colours. Some of them tucked a flower behind one ear. People said that a flower behind the left ear meant you were taken. Behind the right ear meant you were free. There were buses and trolley buses, and old cars with their windows rolled down. On the corners, men sold newspapers and shaved ice with sweet syrup. Nobody seemed to rush. Mainlanders often said the islands ran on their own slow clock. They called it island time. Would you have enjoyed that slower pace? I love that little detail. Most newcomers took about a week to fall in love with it.

[long-pause]

{img:10-fish-market-chinatown-morning-1941}

In Chinatown, the morning market sold fish caught only a few hours before.

[pause]

Every morning, the fish market in Chinatown was crowded. You'd smell fresh fish, ginger and ripe mangoes all at once. People came early to buy the night's catch. There was tuna and mahi-mahi and red snapper, laid out on beds of ice. Fishermen called out prices in several languages. Nearby were little shops full of dried fish and noodles, ginger and rice. There was fruit of every kind. There were mangoes and papayas, bananas and lychees. There were pineapples sold whole, or sliced and served on a stick. Food in Honolulu was a mixture of everything. There was Hawaiian poi, made from pounded taro root. There was laulau, which is pork and fish wrapped in leaves and steamed. There was Japanese miso soup and noodles, and Chinese dumplings. There was Portuguese sweet bread, Korean pickles, Filipino stews, and American hamburgers and apple pie. In the evenings, many families sat down to a bowl of rice with a little of everything on top. You'd leave with a bag of fruit you couldn't even name. It was a whole world, in one bowl.

[long-pause]

{img:11-waikiki-beach-1941-surfers-diamond-head}

The most famous surfer in the world was a gentle Hawaiian with Olympic gold medals.

[pause]

A few miles east of downtown lay Waikiki Beach. If you'd met him on the beach, you'd have found him calm and kind. It was a long curve of golden sand, lined with palm trees. Diamond Head stood at the far end. The waves rolled in slowly from the reef, long and smooth and gentle. They were perfect for swimming and for surfing. On the beach, young Hawaiian beachboys taught the visitors to ride the waves. They used long, heavy wooden surfboards. They also took visitors out in outrigger canoes, beyond the reef. Then they rode a big wave all the way back to the sand, with everyone shouting and laughing. The most famous surfer of them all was Duke Kahanamoku. He was a tall, gentle Hawaiian who had won gold medals for swimming at the Olympic Games. He travelled the world and showed people in Australia and California how to surf. In a way, he gave surfing to the whole world. Yet at home, he was simply Duke, smiling on the beach.

[long-pause]

{img:12-pink-hotel-palms-beach-terrace-evening}

The grandest hotel at Waikiki was painted pink and called the Pink Palace of the Pacific.

[pause]

Along the beach stood a few grand hotels. You'd walk into its cool lobby and hear the waves through the open doors. The most famous was the Royal Hawaiian. Everyone called it the Pink Palace of the Pacific. It was a huge rambling building, painted pale pink. It had towers and arches in a Spanish style. It stood among gardens of palms and flowers, right on the sand. Rich and famous guests came from the mainland and stayed for weeks. There were film stars and businessmen and their families. They arrived with trunks full of clothes, sometimes even their own cars. In the evenings, they dined on the terrace under the stars. A band played soft music, and the waves murmured a few yards away. Yet the beach in front of it belonged to everyone. Children from the town swam there too, and beachboys played their ukuleles on the sand. The grand guests and the local families shared the same warm water. You'd sit on the terrace and watch the surfers until the sun went down. The sea, after all, doesn't care who is rich.

[long-pause]

{img:13-wooden-house-porch-valley-rain-evening}

Most families in Honolulu lived in wooden houses with tin roofs and deep porches.

[pause]

Most people in Honolulu lived much more simply than the hotel guests. You'd sit on the porch in the evening, listening to the rain on the tin roof. They lived in small wooden houses with tin roofs and deep porches. Their neighbourhoods climbed up the green valleys behind the town. Their gardens were full of banana plants, papaya trees and flowers. In the evening, the trade winds often brought a little rain down the valleys. Families sat on their porches and listened to it drumming softly on the roof. The radio played inside. The children walked to school barefoot, or in rubber slippers. After school, they swam at the beach or fished off the piers. They climbed the mango trees in their neighbours' yards. Nobody minded much, as long as they shared the mangoes. At weekends, families went on picnics to beaches on the other side of the island. There, the sand was white and the water very clear. Can you hear it? You'd fall asleep to that soft drumming, I'm sure. Rain on a tin roof, and a radio humming softly in the next room.

[long-pause]

{img:14-steel-guitar-player-porch-evening}

The sliding sound of the steel guitar was invented by a Hawaiian schoolboy.

[pause]

Listen to old Hawaiian music, and you'll hear a soft, gliding sound. You'd hear it drifting from radios all over town. It seems to slide from one note to the next, like a voice sighing. That sound comes from the steel guitar. The guitar is laid flat across the player's lap. Instead of pressing the strings with his fingers, he slides a smooth piece of metal along them. The story goes that a Hawaiian schoolboy named Joseph Kekuku discovered it, in the 1880s. He was walking along a railway line, it's said, with his guitar. He picked up a bolt, ran it along the strings, and liked the sound. He practised for years, until he could play whole songs that way. Later, he travelled the world, playing for audiences in America and Europe. By 1941, the steel guitar could be heard on every radio in the islands. Its sound drifted from porches in the evening, slow and dreamy. Can you hear it now, soft and dreamy? To many people, it was the sound of Hawaii itself.

[long-pause]

{img:15-hawaiian-musicians-ukulele-evening-lanai}

On some nights, a Honolulu radio station played music until dawn, so pilots could find the island.

[pause]

Radio was very important in the islands. There were two radio stations in Honolulu. They broadcast music, news and stories all day long. Many evenings there was a programme of Hawaiian music. Slide guitars and ukuleles played, and soft voices sang old songs about the islands. They sang about the moon over the sea, and about love and parting. And on some nights, something special happened. When planes were flying out from California, one station stayed on the air all night. It played music hour after hour. The pilots tuned in to its signal and followed it across the dark ocean, like a lighthouse made of sound. In the evenings, people also went to the cinema, or to dances. Sometimes they went to a lu'au, a Hawaiian feast. A whole pig was cooked in an underground oven of hot stones. There was poi, fresh pineapple and coconut, and music and hula late into the night.

[long-pause]

{img:16-battleship-row-ford-island-morning-calm}

Out beyond the town, past the cane fields, lies a wide, calm harbour. If you look closely, you'll see grey shapes resting on the water.

[pause]

The great American fleet had only moved to Hawaii the year before.

[pause]

In 1941, Pearl Harbor was the home of the United States Pacific Fleet. You might think the ships had always been there. They hadn't. For many years, the fleet had been based in California, at San Diego and Long Beach. Then, in the spring of 1940, the government in Washington made a decision. It moved the fleet out to Hawaii, far out in the Pacific. It wanted to show that America was watching what was happening in Asia. Not everyone liked the idea. Some admirals thought the fleet was too far from home there. Supplies and fuel all had to come across the ocean by ship. But the fleet stayed. The sailors soon got used to their new home. Many of them thought it was far nicer than California. The harbour filled with grey ships. The roads around it filled with trucks and workers. New barracks, houses and workshops went up almost every week. A quiet bay of fishponds and sugar fields had become one of the busiest naval bases in the world.

[long-pause]

{img:17-destroyers-moored-nests-harbour-hills}

The navy's destroyers were tied up side by side, in little groups called nests.

[pause]

The harbour was full of ships. You'd see them lying together like sleeping whales. There were the great grey battleships, the biggest ships in the navy. Each was more than six hundred feet long, with tall masts like steel towers. There were cruisers and destroyers, long and slim and fast. There were submarines, supply ships, repair ships and tugboats. There was even a hospital ship, painted white. The destroyers were moored side by side, four or five together, in the arms of the harbour. The sailors called these groups nests. To get ashore from the outer ship, you had to walk across the decks of all the others. The submarines lay at their own base, on the eastern side. The aircraft carriers tied up on the western side of the island in the middle, when they were in port. Imagine looking down on all of it from the hills. You'd see a whole city of ships, grey and still on the blue water, with tiny figures moving on their decks.

[long-pause]

{img:18-ford-island-houses-lawns-palms-seaplanes}

In the middle of the harbour lay a flat island, with an air station, houses and swimming pools.

[pause]

The island in the middle of the harbour was called Ford Island. You'd see children cycling along its quiet roads. It was low and flat, about a mile and a half long. It was covered with grass and palm trees. There was a big naval air station on it, with runways, hangars and a control tower. Rows of seaplanes stood on concrete ramps at the water's edge. There were houses for the officers and their families too. They had gardens and lawns, and there was a swimming pool at the officers' club. Children played in the quiet streets. Along the southeastern side of the island, there was a row of big concrete mooring posts in the water. Here, the great battleships tied up, one behind another. Sometimes two lay side by side. People called it Battleship Row. To the families on Ford Island, the battleships were simply part of the view. They were as familiar as the hills, or the palms outside the kitchen window.

[long-pause]

{img:19-battleship-arizona-moored-1941-calm}

The battleships were named after American states, and their crews loved them like home towns.

[pause]

The battleships had the names of American states. If you came from Arizona or Nevada, you'd feel a little proud. At the southern end of the row lay the California. Then came the Maryland and the Oklahoma, side by side. Then the Tennessee and the West Virginia. Then the Arizona, with a small repair ship tied up beside her. At the northern end lay the Nevada. Across the channel, in the big dry dock, lay the Pennsylvania. She was the flagship of the fleet. The Arizona was one of the older battleships. She had been built in New York during the First World War and launched in 1915. She was six hundred and eight feet long. She carried twelve great guns in four turrets. About fifteen hundred men lived and worked aboard her. She had been modernised several times, with new engines and a tall tripod mast. Yet she was still the same proud old ship. Her crew talked about her the way people talk about their home town. Many said she was the finest ship in the fleet.

[long-pause]

{img:20-navy-yard-workers-cranes-dry-dock}

The Navy Yard at Pearl Harbor was like a small town, with its own cinema and post office.

[pause]

Across the water from Ford Island was the Navy Yard. You could buy a stamp, see a film and get a haircut without leaving the yard. It was like a small town of its own. There were workshops and warehouses, offices and power stations. There were dry docks, where ships were lifted out of the water to be repaired. There were giant cranes. There was a hospital, a fire station, a post office and a cinema. There was even a big new hall for sports and concerts. Thousands of people worked there. Some were sailors, and many were civilians from the islands. There were welders, carpenters, machinists and clerks. The noise of hammers and riveting guns went on all day long. The great cranes swung slowly back and forth against the sky. At lunchtime, workers sat in the shade and opened their lunch boxes. At five o'clock, a whistle blew. Then crowds of tired people walked to the buses and the ferries, heading home to supper. You'd soon know your way around like a local. The ships, for the night, were left to the sailors.

[long-pause]

{img:21-army-barracks-green-valley-mountains}

In the middle of the island stood the biggest army post in all of America.

[pause]

Pearl Harbor wasn't the only base on Oahu. Just to the southeast lay Hickam Field, a big army air base. It had long runways and huge hangars. In the middle of the island, on the high plain between the mountains, lay Wheeler Field, for the army's fighter planes. Beside it stood Schofield Barracks. It was the biggest army post in the whole of the United States. Thousands of soldiers lived there, in long rows of pale barracks with lawns and great shade trees. On the far side of the island, at Kaneohe Bay, there was a navy station for big flying boats. Altogether, more than forty thousand soldiers, sailors and airmen lived on Oahu in 1941. On weekends, the streets of Honolulu filled with young men in uniform. The soldiers wore khaki and the sailors wore white. Most of them wrote home that Hawaii was the loveliest posting in the world. Many told their mothers not to worry, because it was like living in paradise.

[long-pause]

{img:22-boy-bicycle-palm-road-battleships-behind}

The children of Ford Island could tell every battleship apart by the shape of its masts.

[pause]

For the children who lived on Ford Island, it was a wonderful place to grow up. You'd hear them calling out the names as the ships came in. They rode their bicycles along the quiet roads under the palm trees. They swam in the pool at the officers' club. They fished off the sea walls with bent pins and bits of bread. They knew the names of all the ships in the harbour. They could tell them apart just by the shapes of their masts. Each morning, they heard the bands on the battleships playing as the flags were raised. Each evening, they heard the bugles sounding across the water. When they wanted to go into Honolulu, they took a little ferry across the channel. Then they caught a bus into town. Many of them remembered those years as the happiest of their childhood. Years later, as grown-ups, some came back to visit. They stood on the same sea walls and pointed out where each ship had been. Could you have told them apart? They still remembered every name.

[long-pause]

{img:23-admiral-office-window-harbour-view}

The admiral and the general played golf together on Sunday mornings, when they could.

[pause]

The commander of the Pacific Fleet was Admiral Husband Kimmel. You can picture them strolling across the green in the early morning light. He was fifty-nine years old, tall and handsome, with white hair. He came from Kentucky and had spent his whole life in the navy. He was serious and careful, and he worked very long days. He expected a great deal of his officers and men. He lived in a house on a hill above the harbour. From his window he could look down on his ships. The commander of the army in Hawaii was General Walter Short. His job was to defend the islands and the base. The two men were friendly, and they played golf together on Sunday mornings, when they could. Kimmel didn't much like parties. He liked to be in bed by ten o'clock. He had held his great command for less than a year. Like most people in Hawaii, he believed any trouble with Japan would come far away, in the western Pacific.

[long-pause]

{img:24-harbour-sunset-ships-lights-reflections}

At sunset, the harbour turned from gold to silver to deep blue.

[pause]

In the evenings, the harbour was peaceful. You'd sit on the shore and watch the colours change, slowly. The sun went down behind the Waianae Mountains, to the west. The sky blazed orange and pink. The calm water turned to gold, then to silver, then to deep blue. Lights came on, on the ships and along the shore. Their reflections lay in long wavering lines across the water. On the decks of the battleships, sailors sat and smoked and talked in the cool air. The sound of a radio drifted across from one of the ships, playing dance music. A launch puttered across the harbour, taking officers ashore. From the shore came the chirping of frogs and crickets, and the scent of flowers. Overhead, the first stars appeared, one by one. Far away, a dog barked and was quiet again. Would you have liked to sit there on the warm deck, watching the lights come on? Many sailors said it was the most peaceful moment of their day.

[long-pause]

{img:25-reveille-sailors-waking-bunks-ship}

The sun has set over the harbour. Tomorrow, you and I will spend a whole day on board one of those great grey ships.

[pause]

A battleship's day began at half past five, with a bugle and a famous old call.

[pause]

Life on board a battleship began early. You'd be woken by that bugle, whether you liked it or not. At about half past five in the morning, a bugle sounded over the loudspeakers. Then a voice called out an old navy phrase: Reveille, reveille, all hands heave out and trice up. Heave out meant get out of bed. Trice up meant fold your bunk out of the way. All through the ship, hundreds of young men climbed out of their bunks, rubbing their eyes and yawning. The sleeping compartments were long and crowded, deep below the decks. They were hot and stuffy, even at night. Fans whirred, and the ship's machinery hummed through the steel. The men slept in narrow canvas bunks, stacked three or four high. There were only a few inches between one man and the next. Each sailor had a small locker for his things. Inside were his uniforms, his shaving kit and his letters from home. There might be a photograph of his girl, a Bible and a deck of cards.

[long-pause]

{img:26-mess-deck-long-tables-breakfast-sailors}

The ship's cooks got up at three in the morning to feed more than a thousand men.

[pause]

The men washed and shaved quickly, in long rows of steel basins. You'd smell fresh bread drifting through the passageways before dawn. They put on their working clothes. These were blue denim trousers and light blue shirts, with white caps pushed to the back of the head. Then came breakfast. The men ate on the mess decks, at long tables that folded away when not in use. The food was plentiful and filling. There were scrambled eggs, bacon and sausages, fried potatoes, and pancakes with syrup. There was toast, and big mugs of strong coffee. On Sundays, there were often baked beans and cornbread. In the galley, the cooks had been up since three in the morning. They baked bread in great ovens and fried bacon in enormous pans. A battleship had to feed more than a thousand men, three times a day, every single day. Many young sailors had grown up poor, in hard years. Some said they had never eaten so well in their lives.

[long-pause]

{img:27-sailors-scrubbing-deck-holystones-sun}

Every morning, sailors scrubbed the wooden decks with blocks of sandstone called holystones.

[pause]

After breakfast, the work of the day began. You'd kneel on the deck with the others, pushing the stone back and forth. There was always something to be done on a ship. The decks were made of wooden planks laid over the steel. Every morning, they were scrubbed with salt water and blocks of sandstone. The sailors called these blocks holystones. Some said that was because you scrubbed with them on your knees, as if in prayer. They scrubbed until the planks were white and smooth. The brass had to be polished until it shone like gold. The paint had to be scraped and painted, again and again. The salt air of the sea was always trying to eat away at the steel. There were drills too. There were gun drills, fire drills and drills for abandoning ship. The men practised loading the great guns with dummy shells. They did it so many times that they could do it in the dark. It was hard, repetitive work. Yet a clean, shining ship was a matter of great pride.

[long-pause]

{img:28-ship-barber-tailor-small-workshop}

A battleship had its own barbers, tailors, bakers and even a dentist.

[pause]

Every man on the ship had his own job. You could find almost anything you needed without ever going ashore. There were gunners and signalmen. There were radiomen and electricians. Deep in the ship were the firemen and machinists, who looked after the boilers and the engines. It was very hot down there, and very loud. There were cooks and bakers, barbers and tailors. There were laundrymen who washed the white uniforms in great steaming machines. There were storekeepers who knew where every tin of peas was kept. There was a doctor and a dentist, and a chaplain for the men's worries. There was a small shop that sold sweets, soap and cigarettes. There was a library, and a little print shop that made the ship's own newspaper. A battleship was really a small floating town. It had everything its people needed, all packed inside a steel hull. A boy from a tiny farm might find himself living in the biggest town he had ever known. You might never want to go ashore at all. And this town could sail across the ocean.

[long-pause]

{img:29-ship-band-practising-deck-instruments}

Every battleship had its own band, and the Arizona's band had trained together at music school.

[pause]

Every battleship had its own band. You'd hear them practising on deck in the afternoons. The band of the Arizona was made up of twenty-one young men. Most of them were in their early twenties. They had trained together at the navy's music school in Washington. Then they were sent to the Arizona as one group. They played every morning at eight o'clock, when the flag was raised over the ship. They played at ceremonies, concerts and dances. In the evenings, some of them played jazz and swing for the other sailors. That was the popular music of the day, the kind you danced to. They practised hard, on the stern of the ship, with the harbour all around them. The other sailors would sometimes gather to listen, tapping their feet. The musicians were good, and they knew it. They were proud of their band, and proud of their ship. Can you hear them? Which instrument would you have played? Trumpets and trombones in the warm morning air, drifting across the calm blue water.

[long-pause]

{img:30-church-service-deck-sailors-folding-chairs}

On Sundays, the chaplain held a church service in the open air, on deck.

[pause]

On Sundays, life on board was easier. You'd sit on a bench on deck, with the sea breeze in your face. There was no work, except what had to be done. In the morning, there was a church service on deck. The chaplain stood in his robes in front of a little altar. The sailors sat on benches and folding chairs. They sang hymns in the open air, while the band played. Sea birds wheeled overhead, and the trade wind lifted the pages of the hymn books. After the service, there was a big Sunday lunch. Then the men were free for the rest of the day. They could sleep, or read, or write letters. They could go ashore, if it was their turn for liberty. Many simply sat on deck in the shade of the big guns. The warm wind blew, and they looked out at the green hills around the harbour. They talked quietly about home. Isn't that what Sunday afternoons are for? A full stomach, a soft breeze, and nowhere you need to be.

[long-pause]

{img:31-young-sailor-writing-letter-bunk-lamp}

On board the Arizona, there were many sets of brothers, and even a father and his son.

[pause]

Most of the sailors were very young. You'd hear brothers calling to each other across the deck. Many had joined the navy straight out of high school, at seventeen or eighteen. They came from farms in Iowa and Kansas. They came from mining towns in Pennsylvania and West Virginia, and from the big cities of the East and the West. For many of them, the navy was a steady job. It meant good food, a bed and a chance to see the world. Jobs at home had been hard to find for years. On board the Arizona, there were many sets of brothers serving together. Families liked their sons to be on the same ship, so they could look after each other. There was even a father serving with his son. The father worked with the engines, and his son was a young seaman. Imagine that. Waking up in the morning and seeing your dad across the mess deck at breakfast. It must have felt a little like home, so far from home.

[long-pause]

{img:32-sailors-relaxing-deck-shade-afternoon}

In the afternoons, some sailors simply fished over the side of their battleship.

[pause]

In the afternoons, when the work was done, there was time to relax. You'd lean over the rail with a line and wait. The men wrote letters and read magazines. They played cards or checkers. They listened to the radio and shined their shoes until they gleamed. Some lay on the deck in the shade, under the big guns, and slept. Some fished over the side, with a line and a bit of bacon. Now and then, someone pulled up a small fish and everyone cheered. Some went swimming, when it was allowed. Others played catch with a baseball on the open deck. Many sailors also had pets on board, though the rules didn't always allow it. There were cats that slept on warm machinery, and a few small, much-loved dogs. It was a gentle, easy rhythm. Work in the morning, rest in the afternoon. You'd probably catch nothing, and you wouldn't mind at all. For young men far from home, it was a life with very little to worry about.

[long-pause]

{img:33-movie-screen-deck-sailors-stars}

In the evenings, the sailors watched Hollywood films on a big white screen, under the stars.

[pause]

In the evenings, there were films. You'd sit on the deck with hundreds of others, looking up at the screen and the stars behind it. A big white screen was set up on the deck. The men sat in rows in their white uniforms, watching the latest picture from Hollywood. The warm trade wind blew across the harbour. The stars shone overhead, brighter than the film. Sometimes the wind made the screen ripple, and the actors' faces wobbled. Everyone laughed. If there was a love scene, the sailors whistled and teased each other. At ten o'clock, the bugle sounded taps, and the lights went out. The men climbed back into their bunks. The ship grew quiet. There was only the hum of machinery and the footsteps of the men on watch. Up on deck, the officer of the deck walked slowly back and forth. He looked out across the dark water, where the lights of the other ships lay reflected. I find that a lovely picture. And out beyond the harbour mouth, the long Pacific swell rolled in and broke softly on the reef.

[long-pause]

{img:34-texas-farm-family-1930s-porch}

Among the thousands of young men on those ships was one quiet giant from Texas. I'd like you to meet him.

[pause]

Dorie Miller grew up on a cotton farm near Waco, the third of four sons.

[pause]

On board the battleship West Virginia, there was a young man named Doris Miller. You'd find him in the fields with his brothers, under a hot Texas sun. Everyone called him Dorie. In 1941, he was twenty-two years old. He was very big and very strong, more than six feet tall. He had a broad, friendly face and a quiet, easy smile. Dorie was born in 1919, near the city of Waco, in Texas. He was the third of four sons in a family of Black farmers. His parents, Conery and Henrietta Miller, worked a small farm. They grew cotton and vegetables and raised chickens and pigs. Dorie grew up working in the fields with his brothers, from early morning until sunset. At school, he played football. He was so strong and so fast that the other boys called him the Raging Bull. Yet at home, he was a gentle boy. He helped his mother around the house and went to church every Sunday. You'd hear his mother calling the boys in for supper. He liked to go hunting for squirrels in the woods with his father's old rifle.

[long-pause]

{img:35-young-man-recruiting-office-1939}

When Dorie joined the navy, only one kind of job was open to Black sailors.

[pause]

When he was nineteen, Dorie decided to join the navy. You might think he could choose any job he liked. He couldn't. Jobs were hard to find in Texas in those years. The navy offered steady pay, and a chance to send money home to his family. It also offered a chance to see the world. In September 1939, he went to the recruiting office in Dallas and signed up. But in 1939, the navy didn't treat Black men in the same way as white men. They couldn't become gunners, radiomen, machinists or officers. Only one job was open to them. They could join the messman branch. That meant cooking and serving food to the officers, and cleaning their cabins. It meant washing their clothes and polishing their shoes. It was unfair, and many people knew it was unfair. Yet young men like Dorie joined anyway. They wanted the work, and they wanted to serve. And many of them hoped, quietly, that things would one day change.

[long-pause]

{img:36-mess-attendant-white-jacket-wardroom}

In his white jacket, Dorie carried trays, made beds and collected the officers' laundry.

[pause]

So Dorie became a mess attendant. You'd see him moving quietly through the wardroom with a tray balanced on one hand. He was trained at a navy school in Virginia. Then he was sent to the West Virginia, one of the great battleships of the Pacific Fleet. He worked in the officers' wardroom and their cabins. He wore a white jacket. He carried trays and set the tables. He made the beds and collected the laundry. Early each morning, before most of the ship was awake, he went from cabin to cabin gathering shirts and sheets. He did his work well and quietly, and the officers liked him. He was always polite and cheerful, and he never complained. His friends on the ship were the other mess attendants. They were young Black men like himself, from Texas and Louisiana, Georgia and Virginia. They worked together and ate together. They slept in the same small compartment, and looked out for one another. On the long ship, they were a small family of their own.

[long-pause]

{img:37-shipboard-boxing-ring-sailors-watching}

Dorie was the heavyweight boxing champion of his whole battleship.

[pause]

Dorie had one great passion on board. You'd see the crowd gather around the ring on deck to watch him. He was the heavyweight boxing champion of the West Virginia. The navy loved boxing. Every ship had its own team and its own matches. A ring was set up on the deck. Hundreds of sailors sat all around it, on the deck and on the gun turrets, cheering. Dorie was big and strong and surprisingly quick on his feet. He had a powerful punch. He won match after match. His shipmates were very proud of him. When he walked along the deck, men slapped him on the back and called out his name. Outside the ring, he was easy-going and good-humoured. He liked to joke and to tell stories about Texas. He liked to play cards in the evenings and listen to music on the radio. And every month, he sent part of his pay home to his parents. It's easy to picture him. A gentle giant, smiling, with a deck of cards in his big hands.

[long-pause]

{img:38-battleship-wake-open-ocean-dolphins}

At sea for the first time, Dorie wrote that the ocean was even bigger than he had imagined.

[pause]

In 1940, the West Virginia sailed with the fleet on long exercises at sea. You can imagine him at the rail, staring out at the endless blue. She visited other ports in the Pacific. Dorie saw the open ocean for the first time. It was endless and blue. Flying fish leapt from the waves. Dolphins raced alongside the ship, as if they wanted to play. At night, he stood at the rail and watched the water glowing with tiny lights. Above him were more stars than he had ever seen in Texas. He wrote to his brothers that the sea was even bigger than he had imagined. Do you remember the first time you saw the sea? Some people never forget it. For a boy from the cotton fields, it must have felt like a whole new world. Back in Pearl Harbor, he liked to go into Honolulu with his friends. They walked along the beaches and went to the cinema. They ate at the little restaurants in town. Do you remember the first time you saw the sea? Dorie wrote home that he liked Hawaii, and that the weather was always fine.

[long-pause]

{img:39-sailor-rail-night-harbour-lights-thinking}

Dorie dreamed of going home one day, and having a farm of his own.

[pause]

Dorie had plans for the future. You can picture him, leaning on the rail, thinking of fields and open sky. He'd finish his time in the navy. Then he'd go home to Texas and help his parents on the farm. Perhaps, one day, he'd have a farm of his own, and a family. He was saving a little money each month for that. In the evenings, after the officers had finished dinner, the wardroom was cleaned and the dishes put away. Then Dorie sometimes went up on deck. He stood by the rail in the warm darkness. He looked out at the lights of the other ships and the shore. He thought about home. He thought about his mother's kitchen and his brothers and the wide Texas sky. Many young men on those ships had the same quiet dreams. A small farm, a garden, a family on the porch. They weren't grand dreams. But they were good ones, and they kept the young men company on long nights far from home.

[long-pause]

{img:40-liberty-launch-sailors-going-ashore}

When the work was done, the sailors couldn't wait to get ashore. Grab your cap, and let's catch the next launch ashore with them.

[pause]

When a sailor had a day off, he went on liberty, and the little boats came and went all day.

[pause]

When a sailor at Pearl Harbor had a day off, he went on liberty. You'd climb down into a little launch and feel it bob across the harbour. On weekends, the liberty boats came and went all day long. They were small launches that puttered across the harbour from the battleships to the landing. They were packed with young men in crisp white uniforms. The sailors laughed and talked, with their caps pushed to the backs of their heads. Some had saved up their pay for weeks. Others had only a few coins in their pockets. From the landing, they took a bus or a taxi into Honolulu. It was about eight miles away, along a road through the sugar cane fields. Through the open windows came the smell of cut cane and flowers. Before going ashore, each sailor's uniform was checked. It had to be spotless, with every crease sharp. An officer might send a man back to change if a single button was dull. So the young men stepped off the launch looking their very best.

[long-pause]

{img:41-hotel-street-1941-neon-sailors-evening}

On Hotel Street, a sailor could have his photograph taken sitting in a pretend aeroplane.

[pause]

In Honolulu, many sailors headed first for Hotel Street. You'd sit in the pretend cockpit and grin for the camera. It was a busy, noisy street in the middle of the old town. It was lined with cafés, cheap restaurants and shooting galleries. There were photograph studios, souvenir shops and tattoo parlours. From morning till night, it was crowded with sailors and soldiers. They walked up and down, looking in the windows and buying postcards to send home. In the photograph studios, you could have your picture taken beside a hula dancer. Or you could sit in a pretend aeroplane, painted on a board, and send that picture to your mother. Tattoo artists would draw an anchor, a ship or your sweetheart's name on your arm. Little restaurants served hamburgers, chili and pie. The Black Cat Café, across from the YMCA, was open day and night. Would you have been tempted by a tattoo? Many sailors were. You'd send that picture home to make your family smile. Some of them regretted it by Monday morning.

[long-pause]

{img:42-sailors-bicycles-quiet-beach-palms}

Some sailors rented bicycles and rode all the way round the island to empty beaches.

[pause]

Other sailors preferred quieter things. You'd feel the wind in your face as you rode along the coast road. They went to the beach at Waikiki. They swam in the warm sea and lay on the sand in the sun. Some learned to surf from the beachboys, falling off again and again with great splashes. Some rented bicycles and rode around the island. They pedalled past pineapple fields and plantation villages. They reached the quiet beaches on the far side, where there was nobody else at all. Some went to the YMCA in town. It had a swimming pool, a reading room and a cheap cafeteria. There were simple rooms where a sailor could stay the night. Others went to church, or to the library, or to the zoo. Some took open buses up to a high mountain pass called the Pali. There, the cliffs dropped away steeply. The whole green windward coast lay spread out below. Wouldn't you like a ride like that? The wind blew so hard that it could almost lift a man off his feet.

[long-pause]

{img:43-sailor-writing-postcard-park-bench}

In December, sailors mailed home grass skirts and ukuleles as Christmas presents.

[pause]

Many sailors spent much of their liberty writing letters. You can imagine a family in a cold northern town opening that parcel. They sat in cafés, on park benches or on the beach. They wrote to their mothers and fathers, brothers and sisters, wives and girlfriends. They described the islands, the flowers, the beaches and the strange food. They told stories about their ship and their friends. They asked about everyone at home. How was the dog? How was the farm? Had the football team won? In December, the letters were full of Christmas. The sailors bought presents in the shops of Honolulu. They chose grass skirts and ukuleles, and silk scarves printed with flowers. They wrapped them carefully and sent them home by ship to California. And parcels from home began to arrive too. They were full of fruitcake and cookies and warm socks. Warm socks, in Hawaii! The sailors opened them in their bunks, laughing, and shared the cookies with their friends.

[long-pause]

{img:44-navy-family-small-house-garden-evening}

For the married men, liberty meant going home to supper and children running down the path.

[pause]

For the married men, liberty meant going home. You'd hear little feet running down the path to meet you. Some men in the fleet had their families with them in Hawaii. They were mostly the older sailors and the officers. They lived in small rented houses and flats in Honolulu and the towns around the harbour. Some lived in navy housing on Ford Island and at the Navy Yard. When the men came home in the evening, supper was waiting. The children ran down the path to meet them. At weekends, families went to the beach, or for a drive around the island. They had picnics in the park, under the trees. In the evening, they sat on the porch and listened to the radio. They watched the children playing on the lawn until it was too dark to see. Many of these families had never lived anywhere so beautiful. They often said they felt lucky. You'd sit on the back step after supper and watch the sky turn pink. Hawaii, with its flowers and its warm sea, felt like a long, happy holiday.

[long-pause]

{img:45-big-band-dance-hall-sailors-couples}

The ships' bands competed against each other in a contest called the Battle of Music.

[pause]

There were dances every weekend, at the clubs, hotels and dance halls. You'd hear the cheering from the hall all the way down the road. Big bands played the latest swing music. Young sailors in white uniforms and young women in their best dresses danced the jitterbug and the foxtrot. Then they walked slowly home along the beach, in the moonlight. In the autumn of 1941, there was a special event that everyone talked about. It was called the Battle of Music. It was a contest between the bands of the ships of the fleet. It was held every few weeks at Bloch Arena, a big new hall at the Navy Yard. Each band played a set of music. Then everyone danced to it, and the audience voted for the best band. The sailors cheered for their own ship's band as if it were a football team. The rivalry was fierce, and friendly. The final was to be held just before Christmas, on the twentieth of December. Who do you think won? The Arizona's band had already won its place in that final.

[long-pause]

{img:46-family-radio-news-living-room-1941}

The bands play on, and the island feels peaceful. But far beyond the horizon, the world was already at war. Let's look out there for a moment, gently.

[pause]

In 1941, war had been raging in Europe for two years, and in China for four.

[pause]

In the autumn of 1941, much of the world beyond the islands was at war. You might think Hawaii was far from all that. It felt that way. In Europe, the war had been going on for more than two years. Germany had conquered most of the continent, from France to Poland. That summer, it had invaded the Soviet Union as well. Britain was fighting on, under attack from the air and at sea. In Asia too, there was war. For four years, Japan had been fighting in China. Its armies had taken much of the coast and many of the great cities. In 1940 and 1941, Japanese troops also moved south into French Indochina. Today, those lands are Vietnam, Laos and Cambodia. The United States wasn't at war. Most Americans didn't want to fight. They remembered the First World War, only twenty-five years before. In Hawaii, families listened to the news on the radio each evening. Then they switched it off, and went out to the porch, where everything was calm.

[long-pause]

{img:47-harbour-oil-tanks-hills-afternoon}

Japan had very little oil of its own, and much of it had come from America.

[pause]

Relations between the United States and Japan had grown worse and worse. You'd need oil for every ship, plane and factory. Japan needed oil, steel and rubber for its factories and its armies. It had very little of these things at home. Much of its oil had come from the United States. In the summer of 1941, the American government stopped selling oil to Japan. It hoped this would make Japan stop its wars in Asia. Without oil, Japan's navy and army would soon run out of fuel. So Japan's leaders faced a hard choice. They could give up their conquests, as America demanded. Or they could go to war, to seize oil fields far to the south, in what are now Indonesia and Malaysia. All through the autumn, diplomats from both countries met in Washington. They talked and talked, trying to find a way out. But they couldn't agree. It's a sad thing to think about. So much depended on a few men in a room, unable to find the right words.

[long-pause]

{img:48-admiral-study-calligraphy-quiet-evening}

The Japanese admiral who planned the attack had studied at Harvard and admired America.

[pause]

In Japan, the commander of the combined fleet was Admiral Isoroku Yamamoto. He was fifty-seven years old, short and broad-shouldered, and very thoughtful. As a young officer, he had studied at Harvard University in America. Later, he had served at the Japanese embassy in Washington. He knew America well. He had travelled across it and seen its great factories and its oil fields. He admired it. He also loved to play cards and board games, and he was very good at them. Yamamoto didn't want war with the United States. He believed Japan couldn't win a long war against such a large and powerful country. He said so, many times, to his country's leaders. But he was a loyal officer. If there had to be a war, he believed Japan's only chance was to strike first and strike hard. He thought the target should be the American fleet at Pearl Harbor.

[long-pause]

{img:49-northern-bay-snow-ships-anchored-fog}

The Japanese fleet gathered secretly in a foggy, snowy bay in the far north.

[pause]

So, in the autumn of 1941, a Japanese fleet was gathered in great secrecy. You'd see only fog, snow and grey water there. There were six aircraft carriers, the largest ships in the Japanese navy. Together, they carried more than four hundred planes. There were battleships, cruisers and destroyers to protect them. There were tankers to carry their fuel. They gathered in a remote bay in the Kuril Islands, in the far north of Japan. The hills there were covered in snow. Fog lay thick over the cold water, and no one could see them. On the twenty-sixth of November, the fleet sailed out of the bay. It turned east, across the North Pacific, toward Hawaii. It took a long, lonely route, far north of the usual shipping lanes. The seas there were cold and stormy, and few other ships were likely to pass. The ships kept radio silence. They showed no lights at night. Day after day, they moved east through the grey waves, unseen.

[long-pause]

{img:50-young-pilots-ship-quarters-letters-lamp}

Many of the young Japanese pilots were barely older than the American sailors in the harbour.

[pause]

On the long voyage, the young pilots on the carriers studied maps and models of Pearl Harbor. They cleaned their planes and checked their equipment. And they wrote letters to their families. Many of them weren't much older than the American sailors they were sailing toward. They too had parents at home, and brothers and sisters, and sweethearts. They too missed home cooking and their mothers' voices. Some wrote poems, as Japanese soldiers often did, about the moon and the sea and the cherry blossoms of spring. Many of them were afraid, though they didn't say so. It's worth remembering this, quietly. On both sides of the ocean, there were young men lying awake at night, thinking about home. None of them had chosen the war. They had simply been born into a time when it came.

[long-pause]

{img:51-christmas-trees-honolulu-store-palm}

In the first week of December, Christmas trees arrived in Honolulu by ship from the mainland.

[pause]

In Hawaii, nobody knew that the fleet was coming. You'd smell the pine needles as soon as you walked into the shop. The American commanders knew that war with Japan might come very soon. On the twenty-seventh of November, they received a warning from Washington. But most people believed that if Japan attacked, it would attack far away. They thought of the Philippines, or Malaya, or the Dutch islands in the south. Few people really believed Japan would come all the way across the Pacific. So in the first week of December, life in Hawaii went on as usual. The ships came and went. The sailors went on liberty. The bands practised for the Battle of Music. In Honolulu, the shops were full of Christmas decorations. Fresh Christmas trees had arrived by ship from the mainland. They stood in rows outside the stores, under the palm trees. The aircraft carriers of the fleet were away at sea that week. You'd find it funny, a pine tree under the palms. But the battleships were in the harbour, as they usually were on weekends.

[long-pause]

{img:52-captain-inspection-sailors-rows-deck}

Back in Hawaii, December has come, warm and green. You'd hardly guess it was winter at all.

[pause]

Every Saturday, the captain checked for dust with a white-gloved finger.

[pause]

Saturday, the sixth of December 1941, was a lovely day in Hawaii. You'd hold your breath as his white glove ran along the shelf. The sky was clear and blue, with a few soft clouds over the mountains. The trade wind blew gently. It was warm, but not hot. It was a perfect day for the beach. At Pearl Harbor, it was a quiet Saturday. On the battleships, there was an inspection in the morning, as there was every week. The men stood in neat rows on deck, in their best white uniforms. The captain walked slowly along the lines. He looked at their uniforms, their shoes and their haircuts. Then he went below to inspect the living spaces. He ran a white-gloved finger along the shelves to check for dust. Everyone held their breath until he moved on. After the inspection, half the crew of each ship was given liberty until the next morning. The launches began to fill up with sailors heading ashore, laughing and pushing each other into the boats.

[long-pause]

{img:53-department-store-christmas-tinsel-palms}

In Honolulu, a Santa Claus sat sweating in his red suit under the palm trees.

[pause]

In Honolulu, the streets were full of people doing their Christmas shopping. You'd feel sorry for him, I think, in that heavy red suit. The department stores were decorated with tinsel and paper bells. Christmas music played on the loudspeakers. There were carols about snow and sleigh bells. They sounded a little strange in the hot sunshine, under the palm trees. Children stood in line to see Santa Claus. He sat in a big chair in his heavy red suit and white beard, sweating and smiling. He must have longed for a cool drink. Parents bought toys and books, and wrapped them in coloured paper. Some families had ordered presents from the mainland months earlier. They hoped the parcels would arrive by ship in time. In the windows of the houses, little Christmas lights were already blinking. Do you think Christmas feels strange in a warm place? Many mainlanders thought so at first. Yet the islanders loved it, and made it their own. Have you ever been too warm at Christmas? They hung paper lanterns beside the stars on their trees.

[long-pause]

{img:54-football-stadium-crowd-sunny-afternoon}

That afternoon, twenty-four thousand people watched a football game against a team from Oregon.

[pause]

In the afternoon, there was a big football game at the stadium in Honolulu. You'd hear the roar of the crowd across half of Honolulu. The University of Hawaii was playing against a team from Willamette University, in Oregon. That team had come all the way across the Pacific by ship to play. About twenty-four thousand people came to watch. They sat in the sun, in hats and summer dresses, eating peanuts and shaved ice. A band played in the stands. The home team won, and the crowd went home happy. Many of the visiting players had never been so far from home. They had spent the week swimming at Waikiki and seeing the sights. The next day, they planned to go sightseeing again. For them, it was the trip of a lifetime. All over the island, Saturday afternoon passed slowly and sweetly. People mowed their lawns and washed their cars. Old men played checkers in the shade. Somewhere a radio was playing the football results, and someone cheered.

[long-pause]

{img:55-waikiki-saturday-families-picnic-sand}

At Waikiki, the families stayed until the shadows of the palms stretched across the sand.

[pause]

At Waikiki, the beach was crowded that Saturday. You'd dig your toes into the warm sand and watch the sun go down. Families spread their towels on the sand and opened their picnic baskets. There were sandwiches and fried chicken, rice balls and sliced pineapple. Children built sandcastles and splashed in the shallow water. Young people played volleyball. Out on the waves, surfers rode the long gentle swells. Outrigger canoes came racing in toward the shore, with everyone paddling and shouting. Lifeguards sat on their tall wooden chairs, watching. In the late afternoon, the light turned golden. The shadows of the palm trees stretched out across the sand. Fathers shook out the towels. Mothers called the children in from the water, once, twice and three times. At last, the families packed up and went home, sunburnt and tired and happy. Can you feel it? Warm sand under your feet, salt drying on your skin, and the sun sinking low over the sea.

[long-pause]

{img:56-band-on-stage-arena-sailors-dancing}

That night, after the Battle of Music, the whole hall sang God Bless America together.

[pause]

In the evening, a big crowd gathered at Bloch Arena, at the Navy Yard. You'd feel the whole hall singing together, a little out of tune and very happy. It was a night of the Battle of Music. Four bands competed that evening. They came from the battleships Pennsylvania and Tennessee, a cruiser and a supply ship. The hall was packed with sailors and their girlfriends and wives. Everyone was dressed up for the evening. Each band played swing tunes, popular songs and a few marches. Then there was a jitterbug contest. Couples danced wildly across the floor while the crowd clapped and cheered. The band of the Arizona was there too, sitting in the audience. They had already won their place in the final, and they cheered for the others. At the end, the band of the Pennsylvania was named the winner. Then all the bands stood up together, and the whole hall sang God Bless America. People who were there remembered that moment for the rest of their lives.

[long-pause]

{img:57-moonlit-harbour-battleships-silhouettes}

On the last night of peace, the moon turned the harbour to silver.

[pause]

All over Oahu, people enjoyed their Saturday night. You'd see the silver light on the water from every porch along the shore. In the hotels at Waikiki, bands played on the terraces under the stars. There were parties at the officers' clubs. The cinemas of Honolulu were full. Admiral Kimmel went to a small dinner party at a hotel by the beach. He left early, as he always did. In the morning, he planned to play golf with General Short. On the ships in the harbour, the lights were dimmed. The sailors on watch walked slowly along the decks. It was a warm, still, beautiful night. The moon was bright. It turned the water to silver and laid the shadows of the great ships across it. The palm trees on Ford Island stood black against the sky. Somewhere a dog barked, and a car drove slowly along the shore road. And on a hill above Honolulu, a radio station stayed on the air all night. It played soft Hawaiian music, so that a flight of bombers from California could find its way in the dark.

[long-pause]

{img:58-sunday-dawn-oahu-mountains-clouds-pink}

The music has ended, and the moon shines on the quiet harbour. We'll go very gently now, into the morning that followed.

[pause]

Sunday, the seventh of December, dawned soft and lovely over Oahu.

[pause]

Sunday, the seventh of December 1941, dawned soft and lovely. You'd wake to birdsong and soft, golden light. The sun came up a little after half past six, behind the mountains. The sky turned from grey to pink to pale gold. A few gentle white clouds lay over the ridges. The air was cool and fresh. The sea was calm. It was going to be another beautiful day. In Honolulu, most people were still asleep. Sunday was the one morning of the week when nobody needed to get up early. A few people were up already. There were the milkmen and the newspaper boys, the bakers, and people walking to early Mass. Somewhere, a rooster was crowing. On the radio, the soft Hawaiian music was still playing, from the station that had stayed on the air all night. In kitchens here and there, the first coffee was bubbling on the stove. It was the kind of morning that makes you want to stay in bed, just a little longer.

[long-pause]

{img:59-small-destroyer-patrol-calm-sea-dawn}

Out at sea, a small old destroyer called the Ward saw something strange in the water.

[pause]

Out at sea, a few miles from the entrance to Pearl Harbor, a small old destroyer was on patrol. You'd see the sun just rising over a calm grey sea. She was called the Ward. Many of her crew were reservists from Minnesota, part-time sailors who had been called up that year. Some had been farmers and shop workers only months before. At about a quarter to seven, her crew saw something strange in the water. It was the small tower of a tiny submarine, following a supply ship toward the harbour. The Ward fired at it, and it disappeared beneath the waves. Her captain sent a message to the navy headquarters on shore. But it was very early on a Sunday morning. The message was passed slowly from one office to another. People wanted to check it before they believed it. In 2002, more than sixty years later, scientists found that little submarine on the sea floor. It was still lying there, quietly, just where the Ward had found it.

[long-pause]

{img:60-radar-station-hilltop-north-shore-dawn}

On a hilltop, two young radar men stayed on a few extra minutes, just to practise.

[pause]

On the northern tip of the island, on a hill above the sea, there was a small army radar station. You'd see them yawning in the little hut, waiting for breakfast. It was called Opana. Radar was still very new in 1941. It used radio waves to spot aeroplanes far away, long before anyone could see or hear them. There were only a few radar sets in Hawaii. The men who used them were still learning how. That morning, two young privates were on duty at Opana. Their names were Joseph Lockard and George Elliott. Their shift was supposed to end at seven o'clock. But Elliott was new and wanted to practise. So they left the set running for a few more minutes. At two minutes past seven, they saw something on the little screen. It was a large blip, bigger than anything they had seen before. It was about a hundred and thirty miles to the north, and it was coming toward the island. The two young men looked at each other, puzzled.

[long-pause]

{img:61-young-soldiers-radar-screen-hut}

The young officer on duty thought the blip must be friendly planes, coming from California.

[pause]

Lockard telephoned the information centre at Fort Shafter, near Honolulu. He spoke to the only officer on duty there that morning. He was a young lieutenant named Kermit Tyler. Tyler had only been in the job a short time, and he had almost no training for it. He thought for a moment. He knew that a flight of American bombers was expected that morning from California. The radio station was playing music all night to guide them in. So he thought the blip must be those bombers. He told Lockard not to worry about it. The two privates watched the blip a little longer as it came closer. Then they switched off the set and went to breakfast. For the rest of his life, Tyler was asked about that phone call. He explained it patiently. He had been told to expect friendly planes, and he had no way of knowing. Most people who heard his story understood.

[long-pause]

{img:62-plantation-village-church-bell-sunday}

Across the island, mothers were ironing Sunday clothes and church bells were ringing.

[pause]

Across the island, in the little plantation villages, families were getting ready for church. You'd hear church bells drifting across the quiet valleys. Mothers ironed the children's Sunday clothes. Fathers shaved at the kitchen sink. Grandmothers already sat on the porch, dressed in their best, waiting for everyone else. In one small town, a Buddhist temple was preparing a Sunday school picnic. In another, a Catholic church was filling up for early Mass. The sound of its bell drifted across the cane fields. In Honolulu, newspaper boys left the Sunday paper on doorsteps. It had comics in colour, and pages of advertisements for Christmas presents. On a golf course near the harbour, a few early players were already out. The grass was green and wet with dew. Their caddies carried their bags. Birds sang in the trees. Would you have been one of those early risers, or one of the sleepers? On a morning like that, most people chose to sleep.

[long-pause]

{img:63-sailors-on-deck-soft-morning-light-ships}

On the West Virginia, Dorie Miller was up at six, collecting the officers' laundry.

[pause]

At Pearl Harbor, the ships were waking up. You'd find him with a bundle of laundry under his arm. The sailors washed and dressed and went down to breakfast. It was a relaxed Sunday morning. Many officers and men were ashore. Those on board were looking forward to a quiet day. Some would go to the church service on deck. Some would go ashore later for a swim or a picnic. On the West Virginia, Dorie Miller had been up since six o'clock. He was going around the ship collecting the officers' laundry, as he always did. On the Arizona, the band was getting ready to play for the raising of the flag at eight o'clock. The musicians put on their white uniforms and took out their instruments. They walked up on deck to take their places at the stern. On Ford Island, families sat at breakfast. Children in pyjamas ate cereal and toast. Through the windows, they could see the battleships lying in a row, grey and still in the morning sun.

[long-pause]

{img:64-flag-detail-stern-morning-band-ready}

At a quarter to eight, the flag guards stood ready on every ship, waiting to raise the colours.

[pause]

At about a quarter to eight, on the ships along Battleship Row, small groups of sailors gathered at the stern. They held the flags folded in their arms. At exactly eight o'clock, they'd raise them, as they did every morning. The buglers stood ready. The bands lifted their instruments. The officers of the deck looked at their watches. It was a peaceful, ordinary Sunday morning, just like a hundred others before it. The water was flat and blue. A gull floated near the hull of a battleship. On the shore, the palm trees barely moved in the light breeze. Somewhere, a sailor was humming a tune from the night before. Another was thinking about breakfast. Another was thinking about a letter he had to finish. And then, a few minutes before eight o'clock, the sailors on deck heard a distant sound. It was the hum of many aeroplanes, coming from the hills to the north. They looked up.

[long-pause]

{img:65-distant-planes-over-green-hills-morning}

We'll only touch the next part softly, you and I. It's sad, and we'll keep it brief.

[pause]

At first, many people thought the planes in the sky were just a Sunday exercise.

[pause]

A few minutes before eight o'clock, the planes came over the hills. There were many of them, small and far away, flying in neat groups across the blue sky. Some came over the mountains from the north. Some came around the coast. At first, many people on the ground didn't understand. They thought it must be a practice by the army or the navy. On a Sunday morning, of all times! Some were annoyed by the noise. Some stood and watched, curious, shading their eyes against the sun. Then, on Ford Island, the commander of the air station looked up and understood. He ran to the radio room. A few moments later, a message went out to every ship and station in the Pacific. It used plain and simple words. Air raid, Pearl Harbor. This isn't drill. Those words have become some of the most famous in American history. They were sent out at about two minutes to eight.

[long-pause]

{img:66-navy-band-stern-flag-morning-sun}

On one battleship, the band played the national anthem all the way to the end.

[pause]

What happened in the next two hours has been told many times, in books and films. We'll not tell it all here. We'll only say that it was very fast, very confusing and very loud. The planes came in two waves, the first just before eight o'clock and the second just before nine. They struck the airfields, where the planes were parked in neat rows. And they struck the ships in the harbour, above all the great battleships along Battleship Row. On the decks, bugles sounded and the loudspeakers called the men to their battle stations. Young sailors ran up the ladders, many still in their Sunday whites, and fought back with whatever they had. On the stern of the Nevada, the band had been waiting to play the anthem for the flag. When the planes came over, the band kept playing. They played all the way to the end, as the flag went up. Only then did the musicians run to their stations.

[long-pause]

{img:67-battleship-leaving-channel-flag-flying}

The Nevada was the only battleship to get her engines going and start moving toward the sea.

[pause]

The Nevada was the only battleship that managed to get her engines going that morning. Slowly, she began to move. She steamed down the channel toward the open sea, with her flag flying. On the other ships and on the shore, sailors cheered her on. It was a brave and moving sight. But the channel was narrow. If she sank there, she would block the harbour for months. So her crew ran her gently aground on the shore, out of the way. Elsewhere, several of the great ships settled to the bottom of the shallow harbour. The old Arizona was lost that morning, with most of her crew. Among them were all the young men of her band, who had cheered at the Battle of Music only the night before. Altogether, more than two thousand four hundred Americans lost their lives at Pearl Harbor. Many families, far away, wouldn't hear the news for days.

[long-pause]

{img:68-quiet-sky-over-harbour-late-morning-haze}

By ten o'clock in the morning, it was over, and the sky was quiet again.

[pause]

In Honolulu, people heard the distant noise and came out of their houses. You'd hear the birds again, after a while. They stood in the streets, looking toward Pearl Harbor and wondering what was happening. On the radio, the music stopped. An announcer came on. This is the real McCoy, he said. That was an old American way of saying this is real. He told all servicemen to report to their stations at once. By about ten o'clock in the morning, it was over. The last planes turned away and flew north, back toward their carriers. They disappeared over the mountains. The sky was quiet again. Over the harbour, a dark haze hung in the still air, and drifted slowly out to sea. Small boats were moving everywhere across the water. They were picking up men who had swum or jumped from the ships. And on the shore, on the ships and in the boats, thousands of people were already at work. They were doing the most important thing of all. They were helping each other.

[long-pause]

{img:69-big-young-sailor-carrying-shipmate-deck}

The sky is quiet again. And in the hours that followed, you'd have seen people helping one another everywhere you looked.

[pause]

Dorie Miller, who was never allowed to train on the guns, carried his shipmates to safety.

[pause]

On the West Virginia, Dorie Miller was below decks, collecting laundry, when the alarm sounded. He ran to his battle station, but found it already damaged. So he went up on deck. An officer saw how big and strong he was. He asked Dorie to help carry the wounded to safety. Dorie lifted his injured shipmates, one after another. He carried them through the haze to sheltered places. Among them was the ship's captain, Mervyn Bennion, who had been badly hurt. Dorie and others carried him to safety and stayed with him. Then Dorie saw a machine gun standing unused on the deck. He had never been trained to use it. Black sailors weren't allowed to train on the guns. But he had watched the others, and he had grown up hunting in Texas. He fired the gun until it ran out. Later, he said simply: It wasn't hard. Then he helped carry the wounded through the water to the shore.

[long-pause]

{img:70-sailor-hangar-ramp-kaneohe-bay-morning}

A chief petty officer jumped out of bed, drove to work, and kept firing for two hours.

[pause]

At the navy air station at Kaneohe Bay, on the other side of the island, lived a sailor named John Finn. He was thirty-two years old, a chief petty officer who looked after the planes' guns. That morning, he was at home in bed with his wife when he heard the planes. He jumped into his car and drove to the air station. There, he found a machine gun and set it up on an open stand, in an open space near the hangars. He fired at the planes for more than two hours. He was hurt many times, but he wouldn't stop. Only when the planes had gone did he let the doctors care for him. Then he went back to help repair the damaged planes. Later, he received the Medal of Honor, the highest award for bravery in America. He was always modest about it. He said he had just done his job. And he lived a long, quiet life, all the way to the age of a hundred.

[long-pause]

{img:71-navy-nurses-hospital-ward-white-uniforms}

A young navy nurse remembered that the wounded men were quiet, brave, and asked first about their friends.

[pause]

At the Naval Hospital beside the harbour, a young navy nurse named Ruth Erickson was having breakfast. She was sitting with the other nurses when they heard the planes. They ran to their wards in their white uniforms. Within minutes, the wounded began to arrive. They came in trucks and cars and ambulances, and on stretchers carried by their friends. The nurses and doctors worked all day and all night without stopping. They cleaned wounds and gave medicine. They held hands and spoke softly. Ruth Erickson later remembered that the men were very quiet and very brave. They almost never complained. Many of them asked first about their friends, before asking about themselves. Isn't that a remarkable thing? Hurt and frightened, their first thought was for someone else. Ruth worked all through the night. In the early morning, she stepped outside for a breath of air. I've always found that very moving. She stood looking up at the stars, and then went back in.

[long-pause]

{img:72-volunteers-line-blood-bank-honolulu}

So many people lined up to give blood that there weren't enough bottles to hold it.

[pause]

All over the island, people came to help. You'd see the line stretching all the way down the street. Nurses who were off duty came in from their homes. Doctors drove to the hospitals from all over Honolulu. Ordinary people lined up outside the blood bank in the city. They came in such numbers that there weren't enough bottles to hold all the blood. People of every background stood in that line, side by side. Women brought food and coffee to the workers at the harbour. Boy Scouts carried messages and directed traffic on the busy roads. Taxi drivers carried wounded men to the hospitals, for free. In the hills around Honolulu, families opened their homes. They took in the wives and children from Ford Island, who were waiting for news. Strangers made beds for strangers. They cooked meals and lent clothes. No one asked who you were, or where your family came from. On that day, the whole island seemed to become one family.

[long-pause]

{img:73-workers-small-boat-ship-hull-tools}

Rescuers heard tapping from inside an upturned ship, and cut through the steel to free thirty-two men.

[pause]

One battleship, the Oklahoma, had rolled over in the harbour. Her hull lay upside down above the water. Inside, in the dark, some men were still alive. They were trapped in pockets of air, deep in the ship. They tapped on the steel walls with hammers and wrenches. They were letting people outside know that they were there. Workers from the Navy Yard came out to the hull in small boats. Most of them were civilians, welders and machinists and riveters. One was a young Hawaiian-born man named Julio DeCastro, from a Portuguese family. They listened carefully for the tapping. Then, very slowly, they cut holes through the thick steel. They worked all through the day and the night, and into the next day. One by one, the trapped men climbed out into the daylight. They were blinking, smiling and crying. Thirty-two men were rescued that way. Many never forgot that first light shining through the hole.

[long-pause]

{img:74-harbour-small-boats-afternoon-calm-light}

Even little private fishing boats came out to help pick men from the water.

[pause]

Out in the harbour, small boats went back and forth all day long. You'd see them coming from every corner of the harbour. There were launches and whaleboats from the ships. There were tugboats from the Navy Yard. There were even little private fishing boats that had come out from the shore. They picked up men from the water and from the decks of the ships. They carried them to the landings, where ambulances were waiting. Many of the men in the boats had no idea where their own ships or friends were. They simply helped whoever they found. A sailor from one ship would pull a sailor from another out of the water. Neither knew the other's name. They'd never meet again. Yet for a few minutes, they were the most important people in each other's lives. By the evening, almost everyone who could be helped had been helped. The boats grew still. The men in them sat quietly, too tired to talk.

[long-pause]

{img:75-blackout-house-candle-family-night}

That night, every light on Oahu went out, and the stars shone brighter than anyone could remember.

[pause]

That evening, as the sun went down, all the lights on Oahu went out. You'd look up and see more stars than you'd ever seen before. Every window was covered with black paper or blankets. The streetlights were switched off. Cars crept along with their headlights painted over. For the first time in many years, the whole island was dark. People sat at home by the light of a single candle, talking quietly and listening to the radio. Above the dark island, the stars came out, brighter than anyone could remember. The frogs and crickets sang in the gardens, just as they had the night before. In the plantation villages, neighbours gathered in each other's houses. They talked in many languages. The Japanese families were especially worried. They were Americans, many born in Hawaii, and their sons were in the army. They feared that some people would now look at them with suspicion. They sat quietly together, hoping their neighbours would remember who they really were. Can you see them, in your mind? And most of their neighbours did.

[long-pause]

{img:76-workers-factory-gathered-around-radio}

That night, the whole island was dark and still. Morning came again, as it always does, and slowly, life on the island found its way forward.

[pause]

The next day, the whole country stopped work to listen to the President on the radio.

[pause]

On Monday, the eighth of December, President Franklin Roosevelt came to speak to Congress. You'd gather around the radio with your family, quiet and listening. He walked slowly into the great chamber, holding the arm of his son. His legs had been weakened by polio for many years. He stood at the desk and opened a black notebook. The whole country was listening on the radio. In homes, offices, factories and schools, people gathered around their radios in silence. In Hawaii, it was early morning. The sailors at Pearl Harbor stood around loudspeakers on the ships and in the barracks. Roosevelt spoke for about seven minutes, in a calm, strong voice. He called the seventh of December a date which will live in infamy. At the end, he asked Congress to declare war. Within an hour, it did. Days later, Germany and Italy declared war on America too. All over the country, long lines of young men stood outside the recruiting offices the next morning. Many had never seen the sea.

[long-pause]

{img:77-honolulu-christmas-1941-quiet-blackout}

That Christmas in Hawaii, families decorated their trees indoors, behind black curtains.

[pause]

In Honolulu, life slowly found a new shape. You'd sit inside with the curtains drawn, the tree glowing softly. The schools stayed closed for some weeks. Everyone had to carry a gas mask in a little canvas bag. Barbed wire was strung along the beach at Waikiki. People dug shelters in their gardens. Every night, the blackout came down and the island went dark. Christmas came, three weeks after the attack. It was a quiet Christmas in Hawaii that year. There were no lights on the trees in the streets. But families still put up their trees indoors, behind black curtains, and decorated them. And the children still found their stockings full on Christmas morning. The churches held their services in daylight, because of the curfew. At the hospitals, the nurses made paper chains and set up little trees in the wards. They gave the wounded men small presents. They sang carols for them, softly. And some of the men, lying in their beds, sang along.

[long-pause]

{img:78-young-soldiers-hawaii-families-farewell-pier}

Thousands of young Japanese Americans from Hawaii joined the army, under the motto Go for Broke.

[pause]

Many people of Japanese descent on the mainland were sent away to camps during the war. In Hawaii, it was different. They made up more than a third of the people on the islands. Most of them were allowed to stay in their homes, though some of their leaders were taken away and held in camps. And their sons wanted to show that they were loyal Americans. When the army asked for volunteers, nearly ten thousand young men from Hawaii stepped forward. Their families came to the pier to see them off, with flower garlands and tears. They joined units made up of Japanese American soldiers. Their motto was Go for Broke. It was island slang for putting everything you have on one throw. They fought in Italy and France, and they were among the most decorated soldiers of the whole war. When they came home, many became teachers, doctors and leaders in the islands. One of them later served Hawaii in the United States Senate for fifty years.

[long-pause]

{img:79-white-house-lawn-christmas-tree-evening}

On Christmas Eve, Winston Churchill stood beside the President and spoke to the children of America.

[pause]

In Washington, on Christmas Eve, President Roosevelt lit the national Christmas tree on the White House lawn. Presidents did this every year. But that year, he had a guest beside him. It was the Prime Minister of Britain, Winston Churchill. He had crossed the Atlantic in secret, on a battleship, to plan the war with Roosevelt. About twenty thousand people stood in the cold darkness to listen. Roosevelt spoke about Christmas, and about peace, and the hard times ahead. Then Churchill spoke, in his deep, slow voice. He talked to the children of America. He said they should have their night of fun and laughter, before the grown-ups turned back to the hard years ahead. Then the lights on the tree came on, glowing softly in the dark. And the crowd sang carols together. Imagine standing in that crowd, your breath white in the air, hearing those two voices in the night.

[long-pause]

{img:80-admiral-nimitz-arriving-flying-boat-harbour}

The new commander arrived on Christmas morning, in the rain, and said very little.

[pause]

At the end of December, a new commander arrived to take over the Pacific Fleet. He was Admiral Chester Nimitz. He was a quiet, calm, white-haired man from the hill country of Texas. He had very blue eyes and a gentle manner. He flew out from Washington and arrived on Christmas morning, in a flying boat, in the rain. He took a small boat across the harbour. He passed the damaged battleships and the small boats still working among them. He looked at it all for a long time, and said very little. Then he went ashore and went to work. Nimitz was famous for his calm. He rarely raised his voice. He liked to pitch horseshoes and take long walks to think. His staff found that his quiet confidence spread to everyone around him. Admiral Kimmel and General Short had been relieved of their commands. They went home and lived quietly. People still argue today about whether they were treated fairly.

[long-pause]

{img:81-salvage-divers-harbour-calm-morning}

Divers in brass helmets worked by touch in the dark water to raise the sunken ships.

[pause]

In the months after the attack, the people of Pearl Harbor set about raising the sunken ships. You'd see bubbles rising slowly to the surface above them. It was one of the greatest salvage jobs ever attempted. Many experts said most of the battleships could never be saved. But the navy divers and the workers of the Navy Yard didn't agree. The divers went down into the dark, oily water. They wore heavy canvas suits and round brass helmets. Air was pumped down to them through long hoses from the boats above. Down in the murky water, they could hardly see. They worked mostly by touch. They felt their way along walls and doors and ladders. They fitted huge patches over the holes in the hulls. The carpenters of the Navy Yard made each patch to fit. Then the water was pumped out. It was hard, slow, careful work, and it went on for months. But one by one, the great ships began to rise to the surface again.

[long-pause]

{img:82-grey-battleships-at-sea-sunset-pacific}

Six of the eight battleships were repaired and went back to sea.

[pause]

The Nevada was the first battleship to be raised, in February 1942. The California followed in March. The West Virginia was raised in May and carefully floated into the dry dock. Of the eight battleships at Pearl Harbor that morning, six were repaired. They went back to sea. The West Virginia and the California were rebuilt and modernised. In 1944, they returned to the Pacific. That autumn, in the Philippines, they and other old battleships from Pearl Harbor fought in the last great battle ever fought between battleships. The men who had lost their ships were proud to see them back. The war in the Pacific lasted nearly four years. Hundreds of thousands of young men served in it, on both sides. At last, in September 1945, the war ended. The surrender was signed in Tokyo Bay, on the deck of the battleship Missouri. Isn't that remarkable? And among the ships watching in the bay was the old West Virginia, raised from the bottom of Pearl Harbor.

[long-pause]

{img:83-medal-ceremony-carrier-deck-1942-sun}

The years went by, and the harbour healed slowly. So what became of them all, and what would you find if you went there today?

[pause]

Dorie Miller became the first Black American to receive the Navy Cross.

[pause]

In May 1942, on the deck of the aircraft carrier Enterprise, there was a ceremony. The crew stood in long rows on the flight deck, in bright Hawaiian sunshine. Admiral Nimitz walked slowly along the line. He stopped in front of a big young man from Texas, and pinned a medal on his chest. It was the Navy Cross, one of the highest awards for bravery in the navy. The young man was Dorie Miller. He was the first Black American to receive it. Newspapers all over America told his story. He went home to Texas for a while and spoke at schools and churches. His mother was very proud. Then he went back to sea, as a cook on a small aircraft carrier. In November 1943, his ship was lost in the Pacific, and Dorie was lost with her. He was twenty-four. Today, schools, parks and streets carry his name. And one of the navy's newest and largest aircraft carriers will be named the Doris Miller.

[long-pause]

{img:84-elderly-woman-telling-story-living-room}

Ruth Erickson later became the head of the whole Navy Nurse Corps.

[pause]

Ruth Erickson, the young nurse, served in the navy for many more years. After the war, she rose to become the head of the whole Navy Nurse Corps, in Washington. Years later, as an old lady, she told her story to historians. She wanted it to be remembered. She described the planes over the hospital and the wounded men arriving. She described the long day and the long night, and the stars she looked up at in the early morning. She lived until 2008. John Finn stayed in the navy until 1956. Then he retired to a ranch in the hills of southern California. There, he raised cattle and lived a quiet life. He loved talking with young people about his years in the navy. He went to many reunions and was always cheerful and modest. The men who survived the ships went on with their lives. They married, raised families and worked on farms and in offices. Some never talked about that morning. Others talked about it often, especially when they were old.

[long-pause]

{img:85-japanese-farmer-rice-field-evening-1950s}

The pilot who led the attack later became a farmer and spoke all over America about forgiveness.

[pause]

On the Japanese side too, there were men who lived on and remembered. The pilot who had led the planes that morning was named Mitsuo Fuchida. He survived the war. Afterwards, he went home to a small farm in Japan. He grew rice and vegetables. Some years later, he read a little pamphlet about forgiveness, and it changed his life. He spent many years travelling in America, talking to people about peace. He even made friends with some of the men he had once fought against. He said he hoped the young people of both countries would never go to war with each other again. And they didn't. In the years after the war, Japan and the United States became close friends. Their peoples traded with each other, visited each other and learned from each other. In 2016, the Prime Minister of Japan came to Pearl Harbor. He stood beside the American President and laid a wreath of flowers. What do you think made him change so much? Then he bowed his head in silence.

[long-pause]

{img:86-arizona-memorial-white-calm-water-morning}

The white memorial over the Arizona dips in the middle and rises at both ends, on purpose.

[pause]

Today, Pearl Harbor is still a working navy base. If you visit, you'll take a small boat across the harbour to reach it. Grey warships still lie at their moorings along the shore. But in the middle of the harbour, beside Ford Island, there's a quiet place that people come from all over the world to see. It's the USS Arizona Memorial. It's a long white building that spans the sunken ship, like a bridge across the water, without touching her. It dips a little in the middle and rises at both ends. The architect, Alfred Preis, explained why. The low middle stands for the sadness of that morning. The rising ends stand for the hope and strength that came afterwards. It opened in 1962. Visitors come out to it by boat, across the calm water. Inside, there are open windows along both sides and above. The light and the wind come through. At the far end is a tall white marble wall. On it are carved the names of the men of the Arizona.

Every day, a few small drops of oil still rise from the Arizona and spread in rainbow rings.

[pause]

Looking down from the memorial into the clear green water, visitors can see the old ship below. Look down into the water, and you'll see them shimmering. They can see the rusted outline of her decks, and the round base of one of her gun turrets. Little fish swim in and out of her. Coral and sea grass grow on her sides. And still, every day, a few small drops of oil rise slowly from the ship. They float to the surface and spread out in thin rings of rainbow colour. People call them the black tears of the Arizona. Some survivors of the Arizona asked to be placed with their shipmates when they died. Navy divers carried their ashes down in small urns and placed them gently inside the hull. A bugler played on the memorial above. Not far away, at the other end of Battleship Row, lies the Missouri. That's the ship on whose deck the war ended. Now the beginning and the end of the war lie in the same harbour, side by side.

Every year on the seventh of December, at five minutes to eight, the harbour falls silent.

[pause]

Every year, on the seventh of December, there's a ceremony at Pearl Harbor. If you're there, you'll hear only the water and the wind. People gather beside the water early in the morning. There are sailors and soldiers and their families. There are visitors from the mainland, and from Japan. At five minutes to eight, the moment the first planes came over the hills, there's a minute of silence. A ship in the harbour sounds its whistle. For many years, the survivors came to the ceremony in their aloha shirts and caps. They sat in the front row, and the crowd stood and applauded them. Now there are only a very few left. Honolulu has grown into a busy modern city. Yet the island is still beautiful. The green mountains still rise into the clouds. Rainbows still come and go over the valleys. The trade winds still blow, and the surfers still ride the long smooth waves at Waikiki. And visitors arriving on the island are still greeted with flowers, and with aloha.
