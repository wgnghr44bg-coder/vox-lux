Tonight we are going about eighty-five years back, to a green island in the middle of the Pacific Ocean.

[long-pause]

It is early morning, and the sun is coming up over the sea.

[pause]

The island rises steeply out of the water, with long green mountain ridges running down its spine, their tops hidden in soft white clouds. Rain falls gently on the high slopes, and rainbows come and go over the valleys. Along the shore, the waves break white on the coral reefs, and the water inside them is pale green and turquoise and very clear.

[pause]

If calm stories from history help you rest, you are welcome to subscribe to the channel. Now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

The island is called Oahu, and it is one of the islands of Hawaii.

[pause]

In 1941, Hawaii is not yet a state of the United States. It is a territory, a faraway part of the country, more than two thousand miles from California. To get here, most people come by ship, on a voyage of four or five days across the open Pacific. A few come by the new flying boats, the Clippers, which land in the harbour after a long flight of nearly twenty hours.

[pause]

{img:passenger-liner-arriving-honolulu-harbour}
About four hundred thousand people live on the islands, most of them on Oahu.

[long-pause]

The arrival of a passenger ship in Honolulu is a great event.

[pause]

The big white liners, with names like the Lurline and the Matsonia, come in to the harbour in the morning, past the lighthouse at Diamond Head, the great old volcanic crater that stands at the eastern end of the beaches. Boys swim out to meet the ship, and dive for the coins that the passengers throw down into the clear water.

[pause]

{img:aloha-tower-boat-day-crowds-leis}
At the pier, beside the tall white clock tower called the Aloha Tower, a crowd is waiting. A band plays Hawaiian music, with guitars and ukuleles and soft singing. There are hula dancers in grass skirts. And as the passengers come down the gangway, people put leis around their necks, garlands of fresh flowers, plumeria and ginger and orchids, sweet-smelling and cool, and say aloha, which means hello, and goodbye, and love.

[pause]

People call this Boat Day. The whole town comes down to watch.

[long-pause]

The air in Hawaii is soft and warm, all year round.

[pause]

It is never very hot, and never cold. The trade winds blow gently from the northeast almost every day, cool and fresh off the ocean, rustling the palm trees and carrying the clouds over the mountains. Short showers of rain come and go, often while the sun is still shining, and leave the air smelling of wet earth and flowers.

[pause]

There are flowers everywhere: hibiscus, red and yellow and pink, in every garden; bougainvillea climbing over the walls; plumeria trees dropping their white and yellow blossoms on the paths; great flame trees, covered in scarlet.

[long-pause]

The people of the islands come from many places.

[pause]

There are Native Hawaiians, whose ancestors sailed here in great canoes, more than a thousand years ago, navigating by the stars. There are people whose families came from Japan, a great many of them, more than a third of everyone in the islands, and from China, Korea, the Philippines and Portugal, to work on the sugar and pineapple plantations, two or three generations ago. And there are Americans from the mainland, businessmen and teachers, soldiers and sailors and their families.

[pause]

{img:plantation-workers-pineapple-fields-mountains}
They live side by side, in the little towns and the plantation villages and the neighbourhoods of Honolulu. Their children go to school together. They eat each other's food, and speak a mixture of each other's languages, which the islanders call pidgin.

[long-pause]

In the old Hawaiian way of life, the island was divided into long slices of land, running from the tops of the mountains down to the sea.

[pause]

Each slice was called an ahupua'a, and it held everything a community needed: the forests on the high slopes, for wood and birds' feathers; the valleys in the middle, where the taro grew in flooded terraces, watered by streams; and the shore and the reef, for fishing. The people of each ahupua'a shared what they had, and looked after the land and the water, so that there would always be enough.

[pause]

In 1941, in some of the quiet valleys on the windward side of the island, the old taro terraces are still green and full of water, and families still tend them, in the old way, standing knee-deep in the cool mud, under the cliffs, with the rain drifting down the mountains above them.

[long-pause]

Much of the island is covered with plantations.

[pause]

On the flat land in the middle, between the two mountain ranges, there are great green fields of sugar cane, taller than a man, swaying in the wind. On the red earth of the uplands, there are fields of pineapples, in long, straight rows, stretching away to the foot of the mountains.

[pause]

The workers go out to the fields early in the morning, in wide straw hats and long sleeves and gloves, to protect themselves from the sun and the sharp leaves. In the evening, they come home to their little wooden houses in the plantation camps, where their families are waiting, and the smell of cooking rice drifts across the yards.

[long-pause]

{img:pearl-harbor-lochs-from-hills-morning}
On the southern side of the island, a few miles west of Honolulu, there is a great natural harbour.

[pause]

It is shaped a little like a hand with spread fingers: a wide, calm inlet of the sea, with several long arms of water reaching inland among low green hills and sugar fields. In the middle of it lies a flat little island.

[pause]

The Hawaiians called this place Wai Momi, which means pearl waters, because long ago, oysters with pearls in them were found in its shallow lagoons.

[pause]

Today, everyone calls it Pearl Harbor.

[long-pause]

{img:honolulu-street-1941-shops-palms}
Honolulu, in 1941, is a pleasant, easy-going town of about two hundred thousand people.

[pause]

Its downtown streets are lined with low buildings of stone and wood, with deep shady verandas and balconies. There are banks and offices, department stores and shops, cinemas and restaurants, and the old palace of the Hawaiian kings, a handsome building with tall columns and wide verandas, set among great banyan trees, where the government of the territory now has its offices.

[long-pause]

The streets are busy, but never in a hurry.

[pause]

People stroll along the pavements in light, loose clothes: the men in white or pale suits, or in short-sleeved shirts printed with flowers and palm trees, which the islanders call aloha shirts. The women wear cotton dresses in bright colours, and some of them tuck a flower behind one ear. There are buses and trolley buses, and old cars with their windows rolled down.

[pause]

{img:fish-market-chinatown-morning-1941}
In the mornings, the fish market in Chinatown is crowded with people buying the night's catch: tuna and mahi-mahi and red snapper, laid out on beds of ice. In the little shops nearby, there are dried fish and noodles, ginger and rice, and fruit of every kind: mangoes and papayas, bananas and lychees, pineapples sold whole, or sliced on a stick.

[long-pause]

A few miles east of downtown is Waikiki Beach.

[pause]

It is a long curve of golden sand, lined with palm trees, with Diamond Head standing at the far end of it, and the warm blue sea in front. The waves roll in slowly from the reef, long and smooth and gentle, perfect for swimming and for surfing.

[pause]

{img:waikiki-beach-1941-surfers-diamond-head}
On the beach, the young Hawaiian beachboys teach the visitors to ride the waves on long wooden surfboards, and take them out in outrigger canoes, paddling out beyond the reef and then riding a big wave all the way back to the sand, with everybody shouting and laughing.

[pause]

The most famous surfer of them all is Duke Kahanamoku, a tall, gentle Hawaiian who won gold medals for swimming at the Olympic Games, and who has taught people all over the world to surf.

[long-pause]

Along the beach stand a few grand hotels.

[pause]

The most famous is the Royal Hawaiian, which everyone calls the Pink Palace of the Pacific. It is a huge, rambling building, painted pale pink, with towers and arches in a Spanish style, set among gardens of palms and flowers, right on the sand.

[pause]

The rich and famous come here from the mainland, film stars and businessmen and their families, and stay for weeks at a time. In the evenings, they dine on the terrace under the stars, while a band plays soft Hawaiian music, and the waves murmur on the beach a few yards away.

[long-pause]

But most of the people of Honolulu live much more simply.

[pause]

They live in small wooden houses with corrugated iron roofs and deep porches, in neighbourhoods that climb up the green valleys behind the town, among gardens full of banana plants and papaya trees and flowers. In the evening, when the trade winds bring a little rain down the valleys, they sit on their porches and listen to the rain on the roof, and the radio playing inside.

[pause]

{img:wooden-house-porch-valley-rain-evening}
The children walk to school barefoot, or in rubber slippers. After school, they swim at the beach, or fish off the piers, or climb the mango trees in their neighbours' yards. At weekends, families go on picnics to the beaches on the other side of the island, where the sand is white and the water is very clear.

[long-pause]

Food in Honolulu is a mixture of everything.

[pause]

There is Hawaiian food, poi made from pounded taro root, and laulau, pork and fish wrapped in leaves and steamed, and lomi salmon with tomatoes and onions. There is Japanese food, rice and miso soup and sushi and noodles. There is Chinese food, and Korean food, and Filipino food, and Portuguese sweet bread and spicy sausage, and American hamburgers and hot dogs and apple pie.

[pause]

At lunchtime, the plantation workers open their tin lunch boxes and share what is inside with each other, a little of this and a little of that, so that everybody gets to taste everything. In the evenings, families sit down to rice with a bit of everything on top, and a big bowl of fresh fruit in the middle of the table.

[long-pause]

Radio is very important in the islands.

[pause]

There are two radio stations in Honolulu, and they broadcast music and news and stories all day long. Many evenings, there is a programme of Hawaiian music, with slide guitars and ukuleles and soft, sweet voices, singing old songs about the islands, about the moon over the sea, about love and parting.

[pause]

On some nights, when aeroplanes are flying out from California to Hawaii, one of the stations stays on the air all night, playing music, so that the pilots can follow its signal across the dark ocean to the islands.

[long-pause]

{img:hawaiian-musicians-ukulele-evening-lanai}
In the evenings, people go to the cinema, or to dances, or to a lu'au, a Hawaiian feast, with a whole pig cooked in an underground oven of hot stones, and poi, and fish wrapped in leaves, and fresh pineapple and coconut, and music and hula late into the night.

[pause]

Or they simply walk along the beach at Waikiki, in the moonlight, with the sound of the waves and the warm wind in the palms.

[long-pause]

The island is full of soldiers and sailors, too.

[pause]

There are big army bases in the middle of the island, and air fields, and the great navy base at Pearl Harbor. More than forty thousand soldiers, sailors and airmen are stationed on Oahu in 1941. On weekends, the streets of Honolulu are full of young men in uniform, the soldiers in khaki, the sailors in white, looking for something to do.

[pause]

{img:sailors-in-whites-walking-honolulu-street}
For most of them, it is the most beautiful place they have ever seen. They write home to their families about the beaches and the flowers and the warm sea, and send postcards of hula girls and palm trees, and tell their mothers not to worry, because Hawaii is like paradise.

[long-pause]

{img:battleship-row-ford-island-morning-calm}
In 1941, Pearl Harbor is the home of the United States Pacific Fleet.

[pause]

For many years, the fleet was based in California, at San Diego and Long Beach. But in the spring of 1940, the government in Washington decided to move it out to Hawaii, far out in the Pacific, to show that America was watching what was happening in Asia. And it has stayed here ever since.

[long-pause]

The harbour is full of ships.

[pause]

There are great grey battleships, the biggest and most powerful ships in the navy, each one more than six hundred feet long, with tall masts like steel towers, and huge guns in turrets on their decks. There are cruisers and destroyers, long and slim and fast. There are submarines, and supply ships, and repair ships, and tugboats, and a hospital ship, painted white.

[pause]

{img:destroyers-moored-nests-harbour-hills}
The destroyers are moored side by side, in little groups called nests, four or five together, in the arms of the harbour. The submarines lie at their own base, on the eastern side. The aircraft carriers, when they are in port, tie up on the western side of the island in the middle.

[long-pause]

That island is Ford Island.

[pause]

It is low and flat, about a mile and a half long, covered with grass and palm trees, with a big naval air station on it: runways, hangars, a control tower, and rows of seaplanes drawn up on the ramps. There are houses for the officers and their families, with gardens and lawns and swimming pools, and children playing in the streets.

[pause]

Along the southeastern side of Ford Island, there is a row of big concrete mooring posts, set in the water, a little way out from the shore. Here, the great battleships tie up, one behind another, in a long line, sometimes two side by side.

[pause]

People call it Battleship Row.

[long-pause]

The battleships have the names of American states.

[pause]

There is the California, at the southern end of the row. Then the Maryland and the Oklahoma, side by side. Then the Tennessee and the West Virginia. Then the Arizona, with a small repair ship, the Vestal, tied up alongside her. And at the northern end, the Nevada.

[pause]

{img:battleship-arizona-moored-1941-calm}
In the big dry dock across the channel, where ships are lifted out of the water to be repaired, lies the Pennsylvania, the flagship of the fleet.

[long-pause]

The Arizona is one of the older battleships.

[pause]

She was built in New York during the First World War, and launched in 1915. She is six hundred and eight feet long, and carries twelve great fourteen-inch guns, in four turrets. Her crew is about fifteen hundred men.

[pause]

She has been modernized several times since she was built, with new engines and new guns and a tall tripod mast. But she is still the same proud old ship, and her crew love her.

[long-pause]

{img:navy-yard-workers-cranes-dry-dock}
Across the water from Ford Island, on the shore of the harbour, is the Navy Yard.

[pause]

It is like a small town. There are workshops and warehouses, offices and power stations, dry docks and cranes, a hospital and a fire station, a hall for sports and concerts, houses and barracks, a post office and a cinema. Thousands of men and women work here, civilians and sailors, repairing and supplying the ships.

[pause]

The noise of hammers and riveting guns goes on all day, and the great cranes swing slowly back and forth against the sky.

[long-pause]

Around the harbour, there are other bases too.

[pause]

To the southeast is Hickam Field, a big army air base, with long runways and huge hangars, and rows of bombers parked wing to wing on the grass. In the middle of the island is Wheeler Field, for the army's fighter planes, and Schofield Barracks, the biggest army post in America, with thousands of soldiers. On the other side of the island, at Kaneohe Bay, there is a navy air station for the big flying boats.

[pause]

All of them together make Oahu one of the most important military places in the world.

[long-pause]

For the children who live on Ford Island, it is a wonderful place to grow up.

[pause]

They ride their bicycles along the quiet roads under the palm trees, and swim in the pool at the officers' club, and fish off the sea walls. They know the names of all the ships, and they can tell them apart by the shapes of their masts. In the mornings, they hear the bands playing on the battleships for the raising of the flags, and in the evenings, the bugles sounding across the water.

[pause]

When they want to go into Honolulu, they take a little ferry across the channel to the mainland side of the harbour, and then a bus into town.

[long-pause]

The commander of the Pacific Fleet is Admiral Husband Kimmel.

[pause]

He is fifty-nine years old, a tall, handsome, hardworking man with white hair, from Kentucky, who has spent his whole life in the navy. He is serious and careful, and he expects a great deal of his officers and men. He lives in a house on a hill above the harbour, from which he can look down on his ships.

[pause]

{img:admiral-office-window-harbour-view}
The commander of the army in Hawaii, whose job is to defend the islands and the base, is General Walter Short. The two men play golf together on Sunday mornings, when they can.

[long-pause]

In the evenings, the harbour is peaceful.

[pause]

The sun goes down behind the Waianae Mountains, to the west, in a blaze of orange and pink, and the calm water of the harbour turns to gold, and then to silver, and then to deep blue. The lights come on, on the ships and on the shore, and their reflections lie in long wavering lines across the water.

[pause]

{img:harbour-sunset-ships-lights-reflections}
On the decks of the battleships, the sailors sit and smoke and talk in the cool evening air. The sound of a radio drifts across the water from one of the ships, playing dance music. A launch putters across the harbour, taking officers ashore. And from the shore comes the chirping of frogs and crickets, and the scent of flowers.

[pause]

It is very quiet, and very beautiful.

[long-pause]

{img:reveille-sailors-waking-bunks-ship}
Life on board a battleship begins early.

[pause]

At about half past five in the morning, a bugle sounds over the loudspeakers, and a voice calls out: Reveille, reveille, all hands heave out and trice up. All through the ship, in the long, crowded sleeping compartments below the decks, hundreds of young men climb out of their bunks, rubbing their eyes and yawning.

[long-pause]

The sleeping compartments are hot and stuffy, even at night, and full of the sound of fans and the hum of the ship's machinery.

[pause]

The men sleep in narrow canvas bunks, stacked three or four high, with only a few inches between them. Each man has a small locker for his clothes and belongings: his uniforms, his shaving kit, his letters from home, a photograph of his girl, a Bible, a deck of cards.

[pause]

{img:sailors-washing-shaving-ship-washroom}
They wash and shave quickly in the washrooms, in a long line of steel basins, and put on their working clothes: blue denim trousers and light blue shirts, with their white caps pushed to the back of their heads.

[long-pause]

Then comes breakfast.

[pause]

The men eat in the mess decks, sitting on benches at long tables, which are folded up out of the way when not in use. The food is plentiful and filling: scrambled eggs, bacon, sausages, fried potatoes, pancakes with syrup, toast, and big mugs of strong coffee. On Sundays, there are often baked beans and cornbread.

[pause]

{img:mess-deck-long-tables-breakfast-sailors}
The cooks have been up since three in the morning, baking bread and frying bacon in enormous pans in the galley. A battleship has to feed more than a thousand men, three times a day, every day.

[long-pause]

After breakfast, the work of the day begins.

[pause]

There is always something to be done on a ship. The decks must be scrubbed, every morning, with salt water and brushes and blocks of sandstone called holystones, until the wooden planks are white and smooth. The brass must be polished until it shines. The paintwork must be scraped and painted, again and again, because the salt air of the sea eats away at the steel.

[pause]

There are drills, too: gun drills, fire drills, drills for abandoning ship. The men practise loading the great guns, with dummy shells, again and again, until they can do it in the dark.

[long-pause]

Every man on the ship has his own job.

[pause]

There are gunners and signalmen, radiomen and electricians, machinists and firemen, who look after the boilers and engines deep in the ship. There are cooks and bakers, barbers and tailors, laundrymen and storekeepers. There is a doctor and a dentist, a chaplain and a band.

[pause]

The ship is like a small floating town, with everything it needs on board.

[long-pause]

{img:ship-band-practising-deck-instruments}
Every battleship has its own band.

[pause]

The band of the Arizona is made up of twenty-one young men, most of them in their early twenties, who trained together at the navy's music school in Washington. They play every morning at eight o'clock, when the flag is raised over the ship, and at concerts and dances and ceremonies. In the evenings, some of them play jazz and swing, the popular music of the day, for the other sailors.

[pause]

They are good, and they are proud of it.

[long-pause]

On Sundays, life on board is easier.

[pause]

There is no work, except what must be done. There is a church service on deck in the morning, with a chaplain in his robes standing in front of a little altar, and the sailors sitting on benches and folding chairs, singing hymns in the open air, with the band playing and the sea birds wheeling overhead.

[pause]

After the service, there is a big Sunday lunch. Then the men are free for the rest of the day. They can sleep, or read, or write letters, or go ashore if they have liberty. Many of them simply sit on the deck in the shade, with the warm wind blowing, looking out at the green hills around the harbour, and talking quietly about home.

[long-pause]

Most of the sailors are very young.

[pause]

Many of them joined the navy straight out of high school, at seventeen or eighteen. They come from farms in Iowa and Kansas, from mining towns in Pennsylvania and West Virginia, from the cities of the East and the West. For many of them, the navy is a steady job, with good food and a bed and a chance to see the world, at a time when jobs at home are hard to find.

[pause]

{img:young-sailor-writing-letter-bunk-lamp}
On board the Arizona, there are several sets of brothers, serving together on the same ship. There is even a father and son.

[long-pause]

In the afternoons, when the work is done, there is time to relax.

[pause]

The men write letters, read magazines, play cards or checkers, listen to the radio, and shine their shoes. Some of them lie on the deck in the shade, under the big guns, and sleep. Some of them fish over the side. Some of them go for a swim, if they are allowed.

[pause]

{img:sailors-relaxing-deck-shade-afternoon}
In the evenings, there are films, shown on a big white screen set up on the deck, under the stars. The men sit on the deck in rows, in their white uniforms, watching the latest picture from Hollywood, while the warm trade wind blows across the harbour.

[long-pause]

At ten o'clock, the bugle sounds taps, and the lights go out.

[pause]

The men climb back into their bunks. The ship grows quiet, except for the hum of the machinery and the footsteps of the men on watch. Up on deck, the officer of the deck walks slowly back and forth, looking out across the dark water of the harbour, where the lights of the other ships are reflected, and the stars shine overhead.

[pause]

And out beyond the harbour mouth, the long Pacific swell rolls in from the darkness, and breaks softly on the reef.

[long-pause]

{img:texas-farm-family-1930s-porch}
On board the battleship West Virginia, there is a young man named Doris Miller.

[pause]

Everyone calls him Dorie. He is twenty-two years old in 1941, very big and very strong, more than six feet tall and over two hundred pounds, with a broad, friendly face and a quiet, easy smile.

[long-pause]

Dorie was born in 1919, near the city of Waco, in Texas, the third of four sons of a family of Black farmers.

[pause]

His parents, Conery and Henrietta Miller, worked a small farm, growing cotton and vegetables, and raising chickens and pigs. Dorie grew up working in the fields with his brothers, from early in the morning until the sun went down. He was a good student, and he loved sports. At school, he played fullback on the football team, and he was so strong and so fast that the other boys called him the Raging Bull.

[pause]

He was a quiet, gentle boy, who helped his mother around the house, went to church every Sunday, and liked to go hunting for squirrels in the woods with his father's old rifle. People who knew him said he was a crack shot.

[long-pause]

When he was nineteen, Dorie decided to join the navy.

[pause]

Jobs were hard to find in Texas in those years, and the navy offered steady pay, and a chance to send money home to his family, and to see the world. In September 1939, he went to the recruiting office in Dallas and signed up.

[pause]

But in 1939, the United States Navy was not open to Black men in the same way as to white men. They could not become gunners or radiomen or machinists or officers. The only job open to them was in the messman branch: cooking and serving food to the officers, cleaning their cabins, washing their clothes, and polishing their shoes.

[long-pause]

{img:mess-attendant-white-jacket-wardroom}
So Dorie became a mess attendant.

[pause]

He was trained at a navy school in Virginia, and then sent to the West Virginia, one of the great battleships of the Pacific Fleet. He worked in the officers' wardroom and their quarters, in a white jacket, carrying trays and setting tables, making beds and collecting laundry.

[pause]

He did his work well, and quietly, and the officers liked him. He was always polite and cheerful, and he never complained.

[long-pause]

Dorie had one great passion on board the ship.

[pause]

He was the heavyweight boxing champion of the West Virginia.

[pause]

{img:shipboard-boxing-ring-sailors-watching}
The navy loved boxing, and every ship had its own team, and its own matches, held in a ring set up on the deck, with hundreds of sailors sitting all around, cheering. Dorie was big and strong and quick on his feet, and he had a powerful punch. He won match after match. His shipmates were very proud of him.

[long-pause]

On board, he was popular with everyone.

[pause]

He was easy-going and good-humoured. He liked to joke and to tell stories about Texas. He liked to play cards in the evenings, and to listen to music on the radio. He wrote letters home to his parents, and sent them part of his pay every month.

[pause]

{img:sailor-writing-letter-home-mess-table}
His friends on the ship were the other mess attendants, young Black men like himself, from Texas and Louisiana and Georgia and Virginia. They worked together, and ate together, and slept in the same small compartment, and looked out for one another.

[long-pause]

In the summer of 1941, the West Virginia was in Pearl Harbor, with the rest of the fleet.

[pause]

On his days ashore, Dorie liked to go into Honolulu with his friends. They walked along the beaches, and went to the cinema, and ate at the little restaurants in the town. Some of them liked to go dancing. Dorie preferred to watch.

[pause]

He thought Hawaii was beautiful. He wrote to his family that he liked it there, and that the weather was always fine.

[long-pause]

In 1940, the West Virginia sailed with the fleet on long exercises at sea, and visited other ports in the Pacific.

[pause]

Dorie saw the open ocean for the first time, endless and blue, with flying fish leaping from the waves and dolphins racing alongside the ship. He stood at the rail at night and watched the phosphorescence glowing in the water, and the stars, more of them than he had ever seen in Texas.

[pause]

He wrote to his brothers that the sea was even bigger than he had imagined.

[long-pause]

He had plans for the future.

[pause]

He was going to finish his time in the navy, and then go home to Texas, and help his parents on the farm. Perhaps, one day, he would have a farm of his own, and a family. He was saving a little money every month, for that.

[pause]

{img:battleship-wardroom-evening-lamps}
In the evenings, after the officers had finished their dinner and the wardroom was cleaned, Dorie sometimes stood on the deck, by the rail, in the warm darkness, looking out at the lights of the other ships and the shore, and thinking about home.

[long-pause]

On Saturday, the sixth of December, Dorie was on board the West Virginia, as usual.

[pause]

It was a quiet day. He did his work, and had his supper, and played a few hands of cards with his friends. Then he went down to his bunk, and went to sleep early.

[pause]

On Sunday morning, he planned to get up before dawn, as he always did, to collect the officers' laundry.

[long-pause]

{img:liberty-launch-sailors-going-ashore}
When a sailor at Pearl Harbor has a day off, he goes on liberty.

[pause]

On weekends, the liberty boats come and go all day, small launches puttering across the harbour from the battleships to the landing at the Navy Yard, packed with young men in their crisp white uniforms, laughing and talking, with their caps pushed to the backs of their heads.

[pause]

From the Navy Yard, they take a bus or a taxi into Honolulu, about eight miles away along a road through the sugar cane fields.

[long-pause]

In Honolulu, most of the sailors head for Hotel Street.

[pause]

It is a busy, noisy street in the middle of the old town, lined with bars and cafés, cheap restaurants and shooting galleries, photograph studios and souvenir shops and tattoo parlours. It is crowded with sailors and soldiers from morning till night, walking up and down, looking in the windows, buying postcards and souvenirs to send home.

[pause]

{img:hotel-street-1941-neon-sailors-evening}
There are photographers who will take your picture with a hula girl, or sitting in a fake aeroplane, so that you can send it to your mother. There are tattoo artists who will draw an anchor or a ship, or the name of your sweetheart, on your arm. There are little restaurants serving hamburgers and chili and pie, and the Black Cat Café, across from the YMCA, which is open day and night.

[long-pause]

Other sailors prefer quieter things.

[pause]

They go to the beach at Waikiki, and swim in the warm sea, and lie on the sand in the sun. Some of them learn to surf. Some of them rent bicycles and ride around the island, past the pineapple fields and the plantation villages, to the quiet beaches on the far side, where there is nobody else at all.

[pause]

{img:sailors-swimming-waikiki-1941-sun}
Some of them go to the YMCA, which has a pool and a reading room and a cafeteria, and cheap rooms where they can stay the night. Some of them go to church, or to the library, or to the zoo.

[long-pause]

Many of the sailors spend their liberty writing letters.

[pause]

They sit in the cafés, or on benches in the parks, or on the beach, and write to their mothers and fathers, their brothers and sisters, their wives and their girlfriends. They tell them about the islands, and the flowers, and the beaches, and the food. They tell them about their ship and their friends. They ask about everyone at home, and about the dog, and the farm, and the football team.

[pause]

In December, the letters are full of Christmas. The sailors buy presents in the shops of Honolulu, grass skirts and ukuleles and silk scarves printed with flowers, and pack them carefully and send them home by the ship to California. And the parcels from home begin to arrive, too, full of fruitcake and cookies and warm socks, which the sailors open in their bunks and share with their friends.

[long-pause]

For the married men, liberty means going home.

[pause]

Some of the men in the fleet, especially the older ones and the officers, have their families with them in Hawaii. They live in small rented houses and apartments in Honolulu and in the towns around the harbour, or in the navy housing on Ford Island and at the Navy Yard.

[pause]

{img:navy-family-small-house-garden-evening}
When the men come home from the ship in the evening, their wives have supper waiting, and the children run down the path to meet them. At weekends, the families go to the beach, or for a drive around the island, or to a picnic in the park. In the evening, they sit on the porch and listen to the radio, and watch the children playing on the lawn.

[long-pause]

Some of the sailors take tours of the island.

[pause]

They ride in open buses up to the Nuuanu Pali, a high pass in the mountains behind Honolulu, where the cliffs drop away steeply on the other side, and the whole windward coast lies spread out below, green and blue, with the wind blowing so hard that it can almost lift a man off his feet. They visit the pineapple cannery, where the workers give them free pineapple juice. They drive around the north shore, where the great winter waves roll in, as tall as houses, and crash on the reefs in clouds of white spray.

[pause]

At the end of the day, they come back to the ship sunburnt and tired, with their pockets full of postcards.

[long-pause]

Many of the sailors have girlfriends in Honolulu, too.

[pause]

There are dances every weekend, at the clubs and the hotels and the dance halls, with big bands playing the latest swing music. The young men in their white uniforms and the young women in their best dresses dance the jitterbug and the foxtrot until late in the night, and then walk slowly home along the beach, in the moonlight, under the palms.

[long-pause]

{img:bloch-arena-band-competition-crowd}
In the autumn of 1941, there is a special event that all the sailors talk about.

[pause]

It is called the Battle of Music. It is a competition between the bands of the ships of the fleet, held every few weeks at Bloch Arena, a big new hall at the Navy Yard. Each band plays a programme of music, and then they all dance to it, and the audience votes for the best one.

[pause]

The sailors cheer for the band of their own ship, as if it were a football team. The rivalry is fierce, and friendly. The final is to be held just before Christmas, on the twentieth of December.

[pause]

The band of the Arizona has already won its place in the final.

[long-pause]

At the end of liberty, the sailors go back to the landing, and wait for the launch to take them back to their ships.

[pause]

{img:launch-crossing-harbour-night-lights}
They sit in the boat, tired and happy, with their arms full of parcels, as it putters slowly across the dark harbour, past the great grey shapes of the battleships with their lights shining. They climb up the ladders onto the decks, and go down to their bunks, and sleep.

[pause]

And the next morning, the bugle sounds, and the work begins again.

[long-pause]

{img:family-radio-news-living-room-1941}
In the autumn of 1941, the world beyond the islands is at war.

[pause]

In Europe, the war has been going on for more than two years. Germany has conquered most of the continent, from France to Poland, and in the summer of 1941, it invaded the Soviet Union. Britain stands alone in the west, under attack from the air, and fighting for its life at sea.

[long-pause]

In Asia, too, there is war.

[pause]

For four years, Japan has been fighting in China, and its armies have conquered much of the coast and the great cities. In 1940 and 1941, Japanese troops moved south, into French Indochina, the lands that are now Vietnam and Laos and Cambodia.

[pause]

{img:newspaper-headlines-cafe-reading-1941}
The United States is not at war. Most Americans do not want to go to war. They remember the First World War, only twenty-five years ago, and they hope that their country can stay out of this one.

[pause]

But the American government is very worried about what is happening, in Europe and in Asia, and it has begun to prepare. It is building ships and planes and tanks, and calling up young men into the army.

[long-pause]

Relations between the United States and Japan have grown worse and worse.

[pause]

Japan needs oil, and steel, and rubber, for its factories and its armies. It has very little of its own. Much of its oil has come from the United States. In the summer of 1941, to try to stop Japan's armies in Asia, the American government stopped selling oil to Japan.

[pause]

Without oil, Japan's navy and army will soon run out of fuel. Its leaders must decide what to do. They can give up their conquests in China and Indochina, as the Americans demand. Or they can go to war, to take the oil fields of the Dutch East Indies, far to the south, by force.

[pause]

All through the autumn, Japanese and American diplomats meet in Washington, and talk, and talk, trying to find a way out. But they cannot agree.

[long-pause]

In Japan, the commander of the combined fleet is Admiral Isoroku Yamamoto.

[pause]

He is fifty-seven years old, a short, broad-shouldered, thoughtful man, who studied at Harvard University in America as a young officer, and later served as a naval attaché in Washington. He knows America well. He has travelled across it, and seen its great factories and its oil fields, and he admires it.

[pause]

{img:admiral-study-calligraphy-quiet-evening}
He does not want war with the United States. He believes that Japan cannot win a long war against such a large and powerful country. He has said so, many times, to his country's leaders.

[pause]

But if there must be a war, he believes that Japan's only chance is to strike first, and to strike hard, at the American fleet at Pearl Harbor, before it can sail west across the Pacific.

[long-pause]

So, in the autumn of 1941, in great secrecy, a Japanese fleet is gathered.

[pause]

There are six aircraft carriers, the largest ships in the Japanese navy, carrying more than four hundred planes. There are battleships and cruisers and destroyers to protect them, and tankers to carry their fuel.

[pause]

{img:northern-bay-snow-ships-anchored-fog}
They gather in a remote, cold bay in the Kuril Islands, in the far north of Japan, where the hills are covered in snow and the fog lies thick over the water, and no one can see them.

[pause]

On the twenty-sixth of November, the fleet sails out of the bay, into the North Pacific, and turns east, toward Hawaii.

[long-pause]

It takes a long, lonely route, far to the north of the usual shipping lanes, through cold and stormy seas, where no other ships are likely to see it. The ships keep radio silence. They burn no lights at night.

[pause]

The young pilots on the carriers spend the long days of the voyage studying maps and models of Pearl Harbor, and writing letters to their families, and cleaning their planes.

[pause]

Many of them are not much older than the American sailors in the harbour they are sailing toward. They, too, have parents at home, and brothers and sisters, and sweethearts. They, too, are a little afraid.

[long-pause]

In Hawaii, nobody knows that the fleet is coming.

[pause]

The American commanders know that war with Japan may come very soon. On the twenty-seventh of November, they receive a warning from Washington. But most people believe that, if Japan attacks, it will attack far away, in the Philippines, or in Malaya, or in the Dutch East Indies. Nobody really thinks that Japan would dare to come all the way across the Pacific, to Hawaii.

[pause]

{img:army-planes-parked-wing-to-wing-field}
At the army air fields, the planes are parked close together in long, neat rows, wing tip to wing tip, so that they can be guarded easily against anyone who might try to damage them on the ground.

[long-pause]

And so, in the first week of December, life in Hawaii goes on as usual.

[pause]

The ships come and go. The sailors go on liberty. The bands practise for the Battle of Music. The families go to the beach. In Honolulu, the shops are full of Christmas decorations, and Christmas trees have arrived by ship from the mainland, and are standing in rows outside the stores.

[pause]

{img:christmas-trees-honolulu-store-palm}
The aircraft carriers of the Pacific Fleet are away at sea, carrying planes to the little islands of Wake and Midway, far to the west. But the battleships are in the harbour, as they usually are on weekends, lined up in their long row beside Ford Island.

[pause]

The weather is fine, and the trade winds blow, and the nights are warm and starry.

[long-pause]

{img:saturday-morning-harbour-sunny-calm}
Saturday, the sixth of December, 1941, is a lovely day in Hawaii.

[pause]

The sky is clear and blue, with a few soft white clouds over the mountains. The trade wind blows gently. The temperature is in the high seventies, warm but not hot. It is a perfect day for the beach.

[long-pause]

At Pearl Harbor, it is a quiet Saturday.

[pause]

On the battleships, there is an inspection in the morning, as there is every Saturday. The men stand in neat rows on the decks, in their best white uniforms, while the captain walks slowly along the lines, looking at their uniforms and their shoes and their haircuts, and then goes below to inspect the compartments, running a white-gloved finger along the shelves to check for dust.

[pause]

{img:captain-inspection-sailors-rows-deck}
After the inspection, half the crew of each ship is given liberty, until the next morning. The launches begin to fill up with sailors heading ashore.

[long-pause]

In Honolulu, the streets are full of people doing their Christmas shopping.

[pause]

The department stores are decorated with tinsel and paper bells, and there is Christmas music playing on the loudspeakers, carols about snow and sleigh bells, which sounds a little strange under the palm trees and the hot sunshine. Children stand in line to see Santa Claus, who is sitting in a big chair, sweating in his red suit and his white beard.

[pause]

In the afternoon, there is a football game at the stadium: the University of Hawaii against a team from Willamette University, in Oregon, which has come all the way across the Pacific to play. About twenty-four thousand people come to watch, and the home team wins.

[long-pause]

At Waikiki, the beach is crowded.

[pause]

Families spread their towels on the sand and open their picnic baskets. Children build sandcastles and splash in the shallow water. Young people play volleyball. Out on the waves, the surfers ride the long, gentle swells, and the outrigger canoes come racing in toward the shore, with everyone paddling and shouting.

[pause]

{img:waikiki-saturday-families-picnic-sand}
In the late afternoon, the light turns golden, and the shadows of the palm trees stretch out across the sand. The families pack up and go home, sunburnt and tired and happy.

[long-pause]

On the West Virginia, Dorie Miller is on board, doing his work in the officers' quarters.

[pause]

On the Arizona, many of the crew have gone ashore, but many others have stayed on board. Some of them are writing letters. Some are reading, or playing cards, or sleeping. Some are listening to the radio, which is playing dance music from one of the Honolulu stations.

[long-pause]

In the evening, a big crowd gathers at Bloch Arena, at the Navy Yard, for the Battle of Music.

[pause]

{img:band-on-stage-arena-sailors-dancing}
Tonight, four bands are competing, from the battleships Pennsylvania and Tennessee, the cruiser Detroit, and the auxiliary ship Argonne. The hall is packed with sailors and their girlfriends and wives, all dressed up for the evening.

[pause]

Each band plays its programme, swing tunes and popular songs and a few marches. Then there is a jitterbug contest, with couples dancing wildly across the floor, while the crowd claps and cheers.

[pause]

The band of the Arizona is there, sitting in the audience, cheering for the others. They have already won their place in the final, which is to be held on the twentieth of December, and they are looking forward to it very much.

[long-pause]

At the end of the evening, the winner is announced: the band of the Pennsylvania.

[pause]

Then, as the last dance ends, all the bands stand up together, and the whole hall sings God Bless America. People will remember that moment for the rest of their lives.

[pause]

Afterwards, the musicians of the Arizona walk back together through the warm night to the landing, and take the launch back across the harbour to their ship. They go down to their bunks, and go to sleep.

[long-pause]

All over Oahu, people are enjoying their Saturday night.

[pause]

In the hotels at Waikiki, there are dinners and dances, with bands playing on the terraces under the stars. At the officers' clubs, there are parties. In the cinemas of Honolulu, people are watching the latest films. On the army bases, the soldiers are playing cards and listening to the radio.

[pause]

{img:hotel-terrace-dance-night-stars-palms}
Admiral Kimmel goes to a small dinner party at the Halekulani Hotel, at Waikiki, and leaves early, because he likes to be in bed by ten o'clock. In the morning, he plans to play golf with General Short.

[long-pause]

On the ships in the harbour, the lights are dimmed, and the sailors on watch walk slowly up and down the decks.

[pause]

It is a warm, still, beautiful night. The moon is bright, and it shines down on the harbour, turning the water to silver, and casting the shadows of the great ships across it. The palm trees on Ford Island stand black against the sky. Somewhere, a dog barks, and a car drives slowly along the shore road.

[pause]

{img:moonlit-harbour-battleships-silhouettes}
On the hills above Honolulu, one of the radio stations is still on the air, playing soft Hawaiian music, all through the night, so that a flight of army bombers coming from California can find its way to the islands in the dark.

[long-pause]

And about two hundred and fifty miles to the north, in the darkness, the Japanese fleet is sailing south, toward Oahu, at full speed.

[pause]

On the carriers, the pilots are trying to sleep. Some of them cannot. They lie awake in their bunks, listening to the hum of the engines and the rush of the sea against the hull, thinking about the morning, and about home.

[pause]

At about half past three in the morning, they will be woken, and given breakfast, and sent up to their planes.

[long-pause]

{img:sunday-dawn-oahu-mountains-clouds-pink}
Sunday, the seventh of December, 1941, dawns soft and lovely over Oahu.

[pause]

The sun comes up a little after half past six, behind the mountains, and the sky turns from grey to pink to pale gold. There are a few clouds over the ridges, white and gentle, and the air is cool and fresh. The sea is calm. It is going to be another beautiful day.

[long-pause]

In Honolulu, most people are still asleep.

[pause]

It is Sunday morning, the one morning of the week when nobody needs to get up early. A few people are up already, the milkmen and the newspaper boys, the bakers, the people going to early Mass. Somewhere, a rooster is crowing.

[pause]

On the radio, the Hawaiian music is still playing, soft and sweet, from the station that has stayed on the air all night.

[long-pause]

Out at sea, a few miles from the entrance to Pearl Harbor, a small, old destroyer called the Ward is on patrol.

[pause]

At about a quarter to seven, her crew sees something strange in the water: the small conning tower of a little submarine, following a supply ship toward the harbour entrance. The Ward attacks it, and it disappears beneath the waves. The captain sends a message to the navy headquarters on shore, to report what has happened.

[pause]

But it is early on a Sunday morning, and the message is passed slowly from one office to another, and nobody quite believes it.

[long-pause]

{img:radar-station-hilltop-north-shore-dawn}
On the northern tip of the island, on a hill above the sea, there is a small army radar station, called Opana.

[pause]

Radar is still very new in 1941. It uses radio waves to detect aeroplanes far away, long before they can be seen or heard. There are only a few radar sets in Hawaii, and the men who use them are still learning how.

[pause]

That morning, two young privates, Joseph Lockard and George Elliott, are on duty at Opana. Their shift is supposed to end at seven o'clock. But Elliott is new, and wants to practise, and so they leave the set running for a few more minutes.

[long-pause]

At two minutes past seven, they see something on the screen.

[pause]

It is a large blip, bigger than anything they have ever seen before, about a hundred and thirty miles to the north, coming toward the island.

[pause]

{img:young-soldiers-radar-screen-hut}
They are puzzled. Lockard telephones the information centre at Fort Shafter, near Honolulu, and speaks to the only officer on duty there, a young lieutenant named Kermit Tyler.

[pause]

Lieutenant Tyler thinks for a moment. He knows that a flight of American bombers is expected that morning, coming from California. He thinks that the blip must be those bombers. He tells Lockard not to worry about it.

[pause]

The two privates watch the blip for a little longer, as it comes closer and closer, and then they switch off the set and go to breakfast.

[long-pause]

Across the island, in the little plantation villages, families are getting ready for church.

[pause]

The mothers are ironing the children's Sunday clothes. The fathers are shaving at the kitchen sink. The grandmothers are already sitting on the porch, dressed in their best, waiting. In one small town, a Japanese Buddhist temple is preparing for a Sunday school picnic. In another, a Catholic church is filling up for early Mass, and the sound of the bell drifts across the cane fields.

[pause]

On a golf course near the harbour, a few early players are already out on the green grass, in the cool of the morning, with their caddies carrying their bags.

[long-pause]

At Pearl Harbor, the ships are waking up.

[pause]

The sailors are washing and dressing, and going down to breakfast in the mess decks. It is a relaxed Sunday morning. Many of the officers and men are ashore. Those on board are looking forward to a quiet day. Some will go to church services on deck. Some will go ashore later, for a swim, or a picnic.

[pause]

{img:sailors-on-deck-soft-morning-light-ships}
On the West Virginia, Dorie Miller has been up since six o'clock, and is going around the ship collecting the officers' laundry.

[pause]

On the Arizona, the band is getting ready to play for the raising of the flag, at eight o'clock. The musicians are putting on their white uniforms, and taking out their instruments, and going up on deck, to take their places on the stern.

[long-pause]

On Ford Island, in the officers' houses, families are having breakfast.

[pause]

The children are still in their pyjamas, eating cereal and toast. The mothers are making coffee. Some of the fathers are reading the Sunday newspaper, the Honolulu Advertiser, which has comics in colour, and advertisements for Christmas presents.

[pause]

Through the windows, they can see the battleships lying in a row along the shore, grey and still on the calm blue water, with their flags not yet raised, and the morning sun shining on their decks.

[long-pause]

At about a quarter to eight, on the ships along Battleship Row, the colour guards begin to gather on the stern of each ship, with the flags folded in their arms, ready to raise them at exactly eight o'clock.

[pause]

The buglers stand ready. The bands take up their instruments. The officers of the deck look at their watches.

[pause]

{img:flag-detail-stern-morning-band-ready}
It is a peaceful, ordinary Sunday morning, just like a hundred others.

[pause]

And then, a few minutes before eight o'clock, the sailors on the decks hear the sound of aeroplanes, coming from the hills to the north, many of them, and look up.

[long-pause]

{img:distant-planes-over-green-hills-morning}
At about five minutes to eight, on the morning of the seventh of December, the planes come over the hills.

[pause]

There are about a hundred and eighty of them in the first wave, small and grey-green, with red circles on their wings, flying in neat groups across the blue sky. Some come in over the mountains from the north. Some come round the coast. They spread out toward the airfields and the harbour.

[long-pause]

At first, many people on the ground do not understand what they are seeing.

[pause]

They think it must be an exercise, a practice by the army or the navy, on a Sunday morning, of all times. Some of them are annoyed by the noise. Some of them stand and watch, curious, shading their eyes against the sun.

[pause]

{img:ford-island-control-tower-morning-distance}
Then, on Ford Island, the commander of the air station, standing outside his office, sees what is happening, and runs to the radio room. And at a few minutes before eight, a message goes out from Ford Island, to every ship and station in the Pacific, in plain words:

[pause]

Air raid, Pearl Harbor. This is not drill.

[long-pause]

What happens in the next two hours has been told many times, in many books and films.

[pause]

We will not tell it all tonight. We will only say that it was very fast, and very confusing, and very loud, and that it was over in less than two hours. The planes came in two great waves, the first a little before eight o'clock, and the second a little before nine.

[pause]

They struck at the airfields, at Hickam and Wheeler and Kaneohe and Ford Island, where the planes were parked in their neat rows. And they struck at the ships in the harbour, and above all at the great battleships lying along Battleship Row.

[long-pause]

On the decks of the ships, the bugles began to sound, and the loudspeakers called the men to their battle stations.

[pause]

The young sailors ran up the ladders from below, half dressed, many of them still in the white uniforms they had put on for the Sunday flag raising, and took their places at the guns. They fought back with whatever they had. Within a few minutes, the guns of the ships were firing up into the sky, and little puffs of black smoke were blossoming all around the planes.

[long-pause]

On the stern of the Nevada, at the northern end of Battleship Row, the band had been waiting to play the national anthem, at eight o'clock, for the raising of the flag.

[pause]

{img:navy-band-stern-flag-morning-sun}
When the planes came over, the band went on playing, all the way to the end of the anthem, while the flag went up the pole, and only then did the musicians scatter and run to their stations.

[long-pause]

At about ten minutes past eight, the Arizona was struck.

[pause]

There was a great sound, which was heard all over the harbour, and a column of smoke rose up high into the sky, above Battleship Row. The great old ship settled slowly down onto the bottom of the harbour, where she still lies today.

[pause]

{img:smoke-column-distant-over-harbour-calm}
Of the men on board the Arizona that morning, more than eleven hundred lost their lives. Among them were all twenty-one men of the ship's band, who had been at the Battle of Music the night before, and who had been getting ready to play for the flag.

[long-pause]

The Oklahoma, lying on the outside of the row, rolled slowly over onto her side, and then turned upside down, with her keel showing above the water. Hundreds of her men were trapped inside.

[pause]

The West Virginia, next to the Tennessee, settled to the bottom of the harbour on an even keel, because her officers flooded her compartments on the other side, to keep her from turning over.

[pause]

The California sank slowly, at her mooring, near the southern end. The Nevada, the only battleship that got her engines going, began to move, very slowly, down the channel toward the open sea, with her flag flying, while the sailors on the other ships cheered her on. Then, to keep her from sinking in the narrow channel and blocking the harbour, her crew ran her gently aground on the shore.

[long-pause]

Out on the airfields, rows of planes were burning, and hangars, and buildings.

[pause]

In Honolulu, people heard the sounds and saw the smoke rising over Pearl Harbor, and came out of their houses, and stood in the streets, wondering what was happening. On the radio, at about half past eight, the music stopped, and an announcer came on.

[pause]

{img:honolulu-people-street-looking-distance}
This is the real McCoy, he said. The island of Oahu is under attack. All army, navy and marine personnel report to your stations at once.

[long-pause]

By about ten o'clock in the morning, it was over.

[pause]

The last of the planes turned away, and flew north, back toward their carriers, and disappeared over the mountains. The sky was quiet again.

[pause]

{img:quiet-sky-over-harbour-late-morning-haze}
Over Pearl Harbor, great clouds of dark smoke hung in the still air, and drifted slowly away to the southwest, over the sea. The water of the harbour was covered with oil. Small boats were moving everywhere, slowly, across the water, picking up the men who had swum or jumped from the ships.

[pause]

And on the shore, and on the ships, and in the boats, thousands of men and women were already at work, helping each other.

[long-pause]

{img:sailors-helping-each-other-small-boat}
In the middle of that terrible morning, there were many acts of courage and kindness.

[pause]

Most of them were done by ordinary people, young sailors and soldiers, nurses and workers, who had never imagined that they would be tested in such a way, and who simply did what needed to be done, for the people around them.

[long-pause]

On the West Virginia, Dorie Miller was below decks, collecting laundry, when the alarm sounded.

[pause]

He ran to his battle station, but found it already damaged. So he went up on deck, where an officer saw how big and strong he was, and asked him to help carry the wounded to safety. Dorie lifted his injured shipmates, one after another, and carried them through the smoke to places where they would be safe.

[pause]

{img:big-young-sailor-carrying-shipmate-deck}
Among them was the ship's captain, Mervyn Bennion, who had been badly hurt. Dorie and others carried him to a sheltered place, and stayed with him.

[pause]

Then Dorie saw an anti-aircraft machine gun standing unused on the deck. He had never been trained to use it, because Black sailors were not allowed to be trained on the guns. But he had watched the others, and he had grown up hunting in Texas. He went to the gun, and fired it at the planes, until there was no ammunition left, and the order came to abandon ship.

[long-pause]

Then he helped to move the wounded men off the ship, through the oily water, to safety on the shore.

[pause]

Afterwards, when people asked him about it, he said simply: It wasn't hard. I just pulled the trigger, and she worked fine.

[long-pause]

At the navy air station at Kaneohe Bay, on the other side of the island, a chief aviation ordnanceman named John Finn was at home in bed with his wife when he heard the planes.

[pause]

He jumped into his car and drove to the air station, and found a machine gun, and set it up on an open stand, in the middle of an open space near the hangars, and fired at the planes for more than two hours. He was wounded many times, but he would not stop. Only when the planes had gone did he let the doctors look after him, and then he went back to help repair the planes.

[pause]

He was thirty-two years old. He would later receive the Medal of Honor, the highest award for bravery in the United States, and he would live to be a hundred years old.

[long-pause]

At the Naval Hospital, beside the harbour, a young navy nurse named Ruth Erickson was having breakfast with the other nurses when they heard the planes.

[pause]

{img:navy-nurses-hospital-ward-white-uniforms}
They ran to their wards, in their white uniforms, and within minutes, the wounded men began to arrive, in trucks and cars and ambulances, and on stretchers carried by their friends. The nurses and the doctors worked all day and all night, without stopping, caring for hundreds of young men, cleaning their wounds, giving them medicine, holding their hands, and talking to them softly.

[pause]

Ruth Erickson later remembered that the men were very quiet, and very brave, and that they almost never complained. Many of them asked first about their friends.

[long-pause]

All over the island, people came to help.

[pause]

Nurses who were off duty came in from their homes. Doctors drove to the hospitals from all over Honolulu. Ordinary people lined up at the blood bank in the city to give their blood, so many of them that there were not enough bottles to hold it. Women brought food and coffee to the workers at the harbour.

[pause]

{img:volunteers-line-blood-bank-honolulu}
Boy Scouts carried messages and directed traffic. Taxi drivers carried wounded men to the hospitals, for free. In the hills around Honolulu, families opened their homes to the wives and children of the men on Ford Island, who had lost their houses, or who were waiting for news.

[long-pause]

On the upturned hull of the Oklahoma, the work of rescue began almost at once.

[pause]

Inside the ship, in the dark, many men were still alive, trapped in air pockets in the compartments, below the water line, which was now above them. They tapped on the steel walls with hammers and wrenches, to let people outside know that they were there.

[pause]

Workers from the Navy Yard came out to the hull in boats, with their cutting tools. They were civilians, mostly, welders and machinists and riveters. One of them was a young man named Julio DeCastro, who had been born in Hawaii, the son of a Portuguese family.

[pause]

They listened for the tapping, and then, very carefully, they cut holes through the thick steel of the hull, working all through the day and the night, and into the next day.

[long-pause]

One by one, the trapped men climbed out through the holes into the daylight, blinking and smiling and crying, and were helped into the boats.

[pause]

Thirty-two men were rescued from the Oklahoma that way.

[pause]

{img:rescued-sailors-small-boat-calm-water}
Years later, some of them would remember, more than anything else from that day, the moment when they saw the first light shining in through the hole, and heard the voice of a stranger calling to them that everything would be all right.

[long-pause]

Out in the harbour, the small boats went back and forth all day long.

[pause]

They were launches and motor whaleboats from the ships, and tugboats from the Navy Yard, and even some little private fishing boats from the shore. They picked up men from the water, and from the decks of the ships, and carried them to the landings, where ambulances were waiting.

[pause]

{img:harbour-small-boats-afternoon-calm-light}
Many of the men in the boats had no idea where their own ships or their own friends were. They simply helped whoever they found.

[pause]

By the evening, almost everyone who could be saved had been saved.

[long-pause]

{img:quiet-afternoon-harbour-haze-palms}
By the afternoon of the seventh of December, Pearl Harbor was quiet again.

[pause]

The smoke still hung over the harbour, drifting slowly away on the trade wind, and the oil lay on the water. But the planes had gone, and the guns were silent, and the only sounds were the engines of the small boats, and the voices of the men working on the ships, and somewhere, a radio, playing.

[long-pause]

All over the base, people were counting, and searching, and waiting.

[pause]

On the ships that were still afloat, the officers called the roll, name by name, to find out who was there and who was missing. On the shore, men walked from one hospital to another, and from one landing to another, looking for their friends and their brothers. In the houses on Ford Island and around the harbour, wives and children sat by the telephone, waiting for news.

[pause]

{img:sailors-gathered-roll-call-pier-afternoon}
For many families, the news would not come for days, or even weeks.

[long-pause]

In Honolulu, the city had changed in a single morning.

[pause]

The streets were nearly empty. The shops were closed. People stayed at home, with their radios on, listening to the news. Soldiers in helmets stood guard on the street corners, and army trucks drove up and down.

[pause]

{img:empty-honolulu-street-soldiers-afternoon}
In the afternoon, the governor of the territory announced on the radio that Hawaii was now under martial law. The army would govern the islands, for as long as the danger lasted. There would be a curfew, and a blackout every night.

[long-pause]

That evening, as the sun went down, all the lights on Oahu went out.

[pause]

Every window was covered with black paper or blankets. The streetlights were turned off. Cars drove slowly through the dark streets with their headlights painted over, or switched off. Anyone who showed a light could be arrested.

[pause]

For the first time in many years, the whole island was completely dark. People sat in their houses by the light of a candle in a closet, or in the dark, talking quietly, listening to the radio. Outside, the streets were silent, except for the footsteps of the soldiers on guard.

[long-pause]

Above the darkened island, the stars came out, very bright, brighter than anyone could remember.

[pause]

The night was warm and still, as it always is in Hawaii. The trade winds had died down. The frogs and the crickets sang in the gardens, just as they had the night before. And the moon rose over the mountains, and shone down on the harbour, and on the great dark shapes of the ships lying in the water.

[long-pause]

{img:hospital-ward-night-nurse-lamp-quiet}
At the hospitals, the doctors and nurses worked on through the night.

[pause]

In the long wards, by the dim light of shaded lamps, the nurses moved quietly from bed to bed, bringing water, changing bandages, sitting with the young men who could not sleep. Some of the men talked about their homes and their families. Some of them asked the nurses to write letters for them. Some of them simply lay quietly, looking up at the ceiling.

[pause]

Ruth Erickson worked all night. In the early morning, she went outside for a moment, for a breath of air, and stood looking up at the stars, and then went back in.

[long-pause]

In the plantation villages, and in the small towns around the island, people gathered in each other's houses, in the dark, to be together.

[pause]

They talked quietly in many languages, in Japanese and Hawaiian, Portuguese and Filipino and English. The Japanese families were especially worried. They were Americans, many of them born in Hawaii, and their sons were in the army. But they knew that some people would look at them with suspicion now, because of their faces and their names. That night, many of them took down the pictures of Japan from their walls, and put away their Japanese books and records, and sat quietly together, hoping that their neighbours would remember who they really were.

[pause]

And most of their neighbours did.

[long-pause]

On the West Virginia, the ship was resting on the bottom of the harbour, with her upper decks above the water. Dorie Miller and the other survivors of her crew had been taken ashore, and given dry clothes, and something to eat.

[pause]

They sat together in the dark, in the barracks at the Navy Yard, talking quietly about the friends they had lost, and the things they had seen. Then, one by one, they lay down on their cots, and tried to sleep.

[long-pause]

{img:mother-letter-mainland-kitchen-radio}
Thousands of miles away, on the mainland of the United States, the news had come over the radio in the early afternoon, on a quiet Sunday.

[pause]

Families had been sitting down to Sunday dinner, or listening to a football game, or reading the funny papers, or taking a Sunday walk, when the music on the radio stopped, and an announcer said that the Japanese had attacked Pearl Harbor.

[pause]

Many people had never even heard of Pearl Harbor. They had to look it up in an atlas. But everyone who had a son or a husband or a brother in the navy in Hawaii sat down by the radio, and waited, and prayed.

[long-pause]

All over America, that evening, people sat up late, listening to the radio, and talking quietly in their kitchens and living rooms.

[pause]

{img:american-family-radio-evening-worried}
They knew that their lives had changed. They knew that the country would go to war now, and that their sons and brothers and husbands would go to fight in it. They did not know how long it would last, or how it would end.

[pause]

But they knew, too, that whatever happened, they would face it together.

[long-pause]

Out in the Pacific, far to the north of Hawaii, the Japanese carriers were sailing back toward Japan, through the dark.

[pause]

And at Pearl Harbor, on the decks of the damaged ships, and on the shore, and in the hospitals, and in the darkened houses around the harbour, the long night went slowly by, until at last the sky over the mountains began to grow pale, and the first birds began to sing, and it was Monday morning.

[long-pause]

{img:capitol-washington-grey-december-day}
On Monday, the eighth of December, at half past twelve in the afternoon, Washington time, President Franklin Roosevelt came to the Capitol to speak to Congress.

[pause]

He came slowly into the great chamber of the House of Representatives, holding on to the arm of his son James, because his legs had been paralyzed by polio for many years. He stood at the desk, in front of the senators and the representatives and the judges of the Supreme Court, and the visitors crowded into the galleries above, and opened a black notebook.

[long-pause]

The whole country was listening on the radio.

[pause]

In homes and offices and factories and schools, people gathered around their radios in silence. In Hawaii, it was early in the morning, and the sailors at Pearl Harbor stood around the loudspeakers on the decks of the ships and in the barracks, listening.

[pause]

{img:workers-factory-gathered-around-radio}
Yesterday, December seventh, 1941, the President began, in his calm, strong voice, a date which will live in infamy, the United States of America was suddenly and deliberately attacked.

[pause]

He spoke for about seven minutes. At the end, he asked Congress to declare that a state of war existed between the United States and Japan.

[long-pause]

Within an hour, Congress had voted, and the United States was at war.

[pause]

Three days later, Germany and Italy declared war on the United States as well. Now America was in the Second World War, in Europe and in the Pacific, and it would stay in it until the end.

[pause]

All over the country, the next morning, long lines of young men stood outside the recruiting offices, in the cold, waiting to join the army and the navy. Many of them had never seen the sea, and could not have found Hawaii on a map. But they wanted to help.

[long-pause]

In Hawaii, the work went on.

[pause]

At Pearl Harbor, the men began to clear away the damage, and to look after the ships that were still afloat, and to plan how to raise the ones that had sunk. In the hospitals, the doctors and nurses cared for the wounded. In the offices, clerks wrote letters to families on the mainland, telling them that their sons were safe, or that they were injured, or that they were missing.

[pause]

{img:clerks-typing-letters-office-quiet-lamp}
It was slow, careful, sad work, and it went on for weeks.

[long-pause]

The people who had lost their lives were laid to rest in cemeteries on Oahu, with simple ceremonies. Their friends stood by, in their white uniforms, and a bugler played Taps, and the flag was folded and put away, to be sent to their families.

[pause]

Of the more than two thousand four hundred Americans who died at Pearl Harbor, almost half were from the Arizona. Many of them are still with their ship, under the water of the harbour, where she rests.

[long-pause]

{img:honolulu-christmas-1941-quiet-blackout}
In Honolulu, life slowly found a new shape.

[pause]

The schools stayed closed for some weeks. Everyone had to carry a gas mask, in a little canvas bag over the shoulder. Barbed wire was strung along the beaches at Waikiki. People dug shelters in their gardens. Every night, the blackout came down, and the island was dark.

[pause]

Most of the people of Japanese ancestry in Hawaii, who made up more than a third of everyone on the islands, were not sent away, as happened to many Japanese Americans on the mainland, though some of their leaders were taken from their homes and held in camps. Many of their sons would later join the army, and serve with great distinction in Europe, in a famous regiment of Japanese American soldiers, whose motto was Go for Broke.

[long-pause]

Christmas came, three weeks after the attack.

[pause]

It was a quiet Christmas in Hawaii that year. There were no lights on the trees in the streets, because of the blackout. But families still put up their Christmas trees indoors, behind the black curtains, and decorated them, and the children still found their stockings full on Christmas morning.

[pause]

The churches held services in the daytime, because of the curfew. And at the hospitals, the nurses decorated the wards with paper chains and little trees, and gave the wounded men presents, and sang carols for them, and some of the men sang too.

[long-pause]

In Washington, on Christmas Eve, President Roosevelt lit the national Christmas tree on the lawn of the White House, as presidents did every year.

[pause]

That year, he had a guest standing beside him: the Prime Minister of Britain, Winston Churchill, who had crossed the Atlantic in secret, on a battleship, to plan the war with him. A crowd of about twenty thousand people stood in the cold darkness to listen.

[pause]

Roosevelt spoke about Christmas, and about peace, and about the hard times ahead. Then Churchill spoke, in his deep, slow voice, to the children of America. Let the children have their night of fun and laughter, he said. Let us share to the full in their unstinted pleasures, before we turn again to the stern tasks and the formidable years that lie before us.

[pause]

Then the lights on the tree came on, glowing softly in the dark, and the crowd sang carols together.

[long-pause]

On the mainland, many families had their first Christmas without a son or a brother or a father.

[pause]

But they also had a new feeling, a feeling that the whole country was together now, all facing the same thing, all doing their part. Women went to work in the factories, building ships and planes. Children collected scrap metal and old rubber. People planted vegetable gardens in their back yards, and bought war bonds, and wrote letters to the soldiers and sailors far away.

[long-pause]

{img:admiral-nimitz-arriving-flying-boat-harbour}
At the end of December, a new commander arrived at Pearl Harbor to take over the Pacific Fleet.

[pause]

He was Admiral Chester Nimitz, a quiet, calm, white-haired man from the hill country of Texas, with very blue eyes and a gentle manner. He had flown out from Washington, and he arrived on Christmas morning, in a flying boat, in the rain.

[pause]

He took a small boat across the harbour, past the sunken battleships, and the oil still floating on the water, and the small boats still working among the wreckage. He looked at it all for a long time, and said very little.

[pause]

Then he went ashore, and went to work.

[long-pause]

His predecessor, Admiral Kimmel, and the army commander, General Short, had been relieved of their commands in the middle of December.

[pause]

They went home to the mainland, and lived quietly for the rest of their lives, and for the rest of their lives, they believed that they had been treated unfairly, because they had not been given all the warnings that Washington knew about. People still argue about it today.

[pause]

{img:harbour-evening-repair-work-lights-calm}
And in the harbour, as the year 1941 came to an end, the work of repair went on, day and night, under the stars.

[long-pause]

{img:salvage-divers-harbour-calm-morning}
In the weeks and months after the attack, the people of Pearl Harbor set about the enormous task of raising the sunken ships.

[pause]

It was one of the greatest salvage works ever attempted. Many experts said that most of the battleships could never be saved. But the navy divers and engineers and the workers of the Navy Yard did not agree.

[long-pause]

The divers went down into the dark, oily water of the harbour, in their heavy canvas suits and brass helmets, with air pumped down to them through long hoses from the boats above.

[pause]

Down in the murky water, they could hardly see anything. They worked mostly by feel, finding their way through the ships by touching the walls and the doors and the ladders. They patched the holes in the hulls with huge wooden and steel patches, made to fit by the carpenters in the Navy Yard. They pumped out the water. They worked for many hours a day, for months on end.

[pause]

It was hard, slow, cold, careful work. But one by one, the great ships began to rise.

[long-pause]

The Nevada was the first of the battleships to be raised. She was floated off the shore where she had been run aground in February 1942, and taken into the dry dock, and repaired, and then sent to the mainland to be modernized.

[pause]

The California was raised in March. The West Virginia was raised in May, and carefully patched, and floated into the dry dock.

[pause]

{img:battleship-in-dry-dock-workers-repair}
Inside the West Virginia, the salvage workers found many sad and touching things. In one of the storerooms, they found a calendar on the wall, on which three young men, trapped inside, had crossed off the days, one by one.

[long-pause]

The Oklahoma, which had turned upside down, was the hardest to save.

[pause]

The engineers built a great row of winches on the shore of Ford Island, and fastened huge steel cables to the hull of the ship. Then, very slowly, over several weeks, the winches pulled the ship around, until she was upright again.

[pause]

She was too badly damaged to be repaired. Later, after the war, she was sold for scrap, and while she was being towed to California, in a storm, she sank, far out in the Pacific, and went down to the bottom of the ocean, where she lies today.

[long-pause]

The Arizona could not be raised at all.

[pause]

Her guns and her masts and parts of her upper decks were taken away, to be used on other ships. But the hull of the great ship was left where she lay, at the bottom of the harbour, beside Ford Island, with many of her crew still inside her.

[pause]

{img:quiet-water-harbour-ford-island-evening}
And every morning, ever since, a few little drops of oil have risen slowly from her hull to the surface of the water, and spread out in thin rainbows of colour, and drifted away. People call them the black tears of the Arizona.

[long-pause]

The Utah, an old battleship that had been used for target practice, and had turned over at her mooring on the other side of Ford Island, was also left where she lay, with some of her crew still inside her.

[pause]

Today, there is a small, quiet memorial beside her too, on the shore of Ford Island, a simple platform looking out over the rusted hull in the water, where visitors can stand and remember.

[long-pause]

Of the eight battleships that were at Pearl Harbor that morning, six were repaired, and went back to sea, and fought in the war.

[pause]

The West Virginia and the California, rebuilt and modernized, returned to the Pacific in 1944. In the autumn of that year, in the Battle of Surigao Strait, in the Philippines, they and the other old battleships from Pearl Harbor took part in the last great battle ever fought between battleships.

[pause]

{img:grey-battleships-at-sea-sunset-pacific}
The men who had lost their ships at Pearl Harbor were proud to see them back.

[long-pause]

The war in the Pacific went on for nearly four years.

[pause]

There were great battles at sea and in the air, at Midway and the Coral Sea, and long, hard battles on islands with names that nobody had heard of before: Guadalcanal and Tarawa, Saipan and Iwo Jima and Okinawa. Hundreds of thousands of young men served in it, on both sides, and many of them did not come home.

[pause]

At home, in America and in Japan, families waited for letters, and prayed, and hoped.

[long-pause]

At last, in August 1945, the war came to an end.

[pause]

On the second of September, 1945, on a bright, clear morning, the formal surrender of Japan was signed in Tokyo Bay, on the deck of the American battleship Missouri, surrounded by hundreds of other ships.

[pause]

{img:battleship-deck-ceremony-tokyo-bay-morning}
Among the ships in the bay that morning was the old West Virginia, which had been sunk at Pearl Harbor less than four years before, and raised from the bottom, and repaired, and sent back to sea.

[pause]

Her crew lined the rails, in their white uniforms, and watched.

[long-pause]

In Hawaii, when the news of the end of the war came, people poured out into the streets of Honolulu, cheering and laughing and crying, and dancing on the pavements.

[pause]

Church bells rang. Ships in the harbour sounded their whistles and sirens. And that night, the lights of Oahu shone out bright and warm, in every window and every street, across the dark water of the Pacific.

[long-pause]

{img:medal-ceremony-carrier-deck-1942-sun}
In May 1942, on the deck of the aircraft carrier Enterprise, in Pearl Harbor, there was a ceremony.

[pause]

The crew stood in long rows on the flight deck, in their white uniforms, in the bright Hawaiian sunshine. And Admiral Nimitz himself walked slowly along the line, and stopped in front of a big young man from Texas, and pinned a medal on his chest.

[long-pause]

It was the Navy Cross, one of the highest awards for bravery in the navy. And the young man was Dorie Miller.

[pause]

He was the first Black American to receive it. The admiral shook his hand, and spoke to him quietly, and said that this was the first time in the war that such a high award had been given to a man of his race in the Pacific, and that it would surely not be the last.

[pause]

{img:young-sailor-medal-proud-quiet-smile}
The newspapers all over America told his story. People wrote songs and poems about him. His picture appeared on posters. He went home to Texas for a while, and visited his family, and spoke at schools and churches, where people came in their hundreds to see him. His mother was very proud.

[long-pause]

Then Dorie went back to sea.

[pause]

He served as a cook on a small aircraft carrier called the Liscome Bay. In November 1943, during the battle for the island of Makin, in the Central Pacific, his ship was lost at sea, and Dorie Miller was lost with her. He was twenty-four years old.

[pause]

His parents received the news at their farm near Waco. Later, a ship was named after him, and a school, and a park, and streets in many cities.

[pause]

And in our own time, the navy announced that one of its newest and largest aircraft carriers will be named the Doris Miller. It will be the first aircraft carrier ever named after an enlisted sailor, and the first named after a Black American.

[long-pause]

John Finn, the sailor who manned the machine gun at Kaneohe Bay, recovered from his wounds, and received the Medal of Honor in September 1942, from Admiral Nimitz.

[pause]

He stayed in the navy until 1956, and then retired to a ranch in the hills of southern California, where he raised cattle and lived a quiet life. He loved to talk with young people about his service, and he went to many ceremonies and reunions, and was always cheerful and modest.

[pause]

{img:old-man-ranch-porch-california-hills}
When he passed away, in 2010, he was a hundred years old, the oldest living holder of the Medal of Honor.

[long-pause]

Ruth Erickson, the young nurse, served in the navy for many more years.

[pause]

After the war, she rose to become the head of the whole Navy Nurse Corps, in Washington. Years later, when she was an old lady, she told her story of that morning to historians, so that it would not be forgotten: the planes over the hospital, the wounded men arriving, the long day and the long night, and the stars she had looked up at in the early morning.

[pause]

{img:elderly-woman-telling-story-living-room}
She lived until 2008.

[long-pause]

The men who survived the Arizona and the other ships went on with their lives, as people do.

[pause]

Many of them fought through the rest of the war. Afterwards, they went home, and married, and raised families, and worked on farms and in factories and offices. Some of them never talked about that morning at all. Others talked about it often, especially in their old age, and went back to Hawaii again and again for the anniversaries, to stand by the water and remember their friends.

[pause]

Some of the survivors of the Arizona asked that, when they died, their ashes should be placed inside the ship, with their shipmates. Navy divers carried them down, in small urns, and placed them gently inside the hull, in a quiet ceremony, while a bugler played Taps on the memorial above.

[long-pause]

Lieutenant Kermit Tyler, the young officer who had told the radar men not to worry about the blip on their screen, served in the air force all through the war, and afterwards became a real estate agent in California.

[pause]

He was always troubled by that morning. For the rest of his life, people asked him about it. He would explain, patiently, that he had been told to expect the bombers from California, and that he had had no training, and no way of knowing. Most people who heard his story understood.

[pause]

Joseph Lockard, the radar operator, went on to become an officer himself, and later worked for many years as an engineer, building electronic equipment.

[long-pause]

On the Japanese side, too, there were men who lived on and remembered.

[pause]

The commander who had led the planes in the air that morning, a pilot named Mitsuo Fuchida, survived the war. Afterwards, he went home to a small farm in Japan, and grew rice and vegetables. Some years later, he read a little pamphlet about forgiveness, and he changed his life. He became a Christian, and spent many years travelling in America, speaking to people about peace and forgiveness, and made friends with some of the men he had once fought against.

[pause]

{img:japanese-farmer-rice-field-evening-1950s}
He said that he hoped that the young people of both countries would never have to go to war against each other again.

[long-pause]

And they did not.

[pause]

In the years after the war, Japan and the United States, who had fought each other so bitterly, became close friends and allies. Their peoples traded with each other, and visited each other, and learned from each other.

[pause]

{img:pacific-harbour-sunrise-peaceful-ships}
In 2016, the Prime Minister of Japan, Shinzo Abe, came to Pearl Harbor, and stood with the President of the United States, Barack Obama, at the memorial over the Arizona, and laid a wreath of flowers, and bowed his head in silence, in memory of those who had died there.

[long-pause]

{img:arizona-memorial-white-calm-water-morning}
Today, Pearl Harbor is still a working navy base. Ships still come and go through the narrow channel from the sea, and the grey warships of the Pacific Fleet still lie at their moorings along the shores.

[pause]

But in the middle of the harbour, beside Ford Island, there is a quiet place that people come from all over the world to see.

[long-pause]

It is the USS Arizona Memorial.

[pause]

It is a long white building that floats over the middle of the sunken ship, without touching her, like a bridge across the water. It sags a little in the middle, and rises at both ends. The architect who designed it, Alfred Preis, said that it was meant to show the low point of that terrible morning, and then the rising up again, of hope and strength, in the years that followed.

[pause]

{img:memorial-interior-open-windows-sky-water}
It was opened in 1962. Visitors come out to it by boat, from the visitor centre on the shore, a short ride across the calm water of the harbour.

[long-pause]

On the shore, at the visitor centre, there is a quiet garden beside the water, with palm trees and benches, and a long path along the edge of the harbour.

[pause]

There are museums, too, where visitors can see the things that people left behind from that morning: a sailor's cap, a letter, a pocket watch that stopped at a certain minute, a bugle, a photograph of a young man in his white uniform, smiling. There are recordings of the voices of the survivors, telling their stories in their old age, calmly and simply, so that they will not be forgotten.

[pause]

Before they get on the boat, the visitors sit together in a small theatre and watch a short film about that morning. Then they walk down to the landing in silence, and the boat carries them gently across the water.

[long-pause]

Inside the memorial, there are open windows along both sides, and above, so that the light and the wind come through, and visitors can look out at the sky and the harbour.

[pause]

Looking down into the clear green water, they can see the old ship below them: the rusted outline of her decks, and the round base of one of her great gun turrets, rising up out of the water. Little fish swim in and out of her. Coral and sea grass grow on her sides.

[pause]

At the far end of the memorial, there is a quiet room with a tall wall of white marble, on which are carved the names of the men who were lost on the Arizona. People stand before it in silence, reading the names. Sometimes they find the name of someone from their own family, and touch it gently with their fingers.

[long-pause]

And still, every day, a few small drops of oil rise slowly from the ship, and float to the surface, and spread out on the water in thin rings of rainbow colour.

[pause]

The black tears of the Arizona.

[pause]

{img:oil-drops-rainbow-rings-calm-water}
Some people say that the ship will go on weeping until the last of her survivors has joined his shipmates.

[long-pause]

Not far from the Arizona, at the other end of Battleship Row, lies another great battleship, painted grey, and open to visitors.

[pause]

It is the Missouri, the ship on whose deck the war ended in Tokyo Bay, in September 1945. She was brought to Pearl Harbor and moored here, so that the beginning and the end of America's war in the Pacific can be seen together, in the same harbour, side by side.

[pause]

{img:battleship-missouri-pier-sunset-today}
Nearby, on Ford Island, the old control tower still stands, and the hangars, and the officers' houses, with their lawns and palm trees, just as they were in 1941.

[long-pause]

Every year, on the seventh of December, there is a ceremony at Pearl Harbor.

[pause]

People gather beside the water, early in the morning, sailors and soldiers and their families, and visitors from the mainland and from Japan. At five minutes to eight, the moment when the first planes came over the hills, there is a moment of silence. A ship in the harbour sounds its whistle. Planes fly over in a slow formation, with one plane leaving the line and climbing away into the sky, in memory of those who were lost.

[pause]

For many years, the survivors of the attack came to the ceremony, in their aloha shirts and their caps, and sat in the front row, and the crowd stood up and applauded them. Now, there are only a very few of them left. This year, in December 2026, it will be eighty-five years since that Sunday morning.

[long-pause]

Honolulu has grown into a busy modern city, with tall hotels along the beach at Waikiki, and highways, and shopping centres.

[pause]

But the island is still beautiful. The green mountains still rise into the clouds, and the rainbows still come and go over the valleys. The trade winds still blow, and the plumeria trees still drop their flowers on the paths. The surfers still ride the long, smooth waves at Waikiki, and Diamond Head still stands at the end of the beach.

[pause]

{img:diamond-head-waikiki-evening-surfers}
And when visitors arrive on the island, there are still leis of fresh flowers waiting for them, and people still say aloha.

[long-pause]

In the evening, at Pearl Harbor, the visitors go home, and the boats stop running, and the memorial stands alone on the water, white and quiet.

[pause]

The sun goes down behind the Waianae Mountains, in a sky of orange and pink and gold, and the calm water of the harbour catches the light. On Ford Island, the palm trees stand dark against the sunset. The flag over the memorial is lowered for the night, as it is every evening, and a bugle plays.
