TOPIC: A day aboard a battleship

HEAD: A battleship's day began at half past five, with a bugle and a famous old call.
BODY: Life on board a battleship began early. At about half past five in the morning, a bugle sounded over the loudspeakers. Then a voice called out an old navy phrase: Reveille, reveille, all hands heave out and trice up. Heave out meant get out of bed. Trice up meant fold your bunk out of the way. All through the ship, hundreds of young men climbed out of their bunks, rubbing their eyes and yawning. The sleeping compartments were long and crowded, deep below the decks. They were hot and stuffy, even at night. Fans whirred, and the ship's machinery hummed through the steel. The men slept in narrow canvas bunks, stacked three or four high. There were only a few inches between one man and the next. Each sailor had a small locker for his things. Inside were his uniforms, his shaving kit and his letters from home. There might be a photograph of his girl, a Bible and a deck of cards.

IMG: mess-deck-long-tables-breakfast-sailors | sailors in blue work shirts eating breakfast at long folding tables on a ship's mess deck, big mugs of coffee and plates of eggs and pancakes, an indoor scene, enclosed by steel walls, a plain ceiling, warm light
HEAD: The ship's cooks got up at three in the morning to feed more than a thousand men.
BODY: The men washed and shaved quickly, in long rows of steel basins. They put on their working clothes. These were blue denim trousers and light blue shirts, with white caps pushed to the back of the head. Then came breakfast. The men ate on the mess decks, at long tables that folded away when not in use. The food was plentiful and filling. There were scrambled eggs, bacon and sausages, fried potatoes, and pancakes with syrup. There was toast, and big mugs of strong coffee. On Sundays, there were often baked beans and cornbread. In the galley, the cooks had been up since three in the morning. They baked bread in great ovens and fried bacon in enormous pans. A battleship had to feed more than a thousand men, three times a day, every single day. Many young sailors had grown up poor, in hard years. Some said they had never eaten so well in their lives.

IMG: sailors-scrubbing-deck-holystones-sun | a line of sailors on their knees scrubbing the pale wooden deck of a battleship with blocks of stone and buckets of water, big gun turret behind, bright morning sun
HEAD: Every morning, sailors scrubbed the wooden decks with blocks of sandstone called holystones.
BODY: After breakfast, the work of the day began. There was always something to be done on a ship. The decks were made of wooden planks laid over the steel. Every morning, they were scrubbed with salt water and blocks of sandstone. The sailors called these blocks holystones. Some said that was because you scrubbed with them on your knees, as if in prayer. They scrubbed until the planks were white and smooth. The brass had to be polished until it shone like gold. The paint had to be scraped and painted, again and again. The salt air of the sea was always trying to eat away at the steel. There were drills too. There were gun drills, fire drills and drills for abandoning ship. The men practised loading the great guns with dummy shells. They did it so many times that they could do it in the dark. It was hard, repetitive work. Yet a clean, shining ship was a matter of great pride.

HEAD: A battleship had its own barbers, tailors, bakers and even a dentist.
BODY: Every man on the ship had his own job. There were gunners and signalmen. There were radiomen and electricians. Deep in the ship were the firemen and machinists, who looked after the boilers and the engines. It was very hot down there, and very loud. There were cooks and bakers, barbers and tailors. There were laundrymen who washed the white uniforms in great steaming machines. There were storekeepers who knew where every tin of peas was kept. There was a doctor and a dentist, and a chaplain for the men's worries. There was a small shop that sold sweets, soap and cigarettes. There was a library, and a little print shop that made the ship's own newspaper. A battleship was really a small floating town. It had everything its people needed, all packed inside a steel hull. A boy from a tiny farm might find himself living in the biggest town he had ever known. And this town could sail across the ocean.

HEAD: Every battleship had its own band, and the Arizona's band had trained together at music school.
BODY: Every battleship had its own band. The band of the Arizona was made up of twenty-one young men. Most of them were in their early twenties. In the evenings, some of them played jazz and swing for the other sailors. That was the popular music of the day, the kind you danced to. They practised hard, on the stern of the ship, with the harbour all around them. The other sailors would sometimes gather to listen, tapping their feet. The musicians were good, and they knew it. They were proud of their band, and proud of their ship. Can you hear them? Trumpets and trombones in the warm morning air, drifting across the calm blue water.

BODY: On Sundays, life on board was easier. There was no work, except what had to be done. In the morning, there was a church service on deck. The chaplain stood in his robes in front of a little altar. The sailors sat on benches and folding chairs. They sang hymns in the open air, while the band played. Sea birds wheeled overhead, and the trade wind lifted the pages of the hymn books. After the service, there was a big Sunday lunch. Then the men were free for the rest of the day. They could sleep, or read, or write letters. They could go ashore, if it was their turn for liberty. Many simply sat on deck in the shade of the big guns. The warm wind blew, and they looked out at the green hills around the harbour. They talked quietly about home. Isn't that what Sunday afternoons are for? A full stomach, a soft breeze, and nowhere you need to be.

They came from mining towns in Pennsylvania and West Virginia, and from the big cities of the East and the West. For many of them, the navy was a steady job. It meant good food, a bed and a chance to see the world. Jobs at home had been hard to find for years. On board the Arizona, there were many sets of brothers serving together. Families liked their sons to be on the same ship, so they could look after each other. There was even a father serving with his son. The father worked with the engines, and his son was a young seaman. Imagine that. Waking up in the morning and seeing your dad across the mess deck at breakfast. It must have felt a little like home, so far from home.

BODY: In the afternoons, when the work was done, there was time to relax. The men wrote letters and read magazines. They played cards or checkers. They listened to the radio and shined their shoes until they gleamed. Some lay on the deck in the shade, under the big guns, and slept. Some fished over the side, with a line and a bit of bacon. Now and then, someone pulled up a small fish and everyone cheered. Some went swimming, when it was allowed. Others played catch with a baseball on the open deck. Many sailors also had pets on board, though the rules did not always allow it. There were cats that slept on warm machinery, and a few small, much-loved dogs. It was a gentle, easy rhythm. Work in the morning, rest in the afternoon. For young men far from home, it was a life with very little to worry about.

If there was a love scene, the sailors whistled and teased each other. At ten o'clock, the bugle sounded taps, and the lights went out. The men climbed back into their bunks. The ship grew quiet. There was only the hum of machinery and the footsteps of the men on watch. Up on deck, the officer of the deck walked slowly back and forth. He looked out across the dark water, where the lights of the other ships lay reflected. And out beyond the harbour mouth, the long Pacific swell rolled in and broke softly on the reef.

BODY: On board the battleship West Virginia, there was a young man named Doris Miller. Everyone called him Dorie. In 1941, he was twenty-two years old. He was the third of four sons in a family of Black farmers. His parents, Conery and Henrietta Miller, worked a small farm. They grew cotton and vegetables and raised chickens and pigs. Dorie grew up working in the fields with his brothers, from early morning until sunset. At school, he played football. He was so strong and so fast that the other boys called him the Raging Bull. Yet at home, he was a gentle boy. He helped his mother around the house and went to church every Sunday. He liked to go hunting for squirrels in the woods with his father's old rifle.

HEAD: When Dorie joined the navy, only one kind of job was open to Black sailors.
BODY: When he was nineteen, Dorie decided to join the navy. Jobs were hard to find in Texas in those years. The navy offered steady pay, and a chance to send money home to his family. It also offered a chance to see the world. In September 1939, he went to the recruiting office in Dallas and signed up. But in 1939, the navy did not treat Black men in the same way as white men. They could not become gunners, radiomen, machinists or officers. Only one job was open to them. They could join the messman branch. That meant cooking and serving food to the officers, and cleaning their cabins. It meant washing their clothes and polishing their shoes. It was unfair, and many people knew it was unfair. Yet young men like Dorie joined anyway. They wanted the work, and they wanted to serve. And many of them hoped, quietly, that things would one day change.

Early each morning, before most of the ship was awake, he went from cabin to cabin gathering shirts and sheets. He did his work well and quietly, and the officers liked him. He was always polite and cheerful, and he never complained. His friends on the ship were the other mess attendants. They were young Black men like himself, from Texas and Louisiana, Georgia and Virginia. They worked together and ate together. They slept in the same small compartment, and looked out for one another. On the long ship, they were a small family of their own.

BODY: When a sailor at Pearl Harbor had a day off, he went on liberty. On weekends, the liberty boats came and went all day long. The sailors laughed and talked, with their caps pushed to the backs of their heads. Some had saved up their pay for weeks. Others had only a few coins in their pockets. From the landing, they took a bus or a taxi into Honolulu. It was about eight miles away, along a road through the sugar cane fields. Through the open windows came the smell of cut cane and flowers. Before going ashore, each sailor's uniform was checked. It had to be spotless, with every crease sharp. An officer might send a man back to change if a single button was dull. So the young men stepped off the launch looking their very best.

They walked up and down, looking in the windows and buying postcards to send home. In the photograph studios, you could have your picture taken beside a hula dancer. Or you could sit in a pretend aeroplane, painted on a board, and send that picture to your mother. Tattoo artists would draw an anchor, a ship or your sweetheart's name on your arm. Little restaurants served hamburgers, chili and pie. The Black Cat Café, across from the YMCA, was open day and night. Would you have been tempted by a tattoo? Many sailors were. TOPIC: A world far away at war

BODY: In the autumn of 1941, much of the world beyond the islands was at war. In Europe, the war had been going on for more than two years. Germany had conquered most of the continent, from France to Poland. That summer, it had invaded the Soviet Union as well. Britain was fighting on, under attack from the air and at sea. In Asia too, there was war. For four years, Japan had been fighting in China. Its armies had taken much of the coast and many of the great cities. In 1940 and 1941, Japanese troops also moved south into French Indochina. Today, those lands are Vietnam, Laos and Cambodia. The United States was not at war. Most Americans did not want to fight. They remembered the First World War, only twenty-five years before. In Hawaii, families listened to the news on the radio each evening. Then they switched it off, and went out to the porch, where everything was calm.

HEAD: Japan had very little oil of its own, and much of it had come from America.
BODY: Relations between the United States and Japan had grown worse and worse. Japan needed oil, steel and rubber for its factories and its armies. It had very little of these things at home. Much of its oil had come from the United States. In the summer of 1941, the American government stopped selling oil to Japan. It hoped this would make Japan stop its wars in Asia. Without oil, Japan's navy and army would soon run out of fuel. So Japan's leaders faced a hard choice. They could give up their conquests, as America demanded. Or they could go to war, to seize oil fields far to the south, in what are now Indonesia and Malaysia. All through the autumn, diplomats from both countries met in Washington. They talked and talked, trying to find a way out. But they could not agree. It is a sad thing to think about. So much depended on a few men in a room, unable to find the right words.

HEAD: The Japanese admiral who planned the attack had studied at Harvard and admired America.
BODY: In Japan, the commander of the combined fleet was Admiral Isoroku Yamamoto. He was fifty-seven years old, short and broad-shouldered, and very thoughtful. As a young officer, he had studied at Harvard University in America. Later, he had served at the Japanese embassy in Washington. He knew America well. He had travelled across it and seen its great factories and its oil fields. He admired it. He also loved to play cards and board games, and he was very good at them. Yamamoto did not want war with the United States. He believed Japan could not win a long war against such a large and powerful country. He said so, many times, to his country's leaders. But he was a loyal officer. If there had to be a war, he believed Japan's only chance was to strike first and strike hard. He thought the target should be the American fleet at Pearl Harbor.

The seas there were cold and stormy, and few other ships were likely to pass. The ships kept radio silence. They showed no lights at night. Day after day, they moved east through the grey waves, unseen.

BODY: On the long voyage, the young pilots on the carriers studied maps and models of Pearl Harbor. They cleaned their planes and checked their equipment. And they wrote letters to their families. Many of them were afraid, though they did not say so. It is worth remembering this, quietly. On both sides of the ocean, there were young men lying awake at night, thinking about home. None of them had chosen the war. They had simply been born into a time when it came.

HEAD: In the first week of December, Christmas trees arrived in Honolulu by ship from the mainland.
BODY: In Hawaii, nobody knew that the fleet was coming. The American commanders knew that war with Japan might come very soon. On the twenty-seventh of November, they received a warning from Washington. But most people believed that if Japan attacked, it would attack far away. They thought of the Philippines, or Malaya, or the Dutch islands in the south. Few people really believed Japan would come all the way across the Pacific. So in the first week of December, life in Hawaii went on as usual. The ships came and went. The sailors went on liberty. The bands practised for the Battle of Music. In Honolulu, the shops were full of Christmas decorations. Fresh Christmas trees had arrived by ship from the mainland. They stood in rows outside the stores, under the palm trees. The aircraft carriers of the fleet were away at sea that week. But the battleships were in the harbour, as they usually were on weekends.
