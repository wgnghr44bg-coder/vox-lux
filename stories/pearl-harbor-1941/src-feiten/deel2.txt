TOPIC: A day aboard a battleship
===
IMG: reveille-sailors-waking-bunks-ship | young sailors climbing out of narrow canvas bunks stacked three high in a long crowded compartment inside a ship, an indoor scene, enclosed by steel walls, a plain ceiling, soft early morning lamplight
HEAD: A battleship's day began at about half past five in the morning with reveille.
BODY: Life aboard an American battleship in 1941 followed a strict daily routine. Shortly after half past five, a bugle sounded reveille over the ship's loudspeakers. A traditional call followed, ordering the men to get out of their bunks and stow them away. Hundreds of sailors rose at once in long, crowded sleeping compartments below decks. The compartments were warm and stuffy, even at night, and filled with the hum of machinery. Men slept in canvas bunks stacked three or four high, with little space between them. Each sailor had a small locker for uniforms, shaving gear and letters from home. Navy regulations of the period set out the daily schedule in detail, from reveille to taps. Memoirs written by former sailors often describe the noise and closeness of these spaces. Even so, many remembered the routine with some affection. The fixed schedule gave shape to life at sea and in port. For young recruits, it was often their first experience of such discipline.
===
IMG: mess-deck-long-tables-breakfast-sailors | sailors in blue work shirts eating breakfast at long folding tables on a ship's mess deck, big mugs of coffee and plates of eggs and pancakes, an indoor scene, enclosed by steel walls, a plain ceiling, warm light
HEAD: A battleship's cooks started work around three in the morning to feed more than a thousand men.
BODY: Feeding a battleship's crew was a large operation. Cooks and bakers began work in the galley in the early hours, baking bread in large ovens and frying bacon in enormous pans. After washing and shaving, the men put on working uniforms of blue denim trousers and light blue shirts. They ate at long tables on the mess decks, which folded away when not in use. Breakfast was plentiful, often including eggs, bacon, sausages, potatoes, pancakes, toast and strong coffee. Navy menus from the period show that sailors received three substantial meals each day. Many recruits had grown up during the Depression, when food at home was often scarce. In letters and interviews, some later said they had never eaten so well. Even so, the work of the cooks was relentless. A ship with more than a thousand men aboard had to be fed three times a day, every day, at sea or in harbour.
===
IMG: sailors-scrubbing-deck-holystones-sun | a line of sailors on their knees scrubbing the pale wooden deck of a battleship with blocks of stone and buckets of water, big gun turret behind, bright morning sun
HEAD: Sailors scrubbed the wooden decks every morning with blocks of sandstone called holystones.
BODY: After breakfast, the working day began. Battleships had wooden planks laid over their steel decks, and these were cleaned every morning. Sailors scrubbed them with salt water and blocks of sandstone known as holystones. One explanation for the name is that men used them while kneeling, as if in prayer. The scrubbing continued until the planks were pale and smooth. Brass fittings were polished, and paintwork was scraped and repainted to protect the steel from salt air. Drills took up much of the day as well. Crews practised loading guns with dummy shells, fighting fires and abandoning ship. They repeated these exercises until the actions became automatic. Navy training manuals of the period emphasised constant practice. Many former sailors remembered the work as dull and repetitive. Even so, it served a clear purpose. A well-drilled crew could act quickly and correctly under pressure, without having to stop and think.
===
IMG: ship-barber-tailor-small-workshop | a ship's barber cutting a young sailor's hair in a small steel room beside a tailor at a sewing machine, an indoor scene, enclosed by steel walls, a plain ceiling, warm lamplight
HEAD: A battleship carried its own barbers, tailors, bakers, doctors and dentist.
BODY: Each man aboard a battleship had a specific role. There were gunners, signalmen, radiomen and electricians. Deep in the hull, firemen and machinists tended the boilers and engines in great heat and noise. The crew also included cooks, bakers, barbers, tailors and laundrymen, who washed white uniforms in large machines. Storekeepers managed supplies of food, clothing and equipment. A doctor, a dentist and a chaplain served the crew. Ships often had a small store selling sweets, soap and cigarettes, as well as a library and a print shop that produced a ship's newspaper. Historians often describe a battleship of this era as a floating town. It was designed to be largely self-sufficient during long periods at sea. Crew lists show how varied the skills on board were. For many young men from small farms or towns, it was the most complex community they had ever lived in. Each job helped keep the whole ship running.
===
IMG: ship-band-practising-deck-instruments | young navy musicians in white uniforms practising with trumpets, trombones and drums on the stern of a grey battleship, the harbour behind them, warm morning light
HEAD: Each battleship had its own band, and the Arizona's band had trained together at the Navy School of Music.
BODY: Battleships carried bands that played at ceremonies, concerts and dances. The band of the Arizona, officially Navy Band Unit 22, had twenty-one members. Most were in their early twenties. They had trained together at the Navy School of Music in Washington, D.C., and were assigned to the Arizona as a unit in 1941. The band played each morning when the flag was raised. It also performed at official events and entertained the crew. In the evenings, members often played swing and jazz, the popular dance music of the time. They practised on the ship's stern, with the harbour around them. Records and family accounts describe a close-knit and talented group. All twenty-one members of the band died on the seventh of December. Historians note that it was the only American military band to lose all its members in a single event. Today, their names are remembered at the Arizona Memorial and in navy music traditions.
===
IMG: church-service-deck-sailors-folding-chairs | sailors in white uniforms sitting on benches and folding chairs on the deck of a battleship for a Sunday service, a chaplain at a small altar, seabirds overhead, soft morning light
HEAD: On Sundays, chaplains held church services in the open air on the decks of the battleships.
BODY: Sunday aboard a battleship was quieter than other days. Apart from essential duties, there was no routine work. In the morning, the ship's chaplain often led a service on deck. A small portable altar was set up, and sailors sat on benches and folding chairs. Hymns were sung in the open air, sometimes accompanied by the band. After the service came a large Sunday meal. For the rest of the day, men were free to rest, read, write letters or go ashore if they had liberty. Many simply sat on deck in the shade and talked. Photographs from Pearl Harbor in 1941 show services held in this way. Navy chaplains served sailors of many denominations and also offered personal support. Memoirs mention the calm of these Sunday mornings. Even so, ships always kept a duty crew on board. On the morning of the seventh of December, preparations for such services were underway on several ships.
===
IMG: young-sailor-writing-letter-bunk-lamp | a very young sailor sitting on a narrow bunk writing a letter by the light of a small lamp, a photograph of his family pinned beside him, an indoor scene, enclosed by steel walls, a plain ceiling, warm lamplight
HEAD: Many brothers served together aboard the Arizona, and there was also a father serving with his son.
BODY: Most sailors in the Pacific Fleet were very young. Many had enlisted at seventeen or eighteen, straight from high school. They came from farms in the Midwest, mining towns in Pennsylvania and West Virginia, and cities across the country. During the Depression, the navy offered steady pay, food and training. Families often encouraged brothers to serve on the same ship, so they could look after each other. Records show that thirty-seven pairs or trios of brothers were serving aboard the Arizona in December 1941. There was also a father and son, Thomas Free and his son William. Thomas was a machinist's mate, and William was a seaman. Both died on the seventh of December. After the war, the navy discouraged brothers from serving together on the same ship. That policy is often linked to the loss of the five Sullivan brothers in 1942. The Arizona's family connections remain one of the most moving parts of its history.
===
IMG: sailors-relaxing-deck-shade-afternoon | young sailors resting in the shade of a big gun turret on the deck of a battleship, some reading magazines, one fishing over the side, calm harbour and hills behind, warm afternoon light
HEAD: In the afternoons, off-duty sailors read, played cards, and sometimes fished over the side of their ships.
BODY: When the day's work was finished, sailors had time to themselves. Many wrote letters, read magazines or listened to the radio. Others played cards or checkers, or polished their shoes. Some rested in the shade of the big gun turrets. Fishing from the side of the ship with a simple line was a common pastime in harbour. Games of catch with a baseball took place on open stretches of deck. Some ships also had unofficial pets, such as cats that slept near warm machinery, despite rules against animals. Photographs and personal snapshots from 1941 show many of these relaxed moments. Former sailors often described the afternoons in Pearl Harbor as easy and pleasant. Even so, the routine could become monotonous. Many young men felt homesick, especially as the holidays approached. The balance of hard work in the morning and quieter afternoons was typical of life aboard a warship in port during peacetime.
===
IMG: movie-screen-deck-sailors-stars | rows of sailors in white uniforms sitting on the deck of a battleship at night watching a film on a big white screen, stars overhead, warm glow from the screen
HEAD: In the evenings, sailors watched Hollywood films projected on screens set up on deck.
BODY: Films were a popular form of entertainment aboard ships in port. In the evenings, a large white screen was set up on deck, and men sat in rows to watch the latest releases from Hollywood. The navy distributed films to ships through a regular exchange system. Showings took place in the open air, with the harbour and the stars around the audience. Sailors' memoirs mention screens rippling in the wind and loud reactions from the crowd. At ten o'clock, the bugle sounded taps, the signal for lights out. Men returned to their bunks, and the ship grew quiet except for the hum of machinery and the steps of the watch. The officer of the deck remained on duty through the night. Even so, life in harbour was not entirely relaxed. Duty sections rotated, and some men always had to remain alert. Yet for most sailors, the evening film was a familiar and welcome end to the day.

TOPIC: Dorie Miller, a farm boy from Texas
===
IMG: texas-farm-family-1930s-porch | a Black farming family standing on the porch of a small wooden farmhouse in Texas, cotton fields and a big tree behind, warm golden evening light
HEAD: Doris Miller, known as Dorie, grew up on a farm near Waco, Texas, the third of four sons.
BODY: Doris Miller was born on the twelfth of October, 1919, near Waco, Texas. He was the third of four sons of Conery and Henrietta Miller, a Black farming family. They grew cotton and vegetables and raised livestock on a small farm. Miller worked in the fields with his brothers from a young age. At school, he played football and was known for his size and strength. Family members and neighbours later described him as kind and helpful at home, and as a regular churchgoer. By adulthood, he was more than six feet tall and powerfully built. Historians have relied on family interviews and local records to reconstruct his early life, since few documents from his childhood survive. Even so, the outline is clear. He came from a modest rural background, typical of many young men who joined the military during these years. His later fame made his family and hometown proud.
===
IMG: young-man-recruiting-office-1939 | a tall young Black man in a plain shirt and cap standing at a wooden desk in a 1939 navy recruiting office, posters of ships on the walls, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft window light
HEAD: When Dorie Miller enlisted in 1939, Black sailors could only serve in the navy's messman branch.
BODY: In September 1939, Miller enlisted in the United States Navy at a recruiting office in Dallas. He was nineteen. The navy offered steady wages, training and a chance to travel, at a time when jobs in Texas were scarce. However, the navy was racially segregated. Black sailors were not permitted to serve as gunners, radiomen, machinists or officers. They could join only the messman branch, which meant cooking, serving meals to officers, cleaning cabins and handling laundry. This policy had been in place since the end of the First World War. Civil rights organisations criticised it strongly, and the Black press reported on it often. Even so, many young Black men enlisted, seeking work and a chance to serve. Historians note that the restrictions began to change during the war, partly because of public pressure. In 1942, the navy began accepting Black recruits for other roles. Full integration came later, after a presidential order in 1948.
===
IMG: mess-attendant-white-jacket-wardroom | a tall young sailor in a white jacket carefully setting a long table with plates and cups in a ship's officers' dining room, an indoor scene, enclosed by steel walls, a plain ceiling, warm lamplight
HEAD: As a mess attendant, Miller served the officers of the battleship West Virginia.
BODY: After training at the Naval Training Station in Norfolk, Virginia, Miller was assigned in early 1940 to the battleship West Virginia. He worked as a mess attendant in the officers' wardroom and quarters. His duties included serving meals, setting tables, making beds and collecting laundry. He wore a white jacket while on duty. Accounts from officers who served with him describe him as reliable and good-natured. His closest friends aboard were other mess attendants, young Black men from Southern and Eastern states. They worked, ate and slept together in a separate compartment. Historians note that this segregation shaped every part of daily life for Black sailors. Even so, many formed strong friendships and supported one another. Navy records show that Miller was promoted to Mess Attendant Second Class during his service. His routine was quiet and largely unnoticed until the morning of the seventh of December, 1941.
===
IMG: shipboard-boxing-ring-sailors-watching | a boxing ring set up on the deck of a battleship surrounded by hundreds of cheering sailors in white uniforms, two boxers in the ring, bright afternoon sunshine
HEAD: Dorie Miller was the heavyweight boxing champion of the battleship West Virginia.
BODY: Boxing was popular in the navy in the 1930s and 1940s. Ships organised their own teams and held matches on deck, with large crowds of sailors watching from all around. Miller became the heavyweight boxing champion of the West Virginia. Contemporary accounts describe him as strong, quick and difficult to beat. His success made him well known throughout the ship. Outside the ring, shipmates described him as friendly and easy-going. He enjoyed cards, music and conversation. He sent part of his pay home to his parents each month, as many young sailors did. Biographers have drawn on interviews with shipmates and family members to build this picture. Even so, relatively few written records describe his daily life in detail. His boxing title is one of the clearest documented facts about his time aboard. It shows that he was respected by many of his shipmates well before the events that made him famous.
===
IMG: battleship-wake-open-ocean-dolphins | the wake of a grey battleship on the open blue ocean, dolphins leaping alongside, flying fish over the waves, a sailor at the rail, warm golden evening light
HEAD: Serving on the West Virginia gave Dorie Miller his first experience of the open ocean.
BODY: In 1940, the West Virginia took part in fleet exercises in the Pacific and visited several ports. For Miller, it was the first time he had been at sea. The open Pacific was vast, with flying fish, dolphins and nights full of stars far from any city lights. Many sailors from inland states described their first ocean voyages with amazement in letters home. Biographers report that Miller wrote to his family about the size of the sea. In Pearl Harbor, he often went ashore with friends, visiting beaches, cinemas and restaurants in Honolulu. Historians point out that Black sailors also faced segregation and prejudice in some places ashore. Even so, many found Hawaii's mixed population more welcoming than the segregated South. For young men from rural Texas, Louisiana or Georgia, the islands were a very different world. Life in the fleet broadened their experience in ways that few of them could have imagined at home.
===
IMG: sailor-rail-night-harbour-lights-thinking | a big young sailor leaning on the rail of a battleship at night, looking out at the lights of other ships and the shore reflected in calm water, stars overhead
HEAD: Like many young sailors, Dorie Miller hoped one day to return home and farm.
BODY: Family members later said that Miller planned to complete his service and return to Texas. He hoped to help his parents and perhaps one day own land of his own. He saved part of his pay toward that future. Such plans were common among sailors from farming families. Letters from servicemen in 1941 often mention land, homes and families as their hopes after their service. In the evenings, after the officers' meals were cleared away, mess attendants sometimes had time to go on deck. From there, the lights of the other ships and the shore were visible across the dark harbour. Historians stress that most men in the fleet expected their service to remain peaceful. War seemed possible, but distant. Even so, events were moving quickly elsewhere. Miller's plans, like those of many others, were changed by the attack. He continued to serve in the navy after the seventh of December, until his death in 1943.

TOPIC: Liberty in Honolulu
===
IMG: liberty-launch-sailors-going-ashore | a small motor launch packed with laughing young sailors in crisp white uniforms crossing a calm harbour, big grey battleships behind, bright sunny morning
HEAD: When sailors had time off at Pearl Harbor, they went on liberty, travelling ashore in small launches.
BODY: Time off for sailors was known as liberty. At weekends, small motor launches carried crowds of men from the battleships to landings around the harbour. Sailors wore clean white uniforms for liberty, and officers inspected them before they left the ship. A man with a stained uniform or a dull button could be sent back to change. From the landings, sailors took buses or taxis into Honolulu, about eight miles away, along roads through sugar cane fields. Some had saved their pay for weeks. Others had very little money. Navy regulations limited how long men could stay ashore and when they had to return. Most liberty ended at midnight. Historians note that the large number of servicemen in Hawaii in 1941 strongly affected Honolulu's economy. Shops, restaurants and entertainment businesses depended on their spending. Even so, relations between servicemen and local residents were sometimes tense. Liberty was nevertheless the highlight of many sailors' weeks.
===
IMG: hotel-street-1941-neon-sailors-evening | a busy old town street in 1941 lined with small cafés, photograph studios and souvenir shops with glowing neon signs, crowds of sailors in white uniforms strolling, warm evening light
HEAD: Hotel Street in downtown Honolulu was a favourite destination for sailors and soldiers on liberty.
BODY: Hotel Street ran through the older part of downtown Honolulu. In 1941, it was lined with cafés, cheap restaurants, shooting galleries, souvenir shops, photograph studios and tattoo parlours. It was crowded with servicemen day and night. Photograph studios offered novelty pictures, such as posing beside a hula dancer or sitting in a painted aeroplane cut-out. These photographs were popular as souvenirs to send home. Tattoo artists worked on anchors, ships and names. Restaurants served hamburgers, chili and pie. The Black Cat Café, across the street from the YMCA, was a well-known meeting place. Historians of wartime Honolulu describe Hotel Street as noisy and lively. It also had a rougher side, with bars and other businesses that worried military authorities. Even so, for many young men, it offered simple entertainment at prices they could afford. The street changed greatly after the war, though some old buildings from the period still stand.
===
IMG: sailors-bicycles-quiet-beach-palms | young sailors in casual clothes riding bicycles along a quiet coastal road past palm trees and a white empty beach, turquoise sea, warm afternoon light
HEAD: Some sailors spent their liberty cycling around Oahu or visiting its quieter beaches.
BODY: Not every sailor spent liberty in downtown Honolulu. Many went to Waikiki to swim, sunbathe and try surfing. Some rented bicycles and rode out across the island, past pineapple fields and plantation villages, to quiet beaches on the far side. The YMCA in Honolulu offered a swimming pool, a reading room, an inexpensive cafeteria and simple rooms for overnight stays. Other sailors visited churches, the library or the Honolulu Zoo. A popular excursion was the trip to the Nuuanu Pali, a mountain pass with dramatic cliffs and wide views over the windward coast. The wind there was famously strong. Letters home from sailors often describe these outings with enthusiasm. Historians use such letters to show the variety of ways servicemen experienced the islands. Even so, transport and money limited where many men could go. Still, for those who explored, Oahu offered landscapes very different from anything they had known on the mainland.
===
IMG: sailor-writing-postcard-park-bench | a young sailor in a white uniform writing a postcard on a park bench under a big banyan tree, a small wrapped parcel beside him, soft afternoon light
HEAD: In December 1941, sailors sent Hawaiian souvenirs home as Christmas presents.
BODY: Letter writing was a major part of military life in 1941. Sailors wrote to parents, siblings, wives and girlfriends, describing the islands, their ships and their friends. Many asked for news from home about family, pets and local sports teams. As December began, letters increasingly mentioned Christmas. Sailors bought presents in Honolulu shops, such as grass skirts, ukuleles and printed silk scarves, and mailed them to the mainland. Parcels from home arrived in return, often containing fruitcake, cookies and knitted socks. Mail had to travel by ship, so delays of several weeks were normal. Museums and archives now hold many letters written by servicemen at Pearl Harbor in late 1941. Some were mailed only days before the attack. Families later treasured them. Historians value them as records of everyday thoughts and plans. Even so, most of these letters are simple and cheerful. They show young men preparing for an ordinary Christmas far from home.
===
IMG: navy-family-small-house-garden-evening | a young navy family on the porch of a small wooden house with a garden, children running down the path to meet their father in a white uniform, warm golden evening light
HEAD: Some married sailors and officers lived in Hawaii with their families.
BODY: Most young sailors were unmarried, but many older enlisted men and officers had families with them in Hawaii. They lived in rented houses and apartments in Honolulu and nearby towns, or in navy housing on Ford Island and at the Navy Yard. When they were off duty, they returned home in the evening. At weekends, families went to beaches, parks and on drives around the island. Interviews with people who were children in Hawaii at the time often describe a relaxed and pleasant family life. Housing was sometimes scarce and expensive, however, because the military population had grown so quickly. Navy records show a large rise in the number of dependents in Hawaii during 1940 and 1941. After the attack, many families were evacuated to the mainland in the following months. Historians note how suddenly their lives changed. Before December, many families considered their posting to Hawaii one of the most enjoyable of their military careers.
===
IMG: big-band-dance-hall-sailors-couples | young couples dancing in a big hall, sailors in white uniforms and young women in their best dresses, a swing band playing on a stage, paper decorations, warm golden light
HEAD: In late 1941, bands from the fleet's ships competed in a contest called the Battle of Music.
BODY: Dances were held every weekend in Honolulu's clubs, hotels and dance halls. Big bands played swing music, and couples danced the jitterbug and the foxtrot. In the autumn of 1941, the navy organised a series of contests between ships' bands, known as the Battle of Music. Competitions took place at Bloch Arena, a large new recreation hall at the Navy Yard. Each band played a set, and the audience judged the performances. Sailors cheered loudly for their own ships. Semi-final rounds were held through the autumn, and the final was planned for the twentieth of December. Bands from several battleships took part, including the band of the Arizona. Accounts from surviving musicians describe the strong but friendly rivalry. The final was never held. After the attack, the trophy was later named in honour of the Arizona's band, all of whose members had died. The contest remains a poignant detail of the last weeks of peace.

TOPIC: A world far away at war
===
IMG: family-radio-news-living-room-1941 | a family sitting quietly around a big wooden radio in a small living room in 1941, a lamp glowing, a newspaper on the table, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm lamplight
HEAD: By late 1941, war had been underway in Europe for more than two years and in China for four.
BODY: In the autumn of 1941, much of the world was at war. In Europe, fighting had begun in September 1939. Germany had conquered much of the continent and in June 1941 had invaded the Soviet Union. Britain continued to resist, under attack from the air and at sea. In Asia, Japan had been at war with China since 1937, occupying much of its coast and many major cities. In 1940 and 1941, Japanese forces also moved into French Indochina, the region of present-day Vietnam, Laos and Cambodia. The United States was not yet at war. Public opinion polls of the period show that most Americans wanted to stay out of the fighting. Many remembered the losses of the First World War. Even so, the United States was supplying Britain and other countries with weapons and materials. In Hawaii, families followed the news on the radio. The fighting still seemed far away from the islands.
===
IMG: harbour-oil-tanks-hills-afternoon | large round fuel tanks standing on a green hillside above a harbour with grey ships, sugar cane fields nearby, soft afternoon light
HEAD: In 1941, Japan depended heavily on imported oil, much of it from the United States.
BODY: Japan had few natural resources of its own. Its industry and armed forces relied on imported oil, steel and rubber. Much of its oil had come from the United States. In July 1941, after Japanese forces moved into southern French Indochina, the American government froze Japanese assets. This effectively cut off oil supplies. Britain and the Dutch government in exile took similar measures. Japanese leaders calculated that their oil reserves would last only a limited time. They faced a choice between withdrawing from China and Indochina, as the United States demanded, or seizing oil-rich territories in Southeast Asia, such as the Dutch East Indies. Diplomats from both countries continued negotiations in Washington through the autumn. Historians have studied these talks in great detail. Neither side was willing to accept the other's main demands. Even so, some officials on both sides hoped until the last weeks that war could be avoided.
===
IMG: admiral-study-calligraphy-quiet-evening | a thoughtful Japanese naval admiral in a dark uniform sitting at a low wooden desk with brushes and paper in a quiet study, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft lamplight
HEAD: Admiral Isoroku Yamamoto, who planned the attack, had studied in the United States and opposed war with it.
BODY: Admiral Isoroku Yamamoto commanded Japan's Combined Fleet in 1941. He was born in 1884, making him fifty-seven at the time. As a young officer, he studied at Harvard University from 1919 to 1921. Later, he served as naval attaché at the Japanese embassy in Washington. He travelled widely in the United States and was well aware of its industrial strength. He was known for his skill at card games and the board game shogi. Yamamoto argued against war with the United States, warning that Japan could not win a long conflict. Historians often quote his reported remark that he could run wild for six months, but had no confidence after that. Once war became the policy of the government, however, he planned for it. He believed a surprise attack on the Pacific Fleet at Pearl Harbor offered the best chance of success. His plan was approved by the Japanese Naval General Staff in the autumn of 1941.
===
IMG: northern-bay-snow-ships-anchored-fog | grey warships anchored in a remote cold bay with snow-covered hills, thick fog lying low over the still dark water, pale grey light
HEAD: The Japanese strike force assembled secretly in a remote bay in the Kuril Islands.
BODY: In November 1941, a Japanese naval force gathered in Hitokappu Bay, also known as Tankan Bay, on the island of Etorofu in the Kuril Islands. The location was remote, cold and often covered in fog. The force included six aircraft carriers, carrying more than four hundred aircraft, along with battleships, cruisers, destroyers, submarines and tankers. On the twenty-sixth of November, the fleet departed and sailed east across the North Pacific. It followed a northern route, far from normal shipping lanes, where few other vessels were likely to see it. The ships maintained strict radio silence and showed no lights at night. Refuelling at sea in rough weather was difficult but successful. Historians describe the voyage as a remarkable feat of planning and seamanship. American intelligence did not detect the fleet's departure. Even so, Japanese commanders were prepared to turn back if the force was discovered or if negotiations in Washington succeeded.
===
IMG: young-pilots-ship-quarters-letters-lamp | young Japanese airmen in plain uniforms sitting quietly in a small ship's room writing letters home by lamplight, an indoor scene, enclosed by steel walls, a plain ceiling, soft warm light
HEAD: Many of the Japanese airmen in the attack were young men, close in age to the American sailors at Pearl Harbor.
BODY: During the voyage east, Japanese pilots and aircrew studied maps and models of Pearl Harbor. They reviewed their targets and checked their aircraft. Many also wrote letters to their families, knowing they might not return. Many of the airmen were in their twenties, close in age to the American sailors in the harbour. Diaries and letters from Japanese servicemen of the period mention families, home towns and seasonal images such as cherry blossoms. Some wrote poems, as was common in Japanese military culture. Historians who study these personal documents emphasise the human side of both forces. Most of the young men involved had not chosen the war. They served within systems and decisions made by their governments. Even so, they carried out their orders. Fifty-five Japanese airmen died during the attack. Their letters, like those of American servicemen, are now held in archives and museums and are read by visitors on both sides of the Pacific.
===
IMG: christmas-trees-honolulu-store-palm | rows of fresh green Christmas trees for sale outside a shop under tall palm trees in a sunny town street, shoppers in light clothes, warm afternoon light
HEAD: In early December 1941, Honolulu prepared for Christmas, unaware that a Japanese fleet was approaching.
BODY: American commanders in Hawaii knew that war with Japan was possible. On the twenty-seventh of November, Washington sent a formal war warning to Pacific commanders. However, most officials expected any Japanese attack to fall on the Philippines, Malaya or the Dutch East Indies. Few believed that Japan would strike as far east as Hawaii. In the first week of December, life on Oahu continued normally. Ships came and went, sailors went on liberty and bands practised for the Battle of Music. Shops in Honolulu displayed Christmas decorations. Fresh Christmas trees arrived by ship from the mainland and were sold outside stores. The fleet's aircraft carriers were away from Pearl Harbor that week, delivering planes to Wake and Midway islands. Historians consider their absence one of the most significant facts of the attack. Even so, no one in Hawaii knew it would matter. To residents, it was simply an ordinary early December.
