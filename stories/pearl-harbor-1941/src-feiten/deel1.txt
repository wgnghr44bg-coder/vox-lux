TOPIC: An island in the middle of the ocean
===
IMG: oahu-green-mountains-sea-dawn-1941 | steep green mountain ridges rising from a calm turquoise sea with coral reefs, soft white clouds on the peaks, a rainbow over a valley, gentle dawn light
HEAD: In 1941, Hawaii was not yet a state, and most visitors reached it after five days at sea.
BODY: Hawaii in 1941 was a territory of the United States, not a state. It lay more than two thousand miles from California, in the middle of the Pacific Ocean. Most people travelled there by passenger ship, a voyage of four or five days. A small number arrived by flying boat. Pan American Airways operated large aircraft called Clippers, which landed in the harbour after a flight of nearly twenty hours. About four hundred thousand people lived in the islands, most of them on Oahu. The island rose steeply from the sea, with long green ridges along its centre and coral reefs along the shore. Rain fell often on the mountains, and rainbows were common over the valleys. Census figures from 1940 show how small and distant the territory still was. Even so, it was growing quickly. For many visitors from the mainland, Hawaii seemed remote and almost foreign, though it was part of their own country.
===
IMG: aloha-tower-boat-day-crowds-leis | a crowd on a pier beside a tall white clock tower welcoming a big white passenger ship, people holding flower garlands, musicians with guitars, warm morning light
HEAD: The arrival of a passenger ship in Honolulu was a public celebration known as Boat Day.
BODY: When a large passenger liner reached Honolulu, much of the town came down to the harbour. Ships of the Matson line, such as the Lurline and the Matsonia, arrived in the morning after passing Diamond Head. Local boys swam out to meet them and dived for coins thrown from the decks. At the pier stood the Aloha Tower, a white clock tower opened in 1926. A band played Hawaiian music, and hula dancers performed for the arriving passengers. As visitors came down the gangway, they received leis, garlands of fresh flowers such as plumeria, ginger and orchids. The word aloha was used as a greeting and a farewell. Photographs and newsreels from the 1930s show large crowds on these occasions. The custom was promoted by the shipping lines and the tourist industry. Even so, it also reflected genuine local hospitality. Boat Day became one of the best-remembered rituals of travel to Hawaii before the war.
===
IMG: palm-trees-hibiscus-garden-trade-wind | a quiet garden with swaying palm trees, red hibiscus and white plumeria flowers, a light shower falling in sunshine, soft golden afternoon light
HEAD: Oahu's climate was mild all year, with frequent short showers that fell even in sunshine.
BODY: The weather on Oahu was warm but rarely hot, and it was never cold. Trade winds blew from the northeast on most days, bringing cool air from the ocean. They carried clouds over the mountains, where much of the rain fell. Short showers passed over the lower ground as well, often while the sun was still shining. Weather records from Honolulu show only small differences between summer and winter temperatures. Gardens were full of flowering plants, many of them introduced from other tropical regions. Hibiscus, bougainvillea, plumeria and flame trees were common in the town. Visitors from mainland cities often wrote about the softness of the light and the scent of flowers after rain. Even so, the climate varied considerably across the island. The windward side was much wetter than the leeward side, and the mountain peaks received heavy rainfall. For residents, the mild climate shaped daily life, from clothing to houses with open verandas.
===
IMG: plantation-workers-pineapple-fields-mountains | farm workers in wide straw hats walking between long rows of pineapple plants on red earth, green mountains behind them, warm evening light
HEAD: By 1941, more than a third of Hawaii's population was of Japanese ancestry.
BODY: Hawaii's population came from many places. Native Hawaiians were descended from Polynesian voyagers who had reached the islands more than a thousand years earlier, navigating by the stars and the ocean swells. In the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, plantation owners recruited workers from across Asia and Europe. Large numbers came from Japan, as well as from China, Korea, the Philippines and Portugal. By 1940, census figures showed that about thirty-seven percent of the territory's people were of Japanese ancestry. Americans from the mainland worked as teachers, merchants, officials and members of the armed forces. Children of different backgrounds attended school together. A local language known as pidgin developed, combining words from Hawaiian, English, Japanese, Portuguese and other languages. Even so, the society was not equal. Plantation owners and mainland Americans held most of the economic and political power. Yet daily life in the islands was unusually mixed for an American community of that time.
===
IMG: taro-terraces-windward-valley-cliffs | flooded green taro terraces in a quiet valley beneath tall green cliffs, a family working knee-deep in water, misty rain on the mountains, soft evening light
HEAD: Traditional Hawaiian society divided each island into land units called ahupua'a, running from the mountains to the sea.
BODY: In the traditional Hawaiian system, an island was divided into long wedges of land. Each one, called an ahupua'a, usually stretched from the mountain ridge down to the reef. It contained everything a community needed. Upland forests provided timber, plants and feathers. Valleys held irrigated terraces where taro was grown. Taro is a plant with a starchy root, and it was the staple food of old Hawaii. The coast and reef provided fish and shellfish. People in each ahupua'a shared resources and were expected to care for the land and water. Historians and Hawaiian scholars describe this as a careful system of resource management. By 1941, most land was organised in very different ways, and plantations covered much of it. Yet in some windward valleys, families still cultivated taro in flooded terraces in the traditional manner. Efforts to restore taro farming and the ahupua'a approach continue in Hawaii today.
===
IMG: sugar-cane-fields-wind-plantation-houses | tall green sugar cane fields swaying in the wind beside a small village of wooden plantation houses, smoke from a cooking fire, warm golden evening light
HEAD: In 1941, sugar and pineapple plantations covered much of Oahu's farmland.
BODY: Plantation agriculture dominated Hawaii's economy before the Second World War. Sugar cane grew on the flat central plains of Oahu, taller than a person and swaying in the wind. Pineapples grew in long rows on the red upland soils. Hawaii was then one of the world's leading producers of canned pineapple. Workers went into the fields early in the morning, wearing wide hats, long sleeves and gloves to protect themselves from the sun and the sharp leaves. Many lived in plantation camps, groups of small wooden houses provided by the companies. At lunchtime, workers often shared food from their tin lunch boxes, a custom that helped create Hawaii's mixed local cuisine. Historians of the plantations describe long hours, low pay and strict supervision. Workers organised strikes at various times to demand better conditions. Even so, the camps also developed strong community life, with shared festivals, sports and schools. Many island families trace their history to these plantations.
===
IMG: pearl-harbor-lochs-from-hills-morning | a wide calm natural harbour with several arms of water reaching inland among low green hills and sugar fields, a flat little island in the middle, soft morning light
HEAD: The Hawaiian name for Pearl Harbor was Wai Momi, meaning pearl waters.
BODY: Pearl Harbor lies on the southern coast of Oahu, a few miles west of Honolulu. It is a large natural harbour with several branching inlets, called lochs, and a flat island in the middle. The Hawaiian name, Wai Momi, means pearl waters. Pearl oysters once lived in its shallow lagoons, and Hawaiians built stone-walled fishponds along its shores. For centuries, the area supported fishing and farming communities. In the late nineteenth century, the United States gained rights to use the harbour, and in the early twentieth century the navy began dredging the entrance channel and building a base. By 1941, the pearl beds had long disappeared, partly because of silt and development. The old fishponds were mostly gone too. Archaeologists have recorded remains of some of them. Even so, the name survived in English as Pearl Harbor. It preserved a trace of the harbour's earlier life as a place of fish, pearls and quiet waters.

TOPIC: Honolulu, an easy-going town
===
IMG: honolulu-street-1941-shops-palms | a downtown street in 1941 with low stone and wooden buildings, shady verandas and balconies, palm trees and a few old cars, people in light clothes, warm morning light
HEAD: Iolani Palace, the former home of Hawaii's monarchs, stood in the centre of Honolulu.
BODY: Honolulu in 1941 had about two hundred thousand residents. Its downtown streets were lined with low stone and wooden buildings, many with shaded verandas. At the centre stood Iolani Palace, completed in 1882 for King Kalakaua. It was a handsome building with columns and wide verandas, surrounded by large banyan trees. The last Hawaiian monarch, Queen Liliuokalani, lived there until the overthrow of the kingdom in 1893. In 1941, the palace was used as the capitol building of the territorial government. Officials worked in rooms that had once been used by royalty. The palace was restored in later decades and opened as a museum in 1978. It is often described as the only official royal residence in the United States. Historians see it as a symbol of Hawaii's history as an independent kingdom. Even in 1941, it was a reminder that the islands had a long political history before they became an American territory.
===
IMG: honolulu-sidewalk-aloha-shirts-cotton-dresses | people strolling along a shady sidewalk in 1941, men in white suits and flowered shirts, women in bright cotton dresses with a flower behind one ear, palm trees, soft afternoon light
HEAD: Brightly printed aloha shirts became popular in Honolulu during the 1930s.
BODY: In the 1930s, shirts printed with flowers, palm trees and island scenes became fashionable in Honolulu. They were known as aloha shirts. Local tailors and shops, some of them run by Chinese and Japanese families, produced them using colourful fabrics. Advertisements from the period show that by the late 1930s they were popular with both residents and tourists. Many men also wore light-coloured suits suited to the warm climate. Women often wore cotton dresses in bright colours, and some wore a flower behind one ear. Local tradition held that a flower behind the left ear signalled that a woman was attached, while one behind the right ear meant she was not. Street vendors sold newspapers and shaved ice with sweet syrup. Visitors often described the pace of life as relaxed. Even so, Honolulu was a working city with offices, shops and a busy port. The aloha shirt later became a lasting symbol of Hawaiian style.
===
IMG: fish-market-chinatown-morning-1941 | a busy covered fish market in the morning with fresh fish laid out on ice, wooden stalls of mangoes, papayas and pineapples, shoppers with baskets, soft morning light
HEAD: Honolulu's Chinatown fish market sold catch landed only hours earlier, and the town's food mixed many cuisines.
BODY: Every morning, the fish markets in Honolulu's Chinatown were busy with buyers. Tuna, mahi-mahi and snapper were laid out on ice, and sellers called out prices in several languages. Nearby shops sold dried fish, noodles, rice, ginger and tropical fruit such as mangoes, papayas and lychees. Food in Honolulu reflected the many communities living there. Hawaiian dishes included poi, made from pounded taro, and laulau, pork and fish wrapped in leaves and steamed. Japanese families brought miso soup and noodles. Chinese restaurants served dumplings, and Portuguese bakers made sweet bread. Korean, Filipino and American dishes were also common. Food historians describe this mixture as the origin of modern Hawaiian local food, including the plate lunch. Even so, many families kept their own traditions at home. The markets of Chinatown were a meeting place for all these influences. Some of the same fish markets and shops still operate in the district today.
===
IMG: waikiki-beach-1941-surfers-diamond-head | a long curve of golden sand lined with palm trees, surfers on long wooden boards riding gentle waves, an outrigger canoe, a volcanic crater at the end of the beach, warm evening light
HEAD: Duke Kahanamoku, a Hawaiian Olympic champion, helped spread surfing around the world.
BODY: Waikiki Beach curved for about two miles east of downtown Honolulu, with Diamond Head at its far end. Waves rolled in from the reef long and gentle, ideal for swimming and surfing. Hawaiian beachboys taught visitors to surf on long wooden boards and took them out in outrigger canoes. The most famous Hawaiian surfer was Duke Kahanamoku. He won Olympic gold medals in swimming in 1912 and 1920. During his travels, he gave surfing demonstrations in Australia and California, which helped introduce the sport in those places. Surfing had deep roots in Hawaiian culture, where it was once practised by chiefs and commoners alike. It had declined during the nineteenth century and was revived in the early twentieth. Historians credit Kahanamoku with much of that revival's international reach. He later served as sheriff of Honolulu. A statue of him now stands at Waikiki. In 1941, he was already a celebrated figure in the islands.
===
IMG: pink-hotel-palms-beach-terrace-evening | a large pale pink hotel with towers and arches among palm trees and flower gardens on a beach, guests on a terrace, warm golden evening light
HEAD: The Royal Hawaiian Hotel at Waikiki was known as the Pink Palace of the Pacific.
BODY: The Royal Hawaiian Hotel opened in 1927 on the beach at Waikiki. It was built in a Spanish and Moorish style, with towers and arches, and painted a distinctive pink. People called it the Pink Palace of the Pacific. Wealthy guests arrived on the Matson liners and often stayed for weeks. Film stars, business leaders and their families were among them. Some brought large trunks of clothing and even their own cars. In the evenings, guests dined on terraces facing the ocean while bands played. The hotel was owned by the same company as the shipping line, which promoted travel to the islands. Even so, the beach itself was used by local residents as well as hotel guests. During the war, the hotel was used by the navy as a rest centre for submarine crews. It reopened to the public afterwards and still operates today. It remains one of the best-known landmarks at Waikiki.
===
IMG: wooden-house-porch-valley-rain-evening | a small wooden house with a tin roof and a deep porch in a green valley, banana plants and papaya trees in the garden, light evening rain, warm lamplight from the windows
HEAD: Most families in Honolulu lived in small wooden houses with corrugated metal roofs and deep porches.
BODY: Ordinary residents of Honolulu lived very differently from hotel guests. Many families occupied small wooden houses with corrugated metal roofs and wide porches, built in neighbourhoods that climbed the valleys behind the town. Yards were often planted with bananas, papayas and flowers. Rain fell frequently in the valleys, especially in the evenings, carried by the trade winds. Families spent much of their time on their porches, which were cooler than the interiors. Radios were common by 1941 and brought news and music into many homes. Children often walked to school barefoot or in simple sandals. After school, they swam, fished and played outdoors. At weekends, families picnicked at beaches around the island. Oral history projects in Hawaii have recorded many memories of this period from people who grew up in these neighbourhoods. Even so, housing conditions varied widely. Some areas were crowded, and many families had modest incomes. Yet the outdoor life of the islands shaped childhood for almost everyone.
===
IMG: steel-guitar-player-porch-evening | a Hawaiian man playing a guitar laid flat across his lap with a small metal bar on the porch of a wooden house, palm trees and a calm sea behind, warm golden evening light
HEAD: The Hawaiian steel guitar is said to have been developed by a schoolboy named Joseph Kekuku in the 1880s.
BODY: Much traditional Hawaiian music features a gliding, sliding sound. It comes from the steel guitar, which is played flat across the lap. Instead of pressing the strings with the fingers, the player slides a smooth metal bar along them. According to the most widely told account, a Hawaiian schoolboy named Joseph Kekuku discovered the technique in the 1880s. He is said to have run a metal object along his guitar strings and liked the sound. He practised for years and later toured the United States and Europe, performing for large audiences. Music historians note that other players may have developed similar techniques around the same time. Even so, Kekuku is widely credited as its pioneer. In the early twentieth century, Hawaiian music became extremely popular on the American mainland. The steel guitar later influenced country music and other styles. By 1941, its sound was closely associated with Hawaii and was heard regularly on island radio.
===
IMG: hawaiian-musicians-ukulele-evening-lanai | Hawaiian musicians with ukuleles and a steel guitar playing on a wooden veranda in the evening, families listening, paper lanterns glowing, palm trees, warm lamplight
HEAD: On some nights, a Honolulu radio station broadcast music until dawn to guide aircraft flying from California.
BODY: Radio played an important role in island life by 1941. Honolulu had two main stations, KGU and KGMB. They broadcast news, music and entertainment throughout the day. Programmes of Hawaiian music, with steel guitars, ukuleles and singing, were popular in the evenings. A radio show called Hawaii Calls, broadcast from Waikiki from 1935, carried Hawaiian music to listeners on the mainland. Radio also served a practical purpose. When aircraft were flying to Hawaii from California, a station sometimes stayed on the air through the night. Pilots used its signal to help guide them to the island. Historians mention that this was one reason a radio station was broadcasting music early on the morning of the seventh of December. In the evenings, many residents also went to the cinema or to dances. Some attended a lu'au, a feast where a whole pig was cooked in an underground oven of heated stones.

TOPIC: The fleet comes to Hawaii
===
IMG: battleship-row-ford-island-morning-calm | a row of big grey battleships moored one behind another beside a low flat island with palm trees, calm blue harbour water, green hills behind, soft Sunday morning light
HEAD: The United States Pacific Fleet moved its main base from California to Pearl Harbor in 1940.
BODY: Until 1940, the main body of the Pacific Fleet was based on the coast of California. In the spring of that year, the fleet went to Hawaii for exercises. President Franklin Roosevelt then decided to keep it there. The aim was to show Japan that the United States was paying close attention to events in Asia. Several senior officers disagreed. The commander at the time, Admiral James Richardson, argued that Pearl Harbor lacked adequate facilities and was too far from supply bases. He was replaced in early 1941. The fleet remained. Fuel, supplies and many workers had to be brought across the ocean by ship. Construction expanded rapidly, with new barracks, housing and workshops. Historians see the decision as part of a wider effort to deter Japan. Even so, the move placed a large part of the American fleet in one harbour, far from the mainland. By late 1941, Pearl Harbor was one of the busiest naval bases in the world.
===
IMG: destroyers-moored-nests-harbour-hills | several slim grey destroyers moored side by side in a group in a calm harbour inlet, green hills and sugar fields behind, soft afternoon light
HEAD: At Pearl Harbor, destroyers were often moored side by side in groups called nests.
BODY: In late 1941, the harbour held many types of warship. Battleships were the largest, each more than six hundred feet long. There were also cruisers, destroyers, submarines, minelayers, supply ships, repair ships and tugboats. A white hospital ship, the Solace, was anchored in the harbour as well. Destroyers were commonly moored together in groups of several ships, tied side by side to a buoy or tender. Sailors called these groups nests. To reach shore from an outer ship, crew members crossed the decks of the others. Submarines were based at their own facility on the eastern side of the harbour. Aircraft carriers usually berthed on the western side of Ford Island, but none were in port on the seventh of December. Navy records list more than ninety vessels present that weekend. Even so, the busy harbour looked calm from the surrounding hills. Photographs taken in 1941 show long grey ships lying still on the water.
===
IMG: ford-island-houses-lawns-palms-seaplanes | white officers' houses with green lawns and palm trees on a flat island, seaplanes on a ramp and hangars in the distance, calm harbour water, soft afternoon light
HEAD: Ford Island, in the middle of Pearl Harbor, held a naval air station and homes for officers' families.
BODY: Ford Island is a low, flat island about a mile and a half long, in the centre of Pearl Harbor. In 1941, it was the site of a naval air station, with runways, hangars and a control tower. Seaplanes were parked on ramps along the water. The island also had housing for officers and their families, with gardens, lawns and a swimming pool at the officers' club. Children lived there and played in its quiet streets. Along the southeastern shore was a line of large concrete mooring quays. Battleships tied up there, one behind another, and sometimes two side by side. This line became known as Battleship Row. Accounts written later by people who grew up on Ford Island describe it as a pleasant, almost suburban place. Even so, it was also an active military installation. Today, the island remains part of the naval base. It contains memorials and museums, including the battleship Missouri and an aviation museum.
===
IMG: battleship-arizona-moored-1941-calm | a large grey battleship with a tall tripod mast and big gun turrets moored in a calm harbour, sailors in white on deck, green hills behind, soft golden light
HEAD: The battleships at Pearl Harbor were named after American states, and the Arizona was launched in 1915.
BODY: American battleships were traditionally named after states. On the morning of the seventh of December, Battleship Row held the California, the Maryland, the Oklahoma, the Tennessee, the West Virginia, the Arizona and the Nevada. The Pennsylvania, the fleet's flagship, was in dry dock across the channel. The Arizona had been built at the New York Navy Yard and launched in 1915. She was six hundred and eight feet long and carried twelve fourteen-inch guns in four turrets. Her crew numbered about fifteen hundred men. She had been modernised in the early 1930s, with new engines and a tripod mast. Sailors often spoke of their ships with strong loyalty, much as people speak of their home towns. Letters and memoirs from crew members reflect this pride. Even so, the Arizona was one of the older battleships in the fleet. Today, her sunken hull lies in the harbour beneath the white memorial that bears her name.
===
IMG: navy-yard-workers-cranes-dry-dock | a busy navy yard with tall cranes, warehouses and workshops beside a dry dock holding a large grey ship, workers in overalls walking, warm afternoon light
HEAD: The Pearl Harbor Navy Yard operated like a small town, with workshops, dry docks and its own services.
BODY: Across the channel from Ford Island lay the Navy Yard. It contained workshops, warehouses, offices, power plants and dry docks, where ships could be lifted out of the water for repair. Large cranes stood along the quays. The yard also had a hospital, a fire station, a post office, a cinema and recreation buildings. Thousands of people worked there in 1941, many of them civilians from Hawaii. They included welders, machinists, carpenters, electricians and clerks. Employment records show that the workforce grew rapidly in the months before the war. Work went on through the day to the sound of hammers and riveting. At the end of the shift, workers left by bus and ferry for homes around Honolulu and the surrounding districts. After the attack, the yard's workers played a major role in repairing and salvaging damaged ships. Historians often highlight their contribution. The yard remains an active naval shipyard today.
===
IMG: army-barracks-green-valley-mountains | long rows of pale army barracks with lawns and big shade trees in a wide green valley between mountains, soldiers walking in khaki, soft afternoon light
HEAD: Schofield Barracks, in central Oahu, was the largest army post in the United States in 1941.
BODY: Pearl Harbor was only one of many military installations on Oahu. Hickam Field, an army air base, lay just to the southeast of the harbour. Wheeler Field, in the centre of the island, was home to army fighter aircraft. Next to it was Schofield Barracks, then the largest army post in the United States. It housed thousands of soldiers in long rows of barracks with lawns and shade trees. On the windward side, at Kaneohe Bay, the navy operated a station for patrol flying boats. In total, more than forty thousand soldiers, sailors and airmen were stationed on Oahu in 1941. At weekends, many of them came into Honolulu. Letters written home by servicemen often described Hawaii as a pleasant posting. Even so, military life involved regular training, inspections and long hours. Schofield Barracks is still an active army post today. It was also the setting for the novel From Here to Eternity, published in 1951.
===
IMG: boy-bicycle-palm-road-battleships-behind | a young boy riding a bicycle along a quiet road lined with palm trees, big grey battleships moored in the harbour behind him, warm late afternoon light
HEAD: Children growing up on Ford Island learned to recognise the battleships by the shapes of their masts.
BODY: For children living on Ford Island, the naval base was an ordinary part of daily life. They rode bicycles along roads lined with palm trees, swam in the officers' club pool and fished from the sea walls. Many learned to identify each battleship by its masts and silhouette. Every morning, they heard ships' bands playing as flags were raised, and in the evening they heard bugles across the water. To reach Honolulu, they took a small ferry across the channel and then a bus. Several people who were children on Ford Island in 1941 later wrote memoirs or gave interviews to oral history projects. They often described those years as a happy time. Some returned decades later and pointed out where each ship had been moored. Even so, their memories also include the events of the seventh of December. Their accounts offer a rare view of the base as a family community, not only as a military installation.
===
IMG: admiral-office-window-harbour-view | a white-haired naval admiral in a white uniform standing at an office window looking down over a harbour full of grey ships, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft afternoon light
HEAD: In 1941, Admiral Husband Kimmel commanded the Pacific Fleet, and General Walter Short commanded the army in Hawaii.
BODY: Admiral Husband Kimmel took command of the United States Pacific Fleet in February 1941. He was fifty-eight years old, from Kentucky, and had spent his entire career in the navy. Colleagues described him as serious, hard-working and demanding. His headquarters overlooked the harbour. General Walter Short commanded the army's Hawaiian Department, which was responsible for defending the islands and the base. The two men were on friendly terms and sometimes played golf together on Sunday mornings. Like most American officials, they expected that if war with Japan came, it would begin far to the west, perhaps in the Philippines or Southeast Asia. After the attack, both men were relieved of their commands. Several official inquiries examined their decisions. Historians continue to debate how much responsibility they bore, given the information available to them. In 1999, the United States Senate passed a resolution suggesting that they had been treated unfairly, though no official change was made.
===
IMG: harbour-sunset-ships-lights-reflections | a calm harbour at sunset with grey ships and their lights reflected in long wavering lines on golden water, palm trees dark against an orange and pink sky
HEAD: In the evenings, Pearl Harbor was usually calm, with the lights of the ships reflected in the water.
BODY: At sunset, the sun went down behind the Waianae Mountains to the west of Pearl Harbor. The sky turned orange and pink, and the calm water reflected the changing colours. As darkness fell, lights came on aboard the ships and along the shore. Sailors who were off duty often sat on deck in the cool evening air. Music from radios drifted across the water. Small boats carried officers and men to and from the landings. Frogs and insects called from the shore. Many sailors later described these evenings as among the most peaceful moments of their time in Hawaii. Letters home from the autumn of 1941 often mention the beauty of the harbour at night. Even so, the fleet maintained watches and duty crews at all times. Training continued during the week, and tension with Japan was growing. Yet in the evening light, the harbour looked quiet and settled, much as it had for months.
