TOPIC: An island in the middle of the ocean
===
IMG: oahu-green-mountains-sea-dawn-1941 | steep green mountain ridges rising from a calm turquoise sea with coral reefs, soft white clouds on the peaks, a rainbow over a valley, gentle dawn light
HEAD: In 1941, Hawaii wasn't yet a state, and it took most people five days to get there.
BODY: The island of Oahu rises steeply out of the Pacific. Picture yourself leaning on the rail of a big white ship, with nothing but blue ocean all around you. Long green ridges run down its middle, with their tops hidden in soft white cloud. Rain falls gently on the high slopes, and rainbows come and go over the valleys. Along the shore, waves break white on the coral reefs. Inside the reefs, the water is pale green and very clear. In 1941, Hawaii wasn't yet a state of the United States. It was a territory, a faraway part of the country. It lay more than two thousand miles from California. Most people came by ship, on a voyage of four or five days across the open ocean. A lucky few came by flying boat. These big planes were called Clippers. They landed in the harbour after a flight of nearly twenty hours. About four hundred thousand people lived on the islands. Most of them lived on Oahu. You'd count the days, watching the sea change colour. For many visitors, it was the most beautiful place they had ever seen.

IMG: aloha-tower-boat-day-crowds-leis | a crowd on a pier beside a tall white clock tower welcoming a big white passenger ship, people holding flower garlands, musicians with guitars, warm morning light
HEAD: When a passenger ship arrived in Honolulu, the whole town came down to watch.
BODY: The arrival of a passenger ship was a great event. You'd hear a band playing on the pier long before the ship was tied up. The big white liners had names like the Lurline and the Matsonia. They came into the harbour in the morning, past the old crater called Diamond Head. Boys swam out to meet them and dived for coins in the clear water. At the pier stood a tall white clock tower, called the Aloha Tower. A crowd waited beside it. A band played Hawaiian music, with guitars and ukuleles and soft singing. There were hula dancers in grass skirts. As the passengers came down the gangway, people placed leis around their necks. These were garlands of fresh flowers, plumeria and ginger and orchids. They smelled sweet and felt cool. People said aloha, which means hello, goodbye and love, all at once. The islanders called this Boat Day. Can you imagine a whole town turning out just to say hello? You'd feel a flower garland slipped over your head as you came ashore. On Boat Day, Honolulu did exactly that.

IMG: palm-trees-hibiscus-garden-trade-wind | a quiet garden with swaying palm trees, red hibiscus and white plumeria flowers, a light shower falling in sunshine, soft golden afternoon light
HEAD: On Oahu, it rained while the sun was shining, and nobody minded at all.
BODY: The air in Hawaii was soft and warm all year round. You'd feel a soft, warm shower on your arms, and look up to see blue sky. It was never very hot and never cold. Almost every day, the trade winds blew gently from the northeast. They came cool and fresh off the ocean. They rustled the palm trees and carried the clouds over the mountains. Short showers came and went, often while the sun was still shining. Afterwards, the air smelled of wet earth and flowers. And there were flowers everywhere. Hibiscus bloomed red and yellow and pink in every garden. Bougainvillea climbed over the walls. Plumeria trees dropped white and yellow blossoms on the paths. Great flame trees glowed scarlet against the green hills. Visitors from cold cities often wrote home about the light. They said it was softer and brighter than anything they knew. Have you ever felt a warm rain on your face, with the sun on your back? Wouldn't you love a shower like that on a hot afternoon? On Oahu, that happened almost every afternoon.
===
IMG: plantation-workers-pineapple-fields-mountains | farm workers in wide straw hats walking between long rows of pineapple plants on red earth, green mountains behind them, warm evening light
HEAD: More than a third of the people in the islands had families that came from Japan.
BODY: The people of the islands came from many places. You'd hear many languages on a single walk through town. There were Native Hawaiians, whose ancestors had sailed here in great canoes more than a thousand years earlier. They had found their way across the ocean by the stars. Then, in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, workers came from far away to work on the plantations. They came from Japan, in great numbers. By 1941, more than a third of everyone in the islands had Japanese parents or grandparents. Others came from China, Korea, the Philippines and Portugal. And there were Americans from the mainland, too. There were teachers and shopkeepers, soldiers and sailors and their families. They all lived side by side. Their children went to school together. They ate each other's food. They even spoke a mixture of each other's languages, which the islanders called pidgin. A child might say a word in Hawaiian, a word in Japanese and a word in English, all in one sentence.

IMG: taro-terraces-windward-valley-cliffs | flooded green taro terraces in a quiet valley beneath tall green cliffs, a family working knee-deep in water, misty rain on the mountains, soft evening light
HEAD: The old Hawaiians divided their island into slices, running from the mountains down to the sea.
BODY: In the old Hawaiian way of life, the island was divided into long slices of land. You could walk from the cool green mountains all the way down to the sea without leaving one slice. Each slice ran from the top of the mountains all the way down to the sea. Each one was called an ahupua'a. It held everything a community needed. On the high slopes were forests, for wood and for birds' feathers. In the valleys were flooded terraces where taro grew, watered by cool streams. Taro is a plant with a starchy root, a little like a potato. And at the bottom were the shore and the reef, for fishing. The people of each slice shared what they had. They looked after the land and the water, so there would always be enough. In 1941, in some quiet valleys on the windward side, the old terraces were still green and full of water. Families still tended them in the old way. They stood knee-deep in the mud under the cliffs, while light rain drifted down the mountains.
===
IMG: sugar-cane-fields-wind-plantation-houses | tall green sugar cane fields swaying in the wind beside a small village of wooden plantation houses, smoke from a cooking fire, warm golden evening light
HEAD: In the plantation camps, the smell of cooking rice drifted across the yards each evening.
BODY: Much of the island was covered with plantations. You'd hear children laughing between the little wooden houses. On the flat land in the middle grew great fields of sugar cane. The cane was taller than a man, and it swayed in the wind like a green sea. On the red earth of the uplands grew pineapples, in long straight rows. They stretched all the way to the foot of the mountains. The workers went out to the fields early in the morning. They wore wide straw hats, long sleeves and gloves. These kept off the sun and the sharp leaves. At lunchtime, they opened their tin lunch boxes and shared what was inside. There was a little rice, a little fish, a pickle and a piece of Portuguese sausage. So everybody tasted everybody's food. In the evening, they walked home to small wooden houses in the plantation camps. Their families were waiting. Children played in the dusty lanes, and the smell of cooking rice drifted across the yards.

IMG: pearl-harbor-lochs-from-hills-morning | a wide calm natural harbour with several arms of water reaching inland among low green hills and sugar fields, a flat little island in the middle, soft morning light
HEAD: The Hawaiians called the harbour Wai Momi, which means pearl waters.
BODY: On the southern side of Oahu, a few miles west of Honolulu, there was a great natural harbour. You'd see the water shine like the inside of a shell. You'd see why, if you stood at the water's edge at sunrise. It was shaped a little like a hand with spread fingers. A wide calm inlet of the sea had several long arms of water reaching inland. Low green hills and sugar fields surrounded it. In the middle lay a flat little island. The old Hawaiians called this place Wai Momi. It means pearl waters. Long ago, oysters with pearls inside them were found in its shallow lagoons. Fish were raised there too, in stone-walled ponds along the shore. For many centuries, it was a quiet place of fishermen and farmers. Then, in the twentieth century, the American navy built a great base there. By 1941, the shallow pearl beds were long gone. But the old name lived on, in English. Everyone called it Pearl Harbor. It's strange to think of it. A name so gentle, for a place that would soon be so famous.

TOPIC: Honolulu, an easy-going town
BRIDGE: The ship is tied up, and the music on the pier fades away. Let's take a slow walk into town together. Can you smell the flowers on the warm air?

IMG: honolulu-street-1941-shops-palms | a downtown street in 1941 with low stone and wooden buildings, shady verandas and balconies, palm trees and a few old cars, people in light clothes, warm morning light
HEAD: The old palace of the Hawaiian kings stood in the middle of town, among giant banyan trees.
BODY: Honolulu in 1941 was a pleasant town of about two hundred thousand people. You'd stand in the shade of the banyan trees and look up at its tall windows. Its downtown streets were lined with low buildings of stone and wood. Many had deep shady verandas and balconies. There were banks and offices, department stores, cinemas and restaurants. And in the middle of it all stood the old palace of the Hawaiian kings and queens. It was called Iolani Palace. It was a handsome building with tall columns and wide verandas. Great banyan trees grew around it, with roots hanging down from their branches like curtains. The last Hawaiian queen had lived there, fifty years earlier. Now the government of the territory had its offices inside. Clerks walked up the same grand staircase that royalty had once used. Children climbed the banyan trees outside. The palace is still there today. It's the only royal palace in the whole of the United States. You'd feel how quiet it was, even in the middle of town. Visitors can walk through its quiet rooms and see the royal thrones.

IMG: honolulu-sidewalk-aloha-shirts-cotton-dresses | people strolling along a shady sidewalk in 1941, men in white suits and flowered shirts, women in bright cotton dresses with a flower behind one ear, palm trees, soft afternoon light
HEAD: In Honolulu, men wore shirts printed with flowers, and called them aloha shirts.
BODY: The streets of Honolulu were busy, but never in a hurry. You'd probably want one yourself. People strolled along the pavements in light, loose clothes. Some men wore white or pale suits. Others wore short-sleeved shirts printed with flowers, palm trees or surfboards. The islanders called them aloha shirts. They had only become popular a few years earlier. Now every tourist wanted one to take home. The women wore cotton dresses in bright colours. Some of them tucked a flower behind one ear. People said that a flower behind the left ear meant you were taken. Behind the right ear meant you were free. There were buses and trolley buses, and old cars with their windows rolled down. On the corners, men sold newspapers and shaved ice with sweet syrup. Nobody seemed to rush. Mainlanders often said the islands ran on their own slow clock. They called it island time. Would you have enjoyed that slower pace? I love that little detail. Most newcomers took about a week to fall in love with it.

IMG: fish-market-chinatown-morning-1941 | a busy covered fish market in the morning with fresh fish laid out on ice, wooden stalls of mangoes, papayas and pineapples, shoppers with baskets, soft morning light
HEAD: In Chinatown, the morning market sold fish caught only a few hours before.
BODY: Every morning, the fish market in Chinatown was crowded. You'd smell fresh fish, ginger and ripe mangoes all at once. People came early to buy the night's catch. There was tuna and mahi-mahi and red snapper, laid out on beds of ice. Fishermen called out prices in several languages. Nearby were little shops full of dried fish and noodles, ginger and rice. There was fruit of every kind. There were mangoes and papayas, bananas and lychees. There were pineapples sold whole, or sliced and served on a stick. Food in Honolulu was a mixture of everything. There was Hawaiian poi, made from pounded taro root. There was laulau, which is pork and fish wrapped in leaves and steamed. There was Japanese miso soup and noodles, and Chinese dumplings. There was Portuguese sweet bread, Korean pickles, Filipino stews, and American hamburgers and apple pie. In the evenings, many families sat down to a bowl of rice with a little of everything on top. You'd leave with a bag of fruit you couldn't even name. It was a whole world, in one bowl.

IMG: waikiki-beach-1941-surfers-diamond-head | a long curve of golden sand lined with palm trees, surfers on long wooden boards riding gentle waves, an outrigger canoe, a volcanic crater at the end of the beach, warm evening light
HEAD: The most famous surfer in the world was a gentle Hawaiian with Olympic gold medals.
BODY: A few miles east of downtown lay Waikiki Beach. If you'd met him on the beach, you'd have found him calm and kind. It was a long curve of golden sand, lined with palm trees. Diamond Head stood at the far end. The waves rolled in slowly from the reef, long and smooth and gentle. They were perfect for swimming and for surfing. On the beach, young Hawaiian beachboys taught the visitors to ride the waves. They used long, heavy wooden surfboards. They also took visitors out in outrigger canoes, beyond the reef. Then they rode a big wave all the way back to the sand, with everyone shouting and laughing. The most famous surfer of them all was Duke Kahanamoku. He was a tall, gentle Hawaiian who had won gold medals for swimming at the Olympic Games. He travelled the world and showed people in Australia and California how to surf. In a way, he gave surfing to the whole world. Yet at home, he was simply Duke, smiling on the beach.

IMG: pink-hotel-palms-beach-terrace-evening | a large pale pink hotel with towers and arches among palm trees and flower gardens on a beach, guests on a terrace, warm golden evening light
HEAD: The grandest hotel at Waikiki was painted pink and called the Pink Palace of the Pacific.
BODY: Along the beach stood a few grand hotels. You'd walk into its cool lobby and hear the waves through the open doors. The most famous was the Royal Hawaiian. Everyone called it the Pink Palace of the Pacific. It was a huge rambling building, painted pale pink. It had towers and arches in a Spanish style. It stood among gardens of palms and flowers, right on the sand. Rich and famous guests came from the mainland and stayed for weeks. There were film stars and businessmen and their families. They arrived with trunks full of clothes, sometimes even their own cars. In the evenings, they dined on the terrace under the stars. A band played soft music, and the waves murmured a few yards away. Yet the beach in front of it belonged to everyone. Children from the town swam there too, and beachboys played their ukuleles on the sand. The grand guests and the local families shared the same warm water. You'd sit on the terrace and watch the surfers until the sun went down. The sea, after all, doesn't care who is rich.

HEAD: Most families in Honolulu lived in wooden houses with tin roofs and deep porches.
BODY: Most people in Honolulu lived much more simply than the hotel guests. You'd sit on the porch in the evening, listening to the rain on the tin roof. They lived in small wooden houses with tin roofs and deep porches. Their neighbourhoods climbed up the green valleys behind the town. Their gardens were full of banana plants, papaya trees and flowers. In the evening, the trade winds often brought a little rain down the valleys. Families sat on their porches and listened to it drumming softly on the roof. The radio played inside. The children walked to school barefoot, or in rubber slippers. After school, they swam at the beach or fished off the piers. They climbed the mango trees in their neighbours' yards. Nobody minded much, as long as they shared the mangoes. At weekends, families went on picnics to beaches on the other side of the island. There, the sand was white and the water very clear. Can you hear it? You'd fall asleep to that soft drumming, I'm sure. Rain on a tin roof, and a radio humming softly in the next room.

IMG: steel-guitar-player-porch-evening | a Hawaiian man playing a guitar laid flat across his lap with a small metal bar on the porch of a wooden house, palm trees and a calm sea behind, warm golden evening light
HEAD: The sliding sound of the steel guitar was invented by a Hawaiian schoolboy.
BODY: Listen to old Hawaiian music, and you'll hear a soft, gliding sound. You'd hear it drifting from radios all over town. It seems to slide from one note to the next, like a voice sighing. That sound comes from the steel guitar. The guitar is laid flat across the player's lap. Instead of pressing the strings with his fingers, he slides a smooth piece of metal along them. The story goes that a Hawaiian schoolboy named Joseph Kekuku discovered it, in the 1880s. He was walking along a railway line, it's said, with his guitar. He picked up a bolt, ran it along the strings, and liked the sound. He practised for years, until he could play whole songs that way. Later, he travelled the world, playing for audiences in America and Europe. By 1941, the steel guitar could be heard on every radio in the islands. Its sound drifted from porches in the evening, slow and dreamy. Can you hear it now, soft and dreamy? To many people, it was the sound of Hawaii itself.

IMG: hawaiian-musicians-ukulele-evening-lanai | Hawaiian musicians with ukuleles and a steel guitar playing on a wooden veranda in the evening, families listening, paper lanterns glowing, palm trees, warm lamplight
HEAD: On some nights, a Honolulu radio station played music until dawn, so pilots could find the island.
BODY: Radio was very important in the islands. There were two radio stations in Honolulu. They broadcast music, news and stories all day long. Many evenings there was a programme of Hawaiian music. Slide guitars and ukuleles played, and soft voices sang old songs about the islands. They sang about the moon over the sea, and about love and parting. And on some nights, something special happened. When planes were flying out from California, one station stayed on the air all night. It played music hour after hour. The pilots tuned in to its signal and followed it across the dark ocean, like a lighthouse made of sound. In the evenings, people also went to the cinema, or to dances. Sometimes they went to a lu'au, a Hawaiian feast. A whole pig was cooked in an underground oven of hot stones. There was poi, fresh pineapple and coconut, and music and hula late into the night.

TOPIC: The fleet comes to Hawaii
BRIDGE: Out beyond the town, past the cane fields, lies a wide, calm harbour. If you look closely, you'll see grey shapes resting on the water.
===
IMG: battleship-row-ford-island-morning-calm | a row of big grey battleships moored one behind another beside a low flat island with palm trees, calm blue harbour water, green hills behind, soft Sunday morning light
HEAD: The great American fleet had only moved to Hawaii the year before.
BODY: In 1941, Pearl Harbor was the home of the United States Pacific Fleet. You might think the ships had always been there. They hadn't. For many years, the fleet had been based in California, at San Diego and Long Beach. Then, in the spring of 1940, the government in Washington made a decision. It moved the fleet out to Hawaii, far out in the Pacific. It wanted to show that America was watching what was happening in Asia. Not everyone liked the idea. Some admirals thought the fleet was too far from home there. Supplies and fuel all had to come across the ocean by ship. But the fleet stayed. The sailors soon got used to their new home. Many of them thought it was far nicer than California. The harbour filled with grey ships. The roads around it filled with trucks and workers. New barracks, houses and workshops went up almost every week. A quiet bay of fishponds and sugar fields had become one of the busiest naval bases in the world.
===
IMG: destroyers-moored-nests-harbour-hills | several slim grey destroyers moored side by side in a group in a calm harbour inlet, green hills and sugar fields behind, soft afternoon light
HEAD: The navy's destroyers were tied up side by side, in little groups called nests.
BODY: The harbour was full of ships. You'd see them lying together like sleeping whales. There were the great grey battleships, the biggest ships in the navy. Each was more than six hundred feet long, with tall masts like steel towers. There were cruisers and destroyers, long and slim and fast. There were submarines, supply ships, repair ships and tugboats. There was even a hospital ship, painted white. The destroyers were moored side by side, four or five together, in the arms of the harbour. The sailors called these groups nests. To get ashore from the outer ship, you had to walk across the decks of all the others. The submarines lay at their own base, on the eastern side. The aircraft carriers tied up on the western side of the island in the middle, when they were in port. Imagine looking down on all of it from the hills. You'd see a whole city of ships, grey and still on the blue water, with tiny figures moving on their decks.

IMG: ford-island-houses-lawns-palms-seaplanes | white officers' houses with green lawns and palm trees on a flat island, seaplanes on a ramp and hangars in the distance, calm harbour water, soft afternoon light
HEAD: In the middle of the harbour lay a flat island, with an air station, houses and swimming pools.
BODY: The island in the middle of the harbour was called Ford Island. You'd see children cycling along its quiet roads. It was low and flat, about a mile and a half long. It was covered with grass and palm trees. There was a big naval air station on it, with runways, hangars and a control tower. Rows of seaplanes stood on concrete ramps at the water's edge. There were houses for the officers and their families too. They had gardens and lawns, and there was a swimming pool at the officers' club. Children played in the quiet streets. Along the southeastern side of the island, there was a row of big concrete mooring posts in the water. Here, the great battleships tied up, one behind another. Sometimes two lay side by side. People called it Battleship Row. To the families on Ford Island, the battleships were simply part of the view. They were as familiar as the hills, or the palms outside the kitchen window.

IMG: battleship-arizona-moored-1941-calm | a large grey battleship with a tall tripod mast and big gun turrets moored in a calm harbour, sailors in white on deck, green hills behind, soft golden light
HEAD: The battleships were named after American states, and their crews loved them like home towns.
BODY: The battleships had the names of American states. If you came from Arizona or Nevada, you'd feel a little proud. At the southern end of the row lay the California. Then came the Maryland and the Oklahoma, side by side. Then the Tennessee and the West Virginia. Then the Arizona, with a small repair ship tied up beside her. At the northern end lay the Nevada. Across the channel, in the big dry dock, lay the Pennsylvania. She was the flagship of the fleet. The Arizona was one of the older battleships. She had been built in New York during the First World War and launched in 1915. She was six hundred and eight feet long. She carried twelve great guns in four turrets. About fifteen hundred men lived and worked aboard her. She had been modernised several times, with new engines and a tall tripod mast. Yet she was still the same proud old ship. Her crew talked about her the way people talk about their home town. Many said she was the finest ship in the fleet.

IMG: navy-yard-workers-cranes-dry-dock | a busy navy yard with tall cranes, warehouses and workshops beside a dry dock holding a large grey ship, workers in overalls walking, warm afternoon light
HEAD: The Navy Yard at Pearl Harbor was like a small town, with its own cinema and post office.
BODY: Across the water from Ford Island was the Navy Yard. You could buy a stamp, see a film and get a haircut without leaving the yard. It was like a small town of its own. There were workshops and warehouses, offices and power stations. There were dry docks, where ships were lifted out of the water to be repaired. There were giant cranes. There was a hospital, a fire station, a post office and a cinema. There was even a big new hall for sports and concerts. Thousands of people worked there. Some were sailors, and many were civilians from the islands. There were welders, carpenters, machinists and clerks. The noise of hammers and riveting guns went on all day long. The great cranes swung slowly back and forth against the sky. At lunchtime, workers sat in the shade and opened their lunch boxes. At five o'clock, a whistle blew. Then crowds of tired people walked to the buses and the ferries, heading home to supper. You'd soon know your way around like a local. The ships, for the night, were left to the sailors.

IMG: army-barracks-green-valley-mountains | long rows of pale army barracks with lawns and big shade trees in a wide green valley between mountains, soldiers walking in khaki, soft afternoon light
HEAD: In the middle of the island stood the biggest army post in all of America.
BODY: Pearl Harbor wasn't the only base on Oahu. Just to the southeast lay Hickam Field, a big army air base. It had long runways and huge hangars. In the middle of the island, on the high plain between the mountains, lay Wheeler Field, for the army's fighter planes. Beside it stood Schofield Barracks. It was the biggest army post in the whole of the United States. Thousands of soldiers lived there, in long rows of pale barracks with lawns and great shade trees. On the far side of the island, at Kaneohe Bay, there was a navy station for big flying boats. Altogether, more than forty thousand soldiers, sailors and airmen lived on Oahu in 1941. On weekends, the streets of Honolulu filled with young men in uniform. The soldiers wore khaki and the sailors wore white. Most of them wrote home that Hawaii was the loveliest posting in the world. Many told their mothers not to worry, because it was like living in paradise.

HEAD: The children of Ford Island could tell every battleship apart by the shape of its masts.
BODY: For the children who lived on Ford Island, it was a wonderful place to grow up. You'd hear them calling out the names as the ships came in. They rode their bicycles along the quiet roads under the palm trees. They swam in the pool at the officers' club. They fished off the sea walls with bent pins and bits of bread. They knew the names of all the ships in the harbour. They could tell them apart just by the shapes of their masts. Each morning, they heard the bands on the battleships playing as the flags were raised. Each evening, they heard the bugles sounding across the water. When they wanted to go into Honolulu, they took a little ferry across the channel. Then they caught a bus into town. Many of them remembered those years as the happiest of their childhood. Years later, as grown-ups, some came back to visit. They stood on the same sea walls and pointed out where each ship had been. Could you have told them apart? They still remembered every name.

HEAD: The admiral and the general played golf together on Sunday mornings, when they could.
BODY: The commander of the Pacific Fleet was Admiral Husband Kimmel. You can picture them strolling across the green in the early morning light. He was fifty-nine years old, tall and handsome, with white hair. He came from Kentucky and had spent his whole life in the navy. He was serious and careful, and he worked very long days. He expected a great deal of his officers and men. He lived in a house on a hill above the harbour. From his window he could look down on his ships. The commander of the army in Hawaii was General Walter Short. His job was to defend the islands and the base. The two men were friendly, and they played golf together on Sunday mornings, when they could. Kimmel didn't much like parties. He liked to be in bed by ten o'clock. He had held his great command for less than a year. Like most people in Hawaii, he believed any trouble with Japan would come far away, in the western Pacific.

HEAD: At sunset, the harbour turned from gold to silver to deep blue.
BODY: In the evenings, the harbour was peaceful. You'd sit on the shore and watch the colours change, slowly. The sun went down behind the Waianae Mountains, to the west. The sky blazed orange and pink. The calm water turned to gold, then to silver, then to deep blue. Lights came on, on the ships and along the shore. Their reflections lay in long wavering lines across the water. On the decks of the battleships, sailors sat and smoked and talked in the cool air. The sound of a radio drifted across from one of the ships, playing dance music. A launch puttered across the harbour, taking officers ashore. From the shore came the chirping of frogs and crickets, and the scent of flowers. Overhead, the first stars appeared, one by one. Far away, a dog barked and was quiet again. Would you have liked to sit there on the warm deck, watching the lights come on? Many sailors said it was the most peaceful moment of their day.
