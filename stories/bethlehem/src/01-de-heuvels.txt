Tonight we are going about two thousand years back, to a small village on a hilltop in the land of Judea, just a few miles south of the city of Jerusalem.

--

It is evening, in the winter, and the light is fading over the hills.

The hills here are steep and rocky, and their slopes are built up into long, narrow terraces, held up by low walls of pale limestone, one above another, like giant steps. On the terraces, there are olive trees, old and twisted and silver-green, and vines, and little fields of wheat and barley. Between the terraces, the rock shows through, white and grey, with wild thyme and sage growing in the cracks.

If gentle stories from the past help you rest, you are welcome to subscribe to the channel. Now, let us begin.

--

On the top of the ridge, there is a village.

It is called Bethlehem. In the language of the people who live here, the name means house of bread, perhaps because of the fields of wheat and barley that grow on the slopes around it.

It is a small place, perhaps a few hundred people, perhaps a thousand: a cluster of square, flat-roofed houses of pale stone, crowded together along the ridge, with narrow lanes winding between them, and courtyards and gardens behind walls, and a few trees, olives and figs and almonds, growing among the houses.

--

From the edge of the village, there is a wide view in every direction.

To the north, along the ridge, the road runs to Jerusalem, about six miles away. On a clear day, from the higher places, you can just see the great city on its hills, and the walls of the Temple, gleaming white and gold in the sun.

{img:view-from-ridge-desert-east-dead-sea-haze}
To the east, the land falls away, down and down, into a wild, empty desert of bare brown hills and deep, winding valleys, all the way to the Dead Sea, a long, strange, shining lake, far below, in the bottom of the great rift, with the mountains of Moab rising beyond it, blue and purple in the evening light.

--

To the south and the west, there are more hills, and more terraces, and little villages on the hilltops, and the road winding away south, toward the old town of Hebron.

And on one hill, a few miles to the southeast, there is a strange shape: a round, flat-topped mountain, like a cone with its top cut off, with a great fortress and palace on its summit. It was built, only a few years before, by the king of the land, Herod. People call it the Herodium.

{img:herodium-cone-hill-fortress-distant-sunset}
From Bethlehem, it is always there on the horizon, a reminder of the power of the king.

--

The land around Bethlehem is not rich. The soil is thin and stony, and the summers are long and hot and dry. But in the winter, from about November to March, the rains come.

They come in from the sea, far to the west, in grey clouds rolling over the hills, and the rain falls hard and cold on the terraces and the rooftops, and runs down the slopes, and fills the cisterns that the people have cut into the rock to catch the water. The hills turn green, and the wild flowers bloom: anemones, red as blood, and cyclamen, and crocuses, and little white stars of Bethlehem.

{img:winter-rain-clouds-over-terraced-hills-green}
On winter nights, it can be cold up here, in the hills, much colder than people from northern lands expect. Sometimes there is frost, and very occasionally, a little snow.

--

Out in the fields around the village, there are flocks of sheep and goats.

They graze on the hillsides, among the rocks and the scrub, watched over by shepherds, who walk with them all day, and lead them to water, and to fresh grazing, and protect them from wild animals. In the evening, the shepherds bring their flocks into sheepfolds, rough enclosures of stone walls, or into the caves that are found everywhere in the soft limestone of the hills.

On this winter evening, as the sun goes down behind the hills to the west, the shepherds are bringing their flocks in for the night, and lighting small fires, and settling down to keep watch.

--

In the village, the lamps are being lit.

They are little clay oil lamps, filled with olive oil, with a wick of flax, which give a small, warm, flickering light. One by one, they glow in the doorways and the small windows of the houses. The smell of wood smoke and baking bread drifts across the rooftops.

{img:clay-oil-lamps-glowing-doorways-village-night}
The stars come out, very bright and very many, over the dark hills. A dog barks. A donkey brays. A mother calls her children in. And the village of Bethlehem settles down for the night, as it has done for a thousand years, and more.
