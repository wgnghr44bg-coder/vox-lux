About two thousand years ago, Bethlehem was a small village of stone houses on a hill in Judea. Bethlehem lies in the hills of Judea, about nine kilometres south of Jerusalem. Around the time of the first Christmas, it was a small farming village. Historians estimate that a few hundred people lived there, perhaps up to a thousand. Their houses were built of local limestone and stood close together along the ridge of a hill, surrounded by terraced fields, olive groves and vineyards. The name Bethlehem means house of bread in Hebrew, a sign of the grain that grew in the fields around it. Even so, the village was not important in politics or trade. It was overshadowed by Jerusalem, the great city of the Temple, just a few hours' walk to the north. Yet Bethlehem had a famous past. It was the home town of King David, the great king of Israel. And according to the Gospels of Matthew and Luke, it was here that Jesus of Nazareth was born.

[long-pause]

{img:02-villagers-morning-street-water-jars}

Each day in Bethlehem began at sunrise, with women fetching water and men heading out to the fields. Life in a village like Bethlehem followed the rhythm of the sun and the seasons. Historians note that people rose at dawn. Women and girls went to fetch water from wells and cisterns, carrying it home in clay jars balanced on their heads. Men and boys went out to work in the fields, the vineyards and the olive groves, or led the sheep and goats out to graze. Even so, the women's work did not stop with water. They ground grain into flour, baked bread, cooked meals, spun wool, wove cloth and cared for the children. Most families grew most of their own food. The village was a close community. Everyone knew everyone, and families had lived there for generations. People spoke Aramaic in daily life, a language related to Hebrew. They greeted each other with the word shalom, meaning peace. At midday, they rested in the shade during the hottest hours, then returned to work until evening.

{img:03-gospel-scroll-oil-lamp-table}

Most of what is told about the first Christmas comes from the Gospels of Matthew and Luke. The story of the birth of Jesus is told in two books of the New Testament, the Gospels of Matthew and Luke, written several decades after his life. Historians note that the two accounts are different. Luke tells of the journey of Mary and Joseph from Nazareth, the birth in Bethlehem, the manger and the shepherds. Matthew tells of the star, the wise men from the east and King Herod. Later Christian tradition combined the two into a single story, the one told in nativity plays and carols today. Even so, historians study these texts carefully, asking which details are historical and which reflect the faith of the early Christians. There are few other sources from the time. Yet much is known about daily life in Judea two thousand years ago, from archaeology, from other ancient writers and from the Jewish scriptures. Together, these help to picture the village of Bethlehem as it was at the time of the first Christmas.

[long-pause]

{img:04-judea-landscape-hills-olive-groves-wide}

Bethlehem stood on the edge of the Judean hills, where green fields gave way to dry desert. Bethlehem lies at the edge of two very different landscapes. Historians note that to the west and north are the Judean hills, with fertile valleys, terraced fields, olive groves and vineyards. To the east, the land drops steeply towards the Judean Desert and the Dead Sea, a dry, rocky wilderness where little grows except after the winter rains. The village stood about seven hundred and seventy metres above sea level. Even so, the land around it was good for farming. The winter rains watered the fields of wheat and barley, and the hillsides were covered with olive trees, vines and fig trees. Shepherds grazed their flocks on the slopes and, in some seasons, in the edges of the desert. The climate was mild, with warm, dry summers and cool, rainy winters. Historians note that in winter, nights in Bethlehem could be cold, and snow fell occasionally on the hills.

[long-pause]

{img:05-family-evening-meal-mat-oil-lamp}

In the evening, families gathered to eat bread, olives and lentils by the light of small oil lamps. The main meal of the day in Bethlehem was eaten in the evening, after the work in the fields was done. Historians note that families sat on mats on the floor and ate from shared bowls. Bread was the most important food, eaten at every meal. It was dipped in olive oil, eaten with vegetables, lentils, beans, cheese or fruit. Meat was eaten only on special occasions, such as festivals or when guests came. Even so, the food was healthy and filling. People drank water, milk and wine, usually mixed with water. Figs, dates, grapes and pomegranates were enjoyed in season. Meals were lit by small clay oil lamps, which burned olive oil and gave a soft, flickering light. Before eating, a blessing was said over the bread. Historians note that sharing a meal was an important sign of friendship and hospitality. Guests were always welcomed and given the best food the family could offer.

[long-pause]

{img:06-village-gathering-elders-gate-shade}

The elders of the village met at the gate to settle disputes and discuss village matters. In ancient villages like Bethlehem, the elders played an important role. Historians note that they were respected older men, heads of families, who met to discuss village affairs, settle disputes and witness agreements. They often gathered at the gate of the village or in an open space, where everyone could see and hear. In the Jewish scriptures, the Book of Ruth describes how a man named Boaz met with the elders at the gate of Bethlehem to arrange his marriage to Ruth. Even so, the village was also part of a larger world. It was under the rule of King Herod, who ruled Judea with the support of Rome. Tax collectors came to collect taxes for the king. Soldiers sometimes passed through. Historians note that most villagers had little contact with the powerful, but the decisions of kings and emperors could change their lives suddenly. The village elders tried to protect their community and keep its traditions alive.

[long-pause]

{img:07-star-over-quiet-village-night-sky}

The story of Christmas has made this small village one of the most famous places in the world. For most of its history, Bethlehem was an ordinary village, much like many others in the hills of Judea. Historians note that it had been home to farmers and shepherds for more than a thousand years before the time of Jesus. Yet the story of the first Christmas made it famous around the world. Today, its name is known in almost every country. It appears in carols, Christmas cards and nativity plays. Even so, the Bethlehem of two thousand years ago was very different from the images often seen today. It was a small, quiet village, with simple stone houses, dusty streets and fields of grain. Its people worked hard to feed their families. They kept their traditions, prayed, celebrated festivals and cared for each other. Historians note that understanding their daily lives helps to bring the story closer. It shows the ordinary world in which, according to the Gospels, an extraordinary event took place.

[long-pause]

{img:08-terraced-hillside-stone-walls-farmers}

Farmers built stone terraces on the steep hillsides around Bethlehem to create flat land for crops. The hills around Bethlehem were steep and rocky. Historians note that to grow crops, farmers built terraces, flat strips of land held in place by walls of stones. They gathered the stones from the fields, piling them carefully into walls. Behind the walls, they filled in soil to create level fields. The terraces stopped the soil from being washed away by the winter rains and held water for the crops. Even so, building and repairing terraces was hard work. Each generation had to maintain the walls, rebuilding them when they collapsed. Some terraces in the hills of Judea are very old, and some are still used today. Historians note that the terraced hillsides gave the landscape its distinctive look, with lines of stone walls following the contours of the hills. Olive trees, vines and fig trees grew on the terraces, along with wheat, barley and vegetables. The terraces were a sign of the hard work of many generations.

[long-pause]

{img:09-olive-trees-old-gnarled-grove-sun}

Olive trees grew everywhere around Bethlehem, providing oil for food, light and medicine. The olive tree was one of the most important plants in ancient Judea. Historians note that olive trees grew everywhere on the hillsides around Bethlehem. They could live for hundreds of years, and some very old trees still survive in the region today. Even so, the olive tree was not just a tree. Its fruit provided olive oil, which was used for cooking, lighting lamps, making medicine and anointing the body. Oil was also used in religious ceremonies. Kings and priests were anointed with oil as a sign of their special role. In the autumn, families harvested the olives by beating the branches with sticks, collecting the fruit on cloths spread beneath the trees. The olives were then crushed in stone presses to extract the oil. Historians note that olive oil was one of the main products of the region, traded across the Mediterranean. Olive trees were a symbol of peace, wealth and blessing in the Jewish scriptures.

[long-pause]

{img:10-vineyard-grapes-harvest-baskets-judea}

In late summer, families harvested grapes from their vineyards and made wine. Vineyards were common on the hillsides around Bethlehem. Historians note that grapes were harvested in late summer, in August and September. The harvest was a time of hard work and celebration. Families picked the grapes and carried them in baskets to a winepress, a flat area carved into the rock. There, people trod the grapes with their feet, and the juice flowed into a lower basin. The juice was collected in jars and left to ferment into wine. Even so, not all the grapes were made into wine. Some were eaten fresh, and others were dried into raisins, which could be stored for the winter. Small stone watchtowers stood in some vineyards, where a family member could keep watch over the ripening grapes, protecting them from thieves and animals. Historians note that wine was drunk at meals and festivals, usually mixed with water. The vine was a symbol of the people of Israel in the Jewish scriptures.

[long-pause]

{img:11-winter-rain-clouds-hills-judea}

The winter rains turned the hills green and filled the cisterns that the villages depended on. In Judea, the year was divided into two main seasons, a dry summer and a rainy winter. Historians note that the rains usually began in October or November and lasted until March or April. The first rains softened the ground for ploughing and sowing. The heavy winter rains filled the cisterns and watered the crops. The late rains in spring helped the grain to ripen. Even so, the rains were not always reliable. In some years, they came late or not at all, and drought brought hunger. Farmers watched the sky anxiously and prayed for rain. Historians note that water was precious in Bethlehem, which had no river or large spring. People collected rainwater in cisterns, underground tanks cut into the rock, and drew from them throughout the dry summer. In winter, the hills around the village turned green, and wild flowers bloomed. The nights were cold, and families gathered around small fires for warmth.

[long-pause]

{img:12-well-cistern-women-drawing-water}

Water came from wells and cisterns, and the women of the village gathered there each morning. In Bethlehem, water came from wells and cisterns. Historians note that the Jewish scriptures mention a well by the gate of Bethlehem, from which King David once longed to drink when he was fighting the Philistines. Three of his warriors broke through the enemy lines to bring him water from it. Even so, most families depended on cisterns, which collected rainwater from roofs and courtyards. Each morning, women and girls went to fetch water for the day. They carried it home in clay jars, balanced on their heads or shoulders. The well was also a place to meet and talk. Women shared news, made friends and arranged marriages. Historians note that in many stories of the Jewish scriptures, important meetings happened at wells. Water was essential for drinking, cooking, washing and watering animals. Families used it carefully, especially in the long, dry summer, when the cisterns slowly emptied.

[long-pause]

{img:13-herodium-fortress-mountain-distance}

From Bethlehem, people could see King Herod's great fortress palace, the Herodium, on a nearby hill. A few kilometres southeast of Bethlehem rises a strange, cone-shaped hill. Historians note that it was largely built by King Herod the Great, who piled up earth and stone to raise its height. On its top, he built a round fortress palace, called the Herodium. It had towers, courtyards, bathhouses and gardens. At its foot, he built a lower palace with a large pool. Even so, the Herodium was not only a palace. It was also a monument to Herod himself. He had won a battle there as a young man, and he chose it as his burial place. When he died in 4 BC, he was buried there. Historians note that his tomb was discovered by the archaeologist Ehud Netzer in 2007. For the people of Bethlehem, the Herodium was a constant reminder of the king's power, visible from their fields and rooftops. It still stands today, its top visible for many kilometres.

[long-pause]

{img:14-stone-house-flat-roof-courtyard-judea}

Most houses in Bethlehem were simple buildings of stone, with flat roofs of wood and earth. The houses of Bethlehem two thousand years ago were built of the local limestone. Historians note that most were simple, with one or two rooms, built around or beside a small courtyard. The walls were made of rough stones, held together with mud mortar. The roof was flat, made of wooden beams covered with branches, reeds and packed clay. Even so, the roof was an important part of the house. It was used for drying fruit and grain, sleeping on hot summer nights and praying. Stairs or a ladder led up to it from the courtyard. After the winter rains, the clay had to be rolled flat again with a stone roller to keep it watertight. Historians note that the courtyard was where much of daily life took place. Families cooked, ate, worked and talked there. Houses were often shared by several generations of a family, with new rooms added as children married and had families of their own.

[long-pause]

{img:15-house-interior-raised-floor-animals-below}

In many village houses, the family lived on a raised floor, and the animals stayed on a lower level at night. In many villages of ancient Judea, houses had a special layout. Historians and archaeologists note that the main room was often divided into two levels. The family lived, ate and slept on a raised platform at the back. Near the entrance was a lower level, where the family's animals, such as a donkey, a few sheep or goats, or an ox, were brought in at night. The animals were protected from thieves and cold, and their warmth helped heat the house. Even so, the animals did not wander freely. Feeding troughs, called mangers, were built at the edge of the raised floor or cut into the stone, so that the animals could eat. Historians note that this kind of house is important for understanding the story of the first Christmas. When Luke describes the baby Jesus being laid in a manger, he may have been describing the lower part of such a house, where the animals were kept.

[long-pause]

{img:16-cave-dwelling-hillside-stone-entrance}

Some houses in Bethlehem were built in front of caves in the limestone hillside. The hills of Bethlehem are made of soft limestone, full of natural caves. Historians note that people in the area often used these caves as part of their homes. A house might be built in front of a cave, with the cave serving as a back room, a storeroom or a shelter for animals. Caves stayed cool in summer and relatively warm in winter, making them very useful. Even so, caves were not only for houses. They were also used for storing grain, oil and wine, for tombs and for sheltering flocks. Historians note that a very old Christian tradition says that Jesus was born in a cave. This tradition was recorded by Justin Martyr, a Christian writer born in the region, around the year 150. In the fourth century, a church was built over a cave in Bethlehem that was believed to be the place of the birth. That church, the Church of the Nativity, still stands today.

[long-pause]

{img:17-clay-oven-tabun-baking-flatbread}

Women baked flat bread every day in small clay ovens in the courtyards of their houses. Bread was baked every day in Bethlehem. Historians note that women ground wheat or barley into flour using a hand mill made of two round stones, one turned on top of the other. This was hard work and took a long time each morning. The flour was mixed with water, salt and a little leaven, a piece of dough saved from the day before. Even so, the bread was not baked in a large oven like today's. Women used small dome-shaped clay ovens, called tabuns, in the courtyard. A fire was lit inside, and when the walls were hot, flat rounds of dough were pressed against them to bake. The bread was ready in a few minutes. Historians note that the name Bethlehem, house of bread, reflects the importance of grain and bread in the life of the village. Bread was eaten at every meal and shared with guests. Breaking bread together was a sign of friendship and trust.

[long-pause]

{img:18-spinning-wool-woman-spindle-doorway}

Women spun wool and wove cloth at home, making clothes for the whole family. In ancient Bethlehem, most clothing was made at home. Historians note that women spun wool from the family's sheep into thread, using a hand spindle, a stick with a weight at the bottom that was twirled to twist the fibres. They also spun flax into linen thread. The thread was woven into cloth on a simple loom, often set up in the courtyard or on the roof. Even so, making cloth took a great deal of time. A single garment might take weeks to make. Women often spun while doing other things, such as watching children, talking with neighbours or walking. The cloth was used to make tunics, cloaks, head coverings and blankets. Historians note that most people owned only a few garments. A man typically wore a tunic, a belt and a cloak, which also served as a blanket at night. Women wore longer tunics and head coverings. Cloth was valuable and was carefully mended and reused.

[long-pause]

{img:19-rooftop-evening-family-resting-stars}

On warm evenings, families rested on their flat roofs, watching the stars appear over the hills. The flat roofs of Bethlehem's houses were more than just a covering. Historians note that they were an important living space, especially in the warm months. Families climbed up to the roof in the evening to enjoy the cool breeze after the heat of the day. They talked, rested and watched the sun set over the hills. Children played, and adults told stories. Even so, the roof was also used for work. Fruits such as figs and grapes were dried there, and flax was spread out to dry. On hot summer nights, some family members slept on the roof. Historians note that the Jewish law required that roofs should have a low wall, or parapet, around the edge, to prevent people from falling. From the rooftops, people could see across the village to the fields and hills beyond. At night, the sky above Bethlehem was full of stars, far brighter than in modern cities, with no lights to dim them.

[long-pause]

{img:20-ploughing-oxen-wooden-plough-autumn}

After the first autumn rains, farmers ploughed their fields with oxen and sowed wheat and barley. The farming year in Bethlehem began in the autumn. Historians note that after the first rains softened the hard, dry ground in October or November, farmers ploughed their fields. They used simple wooden ploughs, sometimes with an iron tip, pulled by oxen or donkeys. The plough cut a shallow furrow in the soil. Even so, the fields were small and rocky, and ploughing was hard work. Farmers walked behind the plough, guiding it and pushing it into the ground. After ploughing, they sowed seeds of wheat and barley by hand, scattering them across the field from a bag or basket. Then they ploughed again to cover the seeds. Historians note that barley was more common than wheat in poorer areas, because it grew better in dry, stony soil. Barley bread was the food of ordinary people, while wheat bread was more expensive. The fields around Bethlehem were famous for their grain.

[long-pause]

{img:21-barley-harvest-sickles-reapers-spring}

In spring, the barley was harvested first, followed a few weeks later by the wheat. The grain harvest in Judea took place in the spring. Historians note that barley ripened first, in April, around the time of the festival of Passover. Wheat followed a few weeks later, in May and June, around the festival of Shavuot, also called the Feast of Weeks. The whole family worked in the fields during the harvest. Men cut the grain with sickles, curved blades of iron set in wooden handles. Women and children gathered the cut stalks and tied them into sheaves. Even so, not all the grain was taken. Jewish law said that farmers should leave some grain at the edges of their fields and should not go back for stalks they had dropped. This was for the poor, widows and strangers, who could gather it for themselves. Historians note that this practice, called gleaning, is described in the Book of Ruth, which tells of a widow who gleaned in the fields of Bethlehem.

[long-pause]

{img:22-threshing-floor-oxen-winnowing-wind}

The grain was threshed on flat stone floors, and the wind blew away the chaff. After the harvest, the grain had to be separated from the stalks and husks. Historians note that this was done on a threshing floor, a flat, open area of hard ground or rock, usually on a hilltop where the wind blew. The sheaves were spread out on the floor, and oxen or donkeys walked over them in circles, sometimes pulling a heavy wooden sledge with stones underneath. This broke the grain from the stalks. Even so, the grain was still mixed with chaff, the light husks. To separate them, farmers tossed the mixture into the air with wooden forks or shovels. The heavier grain fell back to the ground, while the wind carried the chaff away. This was called winnowing. Historians note that the threshing floor was a busy and joyful place at harvest time. In the Book of Ruth, Boaz sleeps on his threshing floor at night to guard his grain. The clean grain was stored in jars and pits.

[long-pause]

{img:23-fig-tree-summer-fruit-picking-ladder}

In summer, families picked figs, grapes and pomegranates, and dried some fruit for the winter. Summer in Judea was hot and dry, but it was also a time of plenty. Historians note that it was the season of fruit. Figs ripened in early and late summer, and grapes in August and September. Pomegranates, dates and other fruits were also harvested. Families picked the fruit and ate it fresh, enjoying its sweetness after the long months of bread and lentils. Even so, much of the fruit was preserved for the winter. Figs were dried in the sun and pressed into cakes. Grapes were made into raisins or wine. Pomegranates could be stored for months. Historians note that the fig tree was especially loved. Its broad leaves gave shade, and its fruit was sweet and nourishing. In the Jewish scriptures, sitting under one's own vine and fig tree was a picture of peace and contentment. Many houses in Bethlehem had a fig tree in the courtyard or nearby.

[long-pause]

{img:24-sabbath-evening-lamp-family-blessing}

Every week, the Sabbath brought a day of rest from all work, from Friday evening to Saturday evening. The people of Bethlehem were Jewish, and their lives were shaped by the Jewish law and calendar. Historians note that the most important weekly event was the Sabbath, the day of rest. It began at sunset on Friday and ended at sunset on Saturday. During the Sabbath, no work was done. Families did not cook, plough, harvest or travel far. Even so, preparations began before. On Friday, women cooked extra food and prepared the house. At sunset, a lamp was lit, and the family shared a special meal with blessings over bread and wine. On Saturday, people rested, prayed and studied the scriptures. Historians note that in larger villages and towns, people gathered in synagogues, meeting houses where the scriptures were read and explained. The Sabbath was a time of peace and family. For people who worked hard every other day of the week, it was a precious gift.

[long-pause]

{img:25-pilgrims-road-to-jerusalem-festival}

Three times a year, families travelled to Jerusalem for the great pilgrimage festivals. The Jewish calendar had three great pilgrimage festivals: Passover in spring, Shavuot in early summer and Sukkot in autumn. Historians note that at these times, many Jews travelled to Jerusalem to worship at the Temple. For the people of Bethlehem, Jerusalem was only a few hours' walk away. Families travelled together, singing psalms on the road. Even so, the festivals were not only religious. They were also joyful celebrations of the harvest and of the history of the Jewish people. Passover remembered the escape of the Israelites from slavery in Egypt. Shavuot celebrated the wheat harvest. Sukkot celebrated the grape harvest, and families lived in shelters made of branches for a week. Historians note that during the festivals, Jerusalem's population swelled enormously, with pilgrims coming from all over Judea and from Jewish communities across the Roman world. The roads near Bethlehem were filled with travellers heading to the holy city.

[long-pause]

{img:26-evening-return-fields-tools-donkey}

At sunset, the farmers came home from the fields, tired after a long day's work. As the sun went down over the hills, the people of Bethlehem returned from their work. Historians imagine the scene: farmers walking home along the paths between the terraces, carrying their tools on their shoulders, donkeys loaded with firewood, grain or fruit. Shepherds brought their flocks back to the village or settled them in folds for the night. Children ran ahead to greet them. Even so, the work of the day was not quite over. Animals had to be fed and watered, tools put away and the evening meal prepared. Smoke rose from the cooking fires, and the smell of bread and stew filled the air. Historians note that families then gathered for their meal, sharing the news of the day. After dark, the village grew quiet. Oil lamps glowed in a few doorways. The stars came out over the hills. The people of Bethlehem went to sleep early, ready to rise again with the sun.

[long-pause]

{img:27-shepherd-boy-flock-sheep-hillside}

Many families in Bethlehem kept sheep and goats, and the shepherds led them out to graze each day. Sheep and goats were an important part of life in Bethlehem. Historians note that they provided milk, cheese, wool, leather and meat. Most families kept a few animals, and some owned larger flocks. Each morning, shepherds led the flocks out of the village to graze on the hillsides and in the valleys. In the dry season, they sometimes had to travel further, into the edges of the desert, to find enough grass. Even so, shepherding was often done by young people. Boys and sometimes girls cared for the family's flock, while their parents worked in the fields. In the Jewish scriptures, the young David was a shepherd near Bethlehem before he became king. Historians note that shepherds carried a staff, a long stick with a curved end, used to guide the sheep, and a rod or club to protect them from wild animals. They also carried a sling for throwing stones.

[long-pause]

{img:28-sheepfold-stone-walls-night-fire}

At night, the sheep were kept in folds with stone walls, and shepherds slept at the entrance to guard them. When shepherds stayed out with their flocks overnight, they kept the sheep in sheepfolds. Historians note that these were enclosures with low walls of stones, often topped with thorny branches to keep out wild animals. Some folds were built against cliffs or caves. There was usually a single entrance, and the shepherd sometimes slept across it, acting as a living door. Even so, guarding the flock at night was not easy. Wolves, jackals, hyenas and even leopards lived in the hills and could attack the sheep. Thieves could also try to steal them. Shepherds took turns to stay awake, keeping a fire burning. Historians note that the Gospel of Luke describes shepherds living out in the fields near Bethlehem, keeping watch over their flocks by night. Scholars have debated whether flocks would have been kept outdoors in winter. Some think this was more likely in spring, while others believe flocks were kept in the fields throughout the year.

[long-pause]

{img:29-shepherd-carrying-lamb-shoulders}

Shepherds knew each of their sheep and carried lost or injured lambs home on their shoulders. Shepherds in ancient Judea developed a close bond with their sheep. Historians note that they knew each animal and often gave them names. The sheep, in turn, recognised their shepherd's voice and followed him. When a lamb was lost, the shepherd searched for it. When it was injured or too weak to walk, he carried it on his shoulders. Even so, shepherding was not a respected profession in all circles. Some educated people of the time looked down on shepherds as poor, rough and unreliable, because they spent so much time away from towns. Yet in the Jewish scriptures, the shepherd was a powerful image. God was described as a shepherd caring for his people, as in the famous psalm that begins, The Lord is my shepherd. Historians note that in the Gospel of Luke, the news of the birth of Jesus is announced first to shepherds. This showed that the message was for ordinary, humble people.

[long-pause]

{img:30-milking-goats-courtyard-clay-jar}

Goats and sheep gave milk, which was made into butter, cheese and yogurt. Milk was an important food in Bethlehem. Historians note that it came mainly from goats and sheep, rather than cows. Goats were especially useful because they could eat rough plants and survive in dry conditions. Milk was drunk fresh or made into other foods. It was poured into a skin bag and shaken to make butter. It was left to sour into a thick yogurt, called leben. It was also made into cheese, which could be dried and stored for months. Even so, milk did not keep long in the warm climate, so it had to be used quickly. Women and children did most of the milking, early in the morning and in the evening. Historians note that the Jewish scriptures describe the promised land as a land flowing with milk and honey, a picture of abundance. For the people of Bethlehem, milk from their goats and sheep was an important part of a healthy diet.

[long-pause]

{img:31-shearing-sheep-wool-spring-gathering}

Sheep shearing in spring was a time of hard work and celebration. Once a year, in spring, the sheep were sheared. Historians note that shepherds cut the wool from the sheep using iron shears. The wool was then washed, combed and spun into thread. Wool was a valuable product. It was used to make clothes, blankets and tents, and it could be sold or traded. Even so, shearing was also a time of celebration. In the Jewish scriptures, sheep shearing is described as a festive occasion, with feasts and gatherings. Families and neighbours came together to help with the work and to share food and drink. Historians note that wool from the area around Bethlehem may have been sent to Jerusalem for sale. Some scholars believe that sheep raised near Bethlehem were used for sacrifices at the Temple in Jerusalem. This idea comes from later Jewish writings, and historians are not certain about it. Yet the connection between Bethlehem's flocks and the nearby holy city was close.

[long-pause]

{img:32-shepherds-field-night-stars-quiet}

East of Bethlehem lie fields where, according to tradition, the shepherds of the Christmas story watched their flocks. East of Bethlehem, the land slopes down towards the village of Beit Sahour and the Judean Desert beyond. Historians note that this area has long been associated with the shepherds of the Christmas story. Since at least the fourth century, Christians have visited fields there, known as the Shepherds' Fields, to remember the angels' announcement to the shepherds. Even so, the exact place cannot be known. The Gospel of Luke says only that the shepherds were in the region near Bethlehem. Several sites are shown to visitors today, each with its own church or chapel. Archaeologists have found remains of ancient buildings, caves and monasteries in the area. Historians note that the landscape has changed greatly over two thousand years, but the hills and the night sky are much the same. On clear nights, the stars still shine brightly over the fields, as they would have done for the shepherds who watched their flocks there long ago.

[long-pause]

{img:33-ruth-gleaning-barley-field-reapers}

According to the Book of Ruth, a young widow from Moab once gathered grain in the fields of Bethlehem. One of the most beloved stories in the Jewish scriptures is set in Bethlehem. Historians note that the Book of Ruth tells of a woman named Naomi from Bethlehem, who moved to the land of Moab with her husband and sons during a famine. Her husband and sons died there. Naomi decided to return home, and her daughter-in-law Ruth, a woman from Moab, insisted on going with her. Ruth promised Naomi that she would go wherever Naomi went, and that Naomi's people would be her people. Even so, the two women arrived in Bethlehem poor. It was the time of the barley harvest, and Ruth went to glean in the fields, gathering the grain left by the reapers. She worked in the field of a kind landowner named Boaz, a relative of Naomi's husband. Historians note that the story celebrates loyalty, kindness and the welcoming of strangers. The book is still read in synagogues at the festival of Shavuot.

[long-pause]

{img:34-boaz-ruth-village-gate-elders}

Ruth married Boaz, and their great-grandson was David, who became king of Israel. In the Book of Ruth, Boaz is impressed by Ruth's loyalty and hard work. Historians note that he protects her, gives her extra grain and eventually marries her. The marriage is arranged at the gate of Bethlehem, in front of the village elders, following the customs of the time. Ruth and Boaz have a son named Obed. Obed becomes the father of Jesse, and Jesse becomes the father of David. Even so, the story is more than a family history. It shows that a foreign woman, from a people often seen as enemies, became an ancestor of Israel's greatest king. Historians note that the Book of Ruth was probably written down several centuries after the events it describes, and scholars debate its date and historical basis. Yet it has always been treasured. In the Gospel of Matthew, Ruth is one of only a few women named in the list of the ancestors of Jesus.

[long-pause]

{img:35-young-david-shepherd-harp-hillside}

David, the youngest son of Jesse, grew up as a shepherd boy in the hills around Bethlehem. According to the Jewish scriptures, David was the youngest of the eight sons of Jesse, a farmer of Bethlehem. Historians note that he cared for his father's sheep on the hills around the village. He was known for his skill with the sling, with which he protected the flock from lions and bears. He also played the lyre, a small stringed instrument, and composed songs. Even so, his life changed suddenly. The prophet Samuel came to Bethlehem, looking for the man God had chosen to be the next king. Jesse presented his older sons, one by one, but Samuel rejected them all. Finally, David was called in from the fields, and Samuel anointed him with oil. Historians note that David later became famous for defeating the giant Goliath and became king of Israel around 1000 BC. Many of the psalms are traditionally said to have been written by him. Bethlehem became known as the city of David.

[long-pause]

{img:36-king-david-jerusalem-palace-old}

David made Jerusalem his capital, but he was always remembered as a son of Bethlehem. After becoming king, David captured the city of Jerusalem and made it his capital. Historians note that he brought the Ark of the Covenant, the most sacred object of the Israelites, to the city, making it a religious as well as a political centre. His son Solomon later built the first Temple there. Even so, David never forgot his home town. The scriptures tell how, during a war with the Philistines, who had taken Bethlehem, he longed for a drink of water from the well by the gate of his village. Three of his mighty warriors broke through the enemy camp to bring it to him. But David would not drink it, because they had risked their lives for it. He poured it out as an offering to God. Historians note that archaeologists have found an inscription from the ninth century BC that mentions the house of David, supporting the idea that David was a real historical king.

[long-pause]

{img:37-prophet-scroll-reading-elders-synagogue}

An old prophecy said that a great ruler would one day come from Bethlehem. In the Jewish scriptures, the prophet Micah, who lived in the eighth century BC, spoke of Bethlehem. Historians note that he wrote that from Bethlehem, though it was small among the clans of Judah, would come a ruler over Israel, whose origins were from of old. Many Jews understood this as a promise that a new king from the family of David would one day arise. This king was often called the Messiah, meaning the anointed one. Even so, ideas about the Messiah varied greatly. Some Jews expected a warrior king who would free them from foreign rule. Others expected a priestly or heavenly figure. Some did not focus on a Messiah at all. Historians note that the Gospel of Matthew quotes Micah's prophecy, saying that it was fulfilled in the birth of Jesus in Bethlehem. For the early Christians, the connection between Jesus and Bethlehem was very important, because it linked him to the family of David.

[long-pause]

{img:38-rachels-tomb-road-bethlehem-ancient}

Near Bethlehem stood the tomb of Rachel, one of the mothers of the people of Israel. On the road from Jerusalem to Bethlehem lies a site traditionally known as the tomb of Rachel. Historians note that according to the Book of Genesis, Rachel, the beloved wife of Jacob, died while giving birth to her son Benjamin on the way to Ephrath, which is Bethlehem. Jacob buried her by the road and set up a pillar over her grave. Even so, the exact location of Rachel's tomb has been debated. Some scholars believe the original site was further north. The current building dates from much later periods, and it has been rebuilt several times. Yet it has been a place of pilgrimage for Jews, Christians and Muslims for many centuries. Historians note that the Gospel of Matthew mentions Rachel weeping for her children, in connection with the sorrow in Bethlehem after Herod's cruelty. The tomb of Rachel is a reminder of the long and layered history of the land around Bethlehem.

[long-pause]

{img:39-herod-the-great-palace-throne}

At the time of the first Christmas, Judea was ruled by King Herod the Great, with the support of Rome. Herod the Great became king of Judea in 37 BC. Historians note that he was not from the old royal family of David. His family came from Idumea, south of Judea, and had converted to Judaism. He owed his throne to the Romans, who had taken control of the region in 63 BC. Herod was a clever, ambitious and ruthless ruler. He kept peace in his kingdom and remained loyal to Rome, first to Mark Antony and then to the emperor Augustus. Even so, he was feared and disliked by many of his subjects. He was suspicious of everyone and killed anyone he saw as a threat, including his own wife Mariamne and three of his sons. Historians note that the Roman emperor Augustus is said to have joked that it was better to be Herod's pig than his son. Herod died in 4 BC, after a painful illness. Most historians therefore date the birth of Jesus a few years before that.

[long-pause]

{img:40-herods-temple-jerusalem-gleaming}

Herod rebuilt the Temple in Jerusalem on a magnificent scale, making it one of the wonders of the ancient world. Herod the Great was one of the greatest builders of the ancient world. Historians note that his most famous project was the rebuilding of the Temple in Jerusalem, which began around 20 BC. He enlarged the platform on which the Temple stood, supporting it with enormous retaining walls. He built a magnificent new Temple of white stone decorated with gold, surrounded by courtyards and colonnades. Even so, the work took decades. It continued long after Herod's death and was only completed in the 60s AD, a few years before the Romans destroyed the Temple in the year 70. Historians note that part of the western retaining wall of Herod's Temple platform survives today. It is known as the Western Wall, and it is the holiest place where Jews pray. For the people of Bethlehem, the Temple was only a few hours' walk away. They went there for the great festivals, bringing offerings and joining the crowds of pilgrims.

[long-pause]

{img:41-emperor-augustus-statue-rome-forum}

Far away in Rome, the emperor Augustus ruled a vast empire that included Judea. At the time of the first Christmas, the Roman Empire was ruled by Augustus, its first emperor. Historians note that he came to power after years of civil war and ruled from 27 BC until his death in AD 14. Under his rule, the empire stretched from Spain to Syria, and from the Rhine to Egypt. He brought a long period of peace, known as the Pax Romana, the Roman peace. Even so, Rome's rule was not gentle everywhere. The empire collected taxes from its provinces and sometimes put down rebellions harshly. In Judea, Rome ruled through Herod, a client king. Historians note that Augustus ordered several censuses, counts of people and property, to organise taxes. The Gospel of Luke says that a decree went out from Caesar Augustus that the whole world should be registered, and that this was why Joseph and Mary travelled to Bethlehem. Historians have debated the details of this census for a long time.

[long-pause]

{img:42-roman-census-officials-scrolls-table}

Luke says a census brought Mary and Joseph to Bethlehem, though historians debate its date. According to the Gospel of Luke, Joseph and Mary travelled from Nazareth to Bethlehem because of a census ordered by the emperor Augustus. Joseph had to register in Bethlehem, because he belonged to the family of David. Historians note that Roman censuses were real, and that the governor Quirinius held a famous census in Judea in AD 6. Even so, there is a difficulty. Luke connects the birth with Quirinius, but Matthew places it during the reign of Herod, who died in 4 BC, about ten years earlier. Historians have offered various explanations. Some think Luke made a mistake in dating. Others suggest there may have been an earlier registration, or that the Greek words can be translated differently. It is also unclear why a Roman census would require people to travel to their ancestral town. Historians agree that the exact year of Jesus's birth is uncertain. Most place it between about 6 and 4 BC.

[long-pause]

{img:43-roman-soldiers-road-judea-patrol}

Roman soldiers and Herod's troops sometimes passed along the roads near Bethlehem. Although Herod was king, Judea was part of the Roman world. Historians note that Herod had his own army, which included soldiers from many lands, and Roman troops were stationed in the region. Soldiers marched along the roads, guarding travellers and trade, collecting taxes and keeping order. The main road from Jerusalem to Hebron passed close to Bethlehem. Even so, most villagers had little direct contact with soldiers. They saw them pass by and paid their taxes, but otherwise they lived their own lives. Historians note that many Jews resented foreign rule and the heavy taxes they had to pay, both to Herod and to Rome. They hoped for a time when they would be free. After Herod's death, there were rebellions in several parts of the country, which the Romans put down harshly. Later, in AD 66, a great revolt broke out, which ended with the destruction of Jerusalem and its Temple in AD 70.

[long-pause]

{img:44-herodium-palace-interior-pool-gardens}

Herod's palace near Bethlehem had gardens, baths and a great pool in the middle of the dry hills. Herod loved luxury. Historians note that his palaces were among the most splendid in the Roman world, with mosaics, painted walls, bathhouses and gardens. At the Herodium, near Bethlehem, he built a lower palace at the foot of the hill, with a large pool about seventy metres long, surrounded by gardens and colonnades. In the middle of the pool stood a round pavilion. Even so, building such a pool in the dry hills was a great challenge. Water was brought from springs several kilometres away, through an aqueduct. Historians note that Herod also built aqueducts that brought water to Jerusalem from pools south of Bethlehem, known today as Solomon's Pools. These works employed many workers from the villages around. For the people of Bethlehem, Herod's palace was a world apart. They saw its walls and towers on the hill, but they would never have entered its gardens or swum in its pool.

[long-pause]

{img:45-matthew-herod-soldiers-sorrow-village}

The Gospel of Matthew tells that Herod ordered the killing of the young boys of Bethlehem. According to the Gospel of Matthew, when King Herod heard from the wise men that a new king of the Jews had been born, he was troubled. Historians note that Matthew says Herod ordered the killing of all the boys in Bethlehem and its surroundings who were two years old or under. Joseph, warned in a dream, fled with Mary and the child to Egypt, and they stayed there until Herod died. Even so, this event is not mentioned by any other ancient writer, including the Jewish historian Josephus, who wrote in detail about Herod's cruelties. Historians debate whether it happened. Some argue that in a small village, only a handful of children would have been killed, which might not have been widely recorded. Others see the story as a theological echo of the story of Moses in Egypt. Yet it fits what is known of Herod's character. Christians remember the children on the feast of the Holy Innocents, on the twenty-eighth of December.

[long-pause]

{img:46-nazareth-small-village-galilee-hills}

Mary and Joseph lived in Nazareth, a small village in the hills of Galilee, far to the north of Bethlehem. According to the Gospel of Luke, Mary and Joseph lived in Nazareth, in Galilee, the northern region of the land. Historians note that Nazareth was a very small village, perhaps home to a few hundred people. It is not mentioned in the Jewish scriptures or in any other writing from before the time of Jesus. Archaeologists have found remains of simple houses, cisterns, storage pits and a few tombs there. Even so, Nazareth was not completely isolated. Only about six kilometres away stood the city of Sepphoris, which was being rebuilt as a grand Roman-style city by Herod's son Antipas. Some historians have suggested that craftsmen from Nazareth may have found work there. The people of Nazareth were farmers and craftsmen, who lived much like the people of Bethlehem. They grew grain, olives and grapes, kept animals and followed the Jewish law. The hills of Galilee were greener and more fertile than those of Judea.

[long-pause]

{img:47-joseph-carpenter-workshop-wood-tools}

Joseph was a craftsman, called a tekton, who worked with wood and perhaps with stone. The Gospels describe Joseph as a tekton, a Greek word usually translated as carpenter. Historians note that the word could mean a craftsman or builder who worked with wood, stone or both. In a village like Nazareth, a tekton might make ploughs, yokes, doors, beams, furniture and tools, and help to build houses. Wood was scarce in the region, so building in stone was also common. Even so, little else is known about Joseph. He appears in the stories of Jesus's birth and childhood, but not in the accounts of his adult life, and many historians think he may have died before Jesus began his work. The Gospels say that he was a descendant of King David. Historians note that later Christian tradition describes him as a kind and protective man. His trade would have been passed on to his son, as was the custom, and Jesus himself is called a tekton in the Gospel of Mark.

[long-pause]

{img:48-young-woman-mary-village-doorway}

Mary was a young woman from Nazareth, engaged to be married to Joseph. According to the Gospels, Mary was a young woman of Nazareth, engaged to Joseph. Historians note that girls in Jewish villages of the time were often engaged in their early or mid teens, and marriages were arranged by families. An engagement was a binding agreement, almost as serious as marriage. After a period of time, often a year, the bride moved to her husband's family home. Even so, the Gospel of Luke tells that before the marriage was completed, the angel Gabriel appeared to Mary and told her that she would bear a son. Mary is described as answering with simple faith. Historians note that later Christian tradition gave Mary great honour, and she became one of the most important figures in Christian art and devotion. Yet in her own time, she lived the ordinary life of a village woman, fetching water, grinding grain, baking bread and spinning wool, like the women of Bethlehem.

[long-pause]

{img:49-donkey-journey-road-hills-couple}

The journey from Nazareth to Bethlehem was about one hundred and fifty kilometres and took several days. The road from Nazareth to Bethlehem was about one hundred and fifty kilometres long. Historians note that the journey would have taken about four to seven days on foot. Travellers usually walked, and those who could afford it used a donkey to carry their goods or a person who could not walk far. The Gospels do not mention a donkey, but later tradition added one, and it has appeared in nativity scenes ever since. Even so, the journey was not easy. The roads were rough and dusty, and there were hills to climb. Travellers had to find water and places to rest each night. They usually travelled in groups for safety, because robbers could attack lonely travellers. Historians note that there were two main routes. One went through Samaria, the region between Galilee and Judea. The other went down the Jordan Valley, avoiding Samaria, since Jews and Samaritans were often unfriendly towards each other.

[long-pause]

{img:50-travellers-camp-night-fire-road}

At night, travellers slept in villages along the way, with relatives or in the open beside the road. When people travelled in ancient Judea, they needed places to stop each night. Historians note that there were few inns, especially on country roads. Travellers often stayed with relatives or friends, or asked for hospitality in villages along the way. Hospitality was a sacred duty in Jewish culture, and families were expected to welcome strangers. Even so, sometimes travellers had to sleep in the open, beside the road. They wrapped themselves in their cloaks and slept by a small fire, while one person kept watch. In winter, the nights could be cold and wet. Historians note that the Jordan Valley route was warmer, because it lay far below sea level. Travellers going that way would climb up from Jericho to Jerusalem through the desert, a steep and lonely road known for its robbers. From Jerusalem, it was only a short walk south to Bethlehem. The final part of the journey led over the hills.

[long-pause]

{img:51-first-sight-bethlehem-ridge-travellers}

After days on the road, the travellers finally saw the little village of Bethlehem on its ridge. Coming from Jerusalem, the road to Bethlehem passed through gentle hills, olive groves and fields. Historians imagine the travellers' first view of the village: a cluster of stone houses on a ridge, with smoke rising from the cooking fires and terraced fields around it. To the east, the land fell away towards the desert, and the Herodium rose in the distance. Even so, the Gospels say little about the journey itself. Luke simply says that Joseph went up from Galilee to Bethlehem with Mary, who was expecting a child. Historians note that Joseph may have had relatives in Bethlehem, since his family came from there. In Jewish culture, families were close, and relatives would usually offer a place to stay. Arriving in a village where one's ancestors had lived, perhaps for generations, would have been an important moment. Yet the house would also have been crowded, especially if other relatives had come too.

[long-pause]

{img:52-mary-resting-courtyard-evening-tired}

Mary arrived in Bethlehem late in her pregnancy, tired from the long journey. According to Luke, Mary was expecting her child when she and Joseph travelled to Bethlehem. Historians note that a long journey late in pregnancy would have been tiring and uncomfortable. In ancient times, giving birth was dangerous for both mother and child. Women usually gave birth at home, helped by other women of the family and by midwives, who had great experience. Even so, Mary was far from her own home and her own mother. Historians note that in a village like Bethlehem, the women of the household where they stayed would have helped her. Childbirth was a shared event, in which the women of the community supported each other. Newborn babies were washed, rubbed with salt and wrapped tightly in strips of cloth, called swaddling bands. People believed that this would help the child's limbs to grow straight and strong. Luke says that Mary wrapped her baby in swaddling clothes, as every mother in the village would have done.

[long-pause]

{img:53-crowded-village-house-relatives-gathering}

Luke writes that there was no room for them in the kataluma, a word that can mean an inn or a guest room. In the Gospel of Luke, the baby is laid in a manger because there was no room for them in the kataluma. Historians note that this Greek word has often been translated as inn, which led to the familiar picture of an unkind innkeeper turning Mary and Joseph away. Yet the same word is used elsewhere in Luke to mean a guest room, the upper room where Jesus later ate his last supper with his disciples. Many scholars now believe that Luke meant a guest room in a family house. Even so, this changes the picture of the story. Mary and Joseph may not have been rejected by strangers. They may have stayed with relatives whose guest room was already full, perhaps with other family members. So they stayed in the main part of the house, where the family lived and where the animals were brought in at night. Historians note that this was a normal arrangement in a crowded village home.

[long-pause]

{img:54-inn-caravanserai-courtyard-animals-night}

Roadside inns did exist, but they were usually rough places with rooms around a courtyard for animals. In ancient Judea, there were some roadside inns, especially on main routes. Historians note that they were usually simple buildings with rooms arranged around an open courtyard, where travellers' animals were kept. Travellers brought their own food and bedding. Such inns were often noisy, dirty and had a bad reputation. In the parable of the Good Samaritan, Luke uses a different Greek word, pandocheion, for an inn on the road from Jerusalem to Jericho. Even so, a small village like Bethlehem probably did not have a proper inn. Visitors usually stayed with relatives or were taken in by local families. Historians note that this is why many scholars prefer to translate kataluma as guest room in the Christmas story. Yet the image of the inn with no room has become part of the Christmas tradition. It has been retold in countless nativity plays, carols and paintings, and remains a beloved part of the story.

[long-pause]

{img:55-stone-manger-feeding-trough-hay}

Mangers in ancient Judea were feeding troughs for animals, often carved from stone. A manger is a feeding trough for animals. Historians note that in ancient Judea, mangers were often carved from blocks of limestone, or built into the floor of the house at the edge of the family's raised platform. Archaeologists have found stone mangers in houses and stables from that period. They were usually about a metre long, big enough to hold hay or grain for animals. Even so, a manger would also have made a suitable resting place for a newborn baby. It was sturdy, safe and raised above the floor. Filled with clean straw and lined with cloth, it would have kept the baby warm and secure. Historians note that the word manger in English comes from the French word for to eat. In Latin, it was called praesepe, which became the name for nativity scenes in Italian, presepe. The image of the baby in the manger became one of the most important symbols of Christmas.

[long-pause]

{img:56-ox-donkey-quiet-stable-lamplight}

The ox and the donkey of the nativity scene come from an old prophecy, not from the Gospels. In nativity scenes, the baby Jesus is often shown with an ox and a donkey looking on. Historians note that these animals are not mentioned in the Gospels. They come from a verse in the Book of Isaiah, which says that the ox knows its owner, and the donkey its master's manger. Early Christian writers connected this verse with the birth of Jesus, and the animals began to appear in Christian art from the fourth century onwards. Even so, it is quite likely that animals were present. In a village house, a family's ox and donkey would normally be kept in the lower part of the house at night, near the manger. Historians note that the first living nativity scene, with real people and animals, was organised by Saint Francis of Assisi in 1223, in the Italian village of Greccio. He wanted people to see how humbly Jesus was born. The custom of nativity scenes spread throughout the Christian world.

[long-pause]

{img:57-midwife-women-helping-birth-lamplight}

In the village, experienced women and midwives helped mothers through childbirth. In the ancient world, childbirth was an important and sometimes dangerous event. Historians note that it took place at home, with the help of women of the family and experienced midwives. Midwives were respected members of the community. They knew how to help a mother through labour, how to care for the newborn baby and what to do if something went wrong. They used simple tools, warm water, oil, salt and cloths. Even so, many mothers and babies died in childbirth. Families prayed for a safe birth and welcomed every healthy child with joy. Historians note that the Gospels do not mention a midwife at the birth of Jesus. Later Christian writings, such as the Gospel of James from the second century, added stories about midwives. Yet in a village like Bethlehem, it would have been natural for the women of the household to help. A birth was an event that brought the women of the community together.

[long-pause]

{img:58-oil-lamp-night-house-silence}

The Gospels describe the birth itself very simply, in just a few words. The Gospel of Luke describes the birth of Jesus in a single sentence. Historians note that it says simply that while they were in Bethlehem, the time came for Mary to give birth, and she gave birth to her firstborn son, wrapped him in swaddling clothes and laid him in a manger, because there was no room for them in the kataluma. Even so, these few words have inspired centuries of art, music and devotion. Painters, poets and composers have imagined the scene in countless ways. Historians note that Luke's account is calm and quiet. There is no description of the birth itself, no crowds and no drama. The scene is humble and ordinary, set in a simple village house among animals and straw. For the early Christians, this humility was important. It showed that the one they believed to be the Messiah was born not in a palace, but among ordinary people, in the most modest of places.

[long-pause]

{img:59-shepherds-night-sudden-light-sky}

According to Luke, an angel appeared to shepherds in the fields near Bethlehem that night. According to the Gospel of Luke, there were shepherds living in the fields near Bethlehem, keeping watch over their flock by night. Suddenly, an angel of the Lord appeared to them, and the glory of the Lord shone around them, and they were terrified. Historians note that the angel told them not to be afraid. He said that he brought good news of great joy for all the people. A saviour had been born in the city of David, and they would find the child wrapped in swaddling clothes and lying in a manger. Even so, historians approach this story differently from the accounts of daily life. It is a story of faith, told by the early Christians to express their belief about who Jesus was. Historians cannot test whether angels appeared. Yet they can note the meaning the story had for its first readers. The good news was given first to humble shepherds, not to kings or priests.

[long-pause]

{img:60-host-of-angels-sky-stars-hills}

Luke says that a great company of angels then appeared, praising God and singing of peace on earth. In Luke's account, after the angel had spoken, a multitude of the heavenly host suddenly appeared with him, praising God. They said, Glory to God in the highest, and on earth peace among those with whom he is pleased. Historians note that these words became one of the most famous phrases in Christian worship. In Latin, they begin Gloria in excelsis Deo. They are sung in church services around the world and appear in many Christmas carols. Even so, the words had a special meaning in their own time. The Roman emperor Augustus was praised as the bringer of peace, and his birthday was celebrated as good news for the world. Historians note that Luke may have been deliberately contrasting the peace brought by Augustus, imposed by armies, with the peace announced at the birth of Jesus. For the shepherds on the hillside, the experience was overwhelming, a moment of light and music in the darkness of the night.

[long-pause]

{img:61-shepherds-hurrying-path-village-night}

The shepherds hurried to Bethlehem and found Mary, Joseph and the baby lying in the manger. After the angels had gone, the shepherds said to one another, let us go to Bethlehem and see this thing that has happened. Historians note that Luke says they went with haste and found Mary and Joseph, and the baby lying in the manger. Even so, it would not have been hard for them to find the child. Bethlehem was a small village, and the shepherds probably knew every house. News of a birth would have spread quickly. A baby lying in a manger would have been unusual enough to be noticed. Historians note that the shepherds then told everyone what they had heard and seen, and all who heard it were amazed. Mary, Luke says, treasured all these words and pondered them in her heart. The shepherds returned to their flocks, glorifying and praising God. For the early Christians, these simple shepherds were the first witnesses of the Christmas story, the first to share the news with others.

[long-pause]

{img:62-shepherds-kneeling-manger-lamplight}

The visit of the shepherds has been a favourite subject for artists for more than a thousand years. The scene of the shepherds visiting the baby in the manger has inspired artists for centuries. Historians note that it appears in early Christian art and became especially popular in the Middle Ages and the Renaissance. Painters showed the shepherds kneeling in wonder, sometimes bringing gifts such as a lamb, milk or cheese. They showed the poor shepherds alongside the holy family in a humble setting. Even so, the artists usually painted the scene in the style of their own time and place. Italian painters showed Italian landscapes, Dutch painters showed Dutch farms, and snow often appeared, although snow is rare in Bethlehem. Historians note that these paintings tell us as much about the artists' world as about ancient Judea. Yet they share a common message. The shepherds represent ordinary people, welcomed into the story. In carols such as While Shepherds Watched Their Flocks by Night, written in 1700, they are remembered every Christmas.

[long-pause]

{img:63-swaddled-baby-mother-quiet-night}

Mary is described as quietly treasuring all these things and pondering them in her heart. In the Gospel of Luke, Mary is a quiet figure. Historians note that after the shepherds' visit, Luke says that Mary treasured all these things, pondering them in her heart. This gentle line has been loved by readers for centuries. It suggests a young mother, tired after the birth, holding her baby and thinking about the strange events of the night. Even so, Luke may have had another purpose. Some scholars suggest that he was hinting that Mary was one of the sources for his account, remembering events that she later shared with others. Historians cannot confirm this. Yet the image of Mary quietly reflecting has been very important in Christian art and devotion. Many paintings show her gazing at her child in silent wonder. For mothers in Bethlehem, as everywhere, the first nights with a newborn were a time of tiredness, love and quiet watchfulness, by the light of a small lamp.

[long-pause]

{img:64-circumcision-naming-family-house}

On the eighth day, the baby was circumcised and given the name Jesus. According to Jewish law, a baby boy was circumcised on the eighth day after his birth. Historians note that this was a sign of the covenant, the special relationship between God and the people of Israel, going back to Abraham. At the same time, the child was given his name. Luke says that the baby was named Jesus, the name the angel had given before he was conceived. Even so, Jesus was a common name at the time. In Hebrew or Aramaic, it was Yeshua, a shorter form of Joshua, meaning God saves. Many Jewish boys were given this name, in honour of Joshua, who had led the Israelites into the promised land. Historians note that the circumcision and naming were family occasions, often celebrated with relatives and neighbours. Prayers and blessings were said, and the family shared a meal. For Mary and Joseph, far from Nazareth, these ceremonies would have connected their child to his people and their traditions.

[long-pause]

{img:65-presentation-temple-elderly-simeon-anna}

Forty days after the birth, Mary and Joseph took the baby to the Temple in Jerusalem. According to Jewish law, forty days after the birth of a son, the mother went to the Temple to offer a sacrifice for her purification. The firstborn son was also presented to God. Historians note that Luke says Mary and Joseph took Jesus to the Temple in Jerusalem and offered a pair of turtledoves or two young pigeons. This was the offering that the law allowed for poor families who could not afford a lamb. Even so, Luke says that something unexpected happened there. An old man named Simeon, who had been waiting for the Messiah, took the child in his arms and praised God. His words became a famous prayer, known in Latin as the Nunc dimittis, which is still sung in evening services. An elderly widow named Anna, who spent her days in the Temple, also gave thanks for the child. Historians note that Bethlehem was close enough to Jerusalem for such a visit to be easy.

[long-pause]

{img:66-magi-astrologers-roof-stars-babylon}

The Gospel of Matthew tells of wise men from the east who followed a star to find a newborn king. According to the Gospel of Matthew, wise men from the east came to Jerusalem, asking where the newborn king of the Jews was, for they had seen his star at its rising. Historians note that Matthew calls them magoi, a Greek word for the magi. The magi were originally priests of the Persian religion, but the word came to mean learned men, especially those who studied the stars. Even so, the Gospel does not say exactly where they came from, or how many they were. They may have come from Persia, from Babylon, where there was a long tradition of studying the stars, or from Arabia. Historians note that in ancient times, many people believed that unusual events in the sky announced the birth or death of great rulers. Astronomers in Babylon kept careful records of the movements of the planets and stars. To such men, a new light in the sky could have seemed like a royal sign.

[long-pause]

{img:67-night-sky-planets-conjunction-bright}

Astronomers have long wondered whether the star of Bethlehem could have been a real event in the sky. For centuries, people have tried to explain the star of Bethlehem. Historians and astronomers have suggested several possibilities. In 1604, the astronomer Johannes Kepler suggested that it might have been a conjunction, a close meeting in the sky, of the planets Jupiter and Saturn. Such a conjunction happened three times in 7 BC, in the constellation of Pisces. Jupiter was often linked with kings, and some have connected Saturn and Pisces with the Jewish people. Even so, other explanations have been offered too. Chinese astronomers recorded a new star or comet in 5 BC, which some have linked to the star of Bethlehem. Others have suggested a supernova, an exploding star. Historians note that none of these explanations is certain. Matthew describes the star moving ahead of the wise men and stopping over the place where the child was, which is not how real stars or planets behave. Many scholars see the star as a symbol of faith rather than an astronomical event.

[long-pause]

{img:68-camel-caravan-desert-road-dusk}

The wise men would have travelled for weeks or months across deserts and mountains. If the wise men came from Persia or Babylon, they would have faced a long journey. Historians note that the distance from Babylon to Jerusalem was more than a thousand kilometres, and the journey could have taken weeks or even months. Travellers in that region usually went in caravans, groups of people and animals travelling together for safety. They followed trade routes along the Euphrates river and across the Syrian desert, stopping at oases and towns. Even so, Matthew does not mention camels. They come from later Christian tradition, inspired by a verse in Isaiah about camels bringing gold and frankincense. Historians note that caravans on these routes did often use camels, which could carry heavy loads and survive long periods without water. The wise men may have travelled with servants and guards. Their journey has been imagined in countless paintings and poems, including T. S. Eliot's Journey of the Magi, which describes a cold, hard journey.

[long-pause]

{img:69-magi-herod-palace-audience-scrolls}

When the wise men arrived in Jerusalem, King Herod called them to his palace in secret. According to Matthew, when the wise men arrived in Jerusalem asking about a newborn king, King Herod was troubled, and all Jerusalem with him. Historians note that this fits what is known about Herod, who was extremely jealous of his power. He called together the chief priests and scribes and asked them where the Messiah was to be born. They answered that it was Bethlehem in Judea, quoting the prophet Micah. Even so, Herod hid his true intentions. He called the wise men secretly and asked them exactly when the star had appeared. Then he sent them to Bethlehem, telling them to search carefully for the child and to bring him word, so that he too could come and worship him. Historians note that Matthew presents Herod as a dangerous and deceitful king, in contrast to the wise men, who came in good faith, and to the child, who was born as a humble king.

[long-pause]

{img:70-gold-frankincense-myrrh-gifts-cloth}

The wise men brought gifts of gold, frankincense and myrrh, which were precious treasures of the ancient world. Matthew says that the wise men followed the star to Bethlehem, to the house where the child was. They knelt down and paid him homage, and they opened their treasure chests and offered gifts of gold, frankincense and myrrh. Historians note that these were among the most valuable goods of the ancient world. Gold was a gift fit for a king. Frankincense was a fragrant resin from trees in southern Arabia and Somalia, burned as incense in temples. Myrrh was another precious resin, used in perfumes, medicines and for anointing the dead. Even so, Christians later gave the gifts symbolic meanings. Gold represented kingship, frankincense represented divinity, and myrrh foreshadowed death. Historians note that because there were three gifts, tradition later decided that there were three wise men. By the sixth century, they had been given names, Caspar, Melchior and Balthasar. Matthew himself never says how many there were, only that they came from the east.

[long-pause]

{img:71-magi-departing-another-road-dawn}

Warned in a dream, the wise men returned home by another road, avoiding Herod. After visiting the child, the wise men were warned in a dream not to return to Herod. Historians note that Matthew says they left for their own country by another road. Joseph, too, was warned in a dream and took Mary and the child to Egypt, where they remained until Herod died. Even so, the wise men then disappear from the Gospel. Nothing more is said about them. Yet later Christian tradition developed many stories about them. They were described as kings, an idea drawn from the Psalms, and as representatives of the different peoples of the world. In medieval art, one of them was often shown as African, to show that the message was for all nations. Historians note that in the twelfth century, relics said to be theirs were brought to Cologne, in Germany, where they are kept in a golden shrine in the cathedral. The feast of the Epiphany, on the sixth of January, celebrates their visit.

[long-pause]

{img:72-epiphany-celebration-church-candles}

Christians remember the wise men on the feast of the Epiphany, twelve days after Christmas. The feast of the Epiphany is celebrated on the sixth of January, twelve days after Christmas. Historians note that the word Epiphany comes from Greek and means appearance or revelation. For many Christians, the feast remembers the visit of the wise men, showing that Jesus was revealed to people of all nations. Even so, the feast has been celebrated in different ways in different places. In the Eastern churches, Epiphany remembers mainly the baptism of Jesus in the river Jordan. In some countries, Epiphany is more important than Christmas for giving presents. In Spain and Latin America, children receive gifts from the Three Kings on the night of the fifth of January. In parts of Europe, children dressed as the three kings go from house to house singing and writing a blessing over the door in chalk. Historians note that the twelve days between Christmas and Epiphany became the twelve days of Christmas, celebrated in the famous English song.

[long-pause]

{img:73-church-nativity-ancient-stone-facade}

In the fourth century, the emperor Constantine built a great church over the cave in Bethlehem. In the early fourth century, the Roman emperor Constantine became a Christian. Historians note that his mother, Helena, travelled to the Holy Land around 326 and helped to found churches at the most important sites of Christian tradition. In Bethlehem, a great church was built over the cave believed to be the place of the birth of Jesus. It was dedicated in 339. Even so, the church that stands today is not quite the same building. Constantine's church was badly damaged, probably in a revolt in the sixth century, and it was rebuilt on a larger scale by the emperor Justinian. Historians note that this sixth century church, the Church of the Nativity, is one of the oldest churches in the world still in daily use. Its main entrance is a very small doorway, called the Door of Humility, which forces visitors to bow as they enter. It was made smaller centuries ago, probably to stop people riding in on horseback.

[long-pause]

{img:74-grotto-silver-star-floor-lamps}

Beneath the church lies the Grotto of the Nativity, where a silver star marks the traditional place of the birth. Beneath the main altar of the Church of the Nativity lies a cave called the Grotto of the Nativity. Historians note that visitors descend narrow stone steps to reach it. The grotto is small and dimly lit, its walls hung with tapestries and dozens of small oil lamps. In the floor is a fourteen-pointed silver star, set in marble, marking the place where tradition says Jesus was born. Around it is a Latin inscription meaning, here Jesus Christ was born of the Virgin Mary. Even so, the star has its own history. It was placed there in 1717. In 1847, it was stolen, and the dispute over its replacement was one of the quarrels between European powers that led up to the Crimean War. A new star was installed in 1853. Historians note that nearby is a small chapel with a stone manger. The church is shared today by several Christian communities, who care for it according to ancient agreements.

[long-pause]

{img:75-jerome-scholar-cave-writing-lamp}

The scholar Jerome lived in Bethlehem for many years and translated the Bible into Latin there. In 386, a learned priest named Jerome came to live in Bethlehem. Historians note that he was one of the greatest scholars of the early church. He had studied in Rome and in the East, and he knew Latin, Greek and Hebrew. In Bethlehem, he founded a monastery, and his friend Paula, a wealthy Roman widow, founded a convent nearby. Jerome lived and worked in caves near the Church of the Nativity. Even so, his most important work was a translation of the Bible into Latin. It took him many years, and he worked directly from Hebrew and Greek texts. His translation, known as the Vulgate, became the standard Bible of the Western church for more than a thousand years. Historians note that Jerome died in Bethlehem in 420. The caves where he is believed to have worked can still be visited, beneath the Church of Saint Catherine, next to the Church of the Nativity.

[long-pause]

{img:76-medieval-pilgrims-arriving-bethlehem}

For centuries, pilgrims travelled from all over Europe and the East to visit Bethlehem. From the fourth century onwards, Bethlehem became a place of pilgrimage. Historians note that Christians from all over the Roman world travelled there to pray at the place of the birth of Jesus. One of the earliest pilgrims to leave a written account was a woman named Egeria, who visited the Holy Land in the 380s. Even so, the journey was long and difficult. Pilgrims travelled by sea and over land, facing storms, bandits and illness. Many did not return home. Over the centuries, Bethlehem was ruled by Byzantines, Arab caliphs, Crusaders, Mamluks and Ottoman Turks. Historians note that in 1100, the Crusader Baldwin was crowned the first king of Jerusalem in the Church of the Nativity on Christmas Day. Pilgrims continued to come in every age. They brought back stories, souvenirs and small objects made of mother-of-pearl and olive wood, crafts for which Bethlehem has been famous for centuries.

[long-pause]

{img:77-olive-wood-carvers-workshop-bethlehem}

For centuries, the craftsmen of Bethlehem have carved nativity figures from olive wood. The olive trees of Bethlehem have provided more than oil. Historians note that for centuries, the craftsmen of the town have carved olive wood into crosses, rosaries, boxes and nativity figures. They also make objects decorated with mother-of-pearl, the shiny inner layer of shells. These crafts may have been introduced or encouraged by Franciscan friars, who came to the Holy Land in the fourteenth century to care for its holy sites. Even so, the crafts became a central part of Bethlehem's life. Families passed the skills down from generation to generation. Pilgrims bought the carvings and took them home to every corner of the world. Historians note that olive wood is hard, with a beautiful grain, and it is cut from pruned branches or old trees, so that living trees are not destroyed. Many families in Bethlehem still earn their living from these traditional crafts, which carry the memory of the town into homes far away.

[long-pause]

{img:78-manger-square-christmas-eve-crowd-lights}

Today, Christmas in Bethlehem is celebrated three times, by different Christian traditions. Today, Bethlehem is a town of about thirty thousand people, in the West Bank. Historians note that its population includes both Muslims and Christians. Christmas is the most important time of year in the town. It is celebrated three times. Catholic and Protestant Christians celebrate on the twenty-fifth of December. Greek Orthodox and some other Eastern churches celebrate on the seventh of January, following the old Julian calendar. Armenian Christians celebrate on the eighteenth or nineteenth of January. Even so, the heart of the celebration is the same. On Christmas Eve, crowds gather in Manger Square, in front of the Church of the Nativity. A great tree is lit, processions arrive, scouts' bands play music, and a midnight mass is held. Historians note that the town's celebrations have been affected at times by conflict and hardship. Yet every year, the story of the first Christmas is told again in the place where, according to tradition, it began.

[long-pause]

{img:79-december-25-date-roman-calendar-scroll}

The date of the twenty-fifth of December was chosen by Christians in Rome in the fourth century. The Gospels do not say on what day Jesus was born. Historians note that the earliest record of Christmas being celebrated on the twenty-fifth of December comes from a Roman calendar of the year 354, which records it for the year 336. Before that, some Christians had suggested other dates, and some did not celebrate the birth at all. Even so, the reason for choosing the twenty-fifth of December is not certain. Historians offer several explanations. Some believe the date was chosen to replace pagan festivals held near the winter solstice, such as Saturnalia or the birthday of the unconquered sun. Others believe early Christians calculated it, counting nine months from the date they believed Jesus was conceived, the twenty-fifth of March. Historians note that the question is still debated. Yet whatever the reason, the date spread across the Christian world. The quiet birth in a small village became a festival celebrated by billions of people every year.

[long-pause]

{img:80-bethlehem-hills-dusk-star-peaceful}

More than two thousand years later, the hills around Bethlehem still turn gold and violet at dusk. The village of Bethlehem has changed greatly over two thousand years. Historians note that it has grown into a town, with streets, shops, churches, mosques, schools and homes. Yet the hills around it would still be familiar to the shepherds of long ago. Olive trees still grow on the terraces, and in places sheep and goats still graze on the slopes. The winter rains still turn the hills green, and the summer sun still bakes them gold. Even so, the most important change is not in the landscape, but in the name. Bethlehem, once a small village known mainly as the birthplace of King David, became known around the world because of a child born in a humble house, laid in a manger. Historians note that every December, its name is sung in carols in hundreds of languages. And at dusk, when the first star appears over the hills, it is easy to imagine the quiet village as it once was.

[long-pause]

So we leave Bethlehem as night settles over the hills of Judea.

The flocks are resting in their stone folds, and the shepherds pull their cloaks around them by the fire. In the little houses, the oil lamps flicker low, and the bread for tomorrow waits beneath a cloth. Above the quiet village, one bright star shines among the many.
