Tonight we are going about two thousand years back, to a small village on a hilltop in the land of Judea, just a few miles south of the city of Jerusalem.

[long-pause]

It is evening, in the winter, and the light is fading over the hills.

[pause]

The hills here are steep and rocky, and their slopes are built up into long, narrow terraces, held up by low walls of pale limestone, one above another, like giant steps. On the terraces, there are olive trees, old and twisted and silver-green, and vines, and little fields of wheat and barley. Between the terraces, the rock shows through, white and grey, with wild thyme and sage growing in the cracks.

[pause]

If gentle stories from the past help you rest, you are welcome to subscribe to the channel. Now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

On the top of the ridge, there is a village.

[pause]

It is called Bethlehem. In the language of the people who live here, the name means house of bread, perhaps because of the fields of wheat and barley that grow on the slopes around it.

[pause]

It is a small place, perhaps a few hundred people, perhaps a thousand: a cluster of square, flat-roofed houses of pale stone, crowded together along the ridge, with narrow lanes winding between them, and courtyards and gardens behind walls, and a few trees, olives and figs and almonds, growing among the houses.

[long-pause]

From the edge of the village, there is a wide view in every direction.

[pause]

To the north, along the ridge, the road runs to Jerusalem, about six miles away. On a clear day, from the higher places, you can just see the great city on its hills, and the walls of the Temple, gleaming white and gold in the sun.

[pause]

{img:view-from-ridge-desert-east-dead-sea-haze}
To the east, the land falls away, down and down, into a wild, empty desert of bare brown hills and deep, winding valleys, all the way to the Dead Sea, a long, strange, shining lake, far below, in the bottom of the great rift, with the mountains of Moab rising beyond it, blue and purple in the evening light.

[long-pause]

To the south and the west, there are more hills, and more terraces, and little villages on the hilltops, and the road winding away south, toward the old town of Hebron.

[pause]

And on one hill, a few miles to the southeast, there is a strange shape: a round, flat-topped mountain, like a cone with its top cut off, with a great fortress and palace on its summit. It was built, only a few years before, by the king of the land, Herod. People call it the Herodium.

[pause]

{img:herodium-cone-hill-fortress-distant-sunset}
From Bethlehem, it is always there on the horizon, a reminder of the power of the king.

[long-pause]

The land around Bethlehem is not rich. The soil is thin and stony, and the summers are long and hot and dry. But in the winter, from about November to March, the rains come.

[pause]

They come in from the sea, far to the west, in grey clouds rolling over the hills, and the rain falls hard and cold on the terraces and the rooftops, and runs down the slopes, and fills the cisterns that the people have cut into the rock to catch the water. The hills turn green, and the wild flowers bloom: anemones, red as blood, and cyclamen, and crocuses, and little white stars of Bethlehem.

[pause]

{img:winter-rain-clouds-over-terraced-hills-green}
On winter nights, it can be cold up here, in the hills, much colder than people from northern lands expect. Sometimes there is frost, and very occasionally, a little snow.

[long-pause]

Out in the fields around the village, there are flocks of sheep and goats.

[pause]

They graze on the hillsides, among the rocks and the scrub, watched over by shepherds, who walk with them all day, and lead them to water, and to fresh grazing, and protect them from wild animals. In the evening, the shepherds bring their flocks into sheepfolds, rough enclosures of stone walls, or into the caves that are found everywhere in the soft limestone of the hills.

[pause]

On this winter evening, as the sun goes down behind the hills to the west, the shepherds are bringing their flocks in for the night, and lighting small fires, and settling down to keep watch.

[long-pause]

In the village, the lamps are being lit.

[pause]

They are little clay oil lamps, filled with olive oil, with a wick of flax, which give a small, warm, flickering light. One by one, they glow in the doorways and the small windows of the houses. The smell of wood smoke and baking bread drifts across the rooftops.

[pause]

{img:clay-oil-lamps-glowing-doorways-village-night}
The stars come out, very bright and very many, over the dark hills. A dog barks. A donkey brays. A mother calls her children in. And the village of Bethlehem settles down for the night, as it has done for a thousand years, and more.

[long-pause]

{img:ancient-judean-stone-house-flat-roof-courtyard}
The houses of Bethlehem are built of stone, from the pale limestone of the hills.

[pause]

They are simple, square buildings, often only one storey high, with thick walls, a single wooden door, and one or two small windows, high up, to let in a little light and air while keeping out the heat of the summer and the cold of the winter.

[long-pause]

The roofs are flat.

[pause]

They are made of wooden beams laid across the walls, covered with branches and reeds, and then with a thick layer of earth and clay, rolled smooth and hard with a stone roller. After every rain, the roof must be rolled again, to keep it watertight. A stone staircase on the outside of the house leads up to the roof.

[pause]

The roof is a very important part of the house. In the summer, the family sleeps there, in the cool night air. During the day, the women dry figs and grapes and flax there, in the sun, and spread out the washing. And in the evening, the family sits there, talking and watching the sunset over the hills, and calling out to their neighbours on the other roofs.

[long-pause]

Inside, many of the houses have just one main room, but it is divided into two levels.

[pause]

The larger part of the room is raised up, a step or two higher than the rest. This is where the family lives, and eats, and sleeps. The floor is of beaten earth or stone, covered with mats of woven reeds or straw. There are a few clay jars along the walls, for water and oil and grain, and a chest or two, and rolled-up sleeping mats and blankets, which are spread out on the floor at night.

[pause]

{img:house-interior-raised-living-area-lower-animals}
The lower part of the room, near the door, is where the animals live, at night and in the winter: the donkey, perhaps a cow or an ox, a few sheep or goats. Along the edge of the raised floor, there are mangers, little troughs cut into the stone or built of stone and clay, where the animals eat their hay and straw, reaching up to the edge of the family's floor.

[long-pause]

So, on a winter night, the family and their animals sleep under the same roof, in the same room, the family on the higher floor, the animals below.

[pause]

The animals are safe from thieves and wild animals, and their bodies help to warm the house. And in the morning, the family wakes up to the sound of the donkey shifting and the goats bleating, and the smell of hay.

[pause]

{img:donkey-goats-manger-stone-trough-lamp-night}
Some of the larger houses have a second room, for guests. It might be built on the roof, or at the back of the house, or beside the main room. In the language of the time, it is called a kataluma, a guest room, or a lodging place.

[long-pause]

Hospitality is very important to the people of Bethlehem, as it is all over this part of the world.

[pause]

A traveller who arrives at a village, especially a relative, or a friend, or a person from the same family or tribe, is welcomed, and given food and water and a place to sleep. To turn away a guest is a great shame. The guest room is kept ready, with mats and blankets, for whoever might come.

[pause]

{img:host-welcoming-traveller-doorway-water-jar}
When there are many guests, at a wedding or a festival, the guest room fills up, and the extra guests sleep wherever there is space: on the roof, in the courtyard, or in the main room, with the family, and the animals.

[long-pause]

Many of the houses in Bethlehem are built against the hillside, and some of them are built in front of caves.

[pause]

The soft limestone of the hills is full of caves, natural ones and ones that have been cut out by people, over many centuries. The people of Bethlehem use them for many things: as stables for their animals, as storerooms for their grain and oil and wine, as workshops, and sometimes as part of their houses. A house might have a stone room at the front, and a cave at the back, cut into the hill, cool in summer and warm in winter.

[pause]

In the caves, the temperature hardly changes all year round, and the animals are snug and dry, even on the coldest winter nights.

[long-pause]

Around the houses, there are courtyards, shared by several families.

[pause]

In the courtyard, there is an oven, a cistern for water, a millstone for grinding grain, and perhaps a fig tree or an olive tree, giving shade. The women work in the courtyard during the day, grinding and baking and spinning and cooking, and talking with their neighbours. The children play there. The chickens scratch about in the dust.

[pause]

{img:shared-courtyard-women-grinding-grain-fig-tree}
In the evening, the families of the courtyard sit together, and share the news of the day, while the bread bakes in the oven, and the lamps are lit, and the stars come out over the rooftops.

[long-pause]

{img:woman-grinding-wheat-hand-quern-dawn-courtyard}
The day in Bethlehem begins at dawn, with the sound of grinding.

[pause]

In every courtyard, as the sky grows pale over the eastern hills, the women and the girls are already at work, kneeling beside the hand mill: two round, flat stones, one on top of the other, with a wooden handle. They pour a handful of wheat or barley into the hole in the top stone, and turn it round and round, and the flour trickles out from between the stones onto a cloth. It is slow, hard work, and it must be done every single day.

[long-pause]

From the flour, they make bread.

[pause]

They mix it with water and a little salt and a piece of yesterday's dough, to make it rise, and knead it, and shape it into round, flat loaves. Then they bake it in the oven in the courtyard.

[pause]

The oven is called a tabun. It is a dome of clay, about as high as a child's waist, with an opening at the top, and a fire of twigs and dried dung burning inside it, on a bed of stones. When the stones are hot, the women slap the flat loaves onto the inside walls of the oven, or onto the hot stones, and in a few minutes, the bread is baked, golden and puffed and smelling wonderful.

[pause]

Bread is the most important food of all. The people eat it at every meal, and use it instead of spoons, to scoop up their food.

[long-pause]

After the grinding and the baking comes the water.

[pause]

There is no river in Bethlehem. The people get their water from cisterns, deep pits cut into the rock, which fill up with rainwater in the winter. There is also a famous well, near the gate of the village, which is called the Well of David, after the great king, who was born here, a thousand years before, and who once, the old stories say, longed to drink its water when he was far away.

[pause]

{img:women-carrying-water-jars-heads-well-morning}
The girls and the women go to the well in the morning and the evening, with tall clay jars on their heads or their shoulders, and wait their turn, and talk and laugh, and carry the heavy jars home, carefully, without spilling a drop.

[long-pause]

The food of Bethlehem is simple and good.

[pause]

There is bread, always, with olive oil, for dipping. There are lentils and chickpeas and beans, cooked into a thick stew with onions and garlic and herbs. There are cucumbers and leeks and onions from the gardens. There is cheese and yogurt, from the milk of the goats and sheep. There are eggs from the hens.

[pause]

{img:simple-meal-bread-olive-oil-lentils-figs-mat}
There is fruit: figs, fresh in the summer, and dried in the winter, pressed into cakes; grapes, fresh and dried into raisins; dates, brought up from the warm valley of Jericho; pomegranates, and almonds, and olives, cured in brine. There is honey, from wild bees, and from the dates.

[pause]

Meat is eaten only on special days: at festivals and weddings, when a lamb or a young goat is roasted, and shared with the whole family and the neighbours. Fish is brought up, dried and salted, from the Sea of Galilee, far to the north.

[long-pause]

And there is wine.

[pause]

The vineyards on the terraces around Bethlehem produce grapes, which are picked in the late summer, and trodden in stone vats cut into the rock, and the juice is left to ferment in clay jars, and becomes wine. Wine is drunk with meals, usually mixed with water, and is a sign of joy and celebration.

[pause]

{img:treading-grapes-rock-cut-winepress-terraces}
In the autumn, there is the olive harvest. The families go out to their olive trees on the terraces, and beat the branches with long sticks, so that the olives fall onto cloths spread on the ground, and gather them in baskets, and take them to the olive press, where they are crushed under a great round stone, turned by a donkey, and the golden oil flows out into jars.

[pause]

The olive oil is used for everything: for cooking, for the lamps, for medicine, and for rubbing on the skin and the hair.

[long-pause]

During the day, the men work in the fields and the vineyards and the olive groves, or with the flocks.

[pause]

Others are craftsmen: builders and carpenters, potters and weavers, blacksmiths and stonemasons. Some of them work in Jerusalem, a few hours' walk away, on the great building works of King Herod, and come home in the evening, or at the end of the week.

[pause]

The women work at home, and in the courtyards and the gardens: grinding and baking, cooking and cleaning, spinning and weaving, looking after the children and the animals, fetching the water, tending the garden. And at harvest time, everyone works in the fields together.

[long-pause]

In the evening, when the work is done, the families gather in their houses or on their roofs, to eat the evening meal together.

[pause]

They sit on mats on the floor, around a large bowl of stew, and a pile of bread, and dip their bread into the bowl, and eat with their fingers. Before they eat, the father of the family says a blessing over the bread: Blessed are you, Lord our God, king of the universe, who brings forth bread from the earth.

[pause]

{img:family-sitting-on-mats-evening-meal-lamplight}
Afterwards, they talk, and tell stories, and sing, and the children fall asleep on their mothers' laps. And then the lamps are put out, and the mats are unrolled, and the family lies down to sleep, close together, on the raised floor of the house, under their woollen blankets, while the animals settle in the straw below.

[long-pause]

{img:first-autumn-rain-terraces-ploughing-oxen}
In the land of Judea, the year turns with the rain.

[pause]

After the long, hot, dry summer, when no rain falls at all for months, and the hills turn brown and dusty, everyone waits for the first rains. They usually come in October or November, in a great storm rolling in from the sea, with thunder and lightning, and heavy rain beating on the dry earth. The farmers call it the early rain.

[long-pause]

As soon as the first rain has softened the hard ground, the ploughing begins.

[pause]

The farmers go out to their terraces and their fields with their ploughs: simple wooden ploughs, with an iron tip, pulled by a pair of oxen, or a single donkey, or sometimes by the farmer himself. They plough the narrow terraces, back and forth, turning over the red-brown earth, while the birds follow behind, looking for worms.

[pause]

Then they sow the seed, wheat and barley, scattering it by hand from a bag over their shoulder, in wide sweeps, and plough again, lightly, to cover it. And then they wait for the rain to make it grow.

[long-pause]

All through the winter, from November to March, the rains come and go.

[pause]

Some days are grey and wet and cold, and the rain pours down, and the water runs in streams down the terraces and the lanes, and fills the cisterns. Other days are clear and bright and crisp, with a pale blue sky, and the hills glowing green and gold in the low winter sun.

[pause]

{img:green-winter-hills-anemones-blue-sky-bethlehem}
The hills turn green. The wild flowers bloom: red anemones, purple cyclamen, white narcissus, yellow chrysanthemums. The almond trees blossom, pink and white, in January and February, the first trees to flower, the sign that spring is coming.

[long-pause]

In the spring, the latter rains come, in March and April, the last rains of the year, which fill out the ears of the wheat and the barley.

[pause]

Then, in April and May, comes the barley harvest, and then the wheat harvest, in May and June. The whole village goes out into the fields, with sickles, and cuts the grain, and ties it into sheaves, and carries it on donkeys to the threshing floor.

[pause]

The threshing floor is a flat, round place, of bare rock or hard-beaten earth, on a hilltop, where the wind blows. The sheaves are spread out on it, and oxen walk round and round over them, dragging a heavy wooden sledge, studded underneath with sharp stones, which separates the grain from the straw.

[pause]

Then, in the evening, when the wind blows from the sea, the farmers toss the threshed grain into the air with wooden forks, and the wind carries the light chaff away, and the heavy grain falls back onto the floor, in a golden heap.

[long-pause]

At harvest time, the farmers often sleep at the threshing floor, at night, to guard the grain.

[pause]

It is a happy time. The work is hard, but there is plenty of food, and the evenings are warm, and the people sing and laugh and tell stories under the stars, beside the heaps of golden grain.

[pause]

{img:harvest-celebration-music-dancing-threshing-floor-night}
At the end of the wheat harvest, there is a festival, called Shavuot, the Feast of Weeks, when people go up to Jerusalem, to the Temple, with the first fruits of their harvest, to give thanks.

[long-pause]

In the summer, from June to September, there is no rain at all.

[pause]

The sun burns down on the hills, and the grass turns brown, and the springs run low. But at night, there is dew, heavy and cool, which waters the vines and the fig trees. The people work in the early morning and the evening, and rest in the heat of the day, in the shade.

[pause]

In the late summer, the grapes ripen on the vines, and the figs on the fig trees, and the people pick them, and eat them fresh, and dry them in the sun on their roofs, and tread the grapes in the wine presses cut into the rock. During the grape harvest, many families live out in the vineyards, in little shelters made of branches, to guard the grapes, and the evenings are full of music and singing.

[long-pause]

In the autumn, there is the feast of Sukkot, the Feast of Booths, at the end of the harvest.

[pause]

During the feast, for seven days, the families live in little huts, built of branches and palm fronds, on their roofs and in their courtyards, to remember the time, long ago, when their ancestors lived in tents in the desert. The children love it. They sleep in the huts, and look up through the branches at the stars.

[pause]

{img:family-in-branch-hut-sukkot-roof-stars-lamps}
And then, in October, the people look up at the sky, and wait for the first rains, and the year begins again.

[long-pause]

{img:shepherd-boy-sheep-goats-rocky-hillside-morning}
In the fields and on the hillsides around Bethlehem, the shepherds watch over their flocks.

[pause]

The flocks are mixed: sheep, with thick creamy-white fleeces and broad, fat tails, and goats, black and brown, with long ears and long shaggy hair. The sheep give wool and milk and meat. The goats give milk and meat and hair, which is woven into the black cloth of tents and sacks and blankets.

[long-pause]

Shepherding is one of the oldest ways of life in this land.

[pause]

The shepherds are often the younger sons of the families of the village, boys and young men, sometimes even girls. Some of them look after their own family's sheep. Others are hired to look after the flocks of other people, for wages, or for a share of the lambs.

[pause]

They spend almost all their time out in the hills with the animals, walking with them in the morning to fresh grazing, leading them in the heat of the day to the shade of a rock or a tree, taking them to water at a well or a spring, and bringing them back to the fold in the evening.

[long-pause]

A shepherd carries very little.

[pause]

He has a staff, a long stick with a crook at the end, to guide the sheep, and to lean on, and to catch a sheep by the leg if it wanders off. He has a club, to protect the flock from wild animals: wolves, and jackals, and hyenas, which still roam the hills in those days. He has a sling, a strip of leather, with which he can throw a stone with great force and accuracy, to drive away a predator, or to turn a straying sheep.

[pause]

{img:shepherd-playing-reed-flute-resting-rock-sheep}
He has a leather bag, with bread and olives and dried figs and cheese, for his meals, and a skin of water. He has a thick cloak, of wool or goat hair, which is his coat by day and his blanket by night. And often, he has a little flute, made of reed, on which he plays tunes to pass the time, and to calm the sheep.

[long-pause]

The shepherds know their sheep very well.

[pause]

They give them names, and the sheep know their shepherd's voice, and follow him when he calls. In this land, the shepherd does not drive his flock from behind, like the shepherds of other lands. He walks in front of them, and they follow him. When two flocks meet at a well, and mix together, each shepherd simply calls, and walks away, and his own sheep follow him, and only his.

[pause]

{img:two-shepherds-flocks-at-stone-well-trough}
When a sheep is lost, the shepherd leaves the others in the fold, or with another shepherd, and goes out into the hills to look for it, and when he finds it, he carries it home on his shoulders.

[long-pause]

At night, the shepherds bring their flocks into the sheepfolds.

[pause]

A sheepfold is a circle of rough stone walls, about as high as a man's chest, with thorn branches on top, to keep out the wild animals, and a single narrow gap for an entrance. Or it is a cave, in the side of a hill, with a wall built across the front.

[pause]

{img:stone-sheepfold-night-fire-shepherds-wrapped-cloaks}
In the evening, the shepherd stands at the entrance of the fold, and counts the sheep as they pass in, one by one, touching each one with his staff. Then, often, he lies down across the entrance himself, wrapped in his cloak, so that nothing can get in or out without passing over him. In this way, the shepherd is himself the gate of the fold.

[long-pause]

In the winter months, the nights in the hills are long and cold.

[pause]

The shepherds gather around little fires of thorn bushes and dried dung, near the folds, and wrap themselves in their cloaks, and talk, and tell stories, and sing, and take turns to sleep and to keep watch. Above them, the stars wheel slowly across the black sky: so many stars, so bright and so close, out here in the hills, far from any town.

[pause]

The shepherds know the stars well. They know their names, and how they move through the seasons, and they can tell the time of night by them.

[long-pause]

The people of the towns do not always think very highly of the shepherds.

[pause]

They are rough and poor and unwashed, and they are away in the hills so much that they cannot always keep all the religious laws, and go to the synagogue on the Sabbath. Some people do not trust them.

[pause]

But in the old stories of the people of Israel, shepherds are honoured. Abraham and Isaac and Jacob, the great ancestors of the people, were shepherds. Moses was a shepherd, in the desert, when he saw the burning bush. And the greatest king of Israel, David, was a shepherd boy, here, in the fields of Bethlehem.

[pause]

{img:ancient-shepherd-king-psalm-harp-hillside-legend}
It was David, people say, who wrote the most famous of all the songs about shepherds: The Lord is my shepherd, I shall not want. He makes me lie down in green pastures. He leads me beside still waters. He restores my soul.

[long-pause]

{img:barley-harvest-fields-bethlehem-reapers-gleaner}
Bethlehem is a small village. But it is a village with a long and famous history, which everyone who lives there knows.

[pause]

The people of Bethlehem tell the old stories of their village to their children, by the fire, in the evenings, as their parents told them, and their grandparents before them. And the two most famous of those stories are the story of Ruth, and the story of David.

[long-pause]

The story of Ruth happened more than a thousand years before.

[pause]

Once, there was a famine in Bethlehem, and a man named Elimelech took his wife Naomi and their two sons, and went to live in the land of Moab, across the Dead Sea, where there was food. There, the two sons married women of Moab. But then Elimelech died, and both of the sons died too, and Naomi was left alone, with her two daughters-in-law, far from home.

[pause]

Naomi decided to go home to Bethlehem. She told her daughters-in-law to stay in Moab, with their own families. One of them, sadly, said goodbye, and went home. But the other, whose name was Ruth, would not leave her.

[long-pause]

Ruth said to Naomi some of the most beautiful words in all the old stories.

[pause]

Where you go, I will go. Where you stay, I will stay. Your people will be my people, and your God will be my God. Where you die, I will die, and there I will be buried.

[pause]

{img:two-women-walking-road-to-bethlehem-hills}
And so the two women, the old widow and the young widow, walked together all the way from Moab to Bethlehem, and arrived just at the beginning of the barley harvest, in the spring.

[long-pause]

They were very poor. So Ruth went out into the fields, to glean.

[pause]

In those days, the law said that when the farmers harvested their fields, they must not gather up every last ear of grain, but must leave some behind, at the edges of the fields and where it fell, for the poor and the widows and the strangers to gather. This was called gleaning.

[pause]

Ruth went to glean in the field of a rich and kindly man named Boaz, who happened to be a relative of Naomi's husband. Boaz noticed her, working hard all day in the hot sun, and asked who she was. When he heard her story, and how faithful she had been to Naomi, he was moved. He told his workers to let her glean among the sheaves, and to leave extra grain for her on purpose. He invited her to eat with his workers at midday, bread dipped in wine, and roasted grain.

[long-pause]

In the end, Boaz married Ruth.

[pause]

They had a son, called Obed. And Naomi, who had lost everything, held her little grandson in her arms, and was happy again. And the women of Bethlehem said to her: Blessed be the Lord, who has not left you without a family.

[pause]

{img:old-woman-holding-baby-grandson-village-women}
Obed grew up, and had a son called Jesse. And Jesse had eight sons. And the youngest of them was called David.

[long-pause]

David was a shepherd boy, in the fields of Bethlehem.

[pause]

He looked after his father's sheep on the hills around the village, and he became strong and brave, protecting them from lions and bears, the old stories say. He was skilful with his sling. And he played the harp, and sang, and made songs, sitting on the hillsides with his flock.

[pause]

{img:shepherd-boy-harp-hillside-sheep-sunset}
One day, the prophet Samuel came to Bethlehem, and asked to see all the sons of Jesse. Jesse brought out seven of his sons, one after another, tall and handsome and strong. But Samuel said that God had not chosen any of these. Do you have any more sons? he asked. There is still the youngest, said Jesse. He is out in the fields, looking after the sheep.

[pause]

So they sent for David. And when he came in, from the fields, with his shepherd's staff, Samuel anointed him with oil, as the one who would one day be king.

[long-pause]

David grew up to become the greatest king of Israel.

[pause]

He made Jerusalem his capital, a few miles to the north of Bethlehem, and ruled over a great kingdom. He wrote many of the songs and prayers that are still sung today, the Psalms. And the people of Israel remembered him for a thousand years, as their greatest king.

[pause]

And they believed, because their prophets had said so, that one day, a new king would be born, of the family of David, who would be like a shepherd to his people, and bring peace. And one of the prophets, named Micah, had said that this king would come from Bethlehem.

[long-pause]

So the people of Bethlehem, two thousand years ago, are proud of their village, and its history.

[pause]

{img:old-man-telling-stories-children-courtyard-evening}
When they work in the fields where Ruth once gleaned, and walk on the hills where David once kept his sheep, and draw water from the well that David once longed for, they remember the old stories. And on winter evenings, by the fire, the old people tell them to the children, as they have always done.

[long-pause]

{img:ancient-children-playing-knucklebones-courtyard}
The children of Bethlehem, two thousand years ago, have busy lives.

[pause]

From a very young age, they help their families. The little ones feed the chickens, and carry small loads, and mind the babies. The older girls help their mothers with the grinding and the baking and the spinning, and carry water from the well. The older boys help their fathers in the fields, and go out with the flocks, learning to be shepherds.

[long-pause]

But there is also time for play.

[pause]

The children play in the lanes and the courtyards, and on the rooftops, and in the fields. They play games of tag and hide and seek among the olive trees. They play with knucklebones, the little ankle bones of sheep, tossing them in the air and catching them on the backs of their hands. They play with spinning tops, and with balls made of rags, and with little clay figures of animals, and dolls made of wood and cloth.

[pause]

Archaeologists have found many toys from this time: little clay whistles shaped like birds, tiny clay pots and dishes for playing house, little carts with wheels, rattles with pebbles inside them.

[long-pause]

The children also play games of pretend.

[pause]

The Gospels mention children sitting in the marketplace, calling to one another: We played the flute for you, and you did not dance. We sang a dirge, and you did not mourn. They are playing at weddings and funerals, copying the grownups, as children have always done.

[pause]

{img:children-pretend-wedding-procession-village-lane}
They pretend to be shepherds, and kings, and soldiers, and farmers. They build little houses of stones in the dust. They make little boats of bark, and float them in the puddles after the rain.

[long-pause]

The boys of the village go to school, at the synagogue.

[pause]

The synagogue is the meeting house of the village, a plain stone building with benches around the walls, where the people gather on the Sabbath, to pray, and to hear the scriptures read and explained. During the week, the teacher, who is called the hazzan, teaches the boys there.

[pause]

The boys sit on the floor, in a circle around the teacher, and learn to read the Hebrew scriptures, from scrolls, and to recite them by heart, chanting the words together, again and again, until they know them. They learn the stories of Abraham and Moses and David, and the laws, and the psalms, and the prophets.

[long-pause]

The girls learn at home, from their mothers and grandmothers.

[pause]

They learn to grind and bake and cook, to spin and weave, to look after children and animals, to care for the sick, and to run a household. They learn the songs and the prayers and the stories of their people, by listening, at home, and in the synagogue on the Sabbath, where the women sit and listen.

[pause]

{img:mother-teaching-daughter-weaving-loom-courtyard}
Some of them learn other skills too: how to use herbs as medicines, how to help a mother give birth, how to make perfumes and ointments.

[long-pause]

The Sabbath is the most special day of the week, for the children as for everyone.

[pause]

It begins at sunset on Friday evening, and lasts until sunset on Saturday. On Friday afternoon, the whole family prepares: the house is cleaned, the food is cooked for the next day, the lamps are filled with oil, everyone washes and puts on clean clothes. Then, as the sun goes down, the mother lights the Sabbath lamp, and says a blessing.

[pause]

For the next day, nobody works. The family rests, and prays, and eats together, and goes to the synagogue, and visits relatives and friends, and walks in the fields. For the children, it is a day of rest and family and quiet joy, every single week.

[long-pause]

In the evenings, the children sit with their families, on the roof, or by the fire, and listen to the stories.

[pause]

The grandparents tell them the old stories of their people, and of their village: of Ruth in the barley fields, and of David the shepherd boy, and of the prophets, and the kings. They tell them about the great festivals in Jerusalem, and about the Temple. They tell them about the Messiah, the king who will come one day, from the family of David, from Bethlehem.

[pause]

{img:grandfather-telling-story-children-roof-evening-stars}
And the children look out from the roof, at the dark hills around the village, and the shepherds' fires, and the stars, and wonder when he will come.

[long-pause]

{img:roman-era-judea-road-soldiers-distant-hills}
Two thousand years ago, the land of Judea is part of the Roman world.

[pause]

The Romans have conquered almost every land around the Mediterranean Sea, from Spain to Syria, from Egypt to Gaul. Their empire is enormous, and powerful, and rich, and at its head is the emperor, Augustus, in the great city of Rome, far away across the sea.

[long-pause]

Augustus is the first Roman emperor.

[pause]

He came to power after many years of terrible civil war, and he has brought peace to the Roman world, for the first time in generations. People call it the Pax Romana, the Roman peace. Roads have been built across the empire, and ships sail safely across the sea, and trade flourishes.

[pause]

Augustus is careful and clever. He likes to know exactly how many people there are in his empire, and how much they own, so that they can be taxed properly. So, from time to time, he orders that a census be taken, a counting of all the people, in one province or another.

[long-pause]

Judea is not ruled directly by the Romans, at this time. It is ruled by a king, who is a friend and ally of Rome: King Herod.

[pause]

Herod is an old man now. He has been king for more than thirty years. He is clever and ambitious and energetic, and he has done great things. But he is also suspicious and cruel, and many of his people do not like him.

[pause]

{img:herod-temple-construction-jerusalem-workers-stones}
He is a great builder. He has built cities and harbours, palaces and fortresses, all over his kingdom. He has built a great new port on the coast, named Caesarea, after the emperor, with a harbour made of huge stone blocks sunk into the sea. He has built fortresses on mountaintops in the desert, like Masada, beside the Dead Sea, and the Herodium, which can be seen from Bethlehem.

[long-pause]

And above all, he is rebuilding the Temple in Jerusalem.

[pause]

The Temple is the holiest place in the world for the Jewish people. It stands on a great hill in Jerusalem, and Herod is making it larger and more magnificent than it has ever been, with vast courtyards and colonnades of marble, and gates covered with gold and silver. Thousands of workers have been labouring on it for years, and it will not be finished for many years more.

[pause]

People say that from a distance, the Temple looks like a mountain covered with snow, because of its white marble, and that when the sun shines on its golden roof, it is too bright to look at.

[long-pause]

The Herodium, the fortress on the hill near Bethlehem, is one of Herod's favourite places.

[pause]

He has built it on a hill that he has made higher by piling up earth, so that it looks like a giant cone with its top cut off. On the top, there is a round palace, with towers and courtyards and gardens. At the foot of the hill, there is a whole town of palaces, with pools and gardens and baths, where Herod and his court can stay.

[pause]

{img:herodium-palace-gardens-pools-foot-of-hill}
Herod has said that he wants to be buried there, when he dies. And from Bethlehem, the people can see it on the horizon, every day.

[long-pause]

The people of Judea live their lives under Herod and the Romans as best they can.

[pause]

They pay their taxes: to the king, and to the Romans, and to the Temple. They go to the synagogue in their villages on the Sabbath, to pray, and to hear the scriptures read. They go up to Jerusalem for the great festivals, three times a year, to pray at the Temple.

[pause]

Many of them hope and pray that one day, God will send them a new king, a king like David, who will free them from the Romans, and from Herod, and bring peace and justice to the land. They call him the Messiah, which means the anointed one.

[long-pause]

Herod knows about these hopes, and he does not like them.

[pause]

He is old, and ill, and suspicious, and he is afraid that someone will take his throne. He has had many of his enemies put to death, and even some of his own family. He listens carefully to every rumour, every whisper, about a new king.

[pause]

{img:old-king-palace-window-looking-out-suspicious}
But in the villages, like Bethlehem, life goes on. The people plough and sow and harvest, and look after their flocks, and bake their bread, and say their prayers, and wait.

[long-pause]

{img:jerusalem-ancient-city-walls-temple-morning-view}
Six miles to the north of Bethlehem, along the ridge road, lies the great city of Jerusalem.

[pause]

For the people of Bethlehem, Jerusalem is the centre of the world. It is the holy city, the city of David, the city of the Temple. They go there to buy and sell at the markets, to work on the building sites, to visit relatives, and above all, three times a year, for the great festivals.

[long-pause]

The walk from Bethlehem to Jerusalem takes about two hours.

[pause]

The road follows the ridge, past olive groves and vineyards and little villages, and the tombs of the ancestors cut into the rocks beside the road. Near Bethlehem, beside the road, there is the tomb of Rachel, the beloved wife of Jacob, who, the old story says, died there long ago, giving birth to her son Benjamin. Travellers stop there to pray.

[pause]

Then, as the road climbs over the last hill, the city appears, spread out across its hills, behind its great walls of golden stone, with its towers and its palaces, and above all, the Temple.

[long-pause]

Jerusalem is a big, busy, crowded city, of perhaps forty or fifty thousand people, or even more.

[pause]

Its streets are narrow and steep and paved with stone, with steps going up and down the hills, and arches over them, and houses built close together, two and three storeys high. There are markets for everything: for grain and fruit and vegetables, for sheep and cattle and doves, for cloth and pottery and spices, for perfume and jewellery.

[pause]

{img:jerusalem-narrow-market-street-stalls-crowds-ancient}
There are workshops of craftsmen: potters and weavers, dyers and tanners, glassblowers and goldsmiths, stonemasons and carpenters. There are money changers, who change foreign coins into the coins that can be used at the Temple. There are inns, and bath houses, and the grand houses of the rich and the priests, with mosaic floors and painted walls.

[long-pause]

And everywhere, there is the sound of building.

[pause]

King Herod is rebuilding the Temple, and thousands of workers are labouring on it: stonemasons cutting the great blocks of white limestone, some of them as big as a house, and hauling them into place with cranes and ropes and rollers, and carpenters and gilders and sculptors, decorating the porticoes and the gates.

[pause]

The Temple stands on an enormous platform, built up on top of the hill, surrounded by great walls. Some of those walls still stand today. One of them, the Western Wall, is still visited and prayed at, every day, by people from all over the world.

[long-pause]

On the great festivals, Jerusalem is crowded with pilgrims.

[pause]

At Passover, in the spring, people come from all over the land, and from every country where Jews live: from Egypt and Syria and Babylon, from Rome and Greece and Asia. The city is full. The pilgrims camp on the hillsides around the walls, in tents and shelters, and the whole valley glows with their fires at night.

[pause]

{img:passover-pilgrims-tents-hillsides-jerusalem-fires-night}
The families of Bethlehem walk up to Jerusalem for the festivals, with their children and their grandparents, singing the old songs of pilgrimage as they go, the Psalms of Ascent: I was glad when they said unto me, let us go into the house of the Lord. Our feet are standing within your gates, O Jerusalem.

[long-pause]

At the Temple, the pilgrims climb up the great stairs, and pass through the gates, into the vast outer courtyard, paved with stone, surrounded by tall colonnades.

[pause]

The courtyard is crowded with people from every nation, speaking every language. There are teachers of the law, sitting in the shade of the colonnades, with their students around them. There are sellers of doves and lambs for the sacrifices. There are Levites, singing psalms, with harps and lyres and trumpets.

[pause]

And in the centre, behind more walls and gates, there is the sanctuary itself, the holiest place, gleaming white and gold, with smoke rising from the great altar in front of it, and the priests in their white linen robes moving about in the courtyard.

[long-pause]

At the end of the festival, the families walk home to Bethlehem, tired and happy, singing.

[pause]

{img:family-walking-home-bethlehem-evening-ridge-road}
And the children talk, all the way, about everything they have seen: the crowds, the Temple, the gold, the music, the priests, the markets, the soldiers, the great walls. And in the evening, at home, the grandparents tell them, once again, that one day, when the Messiah comes, Jerusalem will be the most beautiful city in the whole world, and all the nations will come to it, in peace.

[long-pause]

{img:nazareth-galilee-hillside-village-spring}
Far to the north of Bethlehem, about ninety miles away, in the green hills of Galilee, there is another small village, called Nazareth.

[pause]

It is even smaller than Bethlehem: perhaps a few hundred people, living in simple stone houses, many of them built in front of caves in the soft rock of a hillside, looking down over a wide, fertile valley of fields and orchards.

[long-pause]

Galilee is greener than Judea.

[pause]

There is more rain here, and the soil is richer. The hills are covered with oak trees and terebinths, and the valleys are full of wheat and barley, olive groves and vineyards, fig trees and pomegranates. In the spring, the fields are covered with wild flowers: red anemones, yellow daisies, purple lupins, so many that the hillsides look like a painted carpet.

[pause]

Not far away, to the east, there is the Sea of Galilee, a great freshwater lake, full of fish, with little fishing towns along its shores. And on a clear day, from the hills above Nazareth, you can see, far to the north, the snowy summit of Mount Hermon, white against the blue sky.

[long-pause]

The people of Nazareth are farmers and shepherds and craftsmen.

[pause]

Life here is much the same as in Bethlehem: grinding grain and baking bread in the morning, fetching water from the spring, working in the fields and the olive groves, going to the little synagogue on the Sabbath. Everyone knows everyone else. Many of the families are related to each other.

[pause]

{img:village-spring-women-water-jars-nazareth}
At the bottom of the village, there is a spring, which is the only source of water. Every morning and every evening, the women and the girls of the village come to the spring, with their jars, to fetch water, and to talk.

[long-pause]

Among the young people of Nazareth, there is a girl named Mary.

[pause]

We know very little about her life before the events of our story. She is young, perhaps only thirteen or fourteen years old, which was a common age for a girl to be betrothed in those days. She lives with her family, and helps her mother, grinding and baking and spinning and fetching water, like all the other girls of the village.

[pause]

And she is betrothed to a man named Joseph.

[long-pause]

Joseph is older than Mary, though we do not know by how much.

[pause]

He is a craftsman, a builder, working with wood and stone. The old word for his trade, tekton, means someone who builds things: houses and roofs, doors and furniture, ploughs and yokes for oxen. In a small village like Nazareth, a man like Joseph would do a little of everything.

[pause]

Joseph is a good, quiet, honest man, the old stories say. He is a descendant of King David, from a family that came, long ago, from Bethlehem. His family is not rich. But they are respected.

[long-pause]

In those days, a marriage happened in two stages.

[pause]

First, there was the betrothal, when the two families agreed, and the young couple promised themselves to each other, before witnesses. From that moment, they were considered married. But the bride stayed in her father's house, often for a year or so, while the groom prepared a home for her, perhaps a new room built onto his family's house.

[pause]

{img:betrothal-ceremony-families-courtyard-galilee}
Then, when everything was ready, there was the wedding feast, and the groom came, with his friends, in a joyful procession, at night, with lamps and torches, to take the bride to her new home. And there was a great feast, with music and dancing and wine, which lasted for several days.

[long-pause]

According to the Gospel of Luke, during this time of betrothal, something extraordinary happened to Mary.

[pause]

An angel, Gabriel, came to her, and told her that she would have a son, and that she should name him Jesus, and that he would be called the Son of the Most High, and that he would be given the throne of his ancestor David, and that his kingdom would never end.

[pause]

Mary was troubled, and asked how this could be. And the angel answered her. And Mary said: Here am I, the servant of the Lord. Let it be with me according to your word.

[long-pause]

Some time after this, Mary went to visit her relative, Elizabeth, who lived in a village in the hills of Judea, not far from Jerusalem, and who was also expecting a child, in her old age.

[pause]

She stayed with Elizabeth for about three months. And then she came home to Nazareth.

[pause]

{img:joseph-mary-simple-home-evening-lamp-galilee}
And the Gospel of Matthew tells how Joseph, in a dream, was told by an angel not to be afraid to take Mary as his wife. So Joseph took Mary into his home, and they lived together in Nazareth, quietly, in his house, as they waited for the child to be born.

[long-pause]

{img:galilee-village-wedding-preparations-courtyard}
In the villages of Galilee and Judea, two thousand years ago, a wedding is the greatest celebration of all.

[pause]

The whole village takes part, and the feast lasts for a whole week. Everyone looks forward to it for months.

[long-pause]

For weeks before the wedding, the families prepare.

[pause]

The groom's family builds a new room onto their house, or prepares a room, where the young couple will live. The women of both families bake and cook, and grind flour, and prepare great quantities of food: bread and cakes, roasted lamb and goat, lentils and beans, olives and cheese, figs and dates and raisins and almonds and honey. And they bring out jars and jars of wine.

[pause]

The bride's family prepares her clothes and her jewellery. She will wear her finest dress, embroidered with bright colours, and a veil, and a headdress decorated with coins, which are part of her dowry, and jewellery of silver and gold, if the family can afford it.

[long-pause]

On the evening of the wedding, the bride waits at her father's house, with her friends, the young women of the village, who are her bridesmaids.

[pause]

They wait for the groom to come. They do not know exactly when he will come, so they must be ready, with their oil lamps filled and burning. Sometimes they wait for hours, late into the night, talking and singing and laughing, and the little children fall asleep in their laps.

[pause]

{img:bridesmaids-waiting-oil-lamps-night-courtyard}
Then, at last, someone calls out: Here is the bridegroom! Come out to meet him!

[long-pause]

The groom comes through the dark lanes of the village, with his friends, in a joyful procession, with music and torches and lamps, singing and shouting.

[pause]

The bridesmaids hurry out to meet him, with their lamps lit, and the bride comes out, veiled, and the groom takes her by the hand, and the whole procession sets off together, through the village, to the groom's house, with the drums beating, and the flutes playing, and everyone singing and clapping, and the neighbours coming out of their doors to join in.

[pause]

The children run ahead, shouting. The old people stand at their doors, and bless the young couple as they pass. The whole village glows with the light of the lamps and the torches, in the darkness.

[long-pause]

At the groom's house, the feast begins.

[pause]

The guests sit on mats and cushions, in the courtyard and on the roof, around great bowls of food, and the wine is poured, and the musicians play. There are blessings and prayers, and speeches, and toasts to the young couple. There is dancing: the men dance in a circle, holding hands, and the women dance and sing, and clap and ululate, a high, trilling cry of joy.

[pause]

{img:wedding-feast-guests-courtyard-dancing-circle-lamps}
The feast goes on, day after day, for a whole week. Guests come and go. Relatives arrive from other villages. The food and the wine flow. It is the happiest week in the life of a village.

[long-pause]

The Gospel of John tells about a wedding like this, in a village called Cana, not far from Nazareth, many years later, when Jesus was a grown man.

[pause]

He was there with his mother, Mary, and his friends, as guests. And in the middle of the feast, the wine ran out, which was a terrible embarrassment for the family. And Mary came to her son, and told him: They have no wine.

[pause]

And the story says that Jesus told the servants to fill the six great stone water jars, which stood in the courtyard, with water, and to take some to the steward of the feast. And when the steward tasted it, it was wine, and better than the wine they had served at the beginning.

[long-pause]

But in the time of our story, Mary and Joseph's wedding is still in the future, or has only just taken place.

[pause]

We do not know whether there was a great feast in Nazareth, with a procession of lamps through the dark lanes, and music and dancing for a week. Perhaps there was. Perhaps it was quiet and simple.

[pause]

{img:young-couple-new-room-simple-home-nazareth-lamp}
What we know, from the Gospels, is that Joseph took Mary into his home. And that, a few months later, they set out together, on the long road south, to Bethlehem.

[long-pause]

{img:roman-official-announcing-census-village-square}
According to the Gospel of Luke, in those days, a decree went out from the emperor Augustus, that all the world should be registered.

[pause]

Everyone, Luke says, went to be registered, each to his own town. And so Joseph, who was of the family and the house of David, had to go from Nazareth in Galilee to Bethlehem in Judea, the town of David, to be registered, with Mary, who was expecting a child.

[long-pause]

Historians still discuss exactly which census this was, and when it happened. The Romans did take censuses in their provinces, to count the people and their property, for taxes, and people sometimes had to go to the places where their families came from, or where they owned land.

[pause]

But whatever the exact details, the story tells us that Joseph and Mary set out from Nazareth, on the long journey south, to Bethlehem.

[long-pause]

It is a journey of about ninety miles.

[pause]

On foot, it takes about a week, perhaps a little more, for someone who cannot walk very fast. And Mary is expecting her baby very soon.

[pause]

They take what they need for the journey: some bread, and dried figs and dates and cheese, and water skins, and olive oil, and warm cloaks and blankets for the cold nights, and swaddling cloths for the baby, in case it comes. Perhaps they have a donkey, to carry their things, and for Mary to ride on when she is tired. The Gospels do not say. But many people travelled that way, and the old pictures always show a donkey.

[long-pause]

There are two main ways to go south from Galilee to Judea.

[pause]

One goes straight south, through the hills of Samaria. It is the shortest way. But the Samaritans and the Jews do not get on well, and many Jewish travellers prefer not to go through Samaria.

[pause]

{img:jordan-valley-road-palms-travellers-river}
The other way goes east, down into the deep valley of the river Jordan, and then south along the river, through the warm, green valley, past palm trees and reed beds and little villages, to the old city of Jericho, with its springs and its date palms and its balsam gardens. And then, from Jericho, it climbs up, steeply, for miles, through the bare brown desert hills, to Jerusalem, and then on to Bethlehem.

[long-pause]

On the road, they are not alone.

[pause]

There are many other travellers: farmers going to market, merchants with caravans of camels and donkeys, pilgrims going up to Jerusalem, soldiers marching, families visiting relatives. People travel together when they can, in little groups, for company and for safety, because there are sometimes robbers in the lonely places.

[pause]

{img:travellers-group-resting-shade-roadside-well}
In the heat of the day, the travellers rest in the shade of a tree or a rock. In the evening, they stop at a village, or at a caravanserai: a simple inn, with a courtyard for the animals, and rooms around it for the travellers, or simply a sheltered place to sleep.

[long-pause]

At night, it is cold, in the winter, and Joseph and Mary wrap themselves in their cloaks and blankets.

[pause]

They sleep where they can: in the corner of a courtyard, in a cave, in the house of a kind family in a village who takes them in. Joseph makes a little fire, and they eat their bread and figs and cheese, and drink their water, and look up at the stars, which are very bright over the hills and the desert.

[pause]

{img:small-campfire-night-desert-hills-stars-couple-cloaks}
They talk, quietly, about the baby that is coming, and about the strange things that the angel has said, and wonder what it all means.

[long-pause]

Every day, they walk on, slowly, mile after mile.

[pause]

Mary is tired, and the road is hard, and long. But she is young and strong, and Joseph is careful and patient, and they take their time. They stop often to rest. Kind strangers on the road offer them water, and bread, and help.

[pause]

The climb from Jericho to Jerusalem is the hardest part of all. The road winds up and up, through bare, rocky hills, with no trees and no water, for a whole day. But at last, at the top of the climb, they come over the Mount of Olives, and see Jerusalem in front of them, across the valley, with the great white and gold Temple shining in the evening sun.

[long-pause]

They do not stay in Jerusalem.

[pause]

They walk on, south, along the ridge road, past the olive groves and the terraces and the vineyards, for the last few miles, as the sun goes down.

[pause]

{img:road-ridge-bethlehem-ahead-sunset-tired-travellers}
And then, as the evening light fades over the hills, and the first stars come out, they see the little village of Bethlehem ahead of them, on its ridge, with the lamps glowing in the doorways, and the smoke rising from the rooftops, and the shepherds' fires flickering on the hillsides around it.

[long-pause]

{img:caravanserai-courtyard-camels-donkeys-evening}
On the long road from Galilee to Judea, travellers stop for the night wherever they can.

[pause]

In the villages, they ask for hospitality, and are usually taken in by a family, and given a place to sleep, in the guest room, or on the roof, or in the courtyard. Along the main roads, there are also simple inns, called caravanserais, or in the Greek of the time, pandocheion, a place that receives everyone.

[long-pause]

A caravanserai is a large, square courtyard, surrounded by high stone walls, with a single great gate, which is closed at night, for safety.

[pause]

Inside, around the walls, there are simple rooms, or open arches, where the travellers can sleep, on mats on the floor. In the middle of the courtyard, there is a well, or a cistern, and troughs for the animals. The camels and the donkeys and the horses are tied up in the courtyard, or in stables along one side.

[pause]

The innkeeper sells the travellers food and wine, and fodder for their animals, and charges them a few small coins for the night. It is not comfortable. It is noisy, and crowded, and smells of animals and smoke. But it is safe, and there is water, and company.

[long-pause]

In the evening, the courtyard is a busy, colourful place.

[pause]

There are merchants with their caravans of camels, loaded with goods from faraway places: spices and incense from Arabia, cloth from Damascus, glass from Sidon, salt from the Dead Sea, dates from Jericho, dried fish from Galilee. There are pilgrims going to Jerusalem. There are farmers going to market, with donkeys loaded with sacks of grain. There are Roman soldiers, and messengers, and tax collectors, and travelling craftsmen, and wandering teachers.

[pause]

{img:merchants-unloading-spice-sacks-camels-courtyard}
They come from many places, and speak many languages: Aramaic and Hebrew, Greek and Latin, Arabic and Persian.

[long-pause]

As the night falls, the travellers gather around small fires in the courtyard, and cook their suppers, and talk.

[pause]

They share news and stories from the places they have been. A merchant tells about the great city of Damascus, with its gardens and its rivers. A pilgrim from Egypt tells about Alexandria, with its lighthouse that can be seen for miles out to sea, and its great library. A soldier tells about Rome, and the emperor, and the games in the arena.

[pause]

{img:travellers-around-fire-courtyard-telling-stories}
Someone plays a lyre, and sings an old song. Someone else tells a funny story, and everyone laughs. The children of the travelling families play among the animals, and are scolded, and fall asleep on their mothers' laps.

[long-pause]

On a winter night, in the Jordan valley, it is mild and warm, because the valley is far below the level of the sea, and the air is soft.

[pause]

Down here, at Jericho, one of the oldest cities in the world, there are palm trees, and springs, and gardens of balsam and dates and flowers, even in the winter, when the hills above are cold and wet. King Herod has a winter palace here, beside the river, with pools and gardens, where he comes to escape the cold of Jerusalem.

[pause]

The travellers sleep well in Jericho, in the warm night air, under the palm trees, before the long climb up to Jerusalem in the morning.

[long-pause]

As the fires burn down, the courtyard grows quiet.

[pause]

The travellers roll themselves in their cloaks and blankets, and lie down on their mats, under the arches, or out in the courtyard, under the stars. The camels kneel in the dust, and chew, and grumble softly. The donkeys doze, with their heads down. The innkeeper closes the great gate, and bars it.

[pause]

{img:quiet-inn-courtyard-night-camels-kneeling-stars}
High above, the stars shine over the courtyard, and over the dark valley, and the hills, very bright and very many. And somewhere among the sleeping travellers, perhaps, a young couple from Nazareth lie wrapped in their cloaks, side by side, resting, on their way to Bethlehem.

[long-pause]

{img:crowded-village-lane-evening-travellers-lamps}
When Joseph and Mary arrive in Bethlehem, the village is crowded.

[pause]

Many other people have come for the registration too, people whose families come from Bethlehem, who have travelled from all over the land. Every house is full. Every guest room is taken. Relatives are staying with relatives, and the courtyards and the roofs are full of people sleeping wrapped in their cloaks.

[long-pause]

The Gospel of Luke says simply that there was no place for them in the kataluma.

[pause]

For a long time, people have translated this word as the inn, and imagined a busy inn, with an unkind innkeeper turning them away from the door. But the word can also mean a guest room, the spare room of a family house, where visitors stay.

[pause]

So perhaps Joseph and Mary went to the house of some of Joseph's relatives, as travellers often did, and found that the guest room was already full of other visitors. And so the family made room for them in the only place that was left: down in the lower part of the house, where the animals were kept, or in the cave at the back, where it was quiet and warm and sheltered.

[long-pause]

An old tradition, which goes back almost to the beginning, says that it was a cave.

[pause]

As early as the second century, about a hundred and fifty years later, a Christian writer named Justin, who grew up not far away, wrote that the child was born in a cave near the village. And the people of Bethlehem pointed out the cave to visitors, and remembered it, generation after generation.

[pause]

{img:limestone-cave-stable-straw-manger-oil-lamp}
Wherever it was exactly, it was a humble place, with straw on the floor, and the warm smell of the animals, and a manger, a stone or wooden feeding trough, full of hay.

[long-pause]

The family of the house does what it can for them.

[pause]

The women bring water, and clean straw, and blankets, and a lamp, and some bread and soup. They bring a pot of olive oil, and warm water, and clean cloths. They make a bed of straw and blankets for Mary, in the warmest corner.

[pause]

{img:village-women-bringing-water-blankets-lamp-cave}
The animals shift and stamp a little, in the dim light, and look at the newcomers with their large, mild eyes, and go back to chewing their hay. The donkey is tied up beside them. It is quiet, and warm, and peaceful, after the long journey.

[long-pause]

Outside, the village is settling down for the night.

[pause]

The lamps go out one by one. The voices in the courtyards fall silent. The dogs curl up by the doors. The cold, clear winter night comes down over the hills, and the stars come out, thousands and thousands of them, so bright and so many that they seem close enough to touch.

[pause]

On the hillsides around the village, the shepherds' fires glow red, here and there, in the darkness. Far to the southeast, on its hill, the dark shape of the Herodium stands against the stars. And to the north, along the ridge, a faint glow in the sky shows where Jerusalem lies.

[long-pause]

Inside the stable, Joseph sits by the door, keeping watch, as the night goes on.

[pause]

Mary rests on her bed of straw. The little oil lamp flickers in its niche in the rock. The animals breathe softly in the darkness.

[pause]

{img:man-sitting-by-doorway-watching-night-stars}
And some time in the night, in that quiet place, among the animals, Mary's child is born.

[long-pause]

{img:mother-holding-newborn-straw-lamplight-gentle}
The Gospel of Luke tells it in a single, simple sentence.

[pause]

And she gave birth to her firstborn son, and wrapped him in swaddling cloths, and laid him in a manger, because there was no place for them in the guest room.

[long-pause]

In those days, when a baby was born, his mother would wash him in warm water, and rub him gently with a little salt and olive oil, to keep his skin soft and healthy.

[pause]

Then she would wrap him tightly in long strips of linen cloth, called swaddling cloths, around and around, from his shoulders to his feet, with his arms against his sides, so that he would feel safe and warm and snug, as he had felt before he was born, and would sleep peacefully.

[pause]

Mothers all over this part of the world still swaddled their babies in this way, until not very long ago.

[long-pause]

And then Mary laid her baby in the manger.

[pause]

The manger was the feeding trough of the animals, filled with soft, clean hay. It was the safest and warmest place there was, raised up off the cold floor, where the baby could sleep, wrapped in his swaddling cloths, beside his mother, while she rested.

[pause]

{img:baby-sleeping-manger-hay-soft-light-animals-near}
It was not a palace. It was not a fine house. It was a humble place, of straw and stone and animals. But it was warm, and it was quiet, and the baby slept.

[long-pause]

They named him Jesus.

[pause]

It was a common name in those days. In the language of the people, Aramaic, it was Yeshua, and in Hebrew, Yehoshua, the same name as Joshua, the great leader of long ago. It means God saves.

[pause]

Mary looked at her son, sleeping in the manger, by the light of the little oil lamp. And Joseph looked at them both. And they thought about everything that the angel had said, and wondered.

[long-pause]

Outside, the night was very still.

[pause]

The stars shone over the hills. The village slept. The shepherds sat by their fires on the hillsides. The animals in the stable breathed softly in the darkness, and shifted in the straw.

[pause]

{img:quiet-stable-night-ox-donkey-lamp-glow-door}
An old tradition, which comes from a prophecy, says that there was an ox and a donkey in the stable, beside the manger, warming the baby with their breath. The Gospels do not mention them. But they have been part of the story for more than fifteen hundred years, in paintings and carvings and carols, and in the nativity scenes that families set up in their homes at Christmas, all over the world.

[long-pause]

In the morning, the women of the house came down to see the baby.

[pause]

They brought warm water and bread and milk and honey for Mary, and fresh straw, and more cloths. They held the baby, and admired him, and said what a fine boy he was, and how like his father, as women always do, and gave Mary advice, and fussed over her, and made her rest.

[pause]

The children of the house crept in, shyly, to look at the new baby, and stroke his little hand with one finger. And the news went round the courtyard, and the neighbours came to see.

[long-pause]

To the people of Bethlehem, it was an ordinary event.

[pause]

A young couple from Galilee, who had come for the registration, had had a baby, in the stable of a house in the village. Babies were born every day, all over the land. It was a happy thing, and the women were glad for Mary, and that was all.

[pause]

{img:ordinary-village-morning-smoke-courtyards-sun}
Nobody in Bethlehem that morning, except perhaps Mary and Joseph, had any idea that, two thousand years later, people all over the world would be singing songs about that night, and that little town, and that baby in the manger.

[pause]

But that night, the Gospel of Luke says, out in the fields around Bethlehem, something else had happened.

[long-pause]

{img:shepherds-night-fire-fold-hills-stars-quiet}
In the same region, the Gospel of Luke says, there were shepherds out in the fields, keeping watch over their flock by night.

[pause]

They were the shepherds of Bethlehem, ordinary men and boys, sitting around their little fire beside the sheepfold, on a cold winter night, wrapped in their rough cloaks, talking quietly, or dozing, or looking up at the stars, as they did every night.

[long-pause]

The sheep were asleep in the fold, lying close together for warmth, their fleeces pale in the darkness. The dogs lay by the fire. Somewhere, far away, a jackal called. The fire crackled, and a few sparks rose up into the black sky.

[pause]

It was a night like any other.

[long-pause]

And then, the story says, an angel of the Lord stood before them, and the glory of the Lord shone around them, and they were terrified.

[pause]

But the angel said to them: Do not be afraid. For see, I am bringing you good news of great joy, for all the people. To you is born this day, in the city of David, a Saviour, who is the Messiah, the Lord. This will be a sign for you: you will find a child, wrapped in swaddling cloths, and lying in a manger.

[pause]

{img:soft-golden-light-over-hills-shepherds-looking-up}
And suddenly, Luke says, there was with the angel a multitude of the heavenly host, praising God, and saying: Glory to God in the highest heaven, and on earth peace, among those whom he favours.

[long-pause]

Then, as suddenly as it had come, the light faded, and the angels were gone, and the night was dark and quiet again, with only the stars, and the little fire, and the sleeping sheep.

[pause]

The shepherds looked at each other.

[pause]

Then they said: Let us go now to Bethlehem, and see this thing that has taken place, which the Lord has made known to us.

[long-pause]

So they left their flock, perhaps with one or two of the boys to watch it, and went, in a hurry, through the darkness, along the rocky paths, up the hill to the village.

[pause]

It was not hard for them to find what they were looking for. Bethlehem was a small place, and they knew every house in it. And a sign like that, a newborn baby lying in a manger, in a stable, would be easy to find. Perhaps they saw the light of a lamp still burning in the doorway of the stable, late in the night.

[pause]

They found Mary and Joseph, and the child lying in the manger, just as the angel had said.

[long-pause]

We can imagine them, standing in the doorway of the stable, rough men in their sheepskin cloaks, with their staffs in their hands, smelling of sheep and smoke, shy and awkward, with their caps in their hands.

[pause]

They came in quietly, and looked at the baby, sleeping in the hay, in his swaddling cloths. And they told Mary and Joseph what they had seen and heard, out in the fields: the light, and the angel, and the singing.

[pause]

{img:rough-shepherds-kneeling-by-manger-lamplight-awe}
Mary listened, and said nothing. But Luke says that she treasured all these words, and pondered them in her heart.

[long-pause]

Then the shepherds went back to their flocks, in the fields, in the darkness, glorifying and praising God for all they had heard and seen.

[pause]

And as they went, Luke says, they told everyone what had been told to them about this child. And all who heard it were amazed.

[pause]

By the time they got back to the sheepfold, the sky over the eastern hills was beginning to grow pale, and the morning star was shining, low and bright, over the desert. The sheep were waking up, and bleating, and the boys who had stayed behind were waiting, wanting to know everything.

[long-pause]

It is a strange and beautiful part of the story, that the first people to hear the news were shepherds.

[pause]

Not kings, or priests, or rich men, or the great people of Jerusalem. But poor men, working through the cold night in the fields, ordinary, humble, overlooked people, keeping watch over their sheep, as shepherds had done on those same hills for a thousand years, since the time of David.

[pause]

{img:shepherds-field-dawn-beit-sahour-olive-trees}
And ever since, at Christmas, all over the world, people have remembered those shepherds, and sung about them, and made little figures of them, with their sheep on their shoulders, to put beside the manger in the nativity scene.

[pause]

While shepherds watched their flocks by night, all seated on the ground, the angel of the Lord came down, and glory shone around.

[long-pause]

{img:young-family-walking-to-jerusalem-temple-morning}
After the birth, Joseph and Mary stayed in Bethlehem for a while.

[pause]

The baby was small, and Mary needed to rest. Perhaps the family whose stable they had used found them a better place in the house, once the other guests had gone home. Perhaps Joseph found work in the village, or in Jerusalem, building and mending, as he did at home.

[long-pause]

On the eighth day after his birth, according to the law and the custom of their people, the baby was circumcised, and given his name, in a little ceremony, with prayers and blessings, in the presence of the family and the neighbours.

[pause]

They named him Jesus, as the angel had said.

[pause]

And then, Luke says, when forty days had passed, it was time to take him to the Temple in Jerusalem, to present him to the Lord, as the law said that every firstborn son should be, and for Mary to make the offering of purification, after the birth of her child.

[long-pause]

So one morning, about six weeks after the birth, Joseph and Mary walked the six miles along the ridge road from Bethlehem to Jerusalem, carrying the baby, wrapped in his blankets.

[pause]

The offering for a family that could afford it was a lamb. But for a poor family, the law allowed two turtledoves, or two young pigeons. Joseph and Mary, Luke says, brought two birds. They were not a rich family.

[pause]

{img:temple-court-young-couple-doves-cage-crowds}
They climbed the great stairs, and went in through the gates, into the vast courtyard of the Temple, crowded with people, and found a priest, and made their offering.

[long-pause]

In the Temple, that day, there was an old man named Simeon.

[pause]

He was a good and devout man, Luke says, who lived in Jerusalem, and who was waiting and hoping for the consolation of Israel, for the coming of the Messiah. And it had been revealed to him, the story says, that he would not die until he had seen the Lord's Messiah.

[pause]

That day, something told him to go to the Temple. And when he saw Joseph and Mary, with the baby, he came up to them, and asked to hold the child. And Mary gave him the baby.

[long-pause]

Simeon took the child in his arms, and looked at him, and praised God, and said some words that have been sung in churches every evening, for many centuries, at the end of the day.

[pause]

Now, Lord, you are letting your servant depart in peace, according to your word. For my eyes have seen your salvation, which you have prepared in the presence of all peoples: a light for revelation to the nations, and for glory to your people Israel.

[pause]

Joseph and Mary were amazed at what he said. And Simeon blessed them, and gave the baby back to his mother, gently.

[long-pause]

There was also an old woman in the Temple that day, a prophetess named Anna.

[pause]

She was very old, Luke says: she had been married for seven years when she was young, and then had been a widow for many, many years, and now she was eighty-four years old. She never left the Temple, but worshipped there, day and night, with fasting and prayer.

[pause]

{img:very-old-woman-prophetess-temple-steps-sunlight}
She came up to them at that moment, and began to praise God, and to speak about the child to everyone who was waiting for the redemption of Jerusalem.

[long-pause]

It is a quiet, gentle scene, among the great crowds of the Temple: a young mother and father from Galilee, poor, with their two little birds, and their baby; and two very old people, who had waited all their lives, holding the baby in their arms, and thanking God, and then saying goodbye.

[pause]

Then Joseph and Mary walked back out through the gates, and down the great stairs, into the busy streets of the city, and out through the gate, onto the ridge road, and walked home to Bethlehem in the evening light, with the baby asleep in Mary's arms, and the old man's words in their hearts.

[long-pause]

In many churches, all over the world, this day is still remembered, on the second of February, forty days after Christmas.

[pause]

It is called Candlemas, because on that day, candles are blessed, and carried in procession, in memory of the old man's words, about a light for all the nations. In many places, it is the day when the Christmas decorations are finally taken down, and the nativity scene is put away for another year.

[pause]

{img:candle-procession-old-church-february-evening}
And every evening, in monasteries and cathedrals and little churches, as the light fades, the words of old Simeon are still sung, quietly: Now, Lord, you are letting your servant depart in peace.

[long-pause]

{img:magi-camels-desert-night-bright-star-distant}
The Gospel of Matthew tells another part of the story.

[pause]

It says that, after the child was born, some wise men from the East came to Jerusalem, asking: Where is the child who has been born king of the Jews? For we observed his star at its rising, and have come to pay him homage.

[long-pause]

Matthew calls them magi.

[pause]

The magi were scholars and priests from Persia, or Babylon, or Arabia, far to the east, who studied the stars and the planets, and believed that they could read in them the signs of great events on earth. They were respected and learned men, who advised kings.

[pause]

Matthew does not say how many there were. Because they brought three gifts, people have imagined that there were three of them. Later, people began to call them kings, and gave them names: Caspar, Melchior and Balthasar. But Matthew calls them simply wise men, from the East.

[long-pause]

People have wondered for a very long time what the star could have been.

[pause]

Some have thought that it was a comet, or a new star that suddenly appeared, very bright, in the sky. Some have thought that it was a meeting of two bright planets, Jupiter and Saturn, which came very close together in the sky three times in the year 7 before Christ, in a part of the sky that astronomers of the time connected with the land of the Jews. Others think it was a miracle, which cannot be explained.

[pause]

{img:bright-star-over-desert-horizon-camels-silhouette}
Nobody knows for sure. But the magi saw something in the sky, Matthew says, which told them that a great king had been born, and they set out on a long journey, across the deserts, to find him.

[long-pause]

The journey would have taken them many weeks, perhaps months.

[pause]

They would have travelled with a caravan of camels and servants, across the great deserts and the river valleys, following the old trade roads, stopping at the oases and the caravan cities, sleeping in tents under the stars, and watching the sky at night.

[pause]

When at last they reached Jerusalem, they went to the palace of King Herod, and asked where the new king had been born. And Herod, Matthew says, was frightened, and all Jerusalem with him.

[long-pause]

Herod called together the chief priests and the scholars, and asked them where the Messiah was to be born.

[pause]

They told him: In Bethlehem of Judea. For so it has been written by the prophet: And you, Bethlehem, in the land of Judah, are by no means least among the rulers of Judah. For from you shall come a ruler, who is to shepherd my people Israel.

[pause]

So Herod sent the wise men to Bethlehem, and told them to search carefully for the child, and to come back and tell him where he was, so that he, too, could go and pay him homage. But in his heart, he had no such intention.

[long-pause]

The wise men set out for Bethlehem, along the ridge road, in the evening.

[pause]

And there, Matthew says, ahead of them, the star that they had seen at its rising went before them, until it stopped over the place where the child was. And when they saw the star, they were overwhelmed with joy.

[pause]

{img:magi-arriving-small-village-house-star-above}
They came to the house, and saw the child with Mary, his mother. And they knelt down, and paid him homage. Then, opening their treasure chests, they offered him gifts: gold, and frankincense, and myrrh.

[long-pause]

The gifts were precious, and full of meaning.

[pause]

Gold was the gift for a king. Frankincense was a sweet-smelling resin, from trees that grow in southern Arabia, burned as incense in temples, and was a gift for God. Myrrh was another precious resin, from the same lands, used as a perfume and a medicine, and for preparing the bodies of the dead for burial.

[pause]

To the people of Bethlehem, it must have seemed very strange: these grand, richly dressed foreigners, with their camels and their servants, kneeling in a humble village house, before a little baby, and his young mother, and offering him treasures.

[long-pause]

Then, Matthew says, having been warned in a dream not to return to Herod, they left for their own country by another road.

[pause]

{img:caravan-leaving-at-dawn-different-road-hills}
And soon after, Joseph too was warned in a dream to take the child and his mother, and to go to Egypt, where they would be safe, until King Herod died. So they set out quietly, by night, on the road to the south, and lived for a while in Egypt, where there were many Jewish communities, in the cities along the Nile.

[pause]

After Herod's death, they came home, and went to live, once more, in the little village of Nazareth, in Galilee. And there, Luke says, the child grew, and became strong, and was filled with wisdom.

[long-pause]

{img:nazareth-boy-helping-father-workshop-sawdust}
After their return from Egypt, Joseph and Mary lived in Nazareth, in Galilee, with their son.

[pause]

The Gospels tell us very little about the years when Jesus was growing up. But we can imagine what his life was like, because we know how the children of Galilee lived in those days.

[long-pause]

He would have grown up in a simple stone house, perhaps built against a cave in the hillside, with a courtyard shared with other families, and an oven, and a cistern, and a fig tree.

[pause]

He would have woken every morning to the sound of his mother grinding the grain, and the smell of bread baking. He would have helped to fetch water from the spring at the bottom of the village, and to feed the animals, and to gather firewood on the hills.

[pause]

He would have played with the other children of the village, in the lanes and the fields and among the olive trees, and climbed the hills, and looked out over the wide valley below, and watched the clouds and the birds and the flowers.

[long-pause]

He would have learned to read the scriptures at the synagogue school, sitting on the floor with the other boys, chanting the words of the Law and the Prophets and the Psalms, until he knew them by heart.

[pause]

And he would have learned his father's trade, in Joseph's workshop: how to choose wood, and how to cut and shape it, with an axe and a saw and an adze, how to make a door, or a table, or a yoke for oxen, or a plough. How to lay stones, and build a wall, and put a roof on a house.

[pause]

{img:father-teaching-son-carpentry-adze-yoke}
Later in his life, when he told stories, he often spoke about the things he had seen as a boy: sowers scattering seed in the fields, and the seed falling on rocky ground and good ground. A woman sweeping her house by lamplight, looking for a lost coin. A shepherd going out to look for one lost sheep. Bread rising, with a little yeast. Wineskins, new and old. Children playing in the marketplace. The lilies of the field, and the birds of the air.

[long-pause]

Every year, Luke says, the family went up to Jerusalem for the feast of Passover.

[pause]

They walked the long road south, about a week's journey, with other families from Nazareth and the villages around, in a great group of relatives and friends, singing the pilgrim songs as they went.

[pause]

And when Jesus was twelve years old, the Gospel of Luke tells, something happened on one of those journeys.

[long-pause]

When the festival was over, the families set off home to Nazareth.

[pause]

But Jesus stayed behind in Jerusalem, without telling his parents. Mary and Joseph thought he was somewhere in the group, with his cousins and his friends. Only in the evening, when they stopped for the night, did they realize that he was not there.

[pause]

{img:worried-parents-searching-crowded-jerusalem-streets}
They went back to Jerusalem, very worried, and searched for him for three days. And at last, they found him in the Temple, sitting among the teachers, in the shade of the colonnades, listening to them, and asking them questions. And everyone who heard him was amazed at his understanding and his answers.

[long-pause]

His mother said to him: Child, why have you treated us like this? Your father and I have been searching for you anxiously.

[pause]

And he said to them: Why were you searching for me? Did you not know that I must be in my Father's house?

[pause]

They did not understand what he meant. But he went home with them, to Nazareth, and was obedient to them. And his mother, Luke says, treasured all these things in her heart.

[long-pause]

And Jesus grew, Luke says, in wisdom, and in years, and in favour with God and with people.

[pause]

For nearly twenty more years, he lived quietly in Nazareth, working as a builder, like his father, in the little village among the hills of Galilee. The people of Nazareth knew him as Jesus, the son of Joseph and Mary, the carpenter.

[pause]

{img:nazareth-evening-hills-valley-lamps-quiet}
And then, when he was about thirty years old, he left Nazareth, and began to teach. But that is another story, for another night.

[long-pause]

{img:early-christians-gathering-lamplit-room-night}
For a long time after those events, nobody celebrated Christmas.

[pause]

The first Christians, in the years after Jesus lived, did not celebrate birthdays at all, as the Romans did. They celebrated Easter, and every Sunday, and they remembered the days on which the martyrs had died. The birth of Jesus was remembered, and the story was told, and written down in the Gospels. But it was not yet a feast.

[long-pause]

But the people of Bethlehem remembered the cave.

[pause]

All through the first and second and third centuries, Christians who came to the Holy Land, from all over the Roman world, visited Bethlehem, and asked to see the place where Jesus had been born. And the people of Bethlehem showed them a cave, on the edge of the village, and said: here it is.

[pause]

In the third century, a scholar named Origen, who knew the Holy Land well, wrote that the cave was still shown there, and the manger, and that it was well known in the area, even among people who were not Christians.

[long-pause]

Then, in the fourth century, everything changed.

[pause]

The Roman emperor Constantine became a Christian, and gave Christians freedom to worship openly, after centuries of persecution. And his mother, Helena, an elderly and devout woman, travelled to the Holy Land, around the year 326, to visit the places where Jesus had lived.

[pause]

{img:elderly-empress-pilgrim-holy-land-entourage}
At Bethlehem, over the cave, Constantine and Helena built a great church: the Church of the Nativity. It was a large, beautiful building, with rows of columns of red stone, and floors of coloured mosaics, and at the eastern end, an eight-sided structure built right over the cave, with an opening in the middle, so that pilgrims could look down into the grotto where Jesus was born.

[long-pause]

At about the same time, in Rome, Christians began to celebrate the birth of Jesus on the twenty-fifth of December.

[pause]

The earliest record of this is from the year 336, in a Roman calendar. Nobody knows exactly why that day was chosen. Some think it was because it was close to the winter solstice, when the days begin to grow longer again, and the light returns. Some think it was calculated from the date of Easter. Whatever the reason, the feast spread, slowly, all over the Christian world.

[pause]

In the eastern churches, many celebrated it on the sixth of January, which is still the day of the Epiphany, the feast of the visit of the wise men. And some churches still celebrate Christmas in January today.

[long-pause]

About two hundred years later, in the sixth century, the Church of the Nativity was rebuilt, larger and grander, by another emperor, Justinian.

[pause]

That is the church that still stands in Bethlehem today. It is one of the oldest churches in the world still in use.

[pause]

{img:ancient-church-nativity-stone-facade-courtyard}
There is a story that, in the year 614, when a Persian army invaded the Holy Land and destroyed many churches, the soldiers came to Bethlehem, and saw, on the front of the Church of the Nativity, a mosaic of the three wise men, dressed in Persian clothes, with Persian hats. And they recognized their own people, and spared the church.

[long-pause]

Over the centuries, Christmas became one of the greatest feasts of the year, all over Europe.

[pause]

People went to church at midnight, and decorated their houses and churches with greenery, and lit candles and fires against the darkness of winter, and feasted, and sang, and gave gifts, and remembered the poor.

[pause]

{img:medieval-village-christmas-church-candles-snow}
And slowly, people began to make the story visible, so that everyone could see it, and understand it.

[long-pause]

In the year 1223, an Italian friar, Francis of Assisi, had an idea.

[pause]

He was in a little village called Greccio, in the hills of central Italy, at Christmas. And he wanted the people of the village to see, with their own eyes, how humbly Jesus had been born. So he asked a friend to prepare a cave near the village, with a manger full of hay, and a real ox and a real donkey.

[pause]

On Christmas Eve, the people of Greccio came, at night, with candles and torches, singing, through the woods, to the cave. And there, beside the manger, with the ox and the donkey, a priest said the midnight Mass, and Francis preached to the people about the child of Bethlehem, so tenderly, the old stories say, that everyone wept.

[pause]

It was the first nativity scene. And ever since, people all over the world have made nativity scenes at Christmas, in churches and in squares and in their homes, with little figures of Mary and Joseph, and the baby in the manger, and the shepherds with their sheep, and the wise men with their gifts, and the ox and the donkey.

[long-pause]

And people began to write songs about that night in Bethlehem.

[pause]

There were Latin hymns, in the monasteries, and then carols in every language, sung in the churches and the streets and the houses: Silent Night, and O Come All Ye Faithful, and Away in a Manger, and The First Noel. One of the best loved, especially in America and England, was written in the year 1868 by a minister from Philadelphia named Phillips Brooks, after he had visited the Holy Land, and ridden on horseback from Jerusalem to Bethlehem on Christmas Eve, and looked down on the little town from the hills where the shepherds had watched.

[pause]

O little town of Bethlehem, how still we see thee lie. Above thy deep and dreamless sleep, the silent stars go by.

[long-pause]

Bethlehem is still there today, on its ridge in the hills, six miles south of Jerusalem.

[pause]

It is no longer a little village. It has grown into a busy town of about thirty thousand people, with houses of pale stone climbing up and down the hillsides, and narrow, winding streets, and markets, and churches and mosques, and schools and shops and cafés.

[long-pause]

In the middle of the town, there is a large, open square, paved with stone, called Manger Square.

[pause]

On one side of it stands the Church of the Nativity. From the outside, it does not look like a grand church. It looks more like a fortress, with thick, plain, high walls of old stone, built and rebuilt over many centuries.

[pause]

The entrance is very strange. It is a tiny doorway, so low that almost everyone must bend down to go through it. It is called the Door of Humility. Long ago, the great doorway of the church was partly blocked up, to stop people from riding into the church on horseback, and to make it easier to defend. Now, everyone who enters must bow their head.

[long-pause]

Inside, the church is dim and quiet and very old.

[pause]

There are long rows of tall columns of reddish stone, the same columns that were put up in the time of Justinian, fifteen hundred years ago. High up on the walls, there are glittering mosaics, of gold and green and blue, made by artists in the twelfth century. Under trapdoors in the floor, visitors can look down at the floor of Constantine's church, with its beautiful patterns of coloured stone.

[pause]

{img:nativity-church-interior-red-columns-hanging-lamps}
Hundreds of oil lamps, of silver and brass, hang from the ceiling on long chains, glowing softly in the dim light. The air smells of incense and old stone and candle wax.

[long-pause]

At the front of the church, on either side of the altar, there are two flights of stone steps, going down.

[pause]

Visitors go down the steps, slowly, one by one, into the Grotto of the Nativity: a small, low cave, under the church, lit by candles and lamps, its walls hung with old tapestries.

[pause]

In a little niche at one end of the cave, set into the marble floor, there is a silver star, with fourteen points, surrounded by burning lamps. Around it, there is an inscription in Latin, which says: Here Jesus Christ was born of the Virgin Mary.

[pause]

Visitors kneel down, and touch the star, or kiss it, and say a prayer. Then they stand for a moment, in the quiet, in the soft light of the lamps, and look around them, at the old rock walls of the cave.

[long-pause]

Close by, a few steps away, there is another little niche, which is called the Chapel of the Manger, where, people say, the manger stood.

[pause]

And under the church, there are other caves, connected by passages, where, it is said, Saint Jerome, the great scholar, lived for many years, in the fourth century, translating the Bible into Latin, by the light of an oil lamp, close to the place of the birth.

[pause]

{img:scholar-cave-study-lamp-manuscripts-ancient}
Beside the Church of the Nativity, there is another church, the Church of Saint Catherine, with a cloister and a courtyard, and a statue of Saint Jerome, with a skull at his feet, and a lion.

[long-pause]

On the edge of Bethlehem, in the little town of Beit Sahour, there is a place called the Shepherds' Field.

[pause]

It is a quiet valley, with olive trees and stony fields, and caves in the hillsides, where, people say, the shepherds were watching their flocks on the night when the angels sang. There are little chapels there, and gardens, and the ruins of ancient monasteries.

[pause]

Sheep and goats still graze on the hillsides around Bethlehem, and shepherds still walk with them, with their staffs, and call to them by name.

[long-pause]

All through the year, but especially at Christmas, visitors come to Bethlehem from all over the world.

[pause]

Many of them buy little carvings of olive wood, made by the craftsmen of Bethlehem, who have been carving olive wood for centuries: little nativity scenes, and crosses, and figures of the shepherds and the wise men and the animals. The workshops are full of the sweet smell of olive wood, and the sound of saws and chisels.

[pause]

{img:olive-wood-carver-workshop-nativity-figures}
And at Christmas, Manger Square is decorated with lights, and a great Christmas tree, and there are processions through the streets, with bands of scouts, playing bagpipes and drums. On Christmas Eve, there is a midnight Mass in the Church of Saint Catherine, which is broadcast to people all over the world.

[long-pause]

There are different Christmases in Bethlehem, too.

[pause]

The Catholic and Protestant Christians celebrate on the twenty-fifth of December. The Greek Orthodox and other eastern churches celebrate on the sixth and seventh of January. And the Armenian Christians celebrate on the eighteenth and nineteenth of January. So in Bethlehem, Christmas lasts for almost a month.

[pause]

The church bells ring out over the old town, again and again, through the long winter, for each of the Christmases in turn.

[long-pause]

On a winter evening in Bethlehem, the light fades early over the hills.

[pause]

The sun goes down behind the western ridges, and the sky turns gold and rose and violet. The lights come on in the houses on the hillsides, and in the windows of the old church. The bells ring for the evening. And the cold, clear night comes down, as it did on that night long ago, and the stars come out, one by one, over the hills where the shepherds watched.

[pause]

{img:bethlehem-night-hills-lights-stars-quiet-winter}
O little town of Bethlehem, how still we see thee lie.

[long-pause]

So we leave Bethlehem for tonight, the little town on its hill, under the winter stars, with its old church over the quiet cave, and the shepherds' fields lying dark and still around it.
