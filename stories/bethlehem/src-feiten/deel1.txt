TOPIC: Life in Bethlehem
===
IMG: bethlehem-village-hill-terraces-evening | a small village of white stone houses with flat roofs on a terraced hillside in Judea about two thousand years ago, olive trees and sheep on the slopes, purple and gold evening sky
HEAD: About two thousand years ago, Bethlehem was a small village of stone houses on a hill in Judea.
BODY: Bethlehem lies in the hills of Judea, about nine kilometres south of Jerusalem. Around the time of the first Christmas, it was a small farming village. Historians estimate that a few hundred people lived there, perhaps up to a thousand. Their houses were built of local limestone and stood close together along the ridge of a hill, surrounded by terraced fields, olive groves and vineyards. The name Bethlehem means house of bread in Hebrew, a sign of the grain that grew in the fields around it. Even so, the village was not important in politics or trade. It was overshadowed by Jerusalem, the great city of the Temple, just a few hours' walk to the north. Yet Bethlehem had a famous past. It was the home town of King David, the great king of Israel. And according to the Gospels of Matthew and Luke, it was here that Jesus of Nazareth was born.
===
IMG: villagers-morning-street-water-jars | villagers in simple woven tunics and head coverings walking along a narrow stone street in an ancient Judean village in the early morning, women carrying clay water jars on their heads, a donkey with baskets, soft golden light
HEAD: Each day in Bethlehem began at sunrise, with women fetching water and men heading out to the fields.
BODY: Life in a village like Bethlehem followed the rhythm of the sun and the seasons. Historians note that people rose at dawn. Women and girls went to fetch water from wells and cisterns, carrying it home in clay jars balanced on their heads. Men and boys went out to work in the fields, the vineyards and the olive groves, or led the sheep and goats out to graze. Even so, the women's work did not stop with water. They ground grain into flour, baked bread, cooked meals, spun wool, wove cloth and cared for the children. Most families grew most of their own food. The village was a close community. Everyone knew everyone, and families had lived there for generations. People spoke Aramaic in daily life, a language related to Hebrew. They greeted each other with the word shalom, meaning peace. At midday, they rested in the shade during the hottest hours, then returned to work until evening.

IMG: gospel-scroll-oil-lamp-table | an ancient scroll partly unrolled on a wooden table beside a small clay oil lamp with a flickering flame in a simple stone room, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm lamplight
HEAD: Most of what is told about the first Christmas comes from the Gospels of Matthew and Luke.
BODY: The story of the birth of Jesus is told in two books of the New Testament, the Gospels of Matthew and Luke, written several decades after his life. Historians note that the two accounts are different. Luke tells of the journey of Mary and Joseph from Nazareth, the birth in Bethlehem, the manger and the shepherds. Matthew tells of the star, the wise men from the east and King Herod. Later Christian tradition combined the two into a single story, the one told in nativity plays and carols today. Even so, historians study these texts carefully, asking which details are historical and which reflect the faith of the early Christians. There are few other sources from the time. Yet much is known about daily life in Judea two thousand years ago, from archaeology, from other ancient writers and from the Jewish scriptures. Together, these help to picture the village of Bethlehem as it was at the time of the first Christmas.

IMG: judea-landscape-hills-olive-groves-wide | the rolling stony hills of Judea about two thousand years ago with terraced fields, olive groves and a small village on a ridge, the distant Judean desert fading into haze, soft afternoon light
HEAD: Bethlehem stood on the edge of the Judean hills, where green fields gave way to dry desert.
BODY: Bethlehem lies at the edge of two very different landscapes. Historians note that to the west and north are the Judean hills, with fertile valleys, terraced fields, olive groves and vineyards. To the east, the land drops steeply towards the Judean Desert and the Dead Sea, a dry, rocky wilderness where little grows except after the winter rains. The village stood about seven hundred and seventy metres above sea level. Even so, the land around it was good for farming. The winter rains watered the fields of wheat and barley, and the hillsides were covered with olive trees, vines and fig trees. Shepherds grazed their flocks on the slopes and, in some seasons, in the edges of the desert. The climate was mild, with warm, dry summers and cool, rainy winters. Historians note that in winter, nights in Bethlehem could be cold, and snow fell occasionally on the hills.

IMG: family-evening-meal-mat-oil-lamp | a family in simple tunics sitting on a woven mat on the floor of a small stone house sharing bread, olives and lentils from clay bowls by the light of an oil lamp about two thousand years ago, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm lamplight
HEAD: In the evening, families gathered to eat bread, olives and lentils by the light of small oil lamps.
BODY: The main meal of the day in Bethlehem was eaten in the evening, after the work in the fields was done. Historians note that families sat on mats on the floor and ate from shared bowls. Bread was the most important food, eaten at every meal. It was dipped in olive oil, eaten with vegetables, lentils, beans, cheese or fruit. Meat was eaten only on special occasions, such as festivals or when guests came. Even so, the food was healthy and filling. People drank water, milk and wine, usually mixed with water. Figs, dates, grapes and pomegranates were enjoyed in season. Meals were lit by small clay oil lamps, which burned olive oil and gave a soft, flickering light. Before eating, a blessing was said over the bread. Historians note that sharing a meal was an important sign of friendship and hospitality. Guests were always welcomed and given the best food the family could offer.

IMG: village-gathering-elders-gate-shade | village elders in robes and head coverings sitting together on stone benches in the shade by the gate of an ancient Judean village, talking, a donkey and a few children nearby, warm afternoon light
HEAD: The elders of the village met at the gate to settle disputes and discuss village matters.
BODY: In ancient villages like Bethlehem, the elders played an important role. Historians note that they were respected older men, heads of families, who met to discuss village affairs, settle disputes and witness agreements. They often gathered at the gate of the village or in an open space, where everyone could see and hear. In the Jewish scriptures, the Book of Ruth describes how a man named Boaz met with the elders at the gate of Bethlehem to arrange his marriage to Ruth. Even so, the village was also part of a larger world. It was under the rule of King Herod, who ruled Judea with the support of Rome. Tax collectors came to collect taxes for the king. Soldiers sometimes passed through. Historians note that most villagers had little contact with the powerful, but the decisions of kings and emperors could change their lives suddenly. The village elders tried to protect their community and keep its traditions alive.

IMG: star-over-quiet-village-night-sky | a bright star shining in a deep blue night sky above a quiet ancient village of small stone houses on a hill in Judea, olive trees in silhouette, peaceful mood
HEAD: The story of Christmas has made this small village one of the most famous places in the world.
BODY: For most of its history, Bethlehem was an ordinary village, much like many others in the hills of Judea. Historians note that it had been home to farmers and shepherds for more than a thousand years before the time of Jesus. Yet the story of the first Christmas made it famous around the world. Today, its name is known in almost every country. It appears in carols, Christmas cards and nativity plays. Even so, the Bethlehem of two thousand years ago was very different from the images often seen today. It was a small, quiet village, with simple stone houses, dusty streets and fields of grain. Its people worked hard to feed their families. They kept their traditions, prayed, celebrated festivals and cared for each other. Historians note that understanding their daily lives helps to bring the story closer. It shows the ordinary world in which, according to the Gospels, an extraordinary event took place.

TOPIC: The hills of Judea
===
IMG: terraced-hillside-stone-walls-farmers | farmers in simple tunics repairing low dry stone walls on a terraced hillside planted with olive trees and vines in Judea about two thousand years ago, a village above, warm morning light
HEAD: Farmers built stone terraces on the steep hillsides around Bethlehem to create flat land for crops.
BODY: The hills around Bethlehem were steep and rocky. Historians note that to grow crops, farmers built terraces, flat strips of land held in place by walls of stones. They gathered the stones from the fields, piling them carefully into walls. Behind the walls, they filled in soil to create level fields. The terraces stopped the soil from being washed away by the winter rains and held water for the crops. Even so, building and repairing terraces was hard work. Each generation had to maintain the walls, rebuilding them when they collapsed. Some terraces in the hills of Judea are very old, and some are still used today. Historians note that the terraced hillsides gave the landscape its distinctive look, with lines of stone walls following the contours of the hills. Olive trees, vines and fig trees grew on the terraces, along with wheat, barley and vegetables. The terraces were a sign of the hard work of many generations.
===
IMG: olive-trees-old-gnarled-grove-sun | ancient gnarled olive trees with silver-green leaves growing in a grove on a stony hillside in Judea, sheep resting in their shade, warm golden afternoon light
HEAD: Olive trees grew everywhere around Bethlehem, providing oil for food, light and medicine.
BODY: The olive tree was one of the most important plants in ancient Judea. Historians note that olive trees grew everywhere on the hillsides around Bethlehem. They could live for hundreds of years, and some very old trees still survive in the region today. Even so, the olive tree was not just a tree. Its fruit provided olive oil, which was used for cooking, lighting lamps, making medicine and anointing the body. Oil was also used in religious ceremonies. Kings and priests were anointed with oil as a sign of their special role. In the autumn, families harvested the olives by beating the branches with sticks, collecting the fruit on cloths spread beneath the trees. The olives were then crushed in stone presses to extract the oil. Historians note that olive oil was one of the main products of the region, traded across the Mediterranean. Olive trees were a symbol of peace, wealth and blessing in the Jewish scriptures.
===
IMG: vineyard-grapes-harvest-baskets-judea | men and women in simple tunics harvesting bunches of dark grapes into woven baskets in a terraced vineyard in Judea about two thousand years ago, a small stone watchtower in the corner, warm late summer light
HEAD: In late summer, families harvested grapes from their vineyards and made wine.
BODY: Vineyards were common on the hillsides around Bethlehem. Historians note that grapes were harvested in late summer, in August and September. The harvest was a time of hard work and celebration. Families picked the grapes and carried them in baskets to a winepress, a flat area carved into the rock. There, people trod the grapes with their feet, and the juice flowed into a lower basin. The juice was collected in jars and left to ferment into wine. Even so, not all the grapes were made into wine. Some were eaten fresh, and others were dried into raisins, which could be stored for the winter. Small stone watchtowers stood in some vineyards, where a family member could keep watch over the ripening grapes, protecting them from thieves and animals. Historians note that wine was drunk at meals and festivals, usually mixed with water. The vine was a symbol of the people of Israel in the Jewish scriptures.

IMG: winter-rain-clouds-hills-judea | grey winter rain clouds over the green hills of Judea about two thousand years ago, a small village of stone houses on a ridge, water flowing in small streams down the terraces, soft cool light
HEAD: The winter rains turned the hills green and filled the cisterns that the villages depended on.
BODY: In Judea, the year was divided into two main seasons, a dry summer and a rainy winter. Historians note that the rains usually began in October or November and lasted until March or April. The first rains softened the ground for ploughing and sowing. The heavy winter rains filled the cisterns and watered the crops. The late rains in spring helped the grain to ripen. Even so, the rains were not always reliable. In some years, they came late or not at all, and drought brought hunger. Farmers watched the sky anxiously and prayed for rain. Historians note that water was precious in Bethlehem, which had no river or large spring. People collected rainwater in cisterns, underground tanks cut into the rock, and drew from them throughout the dry summer. In winter, the hills around the village turned green, and wild flowers bloomed. The nights were cold, and families gathered around small fires for warmth.

IMG: well-cistern-women-drawing-water | women in long robes and head coverings drawing water with ropes and clay jars from a stone well at the edge of an ancient Judean village, a few children and a goat nearby, soft morning light
HEAD: Water came from wells and cisterns, and the women of the village gathered there each morning.
BODY: In Bethlehem, water came from wells and cisterns. Historians note that the Jewish scriptures mention a well by the gate of Bethlehem, from which King David once longed to drink when he was fighting the Philistines. Three of his warriors broke through the enemy lines to bring him water from it. Even so, most families depended on cisterns, which collected rainwater from roofs and courtyards. Each morning, women and girls went to fetch water for the day. They carried it home in clay jars, balanced on their heads or shoulders. The well was also a place to meet and talk. Women shared news, made friends and arranged marriages. Historians note that in many stories of the Jewish scriptures, important meetings happened at wells. Water was essential for drinking, cooking, washing and watering animals. Families used it carefully, especially in the long, dry summer, when the cisterns slowly emptied.

IMG: herodium-fortress-mountain-distance | a cone-shaped man-made mountain with a round fortress palace on its flat top rising above the dry hills southeast of Bethlehem about two thousand years ago, a road and travellers below, soft afternoon light
HEAD: From Bethlehem, people could see King Herod's great fortress palace, the Herodium, on a nearby hill.
BODY: A few kilometres southeast of Bethlehem rises a strange, cone-shaped hill. Historians note that it was largely built by King Herod the Great, who piled up earth and stone to raise its height. On its top, he built a round fortress palace, called the Herodium. It had towers, courtyards, bathhouses and gardens. At its foot, he built a lower palace with a large pool. Even so, the Herodium was not only a palace. It was also a monument to Herod himself. He had won a battle there as a young man, and he chose it as his burial place. When he died in 4 BC, he was buried there. Historians note that his tomb was discovered by the archaeologist Ehud Netzer in 2007. For the people of Bethlehem, the Herodium was a constant reminder of the king's power, visible from their fields and rooftops. It still stands today, its top visible for many kilometres.
