TOPIC: Houses of stone
===
IMG: stone-house-flat-roof-courtyard-judea | a simple one-storey house of rough limestone blocks with a flat roof of beams and packed earth, a small courtyard with a clay oven and a donkey, in an ancient Judean village, warm morning light
HEAD: Most houses in Bethlehem were simple buildings of stone, with flat roofs of wood and earth.
BODY: The houses of Bethlehem two thousand years ago were built of the local limestone. Historians note that most were simple, with one or two rooms, built around or beside a small courtyard. The walls were made of rough stones, held together with mud mortar. The roof was flat, made of wooden beams covered with branches, reeds and packed clay. Even so, the roof was an important part of the house. It was used for drying fruit and grain, sleeping on hot summer nights and praying. Stairs or a ladder led up to it from the courtyard. After the winter rains, the clay had to be rolled flat again with a stone roller to keep it watertight. Historians note that the courtyard was where much of daily life took place. Families cooked, ate, worked and talked there. Houses were often shared by several generations of a family, with new rooms added as children married and had families of their own.
===
IMG: house-interior-raised-floor-animals-below | the interior of a simple ancient Judean stone house with a raised living platform where a family sits on mats, and a lower floor at the entrance where a donkey and a few sheep stand beside a stone manger, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft light
HEAD: In many village houses, the family lived on a raised floor, and the animals stayed on a lower level at night.
BODY: In many villages of ancient Judea, houses had a special layout. Historians and archaeologists note that the main room was often divided into two levels. The family lived, ate and slept on a raised platform at the back. Near the entrance was a lower level, where the family's animals, such as a donkey, a few sheep or goats, or an ox, were brought in at night. The animals were protected from thieves and cold, and their warmth helped heat the house. Even so, the animals did not wander freely. Feeding troughs, called mangers, were built at the edge of the raised floor or cut into the stone, so that the animals could eat. Historians note that this kind of house is important for understanding the story of the first Christmas. When Luke describes the baby Jesus being laid in a manger, he may have been describing the lower part of such a house, where the animals were kept.
===
IMG: cave-dwelling-hillside-stone-entrance | a house built against a limestone hillside with a natural cave at the back used as a room and stable, a wooden door, a woman sweeping in front and a goat nearby, in ancient Judea, soft afternoon light
HEAD: Some houses in Bethlehem were built in front of caves in the limestone hillside.
BODY: The hills of Bethlehem are made of soft limestone, full of natural caves. Historians note that people in the area often used these caves as part of their homes. A house might be built in front of a cave, with the cave serving as a back room, a storeroom or a shelter for animals. Caves stayed cool in summer and relatively warm in winter, making them very useful. Even so, caves were not only for houses. They were also used for storing grain, oil and wine, for tombs and for sheltering flocks. Historians note that a very old Christian tradition says that Jesus was born in a cave. This tradition was recorded by Justin Martyr, a Christian writer born in the region, around the year 150. In the fourth century, a church was built over a cave in Bethlehem that was believed to be the place of the birth. That church, the Church of the Nativity, still stands today.
===
IMG: clay-oven-tabun-baking-flatbread | a woman in a long robe and head covering baking flat round bread on the hot inner walls of a small dome-shaped clay oven in the courtyard of a stone house in ancient Judea, smoke rising, warm morning light
HEAD: Women baked flat bread every day in small clay ovens in the courtyards of their houses.
BODY: Bread was baked every day in Bethlehem. Historians note that women ground wheat or barley into flour using a hand mill made of two round stones, one turned on top of the other. This was hard work and took a long time each morning. The flour was mixed with water, salt and a little leaven, a piece of dough saved from the day before. Even so, the bread was not baked in a large oven like today's. Women used small dome-shaped clay ovens, called tabuns, in the courtyard. A fire was lit inside, and when the walls were hot, flat rounds of dough were pressed against them to bake. The bread was ready in a few minutes. Historians note that the name Bethlehem, house of bread, reflects the importance of grain and bread in the life of the village. Bread was eaten at every meal and shared with guests. Breaking bread together was a sign of friendship and trust.
===
IMG: spinning-wool-woman-spindle-doorway | a woman in a simple robe sitting in the doorway of a stone house spinning wool with a hand spindle, a basket of raw wool beside her and a small child playing nearby, ancient Judea, soft afternoon light
HEAD: Women spun wool and wove cloth at home, making clothes for the whole family.
BODY: In ancient Bethlehem, most clothing was made at home. Historians note that women spun wool from the family's sheep into thread, using a hand spindle, a stick with a weight at the bottom that was twirled to twist the fibres. They also spun flax into linen thread. The thread was woven into cloth on a simple loom, often set up in the courtyard or on the roof. Even so, making cloth took a great deal of time. A single garment might take weeks to make. Women often spun while doing other things, such as watching children, talking with neighbours or walking. The cloth was used to make tunics, cloaks, head coverings and blankets. Historians note that most people owned only a few garments. A man typically wore a tunic, a belt and a cloak, which also served as a blanket at night. Women wore longer tunics and head coverings. Cloth was valuable and was carefully mended and reused.
===
IMG: rooftop-evening-family-resting-stars | a family resting on the flat rooftop of a stone house in an ancient Judean village on a warm evening, sitting on mats, the hills and other rooftops around, the first stars appearing in a violet sky
HEAD: On warm evenings, families rested on their flat roofs, watching the stars appear over the hills.
BODY: The flat roofs of Bethlehem's houses were more than just a covering. Historians note that they were an important living space, especially in the warm months. Families climbed up to the roof in the evening to enjoy the cool breeze after the heat of the day. They talked, rested and watched the sun set over the hills. Children played, and adults told stories. Even so, the roof was also used for work. Fruits such as figs and grapes were dried there, and flax was spread out to dry. On hot summer nights, some family members slept on the roof. Historians note that the Jewish law required that roofs should have a low wall, or parapet, around the edge, to prevent people from falling. From the rooftops, people could see across the village to the fields and hills beyond. At night, the sky above Bethlehem was full of stars, far brighter than in modern cities, with no lights to dim them.

TOPIC: A year of work in the fields
===
IMG: ploughing-oxen-wooden-plough-autumn | a farmer guiding a simple wooden plough pulled by two oxen through a small stony field on a Judean hillside in autumn about two thousand years ago, a village on the ridge, soft morning light
HEAD: After the first autumn rains, farmers ploughed their fields with oxen and sowed wheat and barley.
BODY: The farming year in Bethlehem began in the autumn. Historians note that after the first rains softened the hard, dry ground in October or November, farmers ploughed their fields. They used simple wooden ploughs, sometimes with an iron tip, pulled by oxen or donkeys. The plough cut a shallow furrow in the soil. Even so, the fields were small and rocky, and ploughing was hard work. Farmers walked behind the plough, guiding it and pushing it into the ground. After ploughing, they sowed seeds of wheat and barley by hand, scattering them across the field from a bag or basket. Then they ploughed again to cover the seeds. Historians note that barley was more common than wheat in poorer areas, because it grew better in dry, stony soil. Barley bread was the food of ordinary people, while wheat bread was more expensive. The fields around Bethlehem were famous for their grain.
===
IMG: barley-harvest-sickles-reapers-spring | men and women in simple tunics harvesting golden barley with curved sickles in a field below a hillside village in Judea in spring, sheaves stacked behind them, warm bright light
HEAD: In spring, the barley was harvested first, followed a few weeks later by the wheat.
BODY: The grain harvest in Judea took place in the spring. Historians note that barley ripened first, in April, around the time of the festival of Passover. Wheat followed a few weeks later, in May and June, around the festival of Shavuot, also called the Feast of Weeks. The whole family worked in the fields during the harvest. Men cut the grain with sickles, curved blades of iron set in wooden handles. Women and children gathered the cut stalks and tied them into sheaves. Even so, not all the grain was taken. Jewish law said that farmers should leave some grain at the edges of their fields and should not go back for stalks they had dropped. This was for the poor, widows and strangers, who could gather it for themselves. Historians note that this practice, called gleaning, is described in the Book of Ruth, which tells of a widow who gleaned in the fields of Bethlehem.
===
IMG: threshing-floor-oxen-winnowing-wind | a flat stone threshing floor on a hilltop near an ancient Judean village with oxen walking in circles over spread grain, a man tossing grain into the air with a wooden fork so the wind carries away the chaff, warm golden afternoon light
HEAD: The grain was threshed on flat stone floors, and the wind blew away the chaff.
BODY: After the harvest, the grain had to be separated from the stalks and husks. Historians note that this was done on a threshing floor, a flat, open area of hard ground or rock, usually on a hilltop where the wind blew. The sheaves were spread out on the floor, and oxen or donkeys walked over them in circles, sometimes pulling a heavy wooden sledge with stones underneath. This broke the grain from the stalks. Even so, the grain was still mixed with chaff, the light husks. To separate them, farmers tossed the mixture into the air with wooden forks or shovels. The heavier grain fell back to the ground, while the wind carried the chaff away. This was called winnowing. Historians note that the threshing floor was a busy and joyful place at harvest time. In the Book of Ruth, Boaz sleeps on his threshing floor at night to guard his grain. The clean grain was stored in jars and pits.
===
IMG: fig-tree-summer-fruit-picking-ladder | a family picking ripe purple figs from a large leafy fig tree beside a stone house in ancient Judea in summer, baskets on the ground, a child on a wooden ladder, warm bright light
HEAD: In summer, families picked figs, grapes and pomegranates, and dried some fruit for the winter.
BODY: Summer in Judea was hot and dry, but it was also a time of plenty. Historians note that it was the season of fruit. Figs ripened in early and late summer, and grapes in August and September. Pomegranates, dates and other fruits were also harvested. Families picked the fruit and ate it fresh, enjoying its sweetness after the long months of bread and lentils. Even so, much of the fruit was preserved for the winter. Figs were dried in the sun and pressed into cakes. Grapes were made into raisins or wine. Pomegranates could be stored for months. Historians note that the fig tree was especially loved. Its broad leaves gave shade, and its fruit was sweet and nourishing. In the Jewish scriptures, sitting under one's own vine and fig tree was a picture of peace and contentment. Many houses in Bethlehem had a fig tree in the courtyard or nearby.
===
IMG: sabbath-evening-lamp-family-blessing | a family in clean robes gathered around a low table at dusk in a simple stone house, the mother lighting a small clay oil lamp, bread and a cup of wine on the table, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, warm lamplight
HEAD: Every week, the Sabbath brought a day of rest from all work, from Friday evening to Saturday evening.
BODY: The people of Bethlehem were Jewish, and their lives were shaped by the Jewish law and calendar. Historians note that the most important weekly event was the Sabbath, the day of rest. It began at sunset on Friday and ended at sunset on Saturday. During the Sabbath, no work was done. Families did not cook, plough, harvest or travel far. Even so, preparations began before. On Friday, women cooked extra food and prepared the house. At sunset, a lamp was lit, and the family shared a special meal with blessings over bread and wine. On Saturday, people rested, prayed and studied the scriptures. Historians note that in larger villages and towns, people gathered in synagogues, meeting houses where the scriptures were read and explained. The Sabbath was a time of peace and family. For people who worked hard every other day of the week, it was a precious gift.
===
IMG: pilgrims-road-to-jerusalem-festival | groups of families with donkeys walking along a dusty road through the hills towards a walled city with a great temple on the horizon, about two thousand years ago, a festive mood, warm afternoon light
HEAD: Three times a year, families travelled to Jerusalem for the great pilgrimage festivals.
BODY: The Jewish calendar had three great pilgrimage festivals: Passover in spring, Shavuot in early summer and Sukkot in autumn. Historians note that at these times, many Jews travelled to Jerusalem to worship at the Temple. For the people of Bethlehem, Jerusalem was only a few hours' walk away. Families travelled together, singing psalms on the road. Even so, the festivals were not only religious. They were also joyful celebrations of the harvest and of the history of the Jewish people. Passover remembered the escape of the Israelites from slavery in Egypt. Shavuot celebrated the wheat harvest. Sukkot celebrated the grape harvest, and families lived in shelters made of branches for a week. Historians note that during the festivals, Jerusalem's population swelled enormously, with pilgrims coming from all over Judea and from Jewish communities across the Roman world. The roads near Bethlehem were filled with travellers heading to the holy city.
===
IMG: evening-return-fields-tools-donkey | farmers returning home from the fields at sunset along a path to a hillside village of stone houses in ancient Judea, carrying hoes and baskets, a donkey loaded with bundles, warm orange light
HEAD: At sunset, the farmers came home from the fields, tired after a long day's work.
BODY: As the sun went down over the hills, the people of Bethlehem returned from their work. Historians imagine the scene: farmers walking home along the paths between the terraces, carrying their tools on their shoulders, donkeys loaded with firewood, grain or fruit. Shepherds brought their flocks back to the village or settled them in folds for the night. Children ran ahead to greet them. Even so, the work of the day was not quite over. Animals had to be fed and watered, tools put away and the evening meal prepared. Smoke rose from the cooking fires, and the smell of bread and stew filled the air. Historians note that families then gathered for their meal, sharing the news of the day. After dark, the village grew quiet. Oil lamps glowed in a few doorways. The stars came out over the hills. The people of Bethlehem went to sleep early, ready to rise again with the sun.

TOPIC: Shepherds and their flocks
===
IMG: shepherd-boy-flock-sheep-hillside | a young shepherd in a simple woollen cloak with a wooden staff watching a flock of sheep and goats grazing on a stony Judean hillside, a village in the distance, soft golden afternoon light
HEAD: Many families in Bethlehem kept sheep and goats, and the shepherds led them out to graze each day.
BODY: Sheep and goats were an important part of life in Bethlehem. Historians note that they provided milk, cheese, wool, leather and meat. Most families kept a few animals, and some owned larger flocks. Each morning, shepherds led the flocks out of the village to graze on the hillsides and in the valleys. In the dry season, they sometimes had to travel further, into the edges of the desert, to find enough grass. Even so, shepherding was often done by young people. Boys and sometimes girls cared for the family's flock, while their parents worked in the fields. In the Jewish scriptures, the young David was a shepherd near Bethlehem before he became king. Historians note that shepherds carried a staff, a long stick with a curved end, used to guide the sheep, and a rod or club to protect them from wild animals. They also carried a sling for throwing stones.
===
IMG: sheepfold-stone-walls-night-fire | a circular sheepfold of low stone walls on a hillside at night with sheep resting inside, shepherds wrapped in cloaks sitting by a small fire at the entrance, stars above, ancient Judea
HEAD: At night, the sheep were kept in folds with stone walls, and shepherds slept at the entrance to guard them.
BODY: When shepherds stayed out with their flocks overnight, they kept the sheep in sheepfolds. Historians note that these were enclosures with low walls of stones, often topped with thorny branches to keep out wild animals. Some folds were built against cliffs or caves. There was usually a single entrance, and the shepherd sometimes slept across it, acting as a living door. Even so, guarding the flock at night was not easy. Wolves, jackals, hyenas and even leopards lived in the hills and could attack the sheep. Thieves could also try to steal them. Shepherds took turns to stay awake, keeping a fire burning. Historians note that the Gospel of Luke describes shepherds living out in the fields near Bethlehem, keeping watch over their flocks by night. Scholars have debated whether flocks would have been kept outdoors in winter. Some think this was more likely in spring, while others believe flocks were kept in the fields throughout the year.
===
IMG: shepherd-carrying-lamb-shoulders | a shepherd in a rough woollen cloak carrying a small lamb on his shoulders across a rocky Judean hillside, the rest of the flock following, soft evening light
HEAD: Shepherds knew each of their sheep and carried lost or injured lambs home on their shoulders.
BODY: Shepherds in ancient Judea developed a close bond with their sheep. Historians note that they knew each animal and often gave them names. The sheep, in turn, recognised their shepherd's voice and followed him. When a lamb was lost, the shepherd searched for it. When it was injured or too weak to walk, he carried it on his shoulders. Even so, shepherding was not a respected profession in all circles. Some educated people of the time looked down on shepherds as poor, rough and unreliable, because they spent so much time away from towns. Yet in the Jewish scriptures, the shepherd was a powerful image. God was described as a shepherd caring for his people, as in the famous psalm that begins, The Lord is my shepherd. Historians note that in the Gospel of Luke, the news of the birth of Jesus is announced first to shepherds. This showed that the message was for ordinary, humble people.
===
IMG: milking-goats-courtyard-clay-jar | a young woman in a simple robe milking a goat into a clay jar in the courtyard of a stone house in ancient Judea, other goats and a child nearby, soft morning light
HEAD: Goats and sheep gave milk, which was made into butter, cheese and yogurt.
BODY: Milk was an important food in Bethlehem. Historians note that it came mainly from goats and sheep, rather than cows. Goats were especially useful because they could eat rough plants and survive in dry conditions. Milk was drunk fresh or made into other foods. It was poured into a skin bag and shaken to make butter. It was left to sour into a thick yogurt, called leben. It was also made into cheese, which could be dried and stored for months. Even so, milk did not keep long in the warm climate, so it had to be used quickly. Women and children did most of the milking, early in the morning and in the evening. Historians note that the Jewish scriptures describe the promised land as a land flowing with milk and honey, a picture of abundance. For the people of Bethlehem, milk from their goats and sheep was an important part of a healthy diet.
===
IMG: shearing-sheep-wool-spring-gathering | men shearing sheep with iron shears in an open area beside an ancient Judean village in spring, piles of white wool on the ground, women gathering it into baskets, a festive mood, warm light
HEAD: Sheep shearing in spring was a time of hard work and celebration.
BODY: Once a year, in spring, the sheep were sheared. Historians note that shepherds cut the wool from the sheep using iron shears. The wool was then washed, combed and spun into thread. Wool was a valuable product. It was used to make clothes, blankets and tents, and it could be sold or traded. Even so, shearing was also a time of celebration. In the Jewish scriptures, sheep shearing is described as a festive occasion, with feasts and gatherings. Families and neighbours came together to help with the work and to share food and drink. Historians note that wool from the area around Bethlehem may have been sent to Jerusalem for sale. Some scholars believe that sheep raised near Bethlehem were used for sacrifices at the Temple in Jerusalem. This idea comes from later Jewish writings, and historians are not certain about it. Yet the connection between Bethlehem's flocks and the nearby holy city was close.
===
IMG: shepherds-field-night-stars-quiet | shepherds wrapped in cloaks resting on a hillside near a small village at night, their sheep lying quietly around them, a vast starry sky above the dark Judean hills, peaceful mood
HEAD: East of Bethlehem lie fields where, according to tradition, the shepherds of the Christmas story watched their flocks.
BODY: East of Bethlehem, the land slopes down towards the village of Beit Sahour and the Judean Desert beyond. Historians note that this area has long been associated with the shepherds of the Christmas story. Since at least the fourth century, Christians have visited fields there, known as the Shepherds' Fields, to remember the angels' announcement to the shepherds. Even so, the exact place cannot be known. The Gospel of Luke says only that the shepherds were in the region near Bethlehem. Several sites are shown to visitors today, each with its own church or chapel. Archaeologists have found remains of ancient buildings, caves and monasteries in the area. Historians note that the landscape has changed greatly over two thousand years, but the hills and the night sky are much the same. On clear nights, the stars still shine brightly over the fields, as they would have done for the shepherds who watched their flocks there long ago.

TOPIC: Ruth, David and the old stories
===
IMG: ruth-gleaning-barley-field-reapers | a young woman in a simple robe and head covering gathering leftover stalks of barley at the edge of a golden field behind a group of reapers near an ancient hillside village, warm afternoon light
HEAD: According to the Book of Ruth, a young widow from Moab once gathered grain in the fields of Bethlehem.
BODY: One of the most beloved stories in the Jewish scriptures is set in Bethlehem. Historians note that the Book of Ruth tells of a woman named Naomi from Bethlehem, who moved to the land of Moab with her husband and sons during a famine. Her husband and sons died there. Naomi decided to return home, and her daughter-in-law Ruth, a woman from Moab, insisted on going with her. Ruth promised Naomi that she would go wherever Naomi went, and that Naomi's people would be her people. Even so, the two women arrived in Bethlehem poor. It was the time of the barley harvest, and Ruth went to glean in the fields, gathering the grain left by the reapers. She worked in the field of a kind landowner named Boaz, a relative of Naomi's husband. Historians note that the story celebrates loyalty, kindness and the welcoming of strangers. The book is still read in synagogues at the festival of Shavuot.
===
IMG: boaz-ruth-village-gate-elders | a dignified older man in a fine robe speaking with village elders seated at the stone gate of an ancient hillside village, a young woman standing quietly nearby, warm afternoon light
HEAD: Ruth married Boaz, and their great-grandson was David, who became king of Israel.
BODY: In the Book of Ruth, Boaz is impressed by Ruth's loyalty and hard work. Historians note that he protects her, gives her extra grain and eventually marries her. The marriage is arranged at the gate of Bethlehem, in front of the village elders, following the customs of the time. Ruth and Boaz have a son named Obed. Obed becomes the father of Jesse, and Jesse becomes the father of David. Even so, the story is more than a family history. It shows that a foreign woman, from a people often seen as enemies, became an ancestor of Israel's greatest king. Historians note that the Book of Ruth was probably written down several centuries after the events it describes, and scholars debate its date and historical basis. Yet it has always been treasured. In the Gospel of Matthew, Ruth is one of only a few women named in the list of the ancestors of Jesus.
===
IMG: young-david-shepherd-harp-hillside | a young shepherd boy with curly hair playing a small wooden lyre while sitting on a rock above a flock of sheep on a Judean hillside, soft golden evening light
HEAD: David, the youngest son of Jesse, grew up as a shepherd boy in the hills around Bethlehem.
BODY: According to the Jewish scriptures, David was the youngest of the eight sons of Jesse, a farmer of Bethlehem. Historians note that he cared for his father's sheep on the hills around the village. He was known for his skill with the sling, with which he protected the flock from lions and bears. He also played the lyre, a small stringed instrument, and composed songs. Even so, his life changed suddenly. The prophet Samuel came to Bethlehem, looking for the man God had chosen to be the next king. Jesse presented his older sons, one by one, but Samuel rejected them all. Finally, David was called in from the fields, and Samuel anointed him with oil. Historians note that David later became famous for defeating the giant Goliath and became king of Israel around 1000 BC. Many of the psalms are traditionally said to have been written by him. Bethlehem became known as the city of David.
===
IMG: king-david-jerusalem-palace-old | an ancient king with a grey beard and a simple gold crown sitting on a carved wooden throne in a stone palace hall about three thousand years ago, a lyre resting beside him, soft warm light
HEAD: David made Jerusalem his capital, but he was always remembered as a son of Bethlehem.
BODY: After becoming king, David captured the city of Jerusalem and made it his capital. Historians note that he brought the Ark of the Covenant, the most sacred object of the Israelites, to the city, making it a religious as well as a political centre. His son Solomon later built the first Temple there. Even so, David never forgot his home town. The scriptures tell how, during a war with the Philistines, who had taken Bethlehem, he longed for a drink of water from the well by the gate of his village. Three of his mighty warriors broke through the enemy camp to bring it to him. But David would not drink it, because they had risked their lives for it. He poured it out as an offering to God. Historians note that archaeologists have found an inscription from the ninth century BC that mentions the house of David, supporting the idea that David was a real historical king.
===
IMG: prophet-scroll-reading-elders-synagogue | an elderly man in a prayer shawl reading aloud from an unrolled scroll to a small group of listeners seated on stone benches in a simple ancient synagogue, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft light
HEAD: An old prophecy said that a great ruler would one day come from Bethlehem.
BODY: In the Jewish scriptures, the prophet Micah, who lived in the eighth century BC, spoke of Bethlehem. Historians note that he wrote that from Bethlehem, though it was small among the clans of Judah, would come a ruler over Israel, whose origins were from of old. Many Jews understood this as a promise that a new king from the family of David would one day arise. This king was often called the Messiah, meaning the anointed one. Even so, ideas about the Messiah varied greatly. Some Jews expected a warrior king who would free them from foreign rule. Others expected a priestly or heavenly figure. Some did not focus on a Messiah at all. Historians note that the Gospel of Matthew quotes Micah's prophecy, saying that it was fulfilled in the birth of Jesus in Bethlehem. For the early Christians, the connection between Jesus and Bethlehem was very important, because it linked him to the family of David.
===
IMG: rachels-tomb-road-bethlehem-ancient | a small ancient stone tomb with a domed roof beside a dusty road at the entrance to a hillside village, travellers with donkeys passing, olive trees around, soft morning light
HEAD: Near Bethlehem stood the tomb of Rachel, one of the mothers of the people of Israel.
BODY: On the road from Jerusalem to Bethlehem lies a site traditionally known as the tomb of Rachel. Historians note that according to the Book of Genesis, Rachel, the beloved wife of Jacob, died while giving birth to her son Benjamin on the way to Ephrath, which is Bethlehem. Jacob buried her by the road and set up a pillar over her grave. Even so, the exact location of Rachel's tomb has been debated. Some scholars believe the original site was further north. The current building dates from much later periods, and it has been rebuilt several times. Yet it has been a place of pilgrimage for Jews, Christians and Muslims for many centuries. Historians note that the Gospel of Matthew mentions Rachel weeping for her children, in connection with the sorrow in Bethlehem after Herod's cruelty. The tomb of Rachel is a reminder of the long and layered history of the land around Bethlehem.

TOPIC: King Herod and Rome
===
IMG: herod-the-great-palace-throne | an elderly king with a dark beard in rich purple robes and a gold diadem seated in a grand marble palace hall with columns, guards standing nearby, about two thousand years ago, soft warm light
HEAD: At the time of the first Christmas, Judea was ruled by King Herod the Great, with the support of Rome.
BODY: Herod the Great became king of Judea in 37 BC. Historians note that he was not from the old royal family of David. His family came from Idumea, south of Judea, and had converted to Judaism. He owed his throne to the Romans, who had taken control of the region in 63 BC. Herod was a clever, ambitious and ruthless ruler. He kept peace in his kingdom and remained loyal to Rome, first to Mark Antony and then to the emperor Augustus. Even so, he was feared and disliked by many of his subjects. He was suspicious of everyone and killed anyone he saw as a threat, including his own wife Mariamne and three of his sons. Historians note that the Roman emperor Augustus is said to have joked that it was better to be Herod's pig than his son. Herod died in 4 BC, after a painful illness. Most historians therefore date the birth of Jesus a few years before that.
===
IMG: herods-temple-jerusalem-gleaming | the great white and gold Temple of Jerusalem on its vast platform with colonnades and courtyards, crowds of pilgrims below, the walled city around it, about two thousand years ago, bright morning light
HEAD: Herod rebuilt the Temple in Jerusalem on a magnificent scale, making it one of the wonders of the ancient world.
BODY: Herod the Great was one of the greatest builders of the ancient world. Historians note that his most famous project was the rebuilding of the Temple in Jerusalem, which began around 20 BC. He enlarged the platform on which the Temple stood, supporting it with enormous retaining walls. He built a magnificent new Temple of white stone decorated with gold, surrounded by courtyards and colonnades. Even so, the work took decades. It continued long after Herod's death and was only completed in the 60s AD, a few years before the Romans destroyed the Temple in the year 70. Historians note that part of the western retaining wall of Herod's Temple platform survives today. It is known as the Western Wall, and it is the holiest place where Jews pray. For the people of Bethlehem, the Temple was only a few hours' walk away. They went there for the great festivals, bringing offerings and joining the crowds of pilgrims.

HEAD: Far away in Rome, the emperor Augustus ruled a vast empire that included Judea.
BODY: At the time of the first Christmas, the Roman Empire was ruled by Augustus, its first emperor. Historians note that he came to power after years of civil war and ruled from 27 BC until his death in AD 14. Under his rule, the empire stretched from Spain to Syria, and from the Rhine to Egypt. He brought a long period of peace, known as the Pax Romana, the Roman peace. Even so, Rome's rule was not gentle everywhere. The empire collected taxes from its provinces and sometimes put down rebellions harshly. In Judea, Rome ruled through Herod, a client king. Historians note that Augustus ordered several censuses, counts of people and property, to organise taxes. The Gospel of Luke says that a decree went out from Caesar Augustus that the whole world should be registered, and that this was why Joseph and Mary travelled to Bethlehem. Historians have debated the details of this census for a long time.

IMG: roman-census-officials-scrolls-table | Roman officials and local scribes seated at a wooden table under an awning in an ancient town square, writing names on wax tablets and scrolls, a queue of local people in robes waiting, warm daylight
HEAD: Luke says a census brought Mary and Joseph to Bethlehem, though historians debate its date.
BODY: According to the Gospel of Luke, Joseph and Mary travelled from Nazareth to Bethlehem because of a census ordered by the emperor Augustus. Joseph had to register in Bethlehem, because he belonged to the family of David. Historians note that Roman censuses were real, and that the governor Quirinius held a famous census in Judea in AD 6. Even so, there is a difficulty. Luke connects the birth with Quirinius, but Matthew places it during the reign of Herod, who died in 4 BC, about ten years earlier. Historians have offered various explanations. Some think Luke made a mistake in dating. Others suggest there may have been an earlier registration, or that the Greek words can be translated differently. It is also unclear why a Roman census would require people to travel to their ancestral town. Historians agree that the exact year of Jesus's birth is uncertain. Most place it between about 6 and 4 BC.

IMG: roman-soldiers-road-judea-patrol | a small patrol of Roman soldiers in helmets and red tunics marching along a dusty road through the hills of Judea, local villagers with donkeys stepping aside, about two thousand years ago, bright daylight
HEAD: Roman soldiers and Herod's troops sometimes passed along the roads near Bethlehem.
BODY: Although Herod was king, Judea was part of the Roman world. Historians note that Herod had his own army, which included soldiers from many lands, and Roman troops were stationed in the region. Soldiers marched along the roads, guarding travellers and trade, collecting taxes and keeping order. The main road from Jerusalem to Hebron passed close to Bethlehem. Even so, most villagers had little direct contact with soldiers. They saw them pass by and paid their taxes, but otherwise they lived their own lives. Historians note that many Jews resented foreign rule and the heavy taxes they had to pay, both to Herod and to Rome. They hoped for a time when they would be free. After Herod's death, there were rebellions in several parts of the country, which the Romans put down harshly. Later, in AD 66, a great revolt broke out, which ended with the destruction of Jerusalem and its Temple in AD 70.

IMG: herodium-palace-interior-pool-gardens | the lower palace of a great fortress hill with a large pool surrounded by colonnaded gardens and a pavilion in the middle, the cone-shaped mountain behind, about two thousand years ago, warm afternoon light
HEAD: Herod's palace near Bethlehem had gardens, baths and a great pool in the middle of the dry hills.
BODY: Herod loved luxury. Historians note that his palaces were among the most splendid in the Roman world, with mosaics, painted walls, bathhouses and gardens. At the Herodium, near Bethlehem, he built a lower palace at the foot of the hill, with a large pool about seventy metres long, surrounded by gardens and colonnades. In the middle of the pool stood a round pavilion. Even so, building such a pool in the dry hills was a great challenge. Water was brought from springs several kilometres away, through an aqueduct. Historians note that Herod also built aqueducts that brought water to Jerusalem from pools south of Bethlehem, known today as Solomon's Pools. These works employed many workers from the villages around. For the people of Bethlehem, Herod's palace was a world apart. They saw its walls and towers on the hill, but they would never have entered its gardens or swum in its pool.

IMG: matthew-herod-soldiers-sorrow-village | a sorrowful scene in an ancient hillside village at dusk, a few women in dark robes and head coverings grieving in a doorway, the empty street and stone houses in shadow, sombre muted light
HEAD: The Gospel of Matthew tells that Herod ordered the killing of the young boys of Bethlehem.
BODY: According to the Gospel of Matthew, when King Herod heard from the wise men that a new king of the Jews had been born, he was troubled. Historians note that Matthew says Herod ordered the killing of all the boys in Bethlehem and its surroundings who were two years old or under. Joseph, warned in a dream, fled with Mary and the child to Egypt, and they stayed there until Herod died. Even so, this event is not mentioned by any other ancient writer, including the Jewish historian Josephus, who wrote in detail about Herod's cruelties. Historians debate whether it happened. Some argue that in a small village, only a handful of children would have been killed, which might not have been widely recorded. Others see the story as a theological echo of the story of Moses in Egypt. Yet it fits what is known of Herod's character. Christians remember the children on the feast of the Holy Innocents, on the twenty-eighth of December.
