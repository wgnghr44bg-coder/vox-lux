In the summer of 1845, two black wooden ships sailed slowly between the icebergs of the far north, lit by a sun that never set. Their names were Erebus and Terror. On board were about one hundred and thirty men, searching for a passage through the ice.

[long-pause]

In May 1845, two Royal Navy ships sailed from England to find the Northwest Passage. On the nineteenth of May 1845, two ships left the River Thames. They were called Erebus and Terror. They were sturdy wooden ships of the Royal Navy, painted black, with yellow bands along their sides. Together they carried about one hundred and thirty men. Their leader was Sir John Franklin, an experienced Arctic explorer. Their goal was to find the Northwest Passage. This was a sea route from the Atlantic to the Pacific through the icy waters north of Canada. For centuries, sailors had searched for it. Many had failed. The British Admiralty believed that this time, with the best ships and supplies, it would be found. Crowds came to watch the ships leave. Newspapers wrote about the expedition with great confidence. Even so, the Arctic was still largely unknown. No ship had ever sailed all the way through. Yet the mood was cheerful. Few people imagined that none of the men would ever come home.

[long-pause]

{img:02-lower-deck-hammocks-mess-tables-lamplight-ship}

Ordinary sailors slept in hammocks hung from the beams, only a few feet apart. Life aboard a Royal Navy ship in 1845 was crowded. Most of the men lived on the lower deck. They ate, slept and spent their free time there. At night, they slung hammocks from hooks in the wooden beams overhead. Each man had a space only a little wider than his shoulders. In the morning, the hammocks were rolled up and stored. Then the space became a dining room. Tables were lowered from the ceiling on ropes. The men sat on benches and lockers in small groups called messes. Each mess cooked and shared its food together. The air below deck was often damp and smoky. In the Arctic, it was also very cold. Even so, the crowded deck was a warm and friendly place in many ways. Sailors told stories, played games and sang. Yet there was little privacy. A sailor's whole world, for years at a time, was a few square metres of wooden deck.

[long-pause]

If cold seas and quiet ships make the night feel softer, subscribing keeps more of these slow voyages near.

[long-pause]

{img:03-officers-wardroom-table-dinner-candles-ship}

The officers dined together in the wardroom, with silver cutlery and fine china. Officers lived very differently from the ordinary sailors. Each had a small cabin of his own. It was only a few feet wide, with a bunk, a small desk and a shelf for books. Yet it was private. The officers ate together in a room called the wardroom. Their meals were served on china plates with silver cutlery. Many officers brought silver spoons and forks engraved with their family crests. Some of these were later found by searchers in the Arctic. Officers paid for extra food and wine from their own money. They held dinners for each other and celebrated birthdays. Sir John Franklin had a larger cabin at the back of Erebus. He often invited officers to dine with him. Even so, the wardroom was also a place of work. Officers wrote reports, studied charts and discussed plans there. Yet it was the closest thing to a comfortable home that the long voyage offered.

[long-pause]

{img:04-ship-school-sailors-learning-to-read-slates}

On long Arctic voyages, officers often ran schools aboard ship to teach sailors reading and arithmetic. Arctic expeditions spent many months trapped in ice. The long dark winters could be very boring. So expedition leaders tried to keep the men busy. One popular idea was a ship's school. It had been used on earlier voyages by the explorer William Parry. In the evenings, officers taught the sailors to read, write and do arithmetic. Some men had never been to school. Others wanted to improve their chances of promotion. Lessons were held at the mess tables, by the light of oil lamps. The men wrote on slates with chalk to save paper. The Franklin expedition carried slates and school supplies for this purpose. Even so, no records of the lessons survive. The ships' journals were lost. Yet we know the materials were on board. On earlier voyages, sailors said these quiet classes were one of the best parts of the winter. A few left the Arctic able to read for the first time.

[long-pause]

{img:05-barrel-organ-wooden-ship-cabin-hymns}

Each ship carried a barrel organ that could play dozens of tunes, including hymns and dances. Music was important on long voyages. On Franklin's ships, there was a special instrument. Each ship carried a barrel organ. It was a wooden box with pipes inside. By turning a handle, a sailor could make it play. A wooden barrel with pins inside decided which notes sounded. By changing the barrel, the organ could play different tunes. According to reports, each organ could play around fifty tunes. There were hymns and psalms for Sunday services. There were also popular songs and dances for evenings. The men could gather around and sing along. Some sailors also brought their own instruments, such as fiddles or flutes. Even so, the barrel organ was the main source of music in the long Arctic nights. It needed no musician, only someone to turn the handle. Yet it brought comfort and familiar sounds from home into the cold and silent world of the ice.

[long-pause]

{img:06-jacko-monkey-neptune-dog-deck-ship}

The ships carried animals, including a monkey named Jacko and a Newfoundland dog called Neptune. Erebus and Terror did not carry only men. There were animals on board too. Like many ships of the time, they carried live animals for food at the start of the voyage. There were also pets. On Erebus, there was a monkey named Jacko. He had been a gift to the expedition. Officers wrote that he was playful and sometimes naughty. He liked to steal things and run off with them. There was also a large Newfoundland dog named Neptune. Newfoundland dogs were strong swimmers and loyal companions. Some accounts also mention a cat. In letters sent home from Greenland, officers described these animals with affection. Even so, we do not know what happened to them later. They disappeared along with the rest of the expedition. Yet they show a gentle side of life aboard. Far from home, the men took comfort in the company of animals, who knew nothing of the dangers ahead.

[long-pause]

{img:07-officer-writing-letter-cabin-greenland-1845}

The last letters from the expedition were sent home from Greenland in July 1845. In early July 1845, the ships stopped at a small harbour on the west coast of Greenland. A supply ship that had sailed with them unloaded fresh food. Then it prepared to return to England. This was the men's last chance to send letters home. Many wrote long ones. Commander James Fitzjames of Erebus wrote a kind of journal letter to his family. He described the officers, the ships and the daily life aboard. He wrote warmly about Sir John Franklin. He said Franklin was cheerful, kind and full of energy. Other men wrote to wives, mothers and children. Five men were sent home on the supply ship, mostly because of illness. They were the lucky ones. Even so, the letters were full of hope. The men expected to be home in two or three years. Yet these letters became the last words most families ever received.

[long-pause]

{img:08-bomb-vessel-hms-terror-strong-hull-dock}

Erebus and Terror were built as bomb ships, with very strong hulls designed to carry heavy mortars. Erebus and Terror were not ordinary ships. They had been built as bomb vessels. These were warships designed to carry heavy mortars, which fired explosive shells high into the air. To carry such heavy weapons, the ships needed very strong hulls. Their wooden frames were thick and closely spaced. This strength made them good for the Arctic, where ice could crush a ship. Terror was the older of the two. She had been built in 1813. She even took part in the War of 1812 against the United States. Erebus was built in 1826. Both ships were later converted for exploration. Their hulls were strengthened with extra layers of planking and iron plates on the bow. Even so, they were not large. Each was only about thirty metres long. Yet they had already proved themselves. Before Franklin, both ships had sailed to the Antarctic and returned safely.

[long-pause]

{img:09-antarctic-volcano-mount-erebus-ice-sea}

A volcano in Antarctica is named after the ship Erebus. Before their Arctic voyage, Erebus and Terror made a famous journey to the far south. From 1839 to 1843, they sailed to Antarctica under the command of James Clark Ross. Francis Crozier, who later sailed with Franklin, commanded Terror. They explored waters no ship had reached before. They discovered a huge shelf of ice, taller than the masts of the ships. It is now called the Ross Ice Shelf. They also saw two great volcanoes rising from the ice. Ross named them after his ships. They are still called Mount Erebus and Mount Terror. Mount Erebus is an active volcano. It still sends up steam and smoke today. Even so, the Antarctic voyage was very dangerous. The ships were nearly lost several times in storms and among icebergs. Yet they came home safely after four years. Their success made the Admiralty confident that they could also conquer the Arctic.

[long-pause]

{img:10-railway-locomotive-engine-installed-ship-hold}

Each ship was fitted with an engine taken from a railway locomotive. The Franklin expedition used the newest technology of its time. Each ship was given a steam engine. These were not ship engines. They were engines from railway locomotives, adapted for use at sea. The engine for Terror came from the London and Birmingham Railway. The engine for Erebus came from the London and Greenwich Railway. They were placed deep in the hold of each ship. They turned a propeller at the back. The propeller could be lifted out of the water to protect it from the ice. The engines were not powerful. They could push the ships at only about four knots in calm water. They also used a lot of coal. Even so, they were useful for short distances, when there was no wind. Yet the idea was very modern. In 1845, steam power at sea was still quite new. These ships combined old wooden sailing craft with the machinery of the railway age.

[long-pause]

{img:11-hot-water-pipes-heating-ship-lower-deck}

The ships had a heating system that sent warm water through pipes under the decks. Keeping warm was a matter of life and death in the Arctic. Erebus and Terror had a special heating system. The boiler of the steam engine heated water. The hot water flowed through iron pipes running under the decks where the men lived. This warmed the air and helped keep it dry. It also helped prevent dampness, which caused illness. The system was also used to melt snow and ice to make fresh water. This saved the men from having to melt it over fires. In the coldest months, the decks were also covered with a canvas roof. Snow was banked against the outside of the hull for extra insulation. Even so, the ships were never truly warm. Frost formed on the inside of the walls. The men wore thick wool clothes even below deck. Yet the heating made a great difference. It was one more way in which this expedition was better equipped than any before it.

[long-pause]

{img:12-ice-sheathing-bow-iron-plates-wooden-ship}

The bows of the ships were covered with iron plates to help them break through ice. The Arctic sea is full of ice, even in summer. Ships had to push through it or wait for it to move. Ice could damage a wooden hull or crush it completely. So Erebus and Terror were strengthened. Their bows, at the front, were covered with heavy iron plates. This protected them when they pushed into loose ice. Inside, the hulls were braced with extra timber beams. The decks were doubled with extra layers of wood. The ships were among the strongest wooden vessels ever built. Even so, no wooden ship could resist heavy pack ice forever. When the ice closed in from all sides, it could squeeze a ship until the timbers cracked. The officers knew this. Yet they trusted their ships. When Parks Canada later found Terror on the seabed, the hull was still remarkably intact. The strong old ship had survived more than a hundred and sixty years underwater.

[long-pause]

{img:13-daguerreotype-camera-officer-sitting-portrait-1845}

Before leaving, the officers had their portraits taken with a new invention called the daguerreotype. Photography was very new in 1845. The first practical method, the daguerreotype, had been introduced only six years earlier. It made a single, very sharp image on a polished silver plate. Before the expedition sailed, a photographer named Richard Beard came to the ships. He made daguerreotypes of Sir John Franklin and many of the officers. The men had to sit still for several seconds while the image formed. Some look serious. Others look relaxed. Franklin is shown in his uniform, wearing a cap. He looks tired but calm. These pictures are among the earliest photographs of any expedition. Even so, there are no photographs of the ordinary sailors. Only officers were photographed. Yet these images are precious. They show the faces of men who would vanish into the Arctic. Today, they are kept in museums in Britain. Visitors can look into the eyes of the officers who sailed with Franklin.

[long-pause]

{img:14-tin-cans-food-stores-ship-hold-1845}

The expedition carried food for three years, including thousands of tins of meat, soup and vegetables. Erebus and Terror were loaded with supplies for at least three years. There were tons of flour, salt beef and salt pork. There were barrels of biscuit, sugar, tea, chocolate and oatmeal. There was lemon juice, to protect against scurvy. Most modern of all were the tinned foods. Canning was still a new idea. The expedition carried thousands of tins of meat, soup and vegetables. They had been supplied by a man named Stephan Goldner. His company won the contract by offering a low price. The tins were made by hand and sealed with lead solder. Even so, there were problems. Goldner had to work quickly to fill the large order. Some tins were badly made. Later, other ships found that some of Goldner's tins had spoiled. Yet in 1845, tinned food was seen as a great advance. It meant that men could eat meat and vegetables in places where no fresh food could be found.

[long-pause]

{img:15-lemon-juice-bottles-scurvy-ship-store}

Lemon juice was carried to protect the sailors from scurvy. Scurvy was one of the great dangers of long voyages. It is caused by a lack of vitamin C. Sailors who ate only salt meat and biscuit for months began to suffer. Their gums became soft and bled. Old wounds opened. They grew weak and tired. By the 1840s, the Royal Navy knew that citrus juice helped prevent it. Each sailor received a daily ration of lemon juice, mixed with sugar and water. Franklin's ships carried large supplies of it. Even so, there was a problem. Lemon juice slowly loses its vitamin C over time, especially in warm conditions. After two or three years, it may have lost much of its power. Fresh meat also helps prevent scurvy. Inuit hunters stayed healthy by eating fresh seal and caribou. Yet the British sailors rarely ate as the Inuit did. Most historians believe that scurvy played a part in weakening Franklin's men, along with cold, hunger and other illnesses.

[long-pause]

{img:16-ship-library-shelves-books-cabin-1845}

The two ships carried libraries of more than two thousand books between them. The long Arctic winters left plenty of time for reading. So Erebus and Terror carried large libraries. Together, they held more than two thousand books. There were books on science, history, travel and navigation. There were Bibles, prayer books and sermons. There were novels and magazines, including humorous ones like Punch. There were books by Charles Dickens, whose stories were popular at the time. There were also accounts of earlier Arctic voyages. These may have helped the officers plan their route. Many books had been donated by friends and supporters of the expedition. Even so, no one knows exactly which books were read. Most were lost with the ships. Yet a few pages and book fragments were later found on King William Island. One was a small devotional book. These remains show that the men carried their books with them, even when they left the ships. Reading was a comfort to the very end.

[long-pause]

{img:17-christmas-dinner-ship-lower-deck-arctic}

On earlier Arctic expeditions, Christmas was celebrated with plum pudding, extra rations and music. Christmas was a special day on any Royal Navy ship. On Arctic expeditions, it was even more important. It broke up the long darkness of winter. We do not have records of Franklin's Christmases. Yet many earlier and later expeditions described theirs. Sailors decorated the lower deck with flags and paper. Officers gave extra rations of meat, plum pudding and sometimes beer or grog. The men sang songs, danced and played games. Some ships held plays or concerts. Officers sometimes joined the sailors for part of the evening. Franklin's ships carried plenty of supplies for such celebrations. They also had their barrel organs for music. Even so, Christmas in the Arctic could be bittersweet. The men thought of their families far away. Yet these celebrations helped keep spirits high. On a dark ship, frozen into the ice, a warm Christmas dinner was a reminder of home.

[long-pause]

{img:18-arctic-hunting-party-snow-musket-ptarmigan}

The sailors hunted for fresh meat, but game was often hard to find. To add to their supplies, Arctic expeditions often went hunting. Sailors took muskets and walked out across the ice and land. They hunted birds such as ptarmigan, ducks and geese. They hoped to find caribou, musk oxen or seals. Fresh meat was very welcome. It tasted better than salt meat, and it helped prevent scurvy. Franklin's ships carried guns and ammunition for this purpose. Yet hunting was not easy for men who had grown up in British towns. Arctic animals were wary and hard to approach. In winter, many birds flew south, and other animals were scarce. The Inuit were expert hunters. They knew where and when to find animals. They used methods developed over thousands of years. Even so, the British rarely learned these skills. Few expeditions of the time thought of learning from the Inuit. That was one of their greatest weaknesses in the far north.

[long-pause]

{img:19-sir-john-franklin-portrait-older-officer-uniform}

Sir John Franklin was fifty-nine years old when he led the expedition. John Franklin was born in 1786 in Lincolnshire, in the east of England. He went to sea as a boy. He fought at the battle of Copenhagen in 1801 and at the battle of Trafalgar in 1805. Later, he sailed on a voyage of exploration around Australia with his uncle by marriage, Matthew Flinders. He became fascinated by exploration. In the following decades, he led expeditions to the Arctic coasts of Canada. By 1845, he was famous. He was knighted, respected and much liked. People described him as kind, religious and gentle. He hated cruelty and did not like to punish men. Some thought he was too old for another Arctic voyage. He was fifty-nine and quite heavy. Even so, he was eager to go. He saw the Northwest Passage as the great prize of his life. Yet he also knew the dangers. He had seen men suffer in the Arctic before. He accepted the command with joy and determination.

[long-pause]

{img:20-franklin-earlier-expedition-canoes-arctic-river}

On an earlier expedition, Franklin and his men were so hungry that they ate the leather of their own boots. Franklin's first Arctic expedition took place from 1819 to 1822. He travelled overland through northern Canada to explore the Arctic coast. The journey was long and very difficult. On the way back, the expedition ran out of food. Winter came early. The men were forced to eat lichen scraped from rocks. They boiled and ate pieces of leather, including old shoes. Many men died of hunger and cold. Franklin survived, along with a few others. Back in England, he became famous as the man who ate his boots. The story made him a hero. People admired his courage and endurance. Even so, the expedition had been a terrible struggle. It showed how dangerous the Arctic could be. Yet Franklin did not give up. He returned to the Arctic in 1825 on a second, much more successful expedition. He mapped hundreds of miles of unknown coast. He learned a great deal about the north from these journeys.

[long-pause]

{img:21-van-diemens-land-government-house-hobart-1840}

Before his last voyage, Franklin served as governor of Van Diemen's Land, now called Tasmania. From 1837 to 1843, Franklin worked far from the Arctic. He was Lieutenant-Governor of Van Diemen's Land. This was a British colony on an island south of Australia, now called Tasmania. It was largely a penal colony, where convicts were sent from Britain. Franklin tried to be a fair and kind governor. He supported education and science. He founded a scientific society and helped start a museum. His wife, Jane, was also very active. She travelled widely around the island and supported many projects. Yet Franklin's time there was difficult. He clashed with local officials who opposed his ideas. In the end, he was recalled to Britain. He felt hurt and humiliated. Even so, his years in Tasmania are still remembered. Towns, streets and places there carry his name. Yet he wanted to restore his reputation. The Arctic expedition of 1845 gave him that chance.

[long-pause]

{img:22-lady-jane-franklin-portrait-study-letters}

Lady Jane Franklin spent years and much of her fortune searching for her husband. Jane Franklin was John Franklin's second wife. His first wife, Eleanor, had died young. Jane was intelligent, determined and well travelled. She had visited many countries and climbed mountains at a time when few women did such things. When the expedition did not return, she refused to give up hope. She wrote letters to the Admiralty, to newspapers and even to the President of the United States. She asked for help to search for her husband and his men. When the government's searches slowed, she paid for her own expeditions. She sold property and raised money from the public. Even so, she could not save her husband. He had died years earlier, though she did not know it. Yet her persistence led to important discoveries. One of the ships she paid for found the first written record of what had happened. Jane Franklin became one of the most famous women of her time.

[long-pause]

{img:23-crozier-captain-hms-terror-irish-officer}

Captain Francis Crozier of Terror was one of the most experienced polar sailors of his time. Francis Crozier was born in 1796 in Banbridge, in the north of Ireland. He joined the navy at thirteen. He sailed on several Arctic voyages under William Parry in the 1820s. Later he commanded Terror on the great Antarctic voyage with James Clark Ross. By 1845, he had more polar experience than almost anyone in the navy. On the Franklin expedition, he was second in command and captain of Terror. He was a quiet and serious man. In letters, he sometimes sounded worried about the expedition. He seems to have felt lonely and unsure. Even so, he was respected by his men. When Franklin died in 1847, Crozier took command of the whole expedition. He led the survivors when they left the ships in 1848. Yet his fate remains unknown. No grave or remains of Crozier have ever been identified. In Banbridge, a large monument stands in his memory.

[long-pause]

{img:24-fitzjames-commander-erebus-young-officer-smile}

James Fitzjames, the commander of Erebus, was known for his humour and his lively letters. James Fitzjames was a young and popular officer. He was about thirty-two in 1845. He had served bravely in a war in China and on a long expedition along the Euphrates River in the Middle East. He was handsome, cheerful and full of jokes. His friends loved him. On the Franklin expedition, he was the commander of Erebus, under Franklin himself. He wrote long letters home describing the voyage. They were full of humour and kind descriptions of his fellow officers. He described one officer as a clever and quiet man and another as very funny. He wrote that Franklin was delightful and full of energy. Even so, his letters stop in July 1845, in Greenland. Nothing more was heard from him. Yet in 2024, scientists identified some of his remains on King William Island, using DNA from a living relative. After almost one hundred and eighty years, Fitzjames had been found.

[long-pause]

{img:25-young-sailors-ship-deck-rigging-1845-thames}

Most of the sailors on Franklin's ships were young men in their twenties. The crews of Erebus and Terror were mostly young. Many of the sailors were in their twenties. Some were still teenagers. They came from all over Britain. Many were from London, Kent and the ports along the south and east coasts. Others came from Scotland, Ireland and the north of England. A few came from other countries. Some had sailed on whaling ships in the Arctic before. Others had never seen ice. They were chosen carefully. Officers looked for healthy, steady men with good records. Arctic service was popular, because it paid double wages. A sailor could send money home to his family. Even so, the men knew the risks. Arctic voyages were famous for their dangers. Yet many were eager to go. The expedition was the most famous voyage of its time. To be part of it was an honour. For a young sailor, it was also the adventure of a lifetime.

[long-pause]

{img:26-ice-master-whaler-old-sailor-telescope-crows-nest}

Each ship carried an experienced whaling captain, called an ice master, to guide it through the ice. The Royal Navy knew that its officers were not experts in Arctic ice. Whaling captains were. They sailed to the Arctic every year to hunt whales. They knew how ice moved and how to find a safe path through it. So each of Franklin's ships carried an ice master. On Terror, the ice master was Thomas Blanky. He had already sailed on several Arctic expeditions. On Erebus, it was James Reid, a whaler from Aberdeen in Scotland. Ice masters often spent hours in the crow's nest, a lookout high on the mast. From there, they could see far across the ice. They watched for open channels of water, called leads. Even so, the ice masters had no control over the weather. Some years, the ice simply did not open. Yet their skill was valued highly. On earlier voyages, ice masters had saved ships many times by choosing the right route through the floes.

[long-pause]

{img:27-ship-surgeon-cabin-microscope-specimens-jars}

Harry Goodsir, the assistant surgeon on Erebus, was also a talented naturalist. Each ship carried a surgeon and an assistant surgeon. On Erebus, the assistant surgeon was Harry Goodsir. He was a young Scottish doctor from Edinburgh, about twenty-five years old. He was also a keen naturalist. He was especially interested in small sea creatures. He brought a microscope, nets and jars on the voyage. In his letters from Greenland, he described the tiny animals he had collected from the sea. He was cheerful and enthusiastic. The other officers liked him very much. He hoped to make important scientific discoveries in the Arctic. Even so, his collections were lost with the ships. None of his Arctic notes have been found. Yet his letters show the curiosity of the men aboard. They were not only sailors and explorers. Some were also scientists, eager to learn about the natural world. Goodsir's brother John was a famous professor of anatomy, who later worried about him for years.

[long-pause]

{img:28-royal-marines-red-coats-ship-deck-1845}

A small group of Royal Marines sailed on each ship to keep order and help with the work. Royal Navy ships of the 1840s carried soldiers called Royal Marines. Franklin's ships each had a small group of them, led by a sergeant. They wore red coats, unlike the blue of the sailors. On a warship, marines guarded the officers and fought in battles. On an expedition, their role was quieter. They kept watch, guarded the stores and helped keep discipline. They also worked alongside the sailors, hauling ropes and pulling sledges. William Braine, one of the three men buried at Beechey Island, was a marine. Even so, marines and sailors did not always get along. There were old rivalries between them. Yet on a small ship, far from home, they had to live and work closely together. They shared the same food, the same cold and the same dangers. By the end, there was likely little difference between them. They were all simply members of the same lost company.

[long-pause]

{img:29-ships-boys-young-teenagers-deck-chores}

A few young boys sailed with the expedition as ship's boys. Among the crew were a few very young sailors. They were called ship's boys. They were usually in their middle or late teens. They ran errands, helped the cook, cleaned the decks and learned the ways of the sea. Many boys of the time joined the navy young. It was a common start for a sailor's career. On Franklin's ships, the boys were rated as second-class boys or first-class boys. Their names are recorded in the official lists of the crew. They were treated kindly by many of the older men, who looked out for them. Even so, life aboard was hard for them as for everyone. They worked long hours and slept in hammocks like the others. Yet they were also eager and full of hope. For them, the voyage was a chance to learn and rise in the world. They were among the youngest of all the men who never came home.

[long-pause]

{img:30-muster-list-ledger-names-ink-admiralty}

The names of all the men who sailed with Franklin are recorded in the navy's official lists. The Royal Navy kept careful records. For every ship, it kept a list called a muster book. It recorded the name, age, birthplace and rank of every man aboard. It also recorded their pay. The muster books of Erebus and Terror survive. They tell us who the men were. There were officers and engineers, carpenters and cooks, sailmakers and stokers. There were men who looked after the officers, and men who worked in the hold. The lists give the details of their lives in a few words. A sailor born in London, aged twenty-five. A marine from Devon, aged thirty-two. Even so, the lists cannot tell us what the men were like. They do not tell us about their hopes, their families or their humour. Yet they preserve their names. Because of them, historians can trace the families of the crew today. Many descendants still remember an ancestor who sailed into the ice and never returned.

[long-pause]

{img:31-beechey-island-cliffs-frozen-bay-two-ships-winter}

The expedition spent its first winter at a small, bleak island called Beechey Island. After leaving Greenland in the summer of 1845, the ships sailed west into the Arctic. They explored a channel to the north, then turned south. As winter came, they found a sheltered place to stop. It was a bay beside a small island called Beechey Island. The island is barren and rocky, with tall dark cliffs. Here, the ships were frozen in for the winter. The men built a few buildings on shore. There was a forge for metalwork, a storehouse and a workshop. They also set up a place for scientific observations. Searchers later found the remains of these buildings. They also found more than six hundred empty food tins, neatly stacked in a pile. Even so, the expedition left no written message here. This later puzzled searchers very much. Yet the remains at Beechey Island were the first clues to the fate of the expedition, found five years after it had disappeared.

[long-pause]

{img:32-three-graves-wooden-headboards-beechey-island}

Three young sailors died during the first winter and were buried at Beechey Island. The first winter was not easy. Three men died at Beechey Island. Their names were John Torrington, John Hartnell and William Braine. Torrington was only twenty years old. He was a stoker, who worked with the steam engine. Hartnell was an able seaman, and Braine was a Royal Marine. They were buried in graves dug into the frozen gravel of the island. Each grave had a wooden headboard, carved with the man's name, age and date of death. The headboards also carried short verses from the Bible. The graves were found by searchers in 1850. They still stand today, one of the most remote and moving cemeteries in the world. Even so, these three deaths were not unusual for a long Arctic voyage. Illness was common, and medicine was limited. Yet they were a sign of the hardships to come. For the families at home, these graves were the first real news of the lost expedition.

[long-pause]

{img:33-archaeologists-permafrost-tent-arctic-1984}

In the 1980s, scientists studied the bodies at Beechey Island and found them almost perfectly preserved by the ice. In 1984 and 1986, a team led by the Canadian scientist Owen Beattie went to Beechey Island. With permission, they carefully opened the three graves. The permanently frozen ground had preserved the bodies remarkably well. After nearly one hundred and forty years, the men looked almost as they had in life. The scientists took small samples of hair, bone and tissue. Then they reburied the men with care and respect. Tests showed high levels of lead in their bodies. Beattie suggested that lead from the solder in the food tins might have poisoned the men. It could have weakened them and clouded their judgement. This idea became very famous. Even so, later research has questioned it. Some newer studies suggest that the lead levels were not so unusual for people of that time. Many Victorians had high lead levels from water pipes and other sources. Yet the photographs of the men's faces became some of the most haunting images of the Arctic.

[long-pause]

{img:34-polar-night-ship-frozen-stars-aurora}

In the Arctic winter, the sun did not rise at all for weeks at a time. The far north has a very unusual year. In summer, the sun never sets. In winter, it never rises. Near Beechey Island, the sun disappeared below the horizon in early November. It did not return until early February. For about three months, the men lived in darkness. Around noon, there was only a faint glow on the southern horizon. The rest of the time, the sky was dark or lit by the moon and stars. Sometimes the northern lights danced across the sky in green and white curtains. The temperature could fall below minus forty degrees. Everything froze solid. Even so, life on board continued. The men kept a strict routine of meals, work, exercise and rest. They walked on the ice for fresh air. Yet the darkness was hard on the mind. Earlier explorers wrote about the gloom and boredom of the long night. The return of the sun in February was always a time of great joy.

[long-pause]

{img:35-canvas-roof-over-deck-snow-banks-ship}

In winter, the decks were covered with canvas roofs, and snow was piled against the hulls to keep out the cold. When a ship was frozen in for the winter, the crew prepared it carefully. The sails and upper rigging were taken down and stored. A large canvas cover was stretched over the deck like a roof. This created a sheltered space where men could walk and work out of the wind. Snow was piled high against the sides of the ship. Snow is a good insulator, and it helped keep the inside warmer. Paths were cut through the snow to the ice. A hole was kept open in the ice nearby, in case of fire. The men also built snow walls to shelter their work areas on the ice. Even so, the cold always found a way in. Breath froze on the walls of the cabins. Bedding became damp with frost. Yet these methods had been tested on earlier voyages. With them, ships and men could survive a long Arctic winter in reasonable comfort.

[long-pause]

{img:36-magnetic-observatory-hut-instruments-arctic}

The expedition was also a scientific voyage, taking careful measurements of magnetism and weather. Franklin's expedition was not only about finding a route. It was also about science. The ships carried many scientific instruments. The officers measured the earth's magnetism very carefully. In the 1840s, scientists around the world were studying how magnetism changed from place to place. This was important for navigation, because ships used magnetic compasses. Near the North Magnetic Pole, compasses behaved strangely. The expedition was asked to take regular readings. The officers also recorded the weather, temperature and air pressure. They collected plants, rocks and animals. They studied the ice and the tides. At Beechey Island, they set up a small observatory on shore. Even so, almost all their scientific records were lost. Yet their work was part of a great international effort. Observatories across the British Empire were taking the same measurements at the same time, trying to understand the invisible forces of the planet.

[long-pause]

{img:37-inuit-snow-house-igloo-family-lamp-light}

The Inuit had lived in the Arctic for centuries, building warm houses from blocks of snow. The Arctic was not an empty land. It was home to the Inuit. Their ancestors had lived there for about a thousand years. Near King William Island lived a group often called the Netsilik, the people of the seal. In winter, many Inuit families lived in snow houses. These were built from blocks of hard snow, cut with knives and stacked in a spiral to form a dome. Inside, the snow walls kept out the wind. A lamp made of soapstone burned seal oil. It gave light and warmth and was used for cooking. Sleeping platforms were covered with thick caribou furs. Even on very cold nights, a snow house could be surprisingly warm. Even so, life was hard. Food could be scarce in winter. Yet the Inuit knew how to survive in a land that defeated many European explorers. Their knowledge was the result of countless generations of careful learning.

[long-pause]

{img:38-inuit-hunter-seal-breathing-hole-ice-waiting}

Inuit hunters could wait for hours by a hole in the ice for a seal to come up to breathe. In winter, the sea near King William Island was covered with thick ice. Seals lived under the ice. They needed air, so they kept small breathing holes open. Inuit hunters searched for these holes with the help of their dogs. When a hunter found one, he waited beside it. He stood or sat completely still, sometimes for many hours, in bitter cold. He listened for the sound of a seal breathing below. When it came, he struck quickly with his harpoon. Seals were vital to the Inuit. They gave meat, fat for lamps, skin for clothing and boots, and much more. Even so, hunting was never certain. Some days brought nothing. Yet the hunters were patient and skilled. They read the ice, the wind and the behaviour of animals with great care. The British sailors on Franklin's ships had none of this knowledge. In the end, that difference was very important.

[long-pause]

{img:39-inuit-fur-clothing-caribou-parka-woman-sewing}

Inuit clothing made of caribou fur was much warmer than the wool worn by British sailors. Inuit women were expert tailors. They made clothing from the skins of caribou and seals. Caribou fur was especially warm. Each hair is hollow and traps air. Clothes were often made in two layers. The inner layer had the fur facing in, against the body. The outer layer had the fur facing out. Between them, warm air was held close to the skin. Parkas had hoods trimmed with fur to protect the face from wind. Boots of sealskin kept the feet dry and warm. The stitching was so fine that the seams were almost waterproof. It took great skill and many hours to make a full set of clothing. Even so, these clothes were light and flexible. The British sailors, by contrast, wore heavy wool coats and leather boots. These became wet and froze. Yet very few European explorers of the time adopted Inuit clothing. Those who did, like the explorer John Rae, travelled far more easily.

[long-pause]

{img:40-inuit-dog-sled-team-across-sea-ice}

The Inuit travelled long distances across the ice with dog sleds. For travel over snow and ice, the Inuit used sleds pulled by dogs. The sleds were built from wood, bone or even frozen fish wrapped in skins, when wood was scarce. The runners were coated with a layer of ice, made by smoothing on water, so that they glided easily. A team of dogs could pull heavy loads over long distances. The dogs were also useful for hunting. They could smell seals' breathing holes under the snow. The British, by contrast, usually pulled their sledges themselves. Teams of sailors in harness dragged heavy sledges across the ice. This was called man-hauling. It was exhausting, slow work. Even so, many British officers believed it was more honourable than using dogs. Yet this belief cost them dearly. When Franklin's men later left their ships, they dragged heavy sledges and boats across the land. The Inuit, travelling light with dogs, moved much more easily.

[long-pause]

{img:41-inuit-elder-telling-story-family-evening}

Inuit oral history kept the memory of Franklin's men alive for more than a century. The Inuit did not write books. They passed their history down by telling stories. Elders told children about important events, and those children told their own children. Some of these stories were about strange white men who came in two great ships. Inuit hunters saw the ships trapped in the ice. They met groups of sailors walking along the coast. They saw men who were weak and hungry. They found abandoned boats and camps. Later, they found one of the ships, sitting in the ice with no one aboard. These stories were told for generations. Even so, for a long time, many Europeans did not take them seriously. Yet the Inuit accounts were remarkably accurate. In the 2000s and 2010s, they helped searchers find both Erebus and Terror. An Inuit historian named Louie Kamookak spent his life collecting these stories. His work was a key part of the search.

[long-pause]

{img:42-polar-bear-walking-sea-ice-soft-light}

Polar bears often came close to the ships of Arctic expeditions. The polar bear is the largest land predator on earth. It lives on the sea ice of the Arctic, where it hunts seals. Arctic explorers often met polar bears. The bears were curious. Drawn by the smell of food, they sometimes walked right up to frozen ships. Sailors on earlier expeditions described bears climbing onto the ice beside their vessels. Some even tried to climb aboard. Polar bears have thick white fur and a layer of fat to keep them warm. Their fur looks white, but each hair is actually clear and hollow. Underneath, their skin is black, which helps absorb the warmth of the sun. They are strong swimmers and can travel great distances across the sea. Even so, the sailors usually feared them. Many bears were shot for food or for their skins. Yet the bears were also admired. Their strength and calm movements across the ice impressed many who saw them.

[long-pause]

{img:43-walrus-group-resting-ice-floe-arctic}

Walruses use their long tusks to pull themselves out of the water onto the ice. Walruses are large, heavy animals that live in the Arctic seas. A big male can weigh more than a tonne. Both males and females have long white tusks. They use them for many things. They hook them over the edge of the ice to pull their heavy bodies out of the water. They also use them in fights and to show their strength. Walruses feed on the seabed. They search for clams and shellfish with their sensitive whiskers. They suck the soft meat from the shells. They often rest together in large groups on floating ice or beaches. They grunt, bellow and lie close together. Even so, walruses can be dangerous if disturbed. They have been known to attack small boats. The Inuit hunted them carefully for meat, oil and ivory. Yet for the sailors on Arctic expeditions, a group of walruses lying on the ice was one of the most remarkable sights of the north.

[long-pause]

{img:44-narwhals-tusks-surface-calm-arctic-water}

The narwhal's long spiral tusk is actually a tooth that grows through its upper lip. The narwhal is one of the strangest animals of the Arctic. It is a small whale, about four or five metres long. Most males have a single long tusk that spirals forward from the head. It can grow up to three metres long. The tusk is actually a tooth. It grows out through the upper lip. Scientists are still not completely sure what it is used for. It may help males attract females or show their status. It is also full of nerve endings and may help sense the water. In medieval Europe, narwhal tusks were sold as the horns of unicorns. They were very valuable and believed to have magic powers. Even so, the Inuit knew the truth. They hunted narwhals for food and used the ivory. Yet the legend of the unicorn lasted for centuries. Sailors on Arctic voyages were often amazed to see narwhals in the water, with their long tusks rising from the waves.

[long-pause]

{img:45-arctic-fox-white-winter-coat-snow}

The arctic fox changes the colour of its fur from brown in summer to white in winter. The arctic fox is a small animal, about the size of a large cat. It is perfectly suited to the cold. In winter, its thick fur turns white. This helps it hide against the snow. In summer, the fur turns brown or grey to match the rocks and tundra. Its short ears and legs help it lose less heat. When it sleeps, it curls up and wraps its bushy tail around its face like a blanket. Arctic foxes can survive temperatures far below minus forty degrees. They often follow polar bears to eat the leftovers of their meals. They also hunt lemmings, birds and eggs. Sailors on Arctic expeditions often saw foxes around their ships. Some even tamed them as pets. During one of the later search voyages, sailors caught foxes and fixed small copper collars with messages around their necks, hoping Franklin's men might find them. Even so, few such messages were ever recovered. Yet the little foxes became familiar companions in the long Arctic winters.

[long-pause]

{img:46-musk-oxen-herd-tundra-snow-shaggy}

Musk oxen have such thick wool that they can survive the coldest Arctic winters in the open. Musk oxen are large animals with long, shaggy coats. They look a little like small bison. Their outer hair hangs almost to the ground. Underneath, they have a layer of very soft, warm wool. The Inuit call it qiviut. It is one of the warmest natural fibres in the world, many times warmer than sheep's wool. Musk oxen live in small herds on the tundra. In winter, they dig through the snow with their hooves to find plants. When wolves threaten them, the adults form a circle, facing outwards, with the young in the middle. This defence works well against wolves. Even so, it worked poorly against hunters with guns. In the 1800s, many musk oxen were killed. Arctic explorers hunted them for fresh meat. Yet the animals survived. Today, musk oxen are protected in many areas. Their soft qiviut wool is gathered after it falls out naturally each spring and is knitted into very warm clothing.

[long-pause]

{img:47-caribou-herd-crossing-tundra-river-summer}

Every year, great herds of caribou travel hundreds of miles across the Arctic tundra. Caribou are the wild reindeer of North America. They live on the tundra and in the northern forests. Each year, many herds make long journeys. In spring, they travel north to the tundra, where the females give birth. In autumn, they return south to spend the winter in more sheltered areas. Some herds travel more than a thousand kilometres a year. Both male and female caribou grow antlers, which is unusual among deer. Caribou were vital to the Inuit. They provided meat, fur for clothing, sinew for thread and bone for tools. Their winter fur was the warmest of all for clothing. Even so, caribou herds were not always easy to find. Their routes changed from year to year. Yet when the herds came, they brought plenty. The Inuit hunted them carefully, using every part of the animal. Franklin's men may have hoped to find caribou on their long march south, but the herds were often far away.

[long-pause]

{img:48-snow-buntings-birds-spring-tundra-rocks}

In spring, the return of the birds was a joyful sign that the Arctic winter was ending. During the long Arctic winter, very few birds remain in the far north. Most fly south to warmer places. Then, in spring, they return. One of the first to arrive is the snow bunting, a small bird with white and black feathers. Its cheerful song was a welcome sound after months of silence. Soon, geese, ducks, gulls and many other birds follow. The Arctic tern makes the longest journey of all. It flies from the Antarctic to the Arctic and back every year. Sailors on Arctic expeditions often wrote about the joy of seeing the first birds of spring. It meant that the sun was returning and the ice might soon melt. Hunting parties also had a chance to find fresh food again. Even so, spring in the Arctic is short. Yet those first small birds carried great hope. For Franklin's men in the spring of 1848, they may have been one of the last gentle signs of life.

[long-pause]

{img:49-ships-sailing-narrow-channel-ice-floes-summer-1846}

In the summer of 1846, the ships sailed south towards King William Island. When the ice melted in the summer of 1846, the ships left Beechey Island. Franklin followed his orders and sailed south and west. He went down a long channel towards King William Island. This was unknown water. No ship had been there before. Franklin may have hoped to reach the coast of North America. From there, he could sail west along a route that he and others had already mapped. That route would lead to the Pacific. It was a good plan, based on the knowledge of the time. Even so, the maps were incomplete. Franklin believed King William Island was joined to the mainland. In fact, it is an island, with a channel on its eastern side. That channel stays clearer of ice. Yet Franklin went down the western side. There, a great stream of heavy ice flows down from the north every year, pressing against the coast.

[long-pause]

{img:50-ships-beset-pack-ice-victoria-strait-autumn}

In September 1846, the ships were trapped in the ice off King William Island, and they never sailed again. In September 1846, Erebus and Terror reached the waters northwest of King William Island. There, the ice closed in around them. They were beset, which means trapped by ice. The ships could not move forward or back. They drifted slowly with the ice, as it was pushed by winds and currents. This was not unusual in the Arctic. Ships were often trapped for a winter and then freed the next summer. The officers probably expected to be released in 1847. Yet in this area, the ice did not always melt. Heavy, thick ice flowed down from the north, year after year. In some summers, it did not break up at all. Even so, the expedition had plenty of food for now. The ships were strong. Yet they would never sail again. They stayed locked in that ice for about a year and a half. The men spent two winters there, waiting for a summer that never freed them.

[long-pause]

{img:51-officers-sledge-party-coast-king-william-island}

In the spring of 1847, a small party left the ships to explore the coast of King William Island. While the ships were trapped, the officers did not simply wait. In May 1847, a party of eight men set out from the ships. They were led by Lieutenant Graham Gore and a mate named Charles Des Voeux. They dragged a sledge across the ice to King William Island. Their task was to explore the coast and leave messages. On the way, they built small piles of stones, called cairns. Inside one, they left a note in a metal container. The note said that the ships were trapped, that the expedition had wintered at Beechey Island, and that all was well. It also said that Sir John Franklin was in command. Gore and his men returned safely to the ships. Even so, they may have seen something important. From the coast, they may have glimpsed the channel that would have completed the Northwest Passage. Yet the ships were stuck in the ice, unable to follow it.

[long-pause]

{img:52-franklin-cabin-quiet-bunk-lamp-1847}

Sir John Franklin died aboard Erebus on the eleventh of June 1847. Only a few weeks after Gore's party left that hopeful note, the expedition suffered a great loss. Sir John Franklin died on board Erebus. The date was the eleventh of June 1847. He was sixty-one years old. The cause of his death is unknown. It may have been a stroke, a heart problem or an illness. He had been in command for two years. His men respected and loved him. Command now passed to Francis Crozier, captain of Terror. Where Franklin was buried remains a mystery. He may have been buried at sea, through a hole in the ice. He may have been buried on land. Some think his body might still be on board one of the ships. Even so, no grave has ever been found. Yet his death was a heavy blow. The expedition had lost its leader, its most famous member and a calm, steady presence. The hardest times were still ahead.

[long-pause]

{img:53-second-winter-frozen-ships-dim-twilight-1847}

The men spent a second long winter trapped in the ice, as supplies began to run low. The summer of 1847 was cold. The ice around the ships did not melt. The ships stayed trapped. The men had to prepare for another winter in the same place. This was their third Arctic winter in total. The darkness returned in November. The cold was intense. By now, the supplies were running down. The expedition had carried food for three years, but it had been away for more than two. Fresh meat was scarce. Scurvy may have begun to appear. Some men fell ill and died. Even so, the officers kept the routine going. The men still had their duties, their meals and their prayers. The barrel organs may still have played on Sundays. Yet the mood must have changed. The hope of sailing on was fading. The officers began to think about leaving the ships altogether, and walking south towards the mainland of Canada, where there were rivers and fur trading posts.

[long-pause]

{img:54-stone-cairn-note-metal-cylinder-shore}

In 1859, searchers found a note in a stone cairn that told the fate of the expedition. For years, no one knew what had happened to Franklin's men. Then, in May 1859, a search party made a vital discovery. It was led by Lieutenant William Hobson. He was part of an expedition paid for by Lady Franklin and commanded by Francis McClintock. On the northwest coast of King William Island, at a place called Victory Point, Hobson found a stone cairn. Inside it was a metal cylinder. In the cylinder was a printed form. Such forms were carried by naval ships. On them, captains wrote their position and asked whoever found the note to send it to the Admiralty. The form had been written on twice. The first message, from May 1847, was the hopeful note left by Gore. The second message was written around the edges a year later. It was very different. Even so, the note was short, only a few lines. Yet it is the only written record ever found from the Franklin expedition.

[long-pause]

{img:55-handwritten-note-margins-paper-close-up}

The second message said the ships had been abandoned on the twenty-second of April 1848. The second message on the Victory Point note was written in April 1848. It was signed by Captain Crozier and Captain Fitzjames. It said that the ships had been deserted on the twenty-second of April. They had been trapped in the ice since September 1846. It said that Sir John Franklin had died on the eleventh of June 1847. It said that nine officers and fifteen men had died so far. The remaining men, one hundred and five in all, had landed on King William Island. They planned to set out the next day for Back's Fish River. This was a river on the Canadian mainland, far to the south. Even so, the message hid much of what must have been happening. It did not say why so many had died. Yet its words were enough to break hearts in Britain. For the first time, people knew that Franklin was dead and that his men had left the ships.

[long-pause]

{img:56-abandoned-boat-on-sledge-snowy-shore}

Searchers found a ship's boat on a sledge, loaded with a strange mix of objects. During the same search in 1859, McClintock and Hobson found other remains. On the coast of King William Island, they discovered a large wooden boat. It was mounted on a heavy sledge, built to be dragged across the ice. Inside the boat, they found many objects. There were tools, guns, clothing and blankets. There were books, including Bibles and a copy of a popular novel. There were silver forks and spoons, many marked with the crests of officers. There were soap, towels, slippers and small personal treasures. The boat and sledge together were very heavy. McClintock was puzzled. Why had exhausted men dragged such things across the snow? Even so, the answer may be simple. The men may have expected to need these things later, or they could not bear to leave them behind. Yet the heavy loads must have slowed them terribly on their long march south.

[long-pause]

{img:57-inuit-testimony-explorer-listening-tent}

Inuit witnesses told of seeing groups of weak, hungry sailors walking along the coast. Several explorers spoke with the Inuit about Franklin's men. One of the first was a Scottish doctor named John Rae. In 1854, he met Inuit who told him about a group of white men who had died far to the south. They showed him objects they had collected, including silver spoons and a medal. Later, the American explorer Charles Francis Hall spent years living with the Inuit. He recorded many stories. The Inuit described meeting groups of sailors walking across the ice. The men were very thin and weak. Some fell down as they walked. The Inuit sometimes shared seal meat with them. But they could not feed so many people. They had to look after their own families. Even so, some of these accounts were painful to British readers. Many refused to believe them. Yet historians now see the Inuit testimony as the most important source for the final days of the expedition.

[long-pause]

{img:58-lone-cairn-headland-sunset-king-william-island}

None of the one hundred and five men who left the ships in 1848 is known to have survived. The men who left the ships in April 1848 set out to walk south. It was a journey of hundreds of miles. They faced cold, hunger and illness. They dragged heavy boats and sledges. Along the coast of King William Island, searchers later found remains of their camps and graves. Some men may have turned back towards the ships. Inuit stories suggest that some men may have survived for several more years. They speak of a ship that was manned again, and of men seen far to the south. Even so, none of them ever reached a European outpost. None of them came home. The exact story of their last days is still not fully known. Yet the courage of the men is clear. They kept going in impossible conditions. They helped each other and carried their sick. Their long walk south is one of the most moving stories of exploration. It ended quietly, somewhere in the snow.

[long-pause]

{img:59-old-map-arctic-unknown-coast-candle}

For more than three hundred years, Europeans searched for a sea route around the top of North America. The idea of the Northwest Passage was very old. In the 1500s, European merchants wanted to trade with China and India. The routes around Africa and South America were long and controlled by Spain and Portugal. So English and Dutch sailors looked for another way. They hoped to sail west and north, around the top of North America, straight to Asia. This imagined route was called the Northwest Passage. In 1576, Martin Frobisher sailed to the Arctic to look for it. He returned with black rock he believed contained gold. It did not. Other explorers followed. Each mapped a little more of the frozen coasts. Even so, the passage proved very hard to find. The Arctic islands formed a maze of channels, most blocked by ice. Yet the dream did not die. By the 1800s, finding the passage had become a matter of national pride for Britain.

[long-pause]

{img:60-henry-hudson-small-ship-bay-ice-1610}

The explorer Henry Hudson searched for the passage and gave his name to a great bay in Canada. Henry Hudson was an English explorer of the early 1600s. He made several voyages to find a route to Asia. In 1609, sailing for the Dutch, he explored the river in New York that now carries his name. In 1610, he sailed again for England, further north. He entered a huge bay in northern Canada. He believed he had found the passage to the Pacific. But it was a dead end, a vast inland sea surrounded by land. The ship was trapped in the ice for the winter. In the spring, the hungry and unhappy crew turned against him. They put Hudson, his young son and a few loyal men into a small boat and left them. They were never seen again. Even so, the bay still bears his name. It is called Hudson Bay. Yet it also became the centre of a great fur trade. The Hudson's Bay Company, founded in 1670, still exists today.

[long-pause]

{img:61-parry-ships-winter-harbour-melville-island-1820}

In 1819, William Parry sailed further west through the Arctic than anyone before him. After the wars with Napoleon ended in 1815, the British navy had many ships and officers but little to do. A powerful official at the Admiralty, John Barrow, pushed for new voyages of exploration. One of the most successful was led by William Parry. In 1819, he sailed west through the channel north of the Canadian mainland. He travelled much further than any ship before. His ships wintered at Melville Island. To keep the men cheerful, Parry organised a ship's newspaper and a theatre. The men performed plays in the darkness of winter. The next summer, Parry tried to go further west. But heavy ice blocked the way. Even so, his voyage was a great success. Parliament gave him a prize for crossing a certain line of longitude. Yet the remaining passage proved even harder. That same heavy ice would later trap Franklin's ships, further south, nearly thirty years later.

[long-pause]

{img:62-admiralty-boardroom-london-maps-officers-1845}

By 1845, only a short stretch of the Northwest Passage remained unmapped. By the 1840s, explorers had mapped much of the Arctic. Franklin himself had charted hundreds of miles of the northern coast of Canada. Others had done the same from the east and west. On the map, only a short gap remained. It lay somewhere around King William Island. If a ship could sail through that gap, the Northwest Passage would be complete. The Admiralty believed that the right expedition could do it in a single season, or perhaps two. That is why Franklin's ships were so well equipped. The passage would not be useful for trade. It was too far north and too full of ice. Even so, finding it mattered to Britain. It was a question of science, honour and prestige. Yet the gap that looked so small on the map was one of the hardest places in the Arctic. The ice there rarely cleared. The Admiralty did not know that in 1845.

[long-pause]

{img:63-search-ships-arctic-1850-many-sails-ice}

More than thirty expeditions searched for Franklin and his men between 1848 and 1859. When the expedition did not return by 1847, people in Britain began to worry. The first search expeditions set out in 1848. When they found nothing, more followed. Over the next eleven years, more than thirty expeditions went looking for Franklin. Some were sent by the Royal Navy. Others were paid for by Lady Franklin. One was even sent by the United States, funded by a wealthy merchant named Henry Grinnell. Ships came from the Atlantic and from the Pacific. Sledge teams travelled thousands of miles over the ice. The search became one of the largest in history. Even so, the searchers often looked in the wrong places. They did not know which way Franklin had gone. Yet their work had another result. They mapped huge areas of the Canadian Arctic. Many islands, straits and capes were named during these searches. The search for Franklin filled in the map of the north.

[long-pause]

{img:64-sledge-party-man-hauling-snow-wind}

Search parties dragged heavy sledges for hundreds of miles across the frozen sea. Much of the searching was done on foot. In spring, when the days grew longer, teams of sailors left their ships. They pulled sledges loaded with tents, food and fuel. Each sledge carried supplies for weeks. The men were harnessed to it like horses. They pulled for many hours a day. The work was brutally hard. They crossed rough ice ridges and deep snow. They suffered frostbite and snow blindness. At night, they slept in tents on the ice, wrapped in blankets and buffalo robes. Some sledge journeys covered more than a thousand miles. Even so, the men were proud of their work. Each sledge had its own flag and name, often with a motto. Some of these silk flags still survive. Yet for all their effort, the sledge teams found few traces of Franklin. The places where his men had travelled were further south, in areas no one had thought to search.

[long-pause]

{img:65-john-rae-explorer-fur-clothing-snowshoes}

John Rae, a Scottish doctor, travelled the Arctic like the Inuit and brought the first news of Franklin's fate. John Rae was a doctor from the Orkney Islands in Scotland. He worked for the Hudson's Bay Company in Canada. Unlike most British explorers, Rae learned from the Indigenous peoples of the north. He wore fur clothing, used snowshoes and built snow houses. He hunted for his own food and travelled light. He could cover great distances very quickly. In 1854, while mapping the coast, he met Inuit who had news of Franklin's men. They told him that a large group of white men had died, several years before, far to the west. They showed him objects that had belonged to the expedition. Rae bought them and carried them home. Even so, his report caused anger in England. It included painful details that many people did not want to believe. Yet Rae was honest about what he had been told. He also discovered the strait that completes the passage along the coast. Today it is called Rae Strait.

[long-pause]

{img:66-mcclintock-fox-small-yacht-arctic-1857}

Lady Franklin bought a small steam yacht called the Fox to continue the search. By the late 1850s, the government had given up the search. Britain had been fighting a war in the Crimea. The Admiralty declared that Franklin and his men had died in service. Yet Lady Franklin did not stop. With her own money and gifts from the public, she bought a small steam yacht called the Fox. She asked Francis McClintock, an experienced Arctic officer, to command it. In 1857, the Fox sailed for the Arctic. It was trapped in the ice for its first winter and drifted for months. In 1859, McClintock and his men finally reached King William Island. They used sledges to search its coasts. They spoke with Inuit, who sold them silver spoons and other items. It was during this search that Hobson found the note at Victory Point. Even so, the Fox expedition did not find the ships. Yet it answered the question Lady Franklin had asked for years. It confirmed that her husband had died in 1847.

[long-pause]

{img:67-investigator-ship-abandoned-mercy-bay-ice}

The crew of one search ship became the first people to travel the whole Northwest Passage, partly on foot. One of the search ships was called HMS Investigator. It was commanded by Robert McClure. In 1850, it entered the Arctic from the Pacific side, near Alaska. It sailed east and reached the area that Parry had reached from the other side thirty years earlier. McClure realised that the Northwest Passage existed. But his ship became trapped in the ice in a bay he named Mercy Bay. The men spent three winters there. They suffered from hunger and scurvy. In 1853, men from another search expedition found them. The crew abandoned Investigator and walked across the ice to the rescue ships. They then sailed home to England. Even so, they had not sailed the whole way. Yet they had crossed from the Pacific to the Atlantic. McClure and his men received a prize from Parliament as the first to complete the passage. In 2010, archaeologists found Investigator, still resting on the bottom of Mercy Bay.

[long-pause]

{img:68-victorian-newspaper-reading-room-franklin-news}

The fate of Franklin's expedition fascinated the Victorian public for decades. The disappearance of Franklin's expedition became one of the great stories of the Victorian age. Newspapers followed every search. People bought prints, books and songs about the lost ships. Ballads such as Lady Franklin's Lament became popular. Exhibitions displayed objects found in the Arctic. When John Rae brought back his report in 1854, it caused a storm. The famous writer Charles Dickens wrote articles rejecting the Inuit accounts. Later, Dickens and his friend Wilkie Collins wrote a play inspired by the Arctic, called The Frozen Deep. Dickens himself acted in it. Even so, interest never faded. Painters made dramatic pictures of the frozen north. Poets wrote verses about the lost men. Yet the families of the sailors suffered most. Many waited years for news. Some never knew exactly how their loved ones had died. For them, the Franklin story was not an adventure. It was a deep, quiet grief.

[long-pause]

{img:69-franklin-memorial-westminster-abbey-marble}

A memorial to Franklin in Westminster Abbey calls him the discoverer of the Northwest Passage. After his death became known, Franklin was honoured as a national hero. Statues were raised in London, in his hometown of Spilsby and in Tasmania. In 1875, a memorial was placed in Westminster Abbey. Its words were written by the poet Alfred Tennyson, who was Franklin's nephew by marriage. The poem begins with the words, not here. It says that Franklin's body is not in the Abbey, but somewhere in the Arctic. It calls him a heroic sailor-soul, now passing on a happier voyage. In 2009, a memorial to the men of the expedition was rededicated in the Abbey. It also honoured the Inuit and others who had helped in the search. Even so, the claim that Franklin discovered the passage is debated. His ships did not sail through it. Yet his men may have been the first to see the final link. In that sense, many still give him credit for the discovery.

[long-pause]

{img:70-gjoa-small-sloop-arctic-amundsen-1904}

The first ship to sail through the whole Northwest Passage was a tiny fishing boat called the Gjøa. After Franklin, many decades passed before anyone sailed the whole passage. The man who finally did it was Roald Amundsen, from Norway. In 1903, he set out in a small boat called the Gjøa. It was an old herring boat, only about twenty-one metres long. It had a crew of just seven men. Amundsen chose a small ship on purpose. It could pass through shallow channels that larger ships could not enter. He also studied the mistakes of earlier expeditions. He sailed down the eastern side of King William Island, through the channel that Franklin had not known about. His ship spent two winters in a sheltered harbour there. Even so, Amundsen did not hurry. He used the time to learn from the local Inuit. In 1906, the Gjøa finally reached Alaska. The Northwest Passage had been sailed at last, sixty-one years after Franklin set out.

[long-pause]

{img:71-gjoa-haven-inuit-amundsen-learning-skills}

Amundsen learned to wear fur clothing and drive dog teams from the Inuit he met in the Arctic. During his two winters on King William Island, Amundsen lived near Inuit families. He watched them closely. He learned how they made their warm, light fur clothing. He learned how they built snow houses and drove dog sleds. He learned how they hunted seals and caribou. He traded with them for clothing and equipment. He later said that these lessons were very valuable. Even so, he did not always describe the Inuit fairly in his writings. Yet he clearly respected their skills. He used what he learned on his later journeys. In 1911, he became the first person to reach the South Pole. His team used dogs and fur clothing, just as he had learned in the north. The place where Amundsen wintered is now an Inuit community. It is called Gjoa Haven, after his little ship. Many of the people who live there today remember the stories of the explorers and the old ships.

[long-pause]

{img:72-modern-ship-arctic-passage-summer-open-water}

Today, because the Arctic ice is shrinking, ships can sometimes pass through the Northwest Passage in summer. For centuries, the Northwest Passage was almost impossible to sail. Thick ice blocked the channels for most of the year. In recent decades, that has begun to change. As the climate has warmed, the summer sea ice in the Arctic has become thinner and smaller. In some summers, the passage is almost clear of ice. Small boats, research ships and even large cruise ships have sailed through it. In 2016, a cruise ship carrying around a thousand passengers made the journey. This would have astonished Franklin and his officers. Even so, the passage is still dangerous. Ice conditions change from year to year. The weather is harsh, and help is far away. Yet the change is striking. The route that defeated Franklin and so many others can now be crossed by tourists. For many people, it is a powerful sign of how quickly the Arctic is changing.

[long-pause]

{img:73-parks-canada-search-boat-sonar-arctic-water}

For more than a hundred and fifty years, the wrecks of Erebus and Terror could not be found. After the ships were abandoned in 1848, they vanished. No European ever saw them again. For many years, searchers wondered where they had gone. Perhaps they had been crushed by the ice, or perhaps they had sunk where they were left. Inuit stories said that at least one ship had drifted south and sunk near an island. In 2008, the government of Canada began a new search. It was led by Parks Canada, the agency that looks after national parks and historic sites. Each summer, teams used ships, small boats and underwater sonar. Sonar sends sound waves into the water and listens for echoes from objects on the seabed. The search area was huge. The waters were shallow, cold and full of ice. Even so, the searchers kept trying year after year. They worked closely with Inuit and with historians like Louie Kamookak. Yet for six summers, they found nothing. Then, in 2014, everything changed.

[long-pause]

{img:74-underwater-wreck-erebus-seabed-bell-sunlight}

In September 2014, searchers found the wreck of Erebus on the seabed, only eleven metres down. On the second of September 2014, a Parks Canada sonar team saw something on their screen. It was the shape of a wooden ship, lying on the bottom of the sea. Divers went down to look. It was Erebus. She lay in shallow water in a bay south of King William Island. That was close to where Inuit stories had said a ship sank. The wreck was in fairly good condition. Parts of the upper deck had been damaged by ice. Yet much of the ship was still there. Divers soon raised the ship's bronze bell. It was marked with a broad arrow, the sign of the Royal Navy, and the date 1845. Even so, finding Erebus raised new questions. She lay much further south than the place where she was abandoned. It seemed that men may have sailed her there after she was first abandoned. Yet the discovery was a great moment. After almost one hundred and seventy years, Franklin's ship had been found.

[long-pause]

{img:75-inuit-hunter-sammy-kogvik-snowmobile-sea-ice}

An Inuit hunter's memory of a mast sticking out of the ice led searchers to the wreck of Terror. Two years later, the second ship was found. This time, the key clue came from an Inuit hunter named Sammy Kogvik. In 2016, he was working with a search expedition. He told the team about something he had seen years before. While hunting on the sea ice, he had noticed a large piece of wood sticking up through the ice. It looked like a ship's mast. He had taken a photograph, but he lost his camera. He had told few people about it. The search ship changed course and went to the place he described. It was a quiet bay on the southwest coast of King William Island. There, on the third of September 2016, they found Terror. Even so, the place was a surprise. It was far from where most experts had expected. Yet it matched Kogvik's memory exactly. By a quiet coincidence, the place had been named Terror Bay long before, by searchers who never knew the ship was there.

[long-pause]

{img:76-terror-wreck-interior-plates-shelves-underwater}

Inside the wreck of Terror, plates still stand on the shelves, and drawers remain closed. Terror was in amazing condition. She sat upright on the seabed, about twenty-four metres down. Her hull was almost complete. Her masts had broken, but they lay nearby. The hatches were closed, and the windows of the captain's cabin were still in place. In 2019, Parks Canada sent a small underwater drone inside the ship. It travelled through the dark rooms. The images it sent back were extraordinary. Plates and glasses still stood on the shelves of the officers' mess. Beds and desks were still in the cabins. Captain Crozier's cabin had a desk with drawers still closed. Fine silt covered everything, which can protect paper from decay. Even so, no documents have yet been recovered. Archaeologists hope that the closed drawers may contain logbooks, letters or charts. Yet they must work slowly and carefully. Each object could hold part of the story that has been missing for more than a century and a half.

[long-pause]

{img:77-divers-recovering-artifacts-cold-water-erebus}

Every summer, divers recover hundreds of objects from the wreck of Erebus. Since 2014, Parks Canada archaeologists have returned to Erebus almost every summer. The water is very cold, usually just above freezing. The diving season is short, only a few weeks long. Even so, the team has recovered many objects. There are plates, bottles, buttons, tools and pieces of clothing. There is a pair of epaulettes, the decorations worn on an officer's shoulders. There are small personal items, like a hairbrush that still had human hair in it. There is a wooden pencil case and pieces of a sailor's shoe. Each object is carefully recorded, preserved and studied. Some items have helped identify people. Scientists hope that DNA from objects like the hairbrush may be compared with living relatives. Yet the work is slow. It may take many more years to explore the whole ship. Each new object adds a small, human detail to the story of the men who sailed on her.

[long-pause]

{img:78-dna-lab-scientist-bone-sample-relatives}

Using DNA, scientists have begun to put names to the remains of some of Franklin's men. Over the years, remains of Franklin's men have been found on King William Island. Most were found without names. Then scientists began using DNA to identify them. They compared DNA from the remains with DNA from living descendants of the sailors' families. In 2021, a team from the University of Waterloo in Canada identified a man named John Gregory. He had been an engineer on Erebus. A descendant of his in South Africa gave a sample that matched. It was the first time one of the men had been identified this way. In 2024, the same team identified James Fitzjames, the commander of Erebus. Even so, many remains have still not been named. The researchers continue to ask relatives of the crew to come forward. Yet each identification is a gift to the families. For the first time in nearly one hundred and eighty years, they can know where their ancestor lies.

[long-pause]

{img:79-erebus-terror-national-historic-site-quiet-coast}

The wrecks of Erebus and Terror are now a national historic site, protected by Canada and Inuit together. Erebus and Terror belonged to the British Royal Navy. Yet they lay in Canadian waters, in the homeland of the Inuit. In 2018, Britain gave the ships to Canada and to the Inuit Heritage Trust. The two wrecks are now part of a protected national historic site. It is managed jointly by Parks Canada and Inuit. Inuit guardians watch over the sites. They live nearby and keep an eye on the waters, especially in summer. Visitors are not allowed to dive on the wrecks without permission. This protects them from damage and theft. Even so, the Inuit role is about more than protection. Their knowledge helped find the ships. Their stories kept the memory alive. Yet for a long time, that knowledge was ignored. Today, the partnership is seen as an important example. It shows how history can be shared and cared for by the people who live where it happened.

[long-pause]

{img:80-gjoa-haven-community-houses-bay-summer-evening}

In the village of Gjoa Haven, Inuit families still tell stories about the two great ships. Gjoa Haven is a small Inuit community on the southeast coast of King William Island. About thirteen hundred people live there. Many are descendants of the Inuit families who met Franklin's men, and later Amundsen. Stories about the ships and the explorers are part of local history. Children hear them from their grandparents. Some families have kept objects found long ago, such as pieces of wood, metal or cloth from the expedition. The village has a cultural centre and is home to Inuit guardians who help protect the wrecks. Louie Kamookak, the historian who did so much to help find Erebus, was from Gjoa Haven. He died in 2018. Even so, his work lives on. Many people in the village are proud of the role their community played. Yet life there is very much about the present. Families hunt, fish, work and go to school, in a place that is quiet, cold and beautiful.

[long-pause]

{img:81-arctic-tundra-summer-flowers-calm-lake}

In summer, the land where Franklin's men walked is covered with small flowers. The Arctic is not always white. For a short time in summer, the snow melts from the land. The tundra comes to life. Small flowers bloom close to the ground, white, yellow and purple. Mosses and lichens cover the rocks in many colours. Birds arrive from the south to nest. Caribou move across the land in great herds. The sun stays in the sky for twenty-four hours, circling low on the horizon. Shallow lakes reflect the endless light. King William Island in summer is quiet, wide and peaceful. Even so, the season is very short. By September, the cold returns. The flowers fade, and the birds fly south. Yet in those brief weeks, the land looks gentle. It is hard to imagine the hardship that once took place there. The men of the Franklin expedition walked across this same land, nearly two centuries ago, in the cold spring of 1848.

[long-pause]

{img:82-night-sky-stars-calm-frozen-sea-arctic}

More than one hundred and seventy years later, the Franklin expedition remains one of the great mysteries of exploration. The story of Franklin's lost expedition has never been fully told. Many questions remain. No one knows exactly why so many men died, where Franklin was buried or what happened to the last survivors. Records may still lie hidden inside the wrecks. Each new discovery answers some questions and raises others. Scientists, archaeologists and historians continue to study the remains. Inuit communities continue to share their knowledge. Books, plays, songs and films have retold the story many times. Even so, the heart of the story is simple. About one hundred and thirty men sailed into the Arctic. They were brave and hopeful. They faced cold, darkness and hunger far from home. None of them returned. Yet they are not forgotten. Their ships have been found. Some of their names have been given back to their remains. And across the frozen sea, under the stars, the land they walked remains quiet and still.

[long-pause]

And so the two ships rest at last, far beneath the northern ice.

[pause]

Above them, the sea lies white and silent. The wind moves softly over the low islands, and the snow settles on the old stone cairns along the shore. In a small village by the bay, the lamps glow behind the windows, and an old story is told once more to children who are almost asleep.

[pause]

The names, the dates and the long routes across the ice can all be set down now, like oars resting in a boat.
