{img:two-sailing-ships-arctic-sea-icebergs-summer-1845}
Tonight we are going about a hundred and eighty years back, to the frozen seas of the far north of Canada, in the summer of the year 1845.

[long-pause]

Two ships of the Royal Navy are sailing slowly westward, among the icebergs, toward a great maze of islands and channels, where no ship has ever been before. Their names are Erebus and Terror. On board, there are a hundred and twenty-nine men, and their leader is Sir John Franklin.

[pause]

They are looking for a way through the ice, across the top of the world. And they will never come home.

[pause]

Should you like these slow journeys into the past, you are welcome to subscribe to the channel, so that the next one finds you easily.

[long-pause]

Let us imagine the Arctic, as the men of Erebus and Terror saw it.

[pause]

Far to the north of Canada, above the Arctic Circle, there is a great scattering of islands, hundreds of them, large and small, separated by narrow channels and wide sounds of water. For most of the year, the water between the islands is frozen solid. The sun disappears in the winter, for weeks or months, and the temperature falls to forty or fifty degrees below zero.

[long-pause]

In the short summer, for a few weeks, the sun never sets. It circles around the sky, day and night, low over the horizon. The ice in the channels begins to break up into great floating fields and floes, which drift with the wind and the currents, opening and closing, crushing together, and drifting apart again.

[pause]

The land is bare and brown and grey, low and flat in some places, with high cliffs and mountains in others. There are no trees. There is moss, and lichen, and in summer, little flowers: purple saxifrage, and Arctic poppies, yellow and white, and Arctic cotton, waving in the wind.

[pause]

{img:arctic-tundra-summer-poppies-purple-saxifrage-low-sun}
And there are animals: polar bears, and seals, and walruses, and whales, and Arctic foxes, and musk oxen, and caribou, and great flocks of seabirds, in the summer, nesting on the cliffs.

[long-pause]

And there are people.

[pause]

The Inuit have lived here for a thousand years, and their ancestors for thousands of years before that. They know the ice, and the animals, and the weather, better than anyone. They hunt seals at their breathing holes in the ice, and caribou on the land, and fish in the rivers. They build houses of snow in winter, and tents of skin in summer. They travel on sledges, pulled by dogs, and in kayaks.

[pause]

To the Inuit, the Arctic is not a frozen wilderness. It is home.

[long-pause]

For more than three hundred years, the sailors of Europe have dreamed of finding a way through these icy seas.

[pause]

They call it the Northwest Passage: a sea route from the Atlantic Ocean to the Pacific Ocean, over the top of North America. If such a passage could be found, ships could sail from Europe to China and India, the lands of silk and spices and tea, far more quickly than by going all the way around Africa, or around South America.

[pause]

{img:old-sailing-ship-sixteenth-century-ice-floes-explorers}
Since the days of Queen Elizabeth the First, explorer after explorer has sailed into the Arctic, to look for it: Martin Frobisher, and John Davis, and Henry Hudson, and William Baffin, and many others. Their names are on the map today: Frobisher Bay, Davis Strait, Hudson Bay, Baffin Bay. But none of them found the way through.

[long-pause]

In the nineteenth century, after the end of the long wars against Napoleon, the British Royal Navy has many ships, and many officers, and nothing much for them to do.

[pause]

So, from 1818 onward, the Navy sends one expedition after another into the Arctic, to look for the Northwest Passage, and to map the coasts, and to study the magnetism of the earth, and the weather, and the animals and the plants. The man behind these expeditions is Sir John Barrow, the Second Secretary of the Admiralty, who is passionate about the Arctic.

[pause]

By 1845, the explorers have mapped much of the coast of northern Canada, from the east and from the west. Only a few hundred miles in the middle are still unknown. Barrow believes that one more expedition, well equipped, with the best ships and the best men, can find the last link, and sail through the Passage, and complete the work of three hundred years.

[long-pause]

And so, in the spring of 1845, the Admiralty prepares the greatest Arctic expedition ever sent out.

[pause]

Two strong ships, with steam engines, and heating, and food for three years, and the best officers and men of the Navy. It is the pride of Britain. Everyone is sure that it will succeed.

[pause]

{img:river-thames-1845-ships-moored-crowds-flags-spring}
The leader of the expedition is a man of fifty-nine, who has been to the Arctic before, and has become famous there. His name is Sir John Franklin.

[long-pause]

{img:sir-john-franklin-portrait-naval-uniform-kindly-face}
Sir John Franklin is a kind, gentle, rather stout man, with a round, friendly face, and thinning hair.

[pause]

He was born in 1786, in the little market town of Spilsby, in Lincolnshire, in the east of England, the ninth of twelve children of a shopkeeper. His family wanted him to become a clergyman. But when he was a boy, he walked to the coast, and saw the sea for the first time, and decided that he wanted to be a sailor.

[long-pause]

He joined the Navy at the age of fourteen.

[pause]

He sailed with his cousin, Matthew Flinders, on a voyage of exploration around Australia, and was shipwrecked on a coral reef, and rescued. And when he was nineteen, he was a signal midshipman on the ship Bellerophon, at the great sea battle of Trafalgar, in 1805, when Nelson defeated the French and Spanish fleets. Franklin was one of the few men on the deck of his ship who was not hurt, although he became a little deaf from the noise of the guns.

[pause]

After the wars, Franklin went to the Arctic. And he became famous.

[long-pause]

In 1819, he led an expedition overland, across northern Canada, by canoe and on foot, to map the Arctic coast, near the mouth of the Coppermine River.

[pause]

It was a terrible journey. On the way back, in the autumn, the expedition ran out of food, and the men had to walk for weeks across the frozen tundra, eating lichen, scraped from the rocks, which they called tripe de roche, and old pieces of leather, and even their own spare shoes. Many of the men did not survive.

[pause]

{img:canoes-northern-river-canada-voyageurs-autumn-1820}
When Franklin came home, he was a hero. He wrote a book about the journey, which was very popular. And people called him the man who ate his boots.

[long-pause]

But Franklin was not discouraged. In 1825, he went back to Arctic Canada, on a second overland expedition, which was much better planned, and mapped hundreds of miles more of the coast. This time, everyone came home safely. He was knighted by the King, and became Sir John Franklin.

[pause]

He is a deeply religious man, and a very kind one. He cannot bear to hurt any living thing. There is a story that he would not even swat a mosquito, but would blow it gently away, saying that there was room in the world for both of them.

[pause]

{img:franklin-blowing-mosquito-away-gently-camp-smile}
The sailors love him. He never shouts at them, or has them flogged, and he takes care of them, and treats them with respect.

[long-pause]

His first wife, Eleanor Porden, a poet, passed away in 1825, of illness, just as he was leaving on his second expedition. They had one daughter, also called Eleanor.

[pause]

And then, in 1828, he married again: a remarkable woman, Jane Griffin, who became Lady Jane Franklin.

[pause]

{img:franklin-family-drawing-room-1830s-daughter-piano}
From 1837 to 1843, Franklin was governor of Van Diemen's Land, the island that is now called Tasmania, off the south coast of Australia. It was a difficult time for him. The island was a prison colony, full of convicts sent from Britain, and the officials quarrelled with each other, and with him. Franklin was too kind, and too trusting, for the politics of the colony. In the end, he was dismissed, unfairly, and came home to England, unhappy and hurt.

[long-pause]

So when, in 1844, the Admiralty begins to plan a new expedition to find the Northwest Passage, Franklin wants very much to lead it.

[pause]

He wants to go back to the Arctic, which he loves, and to show everyone that he is still a great explorer. Lady Franklin wants it for him too.

[pause]

Some of the Lords of the Admiralty wonder whether he is too old, at fifty-nine. Lord Haddington, the First Lord, says to him that he is sixty. And Franklin answers, no, my lord, I am only fifty-nine. And his old friend, the Arctic explorer Sir Edward Parry, tells them that if they do not let him go, he will die of disappointment.

[pause]

So Sir John Franklin is given the command.

[long-pause]

In the weeks before the expedition sails, Franklin is very busy, and very happy.

[pause]

He goes down to Woolwich almost every day, to see the ships being prepared. He talks to the shipwrights, and the engineers, and the officers. He chooses many of the officers himself, with the help of Fitzjames. He writes letters to his old Arctic friends, asking their advice.

[pause]

{img:franklin-walking-dockyard-1845-shipwrights-cheerful}
His friends notice that he seems younger, and stronger, and more cheerful than he has been for years. The sadness of Van Diemen's Land seems to be forgotten. He is going back to the place where he has always been happiest.

[long-pause]

He has a cold, in the last days before they sail, and is in bed for a while. His family worry about him. But he gets better, and is ready to go.

[pause]

On the last Sunday before they sail, he reads the church service to the officers and men, on the deck of Erebus. Everyone remarks how well he reads, and how sincerely, as if every word meant something to him. And the men are glad to have such a captain: kind, experienced, and calm, a man who has been through the worst that the Arctic can do, and has come home.

[long-pause]

{img:lady-jane-franklin-portrait-1840s-bonnet-determined}
Lady Jane Franklin is one of the most remarkable women of her time.

[pause]

She was born in 1791, in London, the daughter of a rich silk weaver, and she grew up clever and curious and restless. As a girl, she read everything, and wrote long diaries, and made lists of all the books she read, and all the things she wanted to learn.

[long-pause]

She loves to travel. Before and after her marriage, she travels all over Europe, and to Russia, and to Egypt, where she sails up the Nile, and to Greece, and the Holy Land, and Turkey, at a time when very few women travel at all, and certainly not alone.

[pause]

In Australia, when her husband is governor of Van Diemen's Land, she is the first woman to travel overland from Melbourne to Sydney. She climbs mountains. She starts a museum, and a college, and a scientific society. She tries to improve the lives of the women convicts in the colony.

[pause]

Some people think she is too strong-minded, and too interested in her husband's work, for a woman of that time. But Franklin loves her, and admires her, and trusts her judgement completely.

[long-pause]

In the spring of 1845, as the expedition is being prepared, Lady Franklin is busy too.

[pause]

She helps her husband in every way she can. She visits the ships, and talks to the officers. She sews a silk flag, a Union Jack, for her husband to raise when he reaches the Pacific, at the end of the Passage.

[pause]

{img:woman-sewing-silk-union-flag-drawing-room-window}
There is a story that, a few days before he sails, Franklin falls asleep on the sofa, and Lady Franklin, who has just finished the flag, lays it over him, to keep him warm. And Franklin wakes up, and is startled, and says: why, there is a flag thrown over me! Because in the Navy, a flag is laid over a man only when he has passed away. Lady Franklin is upset, and says she meant nothing by it. Nobody knows if the story is true.

[long-pause]

With them, much of the time, is Franklin's niece, Sophia Cracroft, a lively, clever, pretty young woman of about thirty, who lives with them, and goes everywhere with Lady Franklin, as her companion.

[pause]

Sophia is admired by many men. One of them is Captain Francis Crozier, who will be second in command of the expedition, and who has asked her to marry him, more than once. But Sophia has always said no. She likes him, but she does not love him.

[pause]

{img:young-woman-1840s-garden-letter-thoughtful-summer}
Crozier sails away in 1845 still loving her, and still hoping.

[long-pause]

Franklin's daughter, Eleanor, is twenty-one in 1845, and very fond of her father, and close to her stepmother, although they sometimes disagree.

[pause]

The family goes down to the ships, at Greenhithe, on the Thames, a few days before they sail, to say goodbye. Eleanor writes later that her father was cheerful, and full of hope.

[pause]

And Franklin writes letters home to them, from the voyage, as long as he can: from the Orkney Islands, and from Greenland. Long, loving, thoughtful letters, full of news of the ships, and the men, and the weather, and of his faith in God, and his hopes for the voyage.

[long-pause]

Lady Franklin does not know, in May 1845, that she will never see her husband again.

[pause]

And she does not know that, for the next fourteen years, and more, she will spend all her energy, and almost all her money, trying to find out what happened to him. She will become one of the most famous women in the world, because of it.

[pause]

{img:woman-at-window-looking-out-to-sea-dusk-waiting}
But in the spring of 1845, there is no sadness. Only excitement, and pride, and the bustle of preparation, as the two great ships are made ready for the Arctic.

[long-pause]

{img:hms-erebus-terror-dockyard-woolwich-1845-refit}
The two ships, Erebus and Terror, are being prepared at the naval dockyard at Woolwich, on the river Thames, below London.

[pause]

They are not new ships. They were built as bomb vessels, warships designed to carry heavy mortars, for firing shells at enemy forts. Because of this, they are very strongly built, with thick timbers, to take the shock of the heavy guns. And this makes them good ships for the ice.

[long-pause]

They have been to the ice before. Terror sailed to the Arctic in 1836, and was nearly crushed in the ice, and came home badly damaged. And from 1839 to 1843, both ships sailed together, to the Antarctic, on the other side of the world, under the command of Captain James Clark Ross, with Francis Crozier commanding Terror.

[pause]

They sailed further south than anyone had ever been, and discovered a great volcano, smoking in the ice, which Ross named Mount Erebus, after his ship. And a smaller one next to it, which he named Mount Terror. Those names are still on the map of Antarctica today.

[pause]

Erebus is the larger of the two ships, about a hundred and five feet long, and three hundred and seventy tons. Terror is a little smaller. They are both three-masted, rigged as barques.

[long-pause]

For the Arctic, the ships are made even stronger.

[pause]

Their bows are covered with thick plates of iron, to break through the ice. Extra timbers are added inside, crossing from side to side, to strengthen the hull against the pressure of the ice. The decks are made thicker.

[pause]

{img:shipwrights-iron-plating-bow-wooden-ship-dock}
And, for the first time on a Navy expedition to the Arctic, the ships are given steam engines.

[pause]

The engines come from railway locomotives. Erebus gets an engine from the London and Greenwich Railway, and Terror from the London and Birmingham Railway. The engines are lowered into the holds, and connected to a propeller, at the stern, which can be raised up into a well in the hull, to protect it from the ice, when it is not being used. The engines are not very powerful. They can push the ships at about four knots, in calm water. But they will help, when there is no wind, or when the ships must push through the ice.

[long-pause]

The ships have a heating system too, a new invention, which pipes hot water, or warm air, from a stove, through pipes, under the decks, to keep the men warm in the Arctic winter.

[pause]

They have a machine for turning sea water into fresh water, by boiling it. They have the latest scientific instruments, for measuring the magnetism of the earth, and the weather, and for taking photographs, by the new invention of the daguerreotype.

[pause]

{img:ship-lower-deck-hammocks-stove-pipes-lanterns-cosy}
And they have libraries. Erebus carries about seventeen hundred books, and Terror about twelve hundred: books of travel and exploration, and history, and science, and religion, and novels, and old copies of Punch, the comic magazine, and Dickens's new novels. And Bibles and prayer books for every man.

[long-pause]

They also carry a great deal of food. Enough for three years, or even more, if it is used carefully.

[pause]

There are thousands of tins of preserved meat, and soup, and vegetables, bought from a man named Stephan Goldner, who sealed them in tins with lead solder. There is salt beef and salt pork, in barrels. There are tons of flour, and biscuit, and oatmeal, and peas, and sugar, and chocolate, and tea, and rum, and pickles, and lemon juice, to keep away scurvy.

[pause]

The ships are loaded so heavily that they sit low in the water, with their decks only a few feet above the waves.

[long-pause]

The ships carry other things too: a hand organ on each ship, which can play fifty tunes, including hymns, and dances. There are writing desks, and dressing cases, and china and silver for the officers, engraved with their family crests. There are muskets and shotguns, for hunting. There are sledges, and tents, and boats.

[pause]

{img:officers-wardroom-silver-china-books-oak-panels}
Everything has been thought of. Everyone agrees that no expedition has ever been better equipped. And on the nineteenth of May, 1845, Erebus and Terror sail from Greenhithe, on the Thames, with flags flying, and crowds cheering on the banks.

[long-pause]

{img:officers-group-1845-naval-uniforms-deck-portrait}
Who are the men of Erebus and Terror?

[pause]

There are a hundred and thirty-four men on the two ships when they sail from England. Five of them will be sent home from Greenland, because they are ill, or unsuitable. So a hundred and twenty-nine men will go on into the Arctic.

[long-pause]

The captain of Erebus, under Franklin, is Commander James Fitzjames.

[pause]

He is thirty-two years old, tall, handsome, cheerful, and full of jokes and stories. He has served in the Navy all over the world: in the Middle East, where he was wounded, and in the war against China, and once, he swam into a river in Liverpool, with all his clothes on, to save a drowning man. He is very popular with the other officers, and with the men.

[pause]

On the voyage, Fitzjames writes long, lively letters home, to his friends, the family of Sir John Barrow, describing all the officers, one by one, with humour and affection. Thanks to his letters, we know a little about many of the men.

[long-pause]

The captain of Terror, and second in command of the whole expedition, is Captain Francis Crozier.

[pause]

He is forty-nine, an Irishman, from Banbridge, in the north of Ireland, the son of a lawyer. He is quiet, serious, careful, and very experienced. He has been to the Arctic four times, and to the Antarctic once, commanding Terror. He knows more about the ice than anyone else on the expedition.

[pause]

{img:captain-crozier-serious-irish-officer-pipe-cabin-lamp}
But he is not happy, as they sail. He is lonely, and a little melancholy, perhaps because Sophia Cracroft has refused him. He writes to his friend, James Clark Ross, that he is in very low spirits.

[long-pause]

There are other officers.

[pause]

There is Lieutenant Graham Gore, of Erebus, a fine young officer, and a good artist, who plays the flute, and is a good shot. Fitzjames writes that he is a man of most stable character, a capital fellow. There is Lieutenant John Irving, of Terror, a serious, religious young Scotsman. There is Lieutenant Henry Le Vesconte, and Lieutenant James Fairholme, and the ice masters, James Reid and Thomas Blanky, experienced whaling men, who know the ways of the ice.

[pause]

There is the surgeon of Erebus, Stephen Stanley, and the assistant surgeon, Harry Goodsir, a young Scottish doctor and naturalist, of twenty-five, from Edinburgh, who is fascinated by the tiny creatures of the sea, and spends his days collecting them, and studying them under his microscope. Fitzjames writes that Goodsir is very pleasant, and when he gets hold of a jellyfish, he is perfectly happy.

[long-pause]

And there are the men: the petty officers, and the seamen, and the Royal Marines, and the stokers, who look after the steam engines, and the boys.

[pause]

They come from all over Britain, and some from further away: from London, and Kent, and Yorkshire, and Scotland, and Ireland, and Wales. Some of them are experienced Arctic sailors, who have been with Ross or Parry before. Some are young, and have never seen the ice.

[pause]

{img:sailors-1845-on-deck-coiling-ropes-laughing-sunshine}
There is John Torrington, a leading stoker on Terror, who is nineteen. There is John Hartnell, an able seaman on Erebus, and his brother, Thomas. There is William Braine, a Royal Marine. There are the ships' cooks, and carpenters, and sailmakers, and the captains' stewards. There are boys of eighteen, and men of fifty.

[long-pause]

There are also some animals on board.

[pause]

There is a monkey, called Jacko, given to the officers of Erebus as a pet, who is full of mischief. There is a big dog, a Newfoundland, called Neptune. And there are cats, as on every ship, to catch the rats.

[pause]

Fitzjames writes that the officers are a remarkably fine set of fellows, and that they all get on very well together. He writes that he has never been in a ship where there was such a good spirit.

[long-pause]

Before they sail, at Greenhithe, a photographer named Richard Beard comes on board, and takes daguerreotypes, early photographs, of Franklin and his officers, one by one, sitting in their best uniforms.

[pause]

These are some of the first photographs ever taken of an expedition. They still exist today. You can see the faces of the officers: Franklin, round and kindly, a little tired. Fitzjames, handsome, with his hand tucked into his coat. Crozier, serious, with his arms folded. Gore, and Goodsir, and the others, young men, looking calmly into the camera.

[pause]

{img:daguerreotype-studio-officer-sitting-still-1845-camera}
Looking at them, you can almost hear them, waiting patiently for the photograph to be taken, which took several seconds, sitting very still, and perhaps thinking about home, and the voyage ahead.

[long-pause]

{img:ships-sailing-down-thames-estuary-may-1845-steam-tugs}
On the nineteenth of May, 1845, Erebus and Terror leave Greenhithe, and sail down the Thames, toward the sea.

[pause]

They are towed by steam tugs, at first, and then by a steamship of the Navy, the Rattler, to help them on their way north, along the east coast of England and Scotland, heavily loaded, low in the water.

[long-pause]

Some days later, they arrive at Stromness, in the Orkney Islands, off the north of Scotland, where they take on fresh water, and the men are allowed a last walk on shore.

[pause]

And then, at the beginning of June, they sail out into the Atlantic Ocean, westward, toward Greenland. With them is a supply ship, the Barretto Junior, carrying more provisions, and live oxen, to be killed for fresh meat.

[pause]

The voyage across the Atlantic takes about a month. The weather is often rough, and the heavily loaded ships roll and pitch in the waves. But the men are cheerful. Fitzjames writes that they all like Sir John very much. He writes that Franklin is full of fun, and has a great talent for making everyone happy.

[long-pause]

At the beginning of July, they arrive at the coast of Greenland, at a place called Disko Bay, among the Whalefish Islands, where there is a little Danish settlement.

[pause]

It is a beautiful place. There are mountains, and great glaciers, and huge icebergs, floating in the bay, glowing white and blue in the sunshine, which shines all night long. The men see their first icebergs, and their first Inuit, the Greenlanders, who paddle out to the ships in their kayaks, to trade.

[pause]

{img:disko-bay-greenland-huge-icebergs-midnight-sun-kayaks}
Here, the stores from the supply ship are moved onto Erebus and Terror, until the ships are packed to the very top. The oxen are killed, and the meat is hung in the rigging, to keep fresh in the cold air. The men write their last letters home, to be carried back to England by the supply ship.

[long-pause]

Franklin writes to Lady Franklin, and to his daughter. He writes that the officers and the men are all in good spirits, and that he is confident.

[pause]

Fitzjames writes to his friends that they hope to reach the Pacific within a year. He writes: I don't think we shall be out more than two winters.

[pause]

{img:officer-writing-letter-cabin-greenland-window-ice}
Five men are sent home on the supply ship: men who are ill, or not strong enough. They are the lucky ones, although they do not know it.

[pause]

And on the twelfth of July, 1845, the supply ship sails for home. And Erebus and Terror sail on, alone, toward the northwest, across Baffin Bay.

[long-pause]

At the end of July, two whaling ships, the Prince of Wales and the Enterprise, see Erebus and Terror in Baffin Bay.

[pause]

The two Navy ships are moored to a great iceberg, waiting for the ice to open, so that they can cross to Lancaster Sound, the entrance to the Arctic channels. The captain of the Prince of Wales, Robert Martin, and some of the officers visit each other's ships, and talk.

[pause]

Franklin invites the whaling captains to dinner. He tells Captain Martin that he has enough food for three years, and could make it last for five, by hunting birds and seals. The officers are all cheerful and well. The men are busy shooting seabirds, for fresh meat.

[long-pause]

A few days later, the wind changes, and the ice opens.

[pause]

And the whalers watch Erebus and Terror sail away, westward, toward Lancaster Sound, until they disappear among the ice.

[pause]

{img:two-ships-sailing-away-into-ice-mist-whalers-watching}
That is the last time that any European ever sees them.

[long-pause]

{img:ship-deck-arctic-morning-sailors-scrubbing-ice-sails}
Let us spend an ordinary day on board HMS Erebus, in the Arctic summer of 1845, as she sails westward through Lancaster Sound.

[pause]

The day on board a Navy ship is divided into watches, of four hours each. Half of the men are on duty, while the other half rest, or sleep, in their hammocks, below decks. A bell is rung every half hour, to mark the time.

[long-pause]

In the morning, the men on deck scrub the decks, with sand and stones, called holystones, and sea water, as they do every morning on every ship of the Navy. They coil the ropes, and polish the brass, and check the rigging.

[pause]

Then breakfast, at about eight o'clock: oatmeal porridge, or cocoa, and biscuit, the hard, dry ship's bread, with butter, and tea.

[pause]

The men eat together, in messes, groups of six or eight men, sitting at wooden tables, which hang from the beams of the lower deck. Each mess takes turns to fetch the food from the galley, the kitchen, where the cook prepares it on a great iron stove, which also heats the ship.

[long-pause]

During the morning, the men work the ship.

[pause]

If there is a wind, they set the sails, climbing up the rigging, high above the deck, in the cold wind, to loosen or furl them. When the ice is thick, the ships must push through it, slowly, or wait for it to open. Sometimes the men go out onto the ice, with ropes, and pull the ship along a channel of open water, or cut a way through the ice with saws and axes.

[pause]

{img:men-on-ice-pulling-ropes-ship-channel-white-floes}
The ice masters, Reid and Blanky, stand high up in the crow's nest, a barrel at the top of the mainmast, looking out for leads, the channels of open water through the ice, and calling down to the officer on deck which way to steer.

[long-pause]

At noon, the officers take the position of the ship, by measuring the height of the sun, with their sextants. And then there is dinner, the main meal of the day, for the men.

[pause]

Dinner might be salt beef, or salt pork, boiled, with peas, or preserved meat from the tins, with potatoes, also from tins, and soup. On Sundays, there is a pudding, of flour and suet and raisins, called plum duff, which the sailors love. And every day, each man gets his ration of grog: rum, mixed with water, and his ration of lemon juice, to keep away scurvy.

[pause]

When they can, the men hunt, to get fresh meat: seabirds, and ducks, and seals. Fresh meat helps to keep them healthy.

[long-pause]

In the afternoon, the scientific work goes on.

[pause]

The officers take measurements, every hour, of the temperature, and the pressure of the air, and the wind, and the magnetism of the earth, with special instruments, and write them all down, carefully, in their logbooks. Goodsir, the naturalist, lowers his nets into the sea, and pulls up little shrimps, and jellyfish, and sea creatures, and studies them under his microscope, and draws them, and preserves them in jars of spirit.

[pause]

{img:naturalist-microscope-jars-specimens-cabin-lamp-drawing}
The officers keep journals, and write letters, which they will send home whenever they meet a ship, or keep, to post when they reach the Pacific.

[long-pause]

Then supper, at about five o'clock: tea, and biscuit, and perhaps something left over from dinner.

[pause]

In the evening, if the men are not on watch, they have some free time. They mend their clothes, and play cards, and draughts, and chess. They read, from the ship's library, or have someone read to them, because not all of them can read. They sing songs, and tell stories, and play the fiddle, and dance, on the deck. They play the hand organ.

[pause]

And on Sundays, there is a church service on deck, or below, when it is cold, with prayers and hymns, led by Sir John Franklin himself, who reads the service, and sometimes preaches. The men say that he reads very beautifully.

[long-pause]

In the Arctic summer, the sun does not set. It circles around the sky, low over the horizon, all night long, and the light never fades. The ice glitters, and the sea is calm, and the cliffs of the islands glow golden in the midnight sun.

[pause]

The men go to sleep in their hammocks, in the narrow, crowded lower deck, a few inches apart, with the ship creaking around them, and the ice grinding gently against the hull.

[pause]

{img:midnight-sun-golden-cliffs-ship-calm-sea-ice}
And the ship sails on, westward, into the unknown.

[long-pause]

{img:beechey-island-cliffs-bay-ships-anchored-autumn-snow}
In the autumn of 1845, as the ice begins to close in, Erebus and Terror find a place to spend their first winter: a sheltered bay, beside a small island, called Beechey Island, at the southwest corner of Devon Island, at the western end of Lancaster Sound.

[pause]

It is a bleak and lonely place. There are high cliffs of grey and brown rock, and a long, low beach of gravel, curving around the bay. Behind it, there is nothing but stony ground, rising to a bare plateau, without a tree, or a bush, or a blade of grass, as far as the eye can see.

[long-pause]

But before they reach it, they have done something remarkable.

[pause]

They have sailed north, up a channel called Wellington Channel, between the islands, much further north than anyone has ever sailed in this part of the Arctic, and then come back down, around Cornwallis Island, to Beechey Island. Now the first snows are falling. The sea is freezing. And the ships are frozen in, for the winter, in the bay.

[pause]

The men prepare for the long winter. They take down the topmasts, and cover the decks with a great roof of canvas, like a tent, to keep out the snow and the wind. They bank snow up against the sides of the ships, to keep in the warmth. They build storehouses on the beach, and a forge, and a carpenter's workshop, and an observatory, for the scientific instruments.

[long-pause]

And then the darkness comes.

[pause]

At the beginning of November, the sun sinks below the horizon, and does not rise again for three months. There is a little twilight at midday, a grey, blue light on the southern horizon. And then darkness again. The moon and the stars shine brightly, on the white snow. And the temperature falls lower and lower, to forty degrees below zero.

[pause]

{img:polar-night-moon-stars-ships-snow-lantern-light}
Life on board in the Arctic winter follows a strict routine, to keep the men busy, and healthy, and cheerful. The officers have learned this from the earlier expeditions of Parry and Ross.

[long-pause]

Every morning, the men clean the ship, and air their bedding. Every day, they must take exercise, walking up and down on the ice, or around a track marked out with poles, from the ships to the shore. When the weather is too bad to go out, they walk around the deck, under the canvas roof.

[pause]

There is school for the men, in the evenings, taught by the officers: reading and writing and arithmetic and navigation. On earlier expeditions, men who could not read when they left England came home able to read the Bible, and write letters to their families.

[pause]

{img:lower-deck-school-sailors-slates-officer-teaching-lamp}
There are plays, and concerts, and fancy dress balls, organized by the officers. On the expeditions of Parry, twenty years earlier, the officers acted plays on the deck, with scenery painted by the men, and the sailors played the women's parts, in dresses made of sailcloth. There is no record of the plays of Franklin's men, but they almost certainly did the same.

[long-pause]

At Christmas, there would have been a great dinner, with the best food from the stores: tinned meats, and plum puddings, and extra rum, and decorations of flags, and songs, and toasts to absent friends, and to the Queen.

[pause]

And on New Year's Eve, they would have rung in the new year, 1846, with the ship's bells, and cheers, out in the great white silence of the Arctic night.

[pause]

{img:christmas-dinner-ship-lower-deck-flags-puddings-cheer}
But that first winter at Beechey Island also brings sadness.

[long-pause]

Three of the men fall ill, and pass away, during the winter.

[pause]

The first is John Torrington, the young leading stoker of Terror, who passes away on the first of January, 1846, at the age of twenty. Then John Hartnell, an able seaman of Erebus, on the fourth of January, at the age of twenty-five. And in April, William Braine, a Royal Marine of Erebus, at the age of thirty-two.

[pause]

Their shipmates bury them on the gravel beach of Beechey Island, in graves cut into the frozen ground, with great difficulty, with picks. And they put up wooden headboards, painted, with their names, and their ages, and their ships, and a verse from the Bible. On Hartnell's board, they write: Thus saith the Lord of Hosts, consider your ways. On Braine's: Choose you this day whom ye will serve.

[long-pause]

In the spring, the sun returns, and the light comes back.

[pause]

The men go out on sledging trips, across the ice, to explore and hunt. They build a great cairn of stones, on the top of the cliff, made of hundreds of empty tins, filled with gravel, which they have eaten in the winter.

[pause]

{img:spring-sunlight-returning-ice-cairn-cliff-men-sledge}
But, strangely, they leave no message in the cairn, to say where they have gone, or what they plan to do. Perhaps they think they will not need to. Perhaps they leave it somewhere that has never been found. It is one of the many mysteries of the expedition.

[pause]

And in the summer of 1846, when the ice breaks up, Erebus and Terror sail away from Beechey Island, and disappear into the Arctic.

[long-pause]

{img:polar-bear-walking-sea-ice-golden-low-sun}
The men of Erebus and Terror, in their first year in the Arctic, see many animals that they have never seen before.

[pause]

The most famous, and the most impressive, is the polar bear. The polar bear is the largest hunter that lives on land, larger than any lion or tiger. A big male can weigh more than half a ton, and stand taller than a man on his hind legs. Its fur is thick and creamy white, and its black nose and its small dark eyes are the only dark things about it.

[long-pause]

Polar bears live on the sea ice, and hunt seals. They can swim for days, across open water, with their great paws. They can smell a seal from more than a mile away. They walk slowly, swaying their long necks from side to side, across the ice, and they are curious, and often come close to the ships, to see what these strange new things are.

[pause]

The Inuit call the polar bear Nanuq, and respect it more than any other animal. They believe it is wise, and almost like a person.

[pause]

{img:arctic-fox-white-winter-coat-curled-snow-drift}
There are Arctic foxes too, small and pretty, with thick white fur in winter, and brown in summer, and a great bushy tail, which they curl around themselves to sleep. They follow the polar bears, to eat what the bears leave behind. They are bold, and come close to the ships, looking for scraps. On earlier expeditions, the sailors tamed them, and kept them as pets.

[long-pause]

In the sea, there are seals of many kinds: the ringed seal, the small, common seal of the Arctic, and the bearded seal, with its long whiskers, and the harp seal, with its pattern like a harp on its back.

[pause]

There are walruses, huge and heavy, with long ivory tusks, and bristly moustaches, lying in great crowds on the ice floes and the beaches, snorting and grunting and pushing each other, and sleeping in the sun.

[pause]

And there are whales: the great bowhead whale, with its huge head and its smiling mouth, which the whaling ships hunt for its oil. And the white whale, the beluga, which swims in groups, singing, so that the sailors call it the sea canary. And the strangest of all, the narwhal, with a single long, spiral tusk of ivory, growing straight out of its head, like the horn of a unicorn.

[long-pause]

In the Middle Ages, people in Europe bought narwhal tusks, brought from the Arctic by Viking traders, and believed that they were the horns of unicorns, and paid enormous sums for them, because they were thought to protect against poison. The throne of the kings of Denmark is made partly of narwhal tusks.

[pause]

{img:narwhals-surfacing-calm-arctic-water-tusks-mist}
On the land, there are herds of caribou, and musk oxen: shaggy, dark brown animals, like small buffalo, with long hair reaching almost to the ground, and curved horns. When they are threatened, the musk oxen stand together in a circle, with their horns facing outward, and their calves in the middle.

[long-pause]

There are lemmings, little round, furry animals, like hamsters, which live under the snow in winter, and in some years appear in enormous numbers, everywhere.

[pause]

And there are the birds. In the summer, millions of birds come to the Arctic, to nest on the cliffs and the tundra, and to raise their young in the endless daylight: little auks, and guillemots, and fulmars, and kittiwakes, and eider ducks, and snow geese, and terns. The Arctic tern flies all the way from the Antarctic, at the other end of the world, every year, to nest in the Arctic, and back again in the autumn. It sees more daylight than any other creature on earth.

[pause]

{img:seabird-cliffs-arctic-summer-thousands-of-birds-sea}
There is the snowy owl, white and silent, hunting lemmings across the tundra. And the ptarmigan, a kind of grouse, which turns white in winter, and stays in the Arctic all year round.

[long-pause]

The men shoot birds and seals for fresh meat, and the officers collect specimens of the birds and animals, and press the little Arctic flowers in books, to bring home to the scientists of England.

[pause]

Goodsir, the naturalist, is especially happy. He writes in his journal about the little creatures he dredges up from the sea floor, and the birds, and the plants, with great delight. Many of his notes, and his specimens, must still be somewhere, perhaps on board one of the ships, at the bottom of the sea.

[long-pause]

{img:ships-sailing-south-narrow-channel-ice-floes-summer-1846}
In the summer of 1846, Erebus and Terror sail south from Beechey Island, into the unknown.

[pause]

Franklin's orders from the Admiralty are to sail west from Lancaster Sound, and then, from about Cape Walker, to go south and west, toward the coast of America, which has already been mapped, and then along the coast, westward, to the Bering Strait, and the Pacific.

[long-pause]

They sail down a long channel, between two great islands, which is now called Peel Sound. No ship has ever sailed through it before. Two years earlier, it would have been impossible. But in the summer of 1846, the ice is open, and the two ships sail all the way down it, for about two hundred and fifty miles, southward.

[pause]

It must be an exciting time. Every day, they are seeing new coasts, new capes, new islands, that no European has ever seen. The officers are drawing charts, and naming the capes and bays they pass. They must believe that they are very close to the Passage.

[pause]

At the bottom of Peel Sound, the channel opens out into a wider strait, now called Franklin Strait. And to the southwest, there is a large, low, flat island: King William Island.

[long-pause]

Franklin's charts, made by earlier explorers, show King William Island as part of the mainland, a great peninsula, called King William Land. They do not know that there is a channel to the east of it, between the island and the mainland, where the ice is much lighter.

[pause]

So the ships go down the western side of the island, into a wide channel now called Victoria Strait.

[pause]

{img:victoria-strait-heavy-pack-ice-flat-island-grey-sky}
And here, they meet the ice.

[long-pause]

Victoria Strait is one of the worst places for ice in the whole Arctic.

[pause]

Every year, great masses of old, thick ice, many years old, come drifting down from the northwest, from the great ice of the Arctic Ocean, through a wide channel, now called McClintock Channel, and pile up in Victoria Strait, against the west coast of King William Island. This ice is very thick, ten or twenty feet, or more, and hard as rock. Even in summer, it often does not melt or break up at all.

[pause]

{img:ancient-thick-pressure-ridges-ice-blue-white-sun}
In September 1846, Erebus and Terror are caught in this ice, off the northwest corner of King William Island, about fifteen miles from shore.

[pause]

They are beset. They cannot move forward, or backward. The ice closes around them, and freezes solid. And there they stay.

[long-pause]

At first, this would not have been so worrying. The officers would have expected to spend a winter in the ice, and to be freed in the summer, when the ice breaks up. Arctic ships are often beset, and freed again.

[pause]

And they are very close, now, to the Passage. To the south of King William Island, the coast of America has already been mapped by earlier explorers, including Franklin himself, and others, sailing in small boats along the coast. If they can get just a little further south, perhaps a hundred miles, they will have found the last link.

[pause]

So they prepare, once again, for winter. They cover the decks. They bank up the snow. They set up their routine of work and exercise and school and entertainment. And the darkness comes again.

[long-pause]

The second winter, of 1846 and 1847, is spent in the open ice, far from land.

[pause]

It is different from the first winter, at Beechey Island. There is no sheltered bay, no beach to walk on. Just the ice, all around, flat and white and endless, broken by ridges, where the ice has been pushed up into great walls, by the pressure of the floes moving against each other.

[pause]

{img:pressure-ridge-ice-moonlight-ships-small-distance}
And the ships are not still. They move, with the ice, very slowly, drifting southward, a mile or two a week, locked in the great frozen field.

[pause]

The men must hear the ice, all through the winter: groaning, and creaking, and cracking, sometimes with a sound like a gunshot, as the great floes press against each other, and against the ships.

[long-pause]

But we know very little about what happened during that winter. Because no journals, no logbooks, no letters from the expedition, from after the summer of 1845, have ever been found.

[pause]

Except for one short note.

[long-pause]

{img:ship-in-ice-winter-canvas-roof-snow-banked-lantern-glow}
What was it like, the second winter, in the ice off King William Island?

[pause]

We can imagine it, from the journals of other Arctic expeditions of the time, and from what the Inuit later remembered, and from what the archaeologists have found.

[long-pause]

The men would have kept to the routine: exercise, school, work, and entertainment.

[pause]

They would have gone out hunting, across the ice, for seals, and polar bears, and the birds that came in the spring. But there is very little game in this part of the Arctic, especially in winter. Later explorers would find that this was one of the poorest hunting grounds in the whole Arctic. Even the Inuit rarely came here.

[pause]

The tinned food would have been the main part of their diet. Some historians and scientists believe that the lead solder in the tins, and perhaps the lead pipes of the water system on the ships, may have slowly poisoned some of the men, making them weak and ill, and confused. Others think that this did not matter so much. Scientists are still studying it.

[long-pause]

And the cold. The cold of the Arctic winter is beyond anything most people can imagine.

[pause]

At forty degrees below zero, the breath freezes as soon as it leaves the mouth, and forms frost on the beard and eyebrows. Metal sticks to the skin. Ink freezes in the pens, and the men must write in pencil. Below decks, in spite of the heating, the walls and ceilings are covered with frost, from the breath of the men, and the steam of the cooking, which drips down on them when the stove is lit.

[pause]

{img:frost-covered-beams-below-deck-men-wrapped-warm-stove}
The men wear thick woollen clothes, and flannel, and great coats, and fur caps, and mittens. But the Navy's clothes are not as warm as the clothes of the Inuit, made of caribou fur and sealskin, which are the warmest clothes in the world.

[long-pause]

And there is the darkness, for months.

[pause]

Arctic explorers wrote that the long darkness was the hardest thing to bear. Men became sad, and quiet, and could not sleep, or slept too much. The officers worked hard to keep everyone busy, and cheerful.

[pause]

{img:aurora-borealis-green-curtains-sky-ship-silhouette}
But there is also beauty.

[pause]

On clear nights, the Northern Lights, the aurora borealis, shimmer across the sky, in great curtains and waves of green and white and red, moving and dancing, silently, over the frozen sea. The stars are brighter than anywhere else on earth. The moon circles the sky, for days, without setting, and lights up the ice like silver.

[long-pause]

In the spring of 1847, the light comes back.

[pause]

First a faint glow in the south, at midday. Then, one day in February, the first glimpse of the sun, just the top of it, peeping over the horizon, for a few minutes, and then sinking again. The men cheer. Every day after that, it stays a little longer.

[pause]

And by May, the sun is shining all day and all night again, on the dazzling white ice. The men must wear goggles, or veils, to protect their eyes from snow blindness.

[long-pause]

In May 1847, Franklin sends out a party of men, on foot, with a sledge, across the ice, to King William Island, to explore the coast, and perhaps to look for a way south.

[pause]

The party is led by Lieutenant Graham Gore, the flute player, the man of most stable character. With him is the mate, Charles Des Voeux, and six men.

[pause]

{img:sledge-party-eight-men-hauling-across-ice-sunlit}
They pull their sledge across the ice, about fifteen miles, to the shore of King William Island. And at a place called Victory Point, on the northwest coast, they find an old cairn of stones, built many years before by James Clark Ross, who had come here on foot, seventeen years earlier.

[pause]

And in the cairn, they leave a message.

[long-pause]

{img:netsilik-inuit-camp-skin-tents-summer-lake-shore}
While Erebus and Terror lay in the ice, off the coast of King William Island, the Netsilik Inuit were living their lives on the land around them, as their people had done for hundreds of years.

[pause]

Their name means the people of the seal. They are a small group, perhaps a few hundred people, living in family groups, moving from place to place through the year, following the animals.

[long-pause]

In the winter, they live out on the sea ice, in villages of snow houses, igloos, built in an hour or two, of blocks of hard snow, cut with knives of bone or antler, and fitted together in a spiral, into a dome.

[pause]

Inside, a snow house is surprisingly warm. There is a raised platform of snow, at the back, covered with caribou skins, where the family sleeps, all together, under thick fur blankets. And there is a lamp, a shallow dish of soapstone, filled with seal oil, with a wick of moss, which burns with a soft, steady yellow flame, and gives light and warmth, and is used for cooking, and for melting snow for water, and for drying clothes.

[pause]

The woman of the family looks after the lamp. It is the heart of the home.

[long-pause]

In the winter, the men hunt seals, at their breathing holes in the ice.

[pause]

A seal must come up to breathe, every few minutes, and it keeps several small holes open in the ice, all winter long, under the snow. The hunter finds a breathing hole, with the help of his dog, and stands beside it, perfectly still, in the cold and the dark, for hours, waiting, with his harpoon ready, for the seal to come up. It takes great patience.

[pause]

{img:hunter-waiting-still-seal-breathing-hole-twilight-snow}
When a seal is caught, the meat is shared among all the families of the village, according to old rules, so that nobody goes hungry while there is food in the camp.

[long-pause]

In the spring, the families move to the land, and to the lakes and the rivers.

[pause]

They fish for Arctic char, a beautiful fish, like a salmon, with red and silver skin, which swims up the rivers from the sea, in great numbers, in the late summer. They build stone weirs across the rivers, little walls, to trap the fish, and spear them with three-pronged spears. Some of these old stone weirs, hundreds of years old, are still there today.

[pause]

{img:stone-fish-weir-river-tundra-people-spearing-char}
And in the summer and the autumn, they hunt caribou, which come in great herds across the land, and use their skins for clothing and tents, and their antlers and bones for tools, and their meat for food.

[long-pause]

The clothes of the Inuit are made by the women, from caribou skin, which is light and very warm, because each caribou hair is hollow, and holds the warm air.

[pause]

They wear two layers: an inner one with the fur turned inward, against the skin, and an outer one with the fur turned outward. With boots of sealskin, waterproof, and mittens of fur, and a hood, with a ruff of wolverine fur, around the face, which does not freeze. These are the warmest clothes in the world. The men of Erebus and Terror, in their woollen Navy coats, would have been much colder.

[pause]

{img:inuit-woman-sewing-caribou-parka-bone-needle-lamp}
The women sew the clothes with needles made of bone, and thread made from the sinews of caribou, with tiny, careful stitches, so close that no wind or water can get through.

[long-pause]

The Netsilik have their own beliefs, and their own stories.

[pause]

They believe that the animals have souls, and must be treated with respect, or they will not come back to be hunted. There is the sea goddess, Nuliajuk, who lives at the bottom of the sea, and keeps the seals and the other sea creatures, and must be kept happy. There are shamans, angakkuit, who can travel to the spirit world, and speak with the spirits, and heal the sick.

[pause]

And there are long winter evenings, in the snow houses, with games, and songs, and drum dances, and stories: stories of the old times, and of the animals, and of the spirits, and of strange things that have happened. Stories that are remembered, and passed on, for generations.

[long-pause]

In the years after 1848, the Netsilik find strange things on their land.

[pause]

They find wood, which is very precious to them, because there are no trees in their country. And metal, iron and copper and brass, which they can make into knives and tools and harpoon points, much better than stone or bone. They find these things in the places where the white men had been, and on the ship that had been driven into the bay.

[pause]

{img:inuit-craftsman-making-knife-blade-from-old-metal}
And so, the things of the Franklin expedition become part of the life of the Netsilik, for many years: a knife blade made from a piece of the ship, a spoon, a button, a piece of wood made into a sledge runner. And the story of the strangers and their ships becomes part of their stories, told around the lamps.

[long-pause]

{img:printed-admiralty-form-tin-cylinder-cairn-stones}
The message is written on a printed form, which every Navy ship carries.

[pause]

The form says, in six languages, English and French and Spanish and Dutch and Danish and German: whoever finds this paper is requested to forward it to the Secretary of the Admiralty, London, with a note of the time and place at which it was found. In the empty space in the middle, the officers write their message.

[long-pause]

The message says:

[pause]

28 of May 1847. H.M. ships Erebus and Terror wintered in the ice in latitude 70 degrees 5 minutes north, longitude 98 degrees 23 minutes west. Having wintered in 1846 to 47 at Beechey Island, in latitude 74 degrees 43 minutes 28 seconds north, longitude 91 degrees 39 minutes 15 seconds west, after having ascended Wellington Channel to latitude 77 degrees, and returned by the west side of Cornwallis Island. Sir John Franklin commanding the expedition. All well. Party consisting of 2 officers and 6 men left the ships on Monday 24th May 1847. Gm. Gore, Lieut. Chas. F. Des Voeux, mate.

[pause]

All well.

[long-pause]

There is a small mistake in the message. The ships wintered at Beechey Island in 1845 to 1846, not 1846 to 1847. Perhaps Gore was tired, or cold, or in a hurry. It is a little thing. But it is the kind of mistake that makes the note feel very human.

[pause]

Gore and his men roll the paper up, and put it into a small metal cylinder, to protect it from the damp, and place it in the cairn, under the stones. And they leave another copy in another cairn, a few miles to the south.

[pause]

{img:men-placing-stones-cairn-low-coast-ice-beyond}
Then they go on, along the coast, for a little while, exploring, before they return to the ships.

[long-pause]

All well.

[pause]

In May 1847, the expedition has spent two winters in the Arctic. They have discovered hundreds of miles of new coast. They have sailed further than anyone before. And everyone, it seems, is well and hopeful. They are so close to the Passage. Perhaps, this summer, the ice will break up, and they will sail through.

[pause]

But the ice does not break up that summer. The summer of 1847 is cold. The ships stay locked in the ice, off King William Island.

[long-pause]

And on the eleventh of June, 1847, just two weeks after Gore left the note, Sir John Franklin passes away, on board Erebus, at the age of sixty-one.

[pause]

We do not know what he died of. Perhaps it was his heart. Perhaps a stroke. He was an old man, by the standards of the time, and had lived a hard life. We do not know if Gore came back to the ships in time to see him.

[pause]

{img:quiet-cabin-erebus-bible-lamp-chair-empty}
We do not know where he was buried. Perhaps on the land, on King William Island, in a grave dug in the frozen gravel. Perhaps at sea, through a hole cut in the ice, beside the ship, with the flag laid over him, and the officers and men standing around, bare-headed, in the cold, and Captain Crozier reading the burial service.

[pause]

His grave has never been found.

[long-pause]

Now Captain Francis Crozier, the quiet, careful Irishman, is in command of the expedition.

[pause]

And Commander Fitzjames, the cheerful one, is his second in command.

[pause]

{img:captain-crozier-on-deck-looking-out-ice-responsibility}
They spend a third winter in the ice, the winter of 1847 to 1848. It must have been a very hard winter. The food is running low. The men are growing weaker. Some of them are ill. And still the ice does not move.

[long-pause]

In the spring of 1848, almost a year after Gore left the note, someone goes back to the cairn at Victory Point.

[pause]

They take out the note, and unroll it. And they write more, around the edges of the paper, in the margins, in different handwriting, crowded and cramped, as if they were writing in a hurry, with cold hands. It is the handwriting of Fitzjames, and at the end, Crozier signs it, and adds a few words.

[pause]

And this is what they write:

[pause]

April 25, 1848. H.M. ships Terror and Erebus were deserted on the 22nd April, 5 leagues north-north-west of this, having been beset since 12th September 1846. The officers and crews, consisting of 105 souls, under the command of Captain F. R. M. Crozier, landed here, in latitude 69 degrees 37 minutes 42 seconds, longitude 98 degrees 41 minutes. Sir John Franklin died on the 11th June 1847, and the total loss by deaths in the expedition has been to this date 9 officers and 15 men.

[long-pause]

And in the corner, Crozier adds: and start on tomorrow, 26th, for Back's Fish River.

[pause]

Back's Fish River, which we now call the Back River, is a great river, flowing north into the Arctic Ocean, on the mainland of America, south of King William Island, about two hundred and fifty miles away. Up that river, hundreds of miles further, there are the trading posts of the Hudson's Bay Company, and help.

[pause]

{img:king-william-island-flat-snowy-coast-tents-boats-sledges}
The hundred and five men who are left have abandoned the ships. They have come ashore, with their sledges, and their boats, mounted on sledges, and their tents, and their supplies. And they are going to walk south.

[pause]

It is the last message ever received from the Franklin expedition.

[long-pause]

{img:banbridge-irish-town-1800s-river-bridge-houses}
Let us stay for a little while with Francis Crozier, the man who led the expedition in its last year.

[pause]

He was born in 1796, in Banbridge, a small linen town in County Down, in the north of Ireland, the son of a lawyer, one of thirteen children. His family were Presbyterians, serious, hard-working people. As a boy, he loved the sea, and at the age of thirteen, he joined the Royal Navy.

[long-pause]

As a young midshipman, Crozier sailed to the Pacific, and visited Pitcairn Island, where the descendants of the mutineers of the Bounty lived. He met the last survivor of the mutiny, an old man named John Adams, who was by then the gentle and religious leader of the little island community.

[pause]

Then he went to the Arctic, with William Edward Parry, on three voyages in the 1820s, looking for the Northwest Passage, and trying to reach the North Pole over the ice. On those voyages, he learned everything about the ice: how it moves, how to sail through it, how to survive the winter in it, how to travel on it with sledges.

[pause]

And he met another young officer, James Clark Ross, who became his closest friend for life.

[long-pause]

Crozier was a quiet man, and rather shy. He was not brilliant or charming, like Fitzjames, or famous, like Franklin. He was not from a rich or important family. So he was promoted slowly, and had to wait many years for his chances.

[pause]

But he was very good at his work. He studied the magnetism of the earth, and the stars, and became an expert in the scientific measurements that the Navy expeditions made. He was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society, the great society of scientists in London, which was a great honour.

[pause]

{img:officer-taking-magnetic-measurements-instrument-tent-ice}
The sailors liked him and trusted him. He was fair and calm, and he knew what he was doing.

[long-pause]

From 1839 to 1843, Crozier went with Ross to the Antarctic, as captain of Terror.

[pause]

They sailed further south than anyone had ever been, through the pack ice, into a great open sea, which is now called the Ross Sea. They discovered the great volcano, Mount Erebus, and a vast wall of ice, hundreds of feet high, stretching for hundreds of miles, which Ross called the Barrier, and which we now call the Ross Ice Shelf. A cape at the foot of Mount Terror is named Cape Crozier, after him. It is where the emperor penguins nest, in the middle of the Antarctic winter.

[pause]

{img:emperor-penguins-antarctic-ice-cliffs-pale-light}
It was during that voyage, when the ships stopped for some months in Hobart, in Van Diemen's Land, that Crozier met the governor's niece, Sophia Cracroft, and fell in love with her.

[long-pause]

He asked her to marry him, in Hobart. She said no. He asked her again, in England. She said no again.

[pause]

Sophia liked Crozier, and respected him. But she found him a little dull, perhaps. And she did not want to marry a sailor, who would always be away at sea. She never married at all, in the end. She stayed with her aunt, Lady Franklin, all her life, and helped her in the long search for the expedition, and wrote thousands of letters.

[pause]

When the expedition was planned, in 1844, Crozier was not sure whether he wanted to go. He was tired, after the long voyage to the Antarctic, and sad about Sophia. But in the end, he agreed to go, out of loyalty to Franklin, and to his friend Ross, and to the Navy.

[long-pause]

So it was Crozier, the quiet, careful, experienced Irishman, who led the men in their last year, after Franklin had passed away, through the third winter in the ice, and then onto the land, and south, along the coast.

[pause]

We do not know what happened to him. His grave has never been found. The Inuit stories tell of a leader, a tall man, who stayed with his men, and walked with them, and was kind to the Inuit he met. Some people believe that this was Crozier.

[pause]

{img:tall-figure-walking-snow-distance-pale-sky-quiet}
In Banbridge, his home town, there is a monument to him, in the town square, opposite the house where he was born: a tall stone pedestal, with polar bears at the corners, and a statue of Crozier on top, in his naval uniform, looking out to the north.

[long-pause]

{img:long-line-men-hauling-boat-sledge-across-snow-distance}
What happened next, we know only in pieces. From things found, many years later, along the coast of King William Island. And from the memories of the Inuit, who saw some of the men, and told their children and grandchildren about them.

[pause]

We will tell it gently, and from a distance, as if we were watching from far away, across the snow.

[long-pause]

In April 1848, a hundred and five men come ashore at Victory Point, from the ships, and begin to walk south, along the west coast of King William Island, toward the Back River.

[pause]

They pull heavy sledges, with boats on them, loaded with supplies. It is very hard work. The snow is deep and soft. The men are weak, from the long winters, and the poor food, and illness. They take with them many things that seem strange to us now: books, and silver forks and spoons, and writing desks, and clothes brushes, and even a great curtain rod. Perhaps they were not thinking clearly. Perhaps they hoped to need them later.

[pause]

They walk slowly, southward, along the flat, bare, lonely coast.

[long-pause]

Along the way, some of the men, too weak to go on, perhaps turn back, toward the ships, hoping to find food there, or to sail them, if the ice opens.

[pause]

Others go on. Day after day, week after week, through the spring and the summer of 1848.

[pause]

{img:inuit-hunters-meeting-strangers-ice-distance-summer}
The Inuit later remembered that some of their people, out hunting seals on the ice, near Washington Bay, on the west coast of the island, met a group of about forty white men, pulling a boat on a sledge. The white men were thin, and looked tired. One of the leaders, an officer, spoke a few words to the Inuit, and gave them some things. The Inuit gave them some seal meat. And then the Inuit went away, because they did not have enough food to share for so many, and the white men went on, south.

[long-pause]

Some of the men reach the south coast of King William Island, and cross the ice to the mainland, near a place now called Starvation Cove, on the Adelaide Peninsula, not far from the mouth of the Back River.

[pause]

But none of them reach the trading posts. None of them come home.

[pause]

{img:arctic-coast-summer-low-light-stones-flowers-quiet}
Over the next months, and perhaps years, all of the men of the Franklin expedition pass away, in the Arctic, from cold, and hunger, and illness, along the coast of King William Island, and on the mainland, and perhaps on the ships.

[long-pause]

The Inuit tell other stories too.

[pause]

They tell that one of the ships was seen, for some years afterward, drifting in the ice, or anchored near an island, and that there were still some men on board, at least for a while. They tell of footprints seen in the snow, and of a man who came to their camp, alone, a big man, who was very weak. And of the ship, later, empty, with a gangway down onto the ice, and a dead man inside, a very big man, and the ship sinking, in the summer, with only the masts showing above the water.

[pause]

Some people have wondered whether a few of the men might have lived for several years, perhaps with the help of the Inuit, perhaps on the ships. We will never know for certain.

[long-pause]

It is a very sad story. But it is also, in its way, a story of great courage.

[pause]

The men of the Franklin expedition did not give up. They stayed together, and helped each other, and went on, as long as they could, toward home. They followed their officers. They carried their books, and their Bibles, and their letters from home.

[pause]

{img:arctic-sunset-snow-quiet-stones-cairn-golden-light}
And many years later, when one of the search parties found a small group of their belongings, along the coast, among them was a little book, a devotional book, called Christian Melodies, with the inscription: from G. A. to Graham Gore. And a well-worn copy of The Vicar of Wakefield, a gentle, cheerful old novel, which someone had carried all that way.

[long-pause]

{img:lady-franklin-writing-letters-desk-maps-1848-candle}
In England, at first, nobody is worried.

[pause]

The expedition had food for three years. Fitzjames had written that they might be out for two winters. Arctic expeditions often disappear for a long time, and come back safely. So in 1846, and 1847, there is no news, and no one is surprised.

[long-pause]

But by the end of 1847, Lady Franklin is beginning to worry. And some of the old Arctic explorers, like John Ross, are saying that something must be done.

[pause]

In 1848, the Admiralty sends out the first search expeditions: one by sea, from the east, under James Clark Ross, Franklin's old friend, the commander of Erebus in the Antarctic. One from the west, by the Bering Strait. And one overland, down the rivers of Canada, to the Arctic coast, under Sir John Richardson, who had been with Franklin on his first two expeditions, and a younger Scottish explorer named John Rae.

[pause]

None of them find anything.

[long-pause]

And then, over the next ten years, there is the greatest search in the history of exploration.

[pause]

More than thirty expeditions go out, by sea and by land, to look for Franklin. Ships of the Royal Navy, and of the United States, paid for by a rich American merchant, Henry Grinnell, and ships paid for by Lady Franklin herself. They search the islands and the channels of the Arctic, year after year, on sledges and in boats, and on foot.

[pause]

{img:many-ships-wintering-ice-search-expedition-tents-1850}
They map thousands of miles of coast, that no one has ever seen before. Much of the map of Arctic Canada was made by the men searching for Franklin.

[long-pause]

Lady Franklin becomes the heart of the search.

[pause]

She writes letters to the Admiralty, and to the government, and to the President of the United States, and to the Tsar of Russia, asking for help. She writes to newspapers. She meets explorers, and sailors, and politicians. She spends most of her own fortune on ships and expeditions. She travels to the Shetland and Orkney Islands, to talk to whaling captains, and to ask them to look for her husband.

[pause]

She becomes famous all over the world. People write songs about her. There is a sad ballad, called Lady Franklin's Lament, which is still sung today: in Baffin's Bay, where the whale fishes blow, the fate of Franklin no man may know.

[long-pause]

In August 1850, the searchers find the first traces of the expedition, at Beechey Island.

[pause]

They find the remains of the winter camp: the storehouses, and the forge, and the observatory, and a great pile of empty tins, and the remains of a garden, where someone had tried to grow cress, a little salad, for fresh food. And on the beach, they find the three graves, with their painted headboards: John Torrington, John Hartnell, and William Braine.

[pause]

{img:searchers-standing-three-graves-beechey-beach-hats-off}
But there is no message. No note, to say where the ships have gone. The searchers look everywhere, and find nothing.

[long-pause]

So the searchers go on looking, in the wrong places, mostly to the north and the west, for years. Because nobody knows that Franklin went south, down Peel Sound.

[pause]

And in 1850 to 1854, one of the search ships, HMS Investigator, under Captain Robert McClure, coming from the west, through the Bering Strait, is trapped in the ice, far to the north, and abandoned. McClure and his men walk across the ice, and are rescued by another search ship, coming from the east.

[pause]

And so McClure becomes the first man to cross the Northwest Passage, from west to east, partly by ship, and partly on foot, over the ice. He is given a reward of ten thousand pounds by Parliament. But he has not found Franklin.

[long-pause]

Many people in England begin to give up hope. In 1854, the Admiralty declares that the men of the Franklin expedition are to be considered dead, and takes their names off the Navy list.

[pause]

But Lady Franklin will not give up. She refuses to wear mourning clothes, for years. She says that she will not believe her husband is gone until she knows for certain what happened to him.

[pause]

{img:woman-in-bright-dress-refusing-mourning-portrait-calm}
And in that same year, 1854, the first real news comes, not from the Navy, but from a quiet Scottish doctor, travelling on foot, in the far north of Canada.

[long-pause]

{img:john-rae-scottish-explorer-fur-clothing-snowshoes-portrait}
John Rae is a doctor, from the Orkney Islands, in the far north of Scotland.

[pause]

He was born in 1813, and grew up on the islands, sailing and fishing and hunting, and climbing the cliffs. As a young man, he went to work as a surgeon for the Hudson's Bay Company, the great fur trading company of Canada, at its trading posts on Hudson Bay. And he stayed in Canada for many years.

[long-pause]

Rae is very different from the Navy explorers.

[pause]

He learns to live like the Inuit, and the Cree, and the Dene people of the north. He wears clothes of fur and skins. He travels light and fast, on foot, on snowshoes, with only a few companions, Native and European, hunting for food as he goes. He builds snow houses to sleep in, instead of carrying heavy tents. He listens to the Native people, and learns from them.

[pause]

He is one of the greatest walkers in the history of exploration. In one year, he walks more than a thousand miles on snowshoes. Over his life, he maps more than fifteen hundred miles of the Arctic coast of Canada.

[long-pause]

In 1854, Rae is exploring the coast, on foot, for the Hudson's Bay Company, near a place called Pelly Bay, east of King William Island.

[pause]

There, he meets an Inuk hunter, named In-nook-poo-zhe-jook, who tells him something important. He tells Rae that, some years ago, other Inuit had seen a party of white men, about forty of them, walking south over the ice, pulling a boat, near the shore of a large island, which must be King William Island. And that later, at the mouth of a great river, to the south, they had found the places where the men had passed away.

[pause]

{img:inuit-hunter-talking-explorer-snow-camp-sledge-dogs}
And the Inuit have things from the expedition, which they have found, and traded: silver forks and spoons, with the crests and the initials of the officers engraved on them. A gold watch. A medal. A little silver plate, engraved with the words: Sir John Franklin, K.C.H.

[long-pause]

Rae buys these things from the Inuit, and hurries back to England, to bring the news.

[pause]

He writes a report to the Admiralty, telling what the Inuit have told him. It is a very sad report. It tells that the men of the Franklin expedition had all perished, of cold and hunger, in about 1850, far from help, and that their last days had been very hard indeed.

[pause]

{img:silver-spoons-forks-crests-watch-medal-table-lamp}
The report is printed in the newspapers. And the people of England are shocked, and grief-stricken.

[long-pause]

Some of them, too, are angry. Not with what happened, but with Rae, and with the Inuit.

[pause]

Lady Franklin, and many others, refuse to believe parts of the report. The great novelist, Charles Dickens, writes long articles in his magazine, saying that the Inuit cannot be trusted, and that brave English sailors could never have suffered as the report said. He speaks unkindly of the Inuit, who had told the truth.

[pause]

Rae defends the Inuit, and their honesty, all his life. But because of the report, he is never given a knighthood, like the other Arctic explorers. He is given the reward of ten thousand pounds that the Admiralty had offered for news of Franklin. But for many years, he is left out of the history books.

[long-pause]

Today, we know that the Inuit were right. And John Rae is remembered as one of the greatest Arctic explorers of all.

[pause]

He also discovered something important. In 1854, on the same journey, Rae found that King William Island was an island, not a peninsula, as the old maps showed. Between the island and the mainland, there was a strait of water, which is now called Rae Strait. And through this strait, sheltered from the heavy ice of Victoria Strait, a ship could sail.

[pause]

{img:rae-strait-narrow-channel-island-mainland-summer-calm}
This was the last missing link of the Northwest Passage. The one that ships would use, fifty years later.

[long-pause]

Today, in the town of Stromness, in the Orkney Islands, where Erebus and Terror stopped on their way to the Arctic, there is a statue of John Rae, standing on the waterfront, looking out to sea, in his fur clothes, with his rifle.

[pause]

And in Westminster Abbey, in London, where so many famous people are buried and remembered, there is now a stone, put there in 2014, with the words: John Rae, Arctic explorer. Discoverer of the final link in the first navigable Northwest Passage.

[long-pause]

{img:small-steam-yacht-fox-sailing-north-1857-ice}
Lady Franklin does not stop searching, even after Rae's report.

[pause]

She wants to know more. She wants to find the ships, and the journals, and the records, and to know exactly what happened, and where, and whether her husband was the first man to find the Northwest Passage. She wants to give the men a proper memorial.

[long-pause]

The Admiralty will not send any more expeditions. So, in 1857, Lady Franklin buys a small steam yacht, called the Fox, with the help of money from the public, and sends it to the Arctic herself.

[pause]

The captain is Francis Leopold McClintock, an Irish naval officer, and one of the most experienced Arctic sledge travellers in the world. He takes no pay. He has a small crew of twenty-five men, many of whom have been in the Arctic before.

[pause]

The Fox is trapped in the ice in Baffin Bay, for the whole of its first winter, and drifts for more than a thousand miles. But in the second year, it gets through, into the Arctic channels, and spends the winter of 1858 to 1859 near the eastern entrance of Bellot Strait, not far from King William Island.

[long-pause]

In the spring of 1859, McClintock and his men set out on long sledge journeys, with dogs, to search King William Island.

[pause]

McClintock meets Inuit, who tell him more about the white men, and the ships. One ship, they say, was crushed by the ice, and sank. The other was driven ashore, and the Inuit took wood and metal from it. They give him more silver spoons and forks, and buttons, and other things.

[pause]

{img:dog-sledges-crossing-snow-island-spring-1859-sunshine}
McClintock's second in command, Lieutenant William Hobson, goes ahead, to the northwest coast of the island, to Victory Point.

[long-pause]

And on the sixth of May, 1859, Hobson finds the cairn.

[pause]

Inside it, there is a small metal cylinder. And in the cylinder, there is the note. The note written by Graham Gore, in May 1847, saying all well. And around the edges, the words added by Fitzjames and Crozier, in April 1848: Sir John Franklin died on the 11th June 1847. The ships deserted. A hundred and five souls. Start on tomorrow for Back's Fish River.

[pause]

{img:officer-kneeling-cairn-holding-small-paper-wind-snow}
After eleven years, the world knows at last what happened. Or at least, the beginning and the end.

[long-pause]

Along the coast, the searchers find other things, left behind by the men on their last journey: piles of clothing, and tools, and stores, at Victory Point, where the men came ashore.

[pause]

And further south, at a place now called Erebus Bay, they find a ship's boat, on a large sledge, with many things in it: books, and clothes, and shoes, and watches, and silver, and chocolate, and tea, and guns. They find, too, signs that some of the men had stayed there, at the end.

[pause]

McClintock writes about it calmly and kindly, with great respect for the men. He lays a stone over them, and reads the burial service.

[long-pause]

The Fox comes home to England in September 1859.

[pause]

And the news goes around the world. The note proves that the Franklin expedition had found the last link of the Northwest Passage. Because the ships had sailed down Peel Sound, and Franklin Strait, to King William Island. And from there, the coast of America, already mapped, ran all the way to the west. If they had been able to sail just a little further, they would have been through.

[pause]

{img:lady-franklin-receiving-mcclintock-drawing-room-1859}
So Lady Franklin and her friends say that Sir John Franklin was the discoverer of the Northwest Passage. And on his monument in London, which still stands today, in Waterloo Place, and on his memorial in Westminster Abbey, it says so.

[long-pause]

Lady Franklin lives on for many more years, until 1875, travelling, and working, and still hoping that the journals and the records of the expedition might one day be found.

[pause]

She passes away in July 1875, at the age of eighty-three. Two weeks later, the marble memorial to her husband is unveiled in Westminster Abbey, with a poem written by his nephew by marriage, the great poet Alfred Tennyson: Not here! the white North has thy bones; and thou, heroic sailor-soul, art passing on thine happier voyage now toward no earthly pole.

[long-pause]

{img:small-herring-boat-gjoa-sailing-arctic-1903-ice}
The Northwest Passage was finally sailed, from end to end, in a single ship, more than fifty years after Franklin, by a young Norwegian named Roald Amundsen.

[pause]

Amundsen had read about Franklin as a boy, and dreamed of the Arctic. He later wrote that the story of Franklin's expeditions, and their sufferings, had thrilled him more than anything else he had ever read.

[long-pause]

In 1903, at the age of thirty-one, Amundsen set out from Norway in a little old herring boat, called the Gjøa, only seventy feet long, with a small engine, and a crew of six other men.

[pause]

He did not take a great ship, with a hundred and thirty men, like Franklin. He took a small boat, which could sail in shallow water, among the islands, and a small crew, which could live off the land.

[pause]

He sailed across the Atlantic, and through Lancaster Sound, and down Peel Sound, and through Franklin Strait, just as Franklin had. But when he reached King William Island, he did not go down the west side, into the heavy ice of Victoria Strait. He went down the east side, through Rae Strait, the strait that John Rae had discovered.

[long-pause]

On the south coast of King William Island, he found a beautiful little harbour, sheltered from the wind and the ice. He called it Gjøa Haven, after his ship. And he stayed there for almost two years.

[pause]

There, Amundsen and his men lived among the Netsilik Inuit, and learned from them. They learned how to build snow houses, and how to drive dog sledges, and how to dress in caribou skins, and how to travel and hunt in the Arctic. Amundsen later said that he learned more from the Netsilik than from all the books he had ever read.

[pause]

{img:amundsen-learning-dog-sledge-inuit-teachers-snow}
He would use these lessons a few years later, when he became the first man to reach the South Pole, in 1911.

[long-pause]

In 1905, the Gjøa sailed on, westward, along the coast of America, through the waters that Franklin had hoped to reach. And in August, they met an American whaling ship, coming from the west.

[pause]

Amundsen wrote that he could not eat, from excitement. The Northwest Passage had been sailed.

[pause]

{img:whaling-ship-meeting-small-boat-arctic-coast-flags}
After another winter in the ice, the Gjøa reached Nome, in Alaska, in 1906, to a great welcome. The little ship is now kept in a museum in Oslo, in Norway.

[long-pause]

The little harbour where Amundsen stayed, Gjøa Haven, is now a town of about thirteen hundred people, most of them Inuit, many of them the descendants of the Netsilik who taught Amundsen. Its name in Inuktitut is Uqsuqtuuq, which means the place of lots of fat, because of the many seals and fish in the waters around it.

[pause]

It was from Gjoa Haven that Louie Kamookak came, and Sammy Kogvik, who helped to find the ships of Franklin. And there is a little heritage centre in the town, which tells the story of the Netsilik people, and of Amundsen, and of Franklin.

[pause]

In the years after Amundsen, a few more ships sailed through the Passage: a little Canadian police schooner, the St. Roch, in the 1940s, and great icebreakers, and in 1969, an American oil tanker, the Manhattan, specially strengthened, which pushed its way through, to see if it could carry oil from Alaska.

[long-pause]

Today, the Arctic is changing.

[pause]

As the climate of the earth grows warmer, the summer ice in the Arctic is shrinking, year by year. In some summers, now, the channels of the Northwest Passage are almost completely open, for a few weeks, in August and September, and ships can sail through, which would have been impossible in Franklin's time.

[pause]

{img:cruise-ship-arctic-channel-open-water-summer-cliffs}
Every summer, a few cruise ships and yachts and cargo ships sail through the Passage, carrying tourists and supplies, past the places where Erebus and Terror were trapped, and where the men walked south. The Inuit of the Arctic are watching these changes carefully, because the ice is their highway, and their hunting ground, and their home.

[long-pause]

{img:inuit-elder-telling-story-children-snow-house-lamp}
For more than a hundred and fifty years after McClintock, the story of the Franklin expedition went on. And the most important keepers of the story were the Inuit.

[pause]

The Inuit of the King William Island region, the Netsilik people, remembered the strangers who had come to their land, and the great ships in the ice, and the men walking south. They told the story to their children, and their children told it to their grandchildren, around the lamps of seal oil, in the long winter nights.

[long-pause]

In the 1860s, an American named Charles Francis Hall, a newspaper printer from Cincinnati, who had become obsessed with the story of Franklin, went to the Arctic, and lived with the Inuit for years, and learned their language, and wrote down everything they told him about the white men.

[pause]

He heard many stories: of the ships, and the men, and the places where things had been seen. Some of the stories were confused, or mixed with other expeditions. But many of them were true, and very detailed.

[pause]

In 1878 to 1880, another American, Frederick Schwatka, an army officer, made a long journey with Inuit companions, on sledges, across King William Island, and found more traces, and more graves, and listened to more stories.

[long-pause]

But for a long time, many people in the south did not take the Inuit stories very seriously.

[pause]

It was only much later, at the end of the twentieth century, that historians and archaeologists began to study the Inuit memories carefully, and to see how much they could tell.

[pause]

{img:modern-researchers-listening-elder-community-hall-maps}
A Canadian historian named David Woodman spent many years studying all the Inuit stories that had been written down, by Hall, and Schwatka, and others. And he believed that the stories pointed to the places where the ships might be: one of them crushed in the ice, and sunk, off the northwest coast of King William Island. And the other, further south, near an island, in a bay on the west coast of the Adelaide Peninsula, where the Inuit said they had seen it, and gone on board.

[long-pause]

And there was an Inuk man, named Louie Kamookak, from the little town of Gjoa Haven, on the south coast of King William Island, who spent his whole life gathering the stories.

[pause]

His great-grandmother, Hummahuk, had told him, when he was a boy, about the strangers who had come to the island, long ago, and had passed away there, and about the things her people had found. And he grew up to become a historian, travelling across the land, and talking to the elders, and writing down their stories, and searching for the places they described.

[pause]

{img:inuit-historian-man-standing-tundra-summer-looking-far}
Kamookak believed, all his life, that the Inuit stories could solve the mystery of Franklin. He helped many of the searchers who came to the island. He passed away in 2018. And he was right.

[long-pause]

In the summer, the Inuit of King William Island go out on the land and the water, to hunt and fish, as their people always have.

[pause]

They hunt seals, and caribou, and fish for Arctic char, in the lakes and rivers and the sea. They travel by boat, and by snowmobile, and on foot. And some of them, on their travels, have found things from the Franklin expedition: buttons, and pieces of wood, and bits of metal, and bones, and the places where the men stopped.

[pause]

They know the land, every bay and point and island, and every story that belongs to each place.

[long-pause]

In 2008, the Canadian government began a new search for the ships, with the Parks Canada underwater archaeologists, and the Canadian Coast Guard, and the Navy, and scientists, and the Inuit, all working together.

[pause]

For six summers, they searched the bottom of the sea, with sonar, in the cold, grey waters around King William Island, and in the short Arctic summer, when the ice was open, in fog, and wind, and rain.

[pause]

{img:coast-guard-ship-sonar-search-grey-arctic-sea-fog}
And they found nothing. Until 2014.

[long-pause]

{img:small-survey-boat-calm-grey-water-island-september}
In September 2014, a small team from Parks Canada is searching the waters in the south, near the Adelaide Peninsula, in the area where the Inuit stories said one of the ships had been seen.

[pause]

The weather in the north is too bad to search there, where they had planned. So they come south, to the shelter of the islands.

[long-pause]

And one day, on a little island in the area, a helicopter pilot and an archaeologist find, among the stones on the shore, a piece of iron, and a piece of wood, from a ship. It is part of a davit, the iron fitting that held one of the ship's boats, marked with the broad arrow, the sign of the Royal Navy.

[pause]

The searchers turn their sonar on the water nearby. And on the second of September, 2014, on the screen, the picture appears, clear and sharp, on the sea floor, eleven metres down: the shape of a ship.

[pause]

It is HMS Erebus. Franklin's own ship.

[long-pause]

She is sitting upright on the sea floor, in shallow water, in a place now called Wilmot and Crampton Bay, more than a hundred kilometres south of where the ships were abandoned in the ice. Just as the Inuit stories said.

[pause]

Her masts are gone, and part of her deck has been broken by the ice, over the years. But she is remarkably well preserved, in the cold, dark water. Her great iron bow plates are still there. Her ship's wheel is still there. Her bell is lying on the deck.

[pause]

{img:underwater-shipwreck-wooden-hull-kelp-cold-green-light}
The divers bring up the bell, of bronze, with the broad arrow, and the date, 1845. It is the first object recovered from either of Franklin's ships.

[long-pause]

Two years later, in September 2016, the second ship is found.

[pause]

An Inuk man, named Sammy Kogvik, from Gjoa Haven, tells a research ship that, some years before, he and a friend were out hunting, on a frozen bay on the southwest coast of King William Island, and saw a tall piece of wood, like a mast, sticking up out of the ice. He took a photograph, but he lost his camera, and he never told anyone, because he thought that it might be unlucky.

[pause]

The research ship goes to the bay, with Sammy Kogvik on board. And there, on the sea floor, twenty-four metres down, is HMS Terror. In a bay that has been called, for more than a hundred years, Terror Bay.

[long-pause]

Terror is in even better condition than Erebus.

[pause]

She is sitting upright, almost whole, with her masts broken, but her hull intact, and her hatches closed, and her windows still in place. Her propeller is still lowered, as if she had been sailing.

[pause]

{img:shipwreck-intact-deck-hatches-closed-silt-underwater}
When the divers send a little remote camera inside, they see the cabins of the officers, and the shelves, and the bottles, and the plates, still standing in neat rows, and the beds, and the drawers of Captain Crozier's desk, closed, under a layer of fine silt.

[pause]

The scientists believe that, inside those drawers, in the cold, dark, still water, there might be papers, and charts, and even journals, which have survived, perhaps, for more than a hundred and seventy years.

[long-pause]

Today, both wrecks are protected, as a National Historic Site of Canada, managed together by Parks Canada and the Inuit.

[pause]

The Inuit guardians watch over the sites, all through the summer, from a camp on the shore, to make sure that nobody disturbs them. And every summer, when the ice is open, the archaeologists and the divers go down, very carefully, in the cold, dark water, to study the ships, and to bring up some of the things inside.

[pause]

They have brought up hundreds of objects: plates, and cups, and bottles, and buttons, and shoes, and a hairbrush, and pencils, and a pair of officer's epaulettes, still with their golden fringes, and a cannon, and the ship's wheel, and a writing desk, and a pair of glasses.

[long-pause]

And slowly, more and more of the men are being found, and named.

[pause]

The scientists have taken DNA from the remains of some of the men found on King William Island over the years, and compared it with DNA from their living relatives, descendants of their brothers and sisters, in Britain and in other countries.

[pause]

{img:laboratory-scientist-quiet-careful-work-soft-light}
In 2021, one of the men was identified for the first time: John Gregory, an engineer of Erebus, from Portsea, near Portsmouth. And in 2024, another: Captain James Fitzjames himself, the cheerful commander of Erebus, who wrote such lively letters home.

[pause]

After more than a hundred and seventy years, they have their names back.

[long-pause]

{img:beechey-island-three-graves-white-headstones-gravel-today}
Today, you can still visit some of the places of the Franklin expedition, if you travel to the far north of Canada.

[pause]

It is not easy. The Arctic is still remote, and the summers are short, and the ice is unpredictable. But every summer, a few small cruise ships, with explorers and scientists and visitors on board, sail into Lancaster Sound, and stop at Beechey Island.

[long-pause]

On the gravel beach of Beechey Island, the three graves of John Torrington, John Hartnell, and William Braine are still there, with their headboards. The original wooden boards were taken away, many years ago, to keep them safe, and copies have been put in their place.

[pause]

Beside them, there is a fourth grave, of a sailor from one of the search expeditions, who passed away there in 1854. And nearby, there is a memorial to Franklin and his men, put up by the searchers, and a cenotaph, and the ruins of a storehouse built by one of the search expeditions, called Northumberland House.

[pause]

Visitors walk quietly among the graves, and the ruins, and look out over the bay where Erebus and Terror spent their first winter. It is very still, and very cold, even in summer. The wind blows from the north. The cliffs rise up, dark and bare. Some visitors leave a small stone, or a flower, or a few words of prayer.

[long-pause]

On King William Island, there are many places connected with the expedition: Victory Point, where the note was found, and Erebus Bay, and Terror Bay, and the long west coast, where the men walked south.

[pause]

Some of these places have little stone markers now, and cairns, put up by later visitors. The Inuit guardians of Gjoa Haven watch over them, and over the wrecks, in the summer.

[pause]

{img:king-william-island-coast-cairn-stones-summer-tundra}
The land is still bare, and flat, and lonely, with little lakes, and gravel ridges, and the sea ice, white and grey, stretching away to the horizon. In the summer, the tundra is full of little flowers, and the birds come back, and the caribou wander across the land.

[long-pause]

In Gjoa Haven, you can visit the Nattilik Heritage Centre, which tells the story of the Netsilik people, and of Amundsen, and of the Franklin expedition, from the Inuit point of view.

[pause]

And in the town, you can meet the people whose ancestors met the men of the expedition, and who kept the memories of them alive, for more than a hundred and seventy years.

[pause]

In the museums of the south, in Ottawa, and in Greenwich, near London, there are exhibitions of the things found from the expedition: the silver forks and spoons, the buttons, the tins, the bell of Erebus, and now the objects brought up from the wrecks.

[long-pause]

In London, in Waterloo Place, near Pall Mall, there is a statue of Sir John Franklin, in his naval uniform, with a telescope, and on the pedestal, the names of all the officers and men of the expedition, and the words: to the great Arctic navigator, and his brave companions, who sacrificed their lives in completing the discovery of the Northwest Passage.

[pause]

In Spilsby, in Lincolnshire, the little market town where Franklin was born, there is another statue of him, in the market square, where the market is still held every week.

[pause]

{img:spilsby-market-square-bronze-statue-explorer-quiet}
And in Westminster Abbey, there is the marble memorial, with Tennyson's poem. And beside it now, the stone for John Rae.

[long-pause]

And at the bottom of the sea, in the cold, still water off King William Island, Erebus and Terror lie quietly, upright, as they have for more than a hundred and seventy years.

[pause]

Every summer, the divers go down to them, a little at a time, carefully, gently, and bring up more of their secrets. Perhaps, one day, they will find the papers, the logbooks and the journals, in the drawers and the cabins, that will tell us at last the whole story.

[pause]

But perhaps not. Perhaps some of the story will always remain a mystery, kept by the ice, and the sea, and the memories of the Inuit.

[long-pause]

So the story of Erebus and Terror ends here for tonight, with the ships asleep under the Arctic water, and the long summer light falling on the island where the men once walked.

[pause]

A hundred and twenty-nine men, who sailed into the ice with hope, and the people of the north, who remembered them.
