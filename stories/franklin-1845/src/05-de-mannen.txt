Who are the men of Erebus and Terror?

There are a hundred and thirty-four men on the two ships when they sail from England. Five of them will be sent home from Greenland, because they are ill, or unsuitable. So a hundred and twenty-nine men will go on into the Arctic.

--

The captain of Erebus, under Franklin, is Commander James Fitzjames.

He is thirty-two years old, tall, handsome, cheerful, and full of jokes and stories. He has served in the Navy all over the world: in the Middle East, where he was wounded, and in the war against China, and once, he swam into a river in Liverpool, with all his clothes on, to save a drowning man. He is very popular with the other officers, and with the men.

On the voyage, Fitzjames writes long, lively letters home, to his friends, the family of Sir John Barrow, describing all the officers, one by one, with humour and affection. Thanks to his letters, we know a little about many of the men.

--

The captain of Terror, and second in command of the whole expedition, is Captain Francis Crozier.

He is forty-nine, an Irishman, from Banbridge, in the north of Ireland, the son of a lawyer. He is quiet, serious, careful, and very experienced. He has been to the Arctic four times, and to the Antarctic once, commanding Terror. He knows more about the ice than anyone else on the expedition.

{img:captain-crozier-serious-irish-officer-pipe-cabin-lamp}
But he is not happy, as they sail. He is lonely, and a little melancholy, perhaps because Sophia Cracroft has refused him. He writes to his friend, James Clark Ross, that he is in very low spirits.

--

There are other officers.

There is Lieutenant Graham Gore, of Erebus, a fine young officer, and a good artist, who plays the flute, and is a good shot. Fitzjames writes that he is a man of most stable character, a capital fellow. There is Lieutenant John Irving, of Terror, a serious, religious young Scotsman. There is Lieutenant Henry Le Vesconte, and Lieutenant James Fairholme, and the ice masters, James Reid and Thomas Blanky, experienced whaling men, who know the ways of the ice.

There is the surgeon of Erebus, Stephen Stanley, and the assistant surgeon, Harry Goodsir, a young Scottish doctor and naturalist, of twenty-five, from Edinburgh, who is fascinated by the tiny creatures of the sea, and spends his days collecting them, and studying them under his microscope. Fitzjames writes that Goodsir is very pleasant, and when he gets hold of a jellyfish, he is perfectly happy.

--

And there are the men: the petty officers, and the seamen, and the Royal Marines, and the stokers, who look after the steam engines, and the boys.

They come from all over Britain, and some from further away: from London, and Kent, and Yorkshire, and Scotland, and Ireland, and Wales. Some of them are experienced Arctic sailors, who have been with Ross or Parry before. Some are young, and have never seen the ice.

{img:sailors-1845-on-deck-coiling-ropes-laughing-sunshine}
There is John Torrington, a leading stoker on Terror, who is nineteen. There is John Hartnell, an able seaman on Erebus, and his brother, Thomas. There is William Braine, a Royal Marine. There are the ships' cooks, and carpenters, and sailmakers, and the captains' stewards. There are boys of eighteen, and men of fifty.

--

There are also some animals on board.

There is a monkey, called Jacko, given to the officers of Erebus as a pet, who is full of mischief. There is a big dog, a Newfoundland, called Neptune. And there are cats, as on every ship, to catch the rats.

Fitzjames writes that the officers are a remarkably fine set of fellows, and that they all get on very well together. He writes that he has never been in a ship where there was such a good spirit.

--

Before they sail, at Greenhithe, a photographer named Richard Beard comes on board, and takes daguerreotypes, early photographs, of Franklin and his officers, one by one, sitting in their best uniforms.

These are some of the first photographs ever taken of an expedition. They still exist today. You can see the faces of the officers: Franklin, round and kindly, a little tired. Fitzjames, handsome, with his hand tucked into his coat. Crozier, serious, with his arms folded. Gore, and Goodsir, and the others, young men, looking calmly into the camera.

{img:daguerreotype-studio-officer-sitting-still-1845-camera}
Looking at them, you can almost hear them, waiting patiently for the photograph to be taken, which took several seconds, sitting very still, and perhaps thinking about home, and the voyage ahead.
