What was it like, the second winter, in the ice off King William Island?

We can imagine it, from the journals of other Arctic expeditions of the time, and from what the Inuit later remembered, and from what the archaeologists have found.

--

The men would have kept to the routine: exercise, school, work, and entertainment.

They would have gone out hunting, across the ice, for seals, and polar bears, and the birds that came in the spring. But there is very little game in this part of the Arctic, especially in winter. Later explorers would find that this was one of the poorest hunting grounds in the whole Arctic. Even the Inuit rarely came here.

The tinned food would have been the main part of their diet. Some historians and scientists believe that the lead solder in the tins, and perhaps the lead pipes of the water system on the ships, may have slowly poisoned some of the men, making them weak and ill, and confused. Others think that this did not matter so much. Scientists are still studying it.

--

And the cold. The cold of the Arctic winter is beyond anything most people can imagine.

At forty degrees below zero, the breath freezes as soon as it leaves the mouth, and forms frost on the beard and eyebrows. Metal sticks to the skin. Ink freezes in the pens, and the men must write in pencil. Below decks, in spite of the heating, the walls and ceilings are covered with frost, from the breath of the men, and the steam of the cooking, which drips down on them when the stove is lit.

{img:frost-covered-beams-below-deck-men-wrapped-warm-stove}
The men wear thick woollen clothes, and flannel, and great coats, and fur caps, and mittens. But the Navy's clothes are not as warm as the clothes of the Inuit, made of caribou fur and sealskin, which are the warmest clothes in the world.

--

And there is the darkness, for months.

Arctic explorers wrote that the long darkness was the hardest thing to bear. Men became sad, and quiet, and could not sleep, or slept too much. The officers worked hard to keep everyone busy, and cheerful.

{img:aurora-borealis-green-curtains-sky-ship-silhouette}
But there is also beauty.

On clear nights, the Northern Lights, the aurora borealis, shimmer across the sky, in great curtains and waves of green and white and red, moving and dancing, silently, over the frozen sea. The stars are brighter than anywhere else on earth. The moon circles the sky, for days, without setting, and lights up the ice like silver.

--

In the spring of 1847, the light comes back.

First a faint glow in the south, at midday. Then, one day in February, the first glimpse of the sun, just the top of it, peeping over the horizon, for a few minutes, and then sinking again. The men cheer. Every day after that, it stays a little longer.

And by May, the sun is shining all day and all night again, on the dazzling white ice. The men must wear goggles, or veils, to protect their eyes from snow blindness.

--

In May 1847, Franklin sends out a party of men, on foot, with a sledge, across the ice, to King William Island, to explore the coast, and perhaps to look for a way south.

The party is led by Lieutenant Graham Gore, the flute player, the man of most stable character. With him is the mate, Charles Des Voeux, and six men.

{img:sledge-party-eight-men-hauling-across-ice-sunlit}
They pull their sledge across the ice, about fifteen miles, to the shore of King William Island. And at a place called Victory Point, on the northwest coast, they find an old cairn of stones, built many years before by James Clark Ross, who had come here on foot, seventeen years earlier.

And in the cairn, they leave a message.
