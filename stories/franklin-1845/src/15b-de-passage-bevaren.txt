The Northwest Passage was finally sailed, from end to end, in a single ship, more than fifty years after Franklin, by a young Norwegian named Roald Amundsen.

Amundsen had read about Franklin as a boy, and dreamed of the Arctic. He later wrote that the story of Franklin's expeditions, and their sufferings, had thrilled him more than anything else he had ever read.

--

In 1903, at the age of thirty-one, Amundsen set out from Norway in a little old herring boat, called the Gjøa, only seventy feet long, with a small engine, and a crew of six other men.

He did not take a great ship, with a hundred and thirty men, like Franklin. He took a small boat, which could sail in shallow water, among the islands, and a small crew, which could live off the land.

He sailed across the Atlantic, and through Lancaster Sound, and down Peel Sound, and through Franklin Strait, just as Franklin had. But when he reached King William Island, he did not go down the west side, into the heavy ice of Victoria Strait. He went down the east side, through Rae Strait, the strait that John Rae had discovered.

--

On the south coast of King William Island, he found a beautiful little harbour, sheltered from the wind and the ice. He called it Gjøa Haven, after his ship. And he stayed there for almost two years.

There, Amundsen and his men lived among the Netsilik Inuit, and learned from them. They learned how to build snow houses, and how to drive dog sledges, and how to dress in caribou skins, and how to travel and hunt in the Arctic. Amundsen later said that he learned more from the Netsilik than from all the books he had ever read.

{img:amundsen-learning-dog-sledge-inuit-teachers-snow}
He would use these lessons a few years later, when he became the first man to reach the South Pole, in 1911.

--

In 1905, the Gjøa sailed on, westward, along the coast of America, through the waters that Franklin had hoped to reach. And in August, they met an American whaling ship, coming from the west.

Amundsen wrote that he could not eat, from excitement. The Northwest Passage had been sailed.

{img:whaling-ship-meeting-small-boat-arctic-coast-flags}
After another winter in the ice, the Gjøa reached Nome, in Alaska, in 1906, to a great welcome. The little ship is now kept in a museum in Oslo, in Norway.

--

The little harbour where Amundsen stayed, Gjøa Haven, is now a town of about thirteen hundred people, most of them Inuit, many of them the descendants of the Netsilik who taught Amundsen. Its name in Inuktitut is Uqsuqtuuq, which means the place of lots of fat, because of the many seals and fish in the waters around it.

It was from Gjoa Haven that Louie Kamookak came, and Sammy Kogvik, who helped to find the ships of Franklin. And there is a little heritage centre in the town, which tells the story of the Netsilik people, and of Amundsen, and of Franklin.

In the years after Amundsen, a few more ships sailed through the Passage: a little Canadian police schooner, the St. Roch, in the 1940s, and great icebreakers, and in 1969, an American oil tanker, the Manhattan, specially strengthened, which pushed its way through, to see if it could carry oil from Alaska.

--

Today, the Arctic is changing.

As the climate of the earth grows warmer, the summer ice in the Arctic is shrinking, year by year. In some summers, now, the channels of the Northwest Passage are almost completely open, for a few weeks, in August and September, and ships can sail through, which would have been impossible in Franklin's time.

{img:cruise-ship-arctic-channel-open-water-summer-cliffs}
Every summer, a few cruise ships and yachts and cargo ships sail through the Passage, carrying tourists and supplies, past the places where Erebus and Terror were trapped, and where the men walked south. The Inuit of the Arctic are watching these changes carefully, because the ice is their highway, and their hunting ground, and their home.
