Today, you can still visit some of the places of the Franklin expedition, if you travel to the far north of Canada.

It is not easy. The Arctic is still remote, and the summers are short, and the ice is unpredictable. But every summer, a few small cruise ships, with explorers and scientists and visitors on board, sail into Lancaster Sound, and stop at Beechey Island.

--

On the gravel beach of Beechey Island, the three graves of John Torrington, John Hartnell, and William Braine are still there, with their headboards. The original wooden boards were taken away, many years ago, to keep them safe, and copies have been put in their place.

Beside them, there is a fourth grave, of a sailor from one of the search expeditions, who passed away there in 1854. And nearby, there is a memorial to Franklin and his men, put up by the searchers, and a cenotaph, and the ruins of a storehouse built by one of the search expeditions, called Northumberland House.

Visitors walk quietly among the graves, and the ruins, and look out over the bay where Erebus and Terror spent their first winter. It is very still, and very cold, even in summer. The wind blows from the north. The cliffs rise up, dark and bare. Some visitors leave a small stone, or a flower, or a few words of prayer.

--

On King William Island, there are many places connected with the expedition: Victory Point, where the note was found, and Erebus Bay, and Terror Bay, and the long west coast, where the men walked south.

Some of these places have little stone markers now, and cairns, put up by later visitors. The Inuit guardians of Gjoa Haven watch over them, and over the wrecks, in the summer.

{img:king-william-island-coast-cairn-stones-summer-tundra}
The land is still bare, and flat, and lonely, with little lakes, and gravel ridges, and the sea ice, white and grey, stretching away to the horizon. In the summer, the tundra is full of little flowers, and the birds come back, and the caribou wander across the land.

--

In Gjoa Haven, you can visit the Nattilik Heritage Centre, which tells the story of the Netsilik people, and of Amundsen, and of the Franklin expedition, from the Inuit point of view.

And in the town, you can meet the people whose ancestors met the men of the expedition, and who kept the memories of them alive, for more than a hundred and seventy years.

In the museums of the south, in Ottawa, and in Greenwich, near London, there are exhibitions of the things found from the expedition: the silver forks and spoons, the buttons, the tins, the bell of Erebus, and now the objects brought up from the wrecks.

--

In London, in Waterloo Place, near Pall Mall, there is a statue of Sir John Franklin, in his naval uniform, with a telescope, and on the pedestal, the names of all the officers and men of the expedition, and the words: to the great Arctic navigator, and his brave companions, who sacrificed their lives in completing the discovery of the Northwest Passage.

In Spilsby, in Lincolnshire, the little market town where Franklin was born, there is another statue of him, in the market square, where the market is still held every week.

{img:spilsby-market-square-bronze-statue-explorer-quiet}
And in Westminster Abbey, there is the marble memorial, with Tennyson's poem. And beside it now, the stone for John Rae.

--

And at the bottom of the sea, in the cold, still water off King William Island, Erebus and Terror lie quietly, upright, as they have for more than a hundred and seventy years.

Every summer, the divers go down to them, a little at a time, carefully, gently, and bring up more of their secrets. Perhaps, one day, they will find the papers, the logbooks and the journals, in the drawers and the cabins, that will tell us at last the whole story.

But perhaps not. Perhaps some of the story will always remain a mystery, kept by the ice, and the sea, and the memories of the Inuit.

--

So the story of Erebus and Terror ends here for tonight, with the ships asleep under the Arctic water, and the long summer light falling on the island where the men once walked.

A hundred and twenty-nine men, who sailed into the ice with hope, and the people of the north, who remembered them.
