Lady Jane Franklin is one of the most remarkable women of her time.

She was born in 1791, in London, the daughter of a rich silk weaver, and she grew up clever and curious and restless. As a girl, she read everything, and wrote long diaries, and made lists of all the books she read, and all the things she wanted to learn.

--

She loves to travel. Before and after her marriage, she travels all over Europe, and to Russia, and to Egypt, where she sails up the Nile, and to Greece, and the Holy Land, and Turkey, at a time when very few women travel at all, and certainly not alone.

In Australia, when her husband is governor of Van Diemen's Land, she is the first woman to travel overland from Melbourne to Sydney. She climbs mountains. She starts a museum, and a college, and a scientific society. She tries to improve the lives of the women convicts in the colony.

Some people think she is too strong-minded, and too interested in her husband's work, for a woman of that time. But Franklin loves her, and admires her, and trusts her judgement completely.

--

In the spring of 1845, as the expedition is being prepared, Lady Franklin is busy too.

She helps her husband in every way she can. She visits the ships, and talks to the officers. She sews a silk flag, a Union Jack, for her husband to raise when he reaches the Pacific, at the end of the Passage.

{img:woman-sewing-silk-union-flag-drawing-room-window}
There is a story that, a few days before he sails, Franklin falls asleep on the sofa, and Lady Franklin, who has just finished the flag, lays it over him, to keep him warm. And Franklin wakes up, and is startled, and says: why, there is a flag thrown over me! Because in the Navy, a flag is laid over a man only when he has passed away. Lady Franklin is upset, and says she meant nothing by it. Nobody knows if the story is true.

--

With them, much of the time, is Franklin's niece, Sophia Cracroft, a lively, clever, pretty young woman of about thirty, who lives with them, and goes everywhere with Lady Franklin, as her companion.

Sophia is admired by many men. One of them is Captain Francis Crozier, who will be second in command of the expedition, and who has asked her to marry him, more than once. But Sophia has always said no. She likes him, but she does not love him.

{img:young-woman-1840s-garden-letter-thoughtful-summer}
Crozier sails away in 1845 still loving her, and still hoping.

--

Franklin's daughter, Eleanor, is twenty-one in 1845, and very fond of her father, and close to her stepmother, although they sometimes disagree.

The family goes down to the ships, at Greenhithe, on the Thames, a few days before they sail, to say goodbye. Eleanor writes later that her father was cheerful, and full of hope.

And Franklin writes letters home to them, from the voyage, as long as he can: from the Orkney Islands, and from Greenland. Long, loving, thoughtful letters, full of news of the ships, and the men, and the weather, and of his faith in God, and his hopes for the voyage.

--

Lady Franklin does not know, in May 1845, that she will never see her husband again.

And she does not know that, for the next fourteen years, and more, she will spend all her energy, and almost all her money, trying to find out what happened to him. She will become one of the most famous women in the world, because of it.

{img:woman-at-window-looking-out-to-sea-dusk-waiting}
But in the spring of 1845, there is no sadness. Only excitement, and pride, and the bustle of preparation, as the two great ships are made ready for the Arctic.
