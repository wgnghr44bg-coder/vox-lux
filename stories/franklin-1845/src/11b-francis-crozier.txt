Let us stay for a little while with Francis Crozier, the man who led the expedition in its last year.

He was born in 1796, in Banbridge, a small linen town in County Down, in the north of Ireland, the son of a lawyer, one of thirteen children. His family were Presbyterians, serious, hard-working people. As a boy, he loved the sea, and at the age of thirteen, he joined the Royal Navy.

--

As a young midshipman, Crozier sailed to the Pacific, and visited Pitcairn Island, where the descendants of the mutineers of the Bounty lived. He met the last survivor of the mutiny, an old man named John Adams, who was by then the gentle and religious leader of the little island community.

Then he went to the Arctic, with William Edward Parry, on three voyages in the 1820s, looking for the Northwest Passage, and trying to reach the North Pole over the ice. On those voyages, he learned everything about the ice: how it moves, how to sail through it, how to survive the winter in it, how to travel on it with sledges.

And he met another young officer, James Clark Ross, who became his closest friend for life.

--

Crozier was a quiet man, and rather shy. He was not brilliant or charming, like Fitzjames, or famous, like Franklin. He was not from a rich or important family. So he was promoted slowly, and had to wait many years for his chances.

But he was very good at his work. He studied the magnetism of the earth, and the stars, and became an expert in the scientific measurements that the Navy expeditions made. He was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society, the great society of scientists in London, which was a great honour.

{img:officer-taking-magnetic-measurements-instrument-tent-ice}
The sailors liked him and trusted him. He was fair and calm, and he knew what he was doing.

--

From 1839 to 1843, Crozier went with Ross to the Antarctic, as captain of Terror.

They sailed further south than anyone had ever been, through the pack ice, into a great open sea, which is now called the Ross Sea. They discovered the great volcano, Mount Erebus, and a vast wall of ice, hundreds of feet high, stretching for hundreds of miles, which Ross called the Barrier, and which we now call the Ross Ice Shelf. A cape at the foot of Mount Terror is named Cape Crozier, after him. It is where the emperor penguins nest, in the middle of the Antarctic winter.

{img:emperor-penguins-antarctic-ice-cliffs-pale-light}
It was during that voyage, when the ships stopped for some months in Hobart, in Van Diemen's Land, that Crozier met the governor's niece, Sophia Cracroft, and fell in love with her.

--

He asked her to marry him, in Hobart. She said no. He asked her again, in England. She said no again.

Sophia liked Crozier, and respected him. But she found him a little dull, perhaps. And she did not want to marry a sailor, who would always be away at sea. She never married at all, in the end. She stayed with her aunt, Lady Franklin, all her life, and helped her in the long search for the expedition, and wrote thousands of letters.

When the expedition was planned, in 1844, Crozier was not sure whether he wanted to go. He was tired, after the long voyage to the Antarctic, and sad about Sophia. But in the end, he agreed to go, out of loyalty to Franklin, and to his friend Ross, and to the Navy.

--

So it was Crozier, the quiet, careful, experienced Irishman, who led the men in their last year, after Franklin had passed away, through the third winter in the ice, and then onto the land, and south, along the coast.

We do not know what happened to him. His grave has never been found. The Inuit stories tell of a leader, a tall man, who stayed with his men, and walked with them, and was kind to the Inuit he met. Some people believe that this was Crozier.

{img:tall-figure-walking-snow-distance-pale-sky-quiet}
In Banbridge, his home town, there is a monument to him, in the town square, opposite the house where he was born: a tall stone pedestal, with polar bears at the corners, and a statue of Crozier on top, in his naval uniform, looking out to the north.
