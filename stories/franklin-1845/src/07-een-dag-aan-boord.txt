Let us spend an ordinary day on board HMS Erebus, in the Arctic summer of 1845, as she sails westward through Lancaster Sound.

The day on board a Navy ship is divided into watches, of four hours each. Half of the men are on duty, while the other half rest, or sleep, in their hammocks, below decks. A bell is rung every half hour, to mark the time.

--

In the morning, the men on deck scrub the decks, with sand and stones, called holystones, and sea water, as they do every morning on every ship of the Navy. They coil the ropes, and polish the brass, and check the rigging.

Then breakfast, at about eight o'clock: oatmeal porridge, or cocoa, and biscuit, the hard, dry ship's bread, with butter, and tea.

The men eat together, in messes, groups of six or eight men, sitting at wooden tables, which hang from the beams of the lower deck. Each mess takes turns to fetch the food from the galley, the kitchen, where the cook prepares it on a great iron stove, which also heats the ship.

--

During the morning, the men work the ship.

If there is a wind, they set the sails, climbing up the rigging, high above the deck, in the cold wind, to loosen or furl them. When the ice is thick, the ships must push through it, slowly, or wait for it to open. Sometimes the men go out onto the ice, with ropes, and pull the ship along a channel of open water, or cut a way through the ice with saws and axes.

{img:men-on-ice-pulling-ropes-ship-channel-white-floes}
The ice masters, Reid and Blanky, stand high up in the crow's nest, a barrel at the top of the mainmast, looking out for leads, the channels of open water through the ice, and calling down to the officer on deck which way to steer.

--

At noon, the officers take the position of the ship, by measuring the height of the sun, with their sextants. And then there is dinner, the main meal of the day, for the men.

Dinner might be salt beef, or salt pork, boiled, with peas, or preserved meat from the tins, with potatoes, also from tins, and soup. On Sundays, there is a pudding, of flour and suet and raisins, called plum duff, which the sailors love. And every day, each man gets his ration of grog: rum, mixed with water, and his ration of lemon juice, to keep away scurvy.

When they can, the men hunt, to get fresh meat: seabirds, and ducks, and seals. Fresh meat helps to keep them healthy.

--

In the afternoon, the scientific work goes on.

The officers take measurements, every hour, of the temperature, and the pressure of the air, and the wind, and the magnetism of the earth, with special instruments, and write them all down, carefully, in their logbooks. Goodsir, the naturalist, lowers his nets into the sea, and pulls up little shrimps, and jellyfish, and sea creatures, and studies them under his microscope, and draws them, and preserves them in jars of spirit.

{img:naturalist-microscope-jars-specimens-cabin-lamp-drawing}
The officers keep journals, and write letters, which they will send home whenever they meet a ship, or keep, to post when they reach the Pacific.

--

Then supper, at about five o'clock: tea, and biscuit, and perhaps something left over from dinner.

In the evening, if the men are not on watch, they have some free time. They mend their clothes, and play cards, and draughts, and chess. They read, from the ship's library, or have someone read to them, because not all of them can read. They sing songs, and tell stories, and play the fiddle, and dance, on the deck. They play the hand organ.

And on Sundays, there is a church service on deck, or below, when it is cold, with prayers and hymns, led by Sir John Franklin himself, who reads the service, and sometimes preaches. The men say that he reads very beautifully.

--

In the Arctic summer, the sun does not set. It circles around the sky, low over the horizon, all night long, and the light never fades. The ice glitters, and the sea is calm, and the cliffs of the islands glow golden in the midnight sun.

The men go to sleep in their hammocks, in the narrow, crowded lower deck, a few inches apart, with the ship creaking around them, and the ice grinding gently against the hull.

{img:midnight-sun-golden-cliffs-ship-calm-sea-ice}
And the ship sails on, westward, into the unknown.
