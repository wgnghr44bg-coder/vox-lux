While Erebus and Terror lay in the ice, off the coast of King William Island, the Netsilik Inuit were living their lives on the land around them, as their people had done for hundreds of years.

Their name means the people of the seal. They are a small group, perhaps a few hundred people, living in family groups, moving from place to place through the year, following the animals.

--

In the winter, they live out on the sea ice, in villages of snow houses, igloos, built in an hour or two, of blocks of hard snow, cut with knives of bone or antler, and fitted together in a spiral, into a dome.

Inside, a snow house is surprisingly warm. There is a raised platform of snow, at the back, covered with caribou skins, where the family sleeps, all together, under thick fur blankets. And there is a lamp, a shallow dish of soapstone, filled with seal oil, with a wick of moss, which burns with a soft, steady yellow flame, and gives light and warmth, and is used for cooking, and for melting snow for water, and for drying clothes.

The woman of the family looks after the lamp. It is the heart of the home.

--

In the winter, the men hunt seals, at their breathing holes in the ice.

A seal must come up to breathe, every few minutes, and it keeps several small holes open in the ice, all winter long, under the snow. The hunter finds a breathing hole, with the help of his dog, and stands beside it, perfectly still, in the cold and the dark, for hours, waiting, with his harpoon ready, for the seal to come up. It takes great patience.

{img:hunter-waiting-still-seal-breathing-hole-twilight-snow}
When a seal is caught, the meat is shared among all the families of the village, according to old rules, so that nobody goes hungry while there is food in the camp.

--

In the spring, the families move to the land, and to the lakes and the rivers.

They fish for Arctic char, a beautiful fish, like a salmon, with red and silver skin, which swims up the rivers from the sea, in great numbers, in the late summer. They build stone weirs across the rivers, little walls, to trap the fish, and spear them with three-pronged spears. Some of these old stone weirs, hundreds of years old, are still there today.

{img:stone-fish-weir-river-tundra-people-spearing-char}
And in the summer and the autumn, they hunt caribou, which come in great herds across the land, and use their skins for clothing and tents, and their antlers and bones for tools, and their meat for food.

--

The clothes of the Inuit are made by the women, from caribou skin, which is light and very warm, because each caribou hair is hollow, and holds the warm air.

They wear two layers: an inner one with the fur turned inward, against the skin, and an outer one with the fur turned outward. With boots of sealskin, waterproof, and mittens of fur, and a hood, with a ruff of wolverine fur, around the face, which does not freeze. These are the warmest clothes in the world. The men of Erebus and Terror, in their woollen Navy coats, would have been much colder.

{img:inuit-woman-sewing-caribou-parka-bone-needle-lamp}
The women sew the clothes with needles made of bone, and thread made from the sinews of caribou, with tiny, careful stitches, so close that no wind or water can get through.

--

The Netsilik have their own beliefs, and their own stories.

They believe that the animals have souls, and must be treated with respect, or they will not come back to be hunted. There is the sea goddess, Nuliajuk, who lives at the bottom of the sea, and keeps the seals and the other sea creatures, and must be kept happy. There are shamans, angakkuit, who can travel to the spirit world, and speak with the spirits, and heal the sick.

And there are long winter evenings, in the snow houses, with games, and songs, and drum dances, and stories: stories of the old times, and of the animals, and of the spirits, and of strange things that have happened. Stories that are remembered, and passed on, for generations.

--

In the years after 1848, the Netsilik find strange things on their land.

They find wood, which is very precious to them, because there are no trees in their country. And metal, iron and copper and brass, which they can make into knives and tools and harpoon points, much better than stone or bone. They find these things in the places where the white men had been, and on the ship that had been driven into the bay.

{img:inuit-craftsman-making-knife-blade-from-old-metal}
And so, the things of the Franklin expedition become part of the life of the Netsilik, for many years: a knife blade made from a piece of the ship, a spoon, a button, a piece of wood made into a sledge runner. And the story of the strangers and their ships becomes part of their stories, told around the lamps.
