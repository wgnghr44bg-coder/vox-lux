The men of Erebus and Terror, in their first year in the Arctic, see many animals that they have never seen before.

The most famous, and the most impressive, is the polar bear. The polar bear is the largest hunter that lives on land, larger than any lion or tiger. A big male can weigh more than half a ton, and stand taller than a man on his hind legs. Its fur is thick and creamy white, and its black nose and its small dark eyes are the only dark things about it.

--

Polar bears live on the sea ice, and hunt seals. They can swim for days, across open water, with their great paws. They can smell a seal from more than a mile away. They walk slowly, swaying their long necks from side to side, across the ice, and they are curious, and often come close to the ships, to see what these strange new things are.

The Inuit call the polar bear Nanuq, and respect it more than any other animal. They believe it is wise, and almost like a person.

{img:arctic-fox-white-winter-coat-curled-snow-drift}
There are Arctic foxes too, small and pretty, with thick white fur in winter, and brown in summer, and a great bushy tail, which they curl around themselves to sleep. They follow the polar bears, to eat what the bears leave behind. They are bold, and come close to the ships, looking for scraps. On earlier expeditions, the sailors tamed them, and kept them as pets.

--

In the sea, there are seals of many kinds: the ringed seal, the small, common seal of the Arctic, and the bearded seal, with its long whiskers, and the harp seal, with its pattern like a harp on its back.

There are walruses, huge and heavy, with long ivory tusks, and bristly moustaches, lying in great crowds on the ice floes and the beaches, snorting and grunting and pushing each other, and sleeping in the sun.

And there are whales: the great bowhead whale, with its huge head and its smiling mouth, which the whaling ships hunt for its oil. And the white whale, the beluga, which swims in groups, singing, so that the sailors call it the sea canary. And the strangest of all, the narwhal, with a single long, spiral tusk of ivory, growing straight out of its head, like the horn of a unicorn.

--

In the Middle Ages, people in Europe bought narwhal tusks, brought from the Arctic by Viking traders, and believed that they were the horns of unicorns, and paid enormous sums for them, because they were thought to protect against poison. The throne of the kings of Denmark is made partly of narwhal tusks.

{img:narwhals-surfacing-calm-arctic-water-tusks-mist}
On the land, there are herds of caribou, and musk oxen: shaggy, dark brown animals, like small buffalo, with long hair reaching almost to the ground, and curved horns. When they are threatened, the musk oxen stand together in a circle, with their horns facing outward, and their calves in the middle.

--

There are lemmings, little round, furry animals, like hamsters, which live under the snow in winter, and in some years appear in enormous numbers, everywhere.

And there are the birds. In the summer, millions of birds come to the Arctic, to nest on the cliffs and the tundra, and to raise their young in the endless daylight: little auks, and guillemots, and fulmars, and kittiwakes, and eider ducks, and snow geese, and terns. The Arctic tern flies all the way from the Antarctic, at the other end of the world, every year, to nest in the Arctic, and back again in the autumn. It sees more daylight than any other creature on earth.

{img:seabird-cliffs-arctic-summer-thousands-of-birds-sea}
There is the snowy owl, white and silent, hunting lemmings across the tundra. And the ptarmigan, a kind of grouse, which turns white in winter, and stays in the Arctic all year round.

--

The men shoot birds and seals for fresh meat, and the officers collect specimens of the birds and animals, and press the little Arctic flowers in books, to bring home to the scientists of England.

Goodsir, the naturalist, is especially happy. He writes in his journal about the little creatures he dredges up from the sea floor, and the birds, and the plants, with great delight. Many of his notes, and his specimens, must still be somewhere, perhaps on board one of the ships, at the bottom of the sea.
