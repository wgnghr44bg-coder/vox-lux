Tonight we are going about a hundred and eighty years back, to the frozen seas of the far north of Canada, in the summer of the year 1845.

--

Two ships of the Royal Navy are sailing slowly westward, among the icebergs, toward a great maze of islands and channels, where no ship has ever been before. Their names are Erebus and Terror. On board, there are a hundred and twenty-nine men, and their leader is Sir John Franklin.

They are looking for a way through the ice, across the top of the world. And they will never come home.

Should you like these slow journeys into the past, you are welcome to subscribe to the channel, so that the next one finds you easily.

--

Let us imagine the Arctic, as the men of Erebus and Terror saw it.

Far to the north of Canada, above the Arctic Circle, there is a great scattering of islands, hundreds of them, large and small, separated by narrow channels and wide sounds of water. For most of the year, the water between the islands is frozen solid. The sun disappears in the winter, for weeks or months, and the temperature falls to forty or fifty degrees below zero.

--

In the short summer, for a few weeks, the sun never sets. It circles around the sky, day and night, low over the horizon. The ice in the channels begins to break up into great floating fields and floes, which drift with the wind and the currents, opening and closing, crushing together, and drifting apart again.

The land is bare and brown and grey, low and flat in some places, with high cliffs and mountains in others. There are no trees. There is moss, and lichen, and in summer, little flowers: purple saxifrage, and Arctic poppies, yellow and white, and Arctic cotton, waving in the wind.

{img:arctic-tundra-summer-poppies-purple-saxifrage-low-sun}
And there are animals: polar bears, and seals, and walruses, and whales, and Arctic foxes, and musk oxen, and caribou, and great flocks of seabirds, in the summer, nesting on the cliffs.

--

And there are people.

The Inuit have lived here for a thousand years, and their ancestors for thousands of years before that. They know the ice, and the animals, and the weather, better than anyone. They hunt seals at their breathing holes in the ice, and caribou on the land, and fish in the rivers. They build houses of snow in winter, and tents of skin in summer. They travel on sledges, pulled by dogs, and in kayaks.

To the Inuit, the Arctic is not a frozen wilderness. It is home.

--

For more than three hundred years, the sailors of Europe have dreamed of finding a way through these icy seas.

They call it the Northwest Passage: a sea route from the Atlantic Ocean to the Pacific Ocean, over the top of North America. If such a passage could be found, ships could sail from Europe to China and India, the lands of silk and spices and tea, far more quickly than by going all the way around Africa, or around South America.

{img:old-sailing-ship-sixteenth-century-ice-floes-explorers}
Since the days of Queen Elizabeth the First, explorer after explorer has sailed into the Arctic, to look for it: Martin Frobisher, and John Davis, and Henry Hudson, and William Baffin, and many others. Their names are on the map today: Frobisher Bay, Davis Strait, Hudson Bay, Baffin Bay. But none of them found the way through.

--

In the nineteenth century, after the end of the long wars against Napoleon, the British Royal Navy has many ships, and many officers, and nothing much for them to do.

So, from 1818 onward, the Navy sends one expedition after another into the Arctic, to look for the Northwest Passage, and to map the coasts, and to study the magnetism of the earth, and the weather, and the animals and the plants. The man behind these expeditions is Sir John Barrow, the Second Secretary of the Admiralty, who is passionate about the Arctic.

By 1845, the explorers have mapped much of the coast of northern Canada, from the east and from the west. Only a few hundred miles in the middle are still unknown. Barrow believes that one more expedition, well equipped, with the best ships and the best men, can find the last link, and sail through the Passage, and complete the work of three hundred years.

--

And so, in the spring of 1845, the Admiralty prepares the greatest Arctic expedition ever sent out.

Two strong ships, with steam engines, and heating, and food for three years, and the best officers and men of the Navy. It is the pride of Britain. Everyone is sure that it will succeed.

{img:river-thames-1845-ships-moored-crowds-flags-spring}
The leader of the expedition is a man of fifty-nine, who has been to the Arctic before, and has become famous there. His name is Sir John Franklin.
