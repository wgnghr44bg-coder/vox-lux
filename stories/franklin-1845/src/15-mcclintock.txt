Lady Franklin does not stop searching, even after Rae's report.

She wants to know more. She wants to find the ships, and the journals, and the records, and to know exactly what happened, and where, and whether her husband was the first man to find the Northwest Passage. She wants to give the men a proper memorial.

--

The Admiralty will not send any more expeditions. So, in 1857, Lady Franklin buys a small steam yacht, called the Fox, with the help of money from the public, and sends it to the Arctic herself.

The captain is Francis Leopold McClintock, an Irish naval officer, and one of the most experienced Arctic sledge travellers in the world. He takes no pay. He has a small crew of twenty-five men, many of whom have been in the Arctic before.

The Fox is trapped in the ice in Baffin Bay, for the whole of its first winter, and drifts for more than a thousand miles. But in the second year, it gets through, into the Arctic channels, and spends the winter of 1858 to 1859 near the eastern entrance of Bellot Strait, not far from King William Island.

--

In the spring of 1859, McClintock and his men set out on long sledge journeys, with dogs, to search King William Island.

McClintock meets Inuit, who tell him more about the white men, and the ships. One ship, they say, was crushed by the ice, and sank. The other was driven ashore, and the Inuit took wood and metal from it. They give him more silver spoons and forks, and buttons, and other things.

{img:dog-sledges-crossing-snow-island-spring-1859-sunshine}
McClintock's second in command, Lieutenant William Hobson, goes ahead, to the northwest coast of the island, to Victory Point.

--

And on the sixth of May, 1859, Hobson finds the cairn.

Inside it, there is a small metal cylinder. And in the cylinder, there is the note. The note written by Graham Gore, in May 1847, saying all well. And around the edges, the words added by Fitzjames and Crozier, in April 1848: Sir John Franklin died on the 11th June 1847. The ships deserted. A hundred and five souls. Start on tomorrow for Back's Fish River.

{img:officer-kneeling-cairn-holding-small-paper-wind-snow}
After eleven years, the world knows at last what happened. Or at least, the beginning and the end.

--

Along the coast, the searchers find other things, left behind by the men on their last journey: piles of clothing, and tools, and stores, at Victory Point, where the men came ashore.

And further south, at a place now called Erebus Bay, they find a ship's boat, on a large sledge, with many things in it: books, and clothes, and shoes, and watches, and silver, and chocolate, and tea, and guns. They find, too, signs that some of the men had stayed there, at the end.

McClintock writes about it calmly and kindly, with great respect for the men. He lays a stone over them, and reads the burial service.

--

The Fox comes home to England in September 1859.

And the news goes around the world. The note proves that the Franklin expedition had found the last link of the Northwest Passage. Because the ships had sailed down Peel Sound, and Franklin Strait, to King William Island. And from there, the coast of America, already mapped, ran all the way to the west. If they had been able to sail just a little further, they would have been through.

{img:lady-franklin-receiving-mcclintock-drawing-room-1859}
So Lady Franklin and her friends say that Sir John Franklin was the discoverer of the Northwest Passage. And on his monument in London, which still stands today, in Waterloo Place, and on his memorial in Westminster Abbey, it says so.

--

Lady Franklin lives on for many more years, until 1875, travelling, and working, and still hoping that the journals and the records of the expedition might one day be found.

She passes away in July 1875, at the age of eighty-three. Two weeks later, the marble memorial to her husband is unveiled in Westminster Abbey, with a poem written by his nephew by marriage, the great poet Alfred Tennyson: Not here! the white North has thy bones; and thou, heroic sailor-soul, art passing on thine happier voyage now toward no earthly pole.
