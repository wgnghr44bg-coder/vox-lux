In September 2014, a small team from Parks Canada is searching the waters in the south, near the Adelaide Peninsula, in the area where the Inuit stories said one of the ships had been seen.

The weather in the north is too bad to search there, where they had planned. So they come south, to the shelter of the islands.

--

And one day, on a little island in the area, a helicopter pilot and an archaeologist find, among the stones on the shore, a piece of iron, and a piece of wood, from a ship. It is part of a davit, the iron fitting that held one of the ship's boats, marked with the broad arrow, the sign of the Royal Navy.

The searchers turn their sonar on the water nearby. And on the second of September, 2014, on the screen, the picture appears, clear and sharp, on the sea floor, eleven metres down: the shape of a ship.

It is HMS Erebus. Franklin's own ship.

--

She is sitting upright on the sea floor, in shallow water, in a place now called Wilmot and Crampton Bay, more than a hundred kilometres south of where the ships were abandoned in the ice. Just as the Inuit stories said.

Her masts are gone, and part of her deck has been broken by the ice, over the years. But she is remarkably well preserved, in the cold, dark water. Her great iron bow plates are still there. Her ship's wheel is still there. Her bell is lying on the deck.

{img:underwater-shipwreck-wooden-hull-kelp-cold-green-light}
The divers bring up the bell, of bronze, with the broad arrow, and the date, 1845. It is the first object recovered from either of Franklin's ships.

--

Two years later, in September 2016, the second ship is found.

An Inuk man, named Sammy Kogvik, from Gjoa Haven, tells a research ship that, some years before, he and a friend were out hunting, on a frozen bay on the southwest coast of King William Island, and saw a tall piece of wood, like a mast, sticking up out of the ice. He took a photograph, but he lost his camera, and he never told anyone, because he thought that it might be unlucky.

The research ship goes to the bay, with Sammy Kogvik on board. And there, on the sea floor, twenty-four metres down, is HMS Terror. In a bay that has been called, for more than a hundred years, Terror Bay.

--

Terror is in even better condition than Erebus.

She is sitting upright, almost whole, with her masts broken, but her hull intact, and her hatches closed, and her windows still in place. Her propeller is still lowered, as if she had been sailing.

{img:shipwreck-intact-deck-hatches-closed-silt-underwater}
When the divers send a little remote camera inside, they see the cabins of the officers, and the shelves, and the bottles, and the plates, still standing in neat rows, and the beds, and the drawers of Captain Crozier's desk, closed, under a layer of fine silt.

The scientists believe that, inside those drawers, in the cold, dark, still water, there might be papers, and charts, and even journals, which have survived, perhaps, for more than a hundred and seventy years.

--

Today, both wrecks are protected, as a National Historic Site of Canada, managed together by Parks Canada and the Inuit.

The Inuit guardians watch over the sites, all through the summer, from a camp on the shore, to make sure that nobody disturbs them. And every summer, when the ice is open, the archaeologists and the divers go down, very carefully, in the cold, dark water, to study the ships, and to bring up some of the things inside.

They have brought up hundreds of objects: plates, and cups, and bottles, and buttons, and shoes, and a hairbrush, and pencils, and a pair of officer's epaulettes, still with their golden fringes, and a cannon, and the ship's wheel, and a writing desk, and a pair of glasses.

--

And slowly, more and more of the men are being found, and named.

The scientists have taken DNA from the remains of some of the men found on King William Island over the years, and compared it with DNA from their living relatives, descendants of their brothers and sisters, in Britain and in other countries.

{img:laboratory-scientist-quiet-careful-work-soft-light}
In 2021, one of the men was identified for the first time: John Gregory, an engineer of Erebus, from Portsea, near Portsmouth. And in 2024, another: Captain James Fitzjames himself, the cheerful commander of Erebus, who wrote such lively letters home.

After more than a hundred and seventy years, they have their names back.
