{img:printed-admiralty-form-tin-cylinder-cairn-stones}
The message is written on a printed form, which every Navy ship carries.

The form says, in six languages, English and French and Spanish and Dutch and Danish and German: whoever finds this paper is requested to forward it to the Secretary of the Admiralty, London, with a note of the time and place at which it was found. In the empty space in the middle, the officers write their message.

--

The message says:

28 of May 1847. H.M. ships Erebus and Terror wintered in the ice in latitude 70 degrees 5 minutes north, longitude 98 degrees 23 minutes west. Having wintered in 1846 to 47 at Beechey Island, in latitude 74 degrees 43 minutes 28 seconds north, longitude 91 degrees 39 minutes 15 seconds west, after having ascended Wellington Channel to latitude 77 degrees, and returned by the west side of Cornwallis Island. Sir John Franklin commanding the expedition. All well. Party consisting of 2 officers and 6 men left the ships on Monday 24th May 1847. Gm. Gore, Lieut. Chas. F. Des Voeux, mate.

All well.

--

There is a small mistake in the message. The ships wintered at Beechey Island in 1845 to 1846, not 1846 to 1847. Perhaps Gore was tired, or cold, or in a hurry. It is a little thing. But it is the kind of mistake that makes the note feel very human.

Gore and his men roll the paper up, and put it into a small metal cylinder, to protect it from the damp, and place it in the cairn, under the stones. And they leave another copy in another cairn, a few miles to the south.

{img:men-placing-stones-cairn-low-coast-ice-beyond}
Then they go on, along the coast, for a little while, exploring, before they return to the ships.

--

All well.

In May 1847, the expedition has spent two winters in the Arctic. They have discovered hundreds of miles of new coast. They have sailed further than anyone before. And everyone, it seems, is well and hopeful. They are so close to the Passage. Perhaps, this summer, the ice will break up, and they will sail through.

But the ice does not break up that summer. The summer of 1847 is cold. The ships stay locked in the ice, off King William Island.

--

And on the eleventh of June, 1847, just two weeks after Gore left the note, Sir John Franklin passes away, on board Erebus, at the age of sixty-one.

We do not know what he died of. Perhaps it was his heart. Perhaps a stroke. He was an old man, by the standards of the time, and had lived a hard life. We do not know if Gore came back to the ships in time to see him.

{img:quiet-cabin-erebus-bible-lamp-chair-empty}
We do not know where he was buried. Perhaps on the land, on King William Island, in a grave dug in the frozen gravel. Perhaps at sea, through a hole cut in the ice, beside the ship, with the flag laid over him, and the officers and men standing around, bare-headed, in the cold, and Captain Crozier reading the burial service.

His grave has never been found.

--

Now Captain Francis Crozier, the quiet, careful Irishman, is in command of the expedition.

And Commander Fitzjames, the cheerful one, is his second in command.

{img:captain-crozier-on-deck-looking-out-ice-responsibility}
They spend a third winter in the ice, the winter of 1847 to 1848. It must have been a very hard winter. The food is running low. The men are growing weaker. Some of them are ill. And still the ice does not move.

--

In the spring of 1848, almost a year after Gore left the note, someone goes back to the cairn at Victory Point.

They take out the note, and unroll it. And they write more, around the edges of the paper, in the margins, in different handwriting, crowded and cramped, as if they were writing in a hurry, with cold hands. It is the handwriting of Fitzjames, and at the end, Crozier signs it, and adds a few words.

And this is what they write:

April 25, 1848. H.M. ships Terror and Erebus were deserted on the 22nd April, 5 leagues north-north-west of this, having been beset since 12th September 1846. The officers and crews, consisting of 105 souls, under the command of Captain F. R. M. Crozier, landed here, in latitude 69 degrees 37 minutes 42 seconds, longitude 98 degrees 41 minutes. Sir John Franklin died on the 11th June 1847, and the total loss by deaths in the expedition has been to this date 9 officers and 15 men.

--

And in the corner, Crozier adds: and start on tomorrow, 26th, for Back's Fish River.

Back's Fish River, which we now call the Back River, is a great river, flowing north into the Arctic Ocean, on the mainland of America, south of King William Island, about two hundred and fifty miles away. Up that river, hundreds of miles further, there are the trading posts of the Hudson's Bay Company, and help.

{img:king-william-island-flat-snowy-coast-tents-boats-sledges}
The hundred and five men who are left have abandoned the ships. They have come ashore, with their sledges, and their boats, mounted on sledges, and their tents, and their supplies. And they are going to walk south.

It is the last message ever received from the Franklin expedition.
