In the summer of 1846, Erebus and Terror sail south from Beechey Island, into the unknown.

Franklin's orders from the Admiralty are to sail west from Lancaster Sound, and then, from about Cape Walker, to go south and west, toward the coast of America, which has already been mapped, and then along the coast, westward, to the Bering Strait, and the Pacific.

--

They sail down a long channel, between two great islands, which is now called Peel Sound. No ship has ever sailed through it before. Two years earlier, it would have been impossible. But in the summer of 1846, the ice is open, and the two ships sail all the way down it, for about two hundred and fifty miles, southward.

It must be an exciting time. Every day, they are seeing new coasts, new capes, new islands, that no European has ever seen. The officers are drawing charts, and naming the capes and bays they pass. They must believe that they are very close to the Passage.

At the bottom of Peel Sound, the channel opens out into a wider strait, now called Franklin Strait. And to the southwest, there is a large, low, flat island: King William Island.

--

Franklin's charts, made by earlier explorers, show King William Island as part of the mainland, a great peninsula, called King William Land. They do not know that there is a channel to the east of it, between the island and the mainland, where the ice is much lighter.

So the ships go down the western side of the island, into a wide channel now called Victoria Strait.

{img:victoria-strait-heavy-pack-ice-flat-island-grey-sky}
And here, they meet the ice.

--

Victoria Strait is one of the worst places for ice in the whole Arctic.

Every year, great masses of old, thick ice, many years old, come drifting down from the northwest, from the great ice of the Arctic Ocean, through a wide channel, now called McClintock Channel, and pile up in Victoria Strait, against the west coast of King William Island. This ice is very thick, ten or twenty feet, or more, and hard as rock. Even in summer, it often does not melt or break up at all.

{img:ancient-thick-pressure-ridges-ice-blue-white-sun}
In September 1846, Erebus and Terror are caught in this ice, off the northwest corner of King William Island, about fifteen miles from shore.

They are beset. They cannot move forward, or backward. The ice closes around them, and freezes solid. And there they stay.

--

At first, this would not have been so worrying. The officers would have expected to spend a winter in the ice, and to be freed in the summer, when the ice breaks up. Arctic ships are often beset, and freed again.

And they are very close, now, to the Passage. To the south of King William Island, the coast of America has already been mapped by earlier explorers, including Franklin himself, and others, sailing in small boats along the coast. If they can get just a little further south, perhaps a hundred miles, they will have found the last link.

So they prepare, once again, for winter. They cover the decks. They bank up the snow. They set up their routine of work and exercise and school and entertainment. And the darkness comes again.

--

The second winter, of 1846 and 1847, is spent in the open ice, far from land.

It is different from the first winter, at Beechey Island. There is no sheltered bay, no beach to walk on. Just the ice, all around, flat and white and endless, broken by ridges, where the ice has been pushed up into great walls, by the pressure of the floes moving against each other.

{img:pressure-ridge-ice-moonlight-ships-small-distance}
And the ships are not still. They move, with the ice, very slowly, drifting southward, a mile or two a week, locked in the great frozen field.

The men must hear the ice, all through the winter: groaning, and creaking, and cracking, sometimes with a sound like a gunshot, as the great floes press against each other, and against the ships.

--

But we know very little about what happened during that winter. Because no journals, no logbooks, no letters from the expedition, from after the summer of 1845, have ever been found.

Except for one short note.
