TOPIC: The old dream of a northern passage
===
IMG: old-map-arctic-unknown-coast-candle | an old hand-drawn map of the Arctic with many blank areas and uncertain coastlines spread on a wooden table, a brass compass and a candle beside it, warm candlelight
HEAD: For more than three hundred years, Europeans searched for a sea route around the top of North America.
BODY: The idea of the Northwest Passage was very old. In the 1500s, European merchants wanted to trade with China and India. The routes around Africa and South America were long and controlled by Spain and Portugal. So English and Dutch sailors looked for another way. They hoped to sail west and north, around the top of North America, straight to Asia. This imagined route was called the Northwest Passage. In 1576, Martin Frobisher sailed to the Arctic to look for it. He returned with black rock he believed contained gold. It did not. Other explorers followed. Each mapped a little more of the frozen coasts. Even so, the passage proved very hard to find. The Arctic islands formed a maze of channels, most blocked by ice. Yet the dream did not die. By the 1800s, finding the passage had become a matter of national pride for Britain.
===
IMG: henry-hudson-small-ship-bay-ice-1610 | a small wooden sailing ship of the early 1600s in a wide grey northern bay with floating ice, low distant shores, a cold soft light
HEAD: The explorer Henry Hudson searched for the passage and gave his name to a great bay in Canada.
BODY: Henry Hudson was an English explorer of the early 1600s. He made several voyages to find a route to Asia. In 1609, sailing for the Dutch, he explored the river in New York that now carries his name. In 1610, he sailed again for England, further north. He entered a huge bay in northern Canada. He believed he had found the passage to the Pacific. But it was a dead end, a vast inland sea surrounded by land. The ship was trapped in the ice for the winter. In the spring, the hungry and unhappy crew turned against him. They put Hudson, his young son and a few loyal men into a small boat and left them. They were never seen again. Even so, the bay still bears his name. It is called Hudson Bay. Yet it also became the centre of a great fur trade. The Hudson's Bay Company, founded in 1670, still exists today.
===
IMG: parry-ships-winter-harbour-melville-island-1820 | two wooden sailing ships frozen into a quiet harbour on a snowy Arctic island in 1820, low hills and a pale sky, a small observatory hut on the shore, soft twilight
HEAD: In 1819, William Parry sailed further west through the Arctic than anyone before him.
BODY: After the wars with Napoleon ended in 1815, the British navy had many ships and officers but little to do. A powerful official at the Admiralty, John Barrow, pushed for new voyages of exploration. One of the most successful was led by William Parry. In 1819, he sailed west through the channel north of the Canadian mainland. He travelled much further than any ship before. His ships wintered at Melville Island. To keep the men cheerful, Parry organised a ship's newspaper and a theatre. The men performed plays in the darkness of winter. The next summer, Parry tried to go further west. But heavy ice blocked the way. Even so, his voyage was a great success. Parliament gave him a prize for crossing a certain line of longitude. Yet the remaining passage proved even harder. That same heavy ice would later trap Franklin's ships, further south, nearly thirty years later.
===
IMG: admiralty-boardroom-london-maps-officers-1845 | an indoor scene, enclosed by panelled walls, a high plain ceiling, a grand boardroom at the Admiralty in London in the 1840s, naval officers studying large maps of the Arctic on a long table, a wind dial above the fireplace, soft window light
HEAD: By 1845, only a short stretch of the Northwest Passage remained unmapped.
BODY: By the 1840s, explorers had mapped much of the Arctic. Franklin himself had charted hundreds of miles of the northern coast of Canada. Others had done the same from the east and west. On the map, only a short gap remained. It lay somewhere around King William Island. If a ship could sail through that gap, the Northwest Passage would be complete. The Admiralty believed that the right expedition could do it in a single season, or perhaps two. That is why Franklin's ships were so well equipped. The passage would not be useful for trade. It was too far north and too full of ice. Even so, finding it mattered to Britain. It was a question of science, honour and prestige. Yet the gap that looked so small on the map was one of the hardest places in the Arctic. The ice there rarely cleared. The Admiralty did not know that in 1845.

TOPIC: Searching for Franklin
===
IMG: search-ships-arctic-1850-many-sails-ice | several wooden sailing ships of different sizes moving through scattered sea ice in an Arctic channel in 1850, flags flying, snowy hills on the shore, soft hazy light
HEAD: More than thirty expeditions searched for Franklin and his men between 1848 and 1859.
BODY: When the expedition did not return by 1847, people in Britain began to worry. The first search expeditions set out in 1848. When they found nothing, more followed. Over the next eleven years, more than thirty expeditions went looking for Franklin. Some were sent by the Royal Navy. Others were paid for by Lady Franklin. One was even sent by the United States, funded by a wealthy merchant named Henry Grinnell. Ships came from the Atlantic and from the Pacific. Sledge teams travelled thousands of miles over the ice. The search became one of the largest in history. Even so, the searchers often looked in the wrong places. They did not know which way Franklin had gone. Yet their work had another result. They mapped huge areas of the Canadian Arctic. Many islands, straits and capes were named during these searches. The search for Franklin filled in the map of the north.
===
IMG: sledge-party-man-hauling-snow-wind | a team of Victorian sailors in heavy wool coats and caps pulling a loaded wooden sledge with long ropes across a vast flat snowfield, a small flag on the sledge, pale cold light
HEAD: Search parties dragged heavy sledges for hundreds of miles across the frozen sea.
BODY: Much of the searching was done on foot. In spring, when the days grew longer, teams of sailors left their ships. They pulled sledges loaded with tents, food and fuel. Each sledge carried supplies for weeks. The men were harnessed to it like horses. They pulled for many hours a day. The work was brutally hard. They crossed rough ice ridges and deep snow. They suffered frostbite and snow blindness. At night, they slept in tents on the ice, wrapped in blankets and buffalo robes. Some sledge journeys covered more than a thousand miles. Even so, the men were proud of their work. Each sledge had its own flag and name, often with a motto. Some of these silk flags still survive. Yet for all their effort, the sledge teams found few traces of Franklin. The places where his men had travelled were further south, in areas no one had thought to search.
===
IMG: john-rae-explorer-fur-clothing-snowshoes | a Scottish explorer in Inuit-style fur clothing and snowshoes standing on a snowy ridge with a rifle over his shoulder, Arctic tundra behind him, soft clear light
HEAD: John Rae, a Scottish doctor, travelled the Arctic like the Inuit and brought the first news of Franklin's fate.
BODY: John Rae was a doctor from the Orkney Islands in Scotland. He worked for the Hudson's Bay Company in Canada. Unlike most British explorers, Rae learned from the Indigenous peoples of the north. He wore fur clothing, used snowshoes and built snow houses. He hunted for his own food and travelled light. He could cover great distances very quickly. In 1854, while mapping the coast, he met Inuit who had news of Franklin's men. They told him that a large group of white men had died, several years before, far to the west. They showed him objects that had belonged to the expedition. Rae bought them and carried them home. Even so, his report caused anger in England. It included painful details that many people did not want to believe. Yet Rae was honest about what he had been told. He also discovered the strait that completes the passage along the coast. Today it is called Rae Strait.
===
IMG: mcclintock-fox-small-yacht-arctic-1857 | a small steam yacht with a single funnel and sails moving through broken sea ice in the Arctic in 1857, a few men on deck, soft grey light, calm sea
HEAD: Lady Franklin bought a small steam yacht called the Fox to continue the search.
BODY: By the late 1850s, the government had given up the search. Britain had been fighting a war in the Crimea. The Admiralty declared that Franklin and his men had died in service. Yet Lady Franklin did not stop. With her own money and gifts from the public, she bought a small steam yacht called the Fox. She asked Francis McClintock, an experienced Arctic officer, to command it. In 1857, the Fox sailed for the Arctic. It was trapped in the ice for its first winter and drifted for months. In 1859, McClintock and his men finally reached King William Island. They used sledges to search its coasts. They spoke with Inuit, who sold them silver spoons and other items. It was during this search that Hobson found the note at Victory Point. Even so, the Fox expedition did not find the ships. Yet it answered the question Lady Franklin had asked for years. It confirmed that her husband had died in 1847.
===
IMG: investigator-ship-abandoned-mercy-bay-ice | a wooden sailing ship sitting alone in thick sea ice in a quiet frozen bay of an Arctic island, its sails furled, a few tents on the shore, pale soft light
HEAD: The crew of one search ship became the first people to travel the whole Northwest Passage, partly on foot.
BODY: One of the search ships was called HMS Investigator. It was commanded by Robert McClure. In 1850, it entered the Arctic from the Pacific side, near Alaska. It sailed east and reached the area that Parry had reached from the other side thirty years earlier. McClure realised that the Northwest Passage existed. But his ship became trapped in the ice in a bay he named Mercy Bay. The men spent three winters there. They suffered from hunger and scurvy. In 1853, men from another search expedition found them. The crew abandoned Investigator and walked across the ice to the rescue ships. They then sailed home to England. Even so, they had not sailed the whole way. Yet they had crossed from the Pacific to the Atlantic. McClure and his men received a prize from Parliament as the first to complete the passage. In 2010, archaeologists found Investigator, still resting on the bottom of Mercy Bay.
===
IMG: victorian-newspaper-reading-room-franklin-news | an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, Victorian men and women reading newspapers at long tables in a reading room in the 1850s, gas lamps glowing, soft warm light
HEAD: The fate of Franklin's expedition fascinated the Victorian public for decades.
BODY: The disappearance of Franklin's expedition became one of the great stories of the Victorian age. Newspapers followed every search. People bought prints, books and songs about the lost ships. Ballads such as Lady Franklin's Lament became popular. Exhibitions displayed objects found in the Arctic. When John Rae brought back his report in 1854, it caused a storm. The famous writer Charles Dickens wrote articles rejecting the Inuit accounts. Later, Dickens and his friend Wilkie Collins wrote a play inspired by the Arctic, called The Frozen Deep. Dickens himself acted in it. Even so, interest never faded. Painters made dramatic pictures of the frozen north. Poets wrote verses about the lost men. Yet the families of the sailors suffered most. Many waited years for news. Some never knew exactly how their loved ones had died. For them, the Franklin story was not an adventure. It was a deep, quiet grief.
===
IMG: franklin-memorial-westminster-abbey-marble | a white marble memorial plaque with a carved relief of a ship in ice inside an old stone church, an indoor scene, enclosed by stone walls, a high vaulted ceiling, soft light through stained glass
HEAD: A memorial to Franklin in Westminster Abbey calls him the discoverer of the Northwest Passage.
BODY: After his death became known, Franklin was honoured as a national hero. Statues were raised in London, in his hometown of Spilsby and in Tasmania. In 1875, a memorial was placed in Westminster Abbey. Its words were written by the poet Alfred Tennyson, who was Franklin's nephew by marriage. The poem begins with the words, not here. It says that Franklin's body is not in the Abbey, but somewhere in the Arctic. It calls him a heroic sailor-soul, now passing on a happier voyage. In 2009, a memorial to the men of the expedition was rededicated in the Abbey. It also honoured the Inuit and others who had helped in the search. Even so, the claim that Franklin discovered the passage is debated. His ships did not sail through it. Yet his men may have been the first to see the final link. In that sense, many still give him credit for the discovery.
===
TOPIC: The Northwest Passage at last
===
IMG: gjoa-small-sloop-arctic-amundsen-1904 | a very small single-masted wooden sailing sloop anchored in a quiet Arctic harbour beside a low rocky shore, a few small huts, soft golden summer light
HEAD: The first ship to sail through the whole Northwest Passage was a tiny fishing boat called the Gjøa.
BODY: After Franklin, many decades passed before anyone sailed the whole passage. The man who finally did it was Roald Amundsen, from Norway. In 1903, he set out in a small boat called the Gjøa. It was an old herring boat, only about twenty-one metres long. It had a crew of just seven men. Amundsen chose a small ship on purpose. It could pass through shallow channels that larger ships could not enter. He also studied the mistakes of earlier expeditions. He sailed down the eastern side of King William Island, through the channel that Franklin had not known about. His ship spent two winters in a sheltered harbour there. Even so, Amundsen did not hurry. He used the time to learn from the local Inuit. In 1906, the Gjøa finally reached Alaska. The Northwest Passage had been sailed at last, sixty-one years after Franklin set out.
===
IMG: gjoa-haven-inuit-amundsen-learning-skills | a Norwegian explorer in a wool sweater learning to build a snow house from an Inuit man in fur clothing, blocks of snow around them, a small harbour and a little sloop in the distance, soft winter light
HEAD: Amundsen learned to wear fur clothing and drive dog teams from the Inuit he met in the Arctic.
BODY: During his two winters on King William Island, Amundsen lived near Inuit families. He watched them closely. He learned how they made their warm, light fur clothing. He learned how they built snow houses and drove dog sleds. He learned how they hunted seals and caribou. He traded with them for clothing and equipment. He later said that these lessons were very valuable. Even so, he did not always describe the Inuit fairly in his writings. Yet he clearly respected their skills. He used what he learned on his later journeys. In 1911, he became the first person to reach the South Pole. His team used dogs and fur clothing, just as he had learned in the north. The place where Amundsen wintered is now an Inuit community. It is called Gjoa Haven, after his little ship. Many of the people who live there today remember the stories of the explorers and the old ships.
===
IMG: modern-ship-arctic-passage-summer-open-water | a modern research ship sailing through open blue water between low Arctic islands in summer, a few scattered pieces of ice, a clear pale sky, calm sea
HEAD: Today, because the Arctic ice is shrinking, ships can sometimes pass through the Northwest Passage in summer.
BODY: For centuries, the Northwest Passage was almost impossible to sail. Thick ice blocked the channels for most of the year. In recent decades, that has begun to change. As the climate has warmed, the summer sea ice in the Arctic has become thinner and smaller. In some summers, the passage is almost clear of ice. Small boats, research ships and even large cruise ships have sailed through it. In 2016, a cruise ship carrying around a thousand passengers made the journey. This would have astonished Franklin and his officers. Even so, the passage is still dangerous. Ice conditions change from year to year. The weather is harsh, and help is far away. Yet the change is striking. The route that defeated Franklin and so many others can now be crossed by tourists. For many people, it is a powerful sign of how quickly the Arctic is changing.

TOPIC: Finding the lost ships
===
IMG: parks-canada-search-boat-sonar-arctic-water | a small modern survey boat towing sonar equipment across calm grey Arctic water near a low rocky shore, researchers in orange jackets on deck, soft overcast light
HEAD: For more than a hundred and fifty years, the wrecks of Erebus and Terror could not be found.
BODY: After the ships were abandoned in 1848, they vanished. No European ever saw them again. For many years, searchers wondered where they had gone. Perhaps they had been crushed by the ice, or perhaps they had sunk where they were left. Inuit stories said that at least one ship had drifted south and sunk near an island. In 2008, the government of Canada began a new search. It was led by Parks Canada, the agency that looks after national parks and historic sites. Each summer, teams used ships, small boats and underwater sonar. Sonar sends sound waves into the water and listens for echoes from objects on the seabed. The search area was huge. The waters were shallow, cold and full of ice. Even so, the searchers kept trying year after year. They worked closely with Inuit and with historians like Louie Kamookak. Yet for six summers, they found nothing. Then, in 2014, everything changed.
===
IMG: underwater-wreck-erebus-seabed-bell-sunlight | the wooden wreck of an old sailing ship lying on a shallow seabed, kelp and soft sunlight filtering through clear cold water, a bronze bell resting near the deck, calm and quiet
HEAD: In September 2014, searchers found the wreck of Erebus on the seabed, only eleven metres down.
BODY: On the second of September 2014, a Parks Canada sonar team saw something on their screen. It was the shape of a wooden ship, lying on the bottom of the sea. Divers went down to look. It was Erebus. She lay in shallow water in a bay south of King William Island. That was close to where Inuit stories had said a ship sank. The wreck was in fairly good condition. Parts of the upper deck had been damaged by ice. Yet much of the ship was still there. Divers soon raised the ship's bronze bell. It was marked with a broad arrow, the sign of the Royal Navy, and the date 1845. Even so, finding Erebus raised new questions. She lay much further south than the place where she was abandoned. It seemed that men may have sailed her there after she was first abandoned. Yet the discovery was a great moment. After almost one hundred and seventy years, Franklin's ship had been found.
===
IMG: inuit-hunter-sammy-kogvik-snowmobile-sea-ice | an Inuit hunter in a warm parka standing beside a snowmobile on flat sea ice, looking at a tall dark wooden pole sticking up through the ice in the distance, pale winter light
HEAD: An Inuit hunter's memory of a mast sticking out of the ice led searchers to the wreck of Terror.
BODY: Two years later, the second ship was found. This time, the key clue came from an Inuit hunter named Sammy Kogvik. In 2016, he was working with a search expedition. He told the team about something he had seen years before. While hunting on the sea ice, he had noticed a large piece of wood sticking up through the ice. It looked like a ship's mast. He had taken a photograph, but he lost his camera. He had told few people about it. The search ship changed course and went to the place he described. It was a quiet bay on the southwest coast of King William Island. There, on the third of September 2016, they found Terror. Even so, the place was a surprise. It was far from where most experts had expected. Yet it matched Kogvik's memory exactly. By a quiet coincidence, the place had been named Terror Bay long before, by searchers who never knew the ship was there.
===
IMG: terror-wreck-interior-plates-shelves-underwater | the inside of an old wooden shipwreck underwater, rows of plates and glass bottles still standing on wooden shelves in a small cabin, fine silt on everything, soft blue light from a diver's lamp
HEAD: Inside the wreck of Terror, plates still stand on the shelves, and drawers remain closed.
BODY: Terror was in amazing condition. She sat upright on the seabed, about twenty-four metres down. Her hull was almost complete. Her masts had broken, but they lay nearby. The hatches were closed, and the windows of the captain's cabin were still in place. In 2019, Parks Canada sent a small underwater drone inside the ship. It travelled through the dark rooms. The images it sent back were extraordinary. Plates and glasses still stood on the shelves of the officers' mess. Beds and desks were still in the cabins. Captain Crozier's cabin had a desk with drawers still closed. Fine silt covered everything, which can protect paper from decay. Even so, no documents have yet been recovered. Archaeologists hope that the closed drawers may contain logbooks, letters or charts. Yet they must work slowly and carefully. Each object could hold part of the story that has been missing for more than a century and a half.
===
IMG: divers-recovering-artifacts-cold-water-erebus | two divers in thick dry suits carefully lifting a small wooden object from an old wreck on the seabed in cold, clear Arctic water, soft light from above
HEAD: Every summer, divers recover hundreds of objects from the wreck of Erebus.
BODY: Since 2014, Parks Canada archaeologists have returned to Erebus almost every summer. The water is very cold, usually just above freezing. The diving season is short, only a few weeks long. Even so, the team has recovered many objects. There are plates, bottles, buttons, tools and pieces of clothing. There is a pair of epaulettes, the decorations worn on an officer's shoulders. There are small personal items, like a hairbrush that still had human hair in it. There is a wooden pencil case and pieces of a sailor's shoe. Each object is carefully recorded, preserved and studied. Some items have helped identify people. Scientists hope that DNA from objects like the hairbrush may be compared with living relatives. Yet the work is slow. It may take many more years to explore the whole ship. Each new object adds a small, human detail to the story of the men who sailed on her.
===
IMG: dna-lab-scientist-bone-sample-relatives | a scientist in a white lab coat carefully examining a small sample under a lamp in a quiet modern laboratory, an indoor scene, enclosed by walls, a plain ceiling, soft bright light
HEAD: Using DNA, scientists have begun to put names to the remains of some of Franklin's men.
BODY: Over the years, remains of Franklin's men have been found on King William Island. Most were found without names. Then scientists began using DNA to identify them. They compared DNA from the remains with DNA from living descendants of the sailors' families. In 2021, a team from the University of Waterloo in Canada identified a man named John Gregory. He had been an engineer on Erebus. A descendant of his in South Africa gave a sample that matched. It was the first time one of the men had been identified this way. In 2024, the same team identified James Fitzjames, the commander of Erebus. Even so, many remains have still not been named. The researchers continue to ask relatives of the crew to come forward. Yet each identification is a gift to the families. For the first time in nearly one hundred and eighty years, they can know where their ancestor lies.

TOPIC: Remembering the lost expedition
===
IMG: erebus-terror-national-historic-site-quiet-coast | a quiet stretch of rocky Arctic coastline beside calm grey water, a small Canadian flag on a pole, low hills covered in pale tundra plants, soft summer evening light
HEAD: The wrecks of Erebus and Terror are now a national historic site, protected by Canada and Inuit together.
BODY: Erebus and Terror belonged to the British Royal Navy. Yet they lay in Canadian waters, in the homeland of the Inuit. In 2018, Britain gave the ships to Canada and to the Inuit Heritage Trust. The two wrecks are now part of a protected national historic site. It is managed jointly by Parks Canada and Inuit. Inuit guardians watch over the sites. They live nearby and keep an eye on the waters, especially in summer. Visitors are not allowed to dive on the wrecks without permission. This protects them from damage and theft. Even so, the Inuit role is about more than protection. Their knowledge helped find the ships. Their stories kept the memory alive. Yet for a long time, that knowledge was ignored. Today, the partnership is seen as an important example. It shows how history can be shared and cared for by the people who live where it happened.
===
IMG: gjoa-haven-community-houses-bay-summer-evening | a small Arctic village of colourful wooden houses beside a calm bay in summer, a few boats on the water, low hills and a soft golden evening light, peaceful and quiet
HEAD: In the village of Gjoa Haven, Inuit families still tell stories about the two great ships.
BODY: Gjoa Haven is a small Inuit community on the southeast coast of King William Island. About thirteen hundred people live there. Many are descendants of the Inuit families who met Franklin's men, and later Amundsen. Stories about the ships and the explorers are part of local history. Children hear them from their grandparents. Some families have kept objects found long ago, such as pieces of wood, metal or cloth from the expedition. The village has a cultural centre and is home to Inuit guardians who help protect the wrecks. Louie Kamookak, the historian who did so much to help find Erebus, was from Gjoa Haven. He died in 2018. Even so, his work lives on. Many people in the village are proud of the role their community played. Yet life there is very much about the present. Families hunt, fish, work and go to school, in a place that is quiet, cold and beautiful.
===
IMG: arctic-tundra-summer-flowers-calm-lake | a wide Arctic tundra in summer with small white and purple flowers, a calm shallow lake reflecting the sky, low rolling hills, soft golden light of the midnight sun
HEAD: In summer, the land where Franklin's men walked is covered with small flowers.
BODY: The Arctic is not always white. For a short time in summer, the snow melts from the land. The tundra comes to life. Small flowers bloom close to the ground, white, yellow and purple. Mosses and lichens cover the rocks in many colours. Birds arrive from the south to nest. Caribou move across the land in great herds. The sun stays in the sky for twenty-four hours, circling low on the horizon. Shallow lakes reflect the endless light. King William Island in summer is quiet, wide and peaceful. Even so, the season is very short. By September, the cold returns. The flowers fade, and the birds fly south. Yet in those brief weeks, the land looks gentle. It is hard to imagine the hardship that once took place there. The men of the Franklin expedition walked across this same land, nearly two centuries ago, in the cold spring of 1848.
===
IMG: night-sky-stars-calm-frozen-sea-arctic | a calm frozen sea under a vast clear night sky full of stars, a faint glow of the northern lights on the horizon, the silhouette of a low island, perfectly still and silent
HEAD: More than one hundred and seventy years later, the Franklin expedition remains one of the great mysteries of exploration.
BODY: The story of Franklin's lost expedition has never been fully told. Many questions remain. No one knows exactly why so many men died, where Franklin was buried or what happened to the last survivors. Records may still lie hidden inside the wrecks. Each new discovery answers some questions and raises others. Scientists, archaeologists and historians continue to study the remains. Inuit communities continue to share their knowledge. Books, plays, songs and films have retold the story many times. Even so, the heart of the story is simple. About one hundred and thirty men sailed into the Arctic. They were brave and hopeful. They faced cold, darkness and hunger far from home. None of them returned. Yet they are not forgotten. Their ships have been found. Some of their names have been given back to their remains. And across the frozen sea, under the stars, the land they walked remains quiet and still.
