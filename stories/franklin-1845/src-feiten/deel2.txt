TOPIC: The first winter at Beechey Island
===
IMG: beechey-island-cliffs-frozen-bay-two-ships-winter | two wooden sailing ships frozen into the ice of a small bay below tall dark cliffs on a barren Arctic island in winter, snow on the decks, a pale twilight sky, calm and still
HEAD: The expedition spent its first winter at a small, bleak island called Beechey Island.
BODY: After leaving Greenland in the summer of 1845, the ships sailed west into the Arctic. They explored a channel to the north, then turned south. As winter came, they found a sheltered place to stop. It was a bay beside a small island called Beechey Island. The island is barren and rocky, with tall dark cliffs. Here, the ships were frozen in for the winter. The men built a few buildings on shore. There was a forge for metalwork, a storehouse and a workshop. They also set up a place for scientific observations. Searchers later found the remains of these buildings. They also found more than six hundred empty food tins, neatly stacked in a pile. Even so, the expedition left no written message here. This later puzzled searchers very much. Yet the remains at Beechey Island were the first clues to the fate of the expedition, found five years after it had disappeared.
===
IMG: three-graves-wooden-headboards-beechey-island | three simple graves with wooden headboards standing in a row on a gravel beach on a barren Arctic island, a frozen sea and dark cliffs behind, soft grey light, quiet and peaceful
HEAD: Three young sailors died during the first winter and were buried at Beechey Island.
BODY: The first winter was not easy. Three men died at Beechey Island. Their names were John Torrington, John Hartnell and William Braine. Torrington was only twenty years old. He was a stoker, who worked with the steam engine. Hartnell was an able seaman, and Braine was a Royal Marine. They were buried in graves dug into the frozen gravel of the island. Each grave had a wooden headboard, carved with the man's name, age and date of death. The headboards also carried short verses from the Bible. The graves were found by searchers in 1850. They still stand today, one of the most remote and moving cemeteries in the world. Even so, these three deaths were not unusual for a long Arctic voyage. Illness was common, and medicine was limited. Yet they were a sign of the hardships to come. For the families at home, these graves were the first real news of the lost expedition.
===
IMG: archaeologists-permafrost-tent-arctic-1984 | a small research tent on a remote snowy gravel beach in the Arctic, scientists in red parkas working quietly nearby, grey sea and sky, soft polar light
HEAD: In the 1980s, scientists studied the bodies at Beechey Island and found them almost perfectly preserved by the ice.
BODY: In 1984 and 1986, a team led by the Canadian scientist Owen Beattie went to Beechey Island. With permission, they carefully opened the three graves. The permanently frozen ground had preserved the bodies remarkably well. After nearly one hundred and forty years, the men looked almost as they had in life. The scientists took small samples of hair, bone and tissue. Then they reburied the men with care and respect. Tests showed high levels of lead in their bodies. Beattie suggested that lead from the solder in the food tins might have poisoned the men. It could have weakened them and clouded their judgement. This idea became very famous. Even so, later research has questioned it. Some newer studies suggest that the lead levels were not so unusual for people of that time. Many Victorians had high lead levels from water pipes and other sources. Yet the photographs of the men's faces became some of the most haunting images of the Arctic.
===
IMG: polar-night-ship-frozen-stars-aurora | a wooden sailing ship frozen in sea ice under a dark polar night sky with bright stars and gentle green northern lights, a soft glow from the ship's windows, calm and silent
HEAD: In the Arctic winter, the sun did not rise at all for weeks at a time.
BODY: The far north has a very unusual year. In summer, the sun never sets. In winter, it never rises. Near Beechey Island, the sun disappeared below the horizon in early November. It did not return until early February. For about three months, the men lived in darkness. Around noon, there was only a faint glow on the southern horizon. The rest of the time, the sky was dark or lit by the moon and stars. Sometimes the northern lights danced across the sky in green and white curtains. The temperature could fall below minus forty degrees. Everything froze solid. Even so, life on board continued. The men kept a strict routine of meals, work, exercise and rest. They walked on the ice for fresh air. Yet the darkness was hard on the mind. Earlier explorers wrote about the gloom and boredom of the long night. The return of the sun in February was always a time of great joy.
===
IMG: canvas-roof-over-deck-snow-banks-ship | a wooden ship frozen in Arctic ice with a large canvas tent roof covering its whole deck, snow banked up against the dark hull, a lantern glowing at the entrance, blue twilight
HEAD: In winter, the decks were covered with canvas roofs, and snow was piled against the hulls to keep out the cold.
BODY: When a ship was frozen in for the winter, the crew prepared it carefully. The sails and upper rigging were taken down and stored. A large canvas cover was stretched over the deck like a roof. This created a sheltered space where men could walk and work out of the wind. Snow was piled high against the sides of the ship. Snow is a good insulator, and it helped keep the inside warmer. Paths were cut through the snow to the ice. A hole was kept open in the ice nearby, in case of fire. The men also built snow walls to shelter their work areas on the ice. Even so, the cold always found a way in. Breath froze on the walls of the cabins. Bedding became damp with frost. Yet these methods had been tested on earlier voyages. With them, ships and men could survive a long Arctic winter in reasonable comfort.
===
IMG: magnetic-observatory-hut-instruments-arctic | an indoor scene, enclosed by wooden walls, a plain wooden ceiling, a small wooden hut on an Arctic island with brass scientific instruments on stands, a naval officer taking careful notes by lamplight, frost on the window
HEAD: The expedition was also a scientific voyage, taking careful measurements of magnetism and weather.
BODY: Franklin's expedition was not only about finding a route. It was also about science. The ships carried many scientific instruments. The officers measured the earth's magnetism very carefully. In the 1840s, scientists around the world were studying how magnetism changed from place to place. This was important for navigation, because ships used magnetic compasses. Near the North Magnetic Pole, compasses behaved strangely. The expedition was asked to take regular readings. The officers also recorded the weather, temperature and air pressure. They collected plants, rocks and animals. They studied the ice and the tides. At Beechey Island, they set up a small observatory on shore. Even so, almost all their scientific records were lost. Yet their work was part of a great international effort. Observatories across the British Empire were taking the same measurements at the same time, trying to understand the invisible forces of the planet.

TOPIC: The Inuit of the far north
===
IMG: inuit-snow-house-igloo-family-lamp-light | an indoor scene, enclosed by curved snow walls, a domed snow ceiling, the inside of an Inuit snow house in the 1840s with a family sitting on furs, a stone oil lamp burning softly, warm golden light
HEAD: The Inuit had lived in the Arctic for centuries, building warm houses from blocks of snow.
BODY: The Arctic was not an empty land. It was home to the Inuit. Their ancestors had lived there for about a thousand years. Near King William Island lived a group often called the Netsilik, the people of the seal. In winter, many Inuit families lived in snow houses. These were built from blocks of hard snow, cut with knives and stacked in a spiral to form a dome. Inside, the snow walls kept out the wind. A lamp made of soapstone burned seal oil. It gave light and warmth and was used for cooking. Sleeping platforms were covered with thick caribou furs. Even on very cold nights, a snow house could be surprisingly warm. Even so, life was hard. Food could be scarce in winter. Yet the Inuit knew how to survive in a land that defeated many European explorers. Their knowledge was the result of countless generations of careful learning.
===
IMG: inuit-hunter-seal-breathing-hole-ice-waiting | an Inuit hunter in caribou fur clothing standing very still beside a small breathing hole in the flat sea ice, holding a harpoon, a pale winter twilight over the frozen sea, calm and silent
HEAD: Inuit hunters could wait for hours by a hole in the ice for a seal to come up to breathe.
BODY: In winter, the sea near King William Island was covered with thick ice. Seals lived under the ice. They needed air, so they kept small breathing holes open. Inuit hunters searched for these holes with the help of their dogs. When a hunter found one, he waited beside it. He stood or sat completely still, sometimes for many hours, in bitter cold. He listened for the sound of a seal breathing below. When it came, he struck quickly with his harpoon. Seals were vital to the Inuit. They gave meat, fat for lamps, skin for clothing and boots, and much more. Even so, hunting was never certain. Some days brought nothing. Yet the hunters were patient and skilled. They read the ice, the wind and the behaviour of animals with great care. The British sailors on Franklin's ships had none of this knowledge. In the end, that difference was very important.
===
IMG: inuit-fur-clothing-caribou-parka-woman-sewing | an Inuit woman sewing a caribou fur parka with fine stitches by the light of a stone lamp, an indoor scene, enclosed by snow walls, a domed snow ceiling, warm golden light
HEAD: Inuit clothing made of caribou fur was much warmer than the wool worn by British sailors.
BODY: Inuit women were expert tailors. They made clothing from the skins of caribou and seals. Caribou fur was especially warm. Each hair is hollow and traps air. Clothes were often made in two layers. The inner layer had the fur facing in, against the body. The outer layer had the fur facing out. Between them, warm air was held close to the skin. Parkas had hoods trimmed with fur to protect the face from wind. Boots of sealskin kept the feet dry and warm. The stitching was so fine that the seams were almost waterproof. It took great skill and many hours to make a full set of clothing. Even so, these clothes were light and flexible. The British sailors, by contrast, wore heavy wool coats and leather boots. These became wet and froze. Yet very few European explorers of the time adopted Inuit clothing. Those who did, like the explorer John Rae, travelled far more easily.
===
IMG: inuit-dog-sled-team-across-sea-ice | an Inuit dog sled pulled by a team of dogs running across flat white sea ice under a pale Arctic sky, low hills in the distance, soft light
HEAD: The Inuit travelled long distances across the ice with dog sleds.
BODY: For travel over snow and ice, the Inuit used sleds pulled by dogs. The sleds were built from wood, bone or even frozen fish wrapped in skins, when wood was scarce. The runners were coated with a layer of ice, made by smoothing on water, so that they glided easily. A team of dogs could pull heavy loads over long distances. The dogs were also useful for hunting. They could smell seals' breathing holes under the snow. The British, by contrast, usually pulled their sledges themselves. Teams of sailors in harness dragged heavy sledges across the ice. This was called man-hauling. It was exhausting, slow work. Even so, many British officers believed it was more honourable than using dogs. Yet this belief cost them dearly. When Franklin's men later left their ships, they dragged heavy sledges and boats across the land. The Inuit, travelling light with dogs, moved much more easily.
===
IMG: inuit-elder-telling-story-family-evening | an elderly Inuit man telling a story to a family gathered around a soft lamp, children listening closely, an indoor scene, enclosed by snow walls, a domed snow ceiling, warm golden light
HEAD: Inuit oral history kept the memory of Franklin's men alive for more than a century.
BODY: The Inuit did not write books. They passed their history down by telling stories. Elders told children about important events, and those children told their own children. Some of these stories were about strange white men who came in two great ships. Inuit hunters saw the ships trapped in the ice. They met groups of sailors walking along the coast. They saw men who were weak and hungry. They found abandoned boats and camps. Later, they found one of the ships, sitting in the ice with no one aboard. These stories were told for generations. Even so, for a long time, many Europeans did not take them seriously. Yet the Inuit accounts were remarkably accurate. In the 2000s and 2010s, they helped searchers find both Erebus and Terror. An Inuit historian named Louie Kamookak spent his life collecting these stories. His work was a key part of the search.

TOPIC: Animals of the Arctic
===
IMG: polar-bear-walking-sea-ice-soft-light | a large white polar bear walking slowly across flat sea ice in the Arctic, its breath visible in the cold air, a pale pink and blue sky, calm and quiet
HEAD: Polar bears often came close to the ships of Arctic expeditions.
BODY: The polar bear is the largest land predator on earth. It lives on the sea ice of the Arctic, where it hunts seals. Arctic explorers often met polar bears. The bears were curious. Drawn by the smell of food, they sometimes walked right up to frozen ships. Sailors on earlier expeditions described bears climbing onto the ice beside their vessels. Some even tried to climb aboard. Polar bears have thick white fur and a layer of fat to keep them warm. Their fur looks white, but each hair is actually clear and hollow. Underneath, their skin is black, which helps absorb the warmth of the sun. They are strong swimmers and can travel great distances across the sea. Even so, the sailors usually feared them. Many bears were shot for food or for their skins. Yet the bears were also admired. Their strength and calm movements across the ice impressed many who saw them.
===
IMG: walrus-group-resting-ice-floe-arctic | a group of large brown walruses with long white tusks resting together on a floating ice floe in calm Arctic water, soft grey light
HEAD: Walruses use their long tusks to pull themselves out of the water onto the ice.
BODY: Walruses are large, heavy animals that live in the Arctic seas. A big male can weigh more than a tonne. Both males and females have long white tusks. They use them for many things. They hook them over the edge of the ice to pull their heavy bodies out of the water. They also use them in fights and to show their strength. Walruses feed on the seabed. They search for clams and shellfish with their sensitive whiskers. They suck the soft meat from the shells. They often rest together in large groups on floating ice or beaches. They grunt, bellow and lie close together. Even so, walruses can be dangerous if disturbed. They have been known to attack small boats. The Inuit hunted them carefully for meat, oil and ivory. Yet for the sailors on Arctic expeditions, a group of walruses lying on the ice was one of the most remarkable sights of the north.
===
IMG: narwhals-tusks-surface-calm-arctic-water | several narwhals swimming at the surface of a calm dark Arctic sea, their long spiral tusks rising from the water, small pieces of ice floating nearby, soft evening light
HEAD: The narwhal's long spiral tusk is actually a tooth that grows through its upper lip.
BODY: The narwhal is one of the strangest animals of the Arctic. It is a small whale, about four or five metres long. Most males have a single long tusk that spirals forward from the head. It can grow up to three metres long. The tusk is actually a tooth. It grows out through the upper lip. Scientists are still not completely sure what it is used for. It may help males attract females or show their status. It is also full of nerve endings and may help sense the water. In medieval Europe, narwhal tusks were sold as the horns of unicorns. They were very valuable and believed to have magic powers. Even so, the Inuit knew the truth. They hunted narwhals for food and used the ivory. Yet the legend of the unicorn lasted for centuries. Sailors on Arctic voyages were often amazed to see narwhals in the water, with their long tusks rising from the waves.
===
IMG: arctic-fox-white-winter-coat-snow | a small arctic fox with a thick white winter coat curled up on soft snow, its bushy tail wrapped around it, a pale blue twilight, quiet and still
HEAD: The arctic fox changes the colour of its fur from brown in summer to white in winter.
BODY: The arctic fox is a small animal, about the size of a large cat. It is perfectly suited to the cold. In winter, its thick fur turns white. This helps it hide against the snow. In summer, the fur turns brown or grey to match the rocks and tundra. Its short ears and legs help it lose less heat. When it sleeps, it curls up and wraps its bushy tail around its face like a blanket. Arctic foxes can survive temperatures far below minus forty degrees. They often follow polar bears to eat the leftovers of their meals. They also hunt lemmings, birds and eggs. Sailors on Arctic expeditions often saw foxes around their ships. Some even tamed them as pets. During one of the later search voyages, sailors caught foxes and fixed small copper collars with messages around their necks, hoping Franklin's men might find them. Even so, few such messages were ever recovered. Yet the little foxes became familiar companions in the long Arctic winters.
===
IMG: musk-oxen-herd-tundra-snow-shaggy | a small herd of shaggy dark brown musk oxen standing close together on snowy tundra, their thick coats hanging to the ground, a pale winter sky, soft light
HEAD: Musk oxen have such thick wool that they can survive the coldest Arctic winters in the open.
BODY: Musk oxen are large animals with long, shaggy coats. They look a little like small bison. Their outer hair hangs almost to the ground. Underneath, they have a layer of very soft, warm wool. The Inuit call it qiviut. It is one of the warmest natural fibres in the world, many times warmer than sheep's wool. Musk oxen live in small herds on the tundra. In winter, they dig through the snow with their hooves to find plants. When wolves threaten them, the adults form a circle, facing outwards, with the young in the middle. This defence works well against wolves. Even so, it worked poorly against hunters with guns. In the 1800s, many musk oxen were killed. Arctic explorers hunted them for fresh meat. Yet the animals survived. Today, musk oxen are protected in many areas. Their soft qiviut wool is gathered after it falls out naturally each spring and is knitted into very warm clothing.
===
IMG: caribou-herd-crossing-tundra-river-summer | a large herd of caribou with branching antlers crossing a shallow river on the open Arctic tundra in summer, low green hills, soft golden light
HEAD: Every year, great herds of caribou travel hundreds of miles across the Arctic tundra.
BODY: Caribou are the wild reindeer of North America. They live on the tundra and in the northern forests. Each year, many herds make long journeys. In spring, they travel north to the tundra, where the females give birth. In autumn, they return south to spend the winter in more sheltered areas. Some herds travel more than a thousand kilometres a year. Both male and female caribou grow antlers, which is unusual among deer. Caribou were vital to the Inuit. They provided meat, fur for clothing, sinew for thread and bone for tools. Their winter fur was the warmest of all for clothing. Even so, caribou herds were not always easy to find. Their routes changed from year to year. Yet when the herds came, they brought plenty. The Inuit hunted them carefully, using every part of the animal. Franklin's men may have hoped to find caribou on their long march south, but the herds were often far away.
===
IMG: snow-buntings-birds-spring-tundra-rocks | small white and black snow bunting birds hopping among rocks and patches of melting snow on the Arctic tundra in spring, soft golden morning light
HEAD: In spring, the return of the birds was a joyful sign that the Arctic winter was ending.
BODY: During the long Arctic winter, very few birds remain in the far north. Most fly south to warmer places. Then, in spring, they return. One of the first to arrive is the snow bunting, a small bird with white and black feathers. Its cheerful song was a welcome sound after months of silence. Soon, geese, ducks, gulls and many other birds follow. The Arctic tern makes the longest journey of all. It flies from the Antarctic to the Arctic and back every year. Sailors on Arctic expeditions often wrote about the joy of seeing the first birds of spring. It meant that the sun was returning and the ice might soon melt. Hunting parties also had a chance to find fresh food again. Even so, spring in the Arctic is short. Yet those first small birds carried great hope. For Franklin's men in the spring of 1848, they may have been one of the last gentle signs of life.

TOPIC: Trapped in the ice
===
IMG: ships-sailing-narrow-channel-ice-floes-summer-1846 | two wooden sailing ships moving slowly through a narrow channel between drifting white ice floes in the Arctic summer, low brown islands on both sides, soft hazy light
HEAD: In the summer of 1846, the ships sailed south towards King William Island.
BODY: When the ice melted in the summer of 1846, the ships left Beechey Island. Franklin followed his orders and sailed south and west. He went down a long channel towards King William Island. This was unknown water. No ship had been there before. Franklin may have hoped to reach the coast of North America. From there, he could sail west along a route that he and others had already mapped. That route would lead to the Pacific. It was a good plan, based on the knowledge of the time. Even so, the maps were incomplete. Franklin believed King William Island was joined to the mainland. In fact, it is an island, with a channel on its eastern side. That channel stays clearer of ice. Yet Franklin went down the western side. There, a great stream of heavy ice flows down from the north every year, pressing against the coast.
===
IMG: ships-beset-pack-ice-victoria-strait-autumn | two wooden sailing ships held tight in thick pack ice in a wide frozen strait, ridges of broken ice around them, a pale autumn sky over the white landscape, quiet and still
HEAD: In September 1846, the ships were trapped in the ice off King William Island, and they never sailed again.
BODY: In September 1846, Erebus and Terror reached the waters northwest of King William Island. There, the ice closed in around them. They were beset, which means trapped by ice. The ships could not move forward or back. They drifted slowly with the ice, as it was pushed by winds and currents. This was not unusual in the Arctic. Ships were often trapped for a winter and then freed the next summer. The officers probably expected to be released in 1847. Yet in this area, the ice did not always melt. Heavy, thick ice flowed down from the north, year after year. In some summers, it did not break up at all. Even so, the expedition had plenty of food for now. The ships were strong. Yet they would never sail again. They stayed locked in that ice for about a year and a half. The men spent two winters there, waiting for a summer that never freed them.
===
IMG: officers-sledge-party-coast-king-william-island | a small party of 1840s Royal Navy sailors in wool coats pulling a wooden sledge along a flat snowy coastline, their officer walking ahead with a telescope, low grey hills, soft pale light
HEAD: In the spring of 1847, a small party left the ships to explore the coast of King William Island.
BODY: While the ships were trapped, the officers did not simply wait. In May 1847, a party of eight men set out from the ships. They were led by Lieutenant Graham Gore and a mate named Charles Des Voeux. They dragged a sledge across the ice to King William Island. Their task was to explore the coast and leave messages. On the way, they built small piles of stones, called cairns. Inside one, they left a note in a metal container. The note said that the ships were trapped, that the expedition had wintered at Beechey Island, and that all was well. It also said that Sir John Franklin was in command. Gore and his men returned safely to the ships. Even so, they may have seen something important. From the coast, they may have glimpsed the channel that would have completed the Northwest Passage. Yet the ships were stuck in the ice, unable to follow it.
===
IMG: franklin-cabin-quiet-bunk-lamp-1847 | an indoor scene, enclosed by wooden walls, a low wooden ceiling, a quiet captain's cabin aboard a wooden ship in the Arctic, a neatly made bunk, a Bible on a small table, an oil lamp burning low, soft warm light
HEAD: Sir John Franklin died aboard Erebus on the eleventh of June 1847.
BODY: Only a few weeks after Gore's party left that hopeful note, the expedition suffered a great loss. Sir John Franklin died on board Erebus. The date was the eleventh of June 1847. He was sixty-one years old. The cause of his death is unknown. It may have been a stroke, a heart problem or an illness. He had been in command for two years. His men respected and loved him. Command now passed to Francis Crozier, captain of Terror. Where Franklin was buried remains a mystery. He may have been buried at sea, through a hole in the ice. He may have been buried on land. Some think his body might still be on board one of the ships. Even so, no grave has ever been found. Yet his death was a heavy blow. The expedition had lost its leader, its most famous member and a calm, steady presence. The hardest times were still ahead.
===
IMG: second-winter-frozen-ships-dim-twilight-1847 | two wooden ships locked in heavy sea ice in deep winter twilight, snow drifting across their covered decks, a faint glow from below deck, endless white ice around them, still and quiet
HEAD: The men spent a second long winter trapped in the ice, as supplies began to run low.
BODY: The summer of 1847 was cold. The ice around the ships did not melt. The ships stayed trapped. The men had to prepare for another winter in the same place. This was their third Arctic winter in total. The darkness returned in November. The cold was intense. By now, the supplies were running down. The expedition had carried food for three years, but it had been away for more than two. Fresh meat was scarce. Scurvy may have begun to appear. Some men fell ill and died. Even so, the officers kept the routine going. The men still had their duties, their meals and their prayers. The barrel organs may still have played on Sundays. Yet the mood must have changed. The hope of sailing on was fading. The officers began to think about leaving the ships altogether, and walking south towards the mainland of Canada, where there were rivers and fur trading posts.

TOPIC: The note at Victory Point
===
IMG: stone-cairn-note-metal-cylinder-shore | a small pile of stones on a barren snowy shore with a small metal cylinder tucked among the stones, a frozen sea beyond, low grey sky, soft light
HEAD: In 1859, searchers found a note in a stone cairn that told the fate of the expedition.
BODY: For years, no one knew what had happened to Franklin's men. Then, in May 1859, a search party made a vital discovery. It was led by Lieutenant William Hobson. He was part of an expedition paid for by Lady Franklin and commanded by Francis McClintock. On the northwest coast of King William Island, at a place called Victory Point, Hobson found a stone cairn. Inside it was a metal cylinder. In the cylinder was a printed form. Such forms were carried by naval ships. On them, captains wrote their position and asked whoever found the note to send it to the Admiralty. The form had been written on twice. The first message, from May 1847, was the hopeful note left by Gore. The second message was written around the edges a year later. It was very different. Even so, the note was short, only a few lines. Yet it is the only written record ever found from the Franklin expedition.
===
IMG: handwritten-note-margins-paper-close-up | a close view of an old printed naval form on yellowed paper with faded handwriting crowded around its margins, resting on a rock, soft grey daylight
HEAD: The second message said the ships had been abandoned on the twenty-second of April 1848.
BODY: The second message on the Victory Point note was written in April 1848. It was signed by Captain Crozier and Captain Fitzjames. It said that the ships had been deserted on the twenty-second of April. They had been trapped in the ice since September 1846. It said that Sir John Franklin had died on the eleventh of June 1847. It said that nine officers and fifteen men had died so far. The remaining men, one hundred and five in all, had landed on King William Island. They planned to set out the next day for Back's Fish River. This was a river on the Canadian mainland, far to the south. Even so, the message hid much of what must have been happening. It did not say why so many had died. Yet its words were enough to break hearts in Britain. For the first time, people knew that Franklin was dead and that his men had left the ships.
===
IMG: abandoned-boat-on-sledge-snowy-shore | an old wooden ship's boat resting on a heavy wooden sledge on a lonely snowy shore of an Arctic island, a few scattered objects in the snow around it, grey sky, quiet and still
HEAD: Searchers found a ship's boat on a sledge, loaded with a strange mix of objects.
BODY: During the same search in 1859, McClintock and Hobson found other remains. On the coast of King William Island, they discovered a large wooden boat. It was mounted on a heavy sledge, built to be dragged across the ice. Inside the boat, they found many objects. There were tools, guns, clothing and blankets. There were books, including Bibles and a copy of a popular novel. There were silver forks and spoons, many marked with the crests of officers. There were soap, towels, slippers and small personal treasures. The boat and sledge together were very heavy. McClintock was puzzled. Why had exhausted men dragged such things across the snow? Even so, the answer may be simple. The men may have expected to need these things later, or they could not bear to leave them behind. Yet the heavy loads must have slowed them terribly on their long march south.
===
IMG: inuit-testimony-explorer-listening-tent | an Inuit man and woman in fur clothing speaking with a Victorian explorer in a heavy coat inside a small canvas tent, the explorer taking notes, a small lamp glowing, an indoor scene, soft warm light
HEAD: Inuit witnesses told of seeing groups of weak, hungry sailors walking along the coast.
BODY: Several explorers spoke with the Inuit about Franklin's men. One of the first was a Scottish doctor named John Rae. In 1854, he met Inuit who told him about a group of white men who had died far to the south. They showed him objects they had collected, including silver spoons and a medal. Later, the American explorer Charles Francis Hall spent years living with the Inuit. He recorded many stories. The Inuit described meeting groups of sailors walking across the ice. The men were very thin and weak. Some fell down as they walked. The Inuit sometimes shared seal meat with them. But they could not feed so many people. They had to look after their own families. Even so, some of these accounts were painful to British readers. Many refused to believe them. Yet historians now see the Inuit testimony as the most important source for the final days of the expedition.
===
IMG: lone-cairn-headland-sunset-king-william-island | a lonely stone cairn standing on a low headland of a barren Arctic island at sunset, the frozen sea glowing faintly pink and gold, a calm and silent landscape
HEAD: None of the one hundred and five men who left the ships in 1848 is known to have survived.
BODY: The men who left the ships in April 1848 set out to walk south. It was a journey of hundreds of miles. They faced cold, hunger and illness. They dragged heavy boats and sledges. Along the coast of King William Island, searchers later found remains of their camps and graves. Some men may have turned back towards the ships. Inuit stories suggest that some men may have survived for several more years. They speak of a ship that was manned again, and of men seen far to the south. Even so, none of them ever reached a European outpost. None of them came home. The exact story of their last days is still not fully known. Yet the courage of the men is clear. They kept going in impossible conditions. They helped each other and carried their sick. Their long walk south is one of the most moving stories of exploration. It ended quietly, somewhere in the snow.
