TOPIC: Life aboard Erebus and Terror
===
IMG: erebus-terror-ships-icebergs-midnight-sun-1845 | two black-hulled wooden sailing ships of the Royal Navy among large blue-white icebergs in the Arctic in 1845, golden light of the midnight sun, calm grey water, quiet and still
HEAD: In May 1845, two Royal Navy ships sailed from England to find the Northwest Passage.
BODY: On the nineteenth of May 1845, two ships left the River Thames. They were called Erebus and Terror. They were sturdy wooden ships of the Royal Navy, painted black, with yellow bands along their sides. Together they carried about one hundred and thirty men. Their leader was Sir John Franklin, an experienced Arctic explorer. Their goal was to find the Northwest Passage. This was a sea route from the Atlantic to the Pacific through the icy waters north of Canada. For centuries, sailors had searched for it. Many had failed. The British Admiralty believed that this time, with the best ships and supplies, it would be found. Crowds came to watch the ships leave. Newspapers wrote about the expedition with great confidence. Even so, the Arctic was still largely unknown. No ship had ever sailed all the way through. Yet the mood was cheerful. Few people imagined that none of the men would ever come home.
===
IMG: lower-deck-hammocks-mess-tables-lamplight-ship | an indoor scene, enclosed by wooden walls, a low wooden ceiling, the lower deck of a Royal Navy ship in the 1840s with hammocks slung from the beams, long mess tables and benches, sailors sitting together, soft oil lamplight
HEAD: Ordinary sailors slept in hammocks hung from the beams, only a few feet apart.
BODY: Life aboard a Royal Navy ship in 1845 was crowded. Most of the men lived on the lower deck. They ate, slept and spent their free time there. At night, they slung hammocks from hooks in the wooden beams overhead. Each man had a space only a little wider than his shoulders. In the morning, the hammocks were rolled up and stored. Then the space became a dining room. Tables were lowered from the ceiling on ropes. The men sat on benches and lockers in small groups called messes. Each mess cooked and shared its food together. The air below deck was often damp and smoky. In the Arctic, it was also very cold. Even so, the crowded deck was a warm and friendly place in many ways. Sailors told stories, played games and sang. Yet there was little privacy. A sailor's whole world, for years at a time, was a few square metres of wooden deck.
===
IMG: officers-wardroom-table-dinner-candles-ship | an indoor scene, enclosed by wooden walls, a low wooden ceiling, a small wardroom aboard an 1840s naval ship with officers in dark blue uniforms dining at a polished table, silver cutlery, candles and a small stove, warm candlelight
HEAD: The officers dined together in the wardroom, with silver cutlery and fine china.
BODY: Officers lived very differently from the ordinary sailors. Each had a small cabin of his own. It was only a few feet wide, with a bunk, a small desk and a shelf for books. Yet it was private. The officers ate together in a room called the wardroom. Their meals were served on china plates with silver cutlery. Many officers brought silver spoons and forks engraved with their family crests. Some of these were later found by searchers in the Arctic. Officers paid for extra food and wine from their own money. They held dinners for each other and celebrated birthdays. Sir John Franklin had a larger cabin at the back of Erebus. He often invited officers to dine with him. Even so, the wardroom was also a place of work. Officers wrote reports, studied charts and discussed plans there. Yet it was the closest thing to a comfortable home that the long voyage offered.
===
IMG: ship-school-sailors-learning-to-read-slates | an indoor scene, enclosed by wooden walls, a low wooden ceiling, sailors in woollen jumpers sitting at a mess table writing on small slates while an officer teaches them, oil lamps glowing, quiet winter evening on an 1840s ship
HEAD: On long Arctic voyages, officers often ran schools aboard ship to teach sailors reading and arithmetic.
BODY: Arctic expeditions spent many months trapped in ice. The long dark winters could be very boring. So expedition leaders tried to keep the men busy. One popular idea was a ship's school. It had been used on earlier voyages by the explorer William Parry. In the evenings, officers taught the sailors to read, write and do arithmetic. Some men had never been to school. Others wanted to improve their chances of promotion. Lessons were held at the mess tables, by the light of oil lamps. The men wrote on slates with chalk to save paper. The Franklin expedition carried slates and school supplies for this purpose. Even so, no records of the lessons survive. The ships' journals were lost. Yet we know the materials were on board. On earlier voyages, sailors said these quiet classes were one of the best parts of the winter. A few left the Arctic able to read for the first time.
===
IMG: barrel-organ-wooden-ship-cabin-hymns | an indoor scene, enclosed by wooden walls, a low wooden ceiling, a small wooden barrel organ with a handle standing on a table in a ship's cabin in the 1840s, a few sailors listening quietly, warm lamplight
HEAD: Each ship carried a barrel organ that could play dozens of tunes, including hymns and dances.
BODY: Music was important on long voyages. On Franklin's ships, there was a special instrument. Each ship carried a barrel organ. It was a wooden box with pipes inside. By turning a handle, a sailor could make it play. A wooden barrel with pins inside decided which notes sounded. By changing the barrel, the organ could play different tunes. According to reports, each organ could play around fifty tunes. There were hymns and psalms for Sunday services. There were also popular songs and dances for evenings. The men could gather around and sing along. Some sailors also brought their own instruments, such as fiddles or flutes. Even so, the barrel organ was the main source of music in the long Arctic nights. It needed no musician, only someone to turn the handle. Yet it brought comfort and familiar sounds from home into the cold and silent world of the ice.
===
IMG: jacko-monkey-neptune-dog-deck-ship | a small monkey sitting on the wooden rail of an 1840s sailing ship beside a large black Newfoundland dog lying on the deck, coils of rope and a calm grey sea behind, soft daylight
HEAD: The ships carried animals, including a monkey named Jacko and a Newfoundland dog called Neptune.
BODY: Erebus and Terror did not carry only men. There were animals on board too. Like many ships of the time, they carried live animals for food at the start of the voyage. There were also pets. On Erebus, there was a monkey named Jacko. He had been a gift to the expedition. Officers wrote that he was playful and sometimes naughty. He liked to steal things and run off with them. There was also a large Newfoundland dog named Neptune. Newfoundland dogs were strong swimmers and loyal companions. Some accounts also mention a cat. In letters sent home from Greenland, officers described these animals with affection. Even so, we do not know what happened to them later. They disappeared along with the rest of the expedition. Yet they show a gentle side of life aboard. Far from home, the men took comfort in the company of animals, who knew nothing of the dangers ahead.
===
IMG: officer-writing-letter-cabin-greenland-1845 | an indoor scene, enclosed by wooden walls, a low wooden ceiling, a young naval officer in a dark blue coat writing a long letter at a small desk in a cramped ship's cabin, a porthole showing grey sea, soft lamplight
HEAD: The last letters from the expedition were sent home from Greenland in July 1845.
BODY: In early July 1845, the ships stopped at a small harbour on the west coast of Greenland. A supply ship that had sailed with them unloaded fresh food. Then it prepared to return to England. This was the men's last chance to send letters home. Many wrote long ones. Commander James Fitzjames of Erebus wrote a kind of journal letter to his family. He described the officers, the ships and the daily life aboard. He wrote warmly about Sir John Franklin. He said Franklin was cheerful, kind and full of energy. Other men wrote to wives, mothers and children. Five men were sent home on the supply ship, mostly because of illness. They were the lucky ones. Even so, the letters were full of hope. The men expected to be home in two or three years. Yet these letters became the last words most families ever received.

TOPIC: The ships and their machines
===
IMG: bomb-vessel-hms-terror-strong-hull-dock | a sturdy wooden three-masted sailing ship with a very thick reinforced black hull resting in a dry dock in England in the 1840s, workmen and timber around it, soft morning light
HEAD: Erebus and Terror were built as bomb ships, with very strong hulls designed to carry heavy mortars.
BODY: Erebus and Terror were not ordinary ships. They had been built as bomb vessels. These were warships designed to carry heavy mortars, which fired explosive shells high into the air. To carry such heavy weapons, the ships needed very strong hulls. Their wooden frames were thick and closely spaced. This strength made them good for the Arctic, where ice could crush a ship. Terror was the older of the two. She had been built in 1813. She even took part in the War of 1812 against the United States. Erebus was built in 1826. Both ships were later converted for exploration. Their hulls were strengthened with extra layers of planking and iron plates on the bow. Even so, they were not large. Each was only about thirty metres long. Yet they had already proved themselves. Before Franklin, both ships had sailed to the Antarctic and returned safely.
===
IMG: antarctic-volcano-mount-erebus-ice-sea | a large snow-covered volcano with a thin plume of steam rising from its summit beside a frozen sea in Antarctica, pale blue sky and soft polar light, calm and vast
HEAD: A volcano in Antarctica is named after the ship Erebus.
BODY: Before their Arctic voyage, Erebus and Terror made a famous journey to the far south. From 1839 to 1843, they sailed to Antarctica under the command of James Clark Ross. Francis Crozier, who later sailed with Franklin, commanded Terror. They explored waters no ship had reached before. They discovered a huge shelf of ice, taller than the masts of the ships. It is now called the Ross Ice Shelf. They also saw two great volcanoes rising from the ice. Ross named them after his ships. They are still called Mount Erebus and Mount Terror. Mount Erebus is an active volcano. It still sends up steam and smoke today. Even so, the Antarctic voyage was very dangerous. The ships were nearly lost several times in storms and among icebergs. Yet they came home safely after four years. Their success made the Admiralty confident that they could also conquer the Arctic.
===
IMG: railway-locomotive-engine-installed-ship-hold | an indoor scene, enclosed by wooden walls, a low wooden ceiling, a small steam railway locomotive engine fitted into the dark hold of a wooden ship in the 1840s, pipes and a boiler, engineers in shirtsleeves, warm lamplight
HEAD: Each ship was fitted with an engine taken from a railway locomotive.
BODY: The Franklin expedition used the newest technology of its time. Each ship was given a steam engine. These were not ship engines. They were engines from railway locomotives, adapted for use at sea. The engine for Terror came from the London and Birmingham Railway. The engine for Erebus came from the London and Greenwich Railway. They were placed deep in the hold of each ship. They turned a propeller at the back. The propeller could be lifted out of the water to protect it from the ice. The engines were not powerful. They could push the ships at only about four knots in calm water. They also used a lot of coal. Even so, they were useful for short distances, when there was no wind. Yet the idea was very modern. In 1845, steam power at sea was still quite new. These ships combined old wooden sailing craft with the machinery of the railway age.
===
IMG: hot-water-pipes-heating-ship-lower-deck | an indoor scene, enclosed by wooden walls, a low wooden ceiling, iron hot water pipes running along the floor of a ship's lower deck in the 1840s, steam faintly rising, sailors warming their hands nearby, soft lamplight
HEAD: The ships had a heating system that sent warm water through pipes under the decks.
BODY: Keeping warm was a matter of life and death in the Arctic. Erebus and Terror had a special heating system. The boiler of the steam engine heated water. The hot water flowed through iron pipes running under the decks where the men lived. This warmed the air and helped keep it dry. It also helped prevent dampness, which caused illness. The system was also used to melt snow and ice to make fresh water. This saved the men from having to melt it over fires. In the coldest months, the decks were also covered with a canvas roof. Snow was banked against the outside of the hull for extra insulation. Even so, the ships were never truly warm. Frost formed on the inside of the walls. The men wore thick wool clothes even below deck. Yet the heating made a great difference. It was one more way in which this expedition was better equipped than any before it.
===
IMG: ice-sheathing-bow-iron-plates-wooden-ship | the bow of a wooden sailing ship from the 1840s covered with heavy iron plates, chunks of floating sea ice around it, cold grey Arctic water and a pale sky, soft light
HEAD: The bows of the ships were covered with iron plates to help them break through ice.
BODY: The Arctic sea is full of ice, even in summer. Ships had to push through it or wait for it to move. Ice could damage a wooden hull or crush it completely. So Erebus and Terror were strengthened. Their bows, at the front, were covered with heavy iron plates. This protected them when they pushed into loose ice. Inside, the hulls were braced with extra timber beams. The decks were doubled with extra layers of wood. The ships were among the strongest wooden vessels ever built. Even so, no wooden ship could resist heavy pack ice forever. When the ice closed in from all sides, it could squeeze a ship until the timbers cracked. The officers knew this. Yet they trusted their ships. When Parks Canada later found Terror on the seabed, the hull was still remarkably intact. The strong old ship had survived more than a hundred and sixty years underwater.
===
IMG: daguerreotype-camera-officer-sitting-portrait-1845 | an 1840s photographic studio with a large wooden camera on a tripod and a naval officer in dark uniform sitting very still in a chair, a painted backdrop, soft daylight from a large window
HEAD: Before leaving, the officers had their portraits taken with a new invention called the daguerreotype.
BODY: Photography was very new in 1845. The first practical method, the daguerreotype, had been introduced only six years earlier. It made a single, very sharp image on a polished silver plate. Before the expedition sailed, a photographer named Richard Beard came to the ships. He made daguerreotypes of Sir John Franklin and many of the officers. The men had to sit still for several seconds while the image formed. Some look serious. Others look relaxed. Franklin is shown in his uniform, wearing a cap. He looks tired but calm. These pictures are among the earliest photographs of any expedition. Even so, there are no photographs of the ordinary sailors. Only officers were photographed. Yet these images are precious. They show the faces of men who would vanish into the Arctic. Today, they are kept in museums in Britain. Visitors can look into the eyes of the officers who sailed with Franklin.

TOPIC: Food, stores and books
===
IMG: tin-cans-food-stores-ship-hold-1845 | an indoor scene, enclosed by wooden walls, a low wooden ceiling, rows of tin cans and wooden barrels stacked in the hold of an 1840s ship, labels with simple writing, a lantern hanging from a beam, warm lamplight
HEAD: The expedition carried food for three years, including thousands of tins of meat, soup and vegetables.
BODY: Erebus and Terror were loaded with supplies for at least three years. There were tons of flour, salt beef and salt pork. There were barrels of biscuit, sugar, tea, chocolate and oatmeal. There was lemon juice, to protect against scurvy. Most modern of all were the tinned foods. Canning was still a new idea. The expedition carried thousands of tins of meat, soup and vegetables. They had been supplied by a man named Stephan Goldner. His company won the contract by offering a low price. The tins were made by hand and sealed with lead solder. Even so, there were problems. Goldner had to work quickly to fill the large order. Some tins were badly made. Later, other ships found that some of Goldner's tins had spoiled. Yet in 1845, tinned food was seen as a great advance. It meant that men could eat meat and vegetables in places where no fresh food could be found.
===
IMG: lemon-juice-bottles-scurvy-ship-store | rows of glass bottles filled with pale yellow lemon juice packed in straw in wooden crates in a ship's storeroom, an indoor scene, enclosed by wooden walls, a low wooden ceiling, soft lamplight
HEAD: Lemon juice was carried to protect the sailors from scurvy.
BODY: Scurvy was one of the great dangers of long voyages. It is caused by a lack of vitamin C. Sailors who ate only salt meat and biscuit for months began to suffer. Their gums became soft and bled. Old wounds opened. They grew weak and tired. By the 1840s, the Royal Navy knew that citrus juice helped prevent it. Each sailor received a daily ration of lemon juice, mixed with sugar and water. Franklin's ships carried large supplies of it. Even so, there was a problem. Lemon juice slowly loses its vitamin C over time, especially in warm conditions. After two or three years, it may have lost much of its power. Fresh meat also helps prevent scurvy. Inuit hunters stayed healthy by eating fresh seal and caribou. Yet the British sailors rarely ate as the Inuit did. Most historians believe that scurvy played a part in weakening Franklin's men, along with cold, hunger and other illnesses.
===
IMG: ship-library-shelves-books-cabin-1845 | an indoor scene, enclosed by wooden walls, a low wooden ceiling, wooden bookshelves full of leather-bound books in a ship's cabin in the 1840s, a reading chair and an oil lamp on a small table, warm lamplight
HEAD: The two ships carried libraries of more than two thousand books between them.
BODY: The long Arctic winters left plenty of time for reading. So Erebus and Terror carried large libraries. Together, they held more than two thousand books. There were books on science, history, travel and navigation. There were Bibles, prayer books and sermons. There were novels and magazines, including humorous ones like Punch. There were books by Charles Dickens, whose stories were popular at the time. There were also accounts of earlier Arctic voyages. These may have helped the officers plan their route. Many books had been donated by friends and supporters of the expedition. Even so, no one knows exactly which books were read. Most were lost with the ships. Yet a few pages and book fragments were later found on King William Island. One was a small devotional book. These remains show that the men carried their books with them, even when they left the ships. Reading was a comfort to the very end.
===
IMG: christmas-dinner-ship-lower-deck-arctic | an indoor scene, enclosed by wooden walls, a low wooden ceiling, sailors gathered around a long table on the lower deck of a ship in the Arctic, with plum puddings, mugs and small decorations, lanterns glowing warmly
HEAD: On earlier Arctic expeditions, Christmas was celebrated with plum pudding, extra rations and music.
BODY: Christmas was a special day on any Royal Navy ship. On Arctic expeditions, it was even more important. It broke up the long darkness of winter. We do not have records of Franklin's Christmases. Yet many earlier and later expeditions described theirs. Sailors decorated the lower deck with flags and paper. Officers gave extra rations of meat, plum pudding and sometimes beer or grog. The men sang songs, danced and played games. Some ships held plays or concerts. Officers sometimes joined the sailors for part of the evening. Franklin's ships carried plenty of supplies for such celebrations. They also had their barrel organs for music. Even so, Christmas in the Arctic could be bittersweet. The men thought of their families far away. Yet these celebrations helped keep spirits high. On a dark ship, frozen into the ice, a warm Christmas dinner was a reminder of home.
===
IMG: arctic-hunting-party-snow-musket-ptarmigan | a small group of 1840s sailors in heavy wool coats and caps walking across snowy tundra with muskets, a few white ptarmigan birds in the snow, a frozen sea and ships in the distance, pale Arctic light
HEAD: The sailors hunted for fresh meat, but game was often hard to find.
BODY: To add to their supplies, Arctic expeditions often went hunting. Sailors took muskets and walked out across the ice and land. They hunted birds such as ptarmigan, ducks and geese. They hoped to find caribou, musk oxen or seals. Fresh meat was very welcome. It tasted better than salt meat, and it helped prevent scurvy. Franklin's ships carried guns and ammunition for this purpose. Yet hunting was not easy for men who had grown up in British towns. Arctic animals were wary and hard to approach. In winter, many birds flew south, and other animals were scarce. The Inuit were expert hunters. They knew where and when to find animals. They used methods developed over thousands of years. Even so, the British rarely learned these skills. Few expeditions of the time thought of learning from the Inuit. That was one of their greatest weaknesses in the far north.

TOPIC: Sir John Franklin and Lady Jane
===
IMG: sir-john-franklin-portrait-older-officer-uniform | an older Royal Navy officer with a kind round face and receding hair, wearing a dark blue uniform with gold epaulettes, sitting calmly in a study with charts on the table, soft window light
HEAD: Sir John Franklin was fifty-nine years old when he led the expedition.
BODY: John Franklin was born in 1786 in Lincolnshire, in the east of England. He went to sea as a boy. He fought at the battle of Copenhagen in 1801 and at the battle of Trafalgar in 1805. Later, he sailed on a voyage of exploration around Australia with his uncle by marriage, Matthew Flinders. He became fascinated by exploration. In the following decades, he led expeditions to the Arctic coasts of Canada. By 1845, he was famous. He was knighted, respected and much liked. People described him as kind, religious and gentle. He hated cruelty and did not like to punish men. Some thought he was too old for another Arctic voyage. He was fifty-nine and quite heavy. Even so, he was eager to go. He saw the Northwest Passage as the great prize of his life. Yet he also knew the dangers. He had seen men suffer in the Arctic before. He accepted the command with joy and determination.
===
IMG: franklin-earlier-expedition-canoes-arctic-river | small birch-bark canoes with British explorers and Canadian voyageurs paddling down a wide cold river through the tundra in the 1820s, low hills and scattered rocks, soft evening light
HEAD: On an earlier expedition, Franklin and his men were so hungry that they ate the leather of their own boots.
BODY: Franklin's first Arctic expedition took place from 1819 to 1822. He travelled overland through northern Canada to explore the Arctic coast. The journey was long and very difficult. On the way back, the expedition ran out of food. Winter came early. The men were forced to eat lichen scraped from rocks. They boiled and ate pieces of leather, including old shoes. Many men died of hunger and cold. Franklin survived, along with a few others. Back in England, he became famous as the man who ate his boots. The story made him a hero. People admired his courage and endurance. Even so, the expedition had been a terrible struggle. It showed how dangerous the Arctic could be. Yet Franklin did not give up. He returned to the Arctic in 1825 on a second, much more successful expedition. He mapped hundreds of miles of unknown coast. He learned a great deal about the north from these journeys.
===
IMG: van-diemens-land-government-house-hobart-1840 | a colonial government house with white walls and a wide verandah in Hobart, Tasmania, around 1840, gardens and a distant mountain behind, soft warm afternoon light
HEAD: Before his last voyage, Franklin served as governor of Van Diemen's Land, now called Tasmania.
BODY: From 1837 to 1843, Franklin worked far from the Arctic. He was Lieutenant-Governor of Van Diemen's Land. This was a British colony on an island south of Australia, now called Tasmania. It was largely a penal colony, where convicts were sent from Britain. Franklin tried to be a fair and kind governor. He supported education and science. He founded a scientific society and helped start a museum. His wife, Jane, was also very active. She travelled widely around the island and supported many projects. Yet Franklin's time there was difficult. He clashed with local officials who opposed his ideas. In the end, he was recalled to Britain. He felt hurt and humiliated. Even so, his years in Tasmania are still remembered. Towns, streets and places there carry his name. Yet he wanted to restore his reputation. The Arctic expedition of 1845 gave him that chance.
===
IMG: lady-jane-franklin-portrait-study-letters | an elegant Victorian woman in a dark silk dress sitting at a desk covered with letters and maps in a study in the 1850s, a globe beside her, soft window light
HEAD: Lady Jane Franklin spent years and much of her fortune searching for her husband.
BODY: Jane Franklin was John Franklin's second wife. His first wife, Eleanor, had died young. Jane was intelligent, determined and well travelled. She had visited many countries and climbed mountains at a time when few women did such things. When the expedition did not return, she refused to give up hope. She wrote letters to the Admiralty, to newspapers and even to the President of the United States. She asked for help to search for her husband and his men. When the government's searches slowed, she paid for her own expeditions. She sold property and raised money from the public. Even so, she could not save her husband. He had died years earlier, though she did not know it. Yet her persistence led to important discoveries. One of the ships she paid for found the first written record of what had happened. Jane Franklin became one of the most famous women of her time.
===
IMG: crozier-captain-hms-terror-irish-officer | a serious Royal Navy captain in his late forties with dark hair and a calm face, wearing a dark blue uniform, standing on the deck of a wooden ship among Arctic ice, grey light
HEAD: Captain Francis Crozier of Terror was one of the most experienced polar sailors of his time.
BODY: Francis Crozier was born in 1796 in Banbridge, in the north of Ireland. He joined the navy at thirteen. He sailed on several Arctic voyages under William Parry in the 1820s. Later he commanded Terror on the great Antarctic voyage with James Clark Ross. By 1845, he had more polar experience than almost anyone in the navy. On the Franklin expedition, he was second in command and captain of Terror. He was a quiet and serious man. In letters, he sometimes sounded worried about the expedition. He seems to have felt lonely and unsure. Even so, he was respected by his men. When Franklin died in 1847, Crozier took command of the whole expedition. He led the survivors when they left the ships in 1848. Yet his fate remains unknown. No grave or remains of Crozier have ever been identified. In Banbridge, a large monument stands in his memory.
===
IMG: fitzjames-commander-erebus-young-officer-smile | a handsome young Royal Navy commander in his early thirties with curly hair and a friendly smile, wearing a dark blue uniform, standing on a ship's deck in soft morning light
HEAD: James Fitzjames, the commander of Erebus, was known for his humour and his lively letters.
BODY: James Fitzjames was a young and popular officer. He was about thirty-two in 1845. He had served bravely in a war in China and on a long expedition along the Euphrates River in the Middle East. He was handsome, cheerful and full of jokes. His friends loved him. On the Franklin expedition, he was the commander of Erebus, under Franklin himself. He wrote long letters home describing the voyage. They were full of humour and kind descriptions of his fellow officers. He described one officer as a clever and quiet man and another as very funny. He wrote that Franklin was delightful and full of energy. Even so, his letters stop in July 1845, in Greenland. Nothing more was heard from him. Yet in 2024, scientists identified some of his remains on King William Island, using DNA from a living relative. After almost one hundred and eighty years, Fitzjames had been found.

TOPIC: The men of the expedition
===
IMG: young-sailors-ship-deck-rigging-1845-thames | young Royal Navy sailors in short blue jackets and round hats coiling ropes and working in the rigging of a wooden ship on the River Thames in 1845, soft morning light
HEAD: Most of the sailors on Franklin's ships were young men in their twenties.
BODY: The crews of Erebus and Terror were mostly young. Many of the sailors were in their twenties. Some were still teenagers. They came from all over Britain. Many were from London, Kent and the ports along the south and east coasts. Others came from Scotland, Ireland and the north of England. A few came from other countries. Some had sailed on whaling ships in the Arctic before. Others had never seen ice. They were chosen carefully. Officers looked for healthy, steady men with good records. Arctic service was popular, because it paid double wages. A sailor could send money home to his family. Even so, the men knew the risks. Arctic voyages were famous for their dangers. Yet many were eager to go. The expedition was the most famous voyage of its time. To be part of it was an honour. For a young sailor, it was also the adventure of a lifetime.
===
IMG: ice-master-whaler-old-sailor-telescope-crows-nest | a weathered older sailor with a grey beard and a thick wool coat looking through a brass telescope from a barrel lookout high on the mast of a wooden ship, ice floes far below, pale Arctic light
HEAD: Each ship carried an experienced whaling captain, called an ice master, to guide it through the ice.
BODY: The Royal Navy knew that its officers were not experts in Arctic ice. Whaling captains were. They sailed to the Arctic every year to hunt whales. They knew how ice moved and how to find a safe path through it. So each of Franklin's ships carried an ice master. On Terror, the ice master was Thomas Blanky. He had already sailed on several Arctic expeditions. On Erebus, it was James Reid, a whaler from Aberdeen in Scotland. Ice masters often spent hours in the crow's nest, a lookout high on the mast. From there, they could see far across the ice. They watched for open channels of water, called leads. Even so, the ice masters had no control over the weather. Some years, the ice simply did not open. Yet their skill was valued highly. On earlier voyages, ice masters had saved ships many times by choosing the right route through the floes.
===
IMG: ship-surgeon-cabin-microscope-specimens-jars | an indoor scene, enclosed by wooden walls, a low wooden ceiling, a young ship's surgeon in a dark coat studying a small sea creature under a brass microscope in a cabin full of glass specimen jars, warm lamplight
HEAD: Harry Goodsir, the assistant surgeon on Erebus, was also a talented naturalist.
BODY: Each ship carried a surgeon and an assistant surgeon. On Erebus, the assistant surgeon was Harry Goodsir. He was a young Scottish doctor from Edinburgh, about twenty-five years old. He was also a keen naturalist. He was especially interested in small sea creatures. He brought a microscope, nets and jars on the voyage. In his letters from Greenland, he described the tiny animals he had collected from the sea. He was cheerful and enthusiastic. The other officers liked him very much. He hoped to make important scientific discoveries in the Arctic. Even so, his collections were lost with the ships. None of his Arctic notes have been found. Yet his letters show the curiosity of the men aboard. They were not only sailors and explorers. Some were also scientists, eager to learn about the natural world. Goodsir's brother John was a famous professor of anatomy, who later worried about him for years.
===
IMG: royal-marines-red-coats-ship-deck-1845 | a small group of Royal Marines in red coats and white cross belts standing on the deck of a wooden ship in 1845, sailors working around them, grey sky and calm sea
HEAD: A small group of Royal Marines sailed on each ship to keep order and help with the work.
BODY: Royal Navy ships of the 1840s carried soldiers called Royal Marines. Franklin's ships each had a small group of them, led by a sergeant. They wore red coats, unlike the blue of the sailors. On a warship, marines guarded the officers and fought in battles. On an expedition, their role was quieter. They kept watch, guarded the stores and helped keep discipline. They also worked alongside the sailors, hauling ropes and pulling sledges. William Braine, one of the three men buried at Beechey Island, was a marine. Even so, marines and sailors did not always get along. There were old rivalries between them. Yet on a small ship, far from home, they had to live and work closely together. They shared the same food, the same cold and the same dangers. By the end, there was likely little difference between them. They were all simply members of the same lost company.

HEAD: A few young boys sailed with the expedition as ship's boys.
BODY: Among the crew were a few very young sailors. They were called ship's boys. They were usually in their middle or late teens. They ran errands, helped the cook, cleaned the decks and learned the ways of the sea. Many boys of the time joined the navy young. It was a common start for a sailor's career. On Franklin's ships, the boys were rated as second-class boys or first-class boys. Their names are recorded in the official lists of the crew. They were treated kindly by many of the older men, who looked out for them. Even so, life aboard was hard for them as for everyone. They worked long hours and slept in hammocks like the others. Yet they were also eager and full of hope. For them, the voyage was a chance to learn and rise in the world. They were among the youngest of all the men who never came home.

IMG: muster-list-ledger-names-ink-admiralty | an old open ledger with columns of handwritten names in brown ink lying on a wooden desk, a quill pen and an inkwell beside it, soft window light
HEAD: The names of all the men who sailed with Franklin are recorded in the navy's official lists.
BODY: The Royal Navy kept careful records. For every ship, it kept a list called a muster book. It recorded the name, age, birthplace and rank of every man aboard. It also recorded their pay. The muster books of Erebus and Terror survive. They tell us who the men were. There were officers and engineers, carpenters and cooks, sailmakers and stokers. There were men who looked after the officers, and men who worked in the hold. The lists give the details of their lives in a few words. A sailor born in London, aged twenty-five. A marine from Devon, aged thirty-two. Even so, the lists cannot tell us what the men were like. They do not tell us about their hopes, their families or their humour. Yet they preserve their names. Because of them, historians can trace the families of the crew today. Many descendants still remember an ancestor who sailed into the ice and never returned.
