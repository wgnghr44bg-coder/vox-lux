Who built the pyramid?

For a long time, people believed it was built by slaves. Long lines of chained men, whipped by cruel overseers. That is what some old stories and films showed. But the archaeologists have found something different.

--

The builders were Egyptians. Many were farmers who came to work for the king for a few months. Especially during the flood, when their fields were under water. Others were skilled workers who worked on the pyramid all year. Stone cutters and builders and carpenters. Painters and smiths and boatmen. They were paid in bread and beer and cloth. They lived in a town near the pyramid. And many were proud to work on the king's great tomb.

Of course, the work was hard. And people probably had little choice when the king called them. It was a kind of tax, paid in work. But they were not slaves. They had families and homes. They were fed and looked after. And when they died, many were buried in proper tombs right next to the king's pyramid. Slaves would never have been given that honour.

{img:workers-graffiti-red-paint-gang-name-stone-block-friends-of-khufu}
The workers were organised in groups. A big group had perhaps a few hundred men. Each big group was split into smaller teams of about twenty. And each team had a name.

--

We know some of these names. The workers painted them on the stones in red paint. Inside the pyramid, on blocks that nobody was supposed to see. Names like Friends of Khufu. Or the Drunkards of Menkaure, at a later pyramid. The teams competed with each other. Who could move the most blocks? Who could pull the fastest? It was a bit like sports teams today.

{img:two-work-teams-racing-pull-sledges-cheering-ramp-afternoon}
Nobody knows exactly how many people built the pyramid. Probably not as many as people once thought. Perhaps around twenty or twenty-five thousand at a time. Some at the pyramid itself. Many more in the quarries and on the boats and in the bakeries. All of them working together like one great machine.

--

Every team had a leader. Every leader reported to an overseer. Every overseer reported to Hemiunu. Scribes wrote down who was working and where and how much they ate. It was very organised. It had to be. Moving millions of tons of stone is not something you can do by accident.

{img:overseer-scribe-counting-workers-papyrus-shade-awning-giza}
At the end of each day, the teams walk home down the hill. They are tired and dusty. But they joke and sing on the way. Tomorrow, they will be back. And the pyramid will be a little higher.

The builders are not slaves, as people once believed. They are ordinary Egyptians, many of them farmers from the villages along the river. They come to Giza to work for the king, and they are proud of it. They are given food and drink, a place to sleep, and care when they are hurt. Some of them scratch the names of their teams on the stones. The Friends of Khufu, one team calls itself. The Drunkards of Khufu, says another, with a smile. They are names full of pride and good humour.
