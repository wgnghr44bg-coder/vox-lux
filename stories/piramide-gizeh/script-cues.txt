{img:giza-plateau-2560bc-pyramid-under-construction-dawn-nile-mist}
Tonight we go more than four and a half thousand years back. We go to Egypt, to the desert edge west of the river Nile. The year is around 2560 before Christ. And on a rocky hill called Giza, thousands of people are building something enormous.

[long-pause]

It is early morning. The sun rises red over the river in the east. Mist lies on the green fields along the water. Boats with square sails drift slowly on the brown river. And on the hill above the fields, a great shape rises against the pale sky. It is not finished yet. Its top is flat and rough. Ramps of mud and rubble lean against its sides. Tiny figures are climbing them. They are pulling something heavy.

[pause]

It is the Great Pyramid. When it is finished, it will be the tallest building on earth. And it will stay the tallest for nearly four thousand years. But for now, it is still a building site. Noisy and dusty and full of people.

[pause]

If you enjoy calm journeys into the past like this one, you are warmly welcome to subscribe to the channel, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us begin.

[long-pause]

{img:pyramid-workers-gangs-pulling-sledge-limestone-block-ramp-morning}
For a long time, people have wondered how it was done. How could people with copper tools and ropes and wooden sledges build something so huge? Some people even thought it could not have been people at all. But it was. Ordinary people. Farmers and stone cutters and boatmen and bakers. Thousands of them, working together, year after year.

[pause]

In the last fifty years, archaeologists have found their town. They have found their bakeries and their beds. Their graves and their tools. They have even found the diary of one of them. A man who carried stones by boat for the pyramid. His notes are the oldest written papyrus ever found.

[pause]

{img:egyptian-workers-village-giza-mudbrick-houses-ovens-smoke-morning}
Tonight we will spend a season at Giza. We will meet the king who ordered the pyramid, and the man who planned it. We will go to the quarries and the bakeries and the harbour. We will sail with the boatman and his crew on the flooded Nile. We will watch great blocks being pulled up the ramps. And we will see the pyramid slowly rise, layer by layer, toward the sky.

[pause]

And there will be worries all along the way. Will the stone arrive in time? Will the ramps hold? Will the king live to see his pyramid finished?

[long-pause]

{img:great-pyramid-white-casing-gleaming-sun-finished-desert-palms}
So let us walk up from the green fields to the hill of Giza. The sand is cool in the early morning. The air smells of river mud and bread and dust. Somewhere a man is singing a work song. Others answer him. And the great pile of stone waits for the day to begin.

[pause]

Down by the river, the fishermen are already out in their small reed boats. They throw their nets in wide circles and pull them in slowly, full of silver fish. On the bank, women are filling water jars and talking quietly. A little boy leads a donkey loaded with baskets of bread toward the hill. The donkey stops to eat a thistle, and the boy pulls at its rope and laughs. Then they go on together, up the dusty path toward the work.

[long-pause]

{img:old-kingdom-egypt-nile-valley-green-strip-desert-both-sides-aerial}
Egypt in the year 2560 before Christ is already very old. Its kings have ruled for about five hundred years.

[pause]

It is a strange shape for a country. It is long and thin, like a green ribbon. On both sides there is desert, dry and empty and hot. And down the middle runs the river Nile. Every bit of green, every field and village and town, lies along the river. Step out of the green, and you are in the desert at once.

[long-pause]

The Egyptians call their land the Black Land, because of the dark rich mud along the river. They call the desert the Red Land. The black land means life. The red land means the dead and the gods. That is why they bury their dead in the desert, at the edge of the fields. And that is why the pyramids stand there too.

[pause]

Egypt is ruled by a king. Later people will call him pharaoh. The Egyptians believe he is more than a man. He is the son of the sun god. He keeps the world in order. He makes sure the sun rises and the river floods and the crops grow. If the king is strong, Egypt is safe. If the king is weak, everything can fall apart.

[pause]

{img:egyptian-king-old-kingdom-white-crown-false-beard-palace-officials}
Most Egyptians are farmers. They live in small villages of mud brick along the river. They grow wheat and barley and flax. They keep cattle and goats and ducks. They fish in the river and hunt birds in the reeds. They pay part of their harvest to the king every year. And sometimes they work for the king too, on great projects. Like a pyramid.

[long-pause]

The Egyptians write with pictures. Later people will call it hieroglyphs. Each little picture can stand for a word or a sound. An owl, a reed, a mouth, a snake. Writers called scribes learn hundreds of them. They write on stone and on papyrus, a kind of paper made from a river plant.

[pause]

This is the time we now call the Old Kingdom. It is the age of the great pyramids. Egypt is rich and peaceful and well organised. The king's officials keep careful records of everything. Grain and cattle and workers and stone. Without that, no pyramid could ever be built.

[pause]

{img:egyptian-scribe-sitting-cross-legged-papyrus-roll-reed-pen-ink}
And in this age, one family of kings has a great dream. Each king wants a tomb that will last forever. A tomb so big and so perfect that it will carry him safely to the stars. The king in our story wants the biggest one of all.

[pause]

Most Egyptians, of course, are not kings. They are farmers who live in small villages of mud-brick houses along the river. They grow wheat and barley, onions and beans, and they keep goats and geese. In the evenings, families sit on the flat roofs of their houses to catch the cool breeze. The children play with clay dolls and little wooden animals. The grown-ups talk about the river and the harvest. And far away on the hill, the great pyramid rises a little higher every year.

[long-pause]

{img:nile-river-boats-square-sails-papyrus-reeds-fields-palms-afternoon}
Everything in Egypt depends on the Nile.

[pause]

It is one of the longest rivers in the world. It starts far to the south, deep in Africa. It flows north for thousands of kilometres through the desert. And at last it spreads out into many branches and flows into the sea. Without it, Egypt would be desert from end to end.

[long-pause]

Every year, the river floods. In early summer, far to the south, heavy rains fall in the mountains. The water rushes north. In Egypt, the Nile begins to rise. It rises and rises for weeks. It spills over its banks and covers the fields. For about three months, the valley becomes a great shallow lake. The villages stand on little hills above the water like islands.

[pause]

Then the water goes down again. It leaves behind a thick layer of black mud. This mud is very rich. Farmers plant their seeds in it. And soon the fields are green with wheat and barley.

[pause]

{img:nile-flood-season-villages-on-mounds-water-everywhere-palms-boats}
The Egyptians watch the flood very closely. If it is too low, there will not be enough water for the fields. Then there will be hunger. If it is too high, it can wash away villages. So they measure it. They mark the river's height on stone steps. And they pray to the god of the flood. He is a fat smiling god with a big belly. He carries food and water and plants. He stands for plenty.

[long-pause]

The flood is also a gift for the pyramid builders. While the fields are under water, the farmers cannot farm. So many of them come to work for the king. And the flood brings the water close to the building site. Boats can sail right across the flooded fields to the edge of the desert. That means heavy stone can be carried by water, almost all the way to the pyramid.

[pause]

{img:farmer-family-reed-boat-flooded-fields-egypt-calm-water-evening}
The Nile is also the great road of Egypt. There are no carts with wheels for heavy loads. Everything moves by boat. Grain and cattle and wood. Officials and soldiers and the king himself. The current carries boats north. And the wind, which blows from the north most of the year, pushes the sails south. So boats can go both ways with ease. In the old Egyptian writing, the sign for going north is a boat with its sail down. And the sign for going south is a boat with its sail up.

[long-pause]

The river also gives the Egyptians their sense of time. Their year has three seasons, all about the Nile. The season of the flood. The season of growing, when the water goes down and the crops come up. And the season of harvest, when the land is dry and the grain is cut. Every Egyptian knows these seasons in their bones. And the pyramid builders plan their whole year around them.

[pause]

On the banks of the Nile, tall papyrus plants grow in thick green clumps. The Egyptians use papyrus for many things. They make paper from it, and also baskets, mats, sandals and even little boats. Children hide among the papyrus and watch the birds. There are ducks and herons and white ibis, wading slowly in the shallows. In the early morning, the river is calm and silver, and mist hangs over the water. It is the most peaceful time of the day.

[pause]

Every village along the Nile has its own little harbour, where boats are tied up for the night. In the mornings, boats set off with goods for the market. There are jars of oil and baskets of fruit, sacks of grain and bundles of reeds. Traders call out to each other across the water. Children wave from the bank as the boats pass by. And the river carries everything gently along, as it has done for thousands of years.

[long-pause]

{img:king-khufu-small-ivory-statuette-seated-red-crown-museum-light}
The king who ordered the Great Pyramid is called Khufu. The Greeks, much later, will call him Cheops.

[pause]

He comes from a family of builders. His father was a king called Sneferu. Sneferu built not one pyramid, but three. His first one partly fell down. His second one had a problem. The builders had to change the angle halfway, so it is bent. People still call it the Bent Pyramid. And his third one was a true smooth pyramid. Khufu grew up watching these huge building sites. He saw what went wrong and what went right.

[long-pause]

When his father passed away, Khufu became king. He was still fairly young. And almost at once, he began planning his own pyramid. It had to be bigger and better than his father's. That was a big promise to make.

[pause]

We know very little about Khufu as a person. Strangely, for the man who built the biggest pyramid, only one small statue of him has been found. It is made of ivory. It is only about seven centimetres tall, smaller than your hand. It shows him sitting on his throne, wearing a red crown. His face is calm and a little stern.

[pause]

{img:sneferu-bent-pyramid-dahshur-desert-changed-angle-sunset}
Later stories, written long after he passed away, say he was a harsh king. They say he made everyone work for his pyramid and closed the temples. But those stories were written two thousand years later, by people who never knew him. In his own time, people seem to have honoured him. His name was spoken in prayers for centuries after.

[long-pause]

Khufu rules for about twenty-five years. And building his pyramid takes about twenty years. That means a race against time. A king's tomb must be ready when he passes away. If he dies before it is finished, the work will have to be rushed. Or left half done. So from the very first day, everyone knows there is no time to waste.

[pause]

{img:royal-court-old-kingdom-khufu-viewing-plans-officials-bowing}
Each year, the royal officials report to the king on the work. How many blocks have been laid. How high the pyramid has risen. How many workers there are. How much bread and beer they need. Khufu listens carefully. Year by year, the pyramid grows. And year by year, the king grows older.

[long-pause]

Some evenings, the stories say, the king sails across the river to see his pyramid for himself. He stands at the foot of the ramps and looks up. The workers bow low. Hemiunu walks beside him and explains everything. Khufu says little. He looks at the great rising shape for a long time. Then he nods and goes back to his boat. And the next morning, the work goes on a little faster.

[pause]

In the palace at Memphis, the king's day is full of meetings and ceremonies. But even a king has quiet moments. In the evening, he may sit in his garden by a pool of blue water lilies. Servants wave large fans of ostrich feathers to keep him cool. A musician plays softly on a harp. And the king looks out over the river toward Giza, where his great tomb is slowly rising against the sky.

[long-pause]

{img:hemiunu-architect-seated-statue-stout-calm-face-old-kingdom}
To build the pyramid, Khufu needs a master builder. He chooses a member of his own family. His name is Hemiunu.

[pause]

Hemiunu is the king's nephew. He has the title of overseer of all the king's works. That means he is in charge of everything. The plans and the workers. The stone and the boats. The bread and the beer. It is a huge task. One mistake, and the whole project could fail.

[long-pause]

We know what Hemiunu looked like. His statue was found in his own tomb near the pyramid. He is shown sitting down. He is quite a big man, with a round belly and soft arms. In ancient Egypt, that showed you were rich and well fed. His face is calm and serious. He looks like a man who thinks carefully before he speaks.

[pause]

Hemiunu and his team plan everything with great care. The pyramid will stand on a square base. Each side will be about two hundred and thirty metres long. That is longer than two football fields. And the pyramid will be about a hundred and forty-six metres high. That is as high as a building of forty floors.

[pause]

{img:egyptian-surveyors-stars-measuring-north-ropes-plumb-lines-night}
The four sides must face exactly north, south, east and west. How did they find true north with no compass? They probably used the stars. At night they watched certain stars circling the sky around the north. They marked where those stars rose and set. And halfway between, they found north. They got it almost perfectly right. The error is less than a tenth of a degree.

[long-pause]

The base must also be perfectly flat. Across more than five hectares of rock. If one corner were even a little higher than the others, the pyramid would lean. The builders may have cut little channels in the rock and filled them with water. Water lies perfectly flat. They marked the water line. Then they cut the rock down to that line. In the end, the base is level to within about two centimetres, from one corner to the other.

[pause]

{img:workers-cutting-rock-base-level-channels-water-giza-plateau-sun}
Inside the pyramid, Hemiunu plans corridors and rooms. A chamber for the king's coffin. A great sloping hall. Narrow shafts that point toward the stars. Every block must fit. Every angle must be right. And everything must be ready in time.

[pause]

Hemiunu works late into the night in his rooms at Memphis. His table is covered with plans drawn on papyrus and on flat pieces of limestone. He uses a reed pen and black ink, and sometimes red ink for corrections. Lamps of oil burn beside him, and moths flutter around them. His servants bring him bread and figs and a cup of cool water. Sometimes he falls asleep at his table, with his head on his arms. And in the morning, he wakes up and starts again.

[pause]

Hemiunu has a family of his own, though he is rarely at home. His wife looks after the house in Memphis and their children. When he does come home, the children run to meet him at the door. They want to know how high the pyramid is now. He kneels down and draws it for them in the dust with his finger. Then he lifts the smallest one onto his shoulders. And for one evening, he forgets about stones and angles and simply plays.

[long-pause]

{img:giza-plateau-rocky-limestone-hill-desert-edge-nile-fields-below}
Why did Khufu choose Giza for his pyramid?

[pause]

Giza is a rocky hill on the west bank of the Nile. It is just at the edge of the desert, above the green fields. To the west is the setting sun, where the Egyptians believe the land of the dead lies. That makes it a good place for a tomb.

[long-pause]

But there were practical reasons too. The hill is made of solid limestone. That is strong enough to carry a mountain of stone without sinking. And the limestone can be cut right there and used for building. A pyramid of millions of tons needs a lot of stone. The closer the stone is, the better.

[pause]

And Giza is close to the river. During the flood, the water comes almost to the foot of the hill. Boats can come right up to the building site. That makes it possible to bring special stone from far away.

[pause]

{img:egyptian-officials-choosing-site-hill-giza-looking-over-valley}
Giza is also not far from the great city of Memphis. Memphis is the capital of Egypt. It is just across the river and a little to the south. The king's palace is there. So are the offices of his officials and the great temples. From Memphis, the king can watch the work on his pyramid. On a clear day, he can see it from his palace roof.

[long-pause]

When the work begins, the hill is cleared. Workers sweep away the sand and loose stones. They cut the top of the rock flat. They mark out the great square of the base with ropes and stakes. They build roads for hauling stone. They dig a harbour at the foot of the hill. And they build a town for the workers to live in.

[pause]

{img:giza-building-site-early-stage-base-square-marked-ropes-workers}
It takes several years just to prepare the site. Then the first blocks are laid. The first layer is the most important. If it is not perfect, every layer above will be wrong. So Hemiunu checks it again and again. Corner by corner. Block by block. Only when he is satisfied does the work go on.

[pause]

And slowly, very slowly, the first layer of the Great Pyramid begins to spread across the rock.

[long-pause]

One morning, after a night of strong wind, the workers arrive to find the marks half buried in sand. The stakes have blown over. The ropes are tangled. The overseers look worried. Has a whole season of measuring been lost? But Hemiunu is calm. The builders cut their marks into the rock itself, he says, not only in the sand. And he is right. When the sand is swept away, the cut lines are still there, sharp and clear. The work goes on. And from that day, the builders always cut their marks into the stone.

[pause]

The measurers work very early in the morning, before the sun becomes too hot. They carry long ropes with knots tied at equal distances. They stretch the ropes tight between wooden stakes and check them again and again. At night, the priests look at the stars to find true north. They watch a pair of stars turning slowly around the pole of the sky. When the stars line up just right, they mark the direction on the ground. It is slow and patient work, but it must be perfect.

[long-pause]

{img:limestone-quarry-giza-workers-cutting-blocks-copper-chisels-dust}
Most of the stone for the pyramid comes from right next door.

[pause]

Just south of the pyramid, the builders cut a great quarry into the hill. It is a huge hole in the rock, many metres deep. You can still see it today. From this quarry come most of the blocks that make up the inside of the pyramid. More than two million of them.

[long-pause]

How do they cut the stone? They have no iron. Their tools are made of copper, a soft reddish metal, and of stone. They have copper chisels and saws. They have wooden mallets and stone hammers. And they have wooden wedges.

[pause]

First they mark out a block on the rock with red paint. Then they cut deep narrow channels around it with copper chisels. It is slow work. The copper wears down quickly. The tool makers must sharpen the chisels again and again. Then the workers knock wooden wedges under the block. Sometimes they pour water on the wedges. The wood swells. And with a crack, the block comes free from the rock.

[pause]

{img:workers-levering-block-free-wooden-wedges-quarry-floor-shade}
An average block weighs about two and a half tons. That is about as heavy as two cars. Some are much bigger. The workers lever them out with long wooden poles. They tie ropes around them. And they slide them onto wooden sledges, ready to be pulled to the pyramid.

[long-pause]

It is hot work and dusty work. The quarry is a deep hole that traps the sun. The air is full of white dust. It gets in the workers' eyes and noses and mouths. They tie cloths over their faces. Water carriers walk among them with big clay jars. And every few hours, the overseers call a rest in the shade.

[pause]

{img:water-carrier-clay-jars-shoulder-yoke-quarry-workers-drinking}
The quarry workers are skilled men. They know how to read the rock. They can tell where it will split cleanly and where it will crack badly. A bad crack can ruin a block. And sometimes the rock is full of hidden cracks and shells and soft patches. Then the workers must find a better place to cut. Every day, the block counter writes down how many blocks are ready. And every day, Hemiunu wants more.

[long-pause]

The quarry grows bigger every year. It eats into the hill, deeper and wider. The stone that comes out of it goes straight into the pyramid next door. So in a way, the hill is slowly moving. Block by block, it is being lifted up and stacked into a new shape. When the work is done, there will be a great hole on one side, and a great mountain on the other. Some people today say the pyramid is the hill, turned inside out and piled up toward the sky.

[pause]

At midday, the quarry workers rest in the shade of the rock walls. They eat bread and dried fish and drink water from clay jars. Some of them lie down on the cool stone and close their eyes. Others play a board game, scratched into the rock with a chisel. The game uses little stones as counters, and the players argue cheerfully about the rules. Then the overseer calls, and they pick up their tools again.

[long-pause]

{img:copper-smiths-workshop-furnace-blowpipes-molten-metal-egypt}
None of this would be possible without copper.

[pause]

Copper is the metal of the age. It is soft enough to shape and hard enough to cut limestone, if you keep sharpening it. The pyramid builders need huge amounts of it. Chisels and saws and drills and axes. Every tool wears down in days. Some experts think the pyramid used hundreds of tons of copper altogether.

[long-pause]

The copper comes from far away. Most of it comes from the Sinai, a rocky land of mountains east of Egypt, across the sea. The king sends expeditions there. Miners dig the green and blue rock that holds the copper. They heat it in hot fires until the metal melts out. Then it is shaped into bars and sent back to Egypt by boat.

[pause]

Near the pyramid, there are workshops where smiths melt the copper again. They shape it into tools. To get the fire hot enough, they blow into it through long tubes. Several men kneel around the fire and blow at the same time. Their cheeks puff out. The fire roars and glows. Little pictures of this have been found on tomb walls. The smiths look out of breath.

[pause]

{img:smiths-kneeling-blowing-fire-tubes-tomb-painting-style-copper}
Then there are the sharpeners. All day long they sit with piles of blunt chisels. They grind the edges on stones. They hammer them back into shape. Then the chisels go back to the quarry. The next day they come back blunt again. It never stops.

[long-pause]

The workers also use stone tools. Hard round stones of a black rock called dolerite are used to pound and smooth granite. Granite is much harder than limestone. Copper chisels are almost useless against it. So the workers pound it again and again with their stone balls. And they use sand. Sand is full of tiny hard grains. With a copper saw and wet sand, they can slowly cut even granite. The sand does the cutting. The copper just drags it back and forth.

[pause]

{img:workers-sawing-granite-copper-saw-wet-sand-slow-patient-shade}
It is slow, slow work. Cutting through a big granite block can take days. But the Egyptians are patient. They have time and skill and many hands. And slowly, cut by cut, the stones take shape.

[long-pause]

One day the copper runs low. A ship from the Sinai is late. Storms at sea, people say. The sharpeners have fewer and fewer chisels to work with. In the quarry, the work slows down. The overseers send messages to Memphis. Hemiunu walks through the workshops with a worried face. Then, after many anxious days, a messenger runs up from the harbour. The ships have arrived. Bars of copper, gleaming and red. The smiths cheer. The fires are lit. And by the next morning, new chisels are ringing in the quarry again.

[pause]

In the copper workshops, the air is hot and full of smoke. Men sit around small fires, blowing through long reeds to make the flames hotter. When the copper melts, it glows like the setting sun. They pour it carefully into moulds of clay. When it cools, they hammer it into sharp new chisels. The sound of hammering never stops, from dawn until dusk. And a boy runs back and forth, carrying finished chisels up to the quarry in a basket.

[long-pause]

{img:pyramid-work-gang-twenty-men-ropes-overseer-morning-ramp-giza}
Who built the pyramid?

[pause]

For a long time, people believed it was built by slaves. Long lines of chained men, whipped by cruel overseers. That is what some old stories and films showed. But the archaeologists have found something different.

[long-pause]

The builders were Egyptians. Many were farmers who came to work for the king for a few months. Especially during the flood, when their fields were under water. Others were skilled workers who worked on the pyramid all year. Stone cutters and builders and carpenters. Painters and smiths and boatmen. They were paid in bread and beer and cloth. They lived in a town near the pyramid. And many were proud to work on the king's great tomb.

[pause]

Of course, the work was hard. And people probably had little choice when the king called them. It was a kind of tax, paid in work. But they were not slaves. They had families and homes. They were fed and looked after. And when they died, many were buried in proper tombs right next to the king's pyramid. Slaves would never have been given that honour.

[pause]

{img:workers-graffiti-red-paint-gang-name-stone-block-friends-of-khufu}
The workers were organised in groups. A big group had perhaps a few hundred men. Each big group was split into smaller teams of about twenty. And each team had a name.

[long-pause]

We know some of these names. The workers painted them on the stones in red paint. Inside the pyramid, on blocks that nobody was supposed to see. Names like Friends of Khufu. Or the Drunkards of Menkaure, at a later pyramid. The teams competed with each other. Who could move the most blocks? Who could pull the fastest? It was a bit like sports teams today.

[pause]

{img:two-work-teams-racing-pull-sledges-cheering-ramp-afternoon}
Nobody knows exactly how many people built the pyramid. Probably not as many as people once thought. Perhaps around twenty or twenty-five thousand at a time. Some at the pyramid itself. Many more in the quarries and on the boats and in the bakeries. All of them working together like one great machine.

[long-pause]

Every team had a leader. Every leader reported to an overseer. Every overseer reported to Hemiunu. Scribes wrote down who was working and where and how much they ate. It was very organised. It had to be. Moving millions of tons of stone is not something you can do by accident.

[pause]

{img:overseer-scribe-counting-workers-papyrus-shade-awning-giza}
At the end of each day, the teams walk home down the hill. They are tired and dusty. But they joke and sing on the way. Tomorrow, they will be back. And the pyramid will be a little higher.

[pause]

The builders are not slaves, as people once believed. They are ordinary Egyptians, many of them farmers from the villages along the river. They come to Giza to work for the king, and they are proud of it. They are given food and drink, a place to sleep, and care when they are hurt. Some of them scratch the names of their teams on the stones. The Friends of Khufu, one team calls itself. The Drunkards of Khufu, says another, with a smile. They are names full of pride and good humour.

[long-pause]

{img:workers-town-giza-heit-el-ghurab-mudbrick-galleries-streets}
At the foot of the hill, south of the pyramid, there is a whole town for the workers.

[pause]

For a long time, nobody knew it was there. It lay buried under sand and the houses of a modern village. Then, about forty years ago, archaeologists began to dig there. And they found the lost town of the pyramid builders.

[long-pause]

It is a busy place. There are long buildings of mud brick for the workers to sleep in. They are like long halls with rows of low sleeping platforms. Perhaps fifty men sleep in each one. At one end there is a porch and a hearth. In the evening the men sit there and eat and talk.

[pause]

There are bigger houses for the overseers and the scribes. They have several rooms and their own courtyards. There are store rooms for grain. And there is a great wall of stone, ten metres high, at the north edge of the town. People call it the Wall of the Crow. It may have kept out floods. Or it may have kept the town separate from the holy ground of the pyramid.

[pause]

{img:workers-sleeping-gallery-platforms-hearth-evening-men-talking}
The archaeologists found the things of daily life. Pots and bowls. Fish bones and cattle bones. Grinding stones. Little clay seals with names stamped on them. Even the ash from old fires. Every find tells a small part of the story.

[long-pause]

From the bones, they learned something surprising. The workers ate a lot of meat. Beef and sheep and goat. Much more than ordinary farmers at home. Thousands of cattle must have been brought here every year from farms all over Egypt. Someone was taking good care of these workers. They needed strength for their hard work.

[pause]

{img:cattle-herded-toward-workers-town-giza-herdsmen-dust-evening}
The town is noisy and smoky and crowded. In the evening, smoke rises from hundreds of fires. You can smell bread baking and meat roasting. You hear men laughing and arguing. You hear someone playing a simple pipe. And from up on the hill, the great half-built pyramid watches over them all in the fading light.

[long-pause]

Not everyone in the town is a builder. There are cooks and brewers and water carriers. There are potters making jars and bowls. There are men who look after the cattle. There are scribes and guards. And there are women too. Bakers and weavers and the wives of some of the overseers. Some families live here all year. Their children grow up in the shadow of the pyramid. For them, it is not a wonder. It is just the big thing on the hill that their fathers work on every day.

[pause]

In the workers' town, there are narrow streets between long houses of mud brick. Smoke rises from the bakeries and the kitchens. Dogs sleep in the shade of the walls. In the evenings, men sit outside on low benches and talk about the day. Someone plays a reed flute, and someone else claps along. Children of the overseers chase each other between the houses, shrieking with laughter. And the smell of fresh bread drifts over everything.

[pause]

In one corner of the town, there is a small shrine to the local gods. Workers stop there in the morning to leave a little gift. It might be a piece of bread, a few flowers, or a small clay figure. They ask the gods to keep them safe on the ramps and in the quarry. Then they hurry up the hill to join their teams. An old priest sweeps the shrine every evening and lights a small lamp there.

[long-pause]

{img:ancient-egyptian-bakery-bell-shaped-pots-bread-ovens-workers-town}
To build a pyramid, you need stone and copper and rope. But above all, you need bread and beer.

[pause]

Every worker gets a ration of food every day. The main part is bread. And beer. Lots of both. A worker might get about ten loaves of bread and a few litres of beer a day. That sounds like a lot. But it is hard work. And Egyptian beer is thick and mild. It is more like a kind of food than a strong drink. Everyone drinks it, even children.

[long-pause]

In the workers' town, the archaeologists found bakeries. They are rooms with low walls and open fires. In them, bakers made bread in a special way. They used heavy clay pots shaped like a bell. First they heated the empty pots in a fire until they were very hot. Then they poured dough into them. They put another hot pot on top like a lid. And they buried them in hot ashes. The bread baked inside, slowly.

[pause]

The bread comes out as big round loaves with a hard crust. Sometimes it has bits of sand in it from the grinding stones. That is very bad for the teeth. Many Egyptian skeletons have worn-down teeth. All that sandy bread, chewed every day for a whole life.

[pause]

{img:women-grinding-grain-saddle-quern-stones-bakery-flour-dust}
The grain is ground into flour by hand. Women kneel at a long flat stone. They push a smaller stone back and forth over it, grinding the wheat into flour. It is hard tiring work. And it never stops. So many workers need so much bread. The grinding goes on from dawn until night.

[long-pause]

Beer is made from bread. The brewers mix crumbled bread with water in big jars. They add a little sweet date juice. Then they leave it for a few days. It bubbles and ferments. Then they strain it through a basket. And it is ready. The archaeologists found the remains of big beer jars in the town too.

[pause]

There is also fish from the Nile, dried in the sun. Onions and garlic and lentils. Dates and figs. And the meat from all those cattle. On feast days there is more. Roast duck and honey cakes and wine for the overseers.

[pause]

{img:workers-eating-bread-onions-beer-jars-midday-shade-giza-ramp}
Food is so important that it is written down carefully. Scribes count every loaf and every jar. If the food runs short, the work slows down. And if the work slows down, the pyramid will not be finished in time. So the bakers and brewers are some of the most important people at Giza. Even if their names are never written on the stones.

[pause]

The bakers start work long before dawn. They grind the grain on flat stones, pushing a smaller stone back and forth with both hands. Then they mix the flour with water and a little old dough. They pour the dough into heavy clay pots, shaped like bells. The pots are stacked in hot ashes until the bread is baked. When they break open the pots, the warm smell of bread fills the whole bakery. It is the smell of every morning at Giza.

[long-pause]

{img:egyptian-doctor-bandaging-workers-arm-linen-splint-workers-town}
Building a pyramid is dangerous work. Heavy stones. Steep ramps. Sharp tools. Accidents happen. What happens to a worker who gets hurt?

[pause]

The archaeologists found the answer in the workers' graves. Next to the town, on the hill, there is a cemetery. Hundreds of workers and their families are buried there. And their bones tell a story.

[long-pause]

Many of the skeletons show signs of very hard work. Their backs are bent. Their knees and shoulders are worn. Some have arthritis, a painful stiffness of the joints. These were people who lifted and pulled heavy loads every day for years.

[pause]

But many of them also show signs of care. Broken arms and legs that healed straight. That means someone set the bone and tied it with a splint, a straight piece of wood. And the worker was allowed to rest until it healed. One man had lost part of his leg. And yet he lived for years afterwards. Someone looked after him.

[pause]

{img:egyptian-medical-papyrus-scribe-reading-remedies-honey-herbs}
The Egyptians had doctors. They knew a lot about the body for their time. Old Egyptian books of medicine have survived. They describe how to treat wounds and broken bones and burns. Honey was put on wounds. Honey keeps germs away, though they did not know why. Linen bandages were wrapped around injuries. Some doctors could even treat teeth.

[long-pause]

Some scientists think the doctors at Giza were paid by the king, like the other workers. Being hurt while building the king's tomb was not something to be ashamed of. And a good worker was worth looking after. So the injured were cared for. And when they died, they were buried with honour, right beside the great pyramid they had helped to build.

[pause]

{img:workers-cemetery-small-tombs-giza-hillside-pyramid-behind-dusk}
Their tombs are small. Some are little domes of mud brick. Some are small stone buildings shaped like tiny pyramids. Inside, the families left jars of bread and beer for the afterlife. And on some tombs, they wrote the names and titles of the dead. Overseer of the side of the pyramid. Director of the workers. Baker. Proud titles. Even in death, these people wanted the world to know. I helped build the pyramid.

[long-pause]

One worker's grave tells a small sad and hopeful story. He was young when he was hurt. His leg was crushed by something heavy, perhaps a block that slipped. The doctors could not save the lower part. But he lived. He walked with a stick for many years after. He may have stayed on at Giza, doing lighter work. Counting jars. Watching the gate. Telling the young workers to be careful. And when he died, his friends buried him near the pyramid, with bread and beer for his journey.

[pause]

The doctors of Egypt know a great deal about healing. They know how to set a broken arm with wooden splints and linen bandages. They clean wounds with honey, which helps them heal. They make medicines from herbs and plants, mixed with milk or beer. They also say prayers and give little charms to help. A worker who has hurt his hand sits patiently while the doctor wraps it carefully. Rest for a few days, the doctor tells him, and then you can work again.

[long-pause]

{img:wadi-al-jarf-papyri-found-cave-red-sea-coast-archaeologists}
In 2013, archaeologists made an amazing discovery. On the coast of the Red Sea, far east of the Nile, they found an old harbour. It had been used in the time of Khufu. And in caves near the harbour, they found papyrus rolls.

[pause]

They were the oldest written papyrus rolls ever found. And they were from the time of the Great Pyramid. One of them is the diary of a man called Merer.

[long-pause]

Merer was an inspector. He led a team of about forty men. Their job was to carry stone by boat for the pyramid. Every day, Merer wrote down what his team did. Where they went. How many stones they carried. And where they slept.

[pause]

His diary is written in neat black and red ink. In columns, day by day. It is not exciting to read. It is mostly short notes. Spent the day at the quarry. Loaded stone. Sailed downstream. Spent the night here. But it is incredibly precious. Because it is a real eyewitness. A man who actually worked on the Great Pyramid, telling us about his days.

[pause]

{img:merer-inspector-writing-diary-papyrus-boat-deck-evening-nile}
Merer's team carried white limestone from a quarry called Tura. Tura is on the east bank of the Nile, across from Giza. The stone from Tura is very fine and very white. It is used for the smooth outer covering of the pyramid. The part everyone will see.

[long-pause]

Merer writes that his team spent a few days at Tura. They loaded the white blocks onto their boats. Then they sailed across the river and down a canal to Giza. They unloaded the stone at the harbour. And they sailed back for more. Each round trip took about four days. They made two or three trips every ten days.

[pause]

{img:boats-loaded-white-limestone-sailing-across-flooded-nile-tura}
He even mentions Ankhhaf, the half-brother of the king. Ankhhaf was in charge of the harbour at Giza. He was Merer's boss. So in Merer's diary, we meet real people. With real names. Doing real work. On the most famous building in the world.

[long-pause]

Why was the diary found so far away, on the Red Sea? Probably because Merer and his team were sent there later, to work at the harbour. Maybe they took their old papers with them. When the work was done, the papers were left in a cave. And there they stayed for four and a half thousand years. Until someone found them.

[pause]

Merer's diary tells us about ordinary days, not about great events. One day, he writes, the team loaded stone at Tura. The next day, they sailed across the river. Then they spent the night at a place called the Lake of Khufu. Reading it today, we can almost see them. A team of men on a wooden boat, the white stones stacked in the middle. The sail filled with wind, and the green banks sliding past. And Merer sitting in the shade with his papyrus, writing down the day.

[pause]

In the evenings, Merer and his men cook their supper on the river bank. They light a small fire of dry reeds and bake flat bread on the hot stones. Someone catches a fish, and they grill it over the flames. They talk about their families and about the next day's work. Then they lie down on the deck of their boat to sleep. The water laps softly against the wooden hull. And the stars come out over the wide, dark river.

[long-pause]

{img:tura-quarry-white-limestone-cliffs-east-bank-nile-workers-tunnels}
On the east bank of the Nile, across from Giza, there are cliffs of fine white limestone. This is Tura.

[pause]

The stone at Tura is special. It is very fine and very even. When it is cut and polished, it is almost pure white. It shines in the sun. That is exactly what Khufu wants for the outside of his pyramid. A smooth white skin that glows like light.

[long-pause]

The best stone at Tura lies deep inside the cliffs. So the workers dig tunnels into the rock. They follow the good layer of stone. Some tunnels go far into the hill. They cut the blocks out by lamplight. It is cool and dim in the tunnels, but full of dust. The workers cough and wipe their eyes.

[pause]

The blocks are cut carefully, almost to their final shape. Then they are dragged out of the tunnels on sledges. Down to the river bank. And loaded onto the boats of men like Merer.

[pause]

{img:workers-dragging-white-block-out-of-tunnel-tura-lamplight-sledge}
It is a long way, and a risky one. A big white block can weigh several tons. Loading it onto a boat is tricky. If it slips, it could sink the boat. Or crush someone's foot. So the workers move slowly and carefully. They use ramps of earth and wooden rollers. They tie the stone with thick ropes made of palm fibre.

[long-pause]

Then the boats push off. They cross the wide river. In the flood season, the water spreads far over the fields. The boats can sail straight across toward the desert edge. The boatmen pole and paddle and set their sails. Merer stands at the front and watches the water. He knows where the shallow places are. And where the current is strong.

[pause]

{img:boat-crossing-wide-flood-water-toward-giza-pyramid-on-horizon}
As they come closer to Giza, they can see the pyramid on its hill. Each time it is a little higher than the last time. The boatmen look up at it. Some of them have worked on it for years. And they feel proud. Their white stones will cover that great shape. And they will shine for thousands of years.

[long-pause]

The Tura quarry men have a saying. The pyramid is only as beautiful as its skin. Inside, nobody will ever see the rough blocks. But the white casing will be seen by everyone, for as long as the pyramid stands. So they choose their stone with great care. Any block with a crack or a dark stain is put aside. Only the purest white goes on the boats. And sometimes the overseer rejects a block that took days to cut. The workers groan. But they know he is right.

[pause]

The Tura quarry is cool and dim inside, because the men cut deep tunnels into the hill. They work by the light of small oil lamps set in little holes in the walls. The white stone glows softly in the lamplight. Their voices echo strangely in the tunnels. When they come out at the end of the day, the bright sunlight makes them blink. And they sit by the river to wash the white dust from their arms and faces.

[long-pause]

{img:nile-flood-rising-fields-under-water-villages-islands-summer}
In the middle of summer, the Nile begins to rise. It is the start of the flood. And at Giza, the busiest season of the year begins.

[pause]

The water creeps up the river banks. It spills into the canals. It covers the fields. Day by day, the green land disappears under brown water. Villages stand on their mounds like little islands. Palm trees stick up out of the water. Birds fly over it in great flocks.

[long-pause]

For the farmers, this is a time of waiting. They cannot plough or plant. Their fields are under water. So many of them are called to work for the king. They leave their villages and come to Giza. They join a work team. They get a place to sleep in the long halls of the workers' town. And they start work on the pyramid.

[pause]

For some young men, it is their first time away from home. They have never seen anything like Giza. The huge pyramid. The crowds of workers. The noise and the dust. At first they are scared. Will they be strong enough? Will they make friends in their team? But soon they learn the work songs. They find a place in their team. And they start to feel part of something great.

[pause]

{img:young-farmer-arriving-giza-first-time-staring-at-pyramid-bundle}
The flood brings the water close to the pyramid. And that matters a lot. Because now boats can sail right up to the harbour at the foot of the hill. They can bring white stone from Tura and granite from far to the south. And food and wood and copper. Everything comes by water.

[long-pause]

So the flood season is a race. For about three months, the boats come and go day and night. The harbour is crowded. Workers unload stone from dawn to dusk. When the flood goes down, the boats will not be able to come so close. The heavy stones must arrive before the water drops.

[pause]

{img:busy-harbour-giza-many-boats-unloading-stone-workers-flood-season}
Every year, Hemiunu watches the flood with worry. A good flood means plenty of water for the boats. And plenty of food next year. A low flood means trouble. Fewer boats. Less food. Slower work. So when the river rises high and steady, everyone at Giza is glad. And the work goes faster than ever.

[long-pause]

One year, the flood comes late. The river stays low for weeks after it should have risen. At Giza, the boats wait at Tura with their white stones. They cannot get across. The harbour is half dry. People in the villages begin to pray. Priests make offerings at the river. Every morning, the measurers look at the marks on the stone steps. Has it risen? Not yet. Then, one morning, there is a new mark. And the next day another. The river is coming. Everyone breathes again. And within a week, the boats are sailing across once more.

[pause]

When the flood finally comes, everyone is happy and relieved. The water spreads over the fields like a great brown lake. The villages stand on little hills, like islands in the water. People travel from house to house in small boats. The farmers cannot work their fields until the water goes down again. So many of them go to Giza to work on the pyramid. And the children swim and splash in the warm brown water, laughing all day long.

[long-pause]

{img:giza-harbour-basin-canal-boats-moored-unloading-ramp-pyramid-above}
At the foot of the Giza hill, there is a harbour.

[pause]

The builders dug it out of the land at the edge of the desert. They also dug a canal to connect it with the river. During the flood, the water fills the harbour. Boats can sail in from the Nile and tie up there. Close to the building site.

[long-pause]

The harbour is a busy place. Boats arrive loaded with stone and grain and cattle. Workers unload them. They drag the stones up onto the bank on sledges. Scribes count everything. Officials shout orders. Boatmen rest in the shade and eat and wait to go back.

[pause]

Near the harbour there are store rooms and workshops. There are pens for the cattle. There are offices for the scribes. And there is an office for the man in charge of the harbour. In Khufu's time, that is Ankhhaf, the king's half-brother. Merer mentions him in his diary.

[pause]

{img:ankhhaf-official-bust-old-kingdom-serious-face-harbour-office}
A famous statue of Ankhhaf has been found. It is a bust, showing his head and shoulders. It is made of limestone covered with painted plaster. His face is serious and tired. He has bags under his eyes and lines on his forehead. He looks like a man with a lot of responsibility. Like a man who has not slept well in a long time.

[long-pause]

From the harbour, a long stone road leads up the hill to the pyramid. Workers drag the stones up this road on sledges. It is a slow climb. The road is steep in places. Teams of men pull together on the ropes. One man pours water in front of the sledge. Another calls out the rhythm. Pull. Pull. Pull.

[pause]

{img:stone-causeway-up-hill-sledges-teams-pulling-harbour-behind}
At the end of the flood season, the water drops. The harbour starts to dry out. Mud appears at the edges. The last boats hurry to leave before they get stuck. And then the harbour is quiet until next year. The workers who came for the flood go home to their villages. They will plant their fields in the fresh black mud. And next summer, when the river rises again, many of them will come back.

[long-pause]

Some nights, when the moon is full, the work at the harbour goes on after dark. Torches burn along the quay. The boats keep coming. Nobody wants to waste a single day of high water. The workers take turns to sleep for a few hours on the warm stones. Then they get up and unload the next boat. It is tiring. But the flood will not wait for anyone.

[pause]

The harbour at Giza is a busy and noisy place. Boats bump against the stone quay, and the sailors shout to each other. Men walk carefully down wooden planks, carrying smaller loads on their shoulders. Scribes sit in the shade, writing down every block and every sack of grain. Boys sell bread and dates to the hungry sailors. And above the harbour, the great pyramid rises on its hill, watching over it all.

[long-pause]

{img:wooden-sledge-heavy-block-ropes-teams-pulling-sand-water-poured}
How do you move a stone as heavy as two cars, with no wheels and no machines?

[pause]

You put it on a sledge. A sledge is a big flat wooden frame with two long runners underneath, a bit like a sled for snow. The block is tied onto the sledge with ropes. Then a team of men pulls the sledge by long ropes.

[long-pause]

But pulling a heavy sledge over sand is very hard. The runners dig into the sand. It is like trying to drag something heavy through a sandbox. The sand piles up in front of the runners. And the sledge gets stuck.

[pause]

The Egyptians found a clever trick. They poured water on the sand in front of the sledge. Not too much and not too little. Just enough to make the sand damp. Damp sand is firm. It does not pile up. And the sledge slides over it much more easily.

[pause]

{img:egyptian-tomb-painting-style-statue-on-sledge-man-pouring-water}
How do we know? There is an old Egyptian painting in a tomb. It is from a bit later than Khufu's time. It shows a huge statue on a sledge. Many men are pulling it with ropes. And at the front, a man stands on the sledge pouring water onto the sand from a jar. For a long time, people thought this was part of a ceremony. Then, a few years ago, scientists tested it. They found that wet sand really does make pulling much easier. About half as hard. The Egyptians knew exactly what they were doing.

[long-pause]

The builders also made special roads for the sledges. They laid wooden beams across the path, or packed hard clay. That way the sledges slid smoothly. Some of these roads have been found. One has wooden beams still in place after four thousand years.

[pause]

{img:team-pulling-in-rhythm-overseer-clapping-song-dust-afternoon}
Pulling a sledge is teamwork. Twenty or thirty men pull together. If they pull at different times, the sledge jerks and stops. So they sing. One man calls out a line. The others answer and pull at the same moment. Pull and rest. Pull and rest. The songs are simple and loud. Some are funny. Some are about their homes or their families or the king.

[long-pause]

At the end of a long pull, the team stops. The block is in place at the foot of the ramp. The men drop the ropes and sit down in the sand, panting. Someone brings water. Someone tells a joke. Everyone laughs. And then the overseer calls. Another block is waiting.

[pause]

Some of the workers have a special job. They walk in front of the sledge, pouring water onto the sand. The wet sand is firmer, and the sledge slides much more easily. An old painting from Egypt shows exactly this, a man pouring water in front of a heavy statue on a sledge. Scientists have tested it, and it really works. Pulling on wet sand takes half the effort of pulling on dry sand. The Egyptians knew this trick thousands of years ago.

[pause]

The pulling teams often sing as they work. A leader calls out a line, and the whole team answers together. The songs help them to pull at the same moment. Some songs are about the king, and some are about the river. Some are funny songs about the overseers, sung quietly so they will not hear. The rhythm of the singing carries across the whole hill. And the great blocks slide forward, a little with every line of the song.

[long-pause]

{img:pyramid-ramp-mudbrick-rubble-leaning-side-workers-pulling-block}
The biggest question of all. How did they get the stones up?

[pause]

The bottom of the pyramid is easy. You just slide the blocks into place. But the pyramid rises higher and higher. Fifty metres. A hundred metres. How do you get a block weighing two tons all the way up there?

[long-pause]

The answer is ramps. A ramp is a slope you can drag a sledge up. The Egyptians built ramps of mud brick and rubble and limestone chips. They leaned them against the pyramid. And they pulled the stones up.

[pause]

But what kind of ramps exactly? Nobody knows for certain. People have argued about it for a long time. And there are several ideas.

[pause]

{img:straight-long-ramp-toward-pyramid-side-many-teams-climbing}
One idea is one long straight ramp, running up one side. But to reach the top, that ramp would have to be enormous. Almost as big as the pyramid itself. And every time the pyramid grows, the ramp must grow longer too.

[long-pause]

Another idea is a ramp that winds around the outside of the pyramid. Like a path spiraling up a mountain. It would be shorter. But it would cover the sides, so the builders could not check if the corners were straight.

[pause]

{img:spiral-ramp-winding-around-pyramid-teams-on-path-evening-light}
A newer idea says there may have been ramps inside the pyramid. A tunnel-like ramp that spirals up just inside the outer wall. Some scientists think they have found signs of this. But it is not proven.

[long-pause]

And in 2018, archaeologists found something exciting. In an old quarry far away, from the time of Khufu, they found a steep ramp. On each side of it there were stairs. And there were post holes, where big wooden posts once stood. The workers may have used the posts like a pulley. They wrapped the ropes around the posts. Then they could pull the sledge up a much steeper slope, with less effort. Maybe the pyramid builders used the same trick.

[pause]

{img:steep-quarry-ramp-stairs-both-sides-wooden-posts-ropes-sledge}
Perhaps the truth is a mix of all these ideas. Big ramps at the bottom. Smaller steeper ramps higher up. And clever levers and posts at the very top. The builders were practical people. They used whatever worked.

[long-pause]

One thing is sure. Every block was pulled up by people. Team after team. Day after day. Rope in hand, feet in the dust, voices singing. And every evening, when the sun went down behind the desert, the pyramid was a little taller than in the morning.

[pause]

It was dangerous too. A rope could snap. A sledge could slide back. So the overseers watched every pull with sharp eyes. And when a block finally reached the top and slid into its place, the whole team cheered.

[pause]

Nobody knows exactly what the ramps looked like. Perhaps there was one long straight ramp, leading up one side. Perhaps the ramp wound around the pyramid like a path around a hill. Perhaps there were small ramps inside the pyramid itself. Scientists and builders still argue about it today. They draw pictures and build models and test their ideas. And every few years, someone finds a new clue in the sand. It is one of the great puzzles of the pyramid.

[pause]

From the top of the ramps, the workers can see a very long way. They can see the green river valley stretching north and south. They can see the white walls of Memphis in the distance. They can see the boats on the river, with their sails like little white wings. On a clear day, some say, you can see the edge of the desert shimmering in the heat. For a moment, the tired workers stop and look. Then they turn back to their work.

[long-pause]

{img:aswan-granite-quarry-pink-stone-workers-pounding-balls-dolerite}
Most of the pyramid is limestone. But for the most important room inside, Khufu wants something stronger. Granite.

[pause]

Granite is a very hard stone, speckled pink and grey and black. It is much harder than limestone. It does not crumble or crack easily. It will last forever. And it is very beautiful when polished.

[long-pause]

But there is no granite near Giza. The nearest good granite is at Aswan. Aswan is far to the south, near the first great rapids of the Nile. It is more than eight hundred kilometres from Giza. That is a journey of many weeks by boat.

[pause]

At Aswan, the granite lies in big rounded boulders and cliffs along the river. The workers cannot cut it with copper chisels. It is too hard. So they pound it. They take balls of an even harder black stone. And they pound the granite again and again, crushing it bit by bit. Slowly they make a deep trench around the block. Then they lever it free.

[pause]

{img:huge-granite-beam-on-barge-river-nile-sailing-north-crew}
Some of the granite blocks for the pyramid are enormous. The biggest beams weigh fifty or sixty tons, maybe more. That is as heavy as about forty cars. How do you move something like that?

[long-pause]

You wait for the flood. When the river is high, the granite beams are dragged to the water's edge. Then they are loaded onto huge flat boats called barges. Very carefully. If a beam slipped, it could sink the barge. Then the barges drift north with the current, down the long river toward Giza. Rowers and steersmen guide them. It takes weeks.

[pause]

The crews are nervous the whole way. Rapids and sandbanks. Strong winds and sudden currents. A barge carrying fifty tons of stone is hard to steer. One mistake, and the stone would be lost at the bottom of the river forever.

[pause]

{img:granite-beam-arriving-giza-harbour-crowd-watching-ropes-ramps}
At last, the barges arrive at the harbour at Giza. Everyone comes to watch. The granite beams are unloaded with ramps and ropes and levers. Then they are dragged up the hill to the pyramid. And they are pulled up, slowly, higher and higher, toward the room at the heart of the pyramid. The king's chamber.

[long-pause]

The granite workers at Aswan have a hard life. It is the hottest place in Egypt. In summer the rocks are almost too hot to touch. The pounding goes on all day. Thump. Thump. Thump. The black stone balls wear down. The granite dust gets everywhere. But these men are very skilled. They know exactly how to strike the stone. And they are proud. Their granite will lie at the heart of the king's pyramid. Closest to the king of all.

[pause]

In the evenings at Aswan, the granite workers sit on the warm rocks by the river. The Nile is narrow there, and full of little islands and rocks. The water rushes and foams around them. The men eat their supper and watch the sun go down behind the desert hills. The rocks glow red and gold in the last light. Then they lie down to sleep under the stars, tired and content. Tomorrow, the pounding will start again.

[long-pause]

{img:great-pyramid-cross-section-passages-grand-gallery-chambers-diagram-like}
The Great Pyramid is not just a solid pile of stone. Inside it there are passages and rooms.

[pause]

The entrance is on the north side, high up the wall. From there, a narrow passage slopes down deep into the rock under the pyramid. It ends in a rough room that was never finished. Perhaps the builders changed their minds about where to put the king.

[long-pause]

Another passage leads up instead. It is low and narrow. You have to bend over to walk in it. It climbs up through the stone. And then suddenly it opens into something amazing. A great hall.

[pause]

It is called the Grand Gallery. It is long and very tall. Nearly fifty metres long and almost nine metres high. The walls are made of smooth limestone. They step inward as they go up, so the roof is narrow at the top. It is like walking into a tall stone canyon inside the pyramid. Even today, people who visit it stop and stare.

[pause]

{img:grand-gallery-interior-tall-stepped-walls-sloping-floor-lamplight}
Why did they build it? Nobody is quite sure. Perhaps to hold big granite blocks that would later slide down and seal the passage. Perhaps to help move stones. Perhaps simply because it was grand and beautiful. A fitting road for a king.

[long-pause]

From the Grand Gallery, a short low passage leads to another room. It is called the Queen's Chamber. But no queen was ever buried there. That name was given much later, by mistake. It is a quiet room with a pointed roof. Nobody knows what it was really for.

[pause]

From its walls, two very narrow shafts lead into the stone. They are only about twenty centimetres wide. Too small for a person. In our own time, scientists sent a little robot up one of them. After sixty metres it found a small stone door with two copper handles. Behind it, another door. What lies beyond? Nobody knows yet.

[pause]

{img:small-robot-narrow-shaft-stone-door-copper-pins-camera-light}
Some people think these shafts point toward certain stars. The Egyptians believed the king's spirit would rise to the stars in the north. Stars that never set. And so, perhaps, the shafts were roads for his spirit. Paths through the stone, pointing to the sky.

[long-pause]

All these passages had to be planned before the stone was laid around them. Every slope. Every turn. Every room. Hemiunu and his builders had to think everything through from the start. There was no going back. Once the stones were in place, they could not be moved.

[pause]

The builders who worked inside the pyramid had a dim and narrow world. They carried small oil lamps to see by, and the air was warm and still. Their voices sounded strange and close in the stone corridors. They worked slowly and carefully, fitting each block into place. When they came out into the daylight at the end of the day, they took deep breaths of the fresh air. And they looked up at the sky with real gladness.

[pause]

The passages inside the pyramid are cool, even on the hottest days. In the summer, some of the workers are glad to work inside. They carry their lamps and their tools down the long sloping corridors. They talk in low voices, because voices echo in the stone. Now and then, someone starts to hum a song. It sounds strange and beautiful in the dark passage, and the others join in softly.

[long-pause]

{img:kings-chamber-red-granite-walls-empty-sarcophagus-dim-light}
At the top of the Grand Gallery, a low passage leads to the most important room in the pyramid. The King's Chamber.

[pause]

It is built entirely of red granite. The walls, the floor, the ceiling. All granite from far away Aswan. The room is about ten metres long and five metres wide and almost six metres high. Its walls are smooth and bare. There are no paintings, no writing. Just the deep dark red of the stone.

[long-pause]

At one end stands a large box of granite without a lid. This is the sarcophagus. The stone coffin of the king. When Khufu passed away, his body was laid in it. Inside a wooden coffin, perhaps covered in gold.

[pause]

The roof of the room is made of nine huge granite beams. Each one weighs many tons. These are the beams that came all the way from Aswan by barge. Getting them up there was perhaps the hardest job in the whole pyramid.

[pause]

{img:relieving-chambers-above-kings-chamber-granite-beams-red-paint-marks}
But there was a danger. Above the King's Chamber is millions of tons of stone. What if the weight cracked the ceiling? What if it all came crashing down on the king? Hemiunu thought of that too.

[long-pause]

Above the ceiling, he built five more small rooms, one on top of the other. They are low, like crawl spaces. Each has a roof of granite beams. And at the very top there is a pointed roof of big limestone blocks, leaning against each other like a tent. This spreads the great weight to the sides, away from the king's chamber. And it worked. The ceiling has cracked a little. But it has held for four and a half thousand years.

[pause]

In those small rooms above, nobody was ever supposed to go. So the workers did not bother to make them neat. And they left their marks. Red paint on the walls. Building notes and lines. And the names of their teams. One of them reads, the friends of Khufu. Another has the king's name in a little oval frame. These marks are like the workers' signatures. Hidden in the dark, for thousands of years.

[pause]

{img:workers-red-paint-team-name-hidden-chamber-wall-cartouche-khufu}
When Khufu was laid to rest, the passages were sealed with great blocks of granite. They slid down from the Grand Gallery and blocked the way. The king was to rest in peace forever, in the heart of his mountain of stone.

[long-pause]

It did not quite work out that way. Much later, thieves broke in and took everything. When modern explorers entered, the room was empty. Only the great stone coffin was left. But the room itself is still there. Silent and dark and red. Exactly as the builders left it.

[pause]

Above the King's Chamber, the builders placed five small rooms, one on top of the other. They are low and dark, and they help to carry the great weight of the stone above. In these little rooms, the workers left marks in red paint. They painted the names of their teams, and the name of the king. Nobody was ever meant to see them again. But when explorers found them, thousands of years later, it was like hearing the voices of the builders.

[long-pause]

{img:khufu-solar-boat-cedar-wood-long-elegant-prow-museum-hall}
Next to the pyramid, the builders dug long pits in the rock. And in them, they buried boats.

[pause]

In 1954, an Egyptian archaeologist was clearing sand on the south side of the pyramid. He found a row of huge stone blocks set into the ground. He made a small hole between two of them. He looked in. And he smelled something. Cedar wood. After four and a half thousand years, the smell of the wood was still there.

[long-pause]

Under the blocks lay a boat. Not whole, but taken apart into more than twelve hundred pieces. Neatly stacked in layers, like a giant kit. There were planks and oars and beams. There were ropes and cabin walls. And even little marks to show where each piece went.

[pause]

It took years to put it back together. A skilled restorer worked on it for more than ten years. He studied the old boats of Egypt and the boats still used on the Nile. He tried the pieces again and again. And at last, the whole boat stood complete. It is more than forty metres long. Long and slim and elegant, with a tall curved prow like a papyrus flower.

[pause]

{img:boat-builder-restorer-fitting-cedar-planks-ropes-workshop-careful}
The boat is made of cedar wood from Lebanon, far to the north. Its planks are not held together with nails. They are sewn together with ropes. When the boat is put in water, the wood swells and the seams close tightly. It is a clever way to build a boat that does not leak.

[long-pause]

What was the boat for? Some think it carried Khufu's body on the Nile to Giza. Others think it was a boat for his spirit. The Egyptians believed the sun god sailed across the sky each day in a boat. And the king would join him. So the boat might be his ship to heaven.

[pause]

{img:sun-god-boat-sailing-sky-golden-light-egyptian-belief-dawn}
There was a second boat in a pit next to the first. For many years it stayed buried. Recently, archaeologists lifted it out piece by piece. Now it too is being put back together. Both boats can be seen in the great new museum near the pyramids. Long and dark and graceful, as if they could still sail away.

[long-pause]

When the first boat was found, the archaeologist who opened the pit was very careful. The air inside had been sealed for thousands of years. He knew that fresh air and light could damage the old wood. So the pit was opened slowly. The pieces were lifted out one by one and treated to keep them from cracking. Every piece was numbered and drawn. It was like a giant puzzle with no picture on the box. And yet, piece by piece, the boat came back together.

[pause]

The great boat was made of cedar wood, brought all the way from the mountains of Lebanon. Cedar smells sweet and fresh, and it lasts a very long time. The boat builders shaped each plank carefully with copper tools. They tied the planks together with ropes, without using any nails. When the wood got wet, it swelled, and the boat became watertight. Today, if you stand near the old boat, people say you can still smell the cedar.

[long-pause]

{img:egyptian-priests-sunrise-temple-giza-ra-sun-disc-offering}
Why did Khufu want a pyramid at all? Why this shape?

[pause]

To understand, we have to look at what the Egyptians believed. They believed that when a person passes away, their spirit lives on. But the journey to the next life is long and full of dangers. The body must be kept safe. The tomb must be strong. And there must be food and drink and prayers, forever.

[long-pause]

For the king, it is even more important. The king is the son of the sun god. When he passes away, he must join the gods in the sky. If his journey fails, the whole order of the world could be in danger. So his tomb must be the greatest of all.

[pause]

The shape of the pyramid may come from the sun. When the sun breaks through clouds, its rays spread down in a great slanting triangle. Some think the pyramid is like those rays turned into stone. A stairway of light for the king to climb to heaven.

[pause]

{img:sunbeams-through-clouds-over-desert-pyramid-shape-light-rays}
Or it may come from an old story of how the world began. In the beginning, the Egyptians said, there was only dark water. Then a mound of earth rose out of the water. And on it, the sun god appeared and made the world. Every year, when the flood goes down, mounds of earth appear from the water just like that. The pyramid may be a giant version of that first mound. The place where life begins.

[long-pause]

And then there are the stars. Around the north of the sky, some stars circle the pole and never set. The Egyptians called them the stars that never die. They believed the king's spirit would rise up and become one of them. That is why the entrance of the pyramid faces north. And why the shafts point toward the stars.

[pause]

{img:night-sky-giza-north-stars-circling-pyramid-silhouette-calm}
Next to the pyramid, there is a temple where priests make offerings every day. Bread and beer, incense and flowers. They pray for the king. They say his name. In Egypt, to say someone's name is to keep them alive. As long as your name is spoken, you are not truly gone.

[long-pause]

So the pyramid is many things. A tomb and a stairway. A mountain of beginning. A road to the stars. And a promise that the king will never be forgotten. In that, at least, Khufu succeeded. His name is still spoken today, more than four and a half thousand years later. By millions of people, all over the world.

[pause]

On clear nights, the priests of Egypt stand on the roofs of the temples and watch the sky. They know the stars very well. They watch the bright star that rises just before the flood each year. When they see it at dawn, they know the river will rise soon. They watch the stars that circle the north and never set. They call them the stars that never die. And they hope the king will join them there, shining forever in the night sky.

[long-pause]

{img:dawn-workers-town-men-waking-stretching-hearth-pyramid-silhouette}
Let us spend one day with a young worker at Giza.

[pause]

His name is Neferhotep. He is nineteen. He comes from a village a few days up the river. His father is a farmer. Every year, during the flood, Neferhotep comes to Giza to work on the pyramid. This is his third year. He belongs to a team of twenty men. Their team is called the Strong Ones. They are proud of the name.

[long-pause]

He wakes before dawn in the long sleeping hall. Around him, the other men are yawning and stretching. Someone is already blowing on the coals of the hearth. Neferhotep splashes water on his face from a clay jar. He ties his short linen kilt around his waist. That is all he wears. It is going to be hot.

[pause]

Breakfast is bread and onions and a cup of beer. The men eat quickly, sitting on the porch. They talk about yesterday. Another team pulled two more blocks than they did. That cannot happen again, says the team leader. The men grumble and laugh.

[pause]

{img:workers-walking-up-hill-to-pyramid-dawn-ropes-over-shoulders}
Then they walk up the hill in the grey light. The pyramid looms ahead of them, dark against the sky. Ropes hang over their shoulders. All around them, other teams are walking up too. Hundreds of men in the dawn, all going the same way. Their feet kick up dust. Somewhere a man starts singing. Others join in.

[long-pause]

At the foot of the ramp, a block is waiting on a sledge. It came from the quarry yesterday. The team ties their ropes to the sledge. The leader checks every knot. Then he takes his place at the front. He raises his hand.

[pause]

Pull, he calls. And twenty men lean into the ropes together. The sledge creaks. It does not move. Pull! It shifts a little. A man runs forward and pours water on the sand. Pull! And it slides.

[pause]

{img:team-straining-ropes-sledge-moving-up-ramp-water-pourer-sun-rising}
Up the ramp they go, slowly, step by step. The sun climbs higher. Sweat runs down their backs. Their hands burn on the rough ropes. Neferhotep's legs ache. But he does not let go. Nobody lets go. If one man stops, the whole team feels it. Halfway up, the leader calls a rest. They sit on the ramp, panting. Water carriers come by. Neferhotep drinks a whole jar.

[long-pause]

Then up again. At the top of the ramp, other men are waiting. Setters. Their job is to put the block exactly in its place. They use wooden levers and a little plaster. They check it with a long wooden ruler and a string with a weight. Is it straight? Is it level? The overseer looks. He nods. Good. And the team's scribe makes a mark on his tally.

[pause]

{img:setters-levering-block-into-place-top-layer-overseer-checking-line}
By midday, they have moved three blocks. They eat bread and dried fish in the shade of a canvas. They lie on their backs and rest. Above them, the sky is white with heat. Neferhotep closes his eyes. He thinks of his village. Of his mother's bread. Of a girl who lives near the river. Next year, he thinks, maybe I will ask her father.

[long-pause]

In the afternoon they move two more blocks. That makes five. As the sun gets lower, the overseer comes by. Five, he says. The other team made four. The Strong Ones cheer. Some of them hug each other. The leader grins. Tonight, he says, there will be extra beer.

[pause]

At midday, the Strong Ones rest in the shade of a reed awning. Neferhotep eats his bread slowly and looks out over the river. He can see the green fields stretching far away to the south. Somewhere down there is his own village, and his mother and father and little sisters. He wonders what they are doing right now. Perhaps his mother is baking bread, just like the bakers here. He smiles at the thought, and then the overseer calls them back to work.

[long-pause]

{img:workers-walking-down-hill-evening-pyramid-golden-light-long-shadows}
As the sun sets behind the desert, the work stops for the day.

[pause]

The teams coil their ropes and walk back down the hill. The pyramid glows gold and orange in the last light. Long shadows stretch across the sand. Below, in the workers' town, smoke is already rising from the cooking fires.

[long-pause]

Neferhotep and his team walk down slowly. They are tired and dusty and sore. But they are in a good mood. Five blocks. A good day. They joke about the team that only made four. They wonder what the cook has made tonight.

[pause]

At the edge of town there is a long basin of water. The men wash off the dust. They splash each other and laugh. The water is cool and brown and wonderful. Neferhotep lies back in it and looks up at the sky. It is turning from orange to pink to deep blue.

[pause]

{img:workers-washing-water-basin-evening-laughing-town-behind-smoke}
Back at the long hall, the evening meal is ready. Bread and beer, of course. But tonight there is meat too. A stew of beef with onions and lentils. And the extra beer the leader promised. The men sit on the porch and eat and drink. Someone tells a story about a famous magician who could put a cut-off head back on a goose. Everyone has heard it before. Everyone laughs anyway.

[long-pause]

Then someone takes out a little flute made of reed. He plays a simple tune. Others clap the beat. Neferhotep sings along. It is a song from his village, about the river and the flood and a farmer who loves his wife. Some of the older men close their eyes. They are thinking of home too.

[pause]

{img:workers-sitting-porch-night-reed-flute-singing-lamps-stars}
Above them, the stars come out. Thousands and thousands of them, bright and close. The Egyptians know them well. There is the long river of light across the sky. There is the bright star that rises just before the flood. And there are the stars of the north, circling and circling, never setting. Up there, they say, the king will go when his time comes.

[long-pause]

One by one the men go to sleep on their platforms in the long hall. The fires burn down. The town grows quiet. Only the night watchmen walk the streets with their lamps. Neferhotep lies on his mat. His body aches all over. But he falls asleep almost at once. And above him on the hill, the great unfinished pyramid stands silent and dark under the stars, waiting for morning.

[pause]

Before he sleeps, Neferhotep thinks about the end of the flood season. Soon the river will go down, and he will go home to help his father in the fields. He will bring presents for his family, some linen cloth and a little pot of honey. His little sisters will run out to meet him on the path. His mother will cook his favourite stew of lentils and onions. And in the evenings, he will tell them all about the great pyramid.

[long-pause]

{img:pyramid-near-completion-white-casing-stones-top-down-workers-scaffold}
After many years of work, the pyramid is nearly finished.

[pause]

Layer after layer, it has risen up from the rock. Now it is almost at its full height. Higher than any building ever made by people. From the top, the workers can see for many kilometres. The green ribbon of the Nile. The city of Memphis. The endless desert to the west. The people below look as small as ants.

[long-pause]

But the pyramid still looks rough. Its sides are made of big uneven steps. Now it needs its skin. The smooth white covering of fine Tura limestone. The stone that Merer and men like him carried across the river, boat after boat.

[pause]

The white blocks are laid as the pyramid goes up. Each one is cut at an angle on the outside. When they are all in place, the sides will be perfectly smooth and slanting. Like four great white mirrors facing the four directions.

[pause]

{img:polishing-casing-stones-workers-smoothing-white-limestone-sun}
Then comes the most exciting moment of all. The capstone. The very last stone. The point at the top. It is a small pyramid of its own. Some think it may have been covered with gold or bright metal, so it would shine in the sun. Nobody knows for sure. But everyone knows it is special.

[long-pause]

Getting it to the top is tricky. It must be lifted to the very highest point. There is almost no room up there to stand. The workers must be very careful. One slip, and the stone or a man could fall all the way down. Everyone below watches and holds their breath. Slowly, carefully, the capstone is pushed into place. And it fits.

[pause]

Then the workers start at the top and work their way down. They remove the ramps bit by bit. And as they go down, they smooth and polish the white casing. Block by block. Until the whole pyramid shines.

[pause]

{img:capstone-placed-summit-tiny-figures-cheering-sun-glinting-gold}
When it is finished, the Great Pyramid is dazzling. A huge white shape on the edge of the desert, shining in the sun. People in boats on the Nile can see it from far away. At sunrise it glows pink. At noon it glows white. At sunset it glows gold. It looks less like a building and more like a piece of the sky that has come down to earth.

[long-pause]

Today, most of that white skin is gone. Much later, people took the white stones away to build houses and mosques in Cairo. Only a few casing stones are still in place, at the bottom. But if you stand close to them, you can see how smooth they are. And how tightly they fit. You can hardly slide a sheet of paper between them.

[pause]

When the pyramid was new and white, it must have been dazzling to see. Travellers on the river would have seen it shining from many kilometres away. In the morning, it glowed pink in the rising sun. At midday, it was too bright to look at for long. In the evening, it turned gold and then soft blue. And on nights with a full moon, it shone silver and pale above the dark desert, like a mountain made of light.

[long-pause]

{img:three-pyramids-giza-khufu-khafre-menkaure-sphinx-desert-afternoon}
Khufu's pyramid is not alone at Giza for long.

[pause]

His son becomes king after him. His name is Khafre. He builds his own pyramid right next to his father's. It is a little smaller. But it stands on higher ground. So from some places, it looks even taller. Clever. Khafre's pyramid still has a bit of its white casing at the very top. It looks like a little white cap.

[long-pause]

Later, another king of the family, Menkaure, builds a third pyramid. It is much smaller than the first two. But it is covered partly in red granite, which must have looked beautiful.

[pause]

Around the three great pyramids, there are smaller pyramids for the queens. There are temples and long covered roads. There are rows and rows of tombs for princes and officials and priests. Giza becomes a whole city of the dead. A quiet place of stone in the desert.

[pause]

{img:great-sphinx-giza-lion-body-human-face-pyramid-behind-sunrise}
And next to Khafre's pyramid, the builders carve something else. They find a big lump of rock that was left in the quarry. And they shape it into a giant lion lying down. With the head of a king. It is the Great Sphinx.

[long-pause]

The Sphinx is huge. It is more than seventy metres long and twenty metres high. Its face looks east, toward the rising sun. Most experts think it shows Khafre himself. Wearing the striped headcloth of a king. It was once painted in bright colours. Red and yellow and blue. Traces of paint can still be found.

[pause]

Over the centuries, the desert sand blew over the Sphinx again and again. Often only its head stuck out. There is an old story about a young prince who fell asleep in its shade. In a dream, the Sphinx spoke to him. Clear away the sand that is choking me, it said. And I will make you king. The prince cleared away the sand. And he did become king. He set up a stone tablet between the Sphinx's paws to tell the story. It is still there today.

[pause]

{img:dream-stele-between-sphinx-paws-sand-cleared-carved-tablet}
The Sphinx has guarded Giza for more than four and a half thousand years. It has watched kings come and go. It has seen armies and travellers and tourists. It has lost its nose and part of its beard. But it is still there, lying calmly in the sand, looking toward the sunrise.

[pause]

Around the great pyramids, there are many smaller tombs, built in long neat rows like streets. They belong to the princes and princesses, the officials and the priests. Inside some of them, the walls are painted with scenes of daily life. There are farmers ploughing and fishermen with their nets. There are bakers baking bread and brewers making beer. There are musicians and dancers, and children playing games. These paintings show us how ordinary Egyptians lived, all those years ago.

[pause]

Khufu's son Khafre built the second pyramid, and his grandson Menkaure built the third. Each pyramid has its own temples and causeways, leading down toward the river. Priests lived and worked in those temples for many years after the kings had passed away. Every day, they brought offerings of bread and beer and flowers. They burned incense and said the old prayers. And in this way, the kings were remembered and cared for, long after they were gone.

[long-pause]

{img:giza-pyramids-roman-era-travellers-camels-wondering-desert}
Khufu was laid to rest in his pyramid. The priests made offerings at his temple for many years. And slowly, the age of the great pyramids came to an end.

[pause]

Later kings still built pyramids. But they were smaller and less well made. Egypt changed. It had good times and hard times. It was ruled by many kings over thousands of years. But nobody ever built anything like the Great Pyramid again.

[long-pause]

About two thousand years after Khufu, a Greek traveller called Herodotus visited Egypt. He saw the pyramids and wrote about them. He was told they were built by a hundred thousand men, working in shifts of three months. And he was told the workers ate huge amounts of radishes and onions and garlic. He even wrote down how much it cost. Some of what he wrote was wrong. But he was amazed, like everyone after him.

[pause]

The Greeks made a list of the seven wonders of the world. The Great Pyramid was on it. It was already very old then. All the other wonders on that list have disappeared. Only the Great Pyramid is still standing.

[pause]

{img:medieval-cairo-people-taking-casing-stones-camels-pyramid-distance}
Over the centuries, robbers broke into the pyramid. They took everything they could find. Later, people took the white casing stones to build the new city of Cairo. The smooth white skin was stripped away. What was left were the rough steps we see today.

[long-pause]

Then, in the last two hundred years, scientists and explorers came. They measured the pyramid. They crawled into its passages. They found the workers' marks in the small rooms. They dug up the workers' town. They found Merer's diary. They rebuilt the boat. And bit by bit, the story of how the pyramid was built came back to light.

[pause]

There are still mysteries. In 2017, scientists used tiny particles from space that can pass through stone. With them, they looked inside the pyramid without breaking anything. And they found a big empty space above the Grand Gallery. Nobody knew it was there. What is it? A hidden room? A space left to lighten the weight? Nobody knows yet.

[pause]

{img:scientists-instruments-inside-pyramid-passage-lights-measuring-today}
So the Great Pyramid still has secrets. After four and a half thousand years, it is still teaching us. And maybe it always will.

[pause]

Over the centuries, many travellers came to see the pyramids. Greek and Roman visitors came and wrote about them in their books. Some carved their names into the stones, just to say they had been there. Later, explorers came with measuring tapes and notebooks. They climbed up the outside and crawled through the narrow passages inside. And every one of them stood at the foot of the Great Pyramid and looked up in wonder.

[pause]

Today, scientists use very modern tools to study the pyramid. They use special cameras that can see through stone, using tiny particles from space. A few years ago, they found a hidden empty space inside the Great Pyramid, above the Grand Gallery. Nobody knows what it is for. Perhaps it helped the builders in some way. Perhaps it is simply a space that was left empty. They are still studying it, carefully and patiently.

[long-pause]

{img:giza-pyramids-today-cairo-city-edge-desert-sunset-visitors}
Today the pyramids of Giza stand at the edge of a huge city. Cairo, the capital of Egypt, has grown right up to the foot of the hill. More than twenty million people live there. From the top of the hill, you can see busy roads and tall buildings. And then, on the other side, the open desert.

[long-pause]

Millions of visitors come every year. They come from all over the world. They stand at the foot of the Great Pyramid and look up. Most of them say the same thing. It is so much bigger than I thought. Each step is taller than a person. The pyramid seems to go up forever.

[pause]

Some visitors go inside. They climb up the low narrow passage, bent over. Then they reach the Grand Gallery and can stand up straight. They climb up to the King's Chamber. And they stand in the red granite room, in the quiet, in the heart of the pyramid. Some people touch the walls. Some just stand and breathe. It is very still in there.

[pause]

{img:visitors-inside-grand-gallery-climbing-wooden-steps-lights-today}
Near the pyramids, there is a great new museum. It is one of the biggest museums in the world. In it are many of the treasures of ancient Egypt. And Khufu's great cedar boat, long and dark and graceful. The little ivory statue of Khufu is in a museum in Cairo. Smaller than your hand. The only face we have of the man who built the biggest pyramid.

[long-pause]

And in the workers' town, archaeologists are still digging. Every season they find something new. A bakery. A house. A grave. A name. Slowly they are bringing the builders back to life. The real people who made the pyramid.

[pause]

{img:archaeologists-brushing-sand-workers-town-ruins-giza-today-careful}
Near the pyramids, there is a busy modern town, with shops and cafés and noisy streets. Camels and horses wait for visitors at the edge of the desert. Children sell little stone pyramids and postcards. And in the evening, families sit outside their houses and watch the sun go down behind the three great shapes. For them, the pyramids are simply part of home, as they have been for thousands of years.

[pause]

So we leave Giza tonight, in the year 2560 before Christ, as the evening falls.
